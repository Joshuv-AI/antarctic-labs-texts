Emergency vehicle signal priority control method for arterial intersections in an intelligent connected environment
Authors: Sen Cao, Xingchen Zhang, Wenfang Li, Pengfei Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emergency vehicle (EV) progression at consecutive arterial intersections in an intelligent connected environment is often disrupted by upstream queues, downstream spillback, and mismatches in multi-intersection signal coordination. To address this problem, this study proposes a spatiotemporal graph multi-agent proximal policy optimization (STG-MAPPO) method for EV signal priority control. The proposed method models consecutive signalized intersections as cooperative agents, incorporates EV arrival prediction, target-lane yielding capacity, and downstream blockage risk into the state representation, and uses a spatiotemporal graph encoder to capture both topological coupling and traffic-state propagation among intersections. Heterogeneous yielding responses of connected and automated vehicles (CAVs) and human-driven vehicles (HDVs) are explicitly considered in the target-lane yielding model. Simulation experiments based on the Zhengzhou Longhu Autonomous Driving Test Zone show that STG-MAPPO achieves better EV progression efficiency, lower general-traffic delay, and improved safety indicators than fixed-time control, actuated control, rule-based EV priority, DDQN, IPPO, and MAPPO. Under the specified SUMO-based single-EV simulation conditions, the proposed method reduces EV travel time, EV delay, and EV stop frequency by 22.28%, 51.02%, and 60.00%, respectively, compared with DDQN, and by 8.54%, 22.58%, and 33.33%, respectively, compared with MAPPO.

Introduction

As urban traffic demand continues to grow, the operation of consecutive signalized intersections along arterial corridors becomes increasingly complex, making rapid and reliable emergency vehicle (EV) progression more difficult to guarantee. Emergency vehicles, including fire engines, ambulances, and police vehicles, are highly time-sensitive, and their operational efficiency is closely tied to incident response, medical rescue, and public safety. Existing studies generally identify route optimization, dispatch organization, and signal priority control as key components of urban emergency response systems1. Among them, signal preemption and priority release remain central strategies for improving EV mobility2. In real arterial environments, however, EV progression across consecutive intersections is often hindered by upstream queues3, poorly coordinated phase transitions4, downstream spillback, interference from non-priority traffic5, and additional operational losses imposed on general traffic6. These factors typically result in longer travel times, more stops, and degraded corridor performance. How to ensure rapid EV passage while preserving stable traffic operation across consecutive intersections therefore remains a critical challenge in intelligent traffic control.

Most conventional EV priority strategies are built on local preemption and rule-based triggering mechanisms. Green extension, early phase switching7, and priority phase insertion are commonly used to provide intersection-level right-of-way for EVs8. These methods are attractive because they are straightforward to implement and relatively inexpensive to deploy, particularly in isolated-intersection scenarios. They can also relieve local pressure under abnormal traffic conditions to some extent 9. Their main limitation, however, is that control decisions are usually based on local detection and immediate-response logic at the current intersection. As a result, they cannot adequately capture inter-intersection phase coordination, queue propagation, or recovery dynamics along an arterial corridor. Priority granted at one intersection does not necessarily translate into sustained corridor-level benefits for an EV10. When downstream links remain congested or supply is insufficient, the gains achieved locally can quickly be offset by renewed delay and blockage at the next intersection.

This interdependence among consecutive arterial intersections has been well documented in traffic network studies. Research on dynamic system-optimal control under queue spillback conditions shows that queue location and spatial distribution can substantially alter the effectiveness of local control actions11. Studies on optimal queue layout in single-origin–single-destination networks further indicate that system performance depends strongly on whether queues form at critical locations12. Work on dynamic network disequilibrium also suggests that local congestion and structural imbalances may accumulate and propagate over space and time13. Taken together, these findings indicate that EV priority control along consecutive urban arterials is not simply a single-intersection preemption problem; rather, it is a system-level optimization problem with pronounced spatial coupling and dynamic propagation effects. Priority release at an upstream intersection influences downstream nodes through queue discharge, traffic fluctuations, and coordination relationships, while downstream capacity and recovery conditions in turn constrain the actual effectiveness of upstream priority actions14.

The emergence of intelligent connected transportation systems is reshaping the control environment at urban intersections. Studies across different vehicle technology levels show that the transition from conventional vehicles to connected vehicles and then to connected and automated vehicles (CAVs) substantially affects both signal control logic and operational performance15. Under connected traffic conditions, signal control can exploit vehicle platooning capabilities to improve discharge efficiency16. In mixed traffic, joint optimization of signal timing and vehicle trajectories has been shown to improve speed regulation, queue organization, and passing order with greater precision17. Related studies further report that stronger vehicle controllability improves overall intersection performance18, while network-level coordination between speed guidance and signal control enhances the collaborative control capability of multi-intersection systems19. At the same time, research on EV services in Internet-of-Things environments suggests that real-time access to EV location, speed, and route information provides an important foundation for anticipatory priority control20. These advances imply that EV priority control in intelligent connected environments should address not only phase decisions, but also vehicle-type heterogeneity in yielding response and lane-clearing capability.

On the methodological side, deep reinforcement learning (DRL) offers a promising framework for real-time traffic signal control in complex and dynamic environments. Previous studies show that multi-agent deep learning-based control can jointly optimize traffic operations and EV guidance tasks21. Federated multi-agent reinforcement learning frameworks have also demonstrated strong scalability in real-time signal optimization22. Deep Q-learning-based approaches to arterial signal transition control further suggest that DRL is well suited to corridor-level dynamic control problems23. In the EV priority context, learning-based control in connected environments has been shown to reduce EV travel time and improve system responsiveness24. Multi-agent reinforcement learning frameworks that jointly model EV routing and signal control also report significant improvements in EV travel efficiency and network-level coordination25. These findings suggest that DRL, particularly multi-agent DRL, is well suited to EV priority control at consecutive urban intersections because coordinated policies can be learned through continuous interaction with high-dimensional, time-varying, and nonlinear traffic environments.

Existing adaptive or rule-based priority methods usually focus on local response logic and do not fully represent queue propagation, target-lane yielding capability, or multi-node spatial coupling. This limitation may cause forced green extension at an upstream intersection to increase downstream saturation or delay ordinary traffic excessively.

To address these limitations, this study formulates EV priority control for consecutive signalized intersections as a cooperative multi-agent decision problem. Compared with conventional rule-based priority and non-graph reinforcement learning baselines, the proposed method explicitly incorporates EV arrival prediction, CAV/HDV heterogeneous yielding capacity, downstream blockage risk, and spatiotemporal graph-based inter-intersection coordination.

System model and problem description

Study scenario description

This study considered an urban arterial system with consecutive signalized intersections in an intelligent connected environment. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the study corridor consists of a sequence of adjacent intersections arranged along a main arterial, with neighboring intersections connected by arterial links. EVs travel along the arterial to perform emergency response tasks. Traffic in the network was composed of CAVs and HDVs. CAVs were assumed to obtain surrounding traffic states and control information in real time through vehicle-to-vehicle communication and vehicle-to-infrastructure coordination, whereas HDVs made driving decisions primarily based on driver judgment and experience. The system was equipped with sensing devices, signal controllers, and edge-computing units to collect real-time operational data, including lane-level traffic flow, lane speed, signal phase status, and EV position, which were then fed into the control model for dynamic decision-making.Fig. 1EV priority passage at urban arterial signalized intersections in an intelligent connected environment.

EV priority passage at urban arterial signalized intersections in an intelligent connected environment.

Unlike conventional arterial coordination, EV priority control exhibits pronounced dynamic propagation and spatial coupling characteristics. On the one hand, the operating conditions encountered by an EV while traversing consecutive intersections vary continuously because of upstream queue spillback, signal phase transitions, and downstream congestion propagation. On the other hand, the priority control action implemented at one intersection affects downstream neighboring intersections through queue discharge, traffic-flow perturbations, and signal coordination relationships, thereby generating a clear chain-propagation effect. Accordingly, the consecutive arterial intersections were modeled in this study as a coordinated multi-node control system. Particular attention was given to three critical issues in intelligent connected mixed traffic: heterogeneity in yielding responses across vehicle types, anticipatory mechanisms for downstream intersections, and joint optimization across multiple intersections.

Methodology

To address the multi-node coupling characteristics of EV priority control at consecutive arterial intersections, a coordinated control framework based on multi-agent deep reinforcement learning was developed, as shown in Fig. 2. The framework consists of four layers: a traffic-state perception layer, an EV prediction layer, a graph-based collaborative representation layer, and a multi-agent policy optimization layer.Fig. 2Coordinated control framework based on multi-agent deep reinforcement learning.

Coordinated control framework based on multi-agent deep reinforcement learning.

The traffic-state perception layer was used to extract arterial operational data from roadside detectors, vehicle–infrastructure coordination units, and signal controllers, including queue length, average speed, vehicle composition, signal phase status, and EV operating state. Based on the current EV position, speed, and mixed-traffic yielding capacity, the EV prediction layer was used to rollingly estimate the arrival times of the EV at the current and downstream intersections. The graph-based collaborative representation layer abstracted the arterial intersections as a spatiotemporal graph, in which neighborhood relations and dynamic edge weights were used to characterize the spatiotemporal dependencies among adjacent intersections. The multi-agent policy optimization layer learned intersection-level control policies under a centralized-training and decentralized-execution framework, so that rapid EV progression and stable overall traffic operation could be jointly optimized.

State function

This state design allowed the model to capture both the local operational characteristics of individual intersections and the dynamic propagation of the EV along the arterial, thereby providing an informational basis for coordinated multi-intersection control.

Action function

To ensure that the control actions had clear traffic-engineering meaning and could be directly implemented, the control action of agent i at decision epoch t was defined as the joint selection of the next phase to be executed and its corresponding green duration, i.e.,15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{i}^{t} = \left( {p_{i}^{{t,{\mathrm{sel}}}} {,}\,g_{i}^{{t,{\mathrm{sel}}}} } \right)$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{i}^{{t,{\mathrm{sel}}}}$$\end{document} denotes the phase selected for execution in the current decision cycle, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{i}^{{t,{\mathrm{sel}}}}$$\end{document} denotes the green duration assigned to that phase. Under this formulation, signal control was no longer decomposed into semantic actions such as hold, extend, or switch; instead, it was uniformly represented as a joint decision over the next executed phase and its green-time level.

Let the executable phase set of intersection i be16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{i} = \left\{ {p_{i,1} ,p_{i,2} , \cdots \,,p_{{i,P_{i} }} } \right\}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{i}^{t}$$\end{document} denotes the number of executable phases at intersection i. Because signal control must satisfy the minimum green time, yellow interval, all-red clearance time, and phase transition logic, the feasible action set of intersection i at time t was defined as17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{i}^{t} = \left\{ {\left( {p{,}g} \right)\left| {p \in F_{i}^{t} ,g \in G_{i} } \right.} \right\}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{i}^{t}$$\end{document} denotes the set of executable phases at the current decision epoch, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_{i}$$\end{document} denotes the discrete set of green-duration levels.

For the executable phase set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{i}^{t}$$\end{document}, the following definition was used:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{i}^{t} = \left\{ {g_{i}^{t} \ge g_{i}^{{{\mathrm{min}}}} } \\ \end{array} } \right.$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{i}^{t}$$\end{document} denotes the current phase of intersection i at time t, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{i}^{t}$$\end{document} denotes the elapsed green time of the current phase, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{i}^{{{\mathrm{min}}}}$$\end{document} denotes the minimum green time, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{i}^{{{\mathrm{tran}}}} \left( {p_{i}^{t} } \right)$$\end{document} denotes the set of candidate phases reachable from the current phase \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{i}^{t}$$\end{document} under the phase transition rules. This definition implies that, when the current phase has not yet satisfied the minimum green-time requirement, the controller is only allowed to maintain that phase. Once the minimum green-time constraint has been met, the controller may either keep the current phase or switch to a reachable alternative phase.

The discrete green-duration set was defined as19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_{i} = \left\{ {g_{i}^{\min } ,\;g_{i}^{\min } + \Delta g,\;g_{i}^{\min } + 2\Delta g,\; \cdots ,\;g_{i}^{\max } } \right\}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g_{i}^{{{\mathrm{max}}}}$$\end{document} denotes the maximum green time for a single phase at intersection i, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Delta }g$$\end{document} denotes the discretization step size for green duration. This discretization preserved sufficient control resolution while avoiding the training instability often associated with continuous action optimization.

In the EV priority control setting, let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{e,i}^{t}$$\end{document} denote the target phase that serves the EV at intersection i. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{e,i}^{t} \in F_{i}^{t}$$\end{document}, the agent could directly select the target phase and assign an appropriate green duration so that the EV could be released earlier. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{e,i}^{t} \notin F_{i}^{t}$$\end{document}, the agent was required to first satisfy the minimum service requirement of the current phase and the phase-transition safety constraints, and then gradually move toward the target phase through subsequent decisions. In this way, EV priority was not implemented by introducing ad hoc temporary actions or manually designed rules, but rather through a unified phase-selection and green-time allocation mechanism.

Accordingly, the action execution rule at intersection i was expressed as20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( {p_{i}^{t + 1} ,g_{i}^{t + 1} } \right) = \left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}c} {\left( {p_{i}^{t} ,\;g_{i}^{{t,{\mathrm{sel}}}} } \right),} & {p_{i}^{{t,{\mathrm{sel}}}} = p_{i}^{t} } \\ {\left( {p_{i}^{{t,{\mathrm{sel}}}} ,\;g_{i}^{{t,{\mathrm{sel}}}} } \right),} & {p_{i}^{{t,{\mathrm{sel}}}} \ne p_{i}^{t} } \\ \end{array} } \right.$$\end{document}

When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{i}^{{t,{\mathrm{sel}}}} = p_{i}^{t}$$\end{document}, the current phase continued to operate and its target green duration was updated accordingly. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{i}^{{t,{\mathrm{sel}}}} \ne p_{i}^{t}$$\end{document}, the phase was switched to the newly selected phase after satisfying the required yellow and all-red clearance intervals, and the assigned green duration was then applied. Under this unified formulation, green extension, early switching, and priority release were all incorporated into a standardized signal-control action framework with clear semantics and direct engineering interpretability.

For a typical four-phase intersection, if each phase was associated with K discrete green-time levels, the upper bound of the action-space size at a single decision epoch was21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left| {A_{i}^{t} } \right| \le P_{i} \times K$$\end{document}

In the consecutive arterial intersection scenario considered in this study, this action-space scale was sufficient for training the multi-agent deep reinforcement learning model while still maintaining enough flexibility to respond to changes in EV arrival time, target-lane yielding capacity, and downstream blockage risk.

Reward function

EV priority control at consecutive arterial intersections is inherently a multi-objective coordinated optimization problem. The control objective was not limited to reducing EV travel time and the number of stops, but also included maintaining the operating efficiency of general traffic, mitigating queue accumulation, and preserving system safety and stability. If raw variables such as EV delay, number of stops, average delay of general traffic, queue length, downstream blockage risk, and safety surrogate measures were directly combined through a linear weighted sum, the large differences in physical units and numerical scales across indicators would make the reward terms difficult to interpret and would allow large-magnitude variables to dominate the overall reward. This, in turn, would weaken the interpretability of the weighting parameters and impair the stability of reinforcement learning. Therefore, instead of applying simple normalization, the original quantities were first transformed into dimensionless performance indicators with clear traffic-engineering meaning, based on which a hierarchical reward structure was constructed.

The reward function was decomposed into two components, namely, an EV-priority layer and a system-coordination layer. The EV-priority layer was designed to characterize EV progression efficiency and priority-service quality using three dimensionless indicators: the EV delay ratio, the EV stop-loss rate, and the effective priority service level.

These coefficients determine the relative importance of time-loss reduction, stop suppression, and effective priority service in the EV-priority objective. In this study, the EV delay ratio was defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{e,d}^{t} = {{D_{e,i}^{t} } \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{D_{e,i}^{t} } {T_{e,i}^{{\mathrm{f}}} + D_{e,i}^{t} }}} \right. \kern-0pt} {T_{e,i}^{{\mathrm{f}}} + D_{e,i}^{t} }}$$\end{document} to quantify the degree of time loss within the current control region, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_{e,i}^{t}$$\end{document} denotes the actual delay experienced by the EV at intersection i and its associated control segment, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{e,i}^{{\mathrm{f}}}$$\end{document} denotes the travel time under free-flow conditions. The EV stop-loss rate was defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{e,s}^{t} = {{S_{e,i}^{t} } \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {{S_{e,i}^{t} } {N_{e,i}^{{\mathrm{r}}} }}} \right. \kern-0pt} {N_{e,i}^{{\mathrm{r}}} }}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{e,i}^{t}$$\end{document} denotes the number of stops experienced by the EV within the remaining control region, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{e,i}^{{\mathrm{r}}}$$\end{document} denotes the number of controlled intersections that still need to be traversed from the current position to the target arterial exit. The effective priority service level was defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{p}^{t} = {\mathbb{I}}\left( {p_{i}^{t} = p_{e,i}^{t} } \right) \cdot {\Gamma}_{i}^{t} \cdot \left( {1 - I_{{\Omega }}^{t} } \right)$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbb{I}}\left( {p_{i}^{t} = p_{e,i}^{t} } \right)$$\end{document} indicates whether the current signal phase matches the EV target phase, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Gamma}_{i}^{t}$$\end{document} denotes the overall target-lane yielding capacity, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{{\Omega }}^{t}$$\end{document} denotes the downstream blockage risk. Multi-agent policy learning mechanism

Because control of consecutive arterial intersections is inherently a coordinated decision-making problem, a multi-agent learning mechanism with centralized training and decentralized execution was adopted. During training, a centralized critic used global state information to evaluate the value of the joint action taken by all agents. During execution, each intersection agent made its control decision independently based only on its local observation and neighborhood graph embedding.

Case study

Study area

A typical group of arterial intersections in the Zhengzhou Longhu Autonomous Driving Test Zone was selected as the study area, as shown in Fig. 3. The road network in this area is well structured, and the main arterial together with its intersecting branch roads forms a continuous signalized corridor, making it highly representative for practical applications. The study corridor is located along Longhu Inner Ring North Road, which runs primarily in the east–west direction. The road consists of six lanes in two directions, with auxiliary lanes provided on some segments. The speed limit is 60 km/h on the mainline and 40 km/h on the auxiliary lanes. The intersecting north–south roads are mainly urban secondary arterials, most of which have four lanes in two directions and a speed limit of 40 km/h. Because the intersections are moderately spaced and the corridor exhibits strong flow continuity, the area is well suited to capturing the traffic impediments, signal mismatch, and downstream queue propagation that emergency vehicles encounter when traversing consecutive arterial intersections. It therefore provides an appropriate case study for EV signal priority control in an intelligent connected environment.Fig. 3Study area and SUMO simulation road network. From the perspective of problem–scenario matching, this area simultaneously exhibits three important characteristics: arterial coordination, mixed-traffic operation, and vehicle–infrastructure cooperation. First, multiple consecutive signalized intersections are distributed along the arterial, and an EV performing an emergency task typically needs to pass through several control nodes in sequence. Its operating state is jointly affected not only by the current signal phase, but also by upstream–downstream coordination, target-lane queue length, and downstream spillback, which makes the corridor highly representative of a multi-intersection coordinated control problem. Second, the Longhu test zone has a well-developed intelligent connected sensing infrastructure. Its roadway environment and traffic organization support mixed operation of CAVs and HDVs, thereby providing a suitable platform for analyzing differences in yielding responses across vehicle types when an EV approaches. Third, the road layout is standardized and the control boundaries of intersections are clearly defined, which facilitates state construction, action design, and coordinated multi-intersection control based on multi-agent deep reinforcement learning.

To ensure consistency between the simulation network and the real-world road geometry, the base network information of the study area was extracted from OpenStreetMap and then imported, converted, and reconstructed in the SUMO platform. During network reconstruction, the original topology, inter-intersection connectivity, number of lanes, road classes, and speed limits were preserved. In addition, the approach-lane configuration, signal phase organization, and vehicle movement rules at key intersections were further calibrated according to the needs of arterial EV priority control. As a result, a simulated network with realistic traffic attributes was established for the consecutive arterial corridor. In terms of control-object definition, the multiple signalized intersections along the arterial and their connecting links were treated as a unified control scope. EVs traveling along the arterial to perform emergency tasks were defined as the priority service objects, while the operating characteristics of general traffic were also explicitly considered. Ordinary traffic consisted of both CAVs and HDVs. CAVs were assumed to adjust speed and lane-changing behavior according to approaching EV information and signal control information received through vehicle–infrastructure coordination, whereas HDVs yielded and changed lanes based on driver perception and the surrounding traffic environment. Therefore, the study area not only reproduced queueing, delay, and phase-conflict characteristics under conventional peak-hour urban traffic, but also captured the yielding process of surrounding vehicles to EVs and the influence of signal control strategies on coordinated progression across multiple intersections under intelligent connected mixed traffic.

Algorithm settings and procedure

To solve the EV signal priority control problem at consecutive arterial intersections in an intelligent connected environment, a multi-agent proximal policy optimization algorithm based on spatiotemporal graph features was adopted. In this approach, arterial intersections were treated as independent agents, and policy learning was performed under a centralized-training and decentralized-execution framework. Information such as predicted EV arrival time, target-lane yielding capacity, downstream blockage risk, and mixed-traffic composition was incorporated into the state representation. Unlike single-agent value-based methods, the proposed approach enabled exploration through stochastic policy sampling and entropy regularization, while a centralized value network was used to capture coordination relationships among multiple intersections. This design allowed the method to better adapt to cross-intersection traffic propagation, traffic-state nonstationarity, and multi-agent coupling in EV priority control.

In the implementation procedure, local traffic states and EV operating information were first obtained from the traffic simulation environment, and a time-varying arterial graph was constructed accordingly. A graph attention network and a temporal encoding module were then used to extract spatiotemporal embedding features for each intersection. Based on these features, each agent output a joint action consisting of phase selection and green-time allocation. The environment updated signal states and traffic conditions according to the joint action and then computed rewards based on EV delay, number of stops, average delay of general traffic, queue length, downstream blockage risk, and safety surrogate indicators. Interaction samples were stored in a fixed-length trajectory buffer, and the generalized advantage estimation method was used to calculate the advantage function. Finally, the policy and value networks were updated using the clipped objective function. The above process was repeated until convergence. A consistent experimental basis was adopted for the learning-based baselines. Particular attention was paid to the cumulative reward curves, loss evolution, and convergence characteristics. Because fixed-time control, actuated control, and rule-based EV priority methods do not involve parameter updates, their performance could not be assessed through training-related indicators and was therefore excluded from this part of the analysis.

Sensitivity analysis

To clarify the comparison scope, the sensitivity analysis focuses on three representative methods: Rule-EVP, MAPPO, and STG-MAPPO. Rule-EVP represents rule-based EV-priority control, MAPPO represents conventional multi-agent reinforcement learning, and STG-MAPPO represents the proposed spatiotemporal graph-enhanced method. This setting allows the sensitivity analysis to compare typical rule-based, learning-based, and graph-enhanced control mechanisms under varying CAV penetration, traffic demand, and HDV compliance conditions.

Figure 10 shows how the performance of the three methods changed under different CAV penetration rates. Overall, as the CAV penetration rate increased, average EV delay, average delay of ordinary vehicles, and queue spillback rate all decreased across the three methods. This indicates that a higher CAV share improved both EV priority effectiveness and overall system stability. The underlying reason is that, in a coordinated arterial environment, CAVs were able to receive approaching-EV information and control commands in a timely manner and then create passage space for the EV through more stable longitudinal deceleration and lateral yielding behavior, thereby reducing the likelihood of EV obstruction.Fig. 10Performance metrics of different methods under varying CAV penetration rates.

Performance metrics of different methods under varying CAV penetration rates.

A further comparison among the methods shows that Rule-EVP responded to changes in CAV penetration to some extent, but the improvement remained limited. This is because the method mainly relied on local rule-based triggering, phase switching, and green extension, and therefore could not fully exploit connected-vehicle information to build cross-intersection coordination. MAPPO improved more substantially as the CAV penetration rate increased, indicating that the multi-agent cooperative mechanism was able to capture, to some extent, the system-level control benefits brought by a higher CAV proportion. STG-MAPPO performed best at all CAV penetration levels, and the improvement was most pronounced during the transition from low to medium-to-high penetration rates. This suggests that the proposed method more effectively utilized the coordination potential enabled by increased vehicle–infrastructure connectivity. Meanwhile, when the CAV penetration rate became relatively high, the magnitude of further improvement gradually leveled off, indicating diminishing marginal returns as target-lane yielding capacity approached saturation.

Figure 11 shows the performance variation of the three methods under different traffic demand levels. Overall, as traffic demand increased, average EV delay, average delay of ordinary vehicles, and queue spillback rate all rose substantially, indicating that high-demand conditions intensified queue accumulation and traffic conflicts, thereby increasing the difficulty of EV priority control. In particular, when traffic demand approached or exceeded saturation, queue propagation and downstream spillback became much more pronounced, and the stability of EV progression through consecutive intersections declined significantly.Fig. 11Performance metrics of different methods under varying traffic demand levels.

Performance metrics of different methods under varying traffic demand levels.

Among the methods, Rule-EVP was the most sensitive to demand variation. When demand increased to a high level, EV delay and ordinary-traffic delay rose rapidly, and the queue spillback rate also increased markedly. This indicates that local rule-based priority was not able to effectively coordinate release timing across multiple intersections under heavy traffic conditions, making mismatch between local discharge and downstream blockage more likely. MAPPO showed better adaptability to demand variation than Rule-EVP. Although all performance metrics deteriorated as demand increased, the rate of deterioration was clearly lower, indicating that multi-agent cooperative learning improved the balance between EV priority and regular traffic operations to some extent. STG-MAPPO consistently maintained the lowest EV delay, ordinary-traffic delay, and queue spillback rate across all demand levels, and its advantage became even more pronounced under high-demand conditions. This result indicates that the proposed method was able to identify critical intersections and potential blockage locations more effectively through spatiotemporal graph encoding and forward-looking state representation, thereby maintaining better control performance under congested conditions.

Figure 12 presents the performance variation of the three methods under different HDV compliance levels. Overall, as HDV compliance increased, average EV delay, average delay of ordinary vehicles, and queue spillback rate all gradually decreased, indicating that a more active yielding response from HDVs helped relieve target-lane blockage, improve EV priority effectiveness, and enhance overall system stability. From a behavioral perspective, higher HDV compliance means that surrounding vehicles were more likely to decelerate, yield, and adjust lanes promptly when an EV approached, thereby providing greater operational flexibility for the signal control strategy.Fig. 12Performance metrics of different methods under varying HDV compliance levels.

Performance metrics of different methods under varying HDV compliance levels.

The comparison results show that Rule-EVP deteriorated most noticeably under low-compliance conditions. This is because, when HDVs did not respond to yielding prompts in a timely manner, relying solely on local phase priority was not sufficient to ensure effective clearance of the target lane, and the EV could still be obstructed on the approach or within the link. MAPPO performed better than Rule-EVP across different HDV compliance levels because the release strategy could be adjusted through multi-intersection state coordination. However, its performance was still affected by the stochasticity of driver yielding behavior. STG-MAPPO consistently achieved the best performance across all compliance levels, and its advantage was particularly evident under low-compliance conditions. This suggests that, by explicitly incorporating target-lane yielding capacity into the state representation and combining EV anticipatory prediction with downstream risk assessment, the proposed method maintained strong priority-control performance even when yielding behavior from surrounding traffic was insufficient, thus demonstrating stronger robustness.

Conclusions

This study addressed several key challenges in EV priority control at consecutive arterial intersections in an intelligent connected environment, including insufficient EV progression efficiency, weak coordination capability of conventional priority-control methods, and the difficulty of characterizing heterogeneous yielding behavior of surrounding traffic under mixed-traffic conditions. To this end, a coordinated control framework for EV signal priority at consecutive arterial intersections was developed. By integrating signal phase status, arterial queue length, predicted EV arrival time, target-lane yielding capacity, and downstream blockage risk into a unified modeling framework, the EV priority control problem was formulated as a multi-agent coordinated decision-making problem, thereby providing a systematic theoretical basis for subsequent control-method design.

On this basis, a spatiotemporal graph multi-agent proximal policy optimization method for EV signal priority control was proposed. In the proposed method, arterial intersections were modeled as cooperative agents, and joint optimization across multiple intersections was achieved under a centralized-training and decentralized-execution framework. In addition, inter-intersection topological relationships and traffic propagation effects were captured through spatiotemporal graph encoding. Meanwhile, heterogeneous yielding responses of CAVs and HDVs were explicitly modeled, and a next-intersection anticipatory prediction mechanism for EV progression was incorporated. As a result, the signal control strategy was able to shift from conventional reactive response to forward-looking coordinated priority control.

The training results show that the proposed method achieves a higher final cumulative reward and a narrower confidence interval than the non-graph reinforcement learning baselines. Over 10 random seeds, STG-MAPPO obtains a final 20-episode reward of – 5.0135 ± 0.0578, with a 95% confidence interval of [– 5.0494, – 4.9777], indicating improved learning stability under the tested simulation conditions.

The test results further demonstrate that the proposed method performs favorably in EV efficiency, ordinary-traffic efficiency, and safety. STG-MAPPO reduces EV travel time, EV delay, and EV stop frequency by 22.28%, 51.02%, and 60.00%, respectively, compared with DDQN. It also reduces ordinary-vehicle delay, average queue length, harsh braking events, TTC conflicts, and spillback rate compared with MAPPO.

This study was conducted under a SUMO-based single-EV simulation setting. The results therefore demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method under the specified experimental conditions, but they should not be interpreted as direct evidence of field-deployment robustness. Future work will further consider multi-EV priority conflicts, communication delay, sensing uncertainty, calibration with field traffic data, and real-world controller implementation constraints.
