Maximizing Extubation Outcomes Through Educational and Organizational Research (METEOR) Trial: protocol for a batched, stepped-wedge, cluster-randomised, type 2 hybrid effectiveness–implementation trial
Authors: Niall T Prendergast, Jeremy M Kahn, Derek C Angus, Linda Argote, Barbara Barnes, Chung-Chou H Chang, Susan Graff, Dean R Hess, Chukwudi A Onyemekwu, Kimberly J Rak, Jennifer L Russell, Jennifer B Seaman, Kelly M Toth, Timothy D Girard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMany patients who are extubated after receiving mechanical ventilation for acute respiratory failure experience extubation failure (ie, require reintubation hours to days after extubation). High-quality evidence shows that extubating patients directly to non-invasive ventilation (NIV) or high-flow nasal cannula oxygen (HFNC), rather than conventional low-flow oxygen, can prevent extubation failure. These guideline-recommended interventions, however, require care coordination involving multiple intensive care unit (ICU) team members and are infrequently used. Interprofessional education (IPE), which teaches members of multiple professions together, could effectively address this implementation gap in complex, team-based, critical care settings, particularly when paired with a customisable protocol.Methods and analysisThis batched, stepped-wedge, cluster-randomised, type 2 hybrid effectiveness–implementation trial will test three hypotheses: (1) when compared with traditional online education (OE), IPE increases implementation of preventive postextubation respiratory support, (2) the benefits of IPE are increased when paired with a clinical protocol and (3) preventive postextubation NIV for high-risk patients and preventive postextubation HFNC for low-risk patients reduce in-hospital mortality when compared with conventional postextubation oxygen therapy. The trial will recruit 24 clusters made up of one or more ICUs that care for at least 100 mechanically ventilated patients per year in a large multihospital health system in the USA. All clusters will receive OE, IPE and a clinical protocol, with timing determined by randomisation. We will also randomise half of the clusters to education promoting postextubation NIV for patients at high risk of extubation failure and preventive, postextubation HFNC for patients at lower risk, whereas the other half will be randomised to education promoting postextubation HFNC for all eligible patients. We will include all patients who are invasively mechanically ventilated for at least 24 hours. The primary implementation endpoint is the rate of use of postextubation NIV or HFNC among eligible participants. The primary clinical endpoint is in-hospital mortality truncated at 60 days from intubation.Ethics and disseminationThis study was approved by the institutional review board of the University of Pittsburgh and an independent data safety monitoring board. We describe the methods herein using the Standard Protocol Items for Randomised Trials framework and discuss key design decisions. We will disseminate results to participating healthcare providers, through publication in a peer-reviewed medical journal and via presentations at international conferences.Trial registration numberNCT05523479.

Introduction

Acute respiratory failure leading to invasive mechanical ventilation affects millions of patients worldwide each year1 and is fatal for up to 35% of these patients.2 Of those who recover to the point of extubation, 10%–20% experience extubation failure, that is, postextubation respiratory failure that requires reintubation, a major risk factor for in-hospital death.27 Extubation failure is more common in older patients and those with comorbid disease, particularly pulmonary disease.3 4 7 Multiple randomised trials have shown that postextubation non-invasive positive-pressure ventilation (NIV) and heated humidified high-flow nasal cannula oxygen (HFNC) can prevent extubation failure and thereby improve outcomes compared with extubation to conventional low-flow oxygen.811 American and European clinical practice guidelines thus recommend preventive postextubation NIV and HFNC.1216 Despite these recommendations, however, postextubation respiratory support is infrequently used.2 17 18

This implementation gap is particularly challenging due to the nature of critical care, which is delivered by an interprofessional care team in a complex environment.19 Most knowledge transfer strategies focus on individual providers and neglect the role of the team.2023 Such strategies are often ineffective.24 Interprofessional education (IPE), alternatively, teaches members of multiple professions together and can promote shared mental models, accurate understanding of each profession’s specific thinking and development of transactive memory systems, or who knows and does what.2527 In critical care settings, therefore, interprofessional education may be more effective than traditional medical education.28

The objectives of this batched, stepped-wedge, cluster-randomised, type 2 hybrid effectiveness–implementation trial are to test the effectiveness of IPE, with or without a clinical protocol, on the implementation of two preventive, postextubation respiratory support strategies (NIV and HFNC). Furthermore, we will compare the clinical effectiveness of these strategies with that of conventional postextubation low-flow oxygen.

Methods

Trial design

The Maximizing Extubation Outcomes Through Educational and Organizational Research (METEOR) Trial is a batched stepped-wedge cluster-randomised type 2 hybrid effectiveness–implementation trial, which employs three variations on the traditional stepped-wedge design (figure 1). First, a factorial design facilitates estimation of the effects of two different but potentially complementary implementation strategies (IPE and a clinical protocol) separately as well as together.29 Second, a concurrent design facilitates comparison of two postextubation strategies (HFNC for all patients vs NIV for high-risk patients and HFNC for low-risk patients) with conventional postextubation oxygen therapy and with each other.29 Third, a batched design facilitates recruitment of clusters throughout the duration of the trial rather than requiring that all clusters commence participation in the trial at the same time.30

Stepped-wedge deployment schedule. Batches will consist of two to six clusters that enter the trial on the same date. Most clusters will include one ICU, but some clusters will combine multiple ICUs to minimise contamination in hospitals where ICU personnel work in more than one ICU. Clusters will be randomised to the sequence of usual care (UC), then online education (OE), then interprofessional education (IPE), then IPE plus a protocol (IPE+P) or to the sequence of UC, then OE, then P and finally IPE+P. In this report, we adhere to the Standard Protocol Items for Randomised Trials (SPIRIT) statement,31 and we present the SPIRIT checklist and full study protocol in the online supplemental files. The trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05523479).

Patient and public involvement

We did not involve patients when planning trial design, conduct or dissemination. We did, however, pilot test the educational interventions with intensive care unit (ICU) clinicians (the target of the education) and interviewed ICU clinicians regarding the design of the education.

Setting

The trial will include ICUs in the following hospitals in the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center (UPMC) Health System, a multihospital healthcare system in Pennsylvania, Maryland and New York, USA: UPMC Carlisle, UPMC Community Osteopathic, UPMC East, UPMC Hanover, UPMC Harrisburg, UPMC Jameson, UPMC Magee-Womens, UPMC Memorial, UPMC Northwest, UPMC Mercy, UPMC Passavant, UPMC Presbyterian, UPMC Shadyside, UPMC St. Margaret, UPMC West Shore and UPMC Williamsport.

Participants and eligibility criteria

We will enrol UPMC ICUs that care for at least 100 mechanically ventilated patients per year. In most cases, we will enrol and randomise a participating ICU as a standalone cluster (ie, one ICU as one cluster), but in some cases we will combine multiple ICUs into a single cluster to minimise contamination in hospitals where ICU personnel routinely work in more than one ICU.32 Implementation strategies will target all physicians, advanced practice providers, nurses and respiratory therapists who work in participating ICUs. We will analyse all patients who were invasively mechanically ventilated for at least 24 hours.

Randomisation

Batches will consist of two to six clusters (most often six) that enter the trial on the same date (figure 1). We will enrol five batches for a total of 24 clusters. After ICU leadership agrees to participate, we will randomise each cluster—which may be a single ICU or a group of ICUs, depending on local staffing models—in two ways using a computerised system administered by the study statistician.30 First, we will randomise participating clusters 1:1 to receive either the sequence of usual care (UC), then online education (OE), then IPE and finally IPE plus a protocol (IPE+P) or the sequence of UC, then OE, then P and finally IPE+P. As shown in figure 1, the number of months under UC and OE is also determined by the randomly assigned sequence.

Second, we will also randomise participating clusters into 1:1 ratio to receive one of two educational contents: think high flow will promote the use of preventive, postextubation heated humidified HFNC for all eligible patients, whereas think NIV will promote a risk-stratified approach involving preventive, postextubation non-invasive positive-pressure ventilation (NIV) for patients at high risk of extubation failure and preventive, postextubation HFNC for patients at lower risk.

Randomisation will be factorial and will be restricted within each batch such that each batch will contain at least one of each of the four possible randomisation outcomes (protocol first and stressing NIV, protocol first and stressing HFNC, IPE first stressing NIV and NIV first stressing HFNC). As in most stepped-wedge trials, the ICU staff who participate in education cannot be blinded to the order in which the education is deployed, nor can they be blinded to the educational content.

Interventions

Implementation Interventions

We will compare three implementation strategies—traditional OE (an active control), IPE and an evidence-based clinical protocol—with the latter two being examined both alone and in combination. As shown in figure 1, each cluster will undergo four intervention periods, with the first being UC (ie, no formal education, serving as a passive control), the second being OE, the third being either IPE or the clinical protocol and the fourth being IPE plus the protocol. The cluster will then continue in the IPE plus protocol state until the end of the trial.

OE, which we designed to approximate the current state of traditional continuing education for healthcare professionals in the USA and consider an active control, will consist of a 30 min, online, interactive education module that includes a video describing the implementation content and pre-video and post-video quizzes. We customised OE modules to provider type (eg, respiratory therapist, nurse or physician/advanced practice provider), and we will offer learners provider-specific continuing education credits. We constructed the modules according to adult learning theory principles and will make them available on UPMC ULearn, the OE resource used routinely by UPMC. To enhance generalisability, OE will be encouraged—by ICU leadership and using up to three email reminders—but will not be mandated in participating clusters.

We designed IPE interventions based on the Argote-Miron-Spektor theory of organisational learning, which holds that organisational learning is a cycle by which shared experience leads to shared knowledge, which in turn feeds back on the context in which the task is performed, thereby strengthening the underlying rationale and providing flexible tools for knowledge retention.33 IPE will involve two components: workshops and just-in-time education. Each workshop will involve nurses, respiratory therapists, physicians and advanced practice providers and will be facilitated by a trained critical care physician (or advanced practice provider) who works clinically in the cluster receiving the education. During workshops, the facilitator will explain the rationale and evidence supporting preventive postextubation NIV and HFNC, as well as profession-specific implementation content, in a 30 min didactic session. They will then guide a 30 min group discussion during which learners will apply what they learnt to case examples. Following modern principles of adult learning and IPE, we designed the workshops to foster authenticity, reinforce role identity and relate the content to life experience.34 35 We plan two to three sessions per cluster. In keeping with our pragmatic study design, participation will be encouraged but not mandated. During just-in-time IPE, trained local champions will meet daily with the interprofessional ICU team to identify patients who are eligible for the assigned postextubation respiratory support strategy and, as needed, briefly review the evidence supporting proper use of the strategy.

During the protocol period, we will provide an ‘adequately explicit’ clinical protocol to the local champion. The protocol will consist of a customisable template outlining the workflow for the assigned postextubation respiratory support strategy (ie, either think NIV or think high flow). The champion will work with key local stakeholders to revise the protocol, accounting for local needs and resources, and will then disseminate the revised protocol based on local practices. The think NIV and think high flow protocol templates are available in the online supplemental appendix.

Implementation content

The think NIV and think high flow educational interventions will present the evidence supporting the use of preventive postextubation respiratory support over conventional postextubation oxygen, acknowledge equipoise regarding the relative effectiveness of preventive postextubation NIV versus HFNC, indicate that the learner’s ICU will be provided with implementation support (including additional IPE and an evidence-based protocol) for a specific preventive postextubation respiratory therapy to facilitate comparison of the two and describe the role of the ICU team in determining the specific postextubation therapy that is most appropriate for an individual patient.

The think NIV education and protocol will promote a risk-stratified approach involving preventive, postextubation NIV for patients at high risk of extubation failure and preventive, postextubation HFNC for patients at lower risk. Specifically, the ICU team will be instructed to (1) determine extubation readiness per local evidence-based practices (eg, using a respiratory therapy-driven spontaneous breathing trial protocol (SBT)), (2) risk stratify patients who are ready for extubation and (3) apply the appropriate postextubation respiratory support based on risk and contraindications. The durations and settings recommended by the education are shown in table 1. The education and protocol will not apply to patients who are extubated after <24 hours of mechanical ventilation. Among those mechanically ventilated for >24 hours, patients will be classified as high risk if their age >65 years and/or they have chronic cardiac and/or respiratory disease. The ICU team will be instructed to extubate high-risk patients who have passed an SBT to preventive NIV, so long as the patient meets other criteria for extubation (per the ICU team’s judgement) and does not have contraindications to preventive NIV (eg, facial fractures). Alternatively, the ICU team will be instructed to extubate patients who are not high risk or who have contraindications to NIV to preventive HFNC. Additional details are provided in the protocol templates available in the online supplemental appendix.

Concomitant care

Throughout the trial, all patients cared for in participating ICUs will receive medical care as per the treating ICU team. Study personnel will interact with the ICU teams to provide education, but will not be involved in clinical decisions.

Intervention fidelity

We grounded an intervention fidelity plan in best practice recommendations from the US National Institutes of Health Behavior Change Consortium.36 Our goal is to establish and maintain fidelity with a standardised training programme delivered across all clusters.

As part of this plan, interprofessional workshop facilitators and local champions will undergo standardised training built on principles of adult learning theory and established best practices for ‘training the trainer’ and refined based on the results of our pilot study. The training includes brief didactic sessions, which provide an overview of the IPE and basic communication skills followed by focused training on the conduct of each component of IPE. The bulk of the training focuses on skills practice, using simulated learners and personalised feedback.

We will further ensure successful intervention delivery by conducting quarterly booster sessions with interprofessional workshop facilitators and local champions during which key skills are reviewed and by evaluating IPE deployment throughout the trial. Using the key skills checklist, we will directly observe 25% of classroom IPE workshops and will evaluate just-in-time IPE via brief, semistructured interviews with local champions once a week (for a total of 4 weeks) during each cluster’s first month of IPE deployment and then once a quarter after their first month. If problems with IPE fidelity are detected, we will provide tailored education and skills practice to the trainers.

Hypotheses, evaluation framework and primary endpoints

The METEOR Trial will test multiple inter-related hypotheses. The primary implementation hypothesis is that IPE is superior to traditional OE and to UC (no structured education). A secondary implementation hypothesis is that the benefits of IPE are increased when IPE is paired with a clinical protocol. The primary clinical hypothesis is that preventive postextubation NIV for high-risk patients and preventive postextubation HFNC for low-risk patients are both superior to current clinical practice (ie, conventional postextubation oxygen therapy).

We will test these hypotheses using the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance (RE-AIM) evaluation framework (table 2).37 As a type 2 hybrid effectiveness–implementation trial, the trial has two coprimary endpoints. The primary implementation endpoint is the rate of use of postextubation NIV or HFNC among eligible patients throughout the trial. The primary effectiveness outcome is in-hospital mortality truncated at 60 days from intubation.

Secondary endpoints

Implementation

Secondary implementation endpoints include the number of providers who completed an implementation intervention (to assess the ‘reach’ domain of RE-AIM), the number of eligible patients who received care from providers who completed an implementation intervention (also to assess reach), and the rate of use of postextubation NIV or HFNC among eligible patients 6 months after the implementation intervention (IPE plus protocol) is fully deployed (to assess the ‘maintenance’ domain of RE-AIM).

Effectiveness

Secondary effectiveness outcomes include 90-day survival, hospital length of stay, ICU length of stay, postextubation respiratory failure defined as reintubation within 48 hours of a planned extubation, time to successful liberation from mechanical ventilation (defined as breathing without assistance for at least 48 hours), 28-day ventilator-free days (defined as the number of days alive and breathing without assistance during the 28 days after index intubation with death equated to 0 ventilator-free days), rate of ventilator-associated events (according to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention definition) and organ failure (as measured by daily Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) Score).

Data collection and analyses

Data collection

To maximise trial efficiency, we will collect nearly all quantitative data directly from the UPMC electronic health record. These data will include demographics, laboratory values, vital signs, ventilator and oxygen delivery settings, medication use and other key variables. We will measure implementation of preventive postextubation HFNC and NIV using non-invasive ventilator settings and/or FiO2 and oxygen flow rates at the first charted postextubation time point (to assess for preventive rather than rescue use) and for the 24 hours postextubation (to assess continued implementation). We will augment electronic health record data with death dates obtained from the National Death Index. We will quantify the number of patients with complete data collection, the proportion of data loss per collected variable, the proportion of patients lost to follow-up and the reason for such loss.

Follow-up

We will follow patients through hospital discharge and will monitor for mortality via the National Death Index thereafter. All participating ICUs will be followed from the time of batch entry into the study until the end of the trial.

Sample size and power calculations

To inform sample size and power calculations, we queried the UPMC electronic health record for data from ICUs expected to participate. The rate of postextubation NIV or HFNC among eligible patients was 2.3% across all ICUs with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.418. The in-hospital mortality rate was 21.7% across all ICUs with an ICC of 0.064. The number of eligible patients averaged approximately 30 per month per ICU. In power calculations, we conservatively assumed an average of 23.3 patients with complete assessments per cluster per 1 month time period. Based on our batched cluster-randomised, stepped-wedge design and a 5% type 1 error rate, we estimate the trial will have 90% power to detect a 1.4% absolute increase in the primary implementation endpoint and a 4.6% absolute decrease in in-hospital mortality for key comparisons (ie, IPE plus protocol vs OE).

Interim analysis

To monitor for evidence that one of the two implementation interventions resulted in greater effectiveness than the other, we prespecified one interim analysis after 18 months of recruitment, approximately halfway through the trial. This interim analysis tests the hypothesis that the difference in in-hospital mortality for those randomised to preventive postextubation NIV for high-risk patients and preventive postextubation HFNC for low-risk patients versus those randomised to preventive postextubation HFNC is >5%. Using the O’Brien-Fleming method to control for an overall type ie,rror rate of 5% and accounting for one interim analysis after approximately 50% recruitment, the METEOR Trial should be modified if p<0.0055746 at the interim analysis. That is, if in-hospital mortality is significantly lower in clusters receiving education stressing one implementation content, then clusters randomised to the other implementation content will immediately receive OE stressing the superior content, and at the time determined by randomisation, these clusters will deploy either IPE or a clinical protocol promoting the superior implementation content.

Intention to treat

We will complete analyses using a modified intention-to-treat approach. We will consider OE (the active control) the primary comparator. We will consider UC (the passive control) a secondary comparator.

Final analyses

To account for the stepped-wedge design, we will use several approaches, which are described in detail in a statistical analysis plan that will be registered at Open Science Framework before data collection is complete. First, we will incorporate time specifications to model secular trends and allow these trends to be different between the control and intervention conditions. Second, we will include cluster-specific random effects in models to account for within-cluster correlations as well as random cluster-by-time interactions to assess for heterogeneity of secular trend across clusters. Third, we will assess whether and how the intervention effects change over time. Fourth, we will include random interactions between intervention status and cluster to assess for heterogeneity of treatment effects across clusters.

The exact specifications for our implementation endpoints and clinical endpoints are described in table 2. For each endpoint, the primary estimate of the treatment effect will be adjusted and presented with 95% CIs. Time and cluster will be included as random effects. Patient-level covariates will include age, Elixhauser, chronic heart disease, chronic lung disease, body mass index (BMI) and SOFA at intubation. Cluster-level covariates will include teaching versus non-teaching hospitals. For endpoints that are binary, we will use a generalised linear mixed effects model (GLMM) with random clustering effects to estimate adjusted risk ratios with the above-mentioned terms to test secular trends and account for heterogeneous features initially included and tested. The final parsimonious model will only retain terms that are significant at the 0.05 level. We will estimate the primary clinical endpoint using a frailty model. We will analyse continuous endpoints using linear mixed models. We will appropriately transform endpoints for which the distribution is not normal. Components in these models will be similar to the GLMM described above, but instead, we will estimate adjusted HRs using frailty models and adjusted beta coefficients using linear mixed models. In these primary analyses, we will include all episodes of mechanical ventilation for patient participants who have more than one episode during the trial period and account for within-patient clustering. We will also conduct sensitivity analyses that include only the first episode of mechanical ventilation for such patients.

Based on the final models, we will compare the intervention to the control conditions and test the equality of effects between the two schedules (UC → OE → IPE → IPE+P vs UC → OE → P → IPE+P). This approach allows us to assess for important interactions (eg, to test whether the implementation strategies led to improved outcomes and test whether implementation success varied by clinical strategy). Additionally, we will determine whether hospital type (teaching vs non-teaching) modifies the effects of IPE by including an interaction term for education×hospital type in models examining effects on implementation outcomes. We will also determine whether obesity modifies the effects of the postextubation respiratory strategies by including an interaction term for strategy×BMI in models examining effects on clinical outcomes. If these tests for interactions are significant, we will report the effects of IPE and/or postextubation respiratory strategies stratified by the identified effect modifier.

In the case of non-convergence of the GLMM, we will use a smaller number of points or a Laplace approximation. To correct the potential inflation of the type 1 error rate due to a small number of clusters, we will use the Kenward and Roger small sample correction. We will also estimate and account for loss to follow-up. If necessary, we will increase estimation efficiency in the setting of missing data by performing multiple imputation before model fitting, using Rubin’s rule to combine the results. We will report the rate of missingness for variables requiring imputation and conduct complete case sensitivity analyses. We will also perform a path analysis linking our implementation outcomes to our clinical outcomes with a goal of determining the degree to which process change mediates observed clinical effects.38

Monitoring

Safety monitoring

In this trial involving critically ill patients who are at high risk for death or other adverse outcomes due to their underlying critical illness, we will systematically track clinical outcomes, including organ failure and death, and include these outcomes as part of the safety and effectiveness analyses. For the purposes of reporting, death and organ dysfunction will not be recorded as adverse events unless the investigative team believes the event may have been related to study participation or is more severe or prolonged than expected given the underlying critical illness. We anticipate that this determination will be very rare, given that the direct targets of the trial interventions are ICU providers rather than patients, and the postextubation respiratory support strategies under study are both recommended as standard of care and have both been shown to be superior to conventional low-flow oxygen after extubation. Nevertheless, we will specifically track two adverse outcomes of special interest: unplanned death (ie, without a ‘comfort measures only’ order) and reintubation within 24 hours of a planned extubation using real-time, automated, electronic health record surveillance plus clinician investigator adjudication.

Data safety monitoring board (DSMB)

The METEOR Trial DSMB includes five independent voting members who are not study investigators and have no financial, scientific or other conflict of interest with the trial. Collectively, the DSMB has expertise in the following fields: critical care medicine, implementation science, clinical trial methodology, research ethics and biostatistics.

Ethics and dissemination

Research ethics approval

During meetings in June 2022, the DSMB reviewed the study protocol and recommended modifications (described below). The DSMB approved a revised protocol on 9 August 2022, we registered the trial on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05523479) on 29 August 2022 and the protocol was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board on 18 October 2022 (STUDY #20100469).

Justification of UC

Clinical trial evidence supports the use of preventive, postextubation respiratory support therapies (NIV or HFNC), but these therapies are currently used infrequently in routine practice, including in the ICUs that will participate in the METEOR Trial. The infrequent use of preventive, postextubation respiratory support therapies in these ICUs is likely attributable to known barriers to implementation.39 Although the efficacy of preventive, postextubation respiratory support therapies is not in question, equipoise exists regarding how best to address implementation barriers. The inclusion of UC periods in the trial, during which clusters will receive no structured education about preventive, postextubation respiratory support therapies, is justified because the implementation interventions being studied during the trial remain unproven. Additionally, the simultaneous rollout of OE (active control) in all participating ICUs is not feasible due to limitations in health system staff and research staff, both of which are required to effectively deploy OE.

Protocol amendments

Prior to the publication of this protocol, five amendments were made to the protocol. Each was approved by the DSMB, sponsor and institutional review board. A summary of each amendment is described below:

Amendment 1, effective 11 August 2022, justified the inclusion of UC, introduced the use of batches in the stepped wedge design and described an ancillary mixed-methods study that will be conducted concurrently with the trial to examine contextual factors that influence the effectiveness of IPE and preventive, postextubation respiratory therapies.

Amendment 2, effective 10 October 2022, clarified the inclusion criteria by limiting inclusion to adults treated with invasive mechanical ventilation>24 hours in participating ICUs, replaced ventilator-associated pneumonia with ventilator-associated events as a secondary endpoint and clarified the adverse event reporting plan.

Amendment 3, effective 22 December 2022, updated batch size (from 4 to 6) and revised power calculations based on this change, distinguished adverse outcomes (tracked and analysed systematically) from adverse events (tracked and reviewed individually) and described the approach to monitoring for unexpected risks and expanded the list of clinical outcomes to include adverse outcomes of special interest.

Amendment 4, effective 25 September 2023, revised the fidelity monitoring plan to improve the feasibility of just-in-time IPE fidelity monitoring.

Amendment 5, effective 7 March 2025, clarified that each batch can include between 2 and 6 clusters, clarified that each cluster may include one or multiple ICUs, revised the sample size to 24 (instead of 28) clusters, revised the power calculations based on the revised sample size of 24 clusters and revised the list of adverse outcomes of special interest, removing ‘agitation leading to new restraint or psychoactive medication use within 24 hours of planned extubation’.

Informed consent

Because the research interventions are education-directed solely at ICU clinicians, the only research activity directed specifically at individual patients is the collection of protected health information. Thus, the study was approved with a waiver of informed consent for patients since the research involves no more than minimal risk to the subjects, the research could not practicably be carried out without waiver of informed consent, and the waiver will not adversely affect the rights and welfare of the subjects.

Confidentiality and access to data

We will store information about potential and enrolled patients on a secure server and analyse data in a secure virtual desktop. The investigative team will have access to the data, which will be shared along with statistical code with other investigators upon request and after execution of appropriate data-sharing agreements.

Dissemination

We will share results on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05523479), in presentations and publications, and with participating healthcare providers.

Discussion

The design of the METEOR Trial posed several challenges but also provided the investigative team with several opportunities for innovation. First, conducting the trial in a single health system may limit generalisability if practitioners share certain traits, such as educational background, that affect response to IPE. The effects of IPE might be different in another health system. Most healthcare systems in the USA, however, have broadly similar online educational standards, and evidence from multiple systems has shown that the impact of OE is limited.40 Additionally, because UPMC is large and diverse, with academic and community hospitals of varying sizes and in varying geographically separated settings, the findings of our trial are likely to be widely generalisable, and we have the opportunity to test whether certain hospital or ICU characteristics modify the effect of IPE.

Second, there is no consensus about the best way to surveil safety during a hybrid effectiveness–implementation trial. As this trial involves mechanically ventilated ICU patients, numerous adverse outcomes such as death, reintubation and organ dysfunction are expected to occur due to their underlying critical illness. Reintubation rates in previous trials of postextubation respiratory support have varied widely based on different study populations but generally fall around 10%.41 Moreover, the interventions we are studying are educational, and the content of the education, preventive postextubation NIV and HFNC, was superior to conventional low-flow oxygen in multiple previous randomised trials. As such, it is difficult to imagine harm coming to a patient as a result of the METEOR Trial—and indeed, the trial was approved as a minimal-risk study with waiver of informed consent. Nonetheless, the relative benefits of one promoted postextubation respiratory support strategy may be greater than those of another. Therefore, we pre-specified an interim analysis and designed automated safety monitoring to identify deaths and reintubations within the 24 hours postextubation. We will manually review these and will communicate to the DSMB if we discover events thought to be unexpected, serious and related to the study procedures. We also ask clinicians in participating ICUs to report safety events that they view as related to the education.

Third, the timing of the trial relative to the recent COVID-19 pandemic poses potential difficulties. As with most health systems in the USA, UPMC experienced a staffing shortage during and after the pandemic, with many nurses and respiratory therapists choosing to leave the workforce. These staffing shortages may make some ICUs hesitant or even unwilling to participate due to the additional burden created by the interventions. Moreover, the traditional stepped-wedge design, which requires that all participating clusters commit prior to trial initiation (sometimes years before an intervention is deployed in a given cluster), creates numerous problems in the setting of staff shortages. For this reason, we are using a batched stepped-wedge design, which allows us to recruit 4–6 clusters at a time, just before batch entry and randomisation, rather than having to recruit all 24 clusters prior to the beginning of the trial. This allows some potential clusters to postpone participation when necessary and gives the investigative team opportunities to recruit clusters after starting the trial, increasing flexibility.

Fourth, the interventions are not without cost, in terms of money for the health care system, time for practitioners and comfort for patients. We did not include a formal cost-effectiveness analysis when designing the trial, though such an analysis could be conducted. We expect that any time cost to clinicians will be reflected in the clinical endpoints if, for example, respiratory therapists are unable to respond to another patient’s deterioration because they are busy administering postextubation respiratory support. We expect a comfort cost to patients to be captured by the implementation endpoint; if patients do not tolerate NIV, then implementation rates will be lower in the think NIV group. Additionally, we are conducting an ancillary mixed-methods study to characterise barriers and facilitators to implementation and expect this will shed further light on the perceived costs of the interventions.

In summary, the METEOR Trial will use an innovative design to examine the effects of IPE in the complex team-based care environment of the ICU and to compare the effects of preventive, postextubation NIV and HFNC in patients recovering from acute respiratory failure requiring more than 24 hours of mechanical ventilation.
