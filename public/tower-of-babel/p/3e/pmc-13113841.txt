Postpartum women’s motivation for substance use recovery: an exploratory qualitative study of recovery capital
Authors: Jodie Bigalky, April Mackey, Etieno Essien, Ava Bahrey, Pammla Petrucka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCanada is facing a national opioid crisis, with an average of 20 opioid-related deaths reported daily. Postpartum women who use non-prescription opioids and unregulated substances are at high risk of relapse and overdose. They also face significant health and social challenges that create barriers to successful, long-term recovery. While many women reduce or stop substance use during pregnancy, sustained recovery in the postpartum period remains a challenge. Despite this, little research has explored the personal, social, and structural factors, collectively conceptualized as recovery capital, that sustain motivation for recovery after birth. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to explore the factors that influence motivation for recovery among postpartum women with a history of substance use, with particular attention to the personal, social, and structural dimensions of recovery capital.MethodsAn exploratory qualitative design was used to capture the experiences of 16 postpartum women in Saskatchewan, Canada, who had given birth within the previous two years and were in recovery from non-prescription opioid or unregulated substance use for at least three months. Participants were recruited through public health and treatment agencies. Semi-structured interviews were conducted in-person or by phone and analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis.ResultsThe sample (N = 16) was primarily Indigenous (n = 14), predominantly in their twenties, and single (n = 14), with most participants having a high school education or less, low household income, and limited employment. Thematic analysis identified four themes: (1) the impact of limited accessibility on recovery, (2) parenthood as a motivator, (3) the role of peer, family, and professional support systems, and (4) the transformative power of resilience.ConclusionsPostpartum women with substance use disorders are highly motivated to recover, often driven by their roles as mothers. However, motivation alone is not enough without adequate recovery capital, including access to integrated, trauma-informed, and culturally safe treatment, social supports, and structural resources. Findings highlight the importance of expanding substance use programs that address structural barriers, promote resilience, and include peer, family, and professional supports. Comprehensive, strength-based treatment models are critical to sustaining and supporting long-term recovery for postpartum women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13011-026-00718-5.Keywords: Postpartum, Substance use, Recovery, Motivation, Perinatal health, Women’s health, Qualitative research

Background

In recent years, Canada has experienced a concerning shift in the landscape of substance use (SU), marked by the increasing prevalence of non-prescription opioid and unregulated drug use. This shift has been compounded by the widespread availability of toxic, unregulated substances, contributing to an opioid crisis that has become a national public health emergency. This crisis has reached alarming levels, with rising rates of overdose deaths presenting significant challenges [1]. In 2024 alone, Canada reported an average of 20 opioid-related deaths per day, totaling more than 7100 opioid toxicity fatalities [2]. Addressing this crisis requires not only broad public health strategies but also targeted efforts focused on specific populations who are disproportionately affected and often underserved, such as postpartum women.

Postpartum women who use substances, face distinct health and social challenges and are often overlooked in existing programs and recovery supports [3]. Overdose has emerged as a leading cause of maternal mortality among this group, with research showing that the majority of perinatal deaths related to SU occur during the postpartum period, within the first year after birth [4–6]. Social challenges such as stigma, poverty, housing instability, and the fear of child apprehension complicate access to care and sustaining recovery [7–10]. Some women also face underlying issues, including mental health challenges and histories of trauma [11]. These intersecting health and social challenges create significant barriers that negatively impact successful, long-term recovery [12].

An increasingly influential framework for understanding sustained recovery from SU is the concept of recovery capital. Originally developed by Granfield and Cloud, recovery capital refers to the multiple internal and external resources that individuals can draw upon to initiate and sustain recovery, including personal, social, and structural dimensions [13–15]. Within this framework, recovery is conceptualized as a multidimensional and dynamic process shaped by social determinants of health rather than solely by individual motivation, treatment engagement, or abstinence. Consistent with recovery-oriented paradigms, recovery is often defined by improvements in quality of life, wellbeing, and functioning, which may or may not include abstinence as a goal or outcome [16, 17]. Recovery-oriented systems of care include services such as housing programs, educational and employment supports, peer-based recovery services, continuity of care, and recovery community centers, all of which are essential for sustaining recovery by enhancing recovery capital [16].

More recently, recovery capital has been further elaborated within recovery-oriented research to emphasize how access to resources such as stable housing, supportive relationships, financial security, healthcare, and reduced stigma collectively facilitates and sustains recovery [18]. For postpartum women with a history of SU, recovery capital is often constrained by intersecting challenges such as poverty, housing instability, mental health concerns, trauma, and the threat of child apprehension, all of which can undermine recovery efforts despite strong motivation [7–10]. Viewing postpartum recovery through a recovery capital lens highlights how personal motivation alone is insufficient without adequate social and structural supports and underscores the importance of strengthening recovery capital to improve quality of life and support sustained recovery during the postpartum period.

Although the prevalence of perinatal unregulated SU is often cited at approximately 1%, this estimate is based on outdated national data and likely underrepresents the true scope of SU during the perinatal period due to stigma and fear of disclosure (Public Health Agency of Canada [19, 20]. The absence of comprehensive and current national data on non-prescription opioid and unregulated SU among perinatal populations [21] suggests that more women may be navigating pregnancy and the postpartum period with SU related challenges than is currently recognized. This uncertainty further highlights the importance of understanding how postpartum women sustain recovery, particularly when recovery capital is limited and formal systems of support may not be well aligned with needs.

Pregnancy is often a motivational time when many women reduce or stop using substances, viewing it as a critical opportunity to support the health of their baby and themselves [12, 22, 23]. This shift may be influenced by a growing sense of responsibility or a nurturing instinct that emerges during pregnancy [24]. However, the postpartum period presents unique challenges, and without sustained and accessible support, the risk of relapse is high [12, 25]. Research indicates that a large majority of women who achieve abstinence by the end of pregnancy resume SU postpartum, with approximately 80% relapsing to at least one substance by 24 months after birth [25]. At the same time, women may also initiate recovery after birth, motivated by parenthood, concerns for their child’s wellbeing, or access to postpartum supports [3, 6].

Despite substantial literature on SU during the perinatal period, there remains a significant gap in research specifically focused on the postpartum phase [3, 6]. Most studies concentrate on SU during pregnancy, often overlooking the unique challenges and needs that arise after birth [3]. This gap is particularly concerning given the increased risk of relapse and mortality associated with SU in the postpartum period [4–6]. The lack of targeted research limits the development of effective, evidence-based recovery support for this population [5].

To address this challenge, it is essential to establish comprehensive mechanisms that ensure postpartum women have access to care and support for recovery, specifically designed for their unique needs [5, 23]. Support that builds on motivation developed during pregnancy can help sustain recovery, using the transformative experience as a catalyst for long-term change [12]. However, not a lot is known about the factors that sustain recovery engagement and motivation in the postpartum period, particularly in the context of limited recovery capital. Building on this foundation, the purpose of this study was to explore the factors that influence motivation for recovery among postpartum women with a history of SU, with particular attention to the personal, social, and structural dimensions of recovery capital. By identifying what contributes to sustained motivation after birth, this research aims to inform the development of more effective recovery supports that build on the motivation often initiated in pregnancy.

Methods

This study used an exploratory qualitative research design to explore what helps postpartum women with a history of SU remain motivated in recovery. The study focused on personal, social, and structural factors that contribute to recovery capital. The authors would like to acknowledge that not all perinatal people identify as women. However, all participants in this study self-identified as women and therefore gendered language has been used.

Sampling and recruitment

Participants eligible for inclusion in this study were residents of Saskatchewan who were 18 years of age or older, had given birth to a live born infant within the previous two years, self-identified as having a substance use disorder (SUD) involving non-prescription opioids and/or unregulated substances, and had been in a period of self-identified recovery for at least three months. This timeframe was chosen to ensure that participants had experienced some time in recovery to reflect meaningfully on the factors supporting their motivation, while also capturing early postpartum recovery experiences. Any person who met the inclusion criteria was eligible to participate, regardless of gender identity.

Recruitment was conducted through healthcare professionals (HCPs) and staff at agencies where postpartum women with a history of SU sought care, such as public health agencies and treatment centres. Agency staff provided a general overview of the study to potential participants. Individuals who expressed interest were referred to the research team, who met with them privately to offer more detailed information about the study. During these meetings, eligibility screening was conducted to ensure the potential participants met the inclusion criteria. Individuals who did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. If a potential participant met the inclusion criteria and expressed interest in participating, the informed consent process was initiated. In total, 20 women were screened and 16 met the inclusion criteria and provided written informed consent to participate.

Setting

The study was conducted in three urban locations within the province of Saskatchewan, Canada. These cities are geographically dispersed across the province, one located in the southeast, one in the central region, and one near the western border. Each has a population ranging from approximately 17,000 to over 230,000, representing a mix of small to mid-sized urban centres. These communities are regional service points that offer a range of healthcare and other services, including supports for individuals with SUDs.

Data collection

A sociodemographic questionnaire and a semi-structured interview guide (see Supplementary File) were used with all 16 participants. The development of the interview guide was informed by a prior scoping review on approaches to postpartum SU care and support [3], as well as collaborative input and consensus among the research team. The guide structured the interviews, while active listening and follow-up questions deepened understanding of each participant’s experience.

Participants were given the option to complete the interview either in-person or by telephone. Three participants chose to be interviewed by telephone, while the remaining interviews were conducted in-person in private rooms within community agencies. These locations were selected because they offered safe, familiar, and comfortable environments for the participants. With participants’ consent, all interviews were audio-recorded and lasted approximately 45 to 90 minutes. The first three authors collectively conducted all interviews, with each interview carried out by a single author. The number of interviews conducted was guided by the concept of information power, which suggests that studies with a narrow aim, a specific sample, strong interview dialogue, and established theoretical perspective require fewer participants to achieve sufficient information [26]. Given the focused study purpose, specificity of the sample, depth of the semi-structured interviews, and the use of recovery capital as an informing theoretical perspective, sufficient information power was achieved with 16 interviews. Although formal coding and thematic analysis were conducted after all interviews were completed, the transcripts were reviewed iteratively during data collection, and the researchers discussed emerging topics throughout the study. This iterative review also allowed the team to ensure that the interview questions remained relevant and effectively captured participants’ experiences. While traditional data saturation was not used as the primary determinant of sample size, the iterative review ensured that the data were rich and comprehensive, supporting confidence in the adequacy of the sample. Following the interviews, the first three authors transcribed the recordings verbatim for analysis.

Data analysis

This study used thematic analysis, following Braun and Clarke’s [27] six-phase framework. The analysis was guided by a constructivist interpretive perspective [27], which recognized that participants’ experiences are socially situated and shaped by personal, social, and structural contexts. While recovery capital informed the study conceptually, it was used as a guiding analytic lens rather than a coding template, allowing themes to be inductively generated from participants’ experiences. The first, third, and fourth authors collaboratively conducted the data analysis, while the remaining two authors provided review and feedback to ensure rigor and consistency. The first, second, and fifth authors are researchers with backgrounds working with marginalized populations, including individuals with SUDs. Their collective work has focused on advancing equity-oriented approaches to care, with a particular interest in improving support available for perinatal populations affected by SU. The third and fourth authors were research assistants who developed an interest in the topic area through their academic training and participation in the study.

Thematic analysis began with phase one, data familiarization. Team members immersed themselves in the data by thoroughly reading and re-reading the interview transcripts, which were imported into NVivo 14 [28] software, and by taking analytic notes to develop an understanding of participant experiences. In phase two, initial codes were inductively generated by the research team through close engagement with the transcripts with NVivo 14 used solely to support the organization and management of the data and coding. All coding decisions were made by the research team. Initial codes were applied to meaningful segments of text and remained flexible, allowing for modification as analysis progressed. Phase three involved organizing these initial codes into preliminary themes by identifying patterns and relationships across the data. Throughout phases two and three, coding was iterative, with researchers returning repeatedly to the original transcripts to review, refine, collapse, or expand codes as needed. The first, third, and fourth authors met regularly to discuss coding decisions, resolve discrepancies, and collaboratively refine emerging themes. In phase four, the preliminary themes were reviewed and revised by first examining the coherence of coded data within each theme, and second, by assessing how well the themes reflected the overall dataset. Themes were adjusted, split, merged, or redefined, as needed. During phase five, the themes were further refined and clearly defined to ensure that each represented a distinct aspect of the participants’ experiences, with minimal conceptual overlap. Finally, in phase six, the themes were synthesized into a final narrative that captures the participants’ experiences, supported by illustrative data excerpts [27].

Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness was established using Lincoln and Guba’s [29], criteria of credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement with the data, repeated reading of transcripts, and an iterative analytic process supported by regular team discussions. Credibility and dependability through collaborative coding and reflexive dialogue among multiple authors was established. An audit trail, including analytic memos and documentation of coding and theme development decisions was maintained to support dependability and confirmability. Transferability was supported through the use of rich participant quotations and descriptions of the study context and participants.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the University of Saskatchewan Research Ethics Board.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the sample

The demographic characteristics of the sample (N = 16) are presented in Table 1. The majority of participants were in their twenties, with only one under the age of 20 and none over the age of 39. Most participants identified as Indigenous (n = 14) which was defined to include First Nations, Métis, and Inuit Peoples. The majority were single (n = 14). Most had the equivalent of a high school education or less and were not currently employed (n = 15). All reported an annual household income of less than $30,000. The sample was primarily from urban areas (n = 13). All participants self-identified as having a SUD to non-prescription opioids or unregulated substances, and six were currently receiving opioid agonist therapy. All participants were actively engaged in some form of treatment programming or support services aimed at sustaining their recovery. Self-identified time in recovery ranged from three to 19 months, and the age of participants’ youngest child at the time of the interview ranged from three to 23 months. Ten of the participants had other children.

Themes

The thematic analysis resulted in the identification of four themes: (1) the impact of limited accessibility on recovery, (2) parenthood as a motivator, (3) the role of peer, family, and professional support systems, and (4) the transformative power of resilience.

The impact of limited accessibility on recovery

Participants’ ability to engage in recovery was shaped by the accessibility of resources, including healthcare services, SU treatment programs, housing, income, transportation, and culturally appropriate supports. Even with strong personal motivation, barriers in these areas often made recovery difficult. Some participants reflected on the difficulty accessing healthcare early in their recovery, including difficulty finding a physician, psychologist, or prenatal care. Long waitlists for treatment programs further limited engagement, as one participant explained: “I called around, and it was actually hard getting into a rehab center. There was a waitlist … so I didn’t even bother going into rehab” (YP2).

Lack of stable housing and income created additional barriers that directly impacted the ability to stay engaged and motivated in recovery. Participants highlighted how meeting basic needs took precedence over recovery, with one noting, “Access to a safe, stable place to live. Because when I lacked that, there was no helping me at that point like I can’t do anything. You can’t even get a job in that position” (LP1). Without stable housing, meeting even basic needs became difficult. LP1 further stated, “You can’t have access to a shower, a bed. I mean, if you don’t have a house, chances are you probably don’t have food” (LP1). Limited transportation also played a key role in helping participants stay connected to resources and motivated during their recovery: “And then, like, if I have a vehicle, I can just go to [location removed] and see all the resources they have there” (LP7).

Accessibility of culturally appropriate resources was also identified as needed to contribute to motivation in the recovery process. Several women spoke about limited access to ceremonies, Elders, and traditional practices. RP6 noted, “I haven’t had any Elders, which I wish there was more opportunities … It’s very hard to find like an Elder, even then right, because there’s only so much of them out there.” She continued, “We do smudge, but you know, it’s just the bigger parts of our culture that we do not have access to” (RP6).

Parenthood as a motivator

Parenthood was found to be a major source of motivation. Many participants emphasized the importance of parenthood in their recovery. One participant expressed, “I realized he [her son] needed me, and I needed him” (LP1), while another shared, “It [parenthood] gives me the motivation, and gives me a reason to stay sober” (LP3).

All the women in the study currently had custody of their youngest child. In some cases, these children had previously been in alternative care arrangements, and several participants had older children who were not in their care. While many were motivated by the children in their care, others spoke of the desire to make up for lost time or to regain custody of children still in the child welfare system. Some women were motivated by a fear of losing their children which introduced significant stress, highlighting how parenthood can simultaneously motivate recovery while also presenting challenges. One participant stated, “I just need to do it because I don’t want them back in the system” (RP6), referring to staying sober so her children would not be removed from her care. Another expressed the emotional distress of potentially losing connection with children currently in care: “I need to get my daughters back. They need it, you know what really hurt me when my daughter came to visit me, she said, Mom, do you love me?” (LP7).

Parenthood proved to act not only as a motivator for immediate recovery, but also as a means of sustaining recovery and breaking generational cycles. Some women empathized with their children’s experiences, recounting the times they were in similar situations as children. One participant stated, “For me, it means to be sober for my kids and myself, and to be a better mom and to just like, break the cycle, because that’s how my parents were when I got put into care” (LP2). Another recalled, “If it wasn’t for her [her daughter], I probably wouldn’t be here [in treatment]. I just want to give her the best life, and I know what it feels like to be in care, and I don’t want that for my daughter” (LP4).

For many women, recovery was not only about staying sober in the present, but also about building a long-term future for their children. As LP7 expressed, “Kids aren’t gonna remember what you bought them or what you guys did, but they’re gonna remember who was there, right? So, me being there, showing up for my kids is really what makes me want to stay sober.” Others described how recovery allowed them to look forward to the future: “I look forward to the future with my baby. I’ve had days where I want to leave, but then I had to play the tape to the end. And if I do, what I’d be leaving behind. I can’t leave my baby” (LP4).

The role of peer, family, and professional support systems

Participants described a range of support systems that played important roles throughout their recovery journeys, often helping them to stay motivated. These included peer relationships developed within treatment programs, varying dynamics with family members, and supportive connections with professionals, especially those in treatment programs. Meaningful support helped participants stay focused on recovery; however, some relationships posed challenges that participants actively worked to manage.

Peer support was identified as helpful in various forms, including support and encouragement, shared experiences, and guidance on parenting. Several participants described how meaningful this support was, particularly in discussions about their experiences with peers in their treatment program. One participant shared, “If somebody [a program participant] needs something, or you know, just offers that extra support, it is really comforting” (RP3), while another participant stated, “And for the ones that have been here the longest, they give us good support, and they’re helpful in a way” (RP2). Peers also offered encouragement for recovery. In her discussion about how peers helped to encourage her, one participant stated, “I need reminders quite a bit that you know, help me stay sober, and then also, just to give me some motivation or stuff like that” (LP6).

Many participants described the positive impact of being surrounded by peers with similar experiences. Sharing lived experience created a strong sense of understanding and connection. One participant expressed, “It benefits me, because there are other people there [in treatment] that are going through the same thing as me, and it’s always good to have that reassurance that you’re not alone” (YP1).

In addition to supporting one another in recovery, participants also described the importance of peer relationships in providing parenting support. One participant stated, “We do help each other, if one baby is having trouble, then we explain the way we did it” (RP2), while another shared, “I live with a house of women, and they’re all moms, some of them have a lot of kids. So, they helped me” (LP4).

In addition to discussing peer relationships, many participants reflected on the dynamics of their family relationships. Participants’ experiences with family relationships varied considerably, with some describing positive and supportive connections, while others recounted more challenging or negative interactions. Some of the positive experiences included practical and emotional support, as one participant shared: “And my dad, he supports me, like told me to come here [treatment centre]. And he’s happy I’m here” (LP2). Another participant described the encouragement she received from her mother, “My mother, she calls me and tells me how proud she is of me and how, you know, she’s prayed so hard for me” (RP6).

Other participants spoke more negatively about the ongoing challenges with their family members, especially when those family members were still actively using substances: “My mom, we only had a relationship with drugs. So like, my family isn’t really good support” (LP7) and “I guess, yeah, all my family are all addicts” (RP4). Some participants described their own previous SU which created an ongoing barrier in family relationships. One participant noted, “Just like not a lot of contact [with family], I burnt multiple bridges” (RP1).

Building on the complexities of family dynamics, several participants also described complicated relationships with their partners. Many reported that these relationships were complicated by ongoing SU. One participant explained the challenges of ongoing SU in her partner’s life, and her desire for her partner to get treatment: “You have to go to treatment and cause he has been using when we were together, still together” (RP5). While another explained the complex dynamics of using substances together, “My relationships with my kids’ dad is that like, I don’t know, they were abusive, and we were always intoxicated or high” (RP2).

Participants described that they could not maintain involvement in relationships where ongoing SU was present because it threatened their ongoing recovery. In some cases, the lack of support from family and partners created additional challenges in staying motivated. As a result, many participants emphasized the importance of establishing firm boundaries. One participant explained, “So I kind of like to distance myself [from family members] and set boundaries, just to make sure that I don’t like veer off the wrong path, you know” (LP6). Similarly, RP3 shared, “I did have to leave my old friends and family alone, and that was including my mother, because she was still in active addiction. So, in order to protect my recovery, I did have to set my boundaries.”

Participants also described the support they received from HCPs. Caseworkers were frequently mentioned for their consistent understanding and supportive presence. One participant explained, “Yeah, they [caseworkers] are really understanding, like, if I have something to talk about or whatever, they always help” (LP4). Participants also described positive relationships with nurses and physicians; one noted, “I would chat with the nurses and stuff and tell them how I was feeling, and I felt very supported and heard” (LP6), while another stated: “It was pretty positive with healthcare, and I did tell my doctor that I was taking them [substances], and I’d like to come off of them, and he did help me. I guess that’s another support right there” (YP2).

Many participants also spoke positively about the treatment programs themselves, viewing them as essential environments that foster motivation and sustain recovery. One participant described the program’s value to her: “When we come to program here every day, we don’t just sit here and, you know, we actually sit there and talk. We open up about things and we talk about the topic. So that really helps” (LP7). Another participant emphasized the program’s critical role in helping her maintain sobriety: “It [treatment program] helped really a lot. I don’t think I could see myself having a sobriety without it. Like this place [treatment centre] helped a lot” (LP5). One participant recalled the impact of the program on her recovery: “When I start to get complacent, or if I start losing motivation, then I like, I know that I need to come to program or need to go to a meeting, because then it makes that feeling go away” (LP6). Several participants also highlighted the parenting skills they learned though the program. For example, one participant noted, “Well, before I came here [treatment centre], I didn’t know how to be a parent, so, like, I learned most of my parenting skills from here. So, it’s helped me a lot” (LP4).

The transformative power of resilience

Resilience emerged as a central theme across participant narratives, often described as an internal strength that allowed them to persevere through challenges. One participant simply shared, “I’m resilient and just make stuff happen” (RP1). Similarly, another expressed: “I’m very resilient, so seeing how I was then [before recovery], really helps me to be able to act on those values and morals” (LP1). This ability to persevere despite adversity was commonly expressed as captured by one participant’s determination: “I just realized that it was not about having someone to not give up on me. It was me not having to give up on myself” (LP7).

Resilience emerged as a means for transformation. For many women, the resilience they demonstrated allowed them to move from fear and uncertainty to acceptance and a reimagined future. One participant shared, “It was scary [recovery], but after a while I just started accepting everything … it gets easier” (LP4). The transformation allowed participants to gain greater clarity and understanding of themselves and their lives. One participant expressed, “Now that I’m sober, I’m seeing and understanding more about my life” (RP5). Another acknowledged that even when the recovery path was challenging, it felt right: “Not always certainly my way, but the way that’s like on the right path [of recovery]” (RP2). For LP1, this shift was profound: “Yeah, and it’s [recovery] a complete new way of life” (LP1).

With transformation came hope for the future. As participants reflected on their recovery journeys, it became clear that resilience cultivated motivation to keep moving forward, even when the destination was uncertain. One participant stated, “When I say I’m working towards that good life, I have no clue what it is, but if I keep reaching my goals or keep going, I would eventually find out” (RP3). Another participant shared how her transformation led to a shift in perspective over time: “Yeah, and then later in life, when you realize everything, it [life] becomes easier. It’s basically like you have that happiness” (RP2). She went on to say, “Yeah, some days may get bad days, but it’s like a roller coaster. If you keep fighting, you’ll always get the good days” (RP2).

Discussion

Addressing structural and cultural barriers to sustained recovery

Participants in this study identified that motivation alone is not enough to sustain recovery when structural barriers remain unaddressed. Challenges such as housing insecurity, limited income, lack of transportation, and limited access to healthcare and treatment programs, especially for those living in rural and remote communities, are not only logistical issues, but they are also reflective of the social determinants of health. These challenges reflect constraints in structural recovery capital, highlighting that personal motivation is insufficient without adequate resources. When people are left with no choice but to focus on meeting their basic needs such as food and shelter, recovery is often not a priority. These barriers are not new and have been persistently documented over time in the literature as factors that disproportionally impact women who use substances during the perinatal period [8, 9, 30–32]. Additionally, research has found that women living in rural and remote areas face greater disadvantages when SU treatment programs and perinatal care are limited or unavailable, making them less likely to continue seeking care during the postpartum period [33, 34].

This pattern of structural challenges is consistent with findings from Allen et al. [35], who specifically explored opportunities to expand postpartum support for individuals in recovery from opioid use disorder. Their work highlights how gaps in supportive services such as transportation, childcare, and coordinated follow-up care, limit engagement with recovery supports [36]. Allen et al. also point to opportunities for expanding support, including improving continuity of care and offering wraparound services that provide a coordinated approach to comprehensively address individuals’ needs across both acute care and community settings. These structural supports are essential components of recovery capital, demonstrating how social and community resources contribute to recovery motivation, potentially translating into sustained engagement.

Continued work is needed to eliminate structural barriers so that women are not forced to choose between meeting their basic needs and engaging in recovery. Integrated treatment programs, which combine SU treatment with services like housing support, parenting resources, transportation assistance, mental health care, onsite prenatal and postpartum care, and case management addressing social determinants of health have shown promise in addressing the complex needs of perinatal women who use substances [37, 38]. These programs are often perceived more positively by women and are associated with improved outcomes in prenatal appointment attendance, maternal mental health, and parenting and child outcomes, thus improving recovery [37, 39]. By building structural recovery capital, these integrated programs have the potential to contribute to sustained postpartum recovery. Eliminating barriers requires the widespread implementation of integrated treatment programs across urban, rural, and remote locations to promote equitable access, along with ongoing evaluation to assess their effectiveness.

Accessibility of culturally appropriate resources emerged as an important factor in motivating and supporting recovery among participants. With 88% of participants identifying as Indigenous, the limited availability of culturally specific resources represented a gap in treatment programs. This finding aligns with existing research emphasizing the importance of cultural programming in treatment programs [38]. In the Canadian context, Indigenous women are significantly overrepresented among perinatal populations who use substances, often due to intergenerational trauma, systemic racism, and the ongoing effects of colonization [35, 40]. Yet, despite this overrepresentation, culturally safe, trauma-informed, and community-driven resources remain underfunded and inconsistently available [41, 42]. Culturally appropriate resources represent an important component of both social and structural recovery capital, illustrating how supports that are socially and culturally grounded enhance the ability to sustain recovery. It is critical that recovery models and integrated care programs include culturally appropriate resources as foundational, rather than supplementary.

Integrating family-centered and trauma-informed recovery models

Parenthood reflects a key aspect of social recovery capital, providing emotional connection, accountability, and purpose that reinforce personal motivation and resilience. The findings of this study highlighted the significant role of parenthood as both an immediate and long-term motivator in the recovery process. Similar findings have been reported in other studies. Holcomb et al. [10] found that for new mothers facing SU, the presence of their children provided emotional grounding and strengthened their engagement in recovery. In addition, Proulx & Fantasia [31] identified children as a primary motivator in remaining engaged in treatment. In contrast, the separation of mothers and children was shown to have profound negative implications for both, with a failure to prioritize the mother-child dyad contributing to poorer outcomes [10]. Similarly, Renbarger [43] identified pregnancy and early motherhood as key turning points that can prompt an identity shift and renewed engagement with recovery.

The role of parenthood in recovery was also evident among participants who had lost custody of their older children. Darlington et al. [44] support these findings, reporting that while custody loss can complicate recovery through feelings of shame and guilt, it can also strengthen the desire to pursue decreased SU. They also describe custody lost as a traumatic event that often ignites hope for reunification [44]. Additionally, Jirikowic et al. [45] characterizes this period as “high stakes,” where mothers face intense pressure to complete treatment programs and demonstrate sustained recovery in order to regain custody.

Together, these findings highlight the importance of supporting mothers in recovery by keeping families together whenever possible. The apprehension of children can have lasting negative impacts on both mother and child [10, 44, 45]. Policies and practices that prioritize the preservation of the mother-child relationship may not only improve perinatal recovery outcomes but also leverage both personal and structural recovery capital, by aligning parents’ internal motivation with supportive, family-centered models to enable sustained recovery.

Many participants also contextualized their recovery within a broader goal of breaking intergenerational cycles of trauma, SU, and child welfare involvement. Renbarger [43] supports these findings, noting that perinatal SU and recovery are often shaped by intergenerational trauma. Recovery in this context is not only about decreasing SU but about disrupting harmful patterns and protecting the next generation from similar experiences [43]. This perspective highlights the relational dimension of recovery capital, where social and cultural supports interact with personal motivation to shape recovery trajectories. This finding reinforces the importance of family-centered recovery approaches and highlights the need for treatment programs and child welfare policies to support the deep emotional and intergenerational work that mothers are undertaking. It emphasizes the value of policies that prioritize addressing trauma across generations as part of comprehensive recovery care. Such findings point to the need for trauma- and violence-informed approaches in both treatment programs and child welfare systems, approaches that recognize how past and present trauma shape parenting experiences and recovery processes. As Cullen et al. [46] emphasize, support must be not only trauma-informed but also culturally grounded, acknowledging women’s lived experiences.

Strengthening recovery through interpersonal and structural supports

The findings of this study emphasized the significant role that peer support played in the recovery journeys of postpartum women with SUDs, particularly in the context of treatment programs. Peer supports represent a form of social recovery capital, reinforcing motivation by connecting women with others who share similar experiences and can provide guidance, validation, and encouragement. These findings align with those of Stanojlović and Davidson [47], who identified peer support as a means for overcoming critical barriers in SU recovery through empathetic, non-judgemental support grounded in lived experience. This important peer connection has been identified as one of the core “social mechanisms of change” in women’s SU recovery [48, p. 429]. In addition, Holcomb et al. [49], in their study exploring the experiences of postpartum individuals in early recovery, found that connections with other mothers facing similar challenges were a critical source of support, often replacing negative or unsupportive relationships with friends or family members still using substances. With peer support extending into parenting, many women in our study relied on fellow mothers in recovery for reassurance, validation, and advice, creating a community of support. These findings reflect calls in the literature to recognize the co-occurring experiences of parenting and recovery, and to prioritize social environments that support and nurture both, thereby enhancing social recovery capital [49, 50].

This research, along with existing literature highlights the clear need for expanded implementation of peer support programs specifically tailored to postpartum women with SUDs. Peers offer a distinct and personalized perspective rooted in lived experience, which can foster motivation, accountability, and a sense of shared purpose, which are key factors in supporting recovery [48, 51]. Ingel et al. [51] emphasize that further research is needed to determine the most effective contexts for peer support and to clarify the necessary skill levels required of peer mentors. Inconsistencies in peer support programming may result from a lack of implementation and evaluation supported by academic and clinical settings [51]. To ensure these programs are effective and sustainable, they must be consistently implemented and evaluated within both research and practice contexts.

In addition to peer support, family and partner dynamics emerged as significant in the research. These relationships were often complex, with participants’ experiences ranging from supportive to harmful in relation to their recovery journeys. These findings are consistent with literature on the complex influence of family roles in recovery, acting both as a critical source of support and, in some cases, a risk to sustained recovery [22, 48].

For participants who experienced positive family support, these relationships helped reinforce and sustain recovery. As Goodman et al. [22] explain, family support that is stable and non-judgemental can serve as a protective factor, promoting resilience and a sense of purpose during early motherhood. However, many participants in our study described the necessity of setting firm boundaries with family members, especially those who continued to use substances or did not support their recovery. The importance of creating distance from enabling or harmful family influences aligns with existing research, which highlights the role of emotional safety in supporting sustained recovery [52].

Challenges in partner relationships were also prominent in this study’s findings. Fusco et al. [53] describe a phenomenon known as SU coercion, where partners may be unsupportive or actively undermine recovery efforts. Participants in this study described similar experiences [53]. Goodman et al. [22] recommend integrating family focused interventions into perinatal treatment programs while ensuring women maintain autonomy over decisions regarding which relationships to repair or sever. Additionally, programs should provide targeted support for women experiencing complicated or harmful relationships and prioritize their safety. Ultimately, treatment programs need to prioritize individualized approaches that protect women’s safety and long-term recovery, rather than assuming all family or partner support is supportive for everyone.

Support within treatment programs and from staff were essential to participants in this study. These findings contrast with existing literature, which often describes perinatal women with SUDs feeling stigmatized by HCPs [31, 54–56]. However, much of that literature does not focus on integrated or family treatment programs, where staff are often specifically trained in SUDs, trauma-informed care, and perinatal health [39]. Merritt et al. [55] emphasize the importance of educating HCPs to be competent in SUDs, thus improving care quality and recovery experiences. The majority of participants in this study were attending family treatment centres, which may explain their positive experiences that contrasted with the existing literature.

It is also important to emphasize the need for adequately educated and empathetic HCPs. Barnett et al. [57] note that when HCPs lack specialized training at the intersection of SU and perinatal health, women often face barriers to receiving supportive and non-judgemental care. A lack of provider empathy, fragmented services, and stigma have all been shown to reduce engagement in treatment programs [54, 55, 57]. These findings highlight the importance of integrated, trauma- and violence-informed models, like those described by participants in our study, which offer coordinated, holistic care and foster an environment that encourages and sustains recovery.

These findings are further supported by existing research, which highlights the critical role that treatment program staff play in supporting new mothers with SUDs [10, 22]. Participants valued the holistic support they received, including parenting assistance, emotional encouragement, and access to resources, all delivered within integrated and non-judgmental care environments. Similarly, Goodman et al. [22] found that postpartum women in treatment programs valued empathetic and consistent support from staff. Together these studies reinforce that integrated treatment programs can improve engagement, reduce stigma, and promote sustained recovery. Such programs should be universally accessible to all perinatal patients seeking recovery support. Overall, these findings highlight that recovery is not solely an individual endeavor, but emerges from the dynamic interaction between personal, social, and structural recovery capital.

Prioritizing resilience and strength-based approaches

Resilience emerged as a key finding in this study, understood as an evolving internal strength that supported participants’ motivation in sustaining recovery. Resilience can be viewed as a core component of personal recovery capital, which interacts with social and structural supports to sustain recovery in the postpartum period. This finding is consistent with existing research, which characterizes resilience as a dynamic and evolving process through which individuals adapt to and grow from adversity, gradually developing internal resources that support recovery [22, 58]. While external support was important, participants in our study emphasized the value of drawing on internal motivation to continue motivating them to move forward.

This finding highlights the value of using strengths-based approaches in recovery, which offer an alternative to the traditional deficit-based perspectives often seen in SU research and treatment programs [59]. While it is essential to recognize the substantial challenges that postpartum women in SU recovery face, such as stigma, complex family dynamics, and barriers to accessing resources [8, 9, 31, 55], an exclusive focus on these challenges risks overlooking women’s strengths and capacity for resilience. Gorvine et al. [58] argue that framing recovery through a lens of resilience honors the complexity of women’s lives and avoids reducing them to their challenges. Maurice et al. [60] similarly advocate for approaches that center empowerment, goal setting, and autonomy as important components in fostering resilience, particularly within systems that have historically overemphasized challenges.

These findings have important implications for the design and delivery of treatment programs. Interventions and treatment programming should emphasize strengths building and resilience to support long-term motivation [4]. Treatment programs that integrate participants’ strengths into their designs should move beyond a focus on challenges to become spaces where strengths and resilience are nurtured and sustained. Future recovery models must shift away from deficit-based frameworks and instead build on internal motivation and existing strengths to support long-term recovery.

Limitations

While this study offers valuable insights into the motivation of postpartum women in recovery from SUDs, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was small (N = 16) and not demographically diverse, limiting the transferability of the findings. The majority of participants identified as Indigenous, were in their twenties, single, unemployed, and living in low-income households. Although this reflects important intersections between SU and systemic inequities, the perspectives of individuals with different social and economic backgrounds may not be represented.

Second, all participants were actively engaged in some form of treatment programming or support services at the time of the interview. As such, the findings may not reflect the motivation of postpartum individuals who are unable to access such services due to stigma, structural barriers, or geographic isolation. Similarly, all participants had custody of their youngest child, which may be both a reflection of their recovery progress and the level of support they were receiving. The experiences of women who are navigating recovery while separated from their children may differ significantly and warrants further investigation. In addition, experiences with peers and family may differ in other postpartum populations, and caution is warranted in generalizing assumptions about social support across all recovery experiences.

Third, the sample was primarily drawn from urban areas, due to recruitment efforts being concentrated in urban-based service agencies. As a result, the study may not fully capture the unique experiences and contextual factors faced by women in rural or remote communities, where access to services is often more limited. Future research should aim to include participants from rural and remote settings, as well as those who are disengaged from formal treatment programs, to build a more comprehensive understanding of the motivation involved in postpartum recovery.

Conclusion

This study found that postpartum women with SUDs are motivated to engage in recovery, particularly by their roles as mothers and their desire to build healthier, more stable lives for their children. Parenthood served as both an immediate and long-term motivator, fostering a sense of responsibility, identity, and hope for the future. These experiences illustrate an important dimension of social recovery capital, where meaningful relationships and responsibilities reinforce personal motivation. However, this motivation was often challenged by significant structural and social barriers, including limited access to healthcare, treatment programs, housing, income, transportation, and culturally appropriate supports. Such challenges highlight gaps in structural recovery capital, demonstrating that personal motivation alone is insufficient to sustain recovery without adequate social and structural supports. In response, recovery services must address the determinants of health by implementing integrated treatment programs that combine SU care with housing support, parenting resources, transportation, mental health services, and trauma- and violence-informed care. Culturally safe programming must be foundational, particularly for Indigenous women, reflecting both social and structural recovery capital by ensuring programs are responsive to cultural and community needs. In addition, family-centered and trauma- and violence-informed approaches are critical, with policies that prioritize keeping families together and supporting mothers in efforts to disrupt intergenerational cycles of trauma and child welfare involvement. Peer support emerged as another vital component, representing an important element of social recovery capital that provides shared understanding, mutual accountability, and practical parenting support. Finally, resilience must be recognized as a central component of recovery, and future programming should adopt strengths-based frameworks that build on women’s internal motivation, rather than focusing solely on deficits. Together, these findings demonstrate how postpartum recovery is sustained through the dynamic interaction of personal, social, and structural recovery capital. This perspective highlights the need for recovery models that not only honor women’s motivation and resilience but also actively build recovery capital across personal, social, and structural domains, creating supportive and culturally responsive environments required for sustained, long-term recovery.
