Patient mobility and travel distance to receive drug-resistant tuberculosis treatment, and their associations with loss to follow-up in Guizhou Province, China
Authors: Sisi Jian, Yun Wang, Dafu Wang, Xiaoxue Ma, Yuying He, Jinlan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDrug-resistant tuberculosis (DR-TB) remains a public health crisis, with loss to follow-up (LTFU) being a crucial factor influencing its management. To inform effective TB control strategies, this study aimed to assess patient mobility, travel distances, and their associations with LTFU among DR-TB patients in Guizhou Province, China.MethodsData were collected from a national tuberculosis surveillance system from 2019 to 2021. Patients were classified as non-movers (treated at a hospital within the same prefecture) or movers (treated at a hospital in a different prefecture). Two travel distances were measured using AutoNavi: Distance 1 (home to treatment hospital) and Distance 2 (home to local hospital). The difference between these distances was categorized as follows: (1) Equal distance, (2) Long-distance travel (Distance 1 > Distance 2), and (3) Short-distance travel (Distance 1 < Distance 2). Three logistic regression models assessed associations between exposures (movers vs. non-movers in Model 1, long vs. short Distance 1 in Model 2, long-distance travel vs. equal distance in Model 3) and LTFU.ResultsGuizhou Province has nine prefectures, each with a designated DR-TB hospital. Of the 936 patients studied, 703 (75.1%) were from seven less developed prefectures, and 427 (60.7%) sought treatment in two developed prefectures. The two hospitals provided care for 660 (70.5%) patients (Guiyang: 547 patients, 58.4%; Zunyi: 113 patients, 12.1%). Among movers, 72.2% lived more than 100 km away. Overall, 28.5% of patients were LTFU. Adjusted analysis showed that movers had a higher LTFU risk [aOR = 1.49; 95% CI (1.04, 2.13) in Model 1], especially those with over 145 km Distance 1 [aOR = 1.74; 95% CI (1.11, 2.75) in Model 2] and long-distance travel [aOR = 1.45; 95% CI (1.01, 2.10) in Model 3].ConclusionDR-TB patients often travel from less developed prefectures to two major cities in Guizhou for better care. Patient mobility and travel burdens increase the risk of LTFU. Our study emphasizes the need to strengthen hospital infrastructure, improve DR-TB diagnosis and treatment in underdeveloped areas, and establish effective medication management and follow-up systems across all designated hospitals in the province to reduce DR-TB transmission and LTFU rates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11560-8.

Introduction

Drug-resistant Tuberculosis (DR-TB) refers to TB disease caused by a strain of Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex that is resistant to any TB medicines tested [1]. DR-TB poses a significant threat to global prevention and control owing to its strong infectivity, the difficulty in its diagnosis and treatment, long treatment duration, high cost, and poor efficacy [2]. Globally, the estimated number of DR-TB cases in 2023 was 400 000 [3]. Moreover, only approximately 2/5 of patients with DR-TB receive treatment, and the DR-TB treatment success rate is 68% [3]. Loss to follow-up (LTFU) is one of the main factors affecting the successful control and elimination of DR-TB [4], and the rates of LTFU range from 9.2 to 24.4% by country [5–8]. LTFU is not only associated with the potential risk of transmission but can also lead to a waste of medical resources [4]. Therefore, an increasing number of studies have focused on the determinants of LTFU and taken various measures to prevent it [5–8]. Recently, the World Health Organization (WHO) called for accelerated research on patient mobility to identify and eliminate crucial factors influencing LTFU [9].

Patient mobility is a critical factor in shaping healthcare access [10]. Nevertheless, it also presents unique challenges for the treatment and control of TB. First, mobility can contribute to the spread of TB and the emergence of new hotspots through the introduction of TB and increased interactions between individuals who are infectious and those who are susceptible [11]. Second, patients are defined as movers based on their healthcare-related mobility. They often face barriers to TB diagnosis and access to health care, which can lead to delays in seeking care [12]. Third, movers lack access to care in a new location or gaps in the continuity of care [13], and they may have far-reaching economic consequences that lead to adverse outcomes [14, 15]. Finally, the increase in the number of movers deepens already stark health inequities, leading to greater strain on medical services in developed prefectures, whereas other prefectures may face idle medical resources [16]. The mobility of patients with DR-TB is greater than that of patients with TB [17]. However, little is known about the mobility of individuals receiving DR-TB treatment and its effects on treatment outcomes.

Two types of travel distances and their differences are generated by patient mobility among patients. Distance 1 is defined as the travel distance from a patient’s home to designated DR-TB hospitals where they are treated. Previous studies have confirmed an association between Distance 1 and treatment outcomes. Patients with a longer Distance 1 tend to face greater financial burdens and are more likely to experience LTFU [8, 18]. Distance 2 is defined as the travel distance from a patient’s home to their local designated DR-TB hospitals and is used to assess the availability and accessibility of local healthcare facilities. Patients with a longer Distance 2 can delay TB diagnosis and, in some cases, interrupt treatment [5, 19]. The difference between Distance 1 and Distance 2 reflects patients’ active choices in seeking medical care and the accessibility of services. A significantly greater difference suggests that patients prefer distant hospitals, indicating insufficient service capacity in local designated DR-TB hospitals to meet their needs. Previous studies have rarely quantified Distance 1 or examined the difference between Distance 1 and Distance 2 among patients with DR-TB. Therefore, a comprehensive assessment of the mobility of patients with DR-TB, Distance 1, and the difference between Distance 1 and Distance 2 holds significant value for informing medical policy formulation and resource allocation in areas with high DR-TB burdens.

China is a country with a high DR-TB burden [3], with an estimated 29,000 people infected in 2023 [20]. Guizhou is the only mountainous agricultural province in China with 9 prefectures, where the number of DR-TB cases is higher than the national and global averages and has shown an increasing trend [21]. In line with the National TB Control Program, Guizhou Province has made efforts to establish designated DR-TB hospitals for the diagnosis and treatment of DR-TB in each prefecture to improve access to medical care. However, owing to the uneven distribution of medical resources and service capacity across prefectures, patients may not be treated at the designated DR-TB hospitals in their local areas. The current status of patient mobility among patients with DR-TB in Guizhou Province and the utilization of designated DR-TB hospitals in its nine prefectures remain unclear. It is essential for provincial authorities to understand this to develop informed policy recommendations. To address this issue, we aimed to estimate patient mobility, Distance 1, and the difference between Distance 1 and Distance 2 among patients with DR-TB and to explore their associations with LTFU in Guizhou Province, China.

Methods

Study design, patients and study setting

This was a retrospective observational study based on data collected from a national TB Information Management System (TBIMS) for the registration years from 2019 to 2021. The study included a total of 936 patients with DR-TB, of whom 267 were LTFU, 199 completed treatment, and 470 were cured. Patients were excluded if their treatment outcomes were failure, death, adverse reactions, other reasons, or if they had missing variable data (Fig. 1). These patients were registered in nine prefectures in Guizhou Province: Bijie, Zunyi, Guiyang, Liupanshui, Qiandongnan, Tongren, Qianxinan, Qiannan, and Anshun. Guiyang and Zunyi are the two largest cities in Guizhou Province, of which Guiyang is the provincial capital. There is a designated DR-TB hospital for the diagnosis and treatment of DR-TB in each prefecture. The geographical distribution of the nine prefectures and the nine designated DR-TB hospitals is shown in Fig. 2.

Variable definitions

Patient mobility

Patients are classified as non-movers and movers based on their healthcare-related mobility. Non-movers were defined as patients treated at a designated DR-TB hospital within the same prefecture as their residence. Movers were defined as patients treated at a designated DR-TB hospital in a different prefecture from their residence.

Two kinds of travel distances and their difference

Distance 1 was defined as the one-way travel distance based on road-network routes from a patient’s home address to the designated DR-TB hospital where they received treatment.

Distance 2 was defined as the one-way travel distance based on road-network routes from a patient’s home address to the designated DR-TB hospital located in the same prefecture as their home address.

The difference between Distance 1 and Distance 2 was divided into three categories: (1) Equal distance (Distance 1 = Distance 2): no difference in travel distances; (2) Long-distance travel (Distance 1 > Distance 2): patients bypass the local hospital and travel to a more distant hospital in another prefecture; (3) Short-distance travel (Distance 1 < Distance 2), patients bypass the local hospital and travel to a closer hospital in another prefecture.

Treatment outcomes for DR-TB

In this study, the outcomes of DR-TB treatment were categorized into successful treatment (cure and completed treatment) and LTFU, on the basis of the definitions provided by the WHO [1]. (1) Cure: treatment completed as recommended by the national policy without evidence of failure and three or more consecutive cultures taken at least 30 days apart are negative after the intensive phase; (2) Completed treatment: treatment completed as recommended by the national policy without evidence of failure but no record that three or more consecutive cultures taken at least 30 days apart are negative after the intensive phase; and (3) LTFU: a patient whose treatment was interrupted for 2 consecutive months or more.

Types of patient finding

Types of patient finding were classified as a passive case finding (PCF), an active case finding (ACF) and transfer in [22]. PCF was defined as the way in which individuals presented to health facilities for TB diagnosis after they had developed suspicious symptoms of TB and became aware of their symptoms. ACF was defined as a systematic screening and clinical evaluation of people at high risk of developing TB for early detection. ACF included a variety of approaches, such as contact screening of TB patients at home or in the community. Transfer in was defined as original registration in other health facilities for TB care but transfer to the current health facility to continue care.

Treatment history

Treatment history was defined as new patient and previously treated patient [1]. New patients referred to patients with a newly registered episode of TB who had never been treated for TB or had taken anti-TB medicines for less than 1 month. Previously treated patients referred to patients who has been treated for 1 month or more with anti-TB drugs in the past.

Types of DR-TB patient

Our database includes four types of DR-TB patients: rifampicin-resistant TB (RR-TB), multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB), isoniazid-resistant TB (Hr-TB) and extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB) [3]. RR-TB strains were defined as Mycobacterium tuberculosis strains resistant to rifampicin. MDR-TB strains were defined as Mycobacterium tuberculosis strains resistant to rifampicin and isoniazid. Hr-TB strains were defined as Mycobacterium tuberculosis strains resistant to isoniazid. XDR-TB was defined as TB that is resistant to rifampicin, plus any fluoroquinolone, plus at least one of either bedaquiline or linezolid.

Statistical analysis

The data were cleaned and analyzed using Excel and R software version 4.3.1. Statistical analysis was performed by statistical description and statistical inference.

The statistical description included four parts: (1) the patients’ home addresses and the locations of the designated DR-TB hospital were collected from the TBIMS, and Distance 1 and Distance 2 were obtained by using navigation data from the AutoNavi web mapping system (known as the Gaode Map in Chinese). (2) A geographic distribution map of Guizhou Province was drawn with ArcMap software version 10.8.1, and the spatial distributions of patients and their designated DR-TB hospitals was marked by multi-loop buffering analysis. (3) Medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) are reported for continuous variables; otherwise, frequencies and percentages are reported. Two types of distances were described by medians and IQRs after grouping by prefecture for movers. (4) The mobility of patients was visualized via a Sankey diagram.

Statistical inference included three aspects: (1) Univariate analysis was used to compare the differences between movers and non-movers and included the Mann‒Whitney U test (for continuous variables) and the chi-square test (for categorical variables). (2) The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare Distance 1 with Distance 2 for movers in various prefectures. (3) Covariates with p < 0.2 in the univariate analysis were included in the initial binary unconditional logistic regression model. The final binary unconditional logistic regression model was subsequently established via the stepwise backward method. Crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated to evaluate the magnitude of associations between exposures (movers vs. non-movers in Model 1, long vs. short Distance 1 in Model 2, and long-distance travel vs. equal distance in Model 3) and LTFU. Variance inflation factor was used to investigate for variable collinearity. The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was used to assess the fit of the multivariable binary logistic regression model. Sensitivity analyses were performed to ensure that the study findings were not dependent on the predefined travel distance categories. Specifically, two separate multivariable models were used: in Model 1, Distance 1 was categorized based on quintiles; in Model 2, Distance 1 was treated as a continuous variable (per 10 km). All the statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical review

In this study, data were collected from routine TB surveillance system. The approval of the study was obtained from Guizhou Medical University, and the Department of Tuberculosis Prevention and Control of Guizhou Center for Disease Prevention and Control.

Results

Characteristics of patients by mobility category

The characteristics of patients by patient mobility category are listed in Table 1. Among the 936 patients with DR-TB, 461 (49.3%) were movers, and 475 (50.7%) were non-movers. In terms of Distance 1, the median (IQR) was 85 (39, 144) kilometers (km). In terms of the characteristics of the patients, 626 (66.9%) were males, and 310 (33.1%) were female. The median (IQR) age was 42.0 (28.0, 52.0) years. A total of 755 (80.7%) patients were Han, and the patients were mainly migrant workers and unemployed individuals (504, 53.9%), followed by farmers (328, 35.0%). The number of registered patients was 194 (20.7%) in 2019, 311 (33.2%) in 2020, and 431 (46.1%) in 2021. With respect to the clinical characteristics of the patients, a total of 479 (51.2%) patients were found by transfer, followed by PCF (387, 41.3%). Moreover, 129 (13.8%) patients were new patients, and 807 (86.2%) previously received anti-TB therapy. The majority of patients had positive etiological results (765, 81.7%). A total of 323 (34.5%) patients had RR-TB and 572 (61.1%) patients had MDR-TB.

Across patient mobility categories, significant differences were observed in Distance 1 (p < 0.001), age (p = 0.013), ethnic group (p < 0.001), occupation (p < 0.001), registration year (p < 0.001), types of patient finding (p < 0.001), etiological results (p = 0.028), and types of DR-TB patient (p = 0.003). However, no significant differences were observed in gender (p = 0.309) and treatment history (p = 0.215).

Patient mobility

The patient mobility status of the patients is shown in Fig. 3. The left side of Fig. 3 shows the prefectures where the patients lived and the distribution of the 936 patients across 9 prefectures. Among the patients, the highest proportion were from Bijie (212, 22.6%), followed by Zunyi (128, 13.7%). The right side of Fig. 3 shows the prefectures of the designated DR-TB hospitals where the patients finalized treatment. A total of 703 (75.1%) of the patients were registered in seven less developed prefectures, of whom 427 (60.7%) traveled to designated DR-TB hospitals for treatment in two developed prefectures (Guiyang and Zunyi). Among the 9 designated DR-TB hospitals, the hospital located in Guiyang received patients from various prefectures, so the proportion of patients treated in designated hospitals in Guiyang was the highest (547, 58.4%). This was followed by the proportion of patients treated in designated DR-TB hospitals in Zunyi (113, 12.1%). In addition, a total of 461 movers went to the designated DR-TB hospitals in Guiyang (443, 96.1%) and Zunyi (18, 3.9%). The top three mobility rates calculated at the prefecture level were as follows: Anshun (57, 87.7%), Bijie (169, 79.7%) and Qiannan (49, 72.1%).

Travel distances of movers

Distance 1 of the movers is shown in Fig. 4. Taking two designated DR-TB hospitals in Guiyang and Zunyi as the centers of the circles, distance buffer plots with radii of 50, 100, 150 and 200 km were drawn. The results of the multi-loop buffering analysis revealed that only 2.8% (13/461) of the movers lived within a 50-km buffer around the two designated hospitals where they received treatment, whereas 25.0% (115/461) lived within a 50–100 km buffer. Most movers lived outside the 100-km buffer around the hospitals (333, 72.2%), with those in the 100 to 150-km range accounting for 29.9% (138/461) and those in the 150- to 200-km range accounting for 19.7% (91/461). Moreover, more than one-fifth (22.6%, 104/461) of the movers lived beyond the 200-km buffer zone around the hospitals.

Associations among patient mobility, travel distances and LTFU before covariate adjustment

The associations among patient mobility, travel distances and LTFU before covariate adjustment are presented in Table 2. In terms of treatment outcomes, a total of 267 (28.5%) patients reported LTFU, and 669 (71.5%) patients had successful treatment. In the regression model before covariate adjustment, the group with successful treatment was used as the reference group. Compared with patients with 0–39 km Distance 1, patients with over 145 km Distance 1 had an increased risk of LTFU [crude OR = 1.53; 95% CI (1.03, 2.29)]. In addition, age, gender, occupation, registration year and types of patient finding were associated with LTFU (p < 0.05).

Discussion

This retrospective observational study was conducted in Guizhou Province. Data from patients with DR-TB registered in nine prefectures in this province were collected from the TBIMS from 2019 to 2021. Among the 936 DR-TB patients, 49.3% (461) were movers. Compared with non-movers, movers account for a greater proportion of younger patients, Han patients, migrant workers and unemployed patients, transferred patients and MDR-TB patients. All movers went to the designated DR-TB hospitals in Guiyang (96.1%) and Zunyi (3.9%). The top three mobility rates calculated at the prefecture level were as follows: Anshun, Bijie, and Qiannan. Taking two designated DR-TB hospitals in Guiyang and Zunyi as the center of the circle to draw buffer plots, 72.2% of the movers lived outside the 100-km buffer around the two hospitals. In addition, movers in 6 prefectures (Tongren, Liupanshui, Qianxinan, Qiandongnan Anshun, and Bijie) went to designated DR-TB hospitals in other prefectures that were farther away from their homes for treatment rather than to local designated DR-TB hospitals that were closer to their homes. In this study, a total of 267 (28.5%) patients were LTFU, which was significantly associated with patient mobility and travel distances. In addition, age, gender, ethnic group, occupation, registration year and types of patient finding were significant factors influencing LTFU.

This study demonstrated that mobility is common among DR-TB patients in Guizhou Province, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [15, 23]. In addition, our study indicated that movers had a greater proportion of some characteristics, which is supported by previous research. First, movers tend to be younger than non-movers, which may be attributed to the fact that motor function and independence gradually decrease with age [24]. Second, previous studies have shown associations among ethnicity, occupation, and mobility [25, 26]. Han patients have a better quality of life than ethnic minority patients do, which may make them better equipped for mobility [27]. Additionally, migrant workers and unemployed patients have no fixed jobs; they often move to multiple prefectures in search of employment opportunities, which increases their mobility [28]. Third, a greater proportion of movers were patients who were transferred. This is related to the hierarchical diagnosis and treatment regulations for TB in Guizhou Province, which stipulate that rifampicin-sensitive patients are treated in county-level designated TB hospitals, whereas patients with DR-TB are treated in prefecture-level designated DR-TB hospitals. The implementation of this hierarchical diagnosis and treatment model is mainly due to the limited capabilities of diagnosis and treatment, the difficulty in ensuring the supply and management of second-line anti-TB drugs for treating DR-TB, and the limitations in terms of laboratory equipment and ward conditions in county-level hospitals. Therefore, county-level hospitals need to refer patients with DR-TB to prefecture-level hospitals for diagnosis and treatment [29]. Additionally, hospitals in underdeveloped prefectures often encourage patients to seek treatment in hospitals located in developed prefectures, which can affect patient mobility [30]. Finally, MDR-TB patients often require more complex treatment, and they may be referred between different health care facilities, further increasing their mobility [31]. These findings suggest that more attention should be given to patients with these characteristics and that targeted interventions should be developed to reduce patient mobility, thereby improving treatment outcomes and reducing the spread of DR-TB.

The results of our study revealed that all movers went to the designated DR-TB hospitals in Guiyang and Zunyi for treatment. This may be explained by two factors. On the one hand, it is related to the diagnosis and treatment capacity of the hospital. The designated hospitals for DR-TB diagnosis and treatment were first established in Guiyang and Zunyi in 2012 in Guizhou Province [32]. The two designated hospitals have better DR-TB diagnosis and treatment services, relatively advanced facilities, and excellent medical teams [33]. Thus, they have established a good reputation and good credibility for DR-TB patients. On the other hand, this difference may be related to patients’ perceptions of the severity of their illnesses and differences in their trust in and awareness of the health system [34]. Our study also revealed that the prefectures with the top three mobility rates were Anshun, Bijie, and Qiannan. These results may be attributed to the following: (1) the three prefectures neighbor Guiyang and Zunyi, especially Anshun, which may make it easier for patients to visit; (2) the diagnosis and treatment level of designated hospitals in the three prefectures cannot meet the needs of local patients, so after diagnosis, patients either take the initiative to travel to Guiyang and Zunyi hospitals for treatment or are referred by local hospitals to the two hospitals for treatment. In summary, designated DR-TB hospitals in developed prefectures attract more patients with DR-TB and investments, enabling them to operate on a wider scale, which promotes hospital affluence and efficiency [16, 35]. This, in turn, has attracted more high-quality medical professionals to these prefectures, further increasing gaps between prefectures and increasing patient mobility [35]. To address this problem, it is essential not only to increase investment in underresourced designated DR-TB hospitals but also to further strengthen hospital construction. This should include providing more support to hospitals to enhance the capacity and talent development of DR-TB diagnosis and treatment teams. Additionally, the health administration department should establish a convenient patient referral channel for diagnosis and treatment, enabling patients to access medical care more easily and reducing indirect costs associated with diagnosis and treatment.

In this study, a total of 267 patients (28.5%) were LTFU, while 669 patients (71.5%) achieved successful treatment outcomes. Both proportions are higher than those reported in previous studies [3, 5–8]. This discrepancy may be due to the study’s inclusion criteria, which focused solely on DR-TB patients with successful treatment or LTFU, while excluding patients with other treatment outcomes. As a result, the total number of patients in the study cohort was reduced, thereby exaggerating the proportions of both LTFU and successful treatment. Furthermore, this study revealed that movers, patients with over 145 km Distance 1, and long-distance travel (Distance 1 > Distance 2) were more likely to be LTFU from DR-TB care. These results can be explained from two perspectives. First, it is a major challenge for health care workers to follow up on movers’ medications on a regular basis [36]. This increases the risk of LTFU for DR-TB patients [36]. Second, our study found that many patients prefer to travel to more distant hospitals, which may offer more advanced treatments, rather than choosing closer local hospitals. This aligns with previous findings that patients travel farther for more advanced DR-TB care [37, 38]. Moreover, recent studies have shown that traveling long distances leads to additional financial burdens for patients and caregivers [38, 39], including direct medical costs (such as treatment costs) [40, 41], direct nonmedical costs (transportation, food, and accommodation costs during facility visits) [40] and income loss [41]. The increased financial burden may directly affect adherence and lead to the occurrence of LTFU among TB patients [42]. Therefore, we suggest that local designated DR-TB hospitals improve their service level in three ways to attract patients, retain patients in local designated hospitals as much as possible, and reduce the possibility of LTFU: (1) it is essential to strengthen the training and continuing education of medical personnel and enhance their professional skills and service awareness to ensure the quality of medical services; (2) it is crucial to invest in advanced medical equipment to improve the accuracy and efficiency of diagnosis and treatment; and (3) it is important to establish more effective medication management and follow-up tracking mechanisms for patients among designated hospitals at all levels in the province. Additionally, a convenient patient referral channel should be established to facilitate patients’ access to regional designated DR-TB hospitals, thereby reducing patient visit time and optimizing the medical treatment process.

In terms of nonmodifiable factors associated with LTFU for DR-TB patients, in this analysis, older age, belonging to ethnic minority groups, registration in 2020 were more likely to be LTFU, while patients who were female, those with fixed jobs and those found by ACF or transferred in were less likely to be LTFU. Similar results have been reported in other studies. First, older patients may have more comorbidities and are more likely to have adverse drug reactions [43]. In addition, older patients may need more support from family and society to help them seek treatment, which makes them more likely to be LTFU [44]. Second, ethnic minority groups have relatively low economic statuses and education levels, their home addresses are far from health facilities, and the relative lack of infrastructure and resources makes them more vulnerable to LTFU [45]. Third, female patients have more time to manage their medications than male patients do, and they are more aware of their health, so they are less likely to LTFU [46]. Fourth, patients with fixed jobs have stable incomes, which in turn better supports their expenses with respect to treatment costs, making them less likely to LTFU. Fifth, patients registered for treatment in 2020 were more likely to be LTFU compared to those in 2019, possibly due to the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on patient care and medical services [47, 48]. Finally, patients identified through ACF or who transferred in tend to have milder symptoms, allowing for earlier detection, diagnosis, and treatment, which leads to better adherence and reduces the possibility of LTFU [49].

Although this study was conducted in Guizhou Province, its findings may have certain reference value for other regions. Following China’s 13th Five-Year Plan, which called for at least one designated DR-TB hospital in each prefecture-level region [29], provinces have adapted this policy according to their local conditions. For example, Yunnan Province adopted differentiated and personalized comprehensive supportive care services to improve treatment adherence [50]; Xinjiang implemented a centralized inpatient care model to enhance treatment outcomes [51]; and economically developed areas such as Jiangsu and Shanghai established outpatient-based systems supported by digital follow-up and comprehensive subsidies [52, 53]. However, since designated hospitals are based at the prefecture level and most patients live in rural areas, disparities in healthcare access and patient mobility remain key challenges [4, 54]. Our findings on mobility-related LTFU are therefore relevant to similarly underserved areas within and beyond China. This is consistent with WHO’s emphasis on improving service accessibility for mobile and vulnerable populations as a key component of TB control. Thus, the experience in Guizhou may offer valuable insights for regions facing comparable geographic or structural barriers.

Our study has several limitations. First, in our study, the travel distances of the patients were underestimated. This is because, to protect patient privacy, we obtained only the town/community locations according to their home addresses from the TBIMS database, which did not allow for precise identification of their actual home addresses. Second, the data from the TBIMS database contain only a limited number of variables. Some risk factors for LTFU, such as costs (treatment, transportation, food and accommodation costs) [40, 41], complications [43], and adverse drug reactions [43], are not available in the TBIMS database. Thus, their effects on LTFU could not be assessed in this study. Third, this study was conducted in a single province, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to regions with different epidemiological profiles or healthcare systems. Although we introduced cross-regional comparisons, variations in socioeconomic conditions, health policies, and patient behaviors may influence the applicability of our conclusions. Further research in diverse settings is needed to validate these findings. Ultimately, despite these limitations, our findings reveal associations between patient mobility and travel distance to receive DR-TB treatment and LTFU. Therefore, our study can provide a reference for management policies for patients with DR-TB and inspire future research to address these limitations and obtain more precise results.

Conclusions

This study was conducted in Guizhou Province across nine prefectures, and designated DR-TB hospitals providing treatment for DR-TB have been established in each prefecture. A total of 703 (75.1%) patients were registered in seven less developed prefectures, of whom 427 (60.7%) moved to the designated DR-TB hospitals for treatment in two developed prefectures. As a result, these two hospitals treated 660 (70.5%) patients in the province. Moreover, we found that patient mobility and travel distance are important factors affecting LTFU. Therefore, this study suggests that Guizhou provincial authorities should strengthen the construction and investment in designated DR-TB hospitals in less developed prefectures, improve their ability to diagnose and treat DR-TB, and enhance health education for patients so that they can receive treatment as close to home as possible. These measures will help reduce the additional burden and transmission of DR-TB due to patient mobility, further decreasing the LTFU rate and improving the DR-TB cure rate. Thus, it is vital to ensure that research findings are rapidly translated into actionable policies and practices to close the gaps in care and improve outcomes, contributing to global efforts to eliminate TB.
