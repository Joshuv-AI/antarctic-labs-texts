Sleep duration, depression, and cognitive function among older adults in China: A longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Jiduan Jiang, Jin Zhou, Yiyun Li, Xiaoxia Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore interactions between depression and sleep duration and their impact on long-term cognitive trajectories.MethodsThis longitudinal study (2011−2020) analyzed depressive symptoms and sleep duration in relation to 10-year cognitive trajectories in 7649 Chinese adults aged 45–80 years. Exposures included sleep duration and depressive symptoms. Cognitive function was the primary outcome, standardized using regression models adjusted for age, sex, and education. Standardized Z-scores for global cognition, episodic memory, and mental status were derived. Growth mixture modeling identified three cognitive trajectories. Multivariable logistic regression examined exposure-trajectory associations.ResultsThree distinct cognitive trajectories were identified: persistently high, moderately stable, and persistently low. Compared with the “non-depression-optimal sleep” group, all other sleep-depression groups showed significantly reduced associations, with “depression-long sleep” exhibiting the highest risk of low cognition. Within the persistent moderate group, all combinations except “non-depression-short sleep” showed significant negative associations, with “depression-long sleep” exhibiting the greatest risk. Sleep duration had a U-shaped relationship with cognitive scores, whereas depressive symptoms were negatively associated. Optimal cognitive maintenance occurred among participants with no depression and a 6–8 h sleep duration.ConclusionsPromoting regular, healthy sleep and reducing depression risk may help preserve cognitive function in old age.Keywords: Sleep duration, Depression, Cognitive function, Older adults, Longitudinal analysis, Cognitive trajectories, Aging

Introduction

Global population aging poses significant challenges worldwide. China has one of the largest populations of older people, with the fastest rate of population aging (Wang et al., 2024). Cognitive impairment is a severe and common condition among older adults, which often progresses to dementia, making it a key predictor of disability, mortality, and increased medical and social costs (Karr et al., 2018). With no effective treatment available, exploring its pathogenesis and risk factors is crucial for developing prevention and intervention strategies.

Major depression, a comorbidity of chronic physical illness, has a global estimated prevalence of 3.4% (Mo et al., 2024). Depression is associated with new-onset dementia and Alzheimer's disease, and it causes cognitive decline in existing conditions (Arnaud et al., 2022). However, cognitive changes result from interactions among multiple lifestyle factors (Kunugi, 2023). Sleep duration is particularly important and has been shown to exhibit a U-shaped relationship with cognitive ability, indicating that both insufficient and excessive sleep affect cognitive trajectories (Ma et al., 2020).

Sleep duration and depression are interrelated; both short and long sleep durations are associated with increased risks (Austin-Zimmerman et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023; Zhang and Cheng, 2024). Few studies have comprehensively explored the combined relationship between depression, sleep, and cognitive function. Several others have demonstrated that the combined effects of depression and poor sleep duration were associated with cognitive decline; however, these studies were small scaled (N < 500) (Yang et al., 2023) or cross-sectional (Liu et al., 2022; Ding et al., 2024), limiting conclusions regarding long-term trajectories.

This study aimed to analyze the combined impact of depressive symptoms and sleep duration on 10-year cognitive function trajectories in middle-aged and older adults using high-quality, large-scale longitudinal data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). We also investigated independent associations between depressive symptoms, sleep duration, and cognitive function.

Materials and methods

Study setting

The CHARLS is a nationally representative, high-quality longitudinal survey that collects microdata on households and individuals aged ≥45 years in China. Established in 2011, it uses a multi-stage probability-proportional-to-size sampling method, covering 150 districts and counties and 450 villages or communities across 28 provinces, enrolling approximately 10,000 households and 17,000 respondents. Follow-up surveys are conducted every 2–3 years. The CHARLS has publicly released five rounds of survey data, including the 2011 baseline survey (Round 1) and four rounds of follow-up (Rounds 2–5: 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020) (http://charls.pku.edu.cn). This study used publicly available anonymized data from the CHARLS database, and all methods were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations for the protection of human participants. The present study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University; all participants provided written informed consent.

Study population

This study included participants aged 45–80 years who completed the CHARLS Rounds 1–5. Of the 10,498 participants initially screened, 2849 were excluded based on the following exclusion criteria: (1) no baseline cognitive assessment data (n = 2474); (2) no cognitive assessment data across all follow-up periods (n = 200); (3) baseline memory or mental disorders (n = 94); and (4) missing or abnormal baseline data on depression and sleep duration (n = 81). Ultimately, 7649 individuals were included in the study (Fig. S1).

Measures

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (Cheng and Chan, 2005; Chen and Mui, 2014; Du et al., 2023). The scale evaluates the psychological state of the participants in the prior week through 10 items: being bothered by little things, difficulty concentrating, feeling depressed, feeling sluggish, feeling hopeful (reverse-scored), feeling fearful, disturbed sleep, feeling happy (reverse-scored), loneliness, and difficulty in action. Scoring uses a four-point Likert scale: 0, 1 day; 1, 1–2 days; 2, 3–4 days; and 3, 5–7 days. Total scores range from 0 to 30, indicating depression symptom severity. A score of ≥10 was used as the clinical cutoff for significant depressive symptoms (Rong et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2023).

Sleep duration was determined using self-reporting; respondents were asked about their average total daily sleep duration over the past week, including both nighttime and daytime sleep; it was categorized as short (<6 h), optimal (6–8 h), or long (>8 h) (Sewell et al., 2021). We defined the reasonable range for sleep duration as 3–14 h per day, and values outside this range were excluded prior to data analysis.

Cognitive function was assessed using two domains: Episodic Memory and Mental Status (Guo et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025). A 10-word recall test was used to assess episodic memory, testing immediate and delayed (after 10 min) recall. The average number of recalled words in both tests was used as the episodic memory score (0–10 points). Mental status was assessed using a modified version of the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status framework through five core tasks, including five serial subtractions of seven starting at 100, time orientation (assessing date/day/season), and the visuospatial overlapping pentagon copy test. Responses were summed into the mental status score (0–11 points). The episodic memory and mental status scores were then combined into a cognitive composite score (0–21 points).

Covariates

Covariates included age, sex, marital status, residence, household income, education level, body mass index (BMI), current tobacco and alcohol use, comorbidities, activities of daily living (ADL) limitations, pain, and social participation. Details of the covariates included are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analysis

This study focused on dynamic changes in standardized cognitive Z-scores, with secondary outcomes being the trajectories of episodic memory and mental status Z-scores. First, a multiple linear regression model including age, sex, and education duration was constructed to predict individual cognitive levels (Ŷ). Subsequently, the original cognitive scores were transformed using the standardized residual formula Z = (Y - Ŷ)/RMSE, where Y represents the observed value, Ŷ is the predicted value from the model, and RMSE represents the root mean square error of the regression equation (Shirk et al., 2011). This transformation was applied to the overall cognitive function and subdomain scores (episodic memory/mental status), and all analyses used the transformed standardized Z-scores.

A group-based trajectory model (GBTM) was used to identify heterogeneous developmental trajectories of cognitive Z-scores at different ages, incorporating all available cognitive scores in the model estimation, provided that missing scores were randomly missed. Model estimates were based on all available cognitive data, with a censored normal distribution used to model continuous cognitive Z-scores (Yan et al., 2024). The trajectory group number was set from 1 to 6, and models with varying group numbers were gradually fitted using age as the time scale. To determine the optimal trajectory classification, a longitudinal prediction equation for cognitive scores (quadratic polynomial) based on age was established, followed by comparisons of the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) values to select the best model structure. The final model required an average posterior probability within each group of ≥70% to confirm reliability, and a sample proportion in each trajectory group of >5% to ensure statistical power.

Multivariate logistic regression was then used to quantify longitudinal associations between the depression and sleep duration combination and cognitive trajectory categories, with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) as core effect indicators. The fully adjusted model included education level, alcohol use, tobacco use, ADL, income, sex, marital status, chronic pain, social participation, comorbidities, and residence. Subgroup analysis was performed based on sex, and the interaction term for depression and sleep duration × sex was introduced in the fully adjusted model.

Three sensitivity analyses were performed. First, given that trajectory analysis is more stable for participants with three or more observations, sensitivity analysis was conducted for participants with at least three rounds of cognitive function measurements. Second, to minimize the effects of preclinical or clinical dementia symptoms on depression and sleep, participants with cognitive impairment at baseline (defined as a total cognitive score < 7 [1.5 standard deviations below the mean]) were excluded (Chertkow et al., 2007). Finally, a linear mixed-effects model was used with age as the time scale, and individual-level random intercepts and slopes (following a multivariate normal distribution with an unstructured covariance matrix) were incorporated. All continuous variables were centered or standardized. Additionally, to provide context and an interpretative foundation for subsequent longitudinal trajectory outcomes, we employed restricted cubic spline analysis to examine the cross-sectional relationships among sleep duration, depressive symptoms, and cognitive scores based on the baseline data. All analyses were conducted using R software version 4.4.3, and a two-sided P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

In total, 7649 participants (mean age, 57.3 ± 8.0 years; 50.1% men) were included. Optimal sleep duration was noted in 64.6% of the participants, and comorbid depression in 33.7%. Cognitive function score trajectories

Among the six overall cognitive function trajectory models constructed, the average posterior probabilities of all trajectory groups exceeded 80%, and the minimum sample proportion in each group was >5%. When a three-trajectory grouping scheme was adopted, the model achieved the best fit, with the lowest AIC and BICvalues (101,956.10 and 102,075.82, respectively; Table S2). Therefore, the three-trajectory GBTM model was identified as the best model. Fig. 1A illustrates the trajectory patterns of overall cognitive function, categorized into three distinct groups: persistently high, moderately stable, and continuously low levels. Similarly, for the two cognitive subdomains—episodic memory and mental status—the three-trajectory GBTM demonstrated optimal model fit, with corresponding trajectories presented in Fig. 1B–C.

Baseline characteristics of overall cognitive function trajectory subgroups

Table 2 presents the baseline characteristics of the overall cognitive function trajectory subgroups. Compared with those in the continuous high-trajectory group (Class 1), participants in the continuous low-trajectory group (Class 2) were significantly older (60.7 ± 8.3 years vs. 56.1 ± 7.6 years), with a significantly higher proportion of women (70.0% vs. 44.0%) and individuals with no formal education (82.0% vs. 22.0%). Further, a higher proportion of participants lived in rural areas (79.0% vs. 56.0%) and had lower average income (19,368 CNY vs. 26,713 CNY). Additionally, the prevalence of chronic pain (47.0% vs. 27.0%) and proportion of participants with short sleep (38.0% vs. 23.0%), depressive symptoms (54.0% vs. 26.0%), and restricted ADLs (25.0% vs. 10.0%) were all higher in the low-trajectory group.

Relationship between depression-sleep category and cognitive trajectories

For overall cognitive function, compared with participants in the non-depressed optimal sleep group, participants in all other depressed-sleep groups had a significantly lower probability of being in the persistently high cognitive function trajectory. Among these, the depressed-long sleep combination had the lowest probability (OR = 0.30, 95% CI: 0.23, 0.38). Similarly, for the persistently moderate cognitive function trajectory group, except for the non-depressed short sleep group (which showed no significant difference from the reference group, OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.85, 1.09), the probability of participants in all other combinations classified into this group was also significantly lower, also with the depressed-long sleep combination exhibiting the highest risk (OR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.44, 0.68). Similar results were observed for the secondary outcomes of episodic memory and mental status (Table 3). Table S3 shows the association of depression comorbidity and different sleep durations with cognitive trajectories. Both depression and sleep duration were independently associated with different cognitive trajectory groups (overall and subdomains). Subgroup analysis by sex showed similar associations between depression-sleep category and cognitive trajectory groups in men and women (all P > 0.05 for interaction; Fig. S2).

Sensitivity analysis

First, a sensitivity analysis was conducted with 6056 participants who had at least three rounds of cognitive function measurements. The trajectory patterns for overall and subdomain cognitive function were similar to those in the main analysis, with three groups: continuously high, continuously low, and continuously moderate (Fig. S3). The multivariate logistic regression results for depression-sleep duration combinations and cognitive function trajectory groups were consistent with the main analysis (Table S4). After excluding participants with baseline cognitive function scores below the threshold of 7, the remaining 6938 participants were analyzed. The trajectory classification (Fig. S4) and multivariate logistic regression results were consistent with the main analysis (Table S5). Finally, a mixed-effects model was used to analyze repeated measures. The results of Model 1 showed that, with non-depression-optimal sleep as the reference group, the non-depression-short sleep group had a cognitive function decrease of 0.05 units (95% CI: −0.10, −0.01), the depression-short sleep group showed a decrease of 0.25 units (95% CI: −0.30, −0.20), the depression-optimal sleep group showed a decrease of 0.21 units (95% CI: −0.26, −0.16), the non-depression-long sleep group showed a decrease of 0.16 units (95% CI: −0.23, −0.09), and the depression-long sleep group showed a decrease of 0.36 units (95% CI: −0.48, −0.25). Model 2 showed that depression and sleep duration were independently related to cognitive levels (Table S6). The mixed linear effects model revealed that the interaction between sleep and depression was significant across all three distinct cognitive domains (Table S7).

Cross-sectional analysis of baseline sleep duration, depression scores, and cognitive scores

Fig. 2 illustrates the dose–response relationship between sleep duration, depression scores, and cognitive function scores across various domains. Sleep duration was significantly associated with cognitive function scores across all domains (overall P < 0.01), showing a significant U-shaped non-linear relationship. Threshold analysis showed that a sleep duration of 7 h resulted in the highest values in cognitive scores in all domains (Fig. 2A–C). Similarly, depression scores were significantly associated with cognitive function scores across all domains (overall P < 0.01), exhibiting a linear negative correlation with overall cognitive function and mental status scores and a non-linear relationship with episodic memory scores. Despite differences in patterns, the overall trend was consistent: as depression scores increased, cognitive scores across all domains decreased (Fig. 2D–F).

Discussion

This 10-year cognitive trajectory study among middle-aged and older adults demonstrated that the absence of depressive symptoms and an optimal sleep duration (6–8 h) were independently associated with sustained cognitive maintenance. Meanwhile, depression alongside insufficient or excessive sleep was related to poorer cognitive trajectories and faster cognitive decline. Therefore, depression and sleep duration jointly influenced cognitive trajectories, indicating that their combination synergistically affects cognitive development. Specifically, regardless of sleep duration, the presence of depressive symptoms was consistently associated with lower cognitive levels, and depression with long sleep further correlated with poorer cognitive trajectories and faster cognitive decline. These findings highlight the significant combined role of depression and sleep duration in shaping long-term cognitive trajectories in middle-aged and older adults.

Consistent with prior cross-sectional research, the absence of depressive symptoms and an optimal sleep duration (6–8 h) have been associated with cognitive function (Ding et al., 2024). Long sleep combined with depression significantly impaired multiple cognitive domains in older adults, including executive function, verbal memory, and working memory, consistent with our findings (Nielson et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2024). More importantly, this study extended prior evidence using a large cohort. It not only examined long-term associations but also confirmed the U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and cognitive function (with approximately 7 h of sleep being associated with the most favorable outcomes). Additionally, it demonstrated a significant negative correlation between depression symptom severity and cognitive function. These findings provide strong longitudinal evidence that depressive symptoms and sleep duration are key modifiable factors influencing long-term cognitive trajectories.

Sleep may influence cognition (Sewell et al., 2021), potentially through acetylcholine regulation and memory consolidation (Gais and Born, 2004). Depressive symptoms in older adults may reflect underlying neurodegenerative changes (Wei et al., 2024), including increased tau protein deposition in the brain (Babulal et al., 2020), particularly in key regions such as the inferior temporal lobe and entorhinal cortex. Thus, the severity of depression has been positively correlated with tau levels in these areas (Gatchel et al., 2017). Notably, these regions of tau deposition overlap significantly with the pathological features found in Alzheimer's disease (van der Kant et al., 2020). Furthermore, sleep patterns are strongly associated with depression risk. Older adults with moderate or poor sleep patterns have 2.49-fold and 6.76-fold higher risks of depression (Niu et al., 2025), respectively, than those with healthy sleep patterns. Thus, poor sleep quality may both result from and contribute to depression, while depression may trigger or worsen sleep problems (Steiger and Pawlowski, 2019). This bidirectional interaction creates a vicious cycle, with both factors interacting and jointly accelerating cognitive decline.

The strength of this study lied in its use of a large, nationally representative cohort with 10 years of follow-up. Previous studies were often cross-sectional or small-scaled, limiting assessments of long-term cognitive trajectories and causal relationships. Because cognitive decline can precede clinical symptoms by decades (Ramakers et al., 2007), we excluded individuals with baseline memory impairment or mental disorders and those with significant cognitive decline during the follow-up period to reduce reverse causality bias. The sample size ensured good statistical power, enabling the empirical validation of the synergistic effects of depression and sleep duration on 10-year cognitive trajectories.

This study also had some limitations. First, sleep duration data were self-reported, which may have introduced recall bias. However, previous studies indicate a moderate level of consistency between subjective and objective measurements (Ding et al., 2022), partially mitigating this limitation. Second, residual confounding factors could not be excluded. Although variables such as chronic disease and social participation were adjusted for, potential confounders, including genetic markers (e.g., APOEε4), dietary patterns, or environmental exposures, were not assessed. Finally, causal inference was constrained by the bidirectional relationship between depression and cognitive impairment, and reverse causality could not be entirely ruled out. Future research should employ prospective designs and multi-omics data to explore the complex interactions between sleep, depression, and long-term cognitive function.

Conclusion

Using large-scale cohort data, this study revealed a U-shaped association between sleep duration and cognitive function and a negative correlation between depressive symptom severity and cognition. Depression and sleep duration showed significant interaction effects, jointly associated with long-term cognitive trajectories. The most favorable outcomes were reported among individuals without depressive symptoms who slept 6–8 h. Therefore, promoting healthy sleep and lifestyle choices to reduce the risk and severity of depression are key protective factors for preserving cognitive function in older age.
