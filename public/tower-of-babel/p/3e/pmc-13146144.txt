Sex‐Specific Associations of Inflammatory Biomarkers With All‐Cause and Cardiovascular Mortality Across Glycaemic Status: A Prospective UK Biobank Study
Authors: Yawen Zhang, Menghan Li, Manrong Xu, Yun Shen, Lianxi Li, Gang Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo explore sex‐specific heterogeneity in the prognostic discrimination of inflammatory markers for mortality across different glycaemic states.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 450 438 participants from the UK Biobank (median follow‐up: 15.3 years), stratified by sex and glycaemic status. Cox models were applied to evaluate associations between eight inflammatory markers—CRP, WBC, neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), CRP‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (CLR), inflammatory burden index (IBI), systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII), pan‐immune‐inflammation value (PIV), and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI)—and all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality, with markers analysed in parallel using independent models. Dose–response associations and discriminative performance were assessed using restricted cubic splines (RCS) and time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses, respectively. Spearman correlation analyses were conducted to contextualize relationships between inflammatory markers and cardiometabolic phenotypes.ResultsDeteriorating glycaemic status was associated with progressively higher all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality in both sexes, together with sex‐specific differences in inflammatory marker trajectories. As glycaemia worsened, the discriminative performance of inflammatory markers for mortality tended to attenuate in women but remained generally more stable in men. Time‐dependent ROC analyses suggested stage‐ and sex‐specific heterogeneity. In diabetes, NLR showed stronger early discrimination in women, whereas SIRI showed more stable discrimination in men and at later follow‐up in women.ConclusionInflammatory biomarkers show sex‐ and glycaemia‐specific patterns in mortality discrimination, with NLR and SIRI showing comparatively stable discrimination, particularly in diabetes.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with ischaemic heart disease and stroke accounting for the greatest burden [1]. Diabetes, a rapidly growing metabolic disorder projected to affect 693 million adults by 2045 [2], is a major contributor to increased morbidity and mortality through its devastating macrovascular and microvascular complications, including cardiovascular disease, kidney failure, and retinopathy. Importantly, this excess vascular risk emerges even earlier, as individuals with prediabetes also exhibit a higher risk of developing vascular disease [3, 4].

The macrovascular complications of diabetes arise from a complex interplay of hyperglycaemia, insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia, hypertension, thrombogenicity, and chronic systemic inflammation [5]. Chronic low‐grade inflammation is now recognized as a central mechanism linking adiposity to insulin resistance and the development of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) through multiple metabolic and immune pathways [6, 7]. Once hyperglycaemia is established, this inflammatory milieu contributes to endothelial dysfunction, accelerates atherogenesis, and increases vulnerability to cardiovascular events [8, 9]. Circulating inflammatory markers, including CRP and WBC, as well as emerging composite indices, have gained attention as potential tools for CVD risk stratification [10, 11, 12]. However, whether their prognostic discrimination generalises across metabolic states and between sexes remains uncertain.

Clinical studies have shown that inflammatory indices are associated with glycaemic regulation, with higher neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR) levels correlating positively with HbA1c in T2DM [13]. At the outcome level, large prospective studies have consistently linked inflammatory indices to cardiovascular outcomes. In a cohort of 334 674 adults followed for 10.4 years, higher levels of NLR, MLR (monocyte‐to‐lymphocyte ratio), SII (systemic immune‐inflammation index), AISI (aggregate index of systemic inflammation), and systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) were independently associated with cardiovascular mortality, with multivariable‐adjusted hazard ratios ranging from 1.36 to 1.57 [14]. Similarly, a UK Biobank study of 423 701 participants reported clear dose–response associations between NLR, SII, and SIRI and incident cardiovascular disease [15].

Despite growing evidence linking inflammatory markers to cardiovascular outcomes, it remains unclear whether the discriminative performance of systemic inflammation is consistent across different glycaemic states, particularly in the context of sex‐specific divergence. Given the well‐established sex differences in immune function [16], inflammatory responses [17], and adiposity distribution [18], clarifying these patterns may refine risk stratification and prognostic discrimination across the glycaemic spectrum.

Using data from the UK Biobank, we analysed eight inflammatory markers in parallel to examine sex‐specific associations between systemic inflammation and all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality across glycaemic states.

Methods

Subjects and Study Design

The UK Biobank is a large population‐based prospective cohort with over 500 000 participants aged 37–73 years recruited between 2006 and 2010 across England, Scotland, and Wales [19]. Participants provided information through touchscreen questionnaires, verbal interviews, and physical measurements. Blood, urine, and saliva samples were collected for biochemical analyses. Since 2014, detailed imaging data have also been collected in selected subcohorts [20]. The UK Biobank received ethical approval from the North West Multicentre Research Ethics Committee, Manchester, U.K. (REC reference for UK Biobank 11/NW/0382), and all participants provided written informed consent.

In the present study, we excluded participants with missing data on the definition of glycaemic status (n = 8140), those lost to follow‐up (n = 1254), and individuals with missing data for any of the eight inflammatory markers (n = 42 156). Finally, a total of 450 438 participants were included in the study, further stratified by sex and glycaemic status (see Figure S1). To address potential selection bias due to missing inflammatory marker data, baseline characteristics were compared between participants with complete and incomplete measurements (Table S1).

Definitions of Exposures

Participants were classified into normal glycaemia, prediabetes, and diabetes, according to established criteria, based on HbA1c levels, self‐reported diabetes diagnosis, and use of glucose‐lowering medication [21, 22]. For participants with diabetes, the duration of diabetes was calculated as the difference between the age at recruitment and the self‐reported age at diagnosis.

Eight inflammatory markers were included: CRP, WBC, NLR, CLR, IBI, SII, PIV, and SIRI. CRP and WBC were directly measured at baseline, and composite indices were calculated using standard formulas [10, 15]. All exposures were measured at baseline and modelled as time‐fixed variables throughout follow‐up. Detailed definitions of exposures are presented in Text S.

Covariates and Cardiometabolic Phenotypes

Information on sociodemographic factors (age, sex, ethnicity, employment status, education and the Townsend deprivation index), lifestyle habits (smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity), medication use (antihypertensive, lipid‐lowering, and antiplatelet drugs), and medical history of cardiovascular disease were obtained from the baseline touchscreen questionnaire and/or verbal interview. Hypertension was defined as a self‐reported diagnosis and/or current use of antihypertensive drugs.

Cardiometabolic phenotypes, including laboratory parameters, anthropometric measures (BMI, waist circumference, hip circumference), blood pressure, pulse rate, pulse wave arterial stiffness index (PASI), carotid intima‐media thickness (CIMT), and cardiac MRI were assessed at baseline or at the first available imaging visit. Variables extracted from the UK Biobank are listed in Table S2. Additional details of variable measurements can be found in the UK Biobank online protocol (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk).

Definitions of Outcome Variables

The outcomes of this study were all‐cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality. The index date was defined as the date of the participant's first attendance at a UK Biobank assessment centre. Mortality data were obtained from the National Health Service (NHS) Digital for participants in England and Wales, and from the NHS Central Register for participants in Scotland. Follow‐up time was calculated from the baseline assessment date to the date of death or censoring (July 8, 2024), whichever occurred first. Cardiovascular mortality was defined as death with a primary cause coded as I00–I99 according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) [23].

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.0). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using skewness and kurtosis measures and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (if normally distributed) or median (interquartile range) otherwise. Group comparisons across glycaemic strata were conducted using one‐way ANOVA (normal distribution), the Kruskal–Wallis test (skewed distribution), or the chi‐square test (categorical variables). Poisson regression was employed to calculate incidence rates of all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality (per 10 000 person‐years) stratified by sex and glycaemic status. Kaplan–Meier curves were utilized to visualize cumulative mortality, and differences between groups were compared using the log‐rank test.

Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess sex‐specific associations between inflammatory markers and mortality, with proportional hazards assumptions evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. Time‐split analyses at 5, 10, and 15 years were conducted using a counting‐process formulation. Restricted cubic spline Cox models were used to assess dose–response associations, with three knots in primary analyses and four knots in sensitivity analyses. Sex‐specific differences were evaluated by testing sex × marker interaction terms using likelihood ratio tests. p values for sex interactions were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR), applied separately within each glycaemic stratum.

Time‐dependent ROC analyses were conducted at 5, 10, and 15 years to assess discriminative performance, with optimism‐corrected AUCs estimated using 1000 bootstrap resamples. Spearman correlation analysis was applied to evaluate associations between inflammatory markers and cardiometabolic phenotypes. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity Analyses

To assess robustness and minimize potential reverse causation, we conducted sensitivity analyses excluding participants with baseline immunological or inflammatory disorders, haematological diseases, or use of systemic glucocorticoids, as well as those with recent infection or major surgery within 6 months prior to baseline. In addition, deaths occurring within the first 2 years of follow‐up were excluded. For mortality outcomes, cardiovascular deaths were further classified into ischaemic heart disease (I20–I25), stroke (I60–I64), and heart failure (I50) based on ICD‐10 codes recorded as the primary cause of death.

In addition, incident cardiovascular events were analysed after excluding participants with pre‐existing cardiovascular disease at baseline and early events after enrolment. Incident cardiovascular disease was defined as a composite outcome corresponding to the first occurrence of any of the following events: ischaemic heart disease, stroke, or heart failure, with cause‐specific analyses further performed for each component outcome. Participants were followed from the baseline assessment date until the first occurrence of an incident cardiovascular event, death, loss to follow‐up, or August 1, 2024, whichever came first. Detailed definitions and exclusion criteria are provided in Text S1, and the participant selection process is illustrated in Figure S2.

Results

Sex‐Specific Risks of Mortality Across Glycaemic Strata

As shown in Figure 1, during a median follow‐up of 15.3 years, cumulative all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality increased stepwise across glycaemic strata in both sexes, with generally steeper gradients observed in men. Poisson regression analyses confirmed the stepwise increase in mortality rates across glycaemic strata (Table S3).

Sex‐specific cumulative mortality for all‐cause (A) and cardiovascular (B) mortality across glycaemic strata. Differences between glycaemic status groups were assessed using the log‐rank test, stratified by sex (p < 0.001). DM, diabetes; NG, normal glycaemia; PreDM, prediabetes.

Sex‐specific relative risks were further quantified using Cox proportional hazards models (Table S4). For all‐cause mortality, no significant sex interaction was observed across glycaemic categories in either crude or adjusted models. In contrast, for cardiovascular mortality, significant sex interactions were observed in crude models, with women exhibiting a steeper relative increase in risk in both prediabetes and diabetes compared with men. These sex‐specific differences were substantially attenuated after adjustment for sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors.

Baseline Characteristics of Participants Stratified by Sex and Glycaemic Status

As shown in Table 1, participant characteristics varied significantly across glycaemic status groups. In both men and women, a worsening glycaemic profile was associated with higher age, lower socioeconomic status (reflected in education, employment, and Townsend deprivation index). Ethnically, the proportion of White participants decreased, while that of other ethnic groups increased as glycaemic status worsened. Regarding lifestyle factors, individuals with diabetes exhibited significantly higher rates of current alcohol use and smoking, coupled with reduced physical activity, relative to their normoglycaemic counterparts.

The prevalence of cardiovascular conditions and prior revascularization procedures—including hypertension, angina, myocardial infarction, heart failure, stroke, transient ischaemic attack (TIA), and prior coronary revascularization—increased progressively with worsening glycaemic status. In addition, the use of medications (antihypertensive, lipid‐lowering, antiplatelet, and anticoagulant drugs) also rose markedly across glycaemic strata. Sex‐Specific Baseline Patterns of Inflammatory Markers Across Glycaemic Strata

As shown in Figure S3, although inflammatory marker levels differed significantly across glycaemic groups in both sexes, women showed greater variability across glycaemic stages for several markers, including CRP, CLR, and IBI, with higher levels observed in diabetes. In men, some markers appeared to peak at the prediabetes stage, with little further change in diabetes. Baseline sex differences within each glycaemic stratum are quantified in Table S7, showing generally small to moderate effect sizes, with larger differences for NLR, PIV, and SIRI.

Sex‐Specific Associations Between Inflammatory Markers and Mortality Across Glycaemic Strata

Figure 2 summarizes the sex‐specific associations between inflammatory markers and mortality across glycaemic strata in fully adjusted models, with hazard ratios expressed per 1‐SD increase. For all‐cause mortality, all inflammatory markers were positively associated with risk across glycaemic groups. In the NG group, significant sex interactions were observed for WBC, NLR, PIV, and SIRI, while in the prediabetes group, similar interactions were noted for NLR, PIV, and SIRI (p for interaction < 0.05). By contrast, no significant sex interactions were detected in diabetes (Figure 2A).

For cardiovascular mortality, associations tended to attenuate in women with worsening glycaemic status, whereas patterns in men were more stable. In the diabetes group, associations for CRP, CLR, and IBI were no longer statistically significant in women. Correspondingly, no evidence of sex‐specific interaction was observed in the normoglycaemia or prediabetes groups, whereas in diabetes, sex‐specific interactions were detected for CRP and IBI, reflecting differential attenuation of associations between men and women (Figure 2B). Detailed results are presented in Tables S8 and S9. Analyses using natural log‐transformed markers yielded broadly comparable patterns (Table S10). In addition, time‐split Cox models showed generally stronger associations during early follow‐up with attenuation over time, while remaining directionally consistent across glycaemic strata (Table S11).

Figure 3 illustrates sex‐specific dose–response associations between inflammatory markers and mortality across glycaemic strata. RCS models revealed generally positive associations between inflammatory markers and all‐cause mortality in both sexes, with largely monotonic patterns. Marker‐specific and sex‐dependent heterogeneity in dose–response patterns was observed. Interaction tests provided evidence of sex × marker interactions in normoglycaemia for several markers, whereas such interactions were no longer evident in diabetes (Figure 3A).

Sex‐Specific Time‐Dependent Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Analysis Across Glycaemic Strata

As shown in Figure 4, time‐dependent ROC analyses revealed clear sex‐ and stage‐specific heterogeneity in marker discrimination. Overall, AUCs declined over time, were consistently higher for cardiovascular than for all‐cause mortality, and were modestly higher in men than in women. For all‐cause mortality, IBI showed the highest discrimination in normoglycaemia, but its relative performance attenuated with worsening glycaemic status, whereas SIRI and NLR became more prominent in diabetes.

For cardiovascular mortality, sex differences were more pronounced. In men, IBI and SIRI ranked among the highest AUCs, but the relative advantage of IBI diminished with worsening glycaemic status and longer follow‐up, with SIRI showing more stable discrimination. SIRI showed increasingly stable discrimination across glycaemic stages and follow‐up periods. In women with diabetes, NLR showed relatively higher discrimination during early follow‐up (5‐year AUC, 0.685), whereas SIRI became more prominent at later time points. Detailed AUC values are presented in Table S13.

Sex‐Specific Correlations Between Inflammatory Markers and Cardiometabolic Phenotypes Across Glycaemic Strata

Sex‐stratified correlation analyses revealed distinct patterns between inflammatory markers and cardiometabolic phenotypes across the glycaemic spectrum (Figure S4). Overall, CRP, WBC, CLR, and IBI showed stronger correlations with adiposity‐ and metabolism‐related traits in women than in men, with significant sex differences in correlation strength, particularly for BMI (p < 0.05). In contrast, NLR, SII, PIV, and SIRI demonstrated weaker correlations with metabolic indicators in both sexes, without consistent sex‐specific differences across phenotypes.

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses for all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality were largely consistent with the primary analyses. After exclusions to reduce potential reverse causation, the direction of associations in Cox models, the overall shape of dose–response associations in restricted cubic spline analyses, and the time‐dependent discriminative ranking were largely preserved across sex and glycaemic status (Figures S5 and S7).

Cause‐specific analyses of cardiovascular mortality for ischaemic heart disease, stroke, and heart failure were assessed at 10 and 15 years owing to sparse early events. Across these analyses, SIRI showed consistently stable discriminative performance with worsening glycaemic status. Estimates for heart failure were less precise due to limited event numbers (Figures S8 and S9).

In contrast, associations for incident cardiovascular events were generally weaker and more variable than those for mortality, with WBC, IBI, and SIRI often ranking toward the upper range across follow‐up horizons. Notably, SIRI showed relatively stronger discrimination for stroke and heart failure, while NLR demonstrated comparatively stronger discrimination for incident stroke in women during early follow‐up (Figures S10 and S11). Collectively, these analyses support the robustness of the primary mortality findings while indicating greater heterogeneity for incident cardiovascular events.

Discussion

Our large‐scale prospective analysis demonstrates marked sex‐ and glycaemia‐dependent heterogeneity in the prognostic relevance of systemic inflammation. With worsening glycaemic status, associations and time‐dependent discrimination for mortality showed a tendency to attenuate in women, while remaining more stable in men. This pattern may reflect sex‐specific metabolic‐inflammatory relationships across the glycaemic spectrum. Marker ranking also varied across glycaemic stages and follow‐up horizons: NLR contributed more to early discrimination, particularly in women, whereas SIRI showed more stable performance over longer follow‐up, especially in men. Together, these findings highlight the importance of interpreting inflammatory biomarkers in a sex‐ and glycaemic status‐specific context when comparing markers in prognostic research.

In our study, women showed a greater increase in cardiovascular mortality risk than men during the transition from normoglycaemia to diabetes in crude models, consistent with prior evidence of a steeper rise in myocardial infarction and stroke mortality among women progressing to diabetes [24]. However, this sex difference was no longer statistically significant after comprehensive adjustment for sociodemographic, lifestyle, metabolic, and medication factors, suggesting that differences in cumulative metabolic risk burden may partly contribute to this pattern rather than reflecting a direct sex‐specific effect. Prior studies have shown that women may experience a more adverse metabolic profile before diabetes onset, including greater increases in BMI, blood pressure, fasting glucose, and lipid levels [25, 26]. This may relate to sex differences in adipose tissue distribution and its links to insulin resistance and T2D risk [27]. For men, their excessive intra‐abdominal or visceral adipose tissue may substantially increase the risk of developing insulin resistance and T2D, whereas a higher relative distribution of subcutaneous adipose tissue in women has been proposed to be relatively less adverse [28, 29].

Our results indicated that inflammatory markers increased more steeply in women than in men as glycaemic status deteriorated, broadly paralleling sex‐specific metabolic changes. This observation may partly reflect differences in adiposity‐related inflammatory regulation. As an active endocrine organ, adipose tissue is closely associated with systemic inflammation through the secretion of cytokines and adipokines linked to insulin resistance [30]. In addition, sex‐related differences in inflammatory trajectories may partly relate to previously reported anti‐inflammatory effects of oestrogen, which have been suggested to attenuate inflammatory burden in women, particularly at earlier metabolic stages [31]. These effects may become less pronounced with aging, menopause, and metabolic deterioration, potentially contributing to the steeper increase in inflammation observed at later stages [32].

We identified distinct inflammatory‐cardiometabolic correlation patterns between men and women. Interestingly, all metabolic‐sensitive inflammatory markers (CRP, WBC, CLR, IBI) tended to exhibit stronger correlations with metabolic parameters in women than in men, with CRP showing the most notable association. This is consistent with previous reports suggesting a sex‐specific relationship between CRP and adiposity [33, 34]. For example, one study reported that the correlation between BMI and ln(CRP) was 0.24 (95% CI: 0.09–0.37) higher in women than in men, and 0.15 (95% CI: 0.00–0.28) higher in North Americans/Europeans than in Asians [35]. Of note, no clear sex difference was observed for WHR in our study. This observation may be related to previous reports indicating that CRP concentrations in women tend to be more strongly associated with subcutaneous adiposity than with WHR [36]. Among these markers, WBC reflects overall leukocyte counts rather than specific inflammatory cell populations and may therefore be less informative with respect to underlying cellular mechanisms than cell‐ratio indices.

In contrast, inflammatory markers that are less associated with metabolic burden, including NLR, SII, PIV, and SIRI, demonstrated more stable prognostic discrimination for mortality, even among individuals with diabetes. Although IBI showed robust discrimination in normoglycaemia, its relative performance attenuated with worsening glycaemic status, alongside a gradual predominance of SIRI and NLR with progression to prediabetes and diabetes. This observation is consistent with prior evidence indicating that SIRI, but not CRP, is associated with cardiovascular risk in individuals with diabetes [37]. For NLR, previous studies also have shown that it served as a valuable marker for risk stratification in individuals with diabetes [38]. For example, elevated NLR has been independently associated with increased all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality in this population, with each one‐unit increase corresponding to a 13% and 14% higher risk, respectively [11].

Notably, the discriminative performance of NLR for mortality was relatively higher in women, particularly during early follow‐up. This observation is consistent with previous reports of sex‐specific differences in neutrophil‐related inflammatory profiles [39]. For instance, an analysis of 478 479 participants reported that inflammatory signatures related to neutrophil activity accounted for a greater proportion of the association between obesity and incident heart failure in women than in men (47% vs. 33%) [40]. In contrast, SIRI, which incorporates monocyte counts, demonstrated superior discriminative value in men, suggesting sex‐specific variation in the relative contribution of inflammatory cell components to risk prediction.

From a clinical perspective, the primary relevance of the present findings lies in comparative prognostic assessment rather than population screening or direct clinical decision‐making. Inflammatory biomarkers such as NLR and SIRI may offer complementary prognostic information. However, external validation and formal evaluation of incremental discrimination or reclassification beyond established risk models were not performed, and therefore clinical utility for individual‐level risk stratification remains unproven.

The strengths of this study include the large, prospective UK Biobank cohort, comprehensive assessment of clinically relevant inflammatory biomarkers, and detailed stratification by sex and glycaemic status. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the generalisability of our findings may be limited, as UK Biobank participants are predominantly of White British ancestry. Second, glycaemic status, inflammatory markers, and covariates were assessed at baseline only and treated as time‐fixed variables, which may preclude evaluation of longitudinal changes and introduce potential residual confounding by sex‐specific factors. Third, diabetes was analysed as a single category, precluding differentiation between diabetes subtypes (type 1, type 2, and other forms), treatment intensity, and longitudinal glycaemic HbA1c trajectories. In addition, analyses of cardiometabolic and imaging phenotypes were conducted in selected subcohorts, which may introduce selection bias. Misclassification related to self‐reported variables and cause‐of‐death coding cannot be excluded. Finally, the observed discrepancies between incident cardiovascular events and mortality‐based findings suggest that disease onset and fatal progression may involve distinct inflammatory pathways. Future studies incorporating longitudinal measurements and causal inference frameworks are warranted to clarify sex‐specific mechanisms underlying these differences.

Conclusion

In conclusion, inflammatory biomarkers exhibit marked sex‐ and glycaemia‐dependent heterogeneity in their associations with mortality, independent of conventional metabolic and cardiovascular risk factors. Markers closely linked to metabolic burden attenuate with worsening glycaemia, particularly in women, whereas NLR and SIRI demonstrate more stable patterns in diabetes. These findings emphasize biological and prognostic heterogeneity across sex and glycaemic states, which may inform future risk assessment research.
