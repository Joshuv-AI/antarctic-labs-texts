Treatment preferences in Waldenström macroglobulinemia: an international discrete choice experiment in 1455 patients
Authors: Anne-Marie L Becking, Pythia T Nieuwkerk, Michelle Postek, Karima Amaador, Sophie J Bernelot Moens, Linda Bronsgeest, Shirley D’Sa, Pierre Faubert, Carl Harrington, Paul Kitchen, David MacDonald, Monique C Minnema, Alison McKinney, M Lia Palomba, Ibrahim Tohidi-Esfahani, Judith Trotman, Andrew Warden, Marie José Kersten, Josephine M I Vos, WM-VOICE consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•In a DCE including 1455 patients with WM from 5 countries, 6 treatment properties influenced patient preferences.•Longer response durations and avoidance of severe toxicity were prioritized over oral or fixed-duration regimens, guiding clinical practice.

Introduction

Waldenström macroglobulinemia (WM) is a rare and indolent type of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, characterized by an immunoglobulin M monoclonal gammopathy and a lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma in the bone marrow.1,2 With an age-adjusted incidence rate of 2 to 7 per million person-years in Europe and the United States, WM accounts for 2% of all hematologic malignancies.3, 4, 5 WM has a median age of onset of ∼70 years, with a male predominance.6,7 Given the lack of curative treatment options, active monitoring is the preferred approach for patients with asymptomatic WM; however, most patients with WM will require treatment at some point.8 The median overall survival can exceed 10 years, depending on patient age.9,10 The chronic nature of WM and recurrent need for therapy contribute to a substantial and ongoing disease burden, significantly affecting quality of life.11

Treatment options with clinical benefit for WM have expanded rapidly over the course of time and continue to expand.8,12, 13, 14, 15 However, there is limited consensus on an optimal therapeutic approach. The most commonly applied regimens at frontline include chemoimmunotherapy combinations, such as dexamethasone-cyclophosphamide-rituximab or bendamustine-rituximab.8 Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors, introduced into the therapeutic landscape one decade ago, provide an alternative to initial immunochemotherapy.16, 17, 18 Other agents are available for relapsed or refractory disease, including proteasome inhibitors such as bortezomib-based combinations19 and the BCL2 inhibitor, venetoclax.20 In addition, a wide variety of agents are under investigation for WM, including T-cell–engaging therapies such as bispecific antibodies,21 BTK degraders,22 next-generation BTK and BCL2 inhibitors,23,24 and novel combination therapies.25 All these available and upcoming treatment options have different profiles with respect to their characteristics, including efficacy, mode of administration, treatment duration, and toxicity.

Within this context of rapid development and limited follow-up, understanding what patients value most when considering treatment options is essential. Improved insight into patient preferences can guide shared decision-making and individual management, promoting treatment adherence.26 Moreover, it could facilitate therapeutic innovation by setting priorities in clinical trial design and guide drug candidate selection by pharmaceutical companies and regulatory agencies.27 The latter are increasingly mandating the incorporation of patient perspectives to inform benefit-risk assessments.28,29

The model of choice for eliciting health care preferences in cases of multiple eligible treatment options is a discrete choice experiment (DCE).30, 31, 32 A DCE is typically implemented as a choice-based survey, using hypothetical choices that incorporate various properties. In realistic choice scenarios, treatment options are described by their most important characteristics (“attributes”). Each attribute is assigned levels, representing different possible values of that attribute. The scenarios require respondents to choose between 2 options by weighing their characteristics. DCEs enable quantification of the utility of treatment attributes and the trade-offs patients are willing to make. Given the growing array of available therapies for hematologic malignancies, DCEs are increasingly used within hematology research.33

To date, structured data on treatment preferences in WM are limited to 1 small Dutch DCE including 214 patients.34 Although this study was the first to provide valuable insights into the preferences of patients with WM, larger, robust international data on this topic are lacking. The WM-VOICE study was designed to quantify the preferences of patients with WM for treatment properties on an international scale and identify possible preference variation across subgroups and countries, while accounting for potential future treatment options rather than only existing options.

Methods

This study used a qualitative interview-based approach, followed by a quantitative method (DCE survey), to gain insight into treatment preferences of patients with WM. It was performed in Australia, Canada, The Netherlands, the United Kingdom, and the United States. These countries were selected based on the presence of well-established and active WM organizations, which was essential to facilitate nationwide recruitment and ensure representation beyond academic centers. For the development, application, and analysis of the DCE survey, the recommendations of the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research Good Research Practices for Conjoint Analysis were followed.35, 36, 37

Attribute and level selection

Candidate attributes for the DCE were selected through discussions with expert hematologists, followed by one-on-one interviews with patients with WM (n = 4-7 per country). Patient representatives were recruited through patient organizations. To ensure a diverse interviewee population, selective sampling was used, encompassing a wide range of sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. During the interviews, patients were asked to list treatment properties of interest to them, enabling identification of a comprehensive range of properties that influence patient choices. These interviews also contained a ranking exercise to determine prioritization. In addition, patients were asked which specific individual (demographic and clinical) characteristics could influence their preferences. Patient interviews were conducted until attribute saturation was reached.

The country-specific interview results were assessed by the coordinating researchers to select treatment properties to include as attributes in the DCE. The most important properties for patients from all countries were agent specific (treatment duration, mode of administration, and site of administration), efficacy specific (response duration or time to next treatment), side effects (particularly fatigue, nausea and vomiting, infection risk and neutropenia, and irreversible neuropathy), and risk of secondary malignancies. Out-of-pocket payment for health care costs was considered relevant particularly by patients from Australia, Canada, and the United States, but less so in the United Kingdom and The Netherlands. However, they were not incorporated in this DCE because of concerns about their potential to reduce DCE credibility and the complex, multifactorial nature of this attribute.38

The significant overlap in interview results across the 5 countries allowed for the creation of a single DCE that could be used for all countries, enabling comparison of results across countries. Final selection followed good research practice guidelines,37 with a maximum of 6 attributes, a maximum of 4 levels per attribute, and 1 attribute representing treatment efficacy. In addition, because this study intended to account for hypothetical future treatment options, the choice options and results needed to be generalizable to these options rather than only to existing modalities. The attribute levels were chosen based on available literature. The final list of attributes and levels is depicted in Table 1. It included response duration (2, 4, 6, or 8 years), administration mode (in-hospital infusions or oral medication at home), treatment duration (6-month fixed duration, 2-year fixed duration, or ongoing therapy), temporary toxicities (grade 0-1, 2, or 3-4), persistent toxicities (grade 0-1 or 2), and risk of secondary malignancies (increased or not increased). These 6 attributes and their corresponding levels formed the basis of the final DCE design.

Survey design and pilot testing

The DCE was set up through a D-efficient main-effects experimental design using the “dcreate” macro in Stata.36,39 A subset of 16 choice sets was generated to present to participants to ensure feasibility. This subset entailed 16 combinations of 2 hypothetical unlabeled treatment options, with an optimal balance of attributes and levels and minimal correlation. Each set asked respondents to choose between these 2 options by weighing the 6 key attributes. To assess the comprehensibility of the choice tasks, a pilot study using a “think out loud” method was performed in a small group of patient representatives from each country. Based on this, the DCE was adapted and finalized. An example of the final choice sets is shown in Figure 1.

Example of 1 of 16 choice sets included in the final DCE.

The complete survey started with an explanation of the purpose of data collection and user instructions, followed by verification questions on self-reported eligibility and completion of baseline characteristics. These included interview-derived variables that could potentially influence participant preferences, comprising sociodemographics, disease and treatment history, preferred involvement in medical decision-making, employment, education, living situation, financial challenges, and ethnicity. The latter was collected considering previously reported ethnic disparities among patients with WM, including disease presentation, enrollment in clinical trials, and outcomes.40, 41, 42 The baseline questions were followed by an exercise choice set for internal validity, that is, a dominance test, and then the actual DCE with its 16 experimental choice sets. The survey was drafted in 3 languages; in Dutch for patients from The Netherlands, in French for Francophone Canadian patients, and in English for all other patients.

Recruitment and data collection

Data were collected through an online survey using an electronic Case Report Form in Castor, available through an open weblink. Recruitment was promoted via patient organizations and WM physicians’ clinics, distributing the hyperlink through websites, newsletters, patient forums, and posters. Patients participated in the survey independently, without further engagement by physicians or patient federations. Inclusion criteria were a self-reported diagnosis of WM, residency in Australia, Canada, The Netherlands, the United Kingdom, or the United States, and having read and understood the survey instructions and privacy policy. Participation was fully anonymous and collected data were nontraceable. Hence, obtainment of explicit informed consent was not required. The survey was preceded by a statement that, by filling out the survey, participants gave implicit permission to use their anonymous data for research purposes. The Medical Ethics Review Committee of Amsterdam University Centers granted a consent waiver (2023.0867), confirming the study was not subject to the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO). The study was carried out in accordance with all locally applicable data protection legislation.

The necessary sample size was calculated according to the following rule of thumb: N = 500c/(t × a), in which “c” is equal to the largest product of levels of any 2 attributes, “t” represents the number of choice tasks, and “a” is the number of alternatives.43 With 1 4-level attribute and 3-level attributes, 2 alternatives, and 16 choice tasks, the required respondent number for the DCE was 188, that is, 500 × 12/(16 × 2). To enable country-specific analyses, this target sample size of 188 was set per country. Although the target sample size referred exclusively to fully completed DCEs, incomplete responses were also included in the analysis, provided that at least 1 DCE choice set had been completed.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata (version 17). DCE responses were analyzed with a panel mixed logit model, accounting for the repeated choice data per respondent. The binary treatment choice (A or B) was the dependent variable, and all attributes were included as categorical independent variables. Main effects models were used without interaction terms. Coefficients were transformed using the “margins” command in Stata to reflect the preference weights (average marginal effects), indicating the change in probability of choosing a treatment option, given a change in an attribute level. Statistical significance of attribute levels was assessed by comparing coefficients with the reference level, using dummy coding. For figures displaying average marginal effects, effect coding was used in which the mean effects of the attributes were normalized at 0. Preference weights were further used to estimate the conditional relative importance (CRI), reflecting the maximum change in utility achievable with any attribute, conditional on the levels of the attributes selected in the study design. In a separate model, efficacy was included as a continuous variable to calculate willingness to trade, indicating the number of years of response duration patients require in exchange for a 1-unit change in other attribute levels. Willingness to trade was calculated by dividing the level coefficients of the various levels by the marginal utility of a 1-year increase in response duration, assuming linear coding across the 2-, 4-, 6-, and 8-year levels. Confidence intervals (CI) for willingness to trade were calculated using a macro in Stata.39 To identify potential patient characteristics significantly associated with treatment preference, additional analyses were performed incorporating patient characteristics as covariates in the model. Two-sided P values of <.05 were considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Respondents and baseline characteristics

The survey was open from October 2024 to February 2025, during which sample size targets were met for every country. A total of 1691 respondents met the self-reported inclusion criteria and started the survey (Figure 2), and 1629 completed all baseline questions. In total, 1455 respondents filled out at least 1 DCE choice set and were included in the DCE analysis. Of these participants, 127 (9%) ended the DCE prematurely, whereas 1328 (91%) completed the full DCE. The participants who completed the survey spent a median of 23 minutes (interquartile range [IQR], 17-32) to finish it.

Flowchart of participant inclusion for the WM-VOICE study.

Demographic and clinical characteristics, as well as DCE answers, were analyzed for the 1455 DCE participants. Of those, 209 resided in Australia, 219 in Canada, 340 in The Netherlands, 214 in the United Kingdom, and 473 in the United States. Baseline characteristics are depicted in Table 2. Of all participants, 818 (56%) were male and most were aged 61 to 70 years (n = 483 [33%]) or 71 to 80 years (n = 607 [42%]). Highest level of education was at least a bachelor’s degree in higher professional education or university for 940 (65%) respondents, and 295 (20%) participants were employed at the time of the survey. Median time since WM diagnosis was 6 years (IQR, 3-10), and 1131 (78%) had received treatment before completing the survey. Median number of WM treatment lines was 1 (IQR, 1-2), and 306 (21%) had received ≥3 treatment lines. Overall, 857 (59%) participants had received chemotherapy, 858 (59%) immunotherapy including rituximab, 460 (32%) BTK inhibitors, 317 (22%) corticosteroids, 71 (5%) proteasome inhibitors, and 35 (2%) BCL2 inhibitors. A total of 470 (32%) participants were on treatment at the time of completing the survey. Additional baseline characteristics and stratification by country are presented in supplemental Table 1. There was wide geographic representation within each country but limited ethnic diversity (supplemental Table 2).

Preference weights for treatment attributes

In the DCE, all attribute levels had a significant impact on participants’ treatment choices. Figure 3A shows the mean preference weight estimates (average marginal effects) of all 1455 DCE participants for each attribute level. With 1 exception, all levels within the attributes were statistically different from 0 (P < .05), indicating that respondents took the levels into account in their decision-making. They were more likely to choose treatment options with a longer response duration. If response duration increased from 2 years to 4, 6, or 8 years, the probability of choosing a treatment option increased by 13% (95% CI, 13-14), 19% (95% CI, 18-20), and 23% (95% CI, 23-24), respectively. Patients showed a preference for oral medication at home over hospital infusions (increasing probability by 4% [95% CI, 3-4]), and therapies with a fixed duration of 6 months over fixed duration of 2 years (increasing probability by 3% [95% CI, 2-4]) or ongoing therapy (increasing probability by 4% [95% CI, 3-4]). Treatment options with moderate and severe temporary toxicities had a 6% (95% CI, 5-7) and 21% (95% CI, 20-21) lower probability, respectively, of being selected than those with no or mild temporary toxicities. The probability of choosing a treatment option decreased by 14% (95% CI, 13-14) if persistent toxicity grade changed from no or mild to moderate. When risk of secondary malignancies was increased, the probability of choosing a treatment option decreased by 14% (95% CI, 13-15). Preferences were strongly consistent across all 5 countries (Figure 3B).

Average marginal effects (preference weights) for each attribute level. All 1455 DCE respondents (A) and by country (B). UK, United Kingdom; USA, United States.

The CRIs for each attribute are depicted in Figure 4. Participants placed the greatest importance on the increase from a 2-year to an 8-year response duration (CRI, 29.7% [95% CI, 28.8-30.6]) and the change from severe to no or mild temporary toxicities (CRI, 25.6% [95% CI, 24.8-26.5]). This was followed by a change from increased to not-increased risk of secondary malignancies (CRI, 17.7% [95% CI, 16.8-18.6]) and moderate to no or mild persistent toxicities (CRI, 17.4% [95% CI, 16.5-18.2]). The lowest utility was assigned to oral administration instead of IV administration (CRI, 4.9% [95% CI, 4.0-5.8]) and 6-month fixed duration instead of ongoing therapy (CRI, 4.6% [95% CI, 3.8-5.4]). For instance, this indicates that avoiding severe temporary toxicities is almost equally important to participants as going from a 2-year to an 8-year response duration and 5 times more important than oral instead of IV mode of administration or fixed duration instead of ongoing therapy.

CRI for each of the attributes in the 1455 DCE respondents.

Willingness to trade efficacy

Figure 5 shows the willingness to trade efficacy within the context of this DCE. It reflects a specific worsening in an attribute level and the corresponding increase in years of response duration necessary for patients to accept that change as a trade-off. Most notably, participants would require an additional 5.6 years (95% CI, 5.3-5.9) of response duration to accept severe instead of no or mild temporary side effects and 3.8 years (95% CI, 3.6-4.0) for severe instead of moderate temporary side effects. In addition, they would require a substantial increase in response duration in exchange for accepting moderate, rather than no or mild, persistent side effects (3.8 years; 95% CI, 3.6-4.1) and to accept an increased risk of malignancy (3.7 years; 95% CI, 3.4-4.0). Conversely, they were willing to accept intravenous over oral therapy for a relatively low yield in response duration (1.0 year; 95% CI, 0.8-1.2) and ongoing therapy over 6-month fixed-duration therapy (1.0 year; 95% CI, 0.8-1.2) or >2 years of fixed-duration therapy (0.2 year; 95% CI, 0.0-0.4).

Willingness to trade response duration for changes in other attribute levels in the 1455 DCE participants.

Impact of demographic and clinical characteristics

Respondents who had received BTK inhibitor treatment differed significantly in their preferences compared with those who had not (P = .03). The estimated marginal effects of attribute levels suggested this was mainly due to a higher preference for oral over IV therapy (supplemental Table 3). The following characteristics did not have a statistically significant impact on treatment choices: age (≤70 vs ≥71 years and ≤60 vs ≥61 years), sex, treatment status (previous treatment vs treatment-naïve, 0-1 vs ≥2 treatment lines, and currently on treatment vs not on treatment), previous chemotherapy, previous proteasome inhibitors, or travel time to the hospital (<1 hour vs >1 hour). Models including educational level, responses to the exercise DCE, difficulty in paying out-of-pocket health care costs, and employment status as covariates did not converge.

Discussion

The WM-VOICE study is the first international analysis to examine treatment preferences of patients with WM. In light of the rarity of WM, this DCE included a substantial cohort of 1455 patients across 5 countries. Six important treatment attributes were identified, each significantly influencing treatment choices: response duration, administration mode, treatment duration, short- and long-term toxicities, and risk of secondary malignancies. Of all the attributes and levels in this DCE, a longer response duration was considered most important to patients, followed by the reduction in severity of temporary toxicities. Avoidance of moderate persistent side effects and secondary malignancies were subsequently prioritized, whereas mode of administration and treatment duration were significantly less influential. Patients were willing to tolerate intravenous over oral therapy and a long treatment duration in exchange for a relatively small increase in response duration but expected a substantial increase in response duration to accept increased toxicity. Preferences were highly consistent across patients from Australia, Canada, The Netherlands, the United Kingdom, and the United States, and only marginally affected by individual patient characteristics.

Although the high utility assigned to ongoing efficacy is in line with expectations, the relatively low importance of administration mode and treatment duration in this study are noteworthy. Given the differences in study designs and attribute selection, caution should be exercised when directly comparing DCEs with each other. However, these results correspond with the findings of the preceding Dutch DCE, in which selection of long-term oral therapy instead of fixed-duration hospital infusions was considered less important than an increase from 50% to 60% 5-year progression-free survival.34 DCEs in other chronic hematologic malignancies, including chronic lymphocytic leukemia and follicular lymphoma, have also shown administration mode and treatment duration to have low impact on choices compared with efficacy- and toxicity-related properties.44, 45, 46 This finding is particularly relevant in light of the ongoing efforts to establish fixed-duration treatment schedules for WM.47,48 However, as differences in efficacy and toxicity between treatment regimens decline, it can be expected that treatment characteristics such as treatment-free intervals and mode of administration will become increasingly important to patients. Moreover, review studies have demonstrated a strong heterogeneity in treatment preferences across studies and patient populations, emphasizing the importance of disease- and subgroup-tailored research as well as treatment individualization.33 Interestingly, participants appeared to prioritize the avoidance of short-term toxicities compared with long-term toxicities. This pattern may be explained by the difference in the levels between these 2 attributes: the short-term toxicity attribute included a level with severe toxicity, whereas the persistent toxicity attribute did not, because such side effects are not realistic in current WM treatments. Including this level could have affected the observed preference patterns differently. This preference may also reflect a common cognitive bias (hyperbolic discounting) in which respondents give more weight to immediate negative experiences compared with delayed ones.49 In addition, this DCE did not differentiate between specific types of toxicities, which may have influenced patient preferences, as observed in the previous Dutch DCE.34

The results of this study should be considered within the context of methodological limitations intrinsic to this type of research. First, respondents were mainly recruited through patient organizations, which may have resulted in the overrepresentation of patients with high educational levels and presumed greater health literacy as well as patients with Internet access and specific ethnic backgrounds. The latter may be partly attributable to the higher incidence of WM among people of White ethnicity relative to other ethnic groups.41,42,50 Moreover, it is important to interpret DCE findings in light of their design, including the selected attributes and levels, despite careful selection based on patient and expert interviews. The pronounced willingness to trade response duration for lower toxicity encourages the pursuit of such well-tolerated treatment options. However, the specific trade-off value (5.6 years) should not be literally translated to a clinically applicable prescriptive threshold. Furthermore, in clinical practice, individual patient needs and response to previous treatment lines must always be considered when making therapeutic decisions. Although the influence of demographic characteristics on choices was small in this study, it was not powered to estimate interaction effects, that is, interactions between covariates and specific attributes. Finally, contextual constraints such as cost, treatment availability, socioeconomic factors, and health care infrastructure, which might influence individual therapeutic decisions and limit generalizability beyond the high-income 5 countries studied, must always be considered.

Several methodological strengths underpin this study. The investigator-initiated, patient organization–driven design of this study constitutes a significant asset, underscoring its independent nature. Second, the meticulous selection of attributes and levels was informed by comprehensive patient interviews and iterative pilot testing, enhancing content validity. The chosen attributes represent core treatment dimensions, ensuring that the findings remain applicable not only to current treatment options but also to potential future WM drugs or novel combinations. Although agents with severe chronic toxicities could theoretically be considered in clinical trials, their introduction into routine clinical practice appears improbable within the context of the current therapeutic landscape. Hence, these were not included as a level in the current DCE. Third, the sample size is substantial for this rare disease, increasing the statistical reliability and robustness of the results. Finally, patients providing feedback in the survey comments frequently described the process as useful and thought-provoking, prompting them to systematically consider advantages and disadvantages of various treatment options. Such structured preference elicitation may have additional value by enabling the development of WM-specific decision aids to facilitate shared decision making in clinical practice.

In conclusion, our findings reveal that patients with WM place the highest value on longer response duration and the absence of severe acute toxicities in treatment options. Preferences also reflect a strong desire to avoid long-term toxicities and secondary malignancies, whereas administration route and a treatment-free interval were markedly less influential. While the current spectrum of WM treatments is continually evolving, these results can inform shared decision-making in clinical practice. In addition, these data provide a structured reflection of the patient’s voice, important to guide drug development and clinical trial design in WM as well as inform regulatory agencies in cost-effectiveness analyses and decisions regarding approval and reimbursement.
