Epicardial left lateral accessory pathways: an uncommon cause of ablation failure—insights from a case series
Authors: Dimitrios Charitos, Vasileios Sousonis, Dimitrios Asvestas, Emmanouil Vavouris, Stavros Karanikas, Emmanouil P Vardas, Stylianos Tzeis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCatheter ablation is the treatment of choice for patients with recurrent, symptomatic accessory pathway (AP)-related tachycardias and for asymptomatic patients with high-risk pathways. Catheter ablation is associated with high success rates and low risk of complications, particularly when the pathway is located at the mitral annulus. Epicardial left lateral APs are rare but can account for ablation failures, posing a significant therapeutic challenge.Case summaryWe report two young patients with recurrent paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardias related to a left lateral AP. In both cases, endocardial ablation targeting the lateral mitral annulus failed. Subsequent mapping of the coronary sinus and its branches revealed earliest activation sites consistent with an epicardial course of the APs. Subsequent deployment of radiofrequency energy lesions within the coronary venous system using an irrigated-tip catheter resulted in immediate and durable AP elimination.DiscussionEpicardial left lateral APs are an uncommon but important cause of failed ablation. Ablation within the coronary venous system, particularly when guided by three-dimensional electroanatomic mapping and performed with irrigated-tip catheters, can be both effective and safe. However, meticulous mapping and targeted energy delivery are necessary to prevent potential complications, such as venous stenosis, thrombosis vascular injury, and perforation.

Introduction

Left free-wall accessory pathways (APs) are the most frequent form of abnormal atrioventricular connections, accounting for 50%–60% of all APs.1 Catheter ablation is established as first-line therapy for patients with recurrent, symptomatic AP-related tachycardias and for asymptomatic individuals with high-risk pathways, with reported acute success rates above 95%.2,3 In most cases, ablation of left lateral APs is straightforward and can be achieved by either a transseptal or retrograde aortic approach. However, a minority of patients present with epicardial APs, which account for a substantial number of ablation failures. Epicardial APs are most often associated with myocardial extensions into the coronary sinus and its tributaries, particularly the middle cardiac vein, the posterior coronary vein, or the ostium of a coronary sinus diverticulum, and are estimated to underlie up to 10% of ablation failures.4 Less commonly, epicardial connections can be found along the left free wall, with epicardial APs comprising ∼4% of all left free-wall APs.4 These pathways present distinct diagnostic and ablation challenges, as they frequently require mapping and ablation within the coronary venous system. Therefore, their recognition is crucial for achieving durable ablation success. We report a series of patients with epicardial left lateral APs, focusing on the electrophysiological features, ablation strategies, and outcomes.

Case presentation

Patient 1

A 33-year-old woman with electrocardiographic evidence of pre-excitation and episodes of paroxysmal tachycardia was referred to our hospital for electrophysiology (EP) study. The patient had no significant prior medical history, and transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated no structural heart disease. During programmed atrial stimulation resulting in maximal pre-excitation, the 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated a right bundle branch block-type morphology (R/S > 1 in Lead V1), indicating a left-sided AP, with positive pre-excited QRS complex polarity in inferior leads (II, III, aVF) and negative pre-excited QRS polarity in lateral leads (I, aVL), consistent with a left anterolateral AP.5 Following isoproterenol infusion, the anterograde effective refractory period of the AP was 350 ms. Programmed ventricular stimulation demonstrated eccentric activation of the coronary sinus (CS) with a retrograde AP effective refractory period of 330 ms and reproducible induction of a narrow-complex tachycardia with a cycle length of 340 ms, eccentric CS activation, and a V-A-V response with a short return cycle following ventricular overdrive pacing. Thus, the diagnosis of atrioventricular re-entrant tachycardia mediated by a left lateral AP was established, and catheter ablation was planned.

Subsequently, activation mapping of the mitral annulus was performed during rapid atrial pacing using a multipolar mapping catheter (PentaRay, Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA) and a three-dimensional electroanatomic mapping system (CARTO, Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA). The earliest ventricular activation was identified at the lateral mitral annulus (Figure 1A). A 30 W RF lesion was delivered with an irrigated-tip ablation catheter (ThermoCool SmartTouch SF, Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA), resulting in immediate disappearance of pre-excitation, and was followed by additional consolidation lesions. However, pre-excitation recurred shortly thereafter. Remapping of the endocardial aspect of the mitral annulus revealed a broad isochronal area of earliest ventricular activation, raising suspicion of an epicardial AP. Consequently, mapping of the great cardiac vein was performed with the ablation catheter, which identified an earliest ventricular activation site, 8 ms earlier than the previously ablated endocardial site (Figure 1B). A video file showing the combined endocardial and epicardial propagation during atrial pacing is provided in the Supplementary material (Supplementary material online, Video S1). A 25 W RF lesion delivered within the CS led to immediate elimination of pre-excitation (Figure 2), with no recurrence during a 30-min waiting period and after adenosine administration. At 6 months of follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with no recurrence of pre-excitation on ECG.

Patient 2

A 30-year-old woman with recurrent episodes of paroxysmal narrow-complex tachycardia and a history of prior ablation of a concealed left AP was referred to our hospital for EP study. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated no structural cardiac abnormalities. Programmed ventricular stimulation revealed eccentric activation of the CS, consistent with a left lateral AP. Atrial pacing reproducibly induced a narrow-complex tachycardia with a cycle length of 500 ms and eccentric CS activation pattern, showing a V-A-V response and a short return cycle after ventricular overdrive pacing. Based on these findings, access to the left atrium was obtained via a transseptal puncture for mapping and ablation of the AP. Activation mapping of the mitral annulus was performed during ventricular pacing using a three-dimensional electroanatomic mapping system (CARTO, Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA) and a multipolar mapping catheter (PentaRay, Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA). The earliest atrial activation was identified at the lateral mitral annulus. However, the atrial and ventricular signals were separated by an isoelectric interval, indicating distinct local atrial and ventricular activation. This finding argues against conduction via an AP, in which electrical continuity between the atrial and ventricular myocardium typically results in fusion of the atrial and ventricular signals (Figure 3A). Radiofrequency (RF) energy was delivered at this site with an irrigated-tip ablation catheter (ThermoCool SmartTouch SF, Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA) at a maximum power of 30 W, resulting in elimination (within 5 s) of ventriculo-atrial (VA) conduction via the AP as evidenced by a reversion from an eccentric retrograde activation pattern to VA dissociation. Due to recurrence of the retrograde conduction over the AP during the waiting period, mapping of the great cardiac vein with the ablation catheter was performed. During mapping, catheter manipulation transiently abolished VA conduction over the pathway at a site in anatomical correspondence and close vicinity to the earliest recorded endocardial activation. When retrograde conduction over the AP subsequently recovered, fusion of the atrial and ventricular electrograms was recorded at this location, reflecting the anatomical continuity between atrial and ventricular myocardium mediated by the AP (Figure 3B). Delivery of a 25 W RF lesion at this site resulted in immediate and persistent elimination of VA conduction over the AP (Figure 4), with no recurrence after a 30-min waiting period and VA dissociation documented during ventricular pacing. After 6 months, no recurrence of symptoms was reported.

Discussion

Catheter ablation of APs can be challenging, especially when the pathway has an epicardial course. Epicardial AP localization has been reported in ∼8%–10% of cases of unsuccessful ablation attempts.4,6 Epicardial APs are most often found at the ostium of a CS diverticulum, along the middle cardiac vein, or within the posterior coronary vein.7 In contrast, true epicardial APs located along the lateral mitral annulus are rare.

Early work by Haïssaguerre et al.8 demonstrated the feasibility of RF ablation of epicardial left lateral APs via the mid- or distal CS in eight patients after failed endocardial ablation. Energy delivery was effective and free of major complications, although marked, non-specific chest pain was noted and mural thrombus formation was reported in two cases. Similar efficacy was reported by Yamane et al.,9 using an irrigated-tip ablation catheter with target temperature of 50°C and a power limit of 20–30 W, without significant complications.

Langberg et al.4 studied patients with left lateral APs resistant to conventional endocardial ablation techniques and observed that the absence of an identifiable AP potential during endocardial mapping, combined with a relatively prominent potential within the CS, may serve as a useful marker of a subepicardial AP. In such cases, RF ablation from within the CS improved procedural success. More recently, Sun et al.10 reported a series of five patients with left lateral epicardial APs who underwent successful ablation through the coronary venous system after multiple unsuccessful endocardial attempts by experienced operators using both transseptal and retrograde transaortic approaches.

Taken together, these studies demonstrate that RF ablation within the coronary venous system is both effective and generally safe for permanent elimination of epicardial APs. Our cases reinforce this evidence. Moreover, the use of a three-dimensional electroanatomic mapping system facilitated the precise localization of the epicardial AP, while the irrigated-tip ablation catheter facilitated efficient lesion formation, allowing shorter RF applications and potentially minimizing the risk of complications. Consistent with our practice, a maximal power of 20–30 W for a duration of 60 s is generally recommended for irrigated-tip catheters within the CS.9

Nevertheless, a residual risk of complications remains. Potential adverse events of ablation within the coronary venous system include luminal stenosis, thrombosis, pericarditis, perforation leading to cardiac tamponade, and vascular injury to adjacent structures, such as the left circumflex artery. In a recent multicentre study including 211 patients undergoing ablation within the CS, acute coronary artery injury was observed in ∼0.5% of cases, presenting as transient spasm of the left circumflex artery following RF delivery, without permanent coronary sequelae.11 Vascular injury can be prevented by performing a coronary angiogram before ablation within the coronary venous system to assess the proximity of the ablation site to the coronary arteries or by considering cryoablation when the target site lies close to a coronary artery.12 In a series of patients undergoing ablation of epicardial posteroseptal APs via the coronary venous system, coronary artery injury occurred in ∼50% of cases when RF was delivered within 2 mm of a coronary artery, 7% when delivered at a distance of 3–5 mm, and 0% when the distance exceeded 5 mm.13 These findings underscore the critical importance of the spatial relationship between the ablation site and adjacent coronary arteries. In addition, CS venography can facilitate safer navigation and optimal catheter positioning within the CS and its branches. Ablation within the CS should, therefore, be undertaken with caution. However, when successful, it obviates the need for a subxiphoid percutaneous epicardial approach, which carries a substantially higher risk of serious complications.

Conclusion

Failed endocardial ablation of a left lateral AP should raise the suspicion of an epicardial pathway. In such cases, RF ablation within the coronary venous system constitutes an effective and safe alternative to percutaneous or surgical ablation.
