Outcome and factors affecting prognosis in treat and extend treatment for neovascular age-related macular degeneration-a real world study
Authors: Fatma Sema Akkan Aydogmus, Tugba Gursoy, Ozdemir Ozdemir, Ozgul Ucar Elalmis, Defne Kalayci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo evaluate the outcome of treat and extend (T&E) for neovascular age related macular degeneration (nAMD) at 12, 18 and 24 months and the impact of clinical characteristics on treatment success defined as extension of treatment interval to ≥12 weeks and functional improvement or stability.MethodsVisual and anatomical outcomes evaluated by optical coherence tomography (OCT) and extension of treatment interval to ≥12 weeks were analyzed retrospectively. Clinical factors effective on extension of the treatment interval and visual outcome were evaluated.ResultsThere were 138 eyes with at least 18 months of follow-up. Patients exhibited a significant improvement in visual acuity and reduction in central subfield thickness (CST), subretinal fluid (SRF), intraretinal fluid (IRF) and pigment epithelial detachment (PED) at both the 12th and 18th months. There was recurrence of IRF and a reduction in gain of visual acuity at 24 months. In 15.2%, 36.2% and 40.6% of eyes, the treatment interval could be extended to ≥12-weeks at 12, 18 and 24 months. Presence of a large PED was found as the only independent variable effecting extension of treatment interval to 12 weeks at 18 months. Baseline BCVA of >20/100 and no IRF at baseline were identified as independent predictive factors for better visual acuity at 18 months.ConclusionPresence of IRF and a visual acuity of ≤20/100 at baseline were found to be negative predictors for visual prognosis, while large PED was a negative predictive factor for less frequent treatment, but it did not affect visual outcome. Treatment interval should be extended cautiously in patients with baseline intraretinal fluid and visual acuity of ≤20/100 in order to balance decreasing the burden of treatment by longer treatment intervals with improvement of visual gain.

Background

Neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) is responsible for nearly 90% of vision loss associated with age-related macular degeneration (AMD) due to complications related to neovascularization and is considered one of the leading causes of blindness in the population over 60 years of age in developed countries [1]. The vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) family are key factors responsible for angiogenesis and vascular permeability. With the understanding of VEGF’s role in nAMD, intravitreal anti-VEGF has become the primary therapeutic agent for the treatment of nAMD. Currently, fixed-dose and treat-and-extend (T&E) protocols are the preferred intravitreal anti-VEGF treatment options. Although anti-VEGF agents represent a revolution in the treatment of nAMD by not only reducing vision loss but also providing vision gain, the frequent need for injections is a significant burden on both patients and the healthcare system. Today, the aim is to minimize the treatment burden by decreasing the number of visits, and complications related to treatment while maximizing visual gain. Therefore, it is important to identify the factors affecting disease prognosis in order to optimize treatment.

Advanced age, family history, smoking, female gender, hyperlipidemia, genetic polymorphism of complement factor H, and hypertension are some of the identified risk factors. Additionally, some studies have emphasized the prognostic significance of certain anatomical biomarkers [2–4].

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the outcome of T&E treatment at 12, 18 and 24 months, as well as the impact of clinical characteristics including a non-selective beta–blocker agent on treatment success.

Methods

This study was conducted at the ophthalmology clinic of a tertiary healthcare institution, and ethical approval was obtained for the study. The research was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and does not involve any conflicts of interest.

The study comprised of a retrospective case series of patients whose eyes were diagnosed with nAMD between 2017 and 2024 and treated with T&E protocol by the authors. Cases that began treatment with T&E and were followed for at least 18 months were included, as well as those that initially received a pro-re-nata (PRN) treatment and were subsequently transitioned to the T&E regimen, also with a minimum follow-up of 18 months with the T&E treatment protocol (Fig. 1). Cases with a best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of less than 20/400 after three consecutive anti-VEGF injections or those who did not attend regular follow-ups were excluded from the study. Furthermore, patients with additional retinal diseases such as diabetic retinopathy, myopic degeneration, retinal vein occlusion, uveitis, and neovascular glaucoma, as well as those who had undergone vitreoretinal surgery or cataract surgery within the last six months, were not included in the study.Fig. 1Distribution of eyes treated with a treat-and-extend (T&E) regimen according to prior treatment status. Among the included eyes, 97 (70.3%) were treatment-naive, whereas 41 (29.7%) had previously received pro re nata (PRN) treatment

Distribution of eyes treated with a treat-and-extend (T&E) regimen according to prior treatment status. Among the included eyes, 97 (70.3%) were treatment-naive, whereas 41 (29.7%) had previously received pro re nata (PRN) treatment

Some of the patients initially started treatment with a three-dose loading with bevacizumab due to the social security institution’s imposition, before treatment was started with aflibercept or ranibizumab.

T&E protocol was employed as the following: after 3 monthly loading doses, the treatment interval was extended by 1 or 2 weeks if no fluid was observed on optical coherence tomography (OCT) at the next visit the following month or was shortened by 1 or 2 weeks if fluid was observed or there was hemorrhage on fundus examination. The interval could be shortened more than 2 weeks if there were signs of a significant recurrence at the discretion of the evaluating physician. If subretinal fluid was refractory and BCVA remained stable, treatment was continued at the same interval.

Baseline, 12-month, 18-month, and 24-month BCVA values, central subfield thickness (CST) automatically measured from spectral domain OCT macula images (Cirrus [Carl Zeiss Meditec, Inc, Dublin, CA, USA]), presence of subretinal fluid (SRF), intraretinal fluid (IRF), pigment epithelium detachment (PED), and maximum height of subfoveal PED were determined from medical records. PED was defined as a separation of at least 50 µm between the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and the inner layer of Bruch’s membrane [5]. A PED height greater than 250 µm was considered a large PED [6]. The presence of pachychoroid features were assessed. Choroidal thickness (CT) and the presence of subfoveal pachyvessels were recorded using enhanced depth imaging mode of OCT (EDI-OCT), covering a 6 × 6 mm area centered on the fovea with 25-line scans). Pachychoroid features were considered to be present if all the following criteria were met: (1) Subfoveal choroidal thickness ≥ 200 μm in both eyes; (2) no evidence of drusen in both eyes (presence of hard drusen [<63 μm] or either pachydrusen was allowed;

(3) attenuation/thinning of the choriocapillaris and Sattler vessels overlying dilated outer choroidal vessels (pachyvessels) below the macular neovascularisation (MNV) [7].

For statistical analysis, Snellen BCVA values were converted to the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (log-MAR). For nAMD patients with hypertension who were on medication, their medications were switched to carvedilol, a non-selective beta-adrenergic receptor blocker if approved by the cardiology department [8–10]. The use of carvedilol was examined as a factor that could affect treatment success.

To analyze treatment outcomes, mean BCVA, presence of IRF, SRF, and PED, the proportion of patients with BCVA of ≥20/50 and those with BCVA of ≤20/100 at baseline, 12, 18, and 24 months were evaluated. The proportion of eyes in which the treatment interval could be extended to 12 weeks and clinical characteristics that influence the extension of treatment interval were identified.

Statistical analysis: The 29.0 version of SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, Chicago, IL, USA) was used for statistical analysis of the data. Descriptive statistics, including mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range for continuous variables, and frequency and percentage for categorical variables, were calculated. Shapiro-Wilk tests were used to assess normal distribution in the initial analyses. For univariate analyses, Mann-Whitney U test and chi-square tests were utilized for nonparametric data between two groups. Variables with p-values up to 0.25 were included in multiple logistic regression analysis to identify independent variables affecting the outcome using the forward selection technique. Results were evaluated with a confidence interval of 95%, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 138 eyes were identified that met the inclusion criteria and had a minimum follow-up of 18 months. Mean age was 69.3 ± 8.4 years, 72 (52.2%) were women. The mean follow-up duration was 3.02 ± 1.58 years (range, 1.14–7.04 years).

Ninety-seven eyes (70.3%) who received T&E treatment were naive, while 41 eyes (29.7%) underwent T&E treatment following PRN (Fig. 1). The mean PRN duration was 2.51 ± 1.85 years (range, 0.38–9.21 years). One hundred and four eyes (75.4%) of the 138 cases received aflibercept, 18 (13.0%) received ranibizumab, and 16 eyes (11.6%) were treated with multiple agents (Fig. 2). Among the 138 eyes that were followed for 18 months, treatment interval could be extended to at least 12 weeks in 21 eyes (15.2%) at the 12-month follow-up, and by the 18-month follow-up this number increased to 50 eyes (36.2%). Mean duration for which the treatment interval was extended to 12 weeks was 19.31 ± 13.01 months (range: 5.3–66.3 months). The median number of intravitreal injections were 8 (range: 4–12) and 10 (range: 6–16) at 12 and 18 months.

Patients exhibited a significant improvement in visual acuity at both the 12th and 18th months, with significant reductions in CST, presence of IRF, SRF and PED. Of the 107 patients that were followed for 24 months the treatment interval was extended to at least 12 weeks in 40.6% of eyes. Median number of intravitreal injections at 24 months was 13 (range: 7–22). Compared to baseline, there was an increase in visual acuity at the 24-month visit which was not significant. CST and the rates of PED and SRF decreased significantly. However, the rate of IRF presence at 24 months did not differ significantly from baseline. Mean BCVA was 0.69 ± 0.53 logMAR at baseline and 0.61 ± 0.42 logMAR at 24 months (p = 0.05). The CST values were 318.915 ± 111.52 µm (range: 138–827) at baseline and 245.01 ± 74.34 µm (range: 115–634) at 24 months (p < 0.001). The rates of IRF, SRF and PED were 45.1% and 34.9% (p = 0.133), 79.4% and 33.0% (p < 0.001); and 69.2% and 55.7% (p = 0.042) at baseline and 24 months respectively (Table 2). Factors influencing the rate of BCVA of ≥20/50 at 18 months were evaluated. Having a baseline BCVA of >20/100 significantly increased the likelihood of achieving a BCVA of ≥20/50 at the 18-month visit (p < 0.001). Conversely, the presence of baseline IRF was associated with a decreased likelihood of achieving a VA of ≥20/50 at the end of 18 months (p = 0.041). The relationship between the 18-month BCVA and prognostic factors is presented in Table 4. These variables that showed significant relationships with univariate analysis were included in multivariate analysis. Results of multivariate analysis revealed that eyes with a baseline BCVA of >20/100 had four times the odds of having BCVA of ≥20/50 at the 18-month follow-up (p < 0.001, OR = 4.095, 95% CI: 1.88–8.920). Eyes with baseline IRF had a 54% less likelihood of achieving BCVA of ≥20/50 at 18 months (p = 0.05, OR = 0.468, 95% CI: 0.216–1.016). Discussion

Distinguishing the sub-group of patients who need less frequent therapy from others and tailoring the treatment according to each patient is a substantial advantage of T&E treatment. Therefore, it is important to find out clinical characteristics which may affect patients’ response to treatment and predict the prognosis in order to foresee the required treatment interval in advance and plan the treatment better to optimize the outcome. Predicting the prognosis of each patient may also be useful to select between new treatment options at present or when new treatment possibilities are available in the future.

In 36.2% and 40.6% of eyes, the treatment interval could be extended to 12 - week intervals at 18 and 24 months of follow up. This finding is in accord with the findings of a meta-analysis in which 12 weeks extension was reported in 37.7%. and 42.6.7% at 12 and 24 months [11]. However, in a more recent study, under T&E treatment with aflibercept, extension of treatment to 12 weeks was reported as high as 67% at 24 months [12]. The difference of defining treatment extension to 12 weeks is thought to be the main reason for this seemingly lower proportion found in our study; as we defined “extension to 12 weeks” if the treatment was extended to and could be maintained at 12 weeks at the consecutive visit.

When factors predicted to affect the treatment response were analyzed in univariate analysis; large PED, pachychoroid features, and baseline SRF were found to be negative predictive factors for extending the treatment interval to 12 weeks. This data was adjusted through multivariate analysis and it was demonstrated that only the presence of large PED reduced the likelihood of extending the treatment interval to 12 weeks within 18 months by 75%, independent of other factors. Previous studies have shown that the presence of PED increases the frequency of injection [13–15]. Different from previous studies we evaluated the effect of PED taking into account the size and large PED was found to be a negative predictive factor which adds to the clinical knowledge.

There was significant improvement in visual acuity at the 12th and 18th months, as well as a decrease in CST, presence of intraretinal fluid and PED on OCT. This outcome obviously demonstrates the long-term efficacy of T&E treatment in real world consistent with the existing studies [16]. Improvement of BCVA was most significant at 12 months and this gain gradually decreased to barely no significant gain at the 24th month, which implies BCVA gain does not sustain and is replaced with BCVA preservation at 2 years. Since 30% of our cohort were not naïve T&E cases, it may be thought BCVA gain would have been better if all patients had begun treatment with T&E, although this assumption was not proved through univariate analysis. The relatively long duration of PRN treatment might have led to chronic changes and the fact that confounding factors were not addressed through univariate analysis, it may be thought that starting out treatment with T&E better anatomic and visual outcomes may be expected in the real world. There was also an increased rate of presence of IRF at the 24th month, which may explain the drop of BCVA gain at the same time point. Similar to this finding in our study, in a study of metanalysis, it was found that the proportion of eyes with disease stability regressed in the second year but the proportion of eyes with extension to ≥12- week interval increased. This discrepancy was explained as the implication of the difficulty in balancing reduced treatment burden by extending the interval and under-treatment, which may also explain the contradiction of recurrence of intraretinal fluid and a drop in gain of BCVA although the proportion of eyes with a treatment extension of 12 weeks was increased at the 24th month visit in our study [11].

Multivariate analysis revealed that eyes with baseline good visual acuity had a fourfold greater likelihood of achieving a visual acuity of ≥20/50 at the 18th month. In contrast, eyes with baseline IRF exhibited a 54% reduction in the probability of attaining a visual acuity of ≥20/50 by the 18th month.

Previous studies have also shown that baseline VA predicted final visual outcomes but was not associated with injection frequency as in our study [16–19]. We also observed that the presence of large PED, which was an effective factor for increasing injection frequency; was not an effective factor in decreased visual outcome, which was also reported previously [15, 20, 21].

Propranolol, a beta-adrenergic receptor antagonist like carvedilol, has been shown to be effective in the treatment of infantile hemangioma through VEGF and MMP inhibition [22, 23]. We used carvedilol which is a non-selective beta-adrenergic receptor antagonist and an antihypertensive agent and has similar efficacy to propranolol in some of the hypertensive patients through consultation with the cardiology department. We found that use of carvedilol did not influence extending the treatment interval, nor the visual acuity outcomes at the 18th and 24th months. The ineffectiveness observed may be due to the relatively small number of patients using carvedilol in our study.

We determined the group with pachychoroid features and examined the effect of these features on extending the treatment interval and achieving visual acuity ≥ 20/50 and above. We found that pachychoroid features did not effect treatment interval extension to 12 weeks and maintaining VA values of ≥20/50 at the end of the 18th and 24th months.

There have been some studies evaluating prognostic factors in patients treated with anti-VEGF in nAMD [2–4]. However, most of these studies did not aim to specifically evaluate morphological predictors of injection frequency and instead focused on final visual outcomes. There are few studies examining prognostic factors and factors effective on extending the treatment interval and visual outcomes using T&E protocol [24–26]

To our knowledge, our study is unique in examining the effects of the presence of comorbidities, and various OCT markers, on both visual prognosis and treatment interval in the T&E regimen for nAMD at real world setting.

The limitations of our study include the retrospective nature, relatively small number of patients using carvedilol and those with pachychoroid features and lack of evaluation of atrophy on OCT. The question whether presence of baseline intraretinal fluid effects visual prognosis independent of atrophy is not answered in the scope of the study. Heterogeneity of treatment, as inclusion of patients switched from PRN and a small proportion of those treated with multiple anti-VEGF agents and the possibility of inter-eye correlation due to inclusion of both eyes of 11 and 8 patients at 18 and 24 months may also be considered limitations, but on the other hand, reflects the outcome of the real world.

Our findings suggest that although large PED is a negative predictive factor for less frequent treatment, it is not a negative factor for visual prognosis. However, presence of intraretinal fluid at baseline and visual acuity of ≤20/100 are found as negative predictors for visual prognosis, therefore in these patients, to be cautious, treatment interval may be extended by 1 week instead of 2 weeks or second generation anti-VEGF agents may be considered in treatment to prevent a tradeoff between decreasing the burden of treatment and maintaining better visual outcome.
