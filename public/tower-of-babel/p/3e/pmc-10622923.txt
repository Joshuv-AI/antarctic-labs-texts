Novel insecticidal proteins from ferns resemble insecticidal proteins from Bacillus thuringiensis
Authors: Jun-Zhi Wei, Amy Lum, Eric Schepers, Lu Liu, Ross T. Weston, Bruce S. McGinness, Matthew J. Heckert, Weiping Xie, Adane Kassa, Denny Bruck, Gilda Rauscher, Deirdre Kapka-Kitzman, John P. Mathis, Jian-Zhou Zhao, Amit Sethi, Jennifer Barry, Albert L. Lu, Filippa Brugliera, Eunice L. Lee, Nicole L. van derWeerden, Narayanan Eswar, Megan J. Maher, Marilyn A. Anderson
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe expression of transgenes encoding insecticidal proteins from Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) is a well-established technology that enhances protection against lepidopteran larvae, which affect crop production worldwide. However, resistance is emerging, creating an urgent need for new agents. We have discovered a new protein family from ferns (Pteris species), which has strong activity against pests of maize and soybean. The crystal structure of a family variant reveals that despite being derived from plants, these proteins resemble two of the three domains from the structures of Bt bacterial insecticidal proteins. The proteins from ferns control insects resistant to Bt insecticidal proteins, indicating different modes and/or sites of action and therefore provide a potential new avenue for the control of insect pests.

Insect pests are major biotic factors affecting crop production globally (1). Some of the most destructive insects are lepidopteran species, such as corn earworm (Helicoverpa zea), European corn borer (Ostrinia nubilalis), and armyworms (Spodoptera). Their larvae feed on various plant tissues, causing significant crop losses in yield and quality. Insecticidal proteins from bacterial species, particularly insecticidal crystal (Cry) and vegetative insecticidal (Vip) proteins from Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt), have been used extensively in transgenic maize, soybean, and cotton to control these devastating pests. Cry1Ab, Cry1F, Cry1A.105, Cry2Ab2, and Vip3A have been used individually or combined in “pyramids” to control crop pests for the last two decades (2). Field-evolved practical resistance that reduces the efficacy of Bt crops has been documented in at least 26 cases involving 11 pest species in seven countries (3–5). There is increasing concern that the current commercial transgenic crops for insect control face significant challenges for their sustainability in the future (6–10).

Various mechanisms of Bt resistance have been reported (11) and many strategies have been contemplated to modify existing Cry or Vip proteins by domain swapping, protein engineering, and molecular evolution to enhance activity or to overcome insect resistance to these proteins (12–14). The discovery of insecticidal proteins with new modes of action (MOA) or sites of action (SOA) is another approach to overcome resistance. Recently, two novel insecticidal proteins, Tma12 and IPD083, have been isolated from fern species supporting earlier observations that ferns are inherently resistant to insect infestation (15–17). Tma12 is a chitin-binding protein from the family of AA10 monooxygenases that are normally bacterial enzymes. However, genomic analyses revealed that Tma12 is encoded by a plant gene. Sequence similarities with the bacterial proteins led to the hypothesis that the ferns acquired the gene by horizontal gene transfer from a bacterial species (17). The function of IPD083 in its native plant is currently unknown and its DNA sequence is not related to known bacterial proteins (16).

In this study, we screened a broader range of fern species and have discovered a novel family of insecticidal proteins IPD113, that have strong activity against several lepidopteran pests in artificial diet–based assays and efficacy in transgenic plants. IPD113s also show strong activity against Cry1Fa- and Cry2A-resistant insect colonies. We solved the crystal structure of IPD113_Cow, a member of this protein family, and discovered that it has a similar structure to Bt insecticidal proteins. Our genomic analyses revealed that the IPD113 proteins are encoded by plant and not bacterial genes.

Results and Discussion

Extracts from Pteris Ferns Have Insecticidal Activity against Some Lepidopterans.

The clarified and desalted leaf tissue extracts from Pteris cretica cv. albolineata (Ptc) and Pteris umbrosa (Ptu) stunted the growth of soybean looper (SBL, Chrysodeixis includens) and corn earworm (CEW, Helicoverpa zea) when added to artificial diets and tested in insect-feeding bioassays. The insecticidal activity was heat and protease sensitive, indicating that the active molecules were likely to be proteins. After threefold concentration, the extracts were more active against SBL and CEW but had a weak or no effect on European corn borer (ECB, Ostrinia nubilalis), fall armyworm (FAW, Spodoptera frugiperda), and western corn rootworm (WCR, Diabrotica virgifera virgifera) (SI Appendix, Table S1).

IPD113_Ptc from Pteris spp., Which Is Like Bt Insecticidal Proteins, Is Responsible for the Insecticidal Activity.

Due to the limited amount of material, leaf samples from Ptc and Ptu were combined prior to further purification using anion exchange chromatography. Unbound, wash, and elution fractions were tested in an insect diet–feeding bioassay. Proteins in the active fractions were concentrated 10-fold and separated by lauryl dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (LDS-PAGE, SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Individual protein bands were excised, digested in-gel with trypsin and analyzed by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). The resulting peptides were searched against an annotated Corteva Agriscience transcriptome database containing a combination of transcripts from Ptc and Ptu in addition to publicly available protein sequences.

The detected peptides mapped to sequences of catalases, a lipoxygenase, and a dehydrogenase as well as three novel proteins. Peptides from one of these novel proteins were of particular interest because they mapped to a sequence with similarity to the N-terminal domains of Bt insecticidal proteins (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). The complete sequences encoding the three proteins were obtained from the corresponding transcripts from fern Ptc and were cloned individually into an Agrobacterium-based expression vector under the control of a viral promoter (18) for transient expression in bush bean (Phaseolus vulgaris) leaves. Transformed leaf disks were used in feeding assays against SBL, ECB, FAW, CEW, and velvet bean caterpillar (VBC, Anticarsia gemmatalis). Leaf disk damage was scored 3 d after infestation by estimating the percentage of leaf tissue consumed. The protein with sequence similarity to the Bt insecticidal proteins, designated as Pct-1, produced strong protection (less than 5% consumed) against SBL, ECB, and VBC neonates, and weaker protection (40 to 60% consumed) against CEW and FAW neonates (SI Appendix, Table S2). No significant leaf protection was observed in leaves that were infiltrated with Agrobacterium producing the other two novel proteins (Pct-2 and Pct-3) or with Agrobacterium carrying an empty vector or nontransformed leaves (60-100% consumption by all insects) (SI Appendix, Table S2). Thus, Pct-1 was renamed as IPD113_Ptc, an insecticidal protein with efficacy against five important pest species from three lepidopteran families.

The predicted amino acid sequence of IPD113_Ptc (GenBank accession number OK148517) contains 526 residues with a mass of 59.7 kDa (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Domain analysis using Pfam indicated that IPD113_Ptc contains regions homologous to the lectin domain superfamily (IPR036404, SSF51101, https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro) and to the Bt insecticidal protein, N-terminal domain superfamily (PF03945, https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/entry/pfam/PF03945). This led us to ask whether the IPD113 family is encoded by plant genes or genes from a bacterial endophyte because proteins with these domains have only been described for bacteria. We addressed this by sequencing a genomic PCR product encoding IPD113_Ptc from P. cretica and discovered six introns confirming that the IPD113 genes are plant and not bacterial genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S2, GenBank accession number OL348212). This observation, together with the low amino acid sequence identity (11 to 13%) between the bacterial and novel fern proteins described here, suggests that the similarity between the bacterial and fern proteins may have arisen by convergent evolution rather than common ancestry or horizontal gene transfer. This contrasts with what has been proposed for the fern insecticidal protein Tma12 and homologues as well as squalene-hopene cyclase, a protein from cyanobacteria that contributes to membrane stability and confers low-temperature adaptation and stress tolerance (12).

Confirmation of Activity and Spectrum of Homologs.

Homologs of IPD113_Ptc with a range of sequence identities (40 to 96%) were selected from 22 different fern species spread across nine families, three orders, and two subclasses (SI Appendix, Table S3). The wide taxonomic distribution of the IPD113 genes across two of the four subclasses of the Polypodiopsida (ferns) suggests that they originated from an ancestral gene early in fern evolution. The homologs were expressed transiently in bush bean leaves and tested for insecticidal activity. Proteins in one branch of the maximum likelihood tree had the strongest and broadest spectrum of activity against insects (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Three of these proteins, IPD113_Cow from Colysis wrightii, IPD113_Tem from Tectaria milnei, and IPD113_Opp from Ophioglossum pendulum, with sequence identities between 91 and 95%, were recombinantly expressed in Escherichia coli to examine their potency against CEW, SBL, VBC, FAW, ECB, and sugarcane borer (SCB, Diatraea saccharalis) in diet-feeding assays. They all showed strong insecticidal activity against SBL, VBC, and SCB with low values for LC50 (ranging from 1.32 to 16.0 µg/mL) and IC50 (ranging from 0.23 to 9.41 µg/mL) (SI Appendix, Table S4). Furthermore, the responses of VBC, SCB, and ECB to IPD113_Tem and IPD113_Opp were similar. IPD113_Opp was ~eightfold more potent than IPD113_Tem on CEW and ~twofold more active than IPD113_Tem against SBL and FAW. IPD113_Cow had moderate activity against CEW with an LC50 of 97.9 µg/mL (95% CL: 46 to 150) and an IC50 of 6.45 µg/mL (95% CL: 5.1 to 7.9) and weak activity against FAW (SI Appendix, Table S4).

Transgenic Maize and Soybean Plants Producing IPD113 Proteins Show Strong Protection against Lepidopterans.

Constructs producing IPD113_Opp or IPD113_Tem under control of constitutive promoters were generated and transformed into maize plants with Agrobacterium. Production of IPD113_Opp and IPD113_Tem in leaf tissue ranged from 1,670 to 5,050 ppm (total soluble protein) by mass spectrometry analysis. Leaf disks were excised from the transgenic plants and tested for susceptibility to damage by ECB, CEW, and FAW. Transgenic maize plants producing IPD113_Opp and IPD113_Tem were strongly protected against CEW and ECB with leaf damage below 20% and were moderately protected against FAW with leaf damage below 30% (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, maize plants producing IPD113_Opp and IPD113_Tem were protected against ear and stalk damage by CEW and ECB, respectively (Fig. 1 B–E).

FAW with Resistance to Cry1Fa or Cry2A Were Not Cross-Resistant to IPD113 Proteins.

IPD113_Opp and IPD113_Tem were tested on a laboratory susceptible (SS) FAW colony and two Cry protein resistant FAW colonies, Cry1F-Res and Cry2A-Res. The resistance ratio (RR) for Cry1F-Res (a Brazilian field-resistant colony) was 5,830 to Cry1Fa, and the RR for Cry2A-Res colony was 840 to Cry2A.127 (SI Appendix, Table S5). The RRs were less than 2 for both resistant FAW colonies to IPD113_Opp and IPD113_Tem in a diet overlay bioassay (SI Appendix, Table S5). These results indicate that no significant cross-resistance occurred between the IPD113s tested and Cry1Fa or Cry2A.127, which suggests that IPD113s have a different mode of action than these Cry proteins.

The Crystal Structure of IPD113_Cow Resembles Two Domains of the 3-domain Cry Proteins.

The recombinant IPD113_Cow was crystallized and the structure determined to 1.98 Å resolution by X-ray crystallography (SI Appendix, Table S6). The structure shows four monomers of IPD113_Cow per asymmetric unit, arranged as two antiparallel (head-to-tail) dimers (Fig. 3). The four molecules in the asymmetric unit superpose with a rmsd for 475 common Cα positions of between 0.3 and 0.5 Å, indicating the structures of the four monomers are very similar.

(B) Structure of an IPD113_Cow monomer, colored from blue at the N terminus to red at the C terminus. (C) The same structure, but colored according to the domain assignments given in panel A. (D) The PirB protein from Vibrio parahaemolyticus (PDB 3X0U) (19); and (E) Cry1A.105 protein from Bt (PDB 6DJ4) (20). In E, domain III, which is absent in the IPD113_Cow structure, is colored cyan. (F) The IPD113_Cow dimer, with one monomer colored according to panel C and the other in gray.

Each monomer is composed of four regions: an N-terminal region (NTR, residues 26 to 61), composed of two α-helices; domain I, a globular nine helical bundle (residues 62 to 239, 287 to 324); a “linker domain” (residues 240 to 286), composed of a three-stranded antiparallel β-sheet; and domain II (residues 325 to 509), consisting of three antiparallel β-sheets, resembling the β-prism fold of lectins (Fig. 3).

An unbiased search of the Protein Data Bank (PDB) with the coordinates of an entire IPD113_Cow monomer using PDBeFold (21, 22) revealed four unique structures with Z scores (a measure of the statistical significance of the match) ≥ 3.0 that are bacterial (not plant) proteins: the PirB (Pir, Photorhabdus insect related) protein from Vibrio parahaemolyticus (PDB 3X0U), which is toxic to shrimp (19), the Cry4Aa protein from Bt (PDB 2C9K) that kills mosquito larvae (23), the Lepidoptera-specific Cry1Ac from Bt ssp. kurstaki HD-73 (PDB 4ARX), and the Cry1A.105 protein (PDB 6DJ4) (20). These structural homologues show remarkably low sequence identities (8 to 12%) to IPD113_Cow (SI Appendix, Table S7). In distinct contrast to IPD113_Cow, 3-domain Cry protein structures generally include domains I, II, and III (Fig. 3). The PirB protein includes domains I and II only and is part of a heterodimeric protein (PirAB) (19). It is postulated that the PirAB complex assumes the classic 3-domain structure, with domains I and II from PirB subunit (Fig. 3) and the PirA protomer taking the place of domain III (19).

The mechanisms by which Cry proteins induce cell death have been well studied (20, 24). The proteins undergo a cascade of processes that include receptor binding, oligomerization, and pore formation. In the Bt-derived Cry1A.105 protein (20), domain III is a jelly-roll β-sandwich with a putative galactose-binding module, likely involved in the recognition of the aminopeptidase N (APN) and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) receptors. Its interaction with the APN receptor facilitates binding of domain II to another region on the same receptor, which promotes localization and concentration of the activated proteins. The APN-bound protein then binds to cadherin (CAD), which facilitates further events leading to membrane permeation (20, 24). More recently, it has been established that 3-domain Cry proteins also interact with ABC transporters on the cell-surface (24, 25), this interaction being necessary for high toxicity. The apical loops of Cry1Aa domain II are important for binding to these cell-surface receptors (26).

Collectively then, domains II and III in 3-domain Cry proteins are the primary participants in receptor recognition and the key elements determining insecticidal specificity and activity (27, 28). The absence of domain III in IPD113_Cow suggests that this protein engages with receptors through domain II alone or a combination of domains I and II. Alternatively, the linker domain of IPD113_Cow (Fig. 3), with no significant structural similarity to domains in other insecticidal or pore-forming proteins, may provide a novel mechanism for receptor recognition. Certainly, the lack of cross-resistance observed for IPD113, Cry1Fa, and Cry2A in FAW supports a novel mechanism of action for the IPD113 family.

In the 3-domain Cry proteins, domain I is responsible for directing the disruptive processes after receptor recognition (29–32). In the most accepted model, cleavage of helix α-1 exposes buried hydrophobic regions of domain I and triggers the formation of a protein prepore oligomeric structure. The hydrophobic hairpin, formed by helices α-4 and α-5 then inserts into the phospholipid bilayer of the membrane, with the remaining amphipathic helices of domain I spread on the membrane surface in an umbrella-like conformation (29–32). An analysis of the IPD113_Cow domain I structure shows that the positions of helices α-1, α-4, and α-5 are consistent with the structure of the Bt-derived Cry1A.105 (20). The loops between helical pairs α-1/α-2 and α-5/α-6 show higher temperature factors, relative to the rest of the structure, indicating greater flexibility (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). IPD113_Cow may undergo a similar conformational change to facilitate membrane insertion.

The IPD113_Cow protein assembles into head-to-tail homodimers in the crystal (Fig. 3), which buries ~3,200 Å2 (14%) of solvent accessible surface area per protomer, suggesting that IPD113_Cow forms a stable dimer in solution (33). This agrees with our size exclusion chromatography (SEC) data, which showed that IPD113_Cow eluted at a volume corresponding to the size of a dimer (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). The NTR plays a significant role in dimer formation, making a close contact with the other protomer in the dimer. The oligomerization that has been documented as part of the mechanism for Cry protein insecticidal activity requires the condensation of parallel protein assemblies, which is distinct from the head-to-tail conformation observed for the structure reported here. Therefore, the functional relevance of the IPD113_Cow dimerization observed in solution and in the crystal is currently unclear. The absence of domain III may mean that the monomeric form of the protein is unstable or insoluble. The presence of the NTR and associated dimerization may stabilize the protein in situ (in the plant) prior to ingestion by the insect larvae. Structural characterization of the full-length Cry1Ac protein (PDB 4W8J) (34) also revealed an antiparallel, dimeric protein assembly (containing seven domains per monomer) proposed to represent the state of the protein in crystals within Bt cells. It is tempting to speculate that the structure of IPD113_Cow represents a stabilized “pro-protein” structure and that ingestion, followed by possible cleavage of the NTR by trypsin and structural realignment (as detailed in ref. 35), precedes receptor binding, oligomerization, and pore formation. Interestingly, a search of the IPD113_Cow sequence for putative trypsin protease sites (using the ExPASy Peptide Cutter tool) (36) predicts possible cleavage points at residues 66 and 68, which neighbor the NTR.

Conclusion

In this study, we report the discovery of an insecticidal protein family IPD113 from Pteris plants. IPD113 homologs are present in many fern species (SI Appendix, Fig. S3) that have different efficacies on lepidopteran crop pests. Until recently, the best characterized insecticidal proteins from plant sources have been lectins, proteinase and α-amylase inhibitors, and cyclotides. These generally retard rather than prevent insect growth and do not provide the same level of protection as the bacterial insecticidal proteins in transgenic plants (37). Two other insecticidal proteins have been isolated from fern species. The first of these is Tma12 from Tectaria macrodonta plants that has insecticidal activity specific for whiteflies (38). Tma12 is a carbohydrate-binding protein from the AA10 family of monooxygenases (17). Most proteins in the AA10 monooxygenase family are from bacteria, but Tma12 is clearly a plant gene with a single intron and is flanked by plant genes. However, the similarity between the plant sequences and sequences of chitin-binding proteins from Chloroflexi led to the suggestion that the fern genes may have been acquired by horizontal transfer of bacterial proteins into ferns (17, 39). The second example is a family of insecticidal proteins (IPD083) with unknown function from Adiantum spp. and other Polypodiopsa. These proteins bind specifically to insect brush border membrane vesicles but have different target sites to the Bt proteins. Transgenic soybean and maize plants producing IPD083Aa or IPD083Cb proteins were protected from damage by several lepidopterans (16). These results support the use of ferns as an alternative source for insecticidal protein discovery.

In conclusion, we identified an insecticidal protein family from plants which is active against multiple maize and soybean lepidopteran pests. The sequence and structure differences between IPD113s and Bt insecticidal proteins indicate that IPD113s have a different mechanism of action on lepidopterans. This is supported by their activities against Cry1Fa- and Cry2A-resistant insect colonies. The IPD113 family provides a new opportunity to develop plant-incorporated protectants for the control of important crop pests.

Materials and Methods

Plant collection, transcriptome sequencing, purification and identification of the IPD113 proteins from Pteris species and crystallization, X-ray data collection, structure solution, and refinement of the recombinant IPD113_Cow variant are described in detail in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods. DNA encoding the IPD113_Cow protein was cloned into the pET16B plasmid, with a N-terminal 10× His tag and purified from E. coli strain C41. The potencies of IPD113_Cow, IPD113_Tem, and IPD113_Opp were examined using a quantitative bioassay with purified proteins using a published method (16).
