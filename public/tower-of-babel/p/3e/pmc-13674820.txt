Mobility‐enabling birth beds versus traditional gynecological tables: A quasi‐experimental study of birth experiences and clinical outcomes
Authors: Ayşe Taştekin, Özlem Durmuş Tekin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study compares birth experiences and clinical outcomes between women giving birth using mobility‐enabling labor–birth–postpartum (LBP) beds and those using a traditional gynecological table (TGT).MethodsThis quasi‐experimental study was conducted between June and October 2025 in a secondary‐level maternity unit in Turkey. A total of 320 women who had vaginal births were sequentially assigned to either the LBP group (n = 160) or the TGT group (n = 160). Continuous variables were analyzed using Mann–Whitney U‐tests, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons, and categorical variables using χ 2‐tests.ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable between groups. Regarding the primary outcome, no significant difference in labor duration was observed between the groups (P = 0.254). After multiplicity adjustment, episiotomy (45.0% vs. 67.5%) and fundal compression (6.9% vs. 25.0%) were significantly less frequent in the LBP group (P < 0.001). Women using LBP beds reported lower pain scores and higher birth satisfaction (P < 0.001). There were no statistically significant differences between the groups in neonatal variables after adjusting the significance threshold (P > 0.0029).ConclusionThese results suggest that while mobility‐enabling LBP beds do not shorten labor duration, they are associated with a substantial reduction in obstetric interventions from extremely high institutional levels and enhanced maternal birth experiences. By supporting maternal movement and positional flexibility, these beds may facilitate physiologic labor and represent a practical strategy for strengthening woman‐centered intrapartum care. However, environmental modifications should be integrated with broader institutional policy shifts to fully align with global evidence‐based standards and further reduce the need for routine interventions.

INTRODUCTION

The physical arrangement of the birthing environment, the equipment used, and the care approach offered have a decisive impact on women's birthing experience and the progression of the birthing process. In particular, birthing environments that provide privacy, freedom of movement, and individualized care have been reported to reduce unnecessary interventions and increase satisfaction. 1 Although the traditional gynecological table (TGT) has been standard equipment in medical practice for many years, it is associated with negative experiences, such as perceived loss of control over the birthing process, feeling vulnerable, violation of privacy, and feelings of physical restriction. This situation has been shown to elicit negative emotions during childbirth and to affect both labor duration and postpartum satisfaction adversely. 2 In addition, the traditional lithotomy position prevents posterior movement of the sacrum due to the body weight on the sacrum and the resistance from the bed surface, thereby narrowing the pelvic outlet. In contrast, modern birth beds leverage the pelvic bones' physiological flexibility and gravity, enabling maternal mobility and releasing the sacrum. 3

Negative perceptions and fears about the birthing room might stem from the medicalization of childbirth due to limited movement, excessive light and equipment use, uncertainty regarding the provision of privacy, and birthing positions chosen for the ease of work of healthcare professionals. 4 Therefore, individual birthing rooms, which increase women's freedom of movement, support position selection, and allow the use of the same environment throughout all stages of childbirth, have become increasingly important as an alternative approach. Although support for individualized birthing environments is increasing, few studies have evaluated differences between individual birthing rooms and traditional birthing environments. 5

The labor–birth–postpartum (LBP) room refers to a single‐person physical environment, including a bathroom and toilet, where care can be provided during the three stages of labor (labor, birth, and the first hour postpartum/neonatal period). The LBP bed can be converted into both a hospital bed and, in emergencies during delivery, a gynecological table, allowing the patient to give birth in her own bed without being transferred to a separate delivery table. The bed height, back support, and footrest are adjustable. This model supports the pregnant woman's freedom of position and allows for her active participation in the birthing process. 6

The effects of the birthing environment extend beyond physical parameters; the quality of care largely determines the level of psychosocial support and satisfaction women experience throughout the birthing process. Continuous, individualized, and supportive care has been shown to reduce the perceived severity of labor pain, decrease anxiety, and enhance the birthing experience. 7 In line with the mother‐baby friendly hospital criteria, ensuring freedom of position during labor, allowing the mother to move, and ensuring that childbirth occurs in a single‐person, comfort‐oriented environment are fundamental standards in the World Health Organization (WHO) positive childbirth experience guideline. 8

Much of the previous evidence regarding maternal mobility and birthing position has focused on the physiological effects of upright or alternating maternal positions during labor, rather than on the broader implementation of mobility‐supportive birthing environments and equipment in routine childbirth care systems. Current randomized trials and clinical guidelines have generally considered maternal positioning as an isolated intervention under controlled conditions. 1 , 3 , 8 However, in routine clinical practice, maternal mobility is also shaped by environmental design, room organization, equipment configuration, and institutional workflow. 9 Therefore, birthing beds that support positional flexibility can influence not only women's movements during labor but also clinical practices, intervention patterns, and women's overall birthing experience. 10

Despite the international emphasis on woman‐centered care, evidence on implementing mobility‐enabling labor–birth–postpartum (LBP) beds in routine settings is limited. A systematic search across PubMed, Scopus, EBSCO, and Cochrane Central (up to May 13, 2026)—using the terms (“birthing bed” OR “labor–birth–postpartum bed” OR “birthing position”) AND (“maternal outcome” OR “neonatal outcome”) AND (“experimental study” OR “randomized controlled”)—revealed that while recent trials 3 , 11 , 12 investigate maternal positions, they focus on postural efficacy rather than the impact of specific birthing bed configurations. Unlike studies evaluating “positional software,” no qualifying research was identified that directly compares the multidimensional effects of an LBP bed versus a TGT regarding provider behavior and routine institutional interventions. Therefore, this quasi‐experimental study investigated whether LBP beds are associated with differences in birthing experiences (pain and satisfaction) and clinical outcomes (labor duration and maternal/neonatal outcomes) compared with TGT use in a real‐world clinical workflow.

Research question

The research question is as follows: Are there differences between women who gave birth using an LBP bed and those who used a TGT in terms of (1) labor duration, (2) pain intensity, (3) birth satisfaction, and (4) maternal and neonatal outcomes?

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and setting

This quasi‐experimental study was conducted between June and October 2025 in the birth unit of a secondary‐care mother‐friendly hospital in western Turkey, where approximately 1500 vaginal births occur annually. The unit employs 24 midwives and 10 obstetricians, with continuous obstetric coverage available 24 h a day.

The maternity unit included six LBP rooms and two intervention rooms containing TGTs. Each LBP room contained a mobility‐enabling LBP bed, an open neonatal bed, a shower, and a toilet. The intervention rooms contained a TGT, an open neonatal bed, a shower, and a toilet (Figure 1). Both room types were located within the same maternity unit and operated under identical institutional protocols. Staffing ratios, clinical guidelines, and routine intrapartum care practices were consistent across rooms, and the same midwifery and obstetric team provided care. The principal structural difference between the rooms was the birth bed configuration, which either enabled or restricted maternal mobility during labor.

The labor–birth–postpartum room.

Participants were placed sequentially according to room availability at the time of admission, within the routine clinical workflow of the delivery unit. No random assignment procedure or assignment concealment method was used. Therefore, the study was designed as a pragmatic quasi‐experimental comparison reflecting routine clinical practice conditions and was reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of Assessments with Non‐Random Designs (TREND) guidelines. 13

Sample

The sample size was calculated using a two‐way t‐test for two independent groups, with labor duration (min) as the primary outcome. A meta‐analysis found that flexible birthing positions were associated with an average reduction of 21.11 min (confidence interval: 11.84–30.49) in the active phase of labor. 14 In the current study, with α = 0.05 and power = 0.90, we expected a reduction of approximately 10 min in labor duration in the LBP bed group, resulting in n = 143 (total approximately 286). After accounting for a 10% loss, a total of 320 eligible women were sequentially assigned to either the LBP group (n = 160) or the TGT group (n = 160) according to room availability at admission (Figure 2).

Transparent Reporting of Assessments with Non‐Random Designs (TREND) flow diagram of participants' progression through the quasi experimental study. LBP, labor–birth–postpartum; TGT, traditional gynecological table.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were healthy pregnant women over 18 years of age, with a singleton pregnancy, expected spontaneous vaginal birth in vertex position, and without speech/language problems. Cases that did not meet the inclusion criteria, those who underwent Cesarean section, vacuum/forceps, or those who developed serious maternal/fetal complications were excluded from the study.

Intervention

The primary structural difference between the two study groups was the configuration of the birth bed and the degree of maternal mobility supported during labor and birth.

In the LBP group, women remained in the same LBP room throughout labor, birth, and the first postpartum hour. Birth occurred on the mobility‐enabling LBP bed without conversion to a TGT configuration. Women in this group were encouraged to adopt positions of comfort and were able to change positions during labor and birth according to individual preference and clinical condition. Neonatal care was provided on an open neonatal bed located within the same room.

In the TGT group, women similarly remained in the same room throughout labor, birth, and the first postpartum hour. Birth occurred on the TGT, in the lithotomy position, in accordance with routine hospital protocols. Neonatal care was also provided on an open neonatal bed within the room.

In both groups, women were permitted to have a companion present during labor and birth when room conditions allowed. Following completion of the first postpartum hour, mothers and newborns were transferred to the postpartum ward. To ensure clinical standardization and minimize inter‐provider variability in the implementation of the study protocol, intrapartum and immediate postpartum care were provided by the same experienced midwife‐researcher (second author), with 13 years of clinical experience.

Data collection

Data were collected in the birth unit using a questionnaire and a face‐to‐face interview. In addition, objective data such as labor duration and Apgar scores were extracted from birth diaries and electronic records. The questionnaire consisted of the following sections:

The Description Form includes participants' sociodemographic characteristics, gynecological/obstetric characteristics, and maternal–neonatal outcomes (26 questions).

The Visual Analog Scale (VAS) was used to assess the severity of current pain. The VAS is a continuous scale consisting of a 100‐mm horizontal line anchored by two verbal descriptors (i.e., “no pain” and “worst pain imaginable”). Participants are asked to select a point along the line connecting the two endpoints to indicate the perceived severity of pain. 15

The 10‐item Birth Satisfaction Scale (BSS) 16 was used to evaluate birth satisfaction. Each item is scored between 0 “strongly disagree” and 4 “strongly agree.” The lowest possible score on the scale is 0, and the highest is 40. Items 2, 4, 7, and 8 are reverse‐scored. It is thought that birth satisfaction increases with the total score. The Cronbach's alpha for the Turkish scale was 0.73 17 ; in this study, it was 0.84.

The primary outcome of the study was labor duration (hours). Secondary outcomes included maternal interventions (episiotomy and fundal pressure rates), maternal perception of birth (pain intensity measured via VAS and birth satisfaction via BSS), and neonatal outcomes (infant weight, Apgar scores, SpO2, and time to first breastfeeding).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics of women's sociodemographic characteristics were calculated. The distribution's normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test, skewness and kurtosis coefficients, and Q‐Q plots. Because the data did not follow a normal distribution, the median and interquartile range (IQR) were reported for continuous variables. Group comparisons were tested using the Mann–Whitney U‐test. The number (n) and frequency (%) values of categorical variables were provided, and the χ 2‐test was used for group comparisons. Cramer's V effect sizes were calculated for categorical variables, and Cohen's r effect sizes were calculated for continuous variables. Correlations between continuous variables were examined using Spearman's rank correlation. The internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach's alpha.

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 25.0. To account for multiple comparisons in Table 1, a Bonferroni correction was applied for all secondary maternal and neonatal outcomes. Excluding the pre‐specified primary outcome, the significance threshold for the remaining 17 comparisons was adjusted to P < 0.0029 (0.05/17).

Ethical considerations

Permission for the study was obtained from the Non‐Intervention Research Ethics Committee of Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University (dated May 2, 2025 and numbered 2025/6–302) and Afyonkarahisar Provincial Health Directorate (dated May 20, 2026 and numbered 2025/79). The study was conducted in accordance with the hospital's routine protocols. Participants were informed that interventions in birth would be performed in accordance with these protocols, and written consent was obtained. Data were collected in accordance with the Helsinki Principles.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

A total of 320 participants were included in the study, consisting of TGT (n = 160) and LBP (n = 160). When the sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics of the groups were examined, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in age, education level, income status, duration of marriage, number of pregnancies, or gestational week (P > 0.05; Table 1). These results indicate that the two groups are similar with respect to baseline characteristics.

Maternal outcomes

Regarding the primary outcome, no statistically significant difference in total labor duration was observed between the LBP and TGT groups (median 7.0 vs. 9.0 h, respectively; P = 0.254). Similarly, no significant difference was found in the frequency of induced labor (P = 0.264). In exploratory analyses of secondary outcomes, after applying the Bonferroni correction (adjusted threshold P < 0.0029), several maternal outcomes remained highly significant. The rates of episiotomy (67.5% vs. 45.0%) and fundal compression (25.0% vs. 6.9%) were significantly higher in the TGT group (P < 0.001). Further, women in the LBP group reported significantly lower pain intensity and higher birth satisfaction (P < 0.001). The strongest effect sizes were observed for fundal pressure (V = 0.25) and pain scores (r = 0.25) (Table 1).

Neonatal outcomes

There were no differences between the groups in infant sex, weight, SpO2 values, 1‐min and 5‐min Apgar scores, or time to first breastfeeding (P > 0.0029). These results indicate that the groups did not significantly affect neonatal outcomes (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

The present study evaluated a broad environmental and equipment‐based model implemented under routine maternity unit conditions, providing significant evidence comparing mobility‐enabling LBP beds with TGT. While existing randomized controlled trials 1 , 3 , 18 have demonstrated the efficacy of maternal positions and mobility under controlled, often ideal conditions, our study offers a distinct contribution by evaluating these effects within the pragmatic constraints of a busy, secondary‐level public maternity ward. Unlike randomized controlled trials, which often use strict inclusion criteria and specialized support staff, this study reflects “real‐world” clinical practice, where equipment‐based interventions must contend with established institutional habits and high patient volumes. Consequently, our findings fill a critical gap in the literature by demonstrating that, even in settings where clinical culture is heavily intervention‐oriented, the physical birthing environment can catalyze reductions in obstetric procedures and improve maternal satisfaction.

However, it is imperative to interpret these findings through the lens of the WHO (2018) 19 intrapartum care recommendations, which emphasize that episiotomy and fundal pressure should not be routine practices. The WHO explicitly recommends against routine episiotomy (Recommendation 30) and fundal pressure/Kristeller maneuver (Recommendation 33), setting a benchmark for selective episiotomy at <10%. In this context, although the LBP group showed a significant reduction in interventions compared to the TGT group, the remaining rates of episiotomy (45.0%) and fundal pressure (6.9%) remain substantially higher than contemporary international standards. The high rates reflect the persistent gap between international guidelines and routine clinical practice in Turkey, as noted in recent evidence. 20 , 21 The current results provide a perspective on how routine practices not recommended in international guidelines can be significantly reduced by improving the physical environment (LBP bed). These high rates, even in the LBP group, suggest a deeply ingrained “normalization” of medicalized birth within the institutional culture. Therefore, the LBP bed did not necessarily reduce interventions to an ideal evidence‐based level; rather, it shifted the practice from an extremely high intervention baseline toward a relatively lower, yet still clinically significant, intervention frequency. This highlights that while changing birth equipment is an essential step toward facilitating physiological birth, it must be supported by broader institutional policy reforms to align with WHO safety and respect targets fully.

The design and organization of the birthing environment can influence communication patterns, clinical decision‐making processes, and women's experiences during childbirth. Research in maternal health suggests that respectful and supportive care environments can subtly shape both maternal behavior and healthcare professional practices, promoting a more physiological approach to childbirth management. In contrast, environments organized around fixed beds and lithotomy positioning may reinforce intervention‐oriented care models by restricting women's movement and limiting positional choices. A growing body of evidence suggests that birth rooms designed to support mobility and maternal control can reduce unnecessary obstetric interventions while improving women's perceptions of safety and comfort. 9 Within this framework, the lower intervention rates observed in the LBP group might reflect not only the physical characteristics of the birth bed but also the broader clinical and behavioral dynamics facilitated by a mobility‐supportive birth environment. However, these results suggest that while birth environment can mitigate certain practices, institutional norms continue to exert a powerful influence on decision‐making.

Consistent with this perspective, research in perinatal nursing indicates that the organization of the birth environment can influence communication patterns among care providers and adherence to clinical care processes during labor. The structural characteristics and institutional policies of birth spaces, including equipment configuration and spatial organization, might, therefore, play an important role in shaping intrapartum care practices and the overall management of labor. 22

Supporting maternal mobility during labor is a key principle of physiologic and woman‐centered maternity care. International 8 and national 6 guidelines emphasize that women should be encouraged to adopt positions of comfort and move freely during labor, as mobility may facilitate fetal descent, improve uterine efficiency, and enhance maternal coping with labor pain. By directly engaging with these recommendations, current findings underscore that while mobility‐enabling equipment is necessary to align practice with these guidelines, it must be part of a broader commitment to reducing routine interventions such as episiotomy and fundal pressure. The higher birth satisfaction and lower perceived pain reported by women using LBP beds in the present study might reflect greater autonomy and active participation in the birth process.

Although there was no significant difference in labor duration between the groups in this study, women using the LBP bed reported lower pain intensity and higher birth satisfaction. These findings highlight the importance of mobility, positional flexibility, and autonomy during labor in shaping women's perceptions of control and participation in the birthing process. Contemporary research increasingly emphasizes the role of dynamic birthing positions and mobility‐enhancing care practices in improving women's birthing experiences and supporting physiological labor progression. 23 It is critical to emphasize that these improvements in the maternal experience were achieved despite the lack of effect on labor duration, reinforcing that the value of LBP beds lies in the quality of care and the reduction of unnecessary medicalization rather than simply temporal efficiency.

Another important observation of this study was that neonatal outcomes were comparable between the groups. No statistically significant difference in neonatal outcomes was found between the groups. These findings demonstrate that mobility‐enhancing birthing environments do not compromise neonatal safety. Previous research examining maternal birthing positions similarly reports no adverse neonatal effects when women are encouraged to adopt upright or flexible positions during labor. 24 The findings suggest that supportive birthing environments and mobility‐enhancing equipment might help reduce the reliance on unnecessary obstetric interventions, provided that they are implemented alongside institutional reforms aimed at eliminating routine, non‐evidence‐based practices.

Limitations

Although the team consistently applies institutional protocols and standardized birthing environments, there are structural differences between LBP and TGT environments. Because allocation depended on room availability within the routine clinical workflow, residual confounding related to admission timing, staffing conditions, or workload cannot be excluded. The generalizability of these findings is inherently limited by the single‐center design. Crucially, the provision of intrapartum care by the same caregiver (the second author) in both groups introduces a risk of performance and detection bias. While this approach was intended to minimize inter‐provider variability and ensure protocol fidelity, the researcher was unblinded to the environmental allocation. This might have introduced a Hawthorne effect or influenced the supportive atmosphere, potentially affecting subjective outcomes such as pain and satisfaction. Moreover, reliance on postpartum pain and satisfaction measurements necessitates careful consideration of recall bias, which might have influenced the subjectivity of the reported outcomes. Further, a significant limitation of this study is the unit's high baseline intervention rate. Prior to the study, the institutional rates for episiotomy and fundal pressure were consistent with the TGT group's findings (approximately 65–70% and 25%, respectively), highlighting a systemic reliance on these procedures. The study was conducted within this existing clinical reality, and while the LBP bed catalyzed change, it was insufficient to reduce intervention rates to the levels recommended by the WHO. 8

CONCLUSION

This quasi‐experimental study suggests that the configuration of birth beds might influence both clinical practices during labor and women's birth experiences. Women who gave birth using mobility‐enabling LBP beds reported lower pain intensity, higher birth satisfaction, and lower rates of episiotomy and fundal compression compared with those using a TGT. These results suggest that while mobility‐enabling LBP beds do not shorten labor duration, they are associated with reduced obstetric interventions and enhanced maternal birth experiences, with neonatal outcomes remaining comparable between the two groups.

In conclusion, while mobility‐enabling LBP beds do not shorten labor duration, they significantly reduce obstetric interventions from extremely high institutional levels to more moderate, albeit still high, levels. These findings indicate that birth environments designed to support maternal mobility might facilitate less intervention‐oriented care; however, the physical environment alone is not a substitute for addressing underlying institutional practices. While LBP beds represent a feasible strategy for strengthening woman‐centered care and improving maternal experiences, achieving patient safety targets requires a synergistic approach that combines ergonomic birth environments with a fundamental shift in clinical culture away from routine interventions. These results underscore that environmental changes must be accompanied by comprehensive institutional policy reforms and staff retraining to align with evidence‐based practices fully.

CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS FOR PRACTICE

The results of this study highlight that the birthing environment is a dynamic and strategic component of the intrapartum care team. Mobility‐focused birthing beds, which allow women to move freely, adopt vertical or alternative birthing positions, and participate more actively in the process, provide a technical infrastructure that facilitates healthcare professionals in supporting physiological childbirth. Designing environments that physically support mobility helps reduce routine obstetric interventions such as episiotomy and fundal compression, while simultaneously improving women's birthing experiences and satisfaction. Consequently, integrating mobility‐enhancing birthing beds into clinical units should be considered a practical and feasible strategy to strengthen reputable, evidence‐based, and woman‐centered intrapartum care without compromising neonatal safety.

Integrating mobility‐enabling birth beds into maternity units should be viewed as a component of a larger quality‐improvement strategy. While these beds provide the necessary infrastructure for physiological birth, clinical leaders must also address the “normalization” of routine interventions. To fully realize the benefits of such environmental changes, institutions must directly engage with global recommendations to ensure that reductions in interventions are not merely relative but move toward the absolute targets of evidence‐based, respectful maternity care.
