The Tall and Short of It
Authors: Joseph Meyer, Eric M. Isselbacher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

The absolute diameter of the ascending aorta is strongly associated with the risk of acute type A aortic dissection (ATAD).1 Despite this, most aortic events occur at diameters below current class 1 guideline thresholds for prophylactic aortic repair,2 an observation known as the “aortic size paradox.” Alternative measures and indexes, including aortic height index (AHI) (aortic diameter [cm]/patient height [m]), aortic size index (aortic diameter [cm]/body surface area [m2]), cross-sectional area to height ratio (CSAH) (aortic cross sectional area [cm2]/patient height [m]), and ascending aortic length (cm), have been proposed to better individualize risk assessment to guide prophylactic aortic repair, particularly in patients with borderline absolute aortic diameters.3 However, these measures have undergone limited validation in large, real-world cohorts, contributing to hesitancy in adoption in clinical practice. For example, the AHI threshold for elective surgery of 3.21 cm/m (a class 2b recommendation)3 was established by a single-center, retrospective study of 780 patients defining a high-risk cohort.4 Recent studies raised the possibility that lower and sex-specific thresholds may be indicated.5 Further validation across diverse age, sex, and body size is needed, as well as to more rigorously define surgical thresholds for prophylactic repair.

In this issue of JACC: Advances, Desai et al6 further investigate the use of AHI and CSAH in predicting the risk of death in patients with dilated proximal aortas. In the analysis, 11,083 adult patients with aortic diameters of 4 to 5 cm—excluding those with syndromic aortopathy and acute aortic dissection at time of presentation—were followed for a mean of 6 ± 4 years. Most patients were on appropriate guideline-recommended therapies (84.2% on beta-blockers, 67.4% on angiotensin inhibitors/receptor blockers, and 69.9% on statins). A total of 2,196 patients underwent surgery during follow-up. Notably, all surgeries were performed for significant aortic valve disease (stenosis 69%, regurgitation 31%, mean aortic dimension 4.7 ± 0.2 cm). There were no ATAD among operated patients, whereas 132 events (1.5%) occurred in the unoperated group. Long-term mortality was lower for operated than unoperated patients (8.3% vs 11.9%, respectively), and in-hospital death was only 0.6% among operated patients. Among patients with severe aortic valve pathology, these data support the safety of concomitant ascending aortic repair, which was associated with a lower risk of ATAD.

More impactfully, the authors report long-term aortic outcomes of the unoperated patient group. First, they confirm that significant ATAD risk exists at an absolute aortic diameter below current guideline thresholds for prophylactic repair. Although there was a higher rate of death or ATAD in patients with aortic diameters ≥4.5 cm, most deaths and ATADs occurred in patients with aortic diameters <4.5 cm. Second, in rigorous evaluation using derivation and validation cohorts, they found that absolute aortic dimension (<4.5 cm vs ≥ 4.5 cm) did not discriminate freedom of death in men (although it was weakly associated with the hazard of death in women), whereas both an AHI ≥2.5 cm/m (vs <2.5 cm/m) and CSAH of ≥9 cm2/m (vs <9 cm2/m) were associated with an increased risk of death among both men and women. These findings were confirmed when evaluated in the validation sample.

Desai et al6 provide compelling evidence that AHI and CSAH are clinically useful measures for risk stratification in patients with root and ascending aortic aneurysms. The findings in this large, contemporary cohort suggest that these indexed measures may offer superior prognostic value over absolute aortic diameters alone.

But before such tools can be adopted into routine clinical practice, further validation is required. Although the study included a large real-world cohort, it was nevertheless at a single center and consisted of patients who were predominately male (78.6%) and White (89.5%), limiting the generalizability of the findings to women and under-represented groups. It also remains important to evaluate whether these measures remain accurate in assessing risk across age groups, as the authors did not evaluate the impact of age in outcomes for these measures. The current 2022 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Guideline for the Diagnosis and Management of Aortic Disease3 recommends the use of CSAH for patients whose height is >1 SD from the mean (Class 2a recommendation). However, in this report, Desai et al6 broadly apply this measure to all patients in the cohort, and it remains unclear from their data if CSAH is better suited for patients at the extremes of height or is equally predictive among patients of all heights. It would also be relevant to know how many patients in this cohort had familial aortic aneurysms and if outcomes differed in this population. Moreover, it would have been informative if the authors had reported outcomes in patients with bicuspid vs tricuspid aortic valves and among those with aortic root vs ascending aortic aneurysms.

Importantly, the observed difference in mortality among subjects above and below the proposed AHI and CSAH cut points did not include cause-specific mortality data. As such, the observed mortality differences cannot be attributed solely to reduction in ATAD.

In this report, the authors use AHI and CSAH cut points that are lower than the current guideline-specified class 2 surgical thresholds of ≥3.21 cm/m and ≥10 cm2/m, respectively. Lowering current surgical thresholds would significantly increase the number of lower-risk patients undergoing prophylactic aortic repair. To provide clinical perspective, a man and woman of average height in the United States (68.9 and 63.5 inches, respectively7) would reach the proposed surgical threshold of an AHI of 2.5 cm/m at absolute aortic diameters of 4.4 cm and 4.0 cm, respectively, and a CSAH of 9 cm2/m at absolute aortic diameters of 4.5 cm and 4.3 cm, respectively. Given the large number of patients with diameters in these ranges, this would lead to a dramatic increase in the number of patients undergoing prophylactic aortic repair and, consequently, being exposed to both the short- and long-term risks of aortic replacement. As such, caution is paramount at this time, even if the surgical risk at high-volume centers with expert aortic surgeons is relatively low. These data underscore the need for a randomized clinical trial examining short- and long-term outcomes of elective surgery at lower diameter thresholds. The ongoing TITAN: SvS trial is currently enrolling and randomizing patients with an ascending aortic diameter between 5.0 and 5.5 cm to surveillance vs elective ascending aortic repair,8 but this current study by Desai et al6 provides evidence that an even lower diameter threshold for randomization (eg, ≥4.5 cm), or perhaps AHI and/or CSAH indexed thresholds, should be considered in future randomized trials.

This study adds to the growing body of evidence that the use of height-based indexes may better capture ATAD risk more effectively than absolute aortic diameter. It remains to be seen which measures and what thresholds are most appropriate for individual patients when determining the risks and benefits of elective prophylactic surgery.
