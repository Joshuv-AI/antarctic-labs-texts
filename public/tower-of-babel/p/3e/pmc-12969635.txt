Revealing the influence of top-brewing and bottom-brewing methods on the flavor of green tea based on the interaction between EGCG and aroma compounds
Authors: Mengke Zheng, Zhongyi Mao, Yinan Yang, Guihong Qi, Peng Li, Yufeng Luo, Donghao Zhang, Jianping Xie, Shihao Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The brewing process is a crucial step in determining the flavor profile of green tea infusions. However, the flavor differences arising from top-brewing and bottom-brewing methods remain poorly understood, and the underlying mechanisms are largely unexplored. In this study, GC-MS and sensory analysis were employed to investigate the flavor differences in Shucheng Little Cymbidium tea infusions under varying water temperatures and brewing methods. Subsequently, the variations in major polyphenolic compounds were analyzed, and the potential causes of flavor differences were explained from the perspective of the interactions between EGCG and key aroma compounds. Multivariate statistical analysis revealed that compounds such as geraniol, nonanal, and methyl salicylate serve as important markers for discriminating between different brewing conditions. Moreover, EGCG exhibited a significant binding effect with compounds such as geraniol and nonanal, driven by hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. This binding may regulate the transfer of aroma compounds from tea leaves to the infusion during brewing, thereby shaping distinct sensory characteristics. These findings provide a molecular-level rationale for empirical techniques in traditional tea art. More importantly, it inspires researchers to pay attention to the crucial role of the tea infusion matrix in determining flavor.

Introduction

Tea, one of the most widely consumed beverages globally, holds significant cultural and dietary importance across diverse regions. China produces approximately 3 million tons of tea annually, with green tea accounting for nearly 70% of the total annual output (Liu et al., 2023). Its widespread appeal stems from a distinctive sensory profile characterized by fresh, grassy aromas and a balanced interplay of astringency, umami, and subtle sweetness. The sensory quality of tea, particularly its flavor, is a key determinant of market value and consumer acceptance. This makes understanding the chemical basis of tea flavor and its regulatory factors an important area of research.

In recent years, significant progress has been made in research on the flavor characteristics of tea. Technologies such as gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) have been widely used to analyze volatile components in tea, including terpenes, aldehydes, and esters, which contribute characteristic aromas such as floral, fresh, or fruity notes to green tea (Xu et al., 2021; Zhao and Truhlar, 2008, Zhu et al., 2021). Following the identification of key flavor compounds, researchers have sought to improve green tea quality through optimization of production processes, such as raw material selection, spreading, fixing, rolling, and drying. These advances have supported the market growth of high-quality green tea and generated notable economic benefits (Zhang et al., 2025). Currently, green tea is predominantly consumed as an infusion prepared by steeping loose leaves or tea bags in hot water. Researchers have found that the process of brewing tea is also an important factor influencing tea flavor, as it is directly related to consumers’ drinking process and sensory experience (Yin et al., 2022). For example, when Enshi Yulu green tea is brewed with hot water (100 °C, 5 min), the tea infusion was rich in compounds such as linalool and α-cedrol, providing consumers with a strong floral aroma experience. In contrast, cold brewing (4 °C, 6 h) yields higher concentrations of pentanal, hexanal, and heptanal, resulting in a fresher fragrance profile (Liu et al., 2016). Furthermore, brewing water with lower pH and lower Ca2+ concentration has been shown to increase the content of volatile compounds, thereby enhancing the overall aroma quality of the infusion (Cao et al., 2021). Liu et al. also demonstrated that the water-to-tea ratio and steeping time affect the perceived sweetness and umami intensity of tea infusions (Liu et al., 2018). Collectively, these studies on brewing conditions and the properties of brewing water provide important guidance for addressing diverse consumer drinking preferences.

Upon gaining insight into consumers’ brewing habits, it was observed that some consumers adopt the “Top-Brewing” (TB) method, where sufficient hot water is first added to the cup, followed by the addition of tea leaves. However, some consumers adopt the opposite approach, placing the tea leaves in the cup first, and then pouring in hot water, which is referred to as the “Bottom-Brewing” (BB) method (Fig. 1). Based on traditional brewing experience, premium green teas with tight and dense shapes are suitable for top-brewing methods, while green teas with loose, flat or relatively mature shapes are suitable for bottom-brewing methods (Gong and Zhou, 2000; He, 2011). Furthermore, through empirical practical operations, the sensory quality of tea infusions can be improved by optimizing brewing methods. However, the potential mechanisms by which empirically selected brewing methods affect the release and interaction of flavor components at the molecular level remain largely unknown. By altering the initial contact pattern between tea leaves and water, these two methods may influence the release rate of volatile compounds and the dissolution behavior of non-volatile components, ultimately leading to different sensory experiences (Weterings et al., 2020). However, this hypothesis requires relevant research for verification and analysis. From the perspective of mass transfer, there are significant differences in the degree and efficiency of tea particle wetting, the rate of substance diffusion, and the dynamic characteristics of water flow during the brewing process between these two methods (Weterings et al., 2020). Under these conditions, the interaction between non-volatile and volatile components in tea may be the dominant factor driving these differences. Tea polyphenols, including catechins, flavonoids, and other substances, are the main non-volatile components in green tea and determine the bitter and astringent taste of tea infusion (Yin et al., 2022). Du et al. (2025) evaluated the sensory perception differences of key aroma compounds in Wuzhishan black tea under varying tea polyphenol concentrations and found that the olfactory thresholds of hexanal, octanal, hexanol, nonanol, 2-heptanone, benzaldehyde, and 1-octen-3-ol were significantly elevated due to the presence of tea polyphenols. Based on this finding, it can be inferred that variations in the interaction between tea polyphenols and aroma compounds may help to elucidate the differences in the flavor characteristics of tea infusions prepared using different brewing methods.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of brewing methods for the top and bottom of green tea.Fig. 1

Based on the aforementioned issues and hypotheses, this study selected Shucheng Little Cymbidium Tea (SLCT), a unique green tea from Lu'an City, Anhui Province, to systematically compare the differences in flavor components and sensory characteristics under different brewing methods (TB vs. BB). Additionally, by focusing on the interactions between tea polyphenols and characteristic aroma compounds, we employed methods such as partition coefficient, olfactory threshold, spectral analysis, and computer simulation to investigate the underlying mechanisms responsible for the flavor differences under different brewing conditions. The results of this study are expected to guide consumers in preparing tea infusions with superior sensory quality, which will also help better satisfy consumer preferences.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Dimethyl sulfide (99.7%), hexanal (99%), heptanal (98%), benzaldehyde (99.5%), p-cymene (98%), linalool (98%), nonanal (96%), methyl salicylate (99.5%), geraniol (99%), and DMSO-d6 (≥99.9%) were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Methanol and formic acid were both of chromatographic grade. The mixture of n-alkane (C7-C30), 3-methylbutanal (98%), benzeneacetaldehyde (98%), 2-methylbutanal (98%), octanal (99%), D-limonene (95%), and cedrol (98%) were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Phenethyl alcohol (99%), (Z)-hex-3-en-1-yl hexanoate (98%), α-ionone (90%), trans-β-ionone (97%), β-cyclocitral (95%), indole (99%), cis-jasmone (90%), ethyl decanoate (99%), (−)-gallocatechin (98%), (−)-epicatechin (98%), and 2-pentylfuran (98%) were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). (−)-Epicatechin gallate (98%), (−)-epigallocatechin (98%), (−)-epigallocatechin gallate (98%), and (+)-catechin (98%) were purchased from Acmec Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Sample preparation

The SLCT samples were purchased from Shucheng Lvhui Tea Industry Co., Ltd. (Anhui, China) and stored at −25 °C until further analysis. According to GB/T 23776–2018, 5 g of SLCT tea leaves were brewed with 250 mL of hot water at 80 °C, 85 °C, and 90 °C, respectively, using the TB and BB. After 4 min, the tea infusion was quickly filtered. For the determination of aroma compounds, 5 mL of the tea infusion from different temperatures and brewing methods was added to a 20 mL headspace vial, and 10 μL of internal standard solution (decyl acetate, 25 μg/mL) was added. After the operation, the headspace vial cap was immediately tightened. For the analysis of polyphenolic compounds, the tea infusion was filtered using a 0.22 μm membrane filter, appropriately diluted, and subsequently subjected to analysis.

Analysis of aroma compounds and major polyphenolic compounds

Identification of aroma compounds

The volatile compound extraction method was adapted from Chen et al. (2022) with slight modifications. A solid-phase microextraction (SPME) fiber (50/30 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS; Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA) was inserted into the headspace vial for 30 min of adsorption, then immediately transferred into the injector for 5 min desorption at 250 °C. Subsequently, the sample was analyzed using an Agilent 8890 gas chromatography system coupled with a 5977C series mass spectrometer (GC-MS; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Volatile compounds were separated on a DB-5MS fused silica capillary column (60 m × 0.250 mm × 0.25 μm; Agilent Technologies). The sample was injected in splitless mode, with helium as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, and the injector temperature set to 250 °C. The temperature program for GC was as follows: initial temperature of 40 °C held for 5 min, then ramped at 5 °C/min to 280 °C, and held for 5 min. The MS analysis was performed using an electron impact source, with the ion source temperature of 230 °C, electron energy of 70 eV, and quadrupole temperature set to 150 °C. Full scan mode was used for scanning, with a mass scan range of m/z 30-650.

Volatile compounds were qualitatively identified by comparing their mass spectra with the NIST 17 database and their calculated retention indices (RI) against those derived from a C7-C30 n-alkane solution. The concentration of volatile compounds was determined using a semi-quantitative method (Zhao and Truhlar, 2008, Zhu et al., 2021) by comparing the chromatographic peak areas of the volatile compounds with that of the internal standard (ethyl decanoate). The calculation formula is: Ci=Cis×Ai/Ais, where Ci is the relative concentration of the volatile compound, in μg/kg; Cis is the concentration of the internal standard, in μg/kg; Ai is the chromatographic peak area of volatile compound; Ais is the chromatographic peak area of the internal standard. The odor activity value (OAV) of volatile compounds is calculated according to the following formula: OAVi=Ci/OTi , where Ci is the concentration of the volatile compound, in μg/kg; OTi is the odor threshold of the volatile compound in water, in μg/kg.

Identification of polyphenolic compounds

The analysis was performed using a Thermo Scientific™ UltiMate 3000 UHPLC system coupled with a Q Exactive™ mass spectrometer (Waltham, MA, USA). The mobile phase A was 0.1% (v/v) formic acid aqueous solution, and phase B was methanol. The separation process, slightly modified from Li et al. (2022), employed an Acquity BEH C18 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) maintained at 40 °C, with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min and an injection volume of 1 μL. The elution gradient was programmed as follows: 0-3.0 min, 10% B; 3.0-13.0 min, 10%-60% B; 13.0-17.0 min, 60% B; 17.0-18.0 min, 60%-10% B; 18.0-20.0 min, 10% B. For mass spectrometry analysis in this study, specific parameters were configured: operation in negative ion mode with a resolution of 70,000, automatic gain control target set to 3E6 ions, maximum ion trap time of 200 ms, and a scan range of m/z 70-1000.

The polyphenolic compounds in tea infusions were identified using Thermo Scientific™ Compound Discoverer 3.1 software. The identities of the compounds were confirmed by comparing the retention times and characteristic fragment ions (m/z) of chromatographic peaks with those of standards. The external standard method was used to quantify the contents of the polyphenolic compounds. Specifically, 10 mg of (−)-gallocatechin (GC), (−)-epigallocatechin (EGC), (+)-catechin (C), (−)-epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), epicatechin (EC), and (−)-epicatechin gallate (ECG) were accurately weighed respectively and placed in a 10 mL volumetric flask to prepare a mixed standard stock solution with a concentration of 1.0 mg/mL. Then, 10 μL, 50 μL, 100 μL, 250 μL, 500 μL, 750 μL, and 1000 μL of the mixed standard stock solution were transferred into separate 10 mL volumetric flasks and diluted to the mark to obtain mixed standard working solutions with different concentrations. These working solutions were then used to establish calibration curves for each compound. During the sample analysis, the concentrations of polyphenolic compounds were determined by comparing their chromatographic peak areas against these established calibration curves.

Sensory evaluation of tea infusion

Quantitative descriptive analysis was used to evaluate the sensory characteristics of tea infusions under different brewing methods, focusing on both aroma and taste, following the methods of Liang et al. and Wang et al. (Liang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Zhengzhou University, Zhengzhou, China (Ref no. 20250614). The sensory evaluation panel consisted of 10 sensory evaluators (5 male and 5 female, aged 22-30) from the Flavor Research Center at Zhengzhou University. Appropriate protocols for protecting the rights and privacy of all participants were utilized during the research. Samples were brewed using TB and BB according to the sensory evaluation standard (Liang et al., 2024). The sensory panel, after reviewing relevant literature, discussed and finalized the aroma and taste descriptors. The selected aroma descriptors were: fresh, floral, chestnut-like, fruity, sweet, and woody. The selected taste descriptors were: umami, bitter, astringent, sour, and sweet. All samples were evaluated in a standardized sensory analysis room (23 ± 1 °C). Each sensory evaluator independently rated the aroma and taste characteristics of the samples on a scale from 0 to 5, where “0” indicates no perception and “5” indicates an intense presence. To ensure objectivity, no discussions were allowed during the evaluation. The intensity of the aroma and taste of each sample was determined by the average score of all evaluators.

Investigation of the interaction between EGCG and characteristic aroma compounds

Determination of partition coefficient of aroma compounds

The gas-liquid partition coefficient is commonly used to investigate the retention or release of aroma compounds from a matrix. Ettre et al. (1993) proposed the Phase Ratio Variation (PRV) method for determining the partition coefficient. This method establishes a linear relationship between the inverse of the chromatographic peak area and the phase ratio (β), achieved by varying the gas-liquid volume ratio in the headspace vial. The partition coefficient is then determined by the slope and intercept ratio. The functional equation is as follows: 1A=1fi×Ciliq×k+1fi×Ciliq×β, where k is the partition coefficient; A is the chromatographic peak area of the aroma compound in the headspace at thermodynamic equilibrium; fi is the detector response factor; Ciliq is the initial concentration of the compound in the headspace vial; and β is the gas-liquid volume ratio in the headspace vial.

To determine the partition coefficient, EGCG solutions with concentrations of 0, 1, 5, 10, 50, and 100 times the quantitative concentration were prepared and mixed with five key aroma compounds, respectively. A volume of 50, 100, 500, 1000, 1500, and 2000 μL of the mixed sample was placed into 20 mL headspace vials. After equilibration for 12 h, the peak areas of the aroma compounds were determined using automatic headspace sampler coupled with GC-MS. The chromatographic column used was an HP-5MS (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm; Agilent Technologies) with a splitless injection mode, and the flow rate was 1.0 mL/min. The temperature program was as follows: initial temperature of 50 °C held for 3 min, then increased to 100 °C at a rate of 15 °C/min and held for 1 min, followed by a ramp to 230 °C at 50 °C/min.

Determination of olfactory threshold

The olfactory thresholds of aroma compounds were determined using the three-alternative forced-choice (3-AFC) procedure combined with S-curve fitting, with modifications based on the method described by Niu et al. (2025). In the experiment, eight concentration gradient solutions of aroma compounds were prepared with a 2-fold dilution factor, spanning sub-threshold to supra-threshold levels. EGCG concentrations were set at 0 and 50 times the quantitative concentration (0 and 2.0 mg/mL). Each concentration group consisted of one sample and two blank controls, placed in randomly coded brown sniffing bottles. A sensory panel of 10 trained individuals (5 male, 5 female) with no history of smoking was recruited. Appropriate protocols for protecting the rights and privacy of all participants were utilized during the study. The test was conducted in an odor-free environment at 25 °C. Each participant evaluated one set of samples at a time, selecting the odorous sample from the three options. Each group of experiments was repeated three times. After data collection, the correct detection probability (p) at each concentration was calculated, and the probability was then corrected using the formula: P=23p‐12. An S-curve was fitted using the Logistic function, and the concentration corresponding to a 50% corrected probability was finally defined as the olfactory threshold.

Ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy analysis

The ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectra of nonanal, geraniol, 2-pentylfuran, methyl salicylate, and α-ionone, as well as their complexes with EGCG, were recorded using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Evolution™ 220, Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA, USA). The wavelength scan range was 200 to 600 nm.

Fluorescence spectroscopy analysis

Fluorescence emission spectra of the aroma compounds and their complexes with EGCG were measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-4700, Hitachi; Marunouchi, Chiyoda-ku, Tokyo). The excitation wavelength was set at 280 nm, and the emission spectra were scanned from 300 to 500 nm. The scan speed was 240 nm/min, the slit width was 5.0 nm, and the photomultiplier tube voltage was set to 400 V.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy analysis

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of the aroma compounds and their complexes with EGCG were recorded using an FTIR spectrometer (INVENIO, Bruker; Karlsruhe, Germany). Spectra were collected over the range of 400 to 4500 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1.

1H NMR analysis

1H NMR spectra of the aroma compounds and their complexes with EGCG were obtained using a nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometer (Avance NEO 600, Bruker; Karlsruhe, Germany) operating at 600 MHz. Samples were dissolved in deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d6) for analysis (Yang et al., 2022).

Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculation and visualization analysis

All calculations were performed with the Gaussian16 program (Frisch et al., 2016) using density functional theory. The M06-2X (Zhao and Truhlar, 2008) method with the 6-31G(d, p) basis set was employed for all geometry optimizations. Additionally, interaction energy calculations were performed at the M06-2X/6-311++G (2df, 2pd) level. The solvent effect was considered using the implicit IEF-PCM model (Barone and Cossi, 1998) with water as the solvent. Furthermore, Multiwfn (Lu, 2024; Lu and Chen, 2012), VMD (Humphrey et al., 1996), and CYLview (Legault, 2009) were used to visualize molecular structures of transition states and intermediates, as well as to perform and display non-covalent interaction analyses.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were preprocessed and plotted using Origin 2022 (Origin Lab Corporation, USA), and 1H NMR spectra were processed using MestReNova 15 (Mestrelab Research SL, Spain). Statistical analysis of inter-group differences was conducted using an independent samples t-test in SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., USA). Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed with SIMCA 14.1 (Umetrics, Umeå, Sweden). All experiments were repeated three times, and the results are presented as means with standard deviations.

Results and discussion

Effects of brewing methods and water temperature on aroma characteristics of tea infusion

Analysis of aroma composition of tea infusion

In this study, volatile compounds in SLCT samples were analyzed using HS-SPME coupled with GC-MS, and a total of 92 volatile compounds were identified (Table S1). These included 24 terpenes, 15 alcohols, 14 aldehydes, 11 ketones, 11 heterocyclic compounds, 6 esters, 2 alkanes, 3 aromatic hydrocarbons, 3 acids, 1 alkene, 1 phenol, and 1 sulfide. Terpenes are components of plant biosynthesis through the mevalonate or pyruvate pathway, accounting for the highest proportion in plant metabonomics (Wang et al., 2005). Additionally, green tea typically undergoes a fixing process, which can also promote the formation of terpenes compounds. However, despite their high abundance, the contribution of terpenes to the overall aroma profile of green tea is not necessarily proportional to their content, as their odor thresholds vary considerably (Guo et al., 2021). In this study, besides terpenes, alcohols and aldehydes were also identified as abundant compounds, typically contributing to the citrus and grassy flavors of tea infusion. They are important metabolites in the lipoxygenase-mediated lipid oxidation pathway and the Strecker degradation pathway. In terms of absolute numbers, ketones are reported as the second largest category of aroma compounds in tea to date. In this study, ketones were not as numerically dominant, which may be attributed to differences in tea tree varieties and production processes. Heterocycles are also an important class of aromatic active compounds in green tea. They are typically produced during the fixing and drying stages of green tea processing via the Maillard reaction, imparting a caramel and camphor-like aroma to the tea infusion (Wang et al., 2009).

To verify the differences in aroma characteristics under different brewing methods, OPLS-DA was performed (Fig. 2A). The model fitting index (R2X) was 0.648, the cumulative explanatory power (R2Y) was 0.941, and the predictive ability index (Q2) was 0.739. The fact that both R2Y and Q2 exceeded 0.5 indicated that the model had acceptable fitness and reliability. After 200 permutation tests, the intercept of Q2 regression line with the Y-axis was negative, indicating that the prediction ability of the model is consistent with the actual situation of cross validation, and there is no over fitting phenomenon (Qin et al., 2023). Therefore, the OPLS-DA model was deemed robust and suitable for discriminating the brewing methods of SLCT. On this basis, Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) was employed to identify the key volatile compounds contributing significantly to the differentiation of samples (Fig. 2C). Compounds with a VIP value > 1 and p < 0.05 were considered significant aroma markers. A total of 46 volatile compounds met these criteria, primarily belonging to terpenes and aldehydes. This indicates that the flavor divergence is not driven by a single component, but results from the synergistic variation of multiple chemical classes.Fig. 2Analysis of differences in aroma compounds in tea infusion under different brewing methods. Furthermore, it is worth noting that even if the brewing methods are the same, different brewing temperatures will make the aroma characteristics of tea infusion show differences. This suggests that temperature is also a critical factor influencing the aroma characteristics of tea infusion, a finding consistent with Zhang et al. (2024). To gain a comprehensive understanding of the combined influence of brewing methods and brewing temperature on the aroma characteristics of tea infusion, a comparison of the contents of various aroma compounds was performed, with results presented in Fig. 2B. Due to their low content, it was difficult to distinguish differences in alkanes, alkenes, and sulfides between TB and BB methods. For other types of compounds, the TB method significantly increased the aroma compound content in the tea infusion. In addition, it was observed that the TB method consistently yielded higher concentrations of aroma compounds across different brewing temperatures. This suggests that consumers could be advised to utilize the TB method to enhance the aroma quality of their tea infusion. One possible reason is while hot water injection in bottom-brewing disturbs the green tea leaves, facilitating their contact and extraction of aroma compounds, it simultaneously promotes the dissipation and loss of aroma compounds due to the vigorous tumbling and flow of the tea infusion. Conversely, when using the TB method, the initial hot water remains relatively static. The subsequent addition of tea leaves does not disrupt this stillness, thereby allowing flavor compounds from the tea leaves to steadily and continuously migrate into the tea infusion with minimal loss. Moreover, the differences observed between TB and BB are influenced by brewing temperature. This is attributed to the fact that the water temperature determines the differential migration rates of flavor compounds from tea leaves into the water, namely the difference in extraction kinetics of flavor compounds (Liu et al., 2018).

OAV analysis and identification of key aroma-active compounds

Considering the significant influence of water temperature and to provide more informed brewing recommendations, the OAVs of individual aroma compounds were analyzed. The contribution of an aroma compound to the overall aroma of tea infusion depends not only on its concentration but also on its odor threshold; thus, concentration alone is insufficient to determine the sensory characteristics of tea (Zhai et al., 2022). OAV, defined as the ratio of a compound's concentration to its odor threshold, is a widely accepted metric for evaluating the contribution of individual volatile compounds. Compounds with OAV ≥1 are generally regarded as key aroma-active compounds.

A total of 23 compounds exhibited OAVs ≥1 and were identified as the key aroma-active components in the SLCT samples (Table S2). Among these, nonanal (OAV: 98.25–142.24), cis-jasmone (OAV: 86.27–161.78), and α-ionone (OAV: 152.00–260.95) all exhibited OAVs exceeding 100, indicating their dominant roles in shaping the overall aroma. α-Ionone and trans-β-ionone, derived from carotenoid oxidation and thermal degradation of β-carotene, respectively, are recognized as primary contributors to the violet-like aroma in green tea (Kanasawud and Crouzet, 2002; Liang et al., 2024). Dimethyl sulfide, associated with the Maillard reaction and the degradation of sulfur-containing amino acids during heat treatment, provides the characteristic corn-like and chestnut-like notes essential to high-quality green tea (Liao et al., 2020). Floral and fruity nuances are further enhanced by phenylethyl alcohol, geraniol, linalool, and octanal, with linalool and phenylethyl alcohol being closely linked to enzymatic transformations related to aroma formation (Joshi and Gulati, 2015). Nonanal and 2-methylbutanal contribute green and malt-like odors, originating from lipoxygenase-mediated oxidation of linolenic acid and isoleucine degradation, respectively (Ho et al., 2015). In addition, furans such as 2-pentylfuran, formed through lipid degradation and Maillard reactions, impart caramel-like and sweet characteristics that tend to intensify with increasing processing temperature (Zhai et al., 2022). While benzaldehyde, cedrol, and methyl salicylate contribute floral, woody, and fermented-tea-related characteristics (Wang et al., 2008), benzaldehyde has also been identified as a contributor to storage-related stale off-flavor in green tea (Dai et al., 2020). Other fresh notes are supported by hexanal and (Z)-hex-3-en-1-yl hexanoate, collectively forming a complex aroma profile of the infusion.

The summarized OAV results for the tea infusions are presented in Fig. 3A. Across the tested temperature range, tea infusions prepared using the TB method generally exhibited higher total OAVs than those prepared using the BB method, indicating differences in overall aroma-active potential between brewing approaches. Under the BB method, variations in water temperature did not lead to pronounced changes in total OAV, which may be attributed to similar extraction efficiencies of aroma-active compounds under these conditions. In contrast, when the TB method was applied, the highest total OAV was observed at 85 °C. To provide a statistically robust comparison, a t-test was conducted to evaluate the total OAVs of TB and BB infusions at 85 °C, and a significant difference was observed (p < 0.05), confirming that the two brewing methods resulted in markedly different aroma-active profiles under identical temperature conditions. As illustrated in the concentration-based heatmap (Fig. 3B), the majority of these compounds exhibit consistently elevated levels under the 85 °C TB strategy, indicating a coordinated enhancement rather than isolated changes. Although concentration levels or total OAV cannot be directly equated with perceived intensity due to potential non-linear sensory interactions, this simultaneous elevation reflects an optimal extraction balance at 85 °C. Increasing temperature promotes volatile migration (Liu et al., 2018), but excessively high heat favors loss of low-boiling compounds (Yu et al., 2021), while 80 °C results in insufficient extraction. Overall, 85 °C with the TB method provides the best compromise between aroma release and retention.Fig. 3Evaluation of key aromas in green tea infusions based on OAVs and concentrations. Differences in sensory characteristics of tea infusion between top- and bottom-brewing methods

Considering that aroma compounds were most abundant at 85 °C, sensory descriptive analysis was conducted on tea infusions prepared by TB and BB methods to elucidate method-induced differences (Fig. 4). Overall, the aroma quality of green tea is characterized by purity, pleasantness, and persistence, which can be further categorized as fresh, chestnut-like, and orchid-like (Yin et al., 2022). Based on the sensory analysis conducted using the descriptive terms selected in this study, green tea SLCT was found to primarily exhibit fresh, floral, and chestnut-like aromas. In terms of aroma differences attributable to brewing methods, TB yielded stronger fresh and floral notes, while BB produced a more pronounced chestnut-like aroma (Fig. 4A). Based on the identified key aroma compounds, phenylethyl alcohol, geraniol, linalool, 2-methylbutanal, nonanal, α-ionone, and methyl salicylate are likely the main contributors to the enhanced fresh and floral aromas observed under the TB method. When drinking green tea, taste characteristics are also an important evaluation factor, and they may interact perceptually with aroma characteristics (Zhai et al., 2022). Consistent with previous studies, bitterness and astringency were the dominant taste characteristics of tea infusion (Fig. 4B) (Liu et al., 2018). In addition, the tea infusion in this study also exhibited a certain degree of umami and sweetness, which may be the characteristic of SLCT. It is worth noting that the tea infusion brewed using the TB method exhibited a significantly stronger bitter taste, which may be attributed to the higher content of polyphenolic compounds, as these compounds are the primary determinants of bitterness in tea infusions (Deng et al., 2022). The stronger bitterness under TB, likely due to higher polyphenol content, may partly modulate the perception of chestnut-like aroma through taste-aroma interactions.Fig. 4Comparison of sensory characteristics of tea infusion between top and bottom brewing methods. Overall, the differences in flavor characteristics between tea infusions prepared using TB and BB methods are clearly distinguishable. Among them, TB can impart a more intense aroma to the tea infusion, resulting in a more prominent fresh and floral fragrance, whereas the tea infusion brewed using the BB method exhibits a stronger chestnut-like aroma. In addition, compounds such as geraniol, nonanal, and methyl salicylate may be the main chemical components responsible for the differences in aroma characteristics. Beyond aroma-related differences, notable differences in the bitterness of the tea infusion were also observed between the two brewing methods. These findings provide practical guidance for selecting brewing methods according to personal aroma and taste preferences.

Analysis of polyphenols in tea infusion under top- and bottom-brewing methods

Many studies have confirmed the crucial role of non-volatile components in food matrices in regulating the volatility of aroma compounds through structure-dependent interactions and multiple molecular mechanisms. For example, it has been found that matrix substances such as polyphenols in wine have a strong influence on the volatility and solubility of volatile compounds (Pittari et al., 2021). Notably, polyphenolic compounds are also important matrix components in tea infusion (Jiang et al., 2023), and their interaction with volatile compounds may be a significant factor contributing to the differences in aroma characteristics under different brewing methods. Accordingly, the main polyphenolic compounds in the tea infusion prepared using both top- and bottom-brewing methods were first analyzed.

Upon comparison, it was found that the total polyphenol content in the tea infusion prepared by the TB method (101.50 mg/g) was significantly higher than that prepared by the BB method (90.87 mg/g) (Fig. 5A), indicating that the TB method promotes the leaching of a greater amount of phenolic compounds. When the leaching of polyphenolic substances increases, it may facilitate the migration of more aroma compounds into the tea infusion (Du et al., 2025), thereby enhancing the aroma quality of the tea infusion. This could represent a significant reason why the tea infusion brewed using the TB method possesses a more intense aroma. Some studies have indicated that when the water temperature is too high, some polyphenolic substances undergo partial degradation or epimerization (Saklar et al., 2015). Although the water temperature selected for this comparison was the same, the TB method requires injecting hot water first and then adding tea leaves, whereas the BB method involves rapidly injecting hot water into a teacup that already contains tea leaves. This difference in the initial tea-water contact could subject the tea leaves in the BB method to a more intense and immediate thermal shock, potentially promoting greater degradation or structural destabilization of EGCG (Xu et al., 2019), which in turn influences the extraction behavior of polyphenolic substances and their capacity to interact with aroma compounds. To compare the compositional differences among individual polyphenolic substances, a proportional composition graph is shown in Fig. 5B. The results indicate that (−)-epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) is the most abundant polyphenolic substance in tea infusion, and its proportion is relatively consistent under both brewing methods. Previous studies have demonstrated that EGCG is a key active ingredient governing the astringency and color of green tea, and its content is significantly influenced by brewing conditions (Pérez-Burillo et al., 2018).Fig. 5Comparison of polyphenols in tea infusion under top and bottom brewing methods. Analysis of binding interaction effect between EGCG and aroma compounds in tea infusion

The impact of EGCG on the volatilization and release behavior of aroma compounds

Given the importance of EGCG in determining the taste characteristics of tea infusion and its dominance among polyphenolic substances, this study therefore focused on EGCG to explore its interaction effects with characteristic aroma compounds in tea infusion. According to the types and aroma characteristics of aroma compounds, five representative compounds of top- and bottom-brewing methods, geraniol, nonanal, α-ionone, methyl salicylate and 2-pentylfuran, were selected as the target aroma compounds. Typically, by combining the headspace concentration of compounds obtained through the headspace method with their partition coefficient, a mathematical model can be established to quantitatively evaluate or predict the influence of the matrix on the compounds (Wang et al., 2025). Based on this method, it was found that in the absence of EGCG, the partition coefficients of geraniol, nonanal, α-ionone, methyl salicylate, and 2-pentylfuran in water were closely correlated with their saturated vapor pressure and Log P values (Fig. 6A), which is consistent with the general rule governing the gas-liquid distribution of volatile substances (Niu et al., 2025).Fig. 6Partition coefficient (A) and olfactory threshold (B-F) of selected aroma compounds in EGCG model.Fig. 6

Partition coefficient (A) and olfactory threshold (B-F) of selected aroma compounds in EGCG model.

However, the addition of EGCG significantly suppressed the release of all tested compounds, as evidenced by a marked reduction in their gas-liquid partition coefficients. Among them, EGCG exhibited the most significant inhibitory effect on nonanal, α-ionone, and 2-pentylfuran. This differential inhibition indicates that the effect of EGCG on aroma release is structure-dependent, suggesting the presence of selective interactions between EGCG and different aroma compounds (Wang et al., 2022). Furthermore, the analysis of the EGCG concentration effect indicated that this binding inhibition exhibited a nonlinear pattern, with the most significant effects observed at EGCG concentrations of 2 mg/mL and 4 mg/mL. This implies that the inhibitory effect of EGCG on aroma release exhibits a certain concentration dependency, and an increase in concentration aids in the binding of more aroma compounds, which is consistent with reported interaction behaviors between other food matrix components and aroma compounds (Wang et al., 2025). Upon reviewing the previous research on the TB method, it was found that the leaching amount of EGCG in the tea infusion is higher. This may be accompanied by the migration of more aroma compounds into the tea infusion, leading to the detection of a richer array of aroma compounds in the tea infusion.

Furthermore, the interaction between matrix EGCG and aroma compounds was evaluated from a sensory perspective, as sensory perception is a critical indicator of aroma quality. The results further confirmed the binding effect between EGCG and aroma compounds, as the addition of EGCG significantly increased the odor threshold of all tested aroma compounds (Fig. 6B–F), particularly α-ionone and 2-pentylfuran, whose thresholds increased by more than 60%. In contrast, the enhancement of the odor threshold of methyl salicylate by EGCG was relatively low (40.55%), which may be attributed to the weaker binding affinity between esters and EGCG. Similarly, when comparing different ester compounds, Lorrain et al. (2013) found that only the volatility of ethyl caprylate decreased in the presence of catechin, while the differences in other esters were negligible. This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the weak hydrogen-bond-donating ability of ester functional groups, combined with their flexible aliphatic chains, which hinder the formation of stable complexes.

At this stage, it can be concluded that differences in EGCG leaching and the dissolution of EGCG-bound aroma compounds during brewing may play a key role. These factors likely contribute to the more intense aroma characteristics observed in tea infusions prepared using the TB method. Moreover, due to the varying binding strengths between EGCG and different aroma compounds, selective retention and release behaviors may occur, ultimately leading to differences in aroma composition and sensory perception.

Ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy analysis during the interaction process

The binding between matrix components and aroma compounds typically involved both covalent and non-covalent interactions (Wang et al., 2025). To gain a deeper understanding of these interactions at the molecular level, UV-Vis was employed as a reliable method for studying intermolecular effects (Zhu et al., 2023).

As shown in Fig. 7A–E, the main peak of the UV-Vis spectrum of EGCG was located at 274 nm, which was attributed to the π→π∗ electron transition change of catechol in its structure. The absorption valley around 245 nm was caused by the conjugation interruption between the EGCG cyclic structure and the ester group. The shoulder peak around 300 nm corresponded to the n→π∗ transition of gallate in the cyclic structure (Wang et al., 2020). The addition of geraniol, nonanal, and 2-pentylfuran resulted in only a slight increase in the absorption intensity at 274 nm. This minor spectral perturbation suggests that for these ligands, the interaction may primarily involve the hydrophobic microenvironment around the EGCG aromatic rings, without significantly altering its electronic transition energy. In contrast, methyl salicylate and α-ionone induced a significant red shift, broadening, and enhancement of the absorption peak of EGCG. This reflects an increase in the conjugation degree of EGCG, enhanced electron delocalization in the system, and an increased energy difference between the excited and ground states, leading to a red shift in the absorption peak of the π→π∗ transition (Zhang et al., 2019). These findings indicate that in the presence of aroma compounds, the vibrations of characteristic groups in the EGCG structure are affected, showing significant differences. This suggests the existence of binding interactions between EGCG and the aroma compounds.Fig. 7UV spectra (A-E), fluorescence spectra (F-J), and Fourier transform infrared spectra (K-O) of EGCG before and after binding with selected aroma compounds.Fig. 7

Fluorescence spectroscopy analysis during the interaction process

EGCG is also a fluorescent molecule, and its fluorescence intensity exhibits significant solvent-dependent changes (Snitsarev et al., 2013). Similar to the results obtained from UV-Vis spectroscopy, the influence of geraniol, nonanal, and 2-pentylfuran on the fluorescence spectrum of EGCG was primarily manifested as an increase in fluorescence intensity (Fig. 7F–J). This can be attributed to the increased rigidity of the EGCG molecule after binding interactions, which restricts the vibrational freedom of the benzene ring, thereby reducing the non-radiative transition rate and causing changes in fluorescence intensity (Snitsarev et al., 2013). This fluorescence enhancement suggests that the interactions formed with these aroma compounds mainly impose conformational constraints on EGCG, without substantially altering the polarity of the local microenvironment surrounding the fluorophore. Methyl salicylate and α-ionone not only enhanced the fluorescence of EGCG, but also induced a blue shift in the fluorescence emission. This combined effect indicates that the fluorophore is embedded in a lower-polarity microenvironment, likely due to specific interactions such as hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking that cooperatively stabilize the excited-state π∗ orbitals and further constrain the fluorophore's surroundings. These results demonstrate that the impact of aroma compounds on the EGCG microenvironment is highly structure-dependent, leading to distinct binding modes based on the ligand's functional groups and conjugation.

FTIR spectroscopy analysis during the interaction process

The chemical structures of EGCG and its complexes were characterized by FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 7K–O). The results indicated that EGCG exhibits a strong absorption peak at approximately 3400 cm−1 (O-H stretching vibration), suggesting that EGCG is rich in hydroxyl groups (Zhang and Zhao, 2015). Other characteristic absorption peaks of EGCG were observed at 1690 cm−1 (C=O stretching vibration), 1610 cm−1 to 1450 cm−1 (ring stretching vibration), 1340 cm−1 to 1200 cm−1 (C-O stretching vibration), and 1130 cm−1 (C-H stretching vibration in the benzene ring), among others. After the combination of EGCG and aroma compounds, these peaks showed varying degrees of attenuation or enhancement. In the EGCG and α-ionone system, the intensified absorption peak at 1252 cm−1 (assigned to C-O stretching of EGCG's phenolic hydroxyl) indicated the formation of C=O⋯H-O hydrogen bonding between the carbonyl group of α-ionone and the hydroxyl groups of EGCG. This observation suggests that carbonyl functional groups can serve as effective hydrogen bond acceptors in stabilizing EGCG–aroma complexes, thereby enhancing the contribution of hydrogen bonding in the overall interaction. In the EGCG–methyl salicylate system, enhanced and shifted peaks at 1213 cm−1 (asymmetric C-O-C stretching of ester) and 1154 cm−1 (C-O stretching of phenolic hydroxyl) demonstrated synergistic hydrogen bonding interactions, in which EGCG acts as both hydrogen bond donor and acceptor (Wang et al., 2019), thereby modifying the original vibrational modes. Furthermore, in all mixed systems, the absorption intensity in the 1500-1470 cm−1 range (benzene ring skeletal vibrations) (Zhang et al., 2015) was markedly higher than that of EGCG alone. This enhancement reflects perturbations in the conjugated electron cloud of EGCG's benzene ring induced by interactions with aroma ligands, such as hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking, which alter the local electronic environment of the aromatic rings and increase the change in dipole moment during vibration, thereby resulting in enhanced infrared absorption intensity. These results demonstrate that intermolecular interactions between EGCG and aroma compounds modify the vibrational characteristics of specific functional groups, providing a foundation for further exploration of their interaction mechanisms.

1H NMR spectroscopy analysis during the interaction process

1H NMR spectroscopy was employed to probe changes in the local chemical environment of protons upon complex formation between EGCG and the aroma compounds. Comparative analysis showed that the chemical shifts of geraniol, methyl salicylate, 2-pentylfuran, and EGCG exhibited noticeable changes after mixing, whereas the changes observed for nonanal and α-ionone were relatively minor (Fig. 8). For geraniol, the hydrogen-bonding interaction with EGCG caused the signal of the -CH2-OH group (δ∼3.91 ppm) to change from a triplet to a doublet, suggesting a change in the local coupling environment and proton exchange dynamics, in which the coupling between the hydroxyl proton and the adjacent methylene protons becomes averaged out and only vicinal coupling remains observable. The resonance signals of other protons were attenuated, which may be attributed to restricted molecular motion caused by the formation of aggregates through π-π stacking interactions. (Gigl et al., 2021). For nonanal, a slight upfield shift of the α-methylene protons was observed. This shielding effect indicates that for aliphatic aldehydes, the protons are primarily influenced by the hydrophobic microenvironment or the π-electron clouds of EGCG, suggesting that hydrophobic interactions occupy a higher position in its binding hierarchy. In the case of α-ionone, the H-2 proton (δ∼6.00 ppm) exhibited a downfield shift, likely due to the deshielding effect induced by C=O⋯H-O hydrogen bonding, which is further amplified by the conjugated system. Meanwhile, most other protons shifted slightly upfield, which may be associated with shielding effects arising from hydrophobic interactions with EGCG. For methyl salicylate, the phenolic proton (δ ∼10.50 ppm) shifted upfield upon addition of EGCG, suggesting that the original intramolecular O–H⋯O hydrogen bond was perturbed by the intermolecular interactions with EGCG. Concurrently, the aromatic protons (δ 6.50–8.00 ppm) shifted downfield due to π-π stacking-induced deshielding, and methyl ester protons (δ ∼3.90 ppm) shift downfield under C=O⋯H–O hydrogen bonding influence (Gigl et al., 2021). This reflects a multifaceted binding mode where both hydrogen bonding and aromatic stacking coexist, though their contribution to macro-stability may be limited by steric factors. Notably, the α-H proton on the furan ring (δ ∼7.48 ppm) of 2-pentylfuran exhibited an upfield shift, which can be attributed to shielding effects arising from π-π stacking interactions with the aromatic rings of EGCG. This observation suggests that the furan ring serves as the primary interaction site, while the non-polar pentyl chain mainly contributes through hydrophobic interactions. Together, these results indicate that the binding of 2-pentylfuran to EGCG is dominated by hydrophobic interactions and π-π stacking, with minimal involvement of hydrogen bonding due to the weak hydrogen-bond-accepting ability of the furan ring. Overall, these results indicate that EGCG interacts with different aroma compounds through a combination of hydrogen bonding, π-π stacking, and hydrophobic interactions, with the dominant binding mode and binding stability being strongly dependent on molecular structure, functional group polarity, and the relative contribution of different interaction forces.Fig. 81H NMR spectra of aroma compounds in the presence and absence of EGCG. Density functional theory analysis during the interaction process

To analyze the binding and interaction behavior between EGCG and aroma compounds at the microscopic level, the binding strength between the complexes was analyzed using density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Conclusions

This study systematically analyzed the aroma characteristics of green tea infusion under different brewing temperatures and methods, demonstrating that the brewing process significantly affects the aroma profile of the tea infusion. Among the water temperatures tested, 85 °C was found to be optimal, as it yielded the richest variety of aroma compounds and a higher intensity, resulting in a more pronounced fragrance. Based on this temperature, a systematic comparison was conducted between the tea brewed using the top- and bottom-brewing methods. The results confirmed the initial hypothesis of this study, showing that each brewing method imparts distinct characteristics to the tea infusion. Tea brewed using the TB method exhibited significantly higher OAVs for aroma compounds, with a strong fresh and floral fragrance, but also a relatively pronounced bitterness. In contrast, tea brewed using the BB method had a milder bitterness and a stronger chestnut-like aroma. Characteristic aroma compounds such as geraniol, nonanal, and methyl salicylate were identified as key markers distinguishing the two brewing methods.

In order to elucidate the reasons and potential mechanisms behind the differences in aroma characteristics of tea infusions under TB and BB methods from the perspective of the interactions between tea matrix components and aroma compounds, the content of major polyphenolic substances was first measured. EGCG was found to be the dominant polyphenolic compound and an important determinant of the taste characteristics of tea infusion. Selected as target aroma compounds, geraniol, nonanal, α-ionone, methyl salicylate, and 2-pentylfuran exhibited significant binding interactions with EGCG, reducing their volatile release and increasing their olfactory thresholds in aqueous solution. Based on the higher content of EGCG and aroma compounds in tea infusion prepared by the TB method, it can be inferred that the combined effect of EGCG and aroma compounds promotes the migration of more aroma compounds into the tea infusion during brewing, resulting in a richer aroma profile. Furthermore, spectroscopic analysis revealed that different aroma compounds bind to EGCG via distinct interaction modes. Hydrogen bonding was primary binding force. During the binding process of EGCG with geraniol and α-ionone, the presence of π-π stacking and hydrophobic interactions was also observed. The binding of EGCG with 2-pentylfuran primarily relied on π-π stacking and hydrophobic interactions. In the complex of EGCG with methyl salicylate, three types of interactions, namely hydrogen bonding, π-π stacking, and hydrophobic interactions, were identified.

In summary, the systematic analysis conducted in this study on the aroma characteristics of tea infusions under different brewing methods indicated that brewing method is a crucial factor determining the aroma quality of tea infusions. In addition, based on the findings of this study, consumers can choose the appropriate brewing method according to their respective tea drinking preferences, providing important guidance strategies for brewing operations. The analysis of the combined effect of EGCG and aroma compounds not only validated the hypothesis of this study but also highlighted the important role of the tea infusion matrix in determining aroma quality. This also provided an important guiding reference for enhancing the sensory quality of tea infusion flavor from the perspective of matrix components. While our study focused on the interaction between EGCG and aroma compounds, we acknowledge that other catechins may also play a role in aroma release, and only one tea cultivar was used. Future researches should explore the effects of additional catechins, examine the interactions in more complex tea infusion matrices, and consider the dynamic characteristics of tea extract under different brewing methods, so as to better understand and control the flavor quality of tea infusions.
