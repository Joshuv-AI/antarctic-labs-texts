Current perspectives on neuroendocrine tumors
Authors: Sunil Kumar Verma, Renu Khare, Devendra Singh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeoplasms arising from neuroendocrine cells form a heterogeneous group known as neuroendocrine tumors (NETs), which possess both endocrine and neural characteristics. These tumors can occur in various organs throughout the body, with the most prominent sites being the gastrointestinal tract, pancreas, and lungs. Despite their relatively low incidence, NETs have gained significant attention due to their unique biology and clinical behavior. This review intends to provide a widespread gestalt of the present perception of NETs, including their epidemiology, etiology, pathogenesis, classification, clinical presentation, diagnostic modalities, treatment options, and prognosis. Treatment strategies for NETs depend on tumor grade, stage, location, and functional status. Surgical resection remains the pillar of curative treatment for localized disease; on the other hand, systemic therapies take account of targeted therapies like tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT), somatostatin analogs, and immunotherapy have shown promising results in advanced cases. In conclusion, this review provides an up-to-date summary of our current knowledge regarding neuroendocrine tumors. Further research is needed to better understand the underlying molecular mechanisms driving tumor development and progression. This will aid in developing novel therapeutic strategies targeting specific pathways involved in NET pathogenesis to improve patient outcomes.Keywords: cancer, clinical therapy, metastasis, endocrine

Introduction

When healthy cells have their DNA broken, they start to grow and alter hysterically, consequential in mass formation identified as a tumor [1]. A tumor can be malignant or benign. If a tumor is benign, it will not spread and will grow only. Being malignant, a cancerous tumor has the potential to develop and spread to other body areas if treatment is delayed and it is not discovered early [2,3]. Typically, removing a benign tumor doesn't do much damage. The specialized cells of the body's neuroendocrine system are the starting point of neuroendocrine cancer, also known as a neuroendocrine neoplasm or neuroendocrine tumor (NET). These cells combine characteristics of nerve cells with endocrine cells that produce hormones. They exist in every organ in the body and aid in regulating several bodily processes. Hormones are chemical compounds that go across the circulation and function of other bodily organs or cells [4,5]. Genetics and epigenetic mechanisms for endocrine tumor formation

Certain epigenetic and genetic anomalies are the cause of endocrine tumors, which include NETs, pituitary adenomas, and prostate malignancies. The genetic mechanisms, which are pivotal in the pathogenesis of these malignancies, encompass chromosomal translocations that serve to activate dormant proto-oncogenes. Additionally, point mutations within these genes, exemplified by the G and Ras protein families, engender constitutive signaling. The pivotal role of tumor suppressor genes, such as p53, in curbing cell growth is frequently undermined by their deactivation, while E26 transformation-specific (ETS) transcription factor-related gene fusions contribute to the neoplastic phenotype. Notably, in prostate cancer, mutations in chromatin remodeling factors, including CDH1 and SPOP, have been implicated in disease progression.

Parallel to genetic alterations, epigenetic mechanisms play a crucial role in modulating gene expression, with abnormalities therein leading to the dysregulation of cellular functions. For instance, the upregulation of truncated somatostatin receptor variants, such as sst5TMD4, can confer resistance to somatostatin analog treatments, thereby complicating therapeutic interventions.

Beyond the genetic and epigenetic landscapes, other mechanisms contribute to the neoplastic transformation. Growth factor signaling and hormonal stimulation are instrumental in fostering the development and metastasis of tumors. Furthermore, the acquisition of epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity and stem cell-like characteristics, particularly evident in aggressive neuroendocrine prostate tumors, highlights the complexity of the disease process. In conclusion, anomalies in the genetic, epigenetic, and signaling pathways interact intricately to cause endocrine tumors by upsetting normal cellular homeostasis and encouraging unchecked growth.

Neuroendocrine tumors classification

NETs can be categorized or defined in a variety of ways, such as by their location, the appearance of the tumor cells, and the rate at which the cells divide and proliferate. In addition, NETs can be classified as “functional” or “nonfunctional”. Functional NETs produce and release additional symptom-causing chemicals, such as hormones. A “functional” NET is indicated by carcinoid syndrome, which can result in the release of serotonin, which can induce flushing and diarrhea, while nonfunctional NETs either don't release any compounds at all or don't release enough for symptoms to appear. Approximately sixty percent of NETs are not operational. Since NETs don't exhibit any symptoms, they are often identified [6,7].

Anything that makes someone more likely to get a tumor is considered a risk factor. However, maximum risk factors may not unswervingly lead to cancer; they often impact the growth of a tumor. Certain individuals who have many risk factors do not ever have a tumor, whereas others have no identified risk factors [8]. We don't know what causes the majority of NETs, and there are no recognized preventable risk factors [9]. The risk of developing NETs can be greatly affected by environmental as well as lifestyle factors. The first of these factors is exposure to environmental toxins, which encompasses a range of harmful substances such as insecticides, heavy metals, and air pollutants. These toxins have the potential to disrupt the normal physiological processes within cells, thereby increasing the risk of tumorigenesis. The second factor relates to stress and mental wellness. Prolong stress and mental health disorders can have a profound impact on the immune system, leading to its impairment and a subsequent increase in systemic inflammation. This inflammatory environment is conducive to the development of tumors, as it can facilitate the growth and spread of neoplastic cells. Socioeconomic position is another critical determinant of NET susceptibility. Individuals with lower socioeconomic status may face a higher risk of developing NETs due to a combination of factors, including poor lifestyle choices, increased exposure to environmental pollutants, and limited access to quality healthcare services. Lastly, lifestyle factors play a significant role in the etiology of NETs. A sedentary lifestyle, coupled with behaviors such as smoking and obesity, can lead to chronic inflammation and DNA damage. These conditions create a fertile ground for the initiation and progression of NETs, highlighting the importance of a healthy lifestyle in reducing the risk of these malignancies.

The formation of NETs is a multifaceted process influenced by genetic and hormonal factors, but by addressing modifiable environmental and lifestyle factors, individuals can potentially lower their risk of developing such tumors [9].

Symptoms and signs

Since NETs lack a consistent set of symptoms, they might be diagnosed for a variety of reasons [10,11]. Signs and symptoms used together might aid in the description of a medical issue. A NET frequently exhibits no symptoms in the early stages and can only be discovered by a distinct X-ray or during surgery to treat another ailment. If an individual with a NET experiences signs, for instance, carcinoid syndrome, then symptoms can be related to the tumor's size, location, or secretion of hormones [[12], [13], [14]]. The subsequent tumor-related symptoms may be present in a person with a particular form of NET. Neuroendocrine tumors can cause a variety of symptoms, depending on the type, location, and hormone production. Common symptoms include pain from a growing tumor, weight loss, fatigue, a growing lump under the skin, skin rash, increased thirst, frequent urination, skin flushing, diarrhea, dizziness, shakiness, nausea, vomiting, changes in bowel habits, jaundice, unusual bleeding, facial flushing, hyperglycemia, hypoglycemia, ulcer disease, confusion, anxiety, and nutritional deficiencies [15]. Carcinoid syndrome, a functioning neuroendocrine tumor, can cause symptoms such as facial flushing, perspiration, diarrhea, shortness of breath, weakness, rapid heartbeat, high blood pressure, and carcinoid heart disease. It is important to note that not all symptoms signal cancer; therefore, consulting a healthcare expert for proper examination is critical [16,17]—the most prevalent manifestation of a functioning NET in patients with small intestines. The tumor in carcinoid syndrome produces serotonin, which may contribute to symptoms [14]. When serotonin is converted to 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA), it may be quantified most conveniently and accurately in the blood or urine. Some facilities may also be able to screen for 5-HIAA in blood. Carcinoid syndrome does not always emerge in NET patients [18,19].

How the neuroendocrine tumor are diagnosed?

The majority of NETs are discovered by accident during X-rays or other medical procedures that are performed for purposes irrelevant to the tumor. Additionally, a NET may be diagnosed using the following tests to diagnose NET, i.e., a biopsy. The sample(s) taken during the biopsy are subsequently examined by a pathologist. A biopsy shouldn't be performed; if not, it's unconditionally required to approve a diagnosis if a pheochromocytoma is suspected. These tumors can provide a life-threatening risk for a biopsy [20]. The second one is blood/urine tests. To screen for hormone abnormal levels and chemicals, the doctor might need to take samples of your urine and blood. Tests on urine or blood (plasma) measure the concentration of 5-HIAA, a serotonin breakdown product produced by some NETs. Serotonin levels in blood could also be measured, albeit the results are less accurate because of variations. Following diagnosis, a blood test to quantify the protein chromogranin A, which is generated by tumor cells, may also be carried out. Chromogranin A is not a component of diagnostic tests; rather, it's a tumor marker rather than a hormone [21,22]. The third one is tumor biomarker testing. To find certain proteins, genes, and other characteristics peculiar to the tumor, a doctor could advise doing laboratory testing on a tumor. Sometimes, the test results might be used to guide the choice of treatments. Compared to other tumors, NETs get less benefit from this examination [7,23]. The fourth one is endoscopy. Endoscopy comes in a variety of forms; for example, upper endoscopy is used to inspect the stomach and esophagus, while colonoscopy is used to inspect the rectum and colon. The tube is carefully introduced into the body; the patient could be put to sleep. Medication for sedation is used to induce calmness, relaxation, or sleep. Should an anomaly be discovered, a biopsy should be carried out [6]. The fifth one is ultrasound. Sound waves are used in an ultrasound to provide an image of the interior organs. Normal tissue does not produce the same sound wave echoes as tumors do. Ultrasounds come in a variety of forms that are used to look at different body areas. A biopsy may be implemented in some circumstances [24]. The sixth one is the X-ray. It's a minimally invasive method of producing an image of the inside body structures by radiation exposure. The clinician may also suggest further scans since, occasionally, an X-ray may not reveal a NET because of its location or size. The physician may order a barium X-ray if they have reason to suspect a GI tract NET. A liquid that covers the walls of the stomach, intestines, and esophagus is swallowed by an individual [25]. Next, several X-rays are obtained. The seventh one is a computed tomography (CT or CAT) scan. The tumor size may be determined with a computed tomography scan. To determine whether a tumor has migrated to the liver, a computed tomography scan is also performed. In certain cases, a contrast medium—a unique dye—is administered before scanning in order to improve picture detail. The patient may get this dye intravenously, orally, or as a liquid or tablet to ingest [26]. The eighth one is magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The patient may get this dye intravenously, orally, or as a liquid or tablet to ingest [27,28]. And finally, nuclear medicine imaging. A small quantity of radioactive medication known as a tracer is put into the affected person's vein during this test. The areas of the body where radioactivity has accumulated are then visible through scanning. Nonetheless, the material has too little radiation to be dangerous. Nuclear imaging for NETs is done using positron emission tomography (PET)-CT scans. 18F fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG), copper-64 (64Cu) DOTATATE, and Gallium-68 (68Ga) DOTATATE are the three kinds of tracers utilized in a PET scan [[29], [30], [31]].

Somatostatin receptor imaging techniques such as 64Cu DOTATATE PET and 68Ga DOTATATE PET are mostly employed to examine slow-growing NETs (i.e., grades 1 and 2 and well-differentiated grade 3). These imaging agents target proteins called somatostatin receptors that are found on the surface of NET cells [32,33]. Cancerous areas shine like lightbulbs when the 64Cu or 68Ga binds with the somatostatin receptor, due to which an image is captured. Since it is more successful, the 68Ga DOTATATE PET scan has taken place of OctreoScan, a distinct nuclear imaging technique for NETs. 18F-FDG, another kind of PET scan, does not utilize the somatostatin receptor. NETs with rapid growth rates may utilize it sometimes. The findings of the diagnostic tests will be reviewed by the physician. These findings also aid the physician in characterizing the tumor if NET is the diagnosis. We refer to this as staging [34].

Well-differentiated NETs differ significantly from nonendocrine tumors in several important ways. NETs predominantly arise in the lungs and digestive system, contrasting with the varied distribution of non-endocrine tumors. Morphologically, NETs are characterized by a uniform appearance with organoid architecture and salt-and-pepper chromatin, in contrast to the diverse morphologies of non-endocrine tumors. Diagnostically, NETs are identified through immunohistochemistry, which reveals the expression of neuroendocrine markers such as chromogranin and synaptophysin, a profile not found in non-endocrine tumors. The proliferative rate of NETs is gauged by the Ki-67 index and mitotic count, a grading system not utilized for nonendocrine tumors. Therapeutically, NETs often express somatostatin receptors, enabling targeted imaging with 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT and treatment with somatostatin analogs. Conversely, nonendocrine tumors lack this specific receptor expression. While chemotherapy may be less effective for NETs, systemic therapies including mTOR inhibitors, peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitors are more successful. Depending on the particular type of tumor, therapeutic options for non-endocrine tumor therapeutic options for non-endocrine tumors may include immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies. In summary, NETs have distinct pathological characteristics, distribution patterns, immune profiles, and therapeutic approaches compared to non-endocrine tumors. Acknowledging and comprehending these differences is essential to accurate diagnosis and efficient treatment.

Stages of neuroendocrine tumors

When discussing NETs, the terms “stage” and “grade” can be used interchangeably, yet they refer to entirely distinct concepts when determining the risk a tumor provides to an individual. Staging for NETs is covered in this section. The cancer stage is determined by doctors through diagnostic testing. Therefore, staging could only be finalized once all of the tests are completed. The stage aids in the doctor's recommendation of the most appropriate course of action and helps forecast a patient's prognosis or likelihood of recovery. For every kind of NET, there are distinct stage descriptions [35]. On the grounds of the tumor's features and location, several staging methods are used for NETs of the pancreas and GI tract. A few NETs make use of the other cancers' staging methods. For instance, staging a lung NET and non–small cell lung (NSCL) carcinoma are the same. The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) developed these staging systems [9].

A NET may be classified by a physician according to its World Health Organization (WHO) grade (G). The grade aids the clinician in developing a NET treatment strategy. The following are the WHO NET grades, i.e. in low-grade tumors (grade 1), a low rate of cell divides consequently develops gradually, while in intermediate-grade tumors (grade 2), an intermediate rate of cell division and high-grade tumors/grade 3 fast rate of cell division consequently develop rapidly. The two markers of the rate of development and division of the tumor cells—the mitotic count and Ki-67—are utilized to determine the grade. To determine the mitotic count, the physician counts the total dividing cells visible in a certain area (2 mm2) under a microscope. The doctor also calculates the Ki-67 index. More of the protein Ki-67 is produced by cells that are ready to divide. Rapid cell proliferation is indicated by a high Ki-67 percentage in a particular area [22,36]. Treatment of neuroendocrine tumors

This section describes the therapies (sometimes called treatments) that make up a NET's standard of care. The greatest therapies now available are referred to as “standard of care” since they are frequently backed by significant data from prior clinical trials [14,38]. New pharmaceuticals, novel combinations of established therapies, or novel dosages of established medications or other therapies may be tested in clinical trials [39,38]. For cancer at all stages, clinical trials offer a possibility. A patient's complete treatment plan, which combines several therapies are often developed in conjunction with several medical specializations when it comes to cancer care. For those who have a NET in particular, it is crucial to form a multidisciplinary team. The typical methods of treatment utilized for NET are illustrated below [40].

Surgery

The mainstay of treatment for locoregional NETs is surgery, which may be curative in cases of complete resection (R0). The precise position and features of the NET serve as guidance for the surgical intervention selection. Endoscopic excision may be a viable option for early, well-differentiated NETs that have a minimal risk of lymph node dissemination rather than surgery. Local resection, which involves partial excision without lymph node dissection can be used to address lesions that are difficult for endoscopic resection [41]. Enterectomy and lymph node dissection are usually required as part of the treatment plan for NETs with suspected involvement of lymph nodes. Cytoreductive surgery is a useful tool for managing hormone hypersecretion and symptom relief in cases of metastatic illness [42]. For NETs, minimally invasive surgical techniques such as laparoscopic or robotic-assisted surgery are being used more frequently; nonetheless, there is a dearth of information comparing these techniques to open surgery. For NET patients, comprehensive preoperative and perioperative care is crucial. This includes managing hormone-related syndromes, keeping a close eye out for carcinoid crises, and preventing problems such as pancreatic fistulas after surgery [31,43]. A physician with expertise in cancer surgery is called a surgical oncologist. When possible, the standard is to remove the whole tumor. Surgery alone is an effective treatment for the majority of localized NETs. To ensure there are no signs of cancer remaining in the body, the surgeon will often amputate a margin—a portion of tissue surrounding the tumor—when performing surgery [13]. In situations where total tumor removal is not feasible, “debulking surgery” may be suggested. Debulking surgery aims to remove the most amount of the tumor and may offer some symptom alleviation, but in most cases, it is not a cure for NETs [44].

Therapies Using Medication

It is possible to provide medication to cancer cells all over the body through circulation. Systemic therapy is the term used to describe this kind of pharmaceutical administration. In addition, medication can be applied locally, meaning it can be stored in a specific body part or applied directly to the cancer. The doctor who prescribes this treatment is typically an oncologist. Medication can be injected intramuscularly (IM), intravenously (IV) (by inserting a needle into a vein), or orally (by ingesting a pill or capsule). Depending on the type of NET, medications that can be prescribed comprise PRRT, chemotherapy, somatostatin analogs, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. Patients may receive multiple medications at once or just one type of medication at a time. They can also be used in conjunction with radiation therapy or surgery as part of a treatment plan [45].

Somatostatin Analogs

The hormone somatostatin regulates the release of many other hormones in the body, including glucagon and insulin [46]. Medication called a somatostatin analog, which is comparable to somatostatin, is used to manage the symptoms brought on by the hormone-mimicking substances secreted by NET. They don't usually cause the tumors to shrink, but they may also inhibit the NET growth. Lanreotide (Somatuline Depot) and octreotide (Sandostatin) are the two somatostatin analogs used in the treatment of NETs. There are two ways to administer octreotide: subcutaneously (under the skin) for short-acting and intramuscularly (injected into the muscle) for long-acting. A subcutaneous long-acting injection of lanreotide is administered. The most frequent adverse effects include gallstone formation, elevated blood sugar (hyperglycemia), and moderate gastrointestinal distress, such as nausea and bloating [47,48].

Targeted Therapy

Targeted therapy is a type of treatment that targets certain tissues, proteins, or gene environments that the tumor needs in order to proliferate and survive. This type of treatment lessens damage to healthy cells while stopping cancerous cells from proliferating and spreading. Different cancers have different targets. In order to decide the best course of action, doctors may do tests to identify the proteins, genes along other components of the tumor. It allows medical professionals to match patients with the best possible treatment plan when it is practical [49]. Additionally, studies to find out more about specific targets (molecular) and new targeted treatments for them are still being carried out. The USA Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has authorized everolimus (Afinitor), a targeted medication in support of the treatment of advanced NETs of the pancreas, lung, and gastrointestinal system. This medication targets the mTOR protein, which is critical for the survival and proliferation of cells. Although this medication often does not cause tumors to shrink, it can help certain patients' cancers grow more slowly. Fatigue, decreased blood counts, and mouth sores are some of the side effects [50]. A protein known as VEGF is the target of the targeted treatment sunitinib (Sutent). Angiogenesis, the process of creating new blood vessels, depends on VEGF. Antiangiogenesis treatments, such as sunitinib, aim to “starve” tumors since they depend on blood arteries to supply nutrients for tumor growth and dissemination. Treatment for advanced pancreatic NETs is approved by the FDA. This medication frequently causes lethargy, vomiting, diarrhea, nausea, and elevated blood pressure as adverse effects. Clinical trials are being used to investigate further targeted therapeutics for NETs. These include medications that obstruct the new blood vessel or certain routes of development by which cancer cells survive [51].

Peptide Receptor Radionuclide Therapy

A pancreatic NET and advanced GI tract NET therapy known as 177Lu-DOTATATE (Lutathera) was authorized by the FDA in 2018. When GI tract NETs and pancreatic NETs are united, they are commonly stated as “GEP NETs” [44]. Other NETs are also being investigated for therapy with this medication. It is a radioactive medication that attaches itself to somatostatin receptors on cells, which is found in certain cancers. The medication penetrates the cell after attaching to the receptor, enabling radiation to harm the tumor cells [52]. PRRT is the general name used to refer to this course of treatment. Low white blood cell counts, elevated enzyme levels in various organs, vomiting, nausea, and exhaustion are typical adverse effects [53,54].

Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy is the process of giving medication to kill cancer cells, usually by stopping their growth, multiplication, or division. A chemotherapy regimen, often known as a plan, usually entails a set number of cycles given over a predetermined period. A sufferer may receive one medicine or multiple medications given at the same time. In cases when a NET has metastasized to other organs, presents with significant symptoms, or when hormonal or targeted treatments are no longer effective, chemotherapy could be exercised. Chemotherapy can be administered either on its own or in permutation or arrangement with other therapies [25]. Chemotherapy's side effects might include exhaustion, infection risk, vomiting, nausea, hair loss, diarrhea, and lack of appetite. These symptoms show discrepancies varying between the patient and the dosage administered. Hair loss is not always the outcome of cancer therapies. Typically, these adverse reactions dissipate following the completion of the treatment regimen [55].

Immunotherapy

By leveraging the body's natural defenses against the illness, immunotherapy (IT) strengthens the immune system's ability to fight cancer cells. Intron A (interferon alfa-2b), a type of IT, has been utilized in the past to treat neural epithelial cells. Interferon can reduce diarrhea and flushing and improve the functioning of the immune system. Tumors may shrink as well. These days, this therapy is rarely utilized because there are more recent FDA-approved options [56].

Radiation Therapy

Tumor cells are destroyed during radiation therapy (RT) by high-energy X-rays or other particles. A radiation therapy regimen, sometimes known as a program, typically comprises a set number of cures given over a predetermined period. External-beam RT is the predominant type of radiation treatment utilized in the cases of NET, which involves applying radiation to the body's surface via a machine [6]. It is most frequently used in conjunction with supportive treatment to address symptoms, for instance, discomfort that is brought on by a tumor that has progressed to the individual bone and also to the other parts of the body. Patients may experience fatigue, dryness, and redness in the treated area after radiation therapy. A dry cough or a painful, dry throat might result from radiation treatment to the neck or chest. Breathlessness occurs in certain people receiving radiation therapy. After the course of therapy, the majority of adverse effects disappear [57].

Liver-directed Treatment

Several techniques for liver-directed treatment for NETs exist to manage liver metastases caused by neuroendocrine neoplasms. Percutaneous ablation, endovascular therapies, and interventional radiology techniques are some of the available options. They are safe and effective choices for patients with NET-related hepatic metastases who cannot undergo surgery. The primary goals of these treatments are disease control, clinical symptom alleviation, and targeting tumor-related biochemical abnormalities [58]. Toxicology and quality of life are also considered when making therapeutic recommendations. In cases of resectable neuroendocrine liver metastases, liver surgery with the goal of curing is regarded as the best treatment option. This seeks to increase survival as well as enhance hormonal problems that are resistant to medical treatment. Overall, liver-directed therapy, together with systemic molecular targeted medicines and chemotherapy, plays a significant role in the multimodal care of patients [7].

The following therapies may be implemented for cancer if it has progressed to the liver. These treatments often need a hospital stay and are carried out by a skilled interventional radiologist. For a few days following therapy, side effects comprise discomfort surrounding the liver and elevated liver enzyme levels as determined by blood tests [58,59].

Radiofrequency Ablation

Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) uses an electric current to heat a tumor until it is destroyed. It is ineffective against bigger tumors and is often used for minor liver metastases [60,61]. RFA is an approach for treating pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (P-NETs). Thermal ablation is used to generate high temperatures and produces coagulative necrosis within the tumor. RFA has been proposed as a potentially useful and safe treatment for P-NETs, particularly small tumors. Studies have demonstrated that endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided RFA can be considered a therapeutic method for bigger P-NETs when surgery is not an option, albeit its success may vary. Larger trials are required to confirm the efficacy of EUS-RFA and its influence on tumor size [34]. RFA is one of several ablative treatments used to eradicate tumors that are less than 2 cm in size. Ablation therapies include microwave thermotherapy, RFA, and alcohol ablation. RFA, for example, uses radio waves with high energy to heat and destroy cancer cells inside a tumor. These treatments are commonly used to shrink tumors and relieve symptoms, although they are unlikely to cure cancer on their own. RFA is especially beneficial when pancreatic NETs have migrated to other locations, such as the liver [44,62].

Hepatic Artery Embolization

By cutting off the blood arteries that supply the tumor, this technique stops the tumor's blood supply. Bland embolization is the term used for embolization that is done on its own. It is denoted as chemoembolization when paired with chemotherapy. It is represented as radioembolization when paired with radiation treatment. The relative effectiveness of different embolization treatments is yet unknown [63]. While radioembolization may have long-term negative effects, most NET treatment facilities choose alternative courses of care. Patients with disorders that are mainly metastatic to the liver are usually given these medications, particularly if their symptoms are caused by the tumor's development or hormone production [64].

For functional NETs, controlling symptoms is essential, while for nonfunctional NETs, tumor load is a major factor in therapy selection. This method gives medical professionals a well-organized framework to select the best course of action for every patient according to the unique features of their NET. If Treatment Does Not Work

Not all cases of NET recovery are recoverable. The illness may be referred to as advanced and incurable if the tumor is neither treatable nor managed. An advanced NET might be difficult to discuss for some individuals, and receiving this diagnosis can be upsetting. It's crucial to communicate your feelings, opinions, and concerns to your healthcare staff honestly and openly. Ensuring that an individual receives emotional support, is pain-free, and has decent physical comfort is crucial [65,66].

Clinical trials

Researchers and medical experts are always trying for more enhanced ways to treat individuals with NETs. Doctors conduct research with volunteers called clinical trials to advance science. Every drug that the US FDA has currently approved has been tested through clinical trials. Clinical trials are used to treat NETs of all stages and types. Numerous focuses on novel therapies, trying to ascertain whether they are safe, effective, and even better than those that are currently on the market. These trials assess novel drugs, several amalgamations of cures, new methods for therapy (radiation) or surgery, as well as new therapeutic strategies [28]. Therapy may be administered to clinical trial volunteers prior to its release to the general community. Risks are also associated with studies (clinical), though, together with the probability of unfavorable outcomes and the possibility that the innovative cure won't work. The advantages and disadvantages of taking part in a certain study should be discussed with one's health care physician. Research tactics for managing side effects that may appear years or even decades after medication are investigated in clinical studies, along with innovative methods for treating symptoms and side effects [67].

Latest Research

Medical professionals are attempting to increase their knowledge about NETs, including how to prevent and treat patients with this kind of tumor. Clinical trials may open up new choices for patients in the following scientific areas.

Immunotherapy. Immunotherapy boosts the body's immune system against cancer by strengthening the immune system's capacity to target cancerous cells, as explained under the Treatment section. On NETs, researchers are testing novel cancer vaccines [32]. A separate area of immunotherapy research is looking into drugs that block the programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) protein. A subpopulation of white blood cells called T cells aids the immune system in fighting disease. They are covered in PD-1. Inhibiting PD-1's activity increases the immune system's capacity to eradicate the disease since PD-1 stops the body from getting rid of tumor cells [31]. Programmed cell death-1 ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitor atezolizumab (Tecentriq), available as a generic drug has been approved by the FDA for treating metastatic small-cell lung cancer when combined with carboplatin and etoposide treatment. Atezolizumab's effectiveness emphasizes the need to research immunotherapy, especially for lung NET patients, even though lung NETs are distinctly different conditions [68]. Furthermore, research is being done on the use of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy in conjunction with antibody-drug conjugates, a form of targeted therapy. For CAR T-cell therapy, specific T cells are taken out of the patient's blood. Following this, the cells are altered to create distinct proteins called receptors. These modified T cells can recognize cancer cells due to the receptors. Subsequently, the modified T cells are returned to the patient's body. Upon arrival, they target and destroy malignant cells [69].

Targeted therapy and combined treatments. Targeted treatment is still being researched, as mentioned under the Treatment section. One kind of targeted treatment that impedes tumor development in several ways is monoclonal antibodies [70]. Some prevent tumor cells from proliferating and spreading. Some locate tumor cells and assist in their destruction or deliver agents capable of killing tumors to them [71].

Chemotherapy. Researchers are working to create “smart” chemotherapy that targets only tumor cells and leaves healthy cells alone since NETs have certain characteristics that healthy cells do not. A possible method of treating NETs and other tumors is the creation of “smart” chemotherapy, mainly using ADCs along with smart nanoparticles. These treatments try to deliver chemotherapy far more efficiently and safely, minimizing side effects and enhancing patient outcomes, by focusing on particular traits of cancer cells [72,73].

Genetics. A person's likelihood of acquiring a NET can be influenced by their family history of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1), as mentioned in risk factors. To discover more about MEN1 and other inherited risk factors, in addition to the part gene mutations or alterations play in NETs, researchers are delving further into this protein [74].

PRRT. On ways to enhance PRRT for NETs, research is in progress. Research is being conducted on how to enhance the capacity of tumor cells to bind to radiopeptides, how to employ PRRT to remove minute amounts of a NET, in addition to how to lower the amount of radiation that reaches other organs [16].

Supportive Care/Palliative Care

Clinical trials are being carried out to find more efficient ways to reduce signs and adverse effects of current NET therapies to improve patients' comfort as well as the quality of individual life [62,75].

Coping with treatment. Every NET therapy has the potential to alter your body's physiology and psychological state. For a variety of reasons, people may not experience the same side effects even when receiving the same medication for the same sort of cancer. Because of this, it may not be easy to gauge how you will feel during therapy [76,77].

Coping with physical side effects. The Treatment of Neuroendocrine Tumors section describes the typical physical adverse effects associated with each NET therapy method. Overall health, the duration and dosage of therapy, the stage and grade of the tumor, as well as other factors, all affect how your body will change. Discuss your feelings with your medical staff regularly [78]. Notifying them of any new adverse modifications or effects to current side effects is crucial. If they are aware of how the individual is feeling, then they can discover ways to manage and alleviate the individual side effects so they can feel comfortable and stop any negative effects from growing worse [79].

Coping with social and emotional effects. Patients might have social and emotional fallout after learning they have NET. This could mean learning how to manage their stress level or a variety of emotions, such as worry, grief, and rage. Sometimes, it's difficult for people to express their true feelings to the people they care about. Talking to a counselor, cancer social worker, or clergy member could help some individuals come up with better coping strategies and topics of talk regarding their diagnosis. In the future, treatment strategies for obesity and biofilms will play a crucial role in the fight against this illness [75,80].

Coping with the costs of cancer care. The cost of treating a NET might be high. Anxiety and worry related to costs may be experienced by families and patients. Beyond the expense of their therapy, many patients find they have unanticipated costs related to their care [66]. Because medical care can be very expensive, some patients need help to complete their treatment programs. Their health could be at risk, and future costs would go up as a consequence. It is recommended that families and patients discuss financial issues with a member of their healthcare team [22,81,82].

Emerging research areas

Since next-generation sequencing studies have identified important mutations and changes in genes, including DAXX, ATRX, PTEN, MEN1, and mTOR pathway genes, the use of genomic profiling has become essential in the management of NETs. These genetic findings have been crucial in differentiating between NET subtypes and offering insightful information about prognosis [83]. For example, MEN1 mutation-positive pancreatic NETs are often less aggressive, whereas PTEN/PI3K pathway changes are more aggressive. Up to 50% of patients with advanced NETs have potentially targetable genetic changes, which can be identified using genomic profiling to help select targeted therapy [84]. Research is being done on noninvasive liquid biopsy techniques that use circulating tumor DNA to track genomic alterations and treatment outcomes in NETs. The role of immunotherapy in NETs is presently the subject of intensive research. Although it was always thought that NETs were immune system-insensitive, new research suggests that some NET subtypes might react favorably to immune checkpoint inhibitors [85]. When treating high-grade neuroendocrine carcinomas with a high tumor mutational burden, pembrolizumab has shown promising results. To further improve the antitumor immune response in NETs, combined strategies that combine immunotherapy with additional targeted drugs or radiation therapy are being researched [74].

By combining genetic, molecular, and clinical data, therapy regimens for NETs are being more precisely tailored. Chromogranin A, 5-HIAA, and Ki-67 are examples of biomarkers that help with treatment selection and response tracking. More complex multi-gene assays, such as the NETest, provide a more comprehensive assessment of NET properties and can assist in predicting treatment outcomes [86]. Targeted treatments or clinical trials that correspond with the unique genetic alterations driving each patient's tumor can be found with the use of molecular profiling. Individualized, tumor-specific therapeutic techniques are being made possible by theranostic procedures that combine peptide receptor radionuclide therapy with somatostatin receptor-targeted imaging [87].

Concluding remarks and perspectives

The study of NETs has advanced significantly in recent years, mainly in terms of our comprehension of the genetic as well as molecular alterations that propel the growth and spread of NETs. The availability of vital research instruments, including organoid models and NETs cell lines have been essential in advancing our knowledge of NET biology and locating possible therapeutic targets. Still, there are a lot of unsolved issues and new trends that need to be looked into. Even though certain genetic and molecular changes have been found in some NET subtypes, a more thorough knowledge of the molecular landscape in all NET types is required. Larger-scale genomic along with transcriptomic research may be able to identify more driver mutations and signaling pathways that contribute to the formation of neural epithelial cells. Significant variability within and between tumors is a well-known characteristic of NETs. Analyzing the mechanisms of clonal evolution and the rise of subclones resistant to treatment may yield important insights for creating more successful customized therapeutic approaches.

There is rising interest in the role of epigenetic processes, including DNA methylation and histone changes, in the initiation and advancement of NETs. Examining the epigenetic landscape of NETs may lead to the discovery of novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Comprehending the intricate relationship between neural epithelial cells and the immune system, encompassing the possibilities of immune checkpoint inhibitors may facilitate the creation of novel immunotherapeutic strategies aimed at addressing NETs. By permitting earlier intervention and more efficient care, improving early detection of NETs—possibly through the use of liquid biopsy techniques or the identification of novel biomarkers—could have a substantial effect on patient outcomes. Treating NETs may be made more effective by investigating the synergistic potential of combining various therapy techniques, including immunotherapies, radionuclide treatments, and targeted therapies.

In conclusion, NETs are a diverse and complex group of neoplasms that can arise from various organs in the body. They present unique challenges in terms of diagnosis, treatment, and management due to their heterogeneity and potential for hormone production. Advances in imaging techniques, molecular profiling, and targeted therapies have improved our understanding and management of tumors. However, recent advancements in research have provided valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying these tumors, leading to the progress of novel diagnostic tools and targeted therapies. The identification of biomarkers and genetic mutations has also improved our understanding of tumor biology and prognosis. Additionally, ongoing clinical trials are evaluating promising new treatment options that can improve outcomes for patients with NET. While there is still much to learn about these rare malignancies, the latest research offers hope for more effective and personalized approaches to diagnosis and treatment in the future.
