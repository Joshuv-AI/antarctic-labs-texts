Changes in the frequency of food orders and food choices on meal delivery apps during the COVID-19 pandemic in a Brazilian metropolitan region
Authors: Laís Vargas Botelho, Daniela Silva Canella, Dayan Carvalho Ramos Salles de Carvalho, Paula Martins Horta, Maria Eduarda Ribeiro José, Leonardo Soares Bastos, Letícia de Oliveira Cardoso
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rise of the digital food environment, especially through meal delivery apps, reshapes food consumption habits amid convenience- and pandemic-driven changes. We aimed to describe changes in both frequency of food purchase and food choices made in meal delivery apps during the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsA web-based survey was conducted between May and September 2020 using virtual chain sampling with 986 adults residing in the metropolitan region of Rio de Janeiro, Brazil. The online questionnaire, developed in Google Forms, collected data on sociodemographic characteristics, self-rated health, diet quality, and the frequency of food ordering and food choices via meal delivery apps. Participants were categorized according to their usage frequency and changes in usage frequency during the pandemic. Post-stratification weighting was applied based on sex, age, and education level. Descriptive analyses were stratified by usage frequency categories. Pearson’s χ² test and McNemar’s test were used for statistical comparisons.Results59.4% of meal delivery app users were female; 54.7% were aged under 44; 78.6% had a medium level of education. During the pandemic, there was an increase in the frequency of food purchases in meal delivery apps, more than half of the population was using meal delivery apps more frequently, and 15% of the occasional customers (less than once a week) turned into regular ones (once a week or more). The top three items ordered, before or during the pandemic, were fast food, soda, and handmade hamburgers or pizzas. The main items ordered by people who became occasional users were dishes based on animal protein and fast food. A significant proportion of them stopped ordering fast food (5%), soda (9%), international cuisine (9%), and traditional Brazilian meals (8%) during the pandemic. The main orders by people who became regular users were fast food and soda. Respectively, 17%, 13% and 10% of them started ordering traditional Brazilian meals, dishes based on animal protein and desserts.ConclusionsSuch platforms were mainly used to buy meals based on ultra-processed foods at both moments, even among people who also used them to purchase healthy options.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24720-x.

Background

The digital food retail is part of the food environment and it can be defined as digital platforms, apps or websites where food can be purchased for personal consumption, and includes, but is not restrict to, online food delivery platforms [1]. Meal delivery apps (MDA) is a popular example of online food delivery platform for mobile devices, such as smartphones or tablets, that connects food customers to restaurants or food outlets [2]. MDA disrupts traditional food purchasing by digitally expanding the neighborhood food environment and increasing access to foods and beverages delivered over longer distances than would be walkable [2, 3].

Studies from the consumer behavioral field point out that convenience, price and time savings positively affect the intention to use MDA [4, 5]. In the epidemiological field, web surveys conducted mainly with high-income countries’ populations found that younger age, higher education [6, 7], higher BMI, higher income levels [6], male sex, self-identification as an ethnic minority and live with children [7] was associated with online food delivery services use. Online research carried out in June 2020 in the capital of the Brazilian state of Ceará, found that people who used food delivery apps were, on average, younger, less likely to live with adolescents, and less likely to prepare food daily at home compared to those who didn’t use these services [8].

There were few studies on the digital food environment of MDAs prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, and the existing ones evidenced its obesogenic features. In cities like Chicago, Amsterdam, and Melbourne, most food delivery options were unhealthy items, such as burgers and pizza [9]. Similarly, a 2019 study in Belo Horizonte, Brazil, found that around 80% of food outlets on MDAs offered ultra-processed beverages, and nearly 70% of the available dishes were ready-to-eat ultra-processed meals, whereas traditional meals and vegetable-based options appeared less frequently [10].

After governments around the world implemented physical distancing and stay-at-home orders to prevent the spread of SARS-CoV-2, the adoption and use of MDA worldwide have further accelerated since food establishments closed for on-site consumption [2]. In a systematic review of 31 studies examining changes in the use of online food platforms during the COVID-19 pandemic, 40 outcomes related to the use of delivery were identified: 22 indicated a decrease in usage frequency, while 18 reported an increase [11]. Only four web-based surveys included in the review encompassed Latin American populations [12–14]. Among these, three studies were conducted in Brazil during the first wave of the pandemic. Two of them reported a reduction in the frequency of MDA use [12] whereas one observed an increase [13].

However, there is still limited evidence regarding the impact of the pandemic on customers’ eating practices and food choices made via MDAs. Globally, only two studies have assessed changes in foods ordered through MDAs, and both observed a shift toward unhealthier food choices [15, 16].

The present work reports findings from a web survey conducted contemporaneously with the three aforementioned studies carried out in Brazil during the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic. It provides additional information on changes in purchase frequency and the types of dishes and beverages ordered by adult users of MDAs living in the metropolitan region of Rio de Janeiro.

Methods

Study design and participants

This is an open web survey with a virtual chain sampling procedure carried out from May to September 2020. As this period corresponds to the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic, the reported behavioral changes reflect comparisons between pre-lockdown habits and behaviors during this early stage [17].

This methodology does not allow sample size calculation and generates a non-probabilistic sample. Since this type of research has some particularities, we reported our study following the Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-surveys (CHERRIES) [18] (Appendix) to improve the interpretability and transparency of our results.

The eligibility criteria were possessing a Smartphone or tablet, being an MDA user, and being an adult over 18 years living in the Greater Metropolitan Rio de Janeiro, the second-largest metropolitan region of Brazil, with 12.8 million residents [19]. On May 1 st, 2020, the per capita income of the metropolitan region was 1,063.03 reals (equivalent to 195.88 USD), the Human Development Index was 0.826, and the Gini Index was 0.565 [20].

The questionnaire was disseminated through the National School of Public Health institutional website (https://ensp.fiocruz.br/) and social media such as WhatsApp, Instagram, and Facebook. A banner ad was announced on Instagram and Facebook. Moreover, we contacted students and researchers from the institutions of the authors of the present study via e-mail asking them to help share the survey. A total of 986 eligible participants completed the survey. We excluded duplicate entries based on personal email account or Whatsapp number provided (n = 2) and observations with missing data for sociodemographic variables used for the post-stratification procedure (n = 16).

Self-administered online questionnaire

The survey was conducted using the web-based app for online data collection Google Forms®. The final questionnaire encompassed 31 items distributed on 18 pages, with one to six items per page. For the present study, we considered 16 items referring to sociodemographic characteristics, self-rated health, nutritional status, diet quality, and aspects related to the use of MDA before and during the COVID-19 pandemic (purchase frequency and types of dishes and beverages usually ordered at both moments). A draft questionnaire was tested with a purposive sample of 25 undergraduate students. They completed the questionnaire and provided feedback in open text fields on unclear phrasing and response option suitability. Additionally, five volunteers attended a 30-minute meeting to discuss their difficulties and share their opinions on specific points. Their feedback was highly positive, and the only modification made was highlighting key parts of each question using bold and uppercase text. This sample was appropriate for the pre-test, as the final sample had a higher proportion of highly educated participants, aligning with expectations for online surveys.

Respondents could review and change their answers through a back button at the end of each page, but could not change their responses once the formulary was submitted. Response to all survey items was mandatory, and respondents were prompted to complete outstanding items before leaving the survey page. All questions included a ‘Don’t know’ option, except for the items related to eligibility criteria and questions that defined whether other items would be displayed. It was not possible to calculate the number of views and participation rates, as Google Forms does not report, respectively, the number of people who visit or of those who fill out the first page of the survey but give up answering the rest of the questionnaire. Additionally, we could not assess if questionnaires were submitted within an atypical timeframe because the tool only provides the time when the questionnaire was sent.

The questions to assess the MDA usage characteristics were developed for this study (Supplementary Material 1). Meal delivery app usage frequency was assessed by the question “Before the COVID-19 (coronavirus) pandemic, how often were you used to using Meal Delivery Apps?”. The reply categories ranged from “Once a week” to “Daily”, plus “Fortnightly”, “Once a month”, and “Less than once a month”. Then, we asked “During the COVID-19 (coronavirus) pandemic, did you change your purchase frequency in Meal Delivery Apps?”, with the response options “No”, “Yes, increased”, “Yes, decreased”. Except if the answer was negative, we asked “During the COVID-19 (coronavirus) pandemic, how often are you using Meal Delivery Apps?”, with the same reply categories presented for the question regarding the pre-pandemic period.

We dichotomized usage frequency at both moments as “Regular” (at least once a week) versus “Occasional” (less than once a week) based on a previous study that investigated sociodemographic characteristics associated with online food delivery service use (at least once in the 7 days prior to the survey) [7]. Additionally, we created categories defined by usage frequency changes (or not) during the pandemic period: “Sustained occasional frequency”, “Sustained regular frequency”, “Frequency reduction to occasional”, standing for change from regular to occasional, and “Frequency increase to regular”, meaning change from occasional to regular.

We assessed the types of dishes and beverages ordered via MDA by the question “Before the COVID-19 (coronavirus) pandemic, what beverages/dishes were you used to ordering through delivery apps?”. Respondents could select any of the response options described in Table 1. These options were defined based on a inspection of popular items and tags in MDA in Brazil. Moreover, as we adopted the Brazilian Dietary Guidelines as a reference for healthiness [21], we considered the NOVA food system classification. NOVA categorizes foods into four groups based on nature, extent and purpose of food processing: fresh or minimally processed foods (natural foods with little to no processing); culinary ingredients (such as oils, salt, and sugar); processed foods (foods altered for preservation or enhanced flavor, such as canned vegetables, cheeses, and breads); and ultra-processed foods (industrial formulations with additives, often high in sugars, fats, and preservatives, like soft drinks, instant noodles, and packaged snacks). We crafted response options that allowed foods and beverages to be categorized according to the NOVA food system. For example, we included separate choices for handmade hamburgers and pizzas, distinguishing them from conventional fast-food items.

For analytical purposes, we categorized food items as “most healthy” or “most unhealthy” orders based on the Brazilian Dietary Guidelines’ general statement that a healthy eating pattern should be based on fresh or minimally processed foods, added with little amounts of culinary ingredients, and moderate quantities of processed foods [21]. Following the classification algorithm described by Louzada, we grouped the hand-prepared dishes according to their main component into “fresh or minimally processed foods and their culinary preparations”, “processed foods”, and “ultra-processed foods” [22] (Table 1). We then considered the first two categories as “most healthy orders” or “most unhealthy orders”, and the ultra-processed foods along with alcoholic beverages were considered “unhealthy”, since their inclusion in the diet should be avoided [21] (Table 1).

Then, we asked “During the COVID-19 (coronavirus) pandemic, did you change the types of beverages or foods ordered?” and, in case of a positive answer, “During the COVID-19 (coronavirus) pandemic, which beverages/foods have you been ordering from delivery apps?”, with the same reply categories as previously described. For respondents who answered that they did not change the types of items they ordered, we repeated the food and beverage items they said they ordered via MDA before the pandemic.

Participants reported their age and we categorized them into age groups (18–24; 25–34; 35–44; 45–54; 55–64; 65 or over). Education level was reported as the highest level completed and categorized as ‘medium’ (less than a university degree) or ‘high’ (university degree). We chose not to collect the income level due to the sensitive nature of this information, which typically causes a higher non-response rate. We needed to avoid missing data in the variable chosen as a proxy for socioeconomic status, as it was necessary to calibrate the sample. Respondents reported their living arrangement, categorized as ‘couple with children’, ‘couple without children’, ‘extended family’- a non-nuclear family comprising at least one relative, ‘single parent family’, ‘compound’ - a non-nuclear family comprising at least one non-relative, and ‘one-person family’. Self-rated health was evaluated as ‘good’ (good and very good), ‘fair’, and ‘poor’ (poor and very poor). Height and weight were reported, we calculated body mass index (BMI) (kg/m2) and classified respondents’ nutritional status as ‘underweight’ (BMI < 18.5), ‘normal weight’ (BMI 18.5–24.9), ‘overweight’ (BMI 25.0–29.9), or ‘obesity’ (BMI ≥ 30) [23].

We incorporated two simplified tools to assess diet quality to the online questionnaire. These instruments include the subgroups of fresh or minimally processed foods (Group 1 according to NOVA classification) and ultra-processed foods (Group 4 of NOVA) with the greatest participation in the daily energy intake estimated by the Brazilian Dietary Survey 2008–2009 [24]. Both instruments assess the consumption of a list of 10 selected food subgroups on the previous day - independent of meal delivery app usage - and generate two separate scores, each ranging from 0 to 10. The scores represent proxies of the percentage of energy contribution from foods of Groups 1 and 4. In the first case, a higher score marks better diet quality, while in the second case, a lower score means better diet quality. A validation study showed good agreement (kappa from 0.52 to 0.81) and reliability (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.60) between fifths of the score for the consumption of fresh or minimally processed foods and fifths of the contribution of this food group to the total daily energy intake measured by 24-hour food record [24]. Also, substantial agreement (kappa from 0.60 to 0.81, PABAK index = 0.72) was found for the consumption of ultra-processed foods [24]. After calculating both scores, we categorized participants in quintiles according to the two scores’ distribution on the sample.

Statistical analysis

We applied post-stratification weighting by sex, age group, and level of schooling to correct the under or overrepresentation of population segments in the convenience sample obtained. For this purpose, we used data from the Continuous National Household Sample Survey 2019, conducted by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics, for the region covered by the survey. As we reached little representativeness of people with a low level of education (less than complete high school), we decided to exclude the ones who responded to the form to avoid a few observations representing many people due to post-stratification procedure.

Sociodemographic and self-reported health-related characteristics of MDA customers were described according to the usage frequency before the pandemic and stratified by the categories created to describe the changes in the usage frequency of MDA during the pandemic. We also described the prevalence of items usually ordered considering the same strata. We present solely relative frequencies because the weighting adjusts the sample to represent the target population rather than the raw number of respondents. We used Pearson’s χ2 test to analyse differences between categories defined by usage frequency before the pandemic and among usage frequency change classes. When comparisons referred to differences before and during the pandemic, we applied McNemar test. All analyses were conducted using R Software (version 4.1.3), with the aid of the “weights” package to incorporate post-stratification weighting. Since post-stratification mimics the effects of a larger sample, increasing the likelihood of detecting statistically significant differences, we adopted a significance level of 1% (p < 0.01) as a conservative approach to ensure that only robust differences were considered significant.

Ethics considerations

Participation in the survey was entirely voluntary and anonymous. All participants were fully informed about the study objectives and requirements and were asked to provide informed consent to accept the confidentiality and privacy policy, before accessing the survey. The responses were stored in a password-protected and restricted access database to which only the team of analysts have access. Ethics approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the National School of Public Health of the Oswaldo Cruz Foundation (CAAE: 30179220.4.0000.5240).

Results

Overall, more than half of the meal delivery app users were female and over 20% were in the age groups 25–34 and 60 or more years. About 80% had a medium level of education and the predominant living arrangement was a couple with children (31.8%) (Table 2).

Before the pandemic, 59.6% of the MDA customers used to order food regularly (at least once a week), while 40.4% used to do it just occasionally (fortnightly or less frequently). After the onset of the pandemic, 51.5% increased the frequency of meal delivery app usage, and 22.2% decreased it in such a way that 15.0% of the occasional customers became regular, and 12.2% of the regular meal delivery app users turned into occasional ones. Thus, the proportion of regular customers increased to 62.4%, and that of occasional users reduced to 37.6% during the pandemic (data not shown in tables).

Among meal delivery app users who reduced usage frequency to occasional during the pandemic, compared to those who increased to regular use, there was a greater proportion of women (78.9% vs. 57,5%), elderly (65+) (52.8% vs. 7.8%), one-person families (55.0% vs. 16.2%), people with poor self-rated health (8.1% vs. 2.9%) and living with obesity (59.0% vs. 23.7%) (Tables 2 and 3). Among users who increased usage frequency to regular, in comparison to those who decreased usage frequency, there was a higher proportion of male sex (42.5% vs. 21.1%), adults from 18 to 24 years (24.1% vs. 9.4%), middle-aged adults from 45 to 54 (22.7% vs. 1.9%) and from 55 to 64 years (11.2% vs. 2.4%), and people living with a partner and children (38.8% vs. 12.6%) (Tables 2 and 3).

Regarding the self-reported health-related characteristics, during the pandemic, 63.9% of MDA users who reduced their usage to occasional were in the third quintile of the diversity food score (correspondent to 5 health markers consumed) and around 70% were in the second quintile of the ultra-processed food score (correspondent to only 2 health markers consumed), while people who increased usage frequency were more homogeneously distributed (Table 3).

Overall, before or during the COVID-19 pandemic, fast food, soda, handmade hamburgers or pizzas, international cuisine, and traditional Brazilian meals were the food items usually purchased by greater proportions of people (Fig. 1). The percentage of those who were used to ordering fruit and vegetable-based dishes via MDA was lower than for other food items, independent of the frequency of MDA usage (Fig. 1).

During the COVID-19 pandemic, we observed a substantial and statistically significant reduction in the percentage of occasional users who purchased soda (−16.2%), fast food (−15.1%), and international cuisine dishes (−14.1%) while there was a 12.8% increase for dishes based on animal protein orders (Fig. 1). Among people who diminished usage frequency from regular to occasional during the pandemic, about 5% stopped ordering fast food, 9%, soda, 9%, international cuisine, and 8%, traditional Brazilian meals, as can be observed by the difference between the proportions of users who ordered these items in each moment. Their main orders were dishes based on animal protein and fast food at both moments (Fig. 2). Among participants who raised usage frequency from occasional to regular during the pandemic, the percentage of people who ordered traditional Brazilian meals, dishes based on animal protein, and desserts grew by more than grew by more than 17%, 13%, and 10%, respectively, but fast food and soda were still their main purchases (Fig. 2). Additionally, almost 6% of those who increased usage frequency started ordering alcoholic beverages (Fig. 2).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the use of MDA and the types of dishes and beverages purchased before and during the COVID-19 pandemic in Brazil. Almost three-quarters of the population changed their frequency of MDA usage after the outbreak, and around half of them reported an increase in their frequency of use. There were also changes in food items ordered, although such modifications present different degrees and tendencies according to the frequency of purchase over time.

Most studies conducted during the same period as ours reported a decline in food delivery use. However, MDA purchasing behavior varied throughout the COVID-19 pandemic. Between April and June 2020, most studies found a decrease in MDA use, while in the following quarter, a similar number of articles reported both an increase and a decrease in frequency of use [11]. In Brazil, data from the country’s leading MDA platform showed a 45.8% increase in delivery order volume during the early months of the pandemic, rising from 30.6 million orders in March to 44.6 million in August 2020 [25]. Liboredo et al. conducted a web survey during the final two months of our data collection, in which around 50% of participants also reported increased food delivery use [13]. In a subsequent study by the same authors, around 55% of respondents reported decreased use [12]. Still, studies conducted in any country from mid-2021 to late 2022 showed increased MDA use compared to the pre-pandemic period [11].

The profile of people who turned into regular meal delivery app users was characterized by a higher proportion of young and middle-aged adults and people living with a partner and children in comparison with those who decreased usage frequency. The working-age population stratum often reports time scarcity, especially when they are parents, which leads them to buy food prepared away from home to save time for other activities [7]. During the pandemic, difficulties with time availability may have intensified due to adaptations of their own study and/or work activities as well as scholarly tasks from their children to the domestic environment [26].

Remarkably, a smaller but relevant percentage of people reduced frequency to occasional use. Their profile was characterized by a higher proportion of elderly, individuals with obesity and people who self-rated their health as poor compared to users who increased their frequency. In a web survey conducted in Brazil between September and October 2020, over half of the participants reported reducing or ceasing food delivery use, primarily due to fear of COVID-19. Concerns about contamination through packaging, food, or contact with the delivery driver led to more cautious behaviors—such as disinfecting packaging, heating food before eating, and avoiding contact-based payments [12]. It is possible that these concerns disproportionately affected groups identified as high-risk, such as older adults and those with underlying health conditions—which helps explain the profile of users who reduced their usage in our study [5, 12].

Overall, fruit- and vegetable-based dishes and minimally processed beverages made up a small proportion of MDA orders and the ultra-processed food subgroups fast food and soda, which are considered unhealthy by the Brazilian Dietary Guidelines [21], prevailed as the most popular purchases at both moments, with just a discrete decrease during the pandemic. A study among Chinese young adults in the early phase of the pandemic, showed a slight increase for ordering fried food or hamburgers (45.0% vs. 51.4%) [15]. In fact, it seems that online food delivery services users do expect such platforms to facilitate access to unhealthy foods (e.g. pizza), while healthy foods are seen as more adequate for homemade meals instead of to be ordered [7]. It is suggested that replacing homemade food by out-of-home prepared food ordered via delivery could lead to an improved risk of weight gain and chronic diseases, such as hypertension and diabetes [2, 6, 23]. There is a lack of empirical studies on the effect of MDA usage on health outcomes [27], but one study by Albalawi and colleagues (2022) has endorsed this point of view by showing a significant positive and possibly bidirectional relationship between takeaway and/or delivery foods consumed in the previous day and BMI and body fat.

These findings are different from those regarding the general eating practices during the pandemic among Brazilian people with the same level of education that participated in the online cohort NutriNet Brazil: it was observed just a small increase in the consumption of healthy eating markers during the first wave of the pandemic while the consumption of the unhealthy ones remained stable [28]. One possible explanation is that even though meal delivery app is commonly used to access unhealthy foods, it corresponds to a minor, although a considerable, fraction of away-from-home eating in Brazil [29], so it also represents a minor part of total food consumption.

Despite this general tendency, we observed both positive and negative changes in terms of the nutritional quality of the food choices made by people who changed their meal delivery app usage frequency. Dishes based on animal protein and fast food accounted for a large proportion of the orders from customers who reduced their purchase frequency during the pandemic, although they have also reduced the proportion of all food or beverage subgroups ordered. Considering dishes based on animal protein are generally the main courses and fast food are meal substitutes, it is possible that those who started to buy food through MDA only sporadically have prioritized homemade food daily and have used such apps just “to purchase food they could not or would not cook at home; for a break from normality” [7].

Evidence shows that unhealthy meals are predominantly announced in MDA in numerous big cities from high-income countries [3, 9, 30, 31]. In Brazilian capitals, one study found that an online food delivery platform promoted unhealthy eating during the first wave of the pandemic due to the expressive presence of unhealthy foods advertising, with free delivery ads prevailing for ice cream, candies or salty packages snacks and pizza and combos more frequently including natural juices or smoothies, ultra-processed beverages, sandwiches and pizzas [10]. Another study conducted still in the first wave in Latin American capital cities [32], observed that at least 40% of the food outlets available on an online delivery platform in Montevideo, Bogotá, São Paulo, Lima, and Santiago de Chile offered discounts, and that free delivery was available in at least 50% of the outlets in Quito, San José, Mexico City, Santiago de Chile, and Lima. Considering the obesogenic characteristics of the digital food environment in MDAs, it could have been expected that regular users, especially customers who increased usage frequency during the pandemic, acquired mainly ultra-processed food subgroups. In fact, such food items were their main orders, but this preference coexisted with culinary preparations based on fresh or minimally processed foods, such as those usually consumed in main Brazilian meals [33].

Additionally, this type of food order became more important during the pandemic, possibly because there was a 47% growth in lunchtime orders to the leading delivery company in the country, explained by the possibility of paying for meals with the lunche on voucher [34]. As the study sample profile was congruent with that of home office workers in Brazil, that is majoritarian females with a university degree [35], it is plausible that some people have started to use this benefit to order out-of-home prepared food while worked at home.

Regarding the healthiness of food orders, two adjacent aspects should be observed. First, even though animal protein can be part of a healthy diet, the Brazilian Dietary Guidelines recommend a plant-based diet detriment to animal-based foods for human and planetary health [21] since Brazilians already consume great amounts of meat [36]. We observed that dishes based on animal protein were an option for a great proportion of people, suggesting that meal delivery app may facilitate access to it, especially among people who increased usage frequency after the pandemic onset. Second, the easier access to alcohol via meal delivery app may have worsened drinking control during the COVID-19 pandemic, as online alcohol delivery was associated with heavier alcohol consumption in New Zealand while purchasing from supermarkets was not [37].

The COVID-19 pandemic has strengthened the delivery industry in Brazil. Many people have increased the frequency of using this service in a Brazilian metropolis. Despite some changes in food items ordered by different user profiles, the hegemony of ultra-processed food purchases in this digital food environment has not been shaken, as major typical selections are fast food and soda, even among people who order healthy items as well. It endorses the hypothesis that the availability dimension of MDA has a narrow relationship with the main food choices made in this digital food environment.

It assumes a special importance considering the healthiness of other food environment settings to which the residents of the metropolitan region of Rio de Janeiro were exposed. A census of food outlets in bus terminals of the five most populous cities of the region carried out in 2019 showed that ultra-processed foods were, on average, 250% more available than fresh or minimally processed options. Specifically in the city of Rio de Janeiro, it was observed a high prevalence of food swamps and an even greater occurrence of food deserts in the same year [38]. Two other studies on different university campuses in Rio revealed a limited availability of fresh or minimally processed foods, alongside a widespread presence of ultra-processed products [39, 40], a predominance of advertising for ultra-processed foods [38], and lower prices for savory snacks and biscuits compared to fruits [40]. Given this scenario, the consolidation of the meal delivery sector during and after the pandemic took place in a food environment already characterized by an abundant supply of ultra-processed foods. This takes on particular significance in the post-pandemic context since many people have incorporated this food purchase method into their possibilities and many others have started using it more frequently.

Considering the rapid growth of the online delivery services over the past decade [41], it is essential to evaluate potential regulatory measures to mitigate the negative impacts of the digitalization of food environments. Existing nutrition regulations are not fully suitable for protecting public health in the face of the transformation of food environments through digital platforms [42]. This study was conducted during a period of economic and social crisis, which heightened the population’s vulnerability to aggressive marketing strategies and the widespread availability of unhealthy foods. The lack of a regulatory framework in such a critical moment reinforces the urgency of addressing these gaps, especially given that limited regulatory progress has been identified globally since then. In this sense, measures already adopted in other dimensions of the food environment—such as menu labeling and “front-of-package” nutrition labeling policies—should be adapted to the digital space, at least for dishes and beverages offered by large restaurant chains in MDA. Furthermore, financial incentives for purchasing ultra-processed foods, such as price promotions, combos, and free delivery—as well as persuasive digital strategies (e.g., push notifications offering discount coupons), should be regulated to reduce their impact on consumer choices.

Limitations and strengths

Despite our relevant findings, this study has some limitations to consider. (1) Since our study followed a cross-sectional design with self-reported measures, responses regarding pre-pandemic practices relied on retrospective recall of participants’ behaviors. Additionally, responses about practices during the pandemic referred solely to its first wave, which coincided with the period of data collection. (2) The web-based app used to administer the questionnaire does not provide features to address the requirements of two domains outlined in the CHERRIES - calculating response rates and preventing multiple entries from the same individual. (3) Due to the virtual nature of our sampling method, only subjects with internet access and with the necessary technology familiarisation to answer the online questionnaire were eligible for the study. Another aspect that may have influenced the sample pre-selection is that the survey was primarily shared by research institutions and post-graduate students/post-graduated professionals. As a consequence, we did not reach individuals with low levels of schooling. In 2018, 79.1% of Brazilian households had access to the internet and 41.6% of people who did not use the internet did not know how to do so [19]. Therefore, our results are not representative of the whole population of the region. However, we believe the impact of this weakness on our conclusions may have been little considering that these are the natural eligibility criteria for MDA usage. (4) The estimates are subjected to the observation dependency that occurs in chain sample recruiting procedures which can affect their validity. Nonetheless, we applied post-stratification weights to overcome limitations 2 and 3. (5) Finally, considering the multiple objectives of the complete survey, there was limited space in the questionnaire to investigate other aspects of the pandemic context, such as respondents’ COVID-19 infection history and adherence to physical distancing measures, which could be associated with MDA use. However, according to another web survey carried out in Brazil at the same time, 74.2% of the population reported intense adherence to the measures, and the majority of those who did not adhere had low education levels and were from North and Midwest regions of the country, and the present study did not cover these populational strata [43].

On the other hand, our main strength is the novelty of this research of studying the use of MDA in a metropolitan region outside the Global North. In addition, we evaluated the changes that occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic and considered their relationship with aspects of health and nutrition. Other strengths concern the application of simplified questionnaires that are adequate for screening opposite dimensions of general diet quality through digital settings according to the Brazilian Dietary Guidelines as they are based on NOVA food classification. Analyses of dishes and beverages that are usually purchased via MDA also followed NOVA.

Conclusions

The use of MDA platforms to buy culinary preparations based on ultra-processed foods were unanimous among adult residents of a Brazilian metropolitan region during the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic, independent of their usage frequency, even among people who also used MDA to buy healthy options. Advancing the digital food environment policy framework is a critical issue to prevent the food delivery industry from continuing to operate and grow in a largely unregulated environment, especially given that the COVID-19 pandemic has accelerated the digitization of food environments and little to no regulatory progress has been made globally to date.

Further investigation should rely on information about the share of food purchased via MDA relative to other means of accessing food prepared outside the home. It is also important to evaluate the potential impact of this share in the broader context of dietary practices. In Brazil, these aspects could be assessed on a large scale through the Family Budget Survey (in Portuguese, Pesquisa de Orçamentos Familiares – POF), which already collects data on the places of purchase for food consumed outside the home, with little additional effort. Furthermore, specific research is needed on the frequency of purchase of different types of foods and beverages via MDA. As our data reflect only the first wave of the pandemic, further studies using comparable data are also needed to assess long-term changes in the post-pandemic context.
