Prevalence and epidemiological factors associated with urogenital schistosomiasis amongst school children in three health districts of Cameroon; Lagdo (North), Loum and Njombe-Penja (Littoral): a cross-sectional study
Authors: Njegwe Nehemiah Tangu, Edouard Nantia Akono, Flore Nguemaïm Ngoufo, Raoul Polycarpe Tuekam Kayo, Archile Paguem, Pierre Kamtsap, Louis-Albert Tchuem Tchuenté, Alfons Renz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSchistosoma haematobium causes urogenital schistosomiasis (UgS), responsible for the largest number of schistosomiasis infections in sub-Saharan Africa, including Cameroon.ObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the prevalence and epidemiological factors associated with UgS among school children in three Health Districts (HDs) of Cameroon.Study designThis was a school-based cross-sectional study that took place in Lagdo (North region), Loum and Njombe-Penja (Littoral region) HDs of Cameroon, from April 2022 to June 2023.MethodsA total of 600 pupils were enrolled in this study, consisting of 100, 200, and 300 pupils from Loum, Njombe-Penja, and Lagdo HDs, respectively. Of these, 308 boys and 292 girls aged 8–14 years old were included. Urine samples were collected from the pupils and examined microscopically for Schistosoma haematobium eggs. The prevalence of UgS was compared across various groups (e.g., HDs, sex, and age) using the Chi-square test. Epidemiological factors associated with UgS were identified using binary logistic regression and odds ratio (OR). A p < 0.05 at a 95% confidence interval (CI) was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe overall prevalence of UgS was 16.2% (97/600). UgS infections were found in 28.3% (n = 85), 12.0% (n = 12), and nil (n = 0) of pupils in Lagdo, Loum, and Njombe-Penja HDs, respectively. In the Lagdo HD, 1.0% (3/300) urine-derived co-infection of Schistosoma haematobium and Schistosoma mansoni was found. UgS infection was significantly higher in males (19.2%; 59/308) than in females (13.0%; 38/292) (p = 0.041) but not with age groups (in years: ≤ 10, 11–12, and 13–14) (p = 0.073). Moreover, higher risk of UgS infection were observed in pupils from Lagdo HD (OR: 2.899; CI: 1.509–5.572) and boys (OR: 1.584; CI: 1.016–2.468), when compared to others in their respective categories.ConclusionsUrinary eggs of Schistosoma haematobium were present in 28.3% of samples at Lagdo HD, 12.0% at Loum HD, and nil at Njombe-Penja HD. HDs and sex were epidemiological factors associated with UgS infection. This necessitates disease education, improvement of water, sanitation, and hygiene (WaSH) infrastructures, and snail vector control in at-risk localities, to improve schistosomiasis control/elimination efforts.Keywords: School children, Urogenital schistosomiasis, Prevalence, Epidemiological factors, Cameroon

Introduction

Schistosomiasis or bilharzia is a snail-borne neglected tropical disease which is endemic in 78 countries, especially in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), causing over 200,000 deaths annually [1]. The disease is caused by parasitic worms called schistosomes, which are trematodes of the Schistosoma genus [2]. Among the species of schistosomes that parasitize humans, Schistosoma (S.) haematobium, which causes urogenital schistosomiasis (UgS), is responsible for the largest number of infections in SSA [3]. Approximately 150 million individuals are infected with S. haematobium in SSA, which is more than twice the number of cases estimated for S. mansoni [4]. The disease is marked by symptoms such as anaemia, cognitive difficulties, abdominal pain, and haematuria (the presence of blood in urine) [5]. S. haematobium has a two-host life cycle, with a sexual reproduction phase occurring in humans (definitive host) and an asexual phase in a freshwater snail (intermediate host) [6]. The two transmission processes involved are: human-to-snail and snail-to-human transmissions. Once S. haematobium eggs are excreted typically through the urine of infected humans into freshwater environments, under favourable condition, they hatch into miracidia, that infect suitable Bulinus snail vectors. Following asexual reproduction within the infected snail, free-swimming cercariae are released into the water environment. Upon human contact, these cercariae penetrate the skin, develop into adult worms and produce eggs through sexual reproduction, thereby completing the parasite’s life cycle [7].

In Cameroon, a country of approximately 28.4 million inhabitants [8], Schistosomiasis is a public health problem despite efforts made by health authorities. It is estimated that 5 million people are at risk of infection with Schistosomiasis, of which 2 million are infected [9]. The 2010 national epidemiological survey in Cameroon identified the presence of three Schistosoma species: S. haematobium (the urogenital schistosome), S. mansoni and S. guineensis (intestinal schistosomes) [10, 11]. The disease affects individuals of all ages but is most prevalent among school-aged children, primarily due to their playing habits in water bodies that serve as habitats for the intermediate host snail vectors [6]. The reliance of vulnerable populations on natural and man-made water sources such as lakes, rivers, ponds, reservoirs and irrigation canals for domestic, occupational, and recreational purposes presents risk factors for schistosomiasis [12].

Since the official launching of Cameroon’s National Programme for the Control of Schistosomiasis and Intestinal Helminthiasis (NPCSIH) in 2004, Mass Drug Administration (MDA) with Praziquantel (PZQ) has been carried out over the years, with preference to schistosomiasis endemic areas, including the North and Littoral regions of the country [10, 13]. Control efforts are promising, but disease elimination is challenging since PZQ kills adult worms without preventing cercariae re-infection [7, 13]. Given that S. haematobium, the urogenital schistosome, is endemic in Cameroon, there is need for studies that provide micro-epidemiological data in villages and areas with previously reported endemicity in both the northern and southern parts of Cameroon. Such data are essential to guide targeted disease intervention [14]. Moreover, environmental changes, whether natural or man-made, can impact disease endemicity. For instance, the construction of the Lagdo Dam in North Cameroon has enabled stable water conditions, while irrigation canals from Lake Lagdo supplying water to rice fields have provided habitats for snail vectors thereby potentially posing the risk of schistosomiasis transmission. Also, in Loum and Njombe-Penja in the Littoral region of Cameroon, land use modifications, socio-economic development, and climate change may contribute to the spread of schistosomiasis by creating more favourable conditions for the proliferation of intermediate host snails. This study therefore aimed to assess the prevalence and epidemiological factors associated with urogenital schistosomiasis (caused by S. haematobium) amongst school children in three Health Districts (HDs) of Cameroon: Lagdo (North-Cameroon), Loum and Njombe-Penja (Littoral-Cameroon).

Materials and methods

Study area

The school-based study was conducted in three HDs of Cameroon: Lagdo (North region), Loum and Njombe-Penja (Littoral region) (Fig. 1; Table 1). Lagdo HD (9.0433°N, 13.7317°E) is located in the Benue division of the North region, approximately 60 km south of the city of Garoua, the capital of the North region of Cameroon. Its average annual rainfall varies from 600 to 1400 mm, with temperatures averaging between 22 °C and 28 °C. March and April are the hottest months, with air temperatures reaching up to 42 °C [15]. The Lagdo HD was purposefully selected for this study due to the previously reported endemicity of urogenital schistosomiasis [16]. Lagdo HD is also home to the Lagdo hydroelectric dam, created in 1982 from the River Benue, whose construction altered the natural hydrological network, favouring the establishment of snail intermediate hosts of schistosomiasis [1]. Gravity-fed irrigation canals drawing from the Lagdo Lake and supplying water to rice fields provide suitable habitats for snail vectors. Insufficient safe drinking water and reliance on open water reservoirs for domestic, occupational, recreational and sometimes toilet substitutes are risk factors for schistosomiasis transmission. Loum HD (4.7182°N, 9.7351°E) and Njombe-Penja HD (4.5667°N, 9.6500°E) are located in the Moungo division of the Littoral region. Loum is located about 70 km from Douala (the economic capital of Cameroon). It has two main seasons, namely, the wet (March to November) and the dry (December to February) seasons, and an average monthly temperature between 19 and 25 °C with a mean precipitation of 2699 mm per year [17]. It is an endemic zone for schistosomiasis according to previous reports [18, 19]. The Njombe-Penja HD has an annual precipitation between 2000 and 2500 mm with a mean temperature of about 27 °C [20]. Its landscape consists of rocky terrain, plains, plateaus and hills, with predominant forest vegetation. The population practices farming which contributes to the local economy. Moreover, several schistosomiasis information/research have been reported in this locality [20, 21]. Study design, period, and sample size estimation

This was a school-based cross-sectional study designed to determine the prevalence of UgS in the Lagdo, Loum, and Njombe-Penja HDs of Cameroon. The study was conducted from April 2022 to June 2023. The study period was within the first half of the years (Njombe-Penja HD: April 2022, Lagdo and Loum HDs: January–June 2023), to align with periods when human-water contact and UgS transmission are likely to be high, particularly during the dry season. This period also coincides with the school calendar, ensuring that pupils were accessible for sampling. The sample size was determined using the formula for sample size calculation [22]:

A minimum of 348 pupils was estimated to be required for the study, representing the two ecological zones in which this study was conducted, namely, the Sudano-Sahelian zone (Northern setting) and the Monomodal Humid Forest zone (Littoral setting) [23].

Study population, sampling methods, and criteria for selection of participants

A total of 600 pupils, including 308 boys and 292 girls aged 8–14 years, were enrolled in this study (Fig. 2). Of these, using purposive sampling, 300 pupils were equitably sampled in the North and Littoral regions to ensure consistency. In the North region, the 300 pupils enrolled were from six schools (Table 1) in Lagdo HD. In the Littoral region, we initially sampled 200 pupils from four schools from Njombe-Penja HD, and an additional 100 pupils from two schools in a closely located HD (Loum HD) to bring the total number to 300 pupils in the Littoral region to ensure regional consistency (Table 1). These HDs were selected due to previously reported endemicity of S. haematobium [11, 16, 19–21]. The sample distribution per HD was based on targeted surveillance approach and to preserve the geographic context since UgS is a focal disease and the sample allocation by HD enables the estimation of local heterogeneity of UgS to inform control intervention. In total, twelve (12) schools were selected for this study, with 50 pupils recruited from each school (Table 1; Fig. 2), and survey recommendations were taken into consideration [24, 25]. It was relevant conducting the study in primary schools as school children belong to the most at-risk population for schistosomiasis due to habits such as playing, swimming, and bathing in water bodies. They are accessible group for schistosomiasis survey, and often constitute the target of mass drug administration (MDA) (e.g., with Praziquantel). So, sampling this group helps in assessing control impact and brings out updated data for public health intervention.

In each school, the children and their legal parents/guardians were given an awareness talk on schistosomiasis and the purpose of the study was clearly explained emphasizing on the need to diagnose UgS infection. Participants (pupils) were enrolled if they met the following eligibility criteria: permitted age range ≥ 5 years and < 15 years; willingness to participate in the study; signed informed consent from legal parents/guardians; lived in the study area for at least a year; and understand English/French, complemented with local dialects such as Fulfulde or Tupuri when necessary, particularly in the Northern setting. The pupils were free to withdraw from the study at any time, without any reason and with no penalty/charge. Children living in a different locality from which the school is located were excluded. The pupils were recruited following the method described by Tchuem Tchuenté et al. [10]. In brief, pupils were randomly selected from the upper classes, equilibrating males (boys) and females (girls), preferentially selected from the 5th grade, and in other grades when the number of children in the 5th grade was not sufficient. Children who agreed to participate were registered in a database, and their personal information was kept confidential.

Collection of urine samples and screening of parasite eggs

Urine samples were collected from school children, each into clean, dry, pre-labelled 50 mL screw-capped transparent plastic containers, following clear explanation of the collection procedure. This was done between 10:00 a.m. and 2:00 p.m., the period of peak daily activity when physical exercise carried out by the children is likely to result in maximal excretion of parasite eggs [26]. The urine samples were transported to the laboratory in cooler boxes that maintained the temperature at about 4°C in order to avoid the degradation of schistosome eggs if present. In the laboratory, urine samples were examined microscopically for parasite eggs after processing using filtration method [27] at Centre for Schistosomiasis and Parasitology in Yaounde (Cameroon) or centrifugation method [28] at the Integrated Health Center Gounougou in Lagdo (Cameroon) and the Biochemistry Laboratory of The University of Bamenda in Bambili (Cameroon). In brief, with respect to the filtration method, each urine sample was agitated to ensure adequate suspension of eggs, and 10 mL of urine was filtered through a Nucleopore® filter, and the filter was stained with Lugol’s iodine and examined by microscopy for the presence/absence of S. haematobium eggs. Alternatively (using the centrifugation method), after the urine sample was shaken to suspend the parasite eggs, 10 mL of the urine was transferred into a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 3000 revolutions per minute (rpm) for 5 min. The supernatant was decanted and a wet mount of the urine sediment was prepared and examined by microscopy for the presence/absence of urinary eggs of S. haematobium. For urine samples that were tested positive, egg counts were performed manually. Infection intensity expressed as the number of S. haematobium eggs per 10 mL of urine was recorded as light (less than 50 eggs) or heavy (50 eggs or more) [25]. Any observed urine-derived co-infection of S. haematobium (characterized by terminal-spined egg) and S. mansoni (characterized by lateral-spined egg) were recorded.

Statistical analyses

Data obtained in this study were entered into Microsoft Excel sheets, proofread and exported to the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25 (IBM SPSS Statistics, USA) for statistical analyses. The prevalence and intensity of S. haematobium infection were compared across various groups (HDs, schools, sex and age groups) using the Pearson Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, when appropriate. One-way ANOVA was used to compare the egg counts among the different groups. To assess the relationship between S. haematobium infection and demographic factors, logistic regression analysis was explored. Odds ratio (OR) were used to determine the strength of associations observed across groups. Statistical significance was measured at a 95% confidence interval (CI) and differences between comparable sets of results were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Prevalence of S. haematobium infection in the study population

The overall prevalence of UgS was 16.2% (97/600). The prevalence varied significantly across the three HDs: 28.3% in Lagdo HD, 12.0% in Loum HD, and 0.0% in Njombe-Penja HD (X2 = 72.616, p < 0.001) (Table 3). A significant association was also observed between S. haematobium infection and the schools attended (X2 = 230.342, p < 0.001), with 8 out of the 12 targeted schools reporting a case or cases of UgS. In Lagdo HD, S. haematobium infection was detected in all 6 targeted schools and out of 50 pupils examined per school; PS Riao reported the highest prevalence (82.0%), while PS Bengui had the lowest (2.0%). In the Loum HD, S. haematobium prevalence was 14.0% and 10.0% at PS Bonkeng and GSBP La Victoire, respectively. No case of Schistosoma haematobium infection was found in the schools sampled in Njombe-Penja HD (Table 3). UgS infection was significantly higher in males than in females (19.2% versus 13.0%) (X2 = 4.172, p = 0.041). However, no significant association was found between UgS infection and age groups (X2 = 5.233, p = 0.073) (Table 3).

Association of demographics with S. haematobium infection intensity in the study population

The intensity of infection of S. haematobium measured as the number of eggs excreted per 10 mL of urine is shown in Table 4. Egg excretion was recorded for 97 school children among which 83.5% (81/97) had light infection (< 50 eggs/10 mL of urine) while 16.5% (16/97) had heavy infection (≥ 50 eggs/10 mL of urine). Egg excretion was observed in 28.3% (85/300) and 12.0% (12/100) of pupils in the Lagdo and Loum HDs, respectively. Moreover, the infection intensity did not vary significantly among different HDs (p = 0.207), schools (p = 0.132), sex (p = 0.881) or age groups (p = 0.786) (Table 4).

Epidemiological factors associated with Schistosoma haematobium infection in the study population

The risk of S. haematobium infection based on the epidemiological factors of the study population is shown in Table 6. The risk of UgS infection was observed to be higher in pupils in the Lagdo HD when compared to those in the Loum HD (OR: 2.899; 95% CI: 1.509–5.572; p = 0.001). Urine-derived co-infection of S. haematobium and S. mansoni

The proportion of pupils diagnosed positive with urinary co-infection of S. haematobium and S. mansoni is shown in Table 7. Of the 600 pupils examined, 0.5% (3/600) positive cases of urine-derived co-infection of S. haematobium and S. mansoni were recorded. Out of the 300 pupils examined in the Lagdo HD, three cases (1.0%) of these co-infections were detected. These occurred in one pupil each from PS Bessoum, PS Lagdo Group I, and PS Riao. No co-infections were recorded in the Loum and Njombe-Penja HDs. Moreover, of the three co-infected pupils, two were males (0.6%; 2/308) and one was female (0.3%; 1/292). Based on age groups, two pupils (1.0%; 2/201) aged 13–14 years old were affected, while one case (0.4%; 1/283) came from the 11–12 age group (Table 7). Discussion

This study was aimed to determine the prevalence and epidemiological factors associated with UgS among school children in three Health Districts (HDs) of Cameroon; Lagdo (North), Loum and Njombe-Penja (Littoral). Our results showed an overall prevalence of 16.2% (97/600) of UgS among school children of both sexes aged 8–14 years old, which fell in the moderate category (10% ≤ prevalence < 50%) [25]. The prevalence of S. haematobium in the Lagdo and Loum HDs was 28.3% (85/300) and 12.0% (12/100), respectively, and nil in Njombe-Penja HD. Disease prevalence was in the moderate category [25] in both affected HDs. The prevalence recorded in the Lagdo HD was higher compared to those reported in other areas of Cameroon: 22.9% in Maroua in the Far North region [29] and 1.7% in Kékem in the West region [30]. The prevalence was however lower compared to the prevalence of 38.5% reported in the North region by Cameroon’s national control program (2003–2019 Progress Report) [31]. In addition, UgS infection was lower in Loum HD when compared to the prevalence of 34.2% reported by a larger study of Nkengni et al. [19]. The decrease in prevalence in the Lagdo and Loum HDs could be attributed to the annual distribution of PZQ to school-aged children living in schistosomiasis endemic areas in Cameroon, led by the NPCSIH, which have proven promising [13]. The higher infection rates in Lagdo HD compared to Loum HD could be due to the dense hydrological network (such as River Benue and Lagdo Reservoir). These water bodies could potentially increase exposure to S. haematobium infection when children visit water for domestic reasons (such as water collection for household use) and recreational activities (such as playing and swimming). In Lagdo, River Benue meanders along (or near) several villages in which the pupils reside meanwhile the Lagdo Dam, used for hydroelectric power generation in the northern part of Cameroon (Far North, North, and Adamawa regions), has high correlation with the emergence and spread of waterborne parasitic infections, including schistosomiasis, as postulated by Nkemngo et al. [1]. Although the HDs were classified overall as moderate-risk endemic, prevalence varied considerably between the primary schools in each HD. In the Lagdo HD (Northern setting), the highest UgS prevalence of 82.0% was recorded in PS Riao followed by PS Bessoum (40.0%), PS Lagdo Group I (20.0%), PS Gounougou (16.0%), PS Djalingo Kapsiki (10.0%) and lowest at PS Bengui (2.0%). In the Loum HD (Littoral setting), PS Bonkeng and GSBP La Victoire showed prevalence rates of 14.0% and 10.0%, respectively. The variation in the prevalence between the schools might be due to varied proximity of pupils to UgS transmission sites, which aligns with the observation of Sumbele et al. [32]. This could also be attributed to uneven distribution of health and water facilities in the study localities, an observation reported elsewhere [33].

UgS poses health problems to people across all age groups, but is particularly predominant among school-aged children, who are more likely to engage in high-risk behaviours such as swimming and playing in contaminated water [1, 6]. In this study, infection with S. haematobium was observed to be higher in boys than in girls. This is contrary to other studies performed elsewhere [21, 34], who reported higher prevalence of UgS in females than males. The difference may be due to behavioural patterns; boys tend to engage in outdoor activities involving water contact than girls who spend more time at home assisting their mothers in their home duties. The highest infection rates were observed among pupils aged 13–14 years, consistent with findings of Nzenou et al. [21], who reported an increased schistosomiasis prevalence among older school children (14–15 years old). This could be attributed to prolonged exposure and reliance on unsafe water sources for activities such as bathing, swimming, and playing. However, Nkemngo et al. [1] observed higher S. haematobium infection rates in the younger age group (3–9 years). This could be due to the inability of PZQ to eliminate migrating schistosomulae (that cause patent infection) [35, 36]. Surviving schistosomulae can develop into adult worms over time, eventually leading to increased egg excretion in older children [2].

Regarding the infection intensity of S. haematobium, in the Lagdo HD, out of 85 positive cases, 81.2% (69/85) had light infections while 18.8% (16/85) had heavy infections meanwhile in the Loum HD, all twelve positive cases (100.0%) had light infections. The GM egg count per 10 mL of urine was higher in the Lagdo HD (13.40) than in the Loum HD (7.29), suggesting higher transmission of UgS in the northern setting. This variation could be linked with increased water contact due to the proximity of school children to water bodies like River Benue, the Lagdo hydroelectric power plant and irrigation canals in local rice farms in the North region of Cameroon [13]. Moreover, the GM of parasite eggs excreted was higher in older pupils (11–14 years old) than in younger ones. This could be due to the engagement of older pupils in intense water contact activities (e.g., water collection for household use, laundry, and swimming), resulting in higher UgS prevalence, also observed elsewhere [32]. This may also be attributed to longer duration of exposure of the older pupils, leading to more established S. haematobium adult worm burdens, since the worms mature with time, leading to increased production and excretion of eggs. It may also be possible that older children tend to produce larger urine volumes, thereby increasing the likelihood of egg detection.

Urine-derived co-infection with S. haematobium and S. mansoni was detected in a small percentage (1.0%; 3/300) of pupils from Lagdo HD, indicative of polyparasitism. This is consistent with the previous findings of Cunin et al. [37], who reported ectopic S. mansoni eggs in urine samples from North-Cameroon. The excretion of these ectopic eggs could result from sexual interaction between the schistosome species or a spill-over phenomenon, since S. mansoni eggs are preferentially excreted in stool [37, 38]. This suggests possible hybridization between both species [39]. These dual infections warrants the need to expand surveillance to include not only S. haematobium but also S. mansoni and animal schistosomes (like S. bovis), which may influence disease dynamics through hybridization and broadening intermediate host spectrum, thereby having a detrimental impact on control strategies. Freshwater snail intermediate hosts of schistosomiasis play a key role in defining the distribution of schistosomiasis [3, 40]. We noted the presence of intermediate host snails of schistosomiasis (Bulinus and Biomphalaria snails) in some local water bodies in Lagdo HD, which could partially explain the urine-derived co-infection observed between S. haematobium and S. mansoni. Therefore, this finding highlights the need for integrated control strategies, including the provision of safe water, sanitation, and hygiene (WaSH) infrastructures, information, education, and communication (IEC), and snail control measures to effectively disrupt the life cycle of schistosomes and support ongoing control/elimination efforts.

Limitations

This study though led to relevant findings, it however had some limitations. Different parasitological examination methods (filtration and centrifugation) were used to detect S. haematobium eggs in urine samples, due to varied laboratory facilities in field settings. Of the 600 urine samples examined in this study, 200 samples were examined using the filtration method while 400 samples were examined with the centrifugation method. While both methods rely on microscopy, the filtration method is known to have higher sensitivity and may have influenced egg detection rates. Nevertheless, both methods are established and have been used by some authors in detecting and quantifying S. haematobium eggs. Notwithstanding that microscopy has been used for schistosomiasis diagnosis, it presents limitations when compared to diagnostic tools such as antigen detection (e.g., Point-of-Care Circulating Cathodic Antigen; POC-CCA) and molecular diagnostics. Such limitations include low sensitivity in detecting light infections, cannot detect prepatent infections, and tend to underestimate schistosomiasis prevalence in low-endemic settings. Another limitation of this study is that no stool examination was done and the co-infection of S. haematobium and S. mansoni observed in this study was based on urine examination only. This limited the ability to assess co-infection of both schistosomes since S. mansoni eggs are preferentially excreted in stool. The relatively small and uneven number of schools targeted per HD may limit the generalizability of the findings. Further studies involving more schools are recommended to better characterize the epidemiology of UgS across the HDs. Moreover, this study used only statistical methods to determine the epidemiological factors associated with UgS infection. Malacological surveys are crucial to confirm the occurrence and infection status of the freshwater intermediate host snails of S. haematobium and UgS transmission risk in the studied HDs and other endemic settings in Cameroon.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study revealed an overall UgS prevalence of 16.2% (97/600). S. haematobium eggs were found in 28.3% of pupils in the Lagdo HD, 12.0% in Loum HD, and nil in Njombe-Penja HD. Epidemiological factors of UgS infection were HDs and sex, with higher infection rates observed in males. These findings highlight the need for schistosomiasis control strategies, such as the improvement of WaSH infrastructures, environmental sanitation, monitoring and control of intermediate host snails in at-risk localities/populations. Moreover, community-based health education campaigns are needed to raise awareness on the mode of transmission and risk factors, to augment control efforts, prevent the spread and eventually eliminate schistosomiasis.
