RNA sequencing resolves a novel noncanonical splice-region variant in PHKA2 causing glycogen storage disease type IX α2: a case report
Authors: Fan Chen, Jiayi He, Henghui Cheng, Sainan Shu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlycogen storage disease type IX α2 (GSD IX α2) is an X-linked hepatic glycogenosis caused by pathogenic variants in PHKA2. Noncanonical splice-region variants located outside the invariant GT/AG dinucleotides pose significant interpretive challenges, as in silico predictions alone are often insufficient for definitive classification.Case DescriptionWe report a 2.9-year-old boy presenting with short stature, hepatomegaly, markedly elevated aminotransferases, fasting hypoglycemia with ketonuria, hypercholesterolemia, coagulation parameter abnormalities (decreased fibrinogen and prolonged thrombin time), and histological evidence of early hepatic fibrosis as demonstrated by Masson’s trichrome staining (portal fibrosis and perisinusoidal fibrosis). Whole-exome sequencing (WES) identified a hemizygous, previously unreported PHKA2 variant [NM_000292.3:c.2517+5G>T, genomic location (GRCh38): NC_000023.11: g.18907895G>T], initially classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) under American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria. RNA sequencing of peripheral blood leukocytes demonstrated predominant exon 22 skipping in 94.2% of informative junction reads, predicting a frameshift and premature termination codon [p.(Gly788Profs*74)] with predicted loss of the C-terminal CBL 2 subdomain. Incorporating this transcript-level evidence, the variant was reclassified as pathogenic (PVS1 + PM2_Supporting + PP4). Following dietary management with uncooked cornstarch supplementation, the patient showed progressive biochemical improvement over a 2.2-year follow-up.ConclusionsThis case expands the mutational spectrum of PHKA2 and demonstrates that RNA sequencing of accessible tissues is a practical and diagnostically informative strategy for resolving noncanonical splice-region variants in pediatric hepatic GSD. Early hepatic fibrosis detected by histological examination before age 3 years underscores the importance of longitudinal hepatic surveillance in GSD IX α2.

Highlight box

Key findings

• We report a boy with hepatic glycogen storage disease type IX α2 (GSD IX α2) carrying a novel PHKA2 noncanonical splice-region variant (c.2517+5G>T). Blood RNA sequencing demonstrated predominant exon 22 skipping (94.2% of informative junction reads), predicting a frameshift and premature stop (p.Gly788Profs*74). Early hepatic fibrosis was detected by liver biopsy, with histological evidence from Masson’s trichrome staining (portal fibrosis and perisinusoidal fibrosis), consistent with early hepatic fibrotic remodeling.

What is known and what is new?

• PHKA2-related GSD IX α2 shows variable severity; splice-site variants contribute to disease, but noncanonical splice-region changes are frequently classified as variant of uncertain significance (VUS) without transcript evidence. Some patients can develop fibrosis or cirrhosis early in life.

• This report describes the first documented instance of c.2517+5G>T in PHKA2, with RNA sequencing providing direct transcript-level evidence to resolve a noncanonical splice-region VUS, and documents early liver fibrosis in a child under 3 years of age with GSD IX α2.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Transcript-based testing [RNA sequencing/reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)] should be considered when whole-exome sequencing identifies suspected noncanonical splice-region variants in hepatic GSDs, to reduce the time to definitive molecular diagnosis. Early and sustained hepatic surveillance is warranted in all GSD IX α2 patients, irrespective of apparent clinical mildness at presentation.

Introduction

Glycogen storage diseases (GSDs) constitute a heterogeneous group of inherited metabolic disorders arising from defects in glycogen synthesis or degradation, resulting in pathologic glycogen accumulation predominantly in the liver, skeletal muscle, or both (1-3). Molecular advances have progressively refined the enzyme-based classification, enabling more precise subtype delineation (3,4). Among hepatic subtypes, glycogen storage disease type IX (GSD IX) results from deficiency of phosphorylase kinase (PhK), a multimeric enzyme composed of regulatory (α, β), catalytic (γ), and calmodulin (δ) subunits (5,6). GSD IX is further subdivided into IX α1, IX α2, IX β, and IX γ2, corresponding to pathogenic variants in PHKA1, PHKA2, PHKB, and PHKG2, respectively (1).

GSD IX α2 is the most prevalent subtype, accounting for approximately 75% of GSD IX cases. It is inherited in an X-linked recessive pattern and results from pathogenic variants in PHKA2 (Xp22.13) (6-8). PHKA2 encodes the liver-specific α2 subunit of PhK, which is required for normal hepatic glycogenolysis and glucose homeostasis (9). Clinical manifestations include hepatomegaly, elevated aminotransferases, dyslipidemia, and fasting ketotic hypoglycemia (6,8). A particularly challenging aspect of molecular diagnosis concerns noncanonical splice-region variants located outside the invariant GT/AG dinucleotides. For such variants, in silico predictions alone rarely provide the certainty required for definitive clinical classification, and transcript-level evidence is seldom available in routine diagnostic practice.

Here, we describe a male pediatric patient with GSD IX α2 in whom whole-exome sequencing (WES) identified a previously unreported PHKA2 splice-region variant (c.2517+5G>T). RNA sequencing was performed to characterize its splicing consequence and to enable evidence-based reclassification. This case broadens the known mutational spectrum of PHKA2 and illustrates the diagnostic value of RNA-based analysis in resolving noncanonical splice-region variants of uncertain significance. Case presentation

Written informed consent was obtained from the parents of the patient for the publication of this case report and accompanying images. Clinical history and physical examination

A boy was delivered at term following an uneventful pregnancy (birth weight 3.15 kg). He was the first child of a nonconsanguineous Chinese couple. Early developmental milestones, including motor and language acquisition, were reported as age-appropriate. Family history was unremarkable for inherited metabolic disorders. At 2.6 years of age, he was admitted to a local hospital for acute diarrhea. Laboratory investigations revealed markedly elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT, 343 U/L) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST, 430 U/L), as well as low fasting blood glucose (3.34 mmol/L; Table 1). Urinary ketones were positive at this presentation, consistent with ketotic hypoglycemia secondary to impaired hepatic glycogenolysis. Aminotransferases decreased transiently following supportive therapy, which comprised hepatoprotective agents (compound glycyrrhizin administered intravenously), and general supportive care including intravenous hydration, but remained above the reference range. The family also reported intermittent abdominal discomfort following strenuous physical activity; the etiology of this symptom remains uncertain, though exercise-induced impairment of glycogenolysis in the context of PhK deficiency cannot be excluded.

At 2.9 years of age, he was referred to our center for further evaluation. The laboratory values obtained at this visit reflect his baseline metabolic status. According to Chinese pediatric growth standards (10), weighed 13.8 kg (31.5th percentile) and height 85.0 cm (<3rd percentile), consistent with growth impairment potentially reflecting chronic metabolic stress. Abdominal examination revealed distension with a palpable liver edge 5.0 cm below the right costal margin and firm consistency; no splenomegaly was detected. Laboratory investigations confirmed elevated ALT (147 U/L), AST (166 U/L), and total bile acids (28.6 µmol/L). Lipid profiling demonstrated hypercholesterolemia with total cholesterol of 5.50 mmol/L and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol of 3.62 mmol/L (Table 1). Coagulation testing revealed decreased fibrinogen (1.47 g/L; reference 1.80–4.00 g/L) and prolonged thrombin time (25.3 s; reference <21.0 s); notably, the international normalized ratio (INR) was 1.00 and prothrombin time was 10.9 s, both within the normal reference range. Congenital hypofibrinogenemia and dysfibrinogenemia were considered but deemed unlikely in the absence of a relevant family history and given the concurrent hepatocellular injury pattern; the coagulation abnormalities were therefore attributed to early impairment of hepatic synthetic function secondary to glycogen-mediated hepatocellular injury.

Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis was performed as part of the metabolic workup to evaluate for acid-base disturbances in the context of fasting intolerance and suspected metabolic liver disease. ABG indicated mild metabolic acidosis (pH 7.356, bicarbonate 19.1 mmol/L, base excess −5.20 mmol/L; Table 2), which was attributed to ketotic metabolic stress—a recognized metabolic consequence of impaired hepatic glycogenolysis in PhK deficiency. Fasting blood glucose was 3.60 mmol/L, urinary ketones were again positive at this assessment, supporting this interpretation. Lactate dehydrogenase was elevated, whereas blood lactate, pyruvate, ammonia, uric acid, creatinine, and electrolytes were within normal limits. Tandem mass spectrometry of blood amino acids and urinary gas chromatography-mass spectrometry were unremarkable. Serological screening for hepatotropic viruses (hepatitis A, B, C, E), rubella virus, herpes simplex virus, Epstein-Barr virus, human immunodeficiency virus, Treponema pallidum, Toxoplasma gondii, cytomegalovirus, and human parvovirus B19 was negative. Alternative causes were systematically excluded: autoimmune hepatitis was considered but antinuclear antibody, anti-smooth muscle antibody, and anti-liver-kidney microsomal antibody were all negative; Wilson’s disease was excluded by normal serum ceruloplasmin and 24-hour urinary copper; and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease was considered unlikely given the patient’s young age and absence of obesity or metabolic syndrome features.

Imaging and histopathology

Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated hepatomegaly with echogenicity suggestive of steatosis (Figure 1). The spleen was of normal size and echogenicity on ultrasonography. Liver biopsy was performed via percutaneous needle biopsy on clinical grounds given the combination of persistent hepatomegaly, markedly elevated aminotransferases, and coagulation parameter abnormalities. Liver biopsy H&E staining demonstrated enlarged hepatocytes and mild steatosis, accompanied by mild to moderate chronic inflammatory cell infiltration within the portal tracts (Figure 2A). Masson’s trichrome staining revealed fibrous collagen deposition predominantly distributed in the portal areas; additionally, reticular or short streak-like blue-stained collagen fibers were identified between hepatocyte cords and around hepatic sinusoids (Figure 2B). Collectively, these findings were consistent with early hepatic fibrosis. Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining was positive, confirming abundant intracytoplasmic glycogen accumulation (Figure 2C), while PAS with diastase (PAS-D) staining showed loss of the positive signal following diastase digestion (Figure 2D), confirming that the accumulated material was diastase-digestible glycogen, consistent with a hepatic glycogen storage disorder. Transmission electron microscopy revealed marked glycogen accumulation within hepatocytes, along with a mild increase in intracellular lipid droplets, predominantly of small-to-medium sizes. In addition, a scant amount of bundled collagen deposition was observed in the space of Disse (Figure 2E).

Genetic investigations

WES and variant interpretation

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples obtained from the proband and both parents. Detailed WES methods are provided in the Supplementary Appendix. WES identified a hemizygous variant in PHKA2 (NM_000292.3: c.2517+5G>T; GRCh38: NC_000023.11: g.18907895G>T) in the proband; no additional candidate variants were detected in genes associated with hepatic GSDs. Sanger sequencing confirmed maternal heterozygosity, consistent with X-linked inheritance (Figure 3A,3B). The variant was absent from gnomAD and ExAC. SpliceAI predicted disruption of the exon 22 donor site (donor loss score 0.82, acceptor loss score 0). Based on population absence (PM2_Supporting) and a phenotype highly specific for GSD IX α2 (PP4), the variant was initially classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) under American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG)/Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) criteria (11), as available evidence was insufficient for pathogenic or likely pathogenic classification.

RNA sequencing and splice analysis

Total RNA was isolated from peripheral blood leukocytes of the proband. Although PHKA2 expression in leukocytes is relatively low compared with hepatic tissue, PHKA2 transcripts were confirmed detectable at sufficient read depth to support junction-level analysis. Detailed RNA-seq methods are provided in the Appendix 1.

RNA sequencing revealed predominant exon 22 skipping, with approximately 94.2% of informative junction reads (113 of 120 reads spanning the exon 21–exon 23 junction) supporting the exon-skipped isoform (Figure 3C). Notably, while PHKA2 is expressed at very low levels in leukocytes, the detection of a 94.2% aberrant splicing ratio provides robust qualitative evidence confirming the exon 22 skipping event, even though it may not serve as a precise quantitative measure of the overall transcript pool. Full-length transcript analysis revealed no alternative splice isoforms beyond the canonical full-length transcript. Exon 22 skipping is predicted to shift the reading frame and introduce a premature termination codon, yielding a truncated protein lacking the C-terminal CBL 2 subdomain [p.(Gly788Profs*74)]. AlphaFold2-based structural visualization predicted substantial C-terminal truncation relative to the wild-type model (Figure 3D).

Given that loss of function is an established pathogenic mechanism for PHKA2, and that RNA sequencing directly demonstrated a frameshifting splice outcome affecting the overwhelming majority of transcripts (12), the variant was reclassified as pathogenic. Applied evidence criteria were: PVS1 (very strong; RNA-seq confirmed aberrant splicing), PM2_Supporting, and PP4, collectively satisfying the ACMG/AMP threshold for pathogenic.

Treatment and follow-up

Following diagnostic confirmation, dietary management comprised frequent uncooked cornstarch supplementation (1–2 g/kg per dose, 4–6 times daily) to prevent fasting hypoglycemia, with total carbohydrate intake targeted at approximately 50–55% of total energy intake and protein intake maintained at 2–3 g/kg/day. No episode of severe symptomatic hypoglycemia requiring emergency glucose administration was documented under dietary management.

Over a 2.2-year follow-up period (age 2.9 to 5.1 years), liver enzyme activities, lipid parameters, coagulation parameters, and blood glucose showed progressive improvement toward the normal range (Table 1). Hepatomegaly improved on ultrasonography (Figure 1). At the most recent evaluation (age 5.1 years), weight was 19.8 kg (56.3th percentile), and height was 108.5 cm (17.6th percentile). The liver was palpable 2.0 cm below the right costal margin; no splenomegaly was detected. No clinical signs of skeletal abnormalities were identified throughout the follow-up. The patient remains under active clinical surveillance.

Discussion

GSD IX α2 has historically been considered to have a more favorable clinical course compared with GSD IX γ2; however, accumulating evidence indicates that a clinically significant subset of patients develop hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis, and the designation of ‘benign’ should be applied with caution (8). Our patient exhibited this characteristic phenotype—fasting hypoglycemia with ketonuria—but additionally displayed coagulation parameter abnormalities and histological evidence of early hepatic fibrosis, suggesting a comparatively severe disease course within the GSD IX α2 spectrum. The coagulation abnormalities, manifesting as decreased fibrinogen and prolonged thrombin time with a normal INR, most likely reflect early impairment of hepatic synthetic function secondary to glycogen-mediated hepatocellular injury; subtle coagulation test abnormalities have been reported in patients with significant hepatocellular involvement (3).

Biopsy-based cohort data underscore the fibrotic risk in GSD IX α2: Fernandes et al. documented fibrosis or cirrhosis in 47.8% of 46 biopsied patients (13), and Degrassi et al. observed METAVIR scores ranging from F1 to F3 (14), though biopsy cohorts are subject to selection bias toward more severe disease. Beyzaei et al. reported cirrhosis developing as early as 3 years of age in a patient harboring a PHKA2 splicing variant (c.2226+2T>C) (15). In the present case, portal fibrosis and perisinusoidal fibrosis were detected before 3 years of age, underscoring the importance of early and sustained hepatic surveillance. Longitudinal non-invasive fibrosis monitoring, such as liver elastography, is recommended for all GSD IX α2 patients regardless of apparent initial severity.

Accurate subtyping of hepatic GSDs requires molecular confirmation, as GSD I, III, VI, and IX α2 share overlapping biochemical and imaging features (14,16,17). PHKA2 encodes a 1,235 amino acid protein with an N-terminal GHL domain and a C-terminal IBL domain containing the CBL1/CBL2 subdomains (18,19). According to the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD Professional, version 2024.4), 198 germline variants have been documented in PHKA2, with missense/nonsense mutations predominating (51.0%), followed by small deletions (18.5%) and splicing variants (11.5%). The c.2517+5G>T variant lies five nucleotides downstream of the exon 22 donor site, outside the invariant GT dinucleotide, exemplifying the interpretive difficulty posed by noncanonical splice-region changes when only DNA-level data are available.

RNA sequencing provided direct transcript-level evidence that c.2517+5G>T causes near-complete exon 22 skipping, introducing a frameshift and predicted loss of the CBL 2 subdomain. Prior structural and biochemical studies suggest that this subdomain participates in interactions between the α2 regulatory and γ catalytic subunits (19,20), raising the possibility that its loss impairs PhK assembly or catalytic activity or functional studies in patient-derived cells. Furthermore, because splicing was assessed in peripheral blood leukocytes, tissue-specific differences in splicing efficiency or nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD) activity in hepatic tissue cannot be excluded.

From a diagnostic standpoint, this case illustrates a clinically applicable strategy: when in silico predictions suggest a splice effect but are insufficient for definitive classification, RNA sequencing of an accessible tissue can provide the transcript-level evidence needed to upgrade a VUS. This approach is particularly relevant for GSD IX α2, where splicing variants constitute a substantial proportion of the PHKA2 variant spectrum and where timely molecular diagnosis directly informs dietary management and surveillance planning.

Growth monitoring is an essential component of long-term follow-up in GSD IX α2 (8). The height of our patient at presentation (<3rd percentile) is consistent with growth impairment reported in other GSD IX α2 patients (6,21). The improvement in height percentile during follow-up (from <3rd to 17.6th percentile) is encouraging and may reflect improved metabolic control; however, continued longitudinal monitoring is required. As the patient approaches adolescence, endocrine surveillance—encompassing growth hormone secretion, thyroid function, and pubertal development—should be incorporated into the long-term follow-up protocol (6,22,23). Periodic assessment of vitamin D status and, when appropriate, bone mineral density measurement should also be considered.

This report has several limitations. First, splicing was assessed in peripheral blood leukocytes rather than liver tissue; RNA sequencing of the biopsy specimen would have provided more direct evidence, but insufficient residual tissue was preserved for nucleic acid extraction. We recommend prospective tissue banking of liver biopsy material in future cases with suspected noncanonical splice-region variants. Second, NMD inhibition experiments were not performed, and PhK enzyme activity was not measured, precluding direct biochemical confirmation of functional impairment. Third, serial non-invasive fibrosis assessment (e.g., liver elastography, FIB-4 index) was not performed during follow-up; consequently, the trajectory of the early fibrotic changes cannot be determined, and non-invasive fibrosis monitoring is planned for subsequent visits. Finally, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) was not used during follow‑up, as fasting glucose levels progressively normalized with dietary management and no symptomatic hypoglycemia was reported. Nonetheless, CGM could be considered in future assessments, especially during intercurrent illness or prolonged fasting. The approximately 2-year follow-up period is insufficient to exclude long-term hepatic complications.

Conclusions

We identified a novel PHKA2 splice-region variant (c.2517+5G>T) in a boy with hepatic GSD IX α2. RNA sequencing demonstrated predominant exon 22 skipping and a consequent frameshift (p.(Gly788Profs*74)), supporting pathogenic reclassification. The additional findings of coagulation parameter abnormalities and early hepatic fibrosis represent features at the severe end of the GSD IX α2 spectrum and reinforce the need for longitudinal hepatic monitoring regardless of apparent initial mildness. This case further expands the mutational spectrum of PHKA2 and supports transcript-level testing as a practical and diagnostically informative approach to resolving noncanonical splice-region variants in pediatric hepatic GSD.
