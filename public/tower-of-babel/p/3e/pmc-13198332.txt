Safety and efficacy of ultralow-dose fluoroscopy in left atrial appendage occlusion
Authors: Joe Demian, Arwa Younis, Abdel Hadi El Hajjar, Mohamed Kanj, Walid I Saliba, Pasquale Santangeli, Chadi Tabaja, Bryan Baranowski, Tyler L Taigen, Mandeep Bhargava, Oussama M Wazni, Ayman A Hussein

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMinimizing radiation exposure has become a growing priority in electrophysiology, with increasing adoption of zero- or minimal-fluoroscopy techniques. Little is known about left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) using ultralow-dose fluoroscopy.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the safety and efficacy of an ultralow-dose fluoroscopy strategy for LAAO.MethodsIn a prospectively maintained registry at our center, 46 patients underwent LAAO with ultralow-dose fluoroscopy using Watchman FLX devices (Boston Scientific, Minneapolis, MN). The ultralow-dose strategy was a priori planned for these patients with the goal of minimizing radiation exposure (total radiation exposure <10 mGy), while relying on transesophageal echocardiography guidance for device placement. The ultralow-dose fluoroscopy group was compared using propensity score matching with patients treated with standard fluoroscopy guidance (1:3 matching; n = 46 vs 138). Periprocedural adverse events were infrequent and did not differ significantly (none in ultralow dose vs 3 cases [2.2%] in standard dose; P = .6). Introduction

Minimizing radiation exposure has become a growing priority in electrophysiology, with increasing adoption of zero- or minimal-fluoroscopy techniques. Although such strategies are well established in pediatric electrophysiology and atrial fibrillation ablation, their application to left atrial appendage (LAA) occlusion (LAAO) is gaining traction.

LAAO has traditionally relied on fluoroscopic imaging and contrast injection, in combination with echocardiographic guidance, to facilitate transseptal puncture, catheter manipulation, and precise device deployment.1,2 Although individual LAAO procedures typically involve limited fluoroscopy durations, these result in notable cumulative radiation exposure to patients and the electrophysiology laboratory personnel over time.3, 4, 5

Reflecting growing awareness of radiation hazards, adherence to the ALARA (as low as reasonably achievable) principle has been emphasized in electrophysiology to minimize unnecessary radiation exposure.6 Various strategies have emerged to mitigate fluoroscopy exposure during LAAO, including broader adoption of intracardiac echocardiography (ICE),7 which can reduce reliance on continuous fluoroscopic visualization.8

In addition, advanced imaging technologies that integrate real-time echocardiographic-fluoroscopic fusion imaging have significantly decreased radiation doses without compromising procedural effectiveness.9,10 Furthermore, specialized centers have demonstrated the feasibility of near-zero-fluoroscopy approaches using ICE and electroanatomic mapping alone,11,12 substantially limiting radiation exposure.

There is limited evidence on whether or not substantially reduced fluoroscopy doses can be consistently implemented, using primarily transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) guidance for device placement, without compromising procedural or clinical outcomes. This study aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of an ultralow-dose fluoroscopy protocol during LAAO.

Methods

Population

All consecutive patients who underwent LAAO between September 2018 and October 2022 at the Cleveland Clinic were enrolled in a prospectively maintained institutional registry. Patients undergoing LAAO using ultralow-dose fluoroscopy were included in this study. The ultralow-dose strategy was a priori planned for these patients with the goal of minimizing radiation exposure (total radiation dose <10 mGy), while relying on TEE guidance for device placement.

To account for potential baseline differences and minimize selection bias, these patients were matched 1:3 to controls who underwent standard fluoroscopy-guided LAAO using propensity score matching. All procedures used the Watchman FLX device (Boston Scientific, Minneapolis, MN). Clinical, procedural, and follow-up imaging data were collected through 1 year after the procedure. The study was approved by the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board, with a waiver of informed consent for the analysis of deidentified registry data. This work adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Periprocedural anticoagulation

Patients were maintained on therapeutic oral anticoagulation (OAC) with either warfarin or a direct OAC (DOAC). The anticoagulation regimen was determined by the operators, factoring in patient-specific comorbidities. For patients on warfarin, therapeutic levels of international normalized ratio (2–3) were required preprocedurally, on the day of the procedure, and during follow-up. For patients on DOACs, anticoagulants were started ideally 2–3 weeks before the procedure and at least 24–48 hours before the procedure. All patients on DOACs received an appropriate dose of anticoagulation, accounting for kidney function when necessary. Only a single dose was withheld immediately before the procedure, except for rivaroxaban, which was uninterrupted (typically taken in the afternoon the day before). DOACs were then restarted immediately after device implantation.

LAAO procedures

All procedures were conducted under general anesthesia and with TEE guidance. Preprocedural cardiac computed tomography scans were performed in some patients at the operators’ discretion. Femoral venous access was obtained using ultrasound guidance, followed by administration of intravenous heparin to maintain an activated clotting time between 300 and 350 seconds.

A single transseptal puncture was performed to access the left atrium using standard techniques, and the sheath was exchanged over a wire for the Watchman FXD delivery system. In the ultralow-dose group, transseptal access was performed under echocardiographic guidance, with ICE as the primary imaging modality and TEE used selectively to facilitate a low posterior location at the interatrial septum. Under ICE guidance, the sheath was advanced over a wire into the superior vena cava. The Watchman delivery sheath was then advanced over a wire into the LA, with the wire positioned in the left superior pulmonary vein, as confirmed with ICE and TEE. In the standard-dose group, transseptal access was guided by fluoroscopy.

After standard measurements were obtained with TEE, an appropriately sized Watchman device was selected with the goal of achieving 15%–25% compression range. Device deployment and assessment were performed primarily under TEE guidance, with adjunct ICE used selectively to enhance near-field visualization when TEE windows were suboptimal. The Watchman FLX device was then advanced through the delivery sheath and deployed in the target position. Partial or full recapture and repositioning were performed as needed to achieve optimal positioning. The standard PASS (position, anchor, size, and seal) criteria and a stability (tug) test were used to confirm adequate device placement. Once satisfactory positioning was achieved, the device was released, the sheath was removed, and heparin was partially reversed with protamine. Vascular hemostasis was achieved with manual pressure, figure-of-eight sutures, or vascular closure devices, at operators’ discretion.

Outcomes measures and follow-up

Patients were monitored in the hospital for 1 night and typically discharged the next day. All patients were assessed for procedural complications immediately after the procedure and before hospital discharge. Upon discharge, patients were encouraged to immediately report any symptoms, including bleeding. Periprocedural adverse events were defined as any complication occurring within 7 days of device implantation or before hospital discharge. These included all-cause mortality, ischemic stroke, systemic embolism, pericardial effusion requiring intervention, device migration or embolization, and any device- or procedure-related event necessitating surgical or major endovascular intervention. Prospective adjudication of procedure-related complications was performed in concordance with standard published guidelines.

A follow-up visit was scheduled at 45 days after the procedure, where TEE imaging was performed to assess device position, device-related thrombus (DRT), and peridevice leak (PDL). Successful LAA closure was defined as a complete seal without PDL or residual peridevice flow of ≤5 mm as assessed by TEE at 45-day follow-up. DRT was defined on follow-up TEE as an echo-dense mass, suggestive of or consistent with thrombus, in contact with the device on the left atrial aspect, visible in multiple imaging planes, not explained by artifact, and inconsistent with expected device healing/device integration.

Long-term adverse events included all-cause mortality, stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic), transient ischemic attack, systemic embolism, and pericardial effusion requiring intervention beyond the periprocedural period.

Statistical analysis

Patients were matched using nearest-neighbor propensity score matching with a 1:3 ratio (ultralow dose to standard dose), without replacement, based on age, sex, and CHA2DS2-VASc score. The propensity score was estimated using logistic regression (MatchIt package in R). Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance. Covariate balance for the matching variables was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an SMD of <0.10 considered acceptable. Overlap in propensity score distributions between groups was assessed by visual inspection. There were no missing data for propensity score covariates.

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Student t test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Time-to-event outcomes were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier estimates, with comparisons performed using the log-rank test. Cumulative incidence of thromboembolic events was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier-based methodology under the assumption of comparable competing risks across matched groups. A 2-tailed P < .05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.4.3.

Results

Study population

Among 568 registry patients (ultralow dose n = 46; standard dose n = 522), propensity score matching produced a matched cohort of 184 patients: 46 in the ultralow-dose group matched to 138 standard-dose controls. Matched variables were well balanced across groups (SMDs: age 0.003; sex <0.001; CHA2DS2-VASc score 0.036). Median age was similar between groups (75.5 [67.3–79] vs 75 years [68–78.8]; P = .99). Both groups had similar sex (both 41.3% males; P > .99) and CHA2DS2-VASc score distributions (both 4 [3–5]; P = .9). Body mass index, ejection fraction, and HAS-BLED scores were also comparable.

Common comorbidities—including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, atrial fibrillation/flutter, and bleeding history—were also similar, except for coronary artery disease (15.2% vs 34.1%; P = .03), which had a lower proportion in the ultralow-dose group. No significant differences were observed in New York Heart Association functional class, previous pacemaker implantation, or other structural heart conditions. Procedural characteristics

Procedural indications were similar between the groups, including history of major bleeding (69.6% vs 63.8%; P = .6), patient preference (30.4% vs 28.3%; P = .9), and high fall risk (17.4% vs 19.6%; P = .9). Compared with the standard fluoroscopy group, the ultralow-dose group had significantly shorter procedure times (42 [33–50] vs 60 minutes [47–78.2]; P < .001) and fluoroscopy times (4.5 [2.5–5.5] vs 8.6 minutes [6.1–12.4]; P < .001). As expected, radiation dose (0.01 [0.01–0.01] vs 0.05 mGy [0.03–0.09]; P < .001) and total radiation dose (8 [5–9] vs 49.5 mGy [25–90.75]; P < .001) were markedly lower in the ultralow-dose group.

Contrast injection was less frequently used in the ultralow-dose group (32.6% vs 55.1%; P = .01). Rates of device recaptures (P = .999), device repositioning (P = .999), and anatomic difficulties (P = .1) did not differ significantly. Similarly, there were no significant differences in concomitant atrial fibrillation ablation (P = .8), LAA orifice maximal width (23 [20–25] vs 22 mm [19–24]; P = .4), device maximal compression (23.5 [21–27.25] vs 22 mm [19–25]; P = .05), device minimal compression (17 [15.5–20] vs 17.5 mm [15–22]; P = .8), or device size distribution (P = .4).

Procedural success was achieved in 100% of cases in both groups. There was no significant difference in bleeding events occurring after the procedure—1 gastrointestinal bleed in the ultralow-dose group (2.2%) vs 2 events in the standard-dose group (1.4%), including 1 gastrointestinal bleed and 1 case of hematuria (P > .99). Anemia requiring transfusion occurred in none of the ultralow-dose cases and in 1.4% of the standard group (P > .99). Detailed procedural parameters and implant data are presented in Table 2.

Periprocedural adverse events

Within the periprocedural window, there were no cases of all-cause mortality, ischemic stroke, systemic embolism, device dislodgement, or TEE-related esophageal injury in either group. Pericardial effusion requiring intervention occurred in 1 patient (0.7%) in the standard fluoroscopy group—managed with open-heart surgery—and none in the ultralow-dose group (P > .99). Similarly, 1 pseudoaneurysm repair and 1 hemothorax requiring thoracocentesis were observed in the standard group vs none in the ultralow-dose group (P > .99 for each). The overall periprocedural adverse event rate was 0% in the ultralow-dose group vs 2.2% in the standard group, with no statistically significant difference (P = .6). OAC was discontinued prematurely before the 45-day follow-up owing to bleeding events—all gastrointestinal—in 5 ultralow-dose patients (10.9%) and 5 standard-dose patients (3.6%) (P = .1). At the 45-day follow-up visit, OACs were discontinued in 32 ultralow-dose patients (78%) and 117 standard-dose patients (88%) based on adequate seal and clinical judgment (P = .2). An additional 6 (14.6%) and 9 patients (6.8%), respectively, discontinued OAC by 6 months (P = .2). The remaining patients continued OAC beyond 6 months at the discretion of the treating physician (P = .7).

After anticoagulation discontinuation, 19 ultralow-dose patients (57.6%) and 63 standard-dose patients (52.5%) were transitioned to dual antiplatelet therapy (P = .8), whereas 12 (36.4%) and 56 patients (46.7%), respectively, received aspirin (P = .4). At 1 year, 36 ultralow-dose (85.7%) and 124 standard-dose patients (93.2%) remained on aspirin (P = .2). Clopidogrel was used in 1 standard-dose patient (0.8%), whereas OAC was continued in 1 ultralow-dose patient (2.4%) vs 7 standard-dose patients (5.3%) (P = .7). 5 patients (11.9%) in the ultralow-dose group were no longer on any antithrombotic therapy at 1 year compared with 1 (0.8%) in the standard-dose group (P = .003).

Closure outcomes

At 45-day follow-up, a significant PDL (>5 mm) was observed in 1 patient (2.2%) in the ultralow-dose group and in none of the standard-dose group, yielding successful LAA closure rates of 97.8% (45 of 46) and 100% (138 of 138), respectively (P = .3). The patient with significant PDL had OAC discontinued at the follow-up visit but did not return for subsequent imaging; no adverse clinical events were reported in this patient. Procedural and 45-day closure success rates by fluoroscopy group are presented in Supplemental Figure 1.

DRT occurred in 1 patient (2.2%) in the ultralow-dose group, with none in the standard group (P = .3). Notably, the affected patient had both DOAC and antiplatelet therapy discontinued before the 45-day visit owing to gastrointestinal bleeding. Anticoagulation with apixaban was subsequently resumed after the diagnosis of DRT. Follow-up TEE at 6 months demonstrated an interval decrease in thrombotic material on the device, and the patient was confirmed to be free of DRT during a 1-year TEE.

Long-term adverse events

Kaplan-Meier-based incidence rates for major clinical events are presented in Table 5. The incidence of long-term adverse events over 1 year was low and did not differ significantly between groups. All-cause mortality occurred in 4 of 46 patients (8.7%) in the ultralow-dose group and 4 of 138 patients (2.9%) in the standard group, although the difference was not significant (P = .1). All deaths in the ultralow-dose group were attributed to noncardiac causes, whereas 1 death in the standard-dose group was classified as cardiac (owing to heart failure) and another as of unknown cause.

Ischemic stroke rates were comparable, occurring in 2 of 46 patients (4.3%) in the ultralow-dose group and 3 of 138 patients (2.2%) in the standard group (P = .4), with no reported cases of hemorrhagic stroke in either group. Transient ischemic attack occurred in 1 patient (2.2%) in the ultralow-dose group and 4 patients (3.0%) in the standard group (P = .8). No cases of systemic embolism were observed in either group. In addition, no patients developed a delayed pericardial effusion requiring intervention.

Thromboembolic events

Overall, thromboembolic events occurred infrequently and were similar between groups. The annualized Kaplan-Meier event rate at 1 year of ischemic stroke or systemic embolism was 4.3% (95% confidence interval 0.0–10.1) in the ultralow-dose fluoroscopy group (2 patients of 46) and 2.2% (95% confidence interval 0.0–4.7) in the standard-dose group (3 patients of 138), with no statistically significant difference (log-rank P = .4). Discussion

In this propensity-matched study at a tertiary care center, we demonstrated that an ultralow-dose fluoroscopy strategy for TEE-guided LAAO can be successfully implemented without compromising procedural efficacy or safety compared with the standard-dose approach. Both the ultralow-dose and standard-dose groups achieved high success rates, with no significant differences in major complication rates.

Our results align with the established safety and efficacy profile of the Watchman FLX device reported in previous trials. In the Pinnacle FLX study, which used conventional fluoroscopic and TEE guidance, the device was successfully implanted in 98.8% of patients with a 0.5% major procedure-related complication rate.13 Similarly, we achieved 100% procedural success in both groups, with high LAA closure success rates sustained—and minimal PDL—at 45-day follow-up. Periprocedural adverse events were uncommon in both cohorts, and thromboembolic events were infrequent with similar event rates, further supporting procedural safety. Our observed outcomes are also consistent with contemporary real-world registries demonstrating low adverse event rates with Watchman FLX in general practice.14 DRT occurred in 1 ultralow-dose case (2.2%), consistent with published rates across the Pinnacle trial13 and large-scale registries.15 Importantly, DRT occurred in the context of early OAC discontinuation and resolved without clinical sequelae. These findings suggest that ultralow-dose fluoroscopy does not adversely affect closure durability or thrombotic risk.

Radiation minimization in LAAO is increasingly viewed as a continuum, ranging from conventional fluoroscopy-plus-echocardiography to minimal- and fluoroscopy-free workflows. By capping fluoroscopy to <10 mGy per case, we were able to achieve a marked reduction in dose compared with conventional LAAO, translating to a lower radiation exposure for patients. The approach is beneficial beyond patient radiation burden, given that it has practical occupational advantages for operators and their team by reducing cumulative exposure and the orthopedic burden of prolonged lead use. Consistent with an echo-forward workflow, contrast was used less frequently in the ultralow-dose group, reinforcing that fluoroscopy and contrast reduction can be achieved in parallel when echocardiographic imaging is sufficient.

The substantial dose reduction reflects a deliberate workflow shift in which echocardiography was used to replace fluoroscopy during procedural steps that commonly generate “background” fluoroscopy time, particularly transseptal targeting and left-sided wire/sheath navigation. In conventional workflows, operators often default to continuous fluoroscopic tracking during these navigation steps, even when the device deployment itself is largely TEE guided. In the ultralow-dose cohort, septal crossing and early left atrial navigation were guided primarily by ICE with TEE used for confirmation, whereas device placement and release remained primarily TEE-guided with adjunct ICE to improve near-field visualization when TEE windows were limited. Collectively, these steps reduced reliance on fluoroscopic confirmation during both navigation and deployment.

Our approach complements previous proof-of-concept studies showing that LAA closure may be performed with minimal or even zero fluoroscopy. Chu et al11 first demonstrated the feasibility of completely fluoroscopy-free LAAO using ICE and an electroanatomic mapping system for catheter navigation. In their initial series of ICE-guided LAAO (7 patients vs 7 matched controls), implant success was achieved in all cases, with no reported complications. More recently, Flautt et al12 reported that LAAO can be accomplished entirely under ICE guidance without any fluoroscopy, particularly with the use of a novel 4-dimensional (4D) ICE catheter. Our study builds upon these experiences by confirming in a larger, matched cohort that near-elimination of fluoroscopy is achievable without compromising safety or efficacy. Unlike previously described approaches that leverage electroanatomic mapping11 or advanced 4D ICE12 platforms, ours preserved an echocardiography-guided approach to both navigation and implantation. ICE and TEE provided complementary visualization and confirmation across procedural steps, allowing fluoroscopy to be limited to brief, targeted checks rather than continuous guidance.

A recent large TEE-guided zero-fluoroscopy study by Chakravarty et al16 using Watchman FLX further supports the feasibility of a fluoroscopy-eliminating approach. In that series, successful zero-fluoroscopy implantation was achieved in 85.8% of 239 attempts, whereas 14.2% required bailout fluoroscopy owing to imaging or procedural/anatomic challenges. Importantly, the need for bailout fluoroscopy decreased substantially with experience, consistent with an operator and team learning curve. These observations highlight both the promise of fluoroless LAAO and the reality that fluoroscopy may still be required in a meaningful minority of cases, particularly early in adoption or when echocardiographic visualization is limited. In this context, an ultralow-dose strategy offers a pragmatic, bailout-capable pathway: fluoroscopy remains available when needed for safety or efficiency, but its use is intentionally constrained to the minimum required while preserving an echocardiography-forward workflow rather than reverting to standard-dose continuous guidance.

Limitations

Certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a nonrandomized observational analysis, and although propensity score matching was used to balance baseline variables, there may still be some residual selection bias. It is possible that operators selected the ultralow-fluoroscopy strategy for cases they perceived to be more straightforward, which could confound the comparison; however, the matched design and similar procedural characteristics between groups mitigate this concern. Second, because propensity score matching restricts analysis to matched patients, results may not be fully generalizable to the entire registry cohort. Third, the ultralow-dose approach was implemented in a high-volume center with substantial resources and experienced operators, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to institutions without similar infrastructure or experience. Finally, although our protocol dramatically reduced radiation, it did not eliminate it entirely. Future advances, including integration of 4D ICE or electroanatomic mapping for catheter navigation, may further enable completely fluoroscopy-free LAAO at a broader scale.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that LAAO may be performed with ultralow-dose fluoroscopy, without sacrificing efficacy or safety. These findings support the broader adoption of radiation-sparing techniques in LAAO to enhance procedural safety while maintaining excellent outcomes.
