A novel risk model combining clinical and intravascular ultrasound-based calcific features to predict adverse outcomes in patients with severe coronary artery calcification undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention: the mACEF- Ca model
Authors: Xi Wu, Mingxing Wu, Haobo Huang, Zhe Liu, He Huang, Lei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere coronary artery calcification (CAC) significantly complicates percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), leading to increased procedural challenges and adverse outcomes. Existing clinical risk models such as the age, creatinine and ejection fraction (ACEF) score often lack lesion-specific anatomical data, limiting their predictive performance in high-risk cohorts.MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled patients with intravascular ultrasound (IVUS)-confirmed severe CAC who underwent non-emergent PCI with drug-eluting stent implantation. Independent predictors of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) over a median 2-year follow-up were identified using multivariate Cox regression. A novel modified ACEF score (mACEF) incorporating calcific features (mACEF-Ca) was constructed based on the original mACEF, presence of calcified nodule (CN), and a maximum calcium arc of 360°.ResultsOf the 785 included patients, 121 (15.4%) experienced MACE during the 2-year follow-up. Presence of a 360° calcium arc and CN were identified as independent predictors of MACE (HR = 1.63 and 2.61, respectively; both P < 0.001). The simplified mACEF-Ca formula was: mACEF + 1 (if maximum calcium arc = 360°) + 1 (if CN present). This model demonstrated superior discrimination (AUC = 0.778) compared to traditional ACEF-based models (all P < 0.05 by DeLong test). Kaplan–Meier analysis confirmed significantly poorer event-free survival in patients with higher mACEF-Ca scores (log-rank P < 0.001).ConclusionThe mACEF-Ca score, combining clinical and IVUS-derived calcification parameters, enhances risk stratification in patients with severe CAC undergoing PCI. It offers a practical and effective tool for identifying high-risk individuals, guiding procedural planning, and informing post-procedural management strategies.Keywords: Coronary artery calcification, Intravascular ultrasound, ACEF score, Percutaneous coronary intervention, Calcified nodule

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains the leading cause of mortality worldwide [1]. Among revascularization strategies, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) plays a key role in restoring myocardial perfusion [2]. However, heavily calcified coronary lesions pose substantial challenges, impeding optimal balloon expansion and stent deployment, and increasing the risk of complications such as arterial dissection, no-reflow, and vessel perforation [1, 3]. As a marker of advanced atherosclerosis, coronary artery calcification (CAC) indicates significant vascular degeneration. While the coronary artery calcium score (CACS) derived from coronary CT angiography is a well-established tool for risk assessment [4, 5]. Nevertheless, in symptomatic patients undergoing PCI—particularly those with severe severely calcified lesions—the prognostic value of CACS remains debated [6].

To improve outcome prediction after revascularization, several risk models have been developed, including the European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE) and the SYNTAX (Synergy Between PCI With Taxus and Cardiac Surgery) score [7, 8]. Among them, the age, creatinine and ejection fraction (ACEF) score—based on age, serum creatinine, and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF)—offers a simple yet reliable tool for outcome prediction in both surgical and interventional settings [9, 10]. It has been validated across diverse cardiovascular populations, including those undergoing PCI [11, 12]. However, it lacks lesion-specific anatomical information, which may be critical in patients with severe CAC [13]. To address this, modified versions have been proposed. For instance, the ACEF- Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) model incorporates the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in place of serum creatinine to enhance renal function assessment [14]. Additionally, adaptations such as the ACEF II and the ACEF- ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) were developed for targeted patient groups [15]. Another variation, the modified ACEF (mACEF) score, integrates the MDRD formula for refined renal evaluation [14]. Nonetheless, these variations have primarily been validated in broader or acute coronary syndrome cohorts, and evidence in patients with advanced CAC undergoing PCI remains limited [16]. Emerging evidence indicates that the ACEF score may be effective in forecasting major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in patients with calcified lesions [17]. However, there is still a paucity of data evaluating its prognostic accuracy specifically in real-world PCI patients with advanced CAC, especially concerning long-term outcomes [18].

Therefore, this study aims to assess the prognostic performance of ACEF and mACEF scores in patients with severe CAC treated with intravascular ultrasound (IVUS)-guided PCI using drug-eluting stents (DES), and to explore whether incorporating additional risk markers can enhance predictive accuracy in this high-risk population.

Materials and methods

Study participants

This retrospective observational study enrolled patients diagnosed with heavily CAC who were admitted to the Department of Cardiology at Xiangtan Central Hospital between October 2016 and December 2022. Eligible participants were aged 18 years or older and had angiographically or IVUS-confirmed severe CAC. All patients underwent non-emergent PCI with successful implantation of drug-eluting stents (DES) and had provided informed consent prior to the procedure. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) presence of cardiogenic shock; (2) absence of critical pre-procedural data, including serum creatinine or LVEF; (3) coexisting severe chronic diseases such as advanced hepatic or renal insufficiency, uncontrolled asthma, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD); (4) diagnosis of spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD); (5) history of coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG); (6) diagnosis of malignancy; (7) existing immune-mediated or hematologic disorders; (8) significant arrhythmic conditions, including sustained ventricular tachycardia or advanced atrioventricular block; and (9) incomplete post-procedural follow-up data (Fig. 1). The study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Xiangtan Central Hospital (Approval No. X20200835). All subjects gave written informed consent prior to undergoing the procedure; verbal consent was obtained where required by institutional policy.

Procedural protocol

All patients received coronary angiography (CAG) and PCI in alignment with prevailing clinical practice standards. The choice of interventional strategies and procedural tools—including the type of stents, balloons, and pre-dilation techniques—was determined at the discretion of the attending interventional cardiologists [19]. All procedures were conducted under IVUS guidance to enhance lesion characterization and ensure optimal stent deployment. Severe CAC was defined using the following imaging criteria: (1) On CAG, the presence of dense, radiopaque calcific deposits aligned with the arterial wall, persistently visible during contrast injection and in cardiac diastole [20]; (2) On IVUS, identification of hyperechoic arc-like calcification with posterior acoustic shadowing encompassing ≥ 180° of the arterial circumference [21]. Antithrombotic management comprised dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), including daily aspirin (75–100 mg) and clopidogrel, administered either as a 600 mg loading dose at least 6 h prior to PCI or as a maintenance regimen (75 mg/day) for a minimum of 5 days before the intervention. Patients already maintained on chronic clopidogrel therapy were not given an additional loading dose. Intravenous unfractionated heparin (100 U/kg) was administered during PCI as per anticoagulation protocol. Procedural success was defined as achieving < 30% residual luminal stenosis with restoration of Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) grade 3 flow in the target vessel and its side branches (> 2 mm in diameter). Following PCI, patients receiving DES continued DAPT for 12 months, transitioning to single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT) if no bleeding risks were identified. Long-term aspirin therapy was prescribed indefinitely. Additional secondary prevention pharmacotherapies were prescribed at discharge in accordance with clinical indications and guideline-based recommendations [19].

Clinical data collection

Clinical information was collected through a comprehensive review of electronic medical records. Baseline characteristics encompassed demographic parameters, cardiovascular risk factors and laboratory profiles. Procedural and angiographic details included assessment of left main disease, bifurcation involvement, multivessel disease, total lesion length, utilization of pre-dilation, number and dimensions (diameter and length) of implanted stents, and use of adjunctive techniques such as IVUS, rotational atherectomy (RA), and intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) support. At discharge, prescribed pharmacological therapies were recorded.

Scoring method

To comprehensively evaluate both clinical and anatomical risks in patients with severe CAC, six ACEF-based scoring models were assessed in this study. The original ACEF score is calculated as age (years) divided by LVEF (%), with an additional point added if serum creatinine is ≥ 2.0 mg/dL (or ≥ 176.8 µmol/L) [10]. The mACEF score refines renal function assessment by incorporating eGFR; 1 point is added for every 10 mL/min/1.73 m² decrease below 60, up to a maximum of 6 points [14]. The ACEF-MDRD score uses MDRD-based eGFR, calculated as age/LVEF (%) + 1/eGFR, allowing a continuous renal function variable [14]. The ACEF II score adds 1 point each for emergency PCI and pre-procedural anemia (defined as hemoglobin < 13 g/dL in males or < 12 g/dL in females) [22]. The ACEF-STEMI score, developed for STEMI, adds four specific variables to the core formula—dynamic renal impairment (post-PCI eGFR decline > 10 mL/min/1.73 m²), anterior infarction with LAD involvement, elevated CRP (> 1 mg/dL on day 2), and presence of diabetes mellitus (DM)- each contributing 1 point [15]. Finally, the ACEF–residual SYNTAX score (rSS) incorporates anatomical complexity by adding 1 point for serum creatinine ≥ 2.0 mg/dL and another if the rSS exceeds 8 [13].

Intravascular ultrasound imaging and quantitative assessment

IVUS imaging was selectively performed based on lesion morphology and operator judgment during PCI. To prevent coronary vasospasm, 100–200 µg of intracoronary nitroglycerin was routinely administered. Two catheters were used: the 40 MHz Atlantis SR (2016–2019) and the OptiCross (2019–2022), both compatible with the iLab IVUS platform and offering similar resolution. The catheter was advanced distally and withdrawn proximally at a constant speed (0.5–1.0 mm/s) under fluoroscopy, with all images digitally recorded. Offline analysis was conducted using QIvus® software (Medis, Netherlands) by two experienced, blinded analysts. Discrepancies were resolved by a senior analyst. Identical evaluation protocols were applied across catheter types to ensure consistency. All image parameters followed expert consensus definitions and standards [23].

Specifically, a “360° calcium arc” was defined as a continuous hyperechoic ring with posterior acoustic shadowing involving the full circumference of the vessel, consistent with established imaging definitions [23]. The calcium arc was defined as the angular extent of calcified plaque along the vessel circumference on cross-sectional IVUS images, delineated by hyperechoic regions with accompanying posterior acoustic shadowing. A “calcified nodule (CN)” was defined as a convex calcific protrusion into the lumen causing disruption of the normal vessel contour, with accompanying shadowing [23].

Both findings were modeled as binary variables (present vs. absent) in the risk prediction model due to their distinct morphologies, high reproducibility, and robust clinical significance. Their presence has been associated with increased procedural complexity and worse long-term outcomes, thereby justifying their inclusion in simplified scoring models.

Stent expansion was assessed using IVUS-derived measurements. Specifically, it was calculated as the ratio of the minimal stent area (MSA) to the average reference lumen area, defined as the mean of the proximal and distal reference lumen areas adjacent to the stented segment.

Clinical outcomes and follow-up

The primary study endpoint was the occurrence of MACE, defined as a composite of cardiac death, myocardial infarction (MI), target vessel revascularization (TVR) and in-stent thrombosis. Event classification adhered to the standardized definitions established by the Academic Research Consortium [24]. All patients underwent scheduled follow-up at 1, 6, and 12 months following the index PCI procedure, with continued annual monitoring up to 2 years. Follow-up assessments were carried out by trained clinical staff using a combination of outpatient clinic visits, electronic medical record reviews, telephone interviews, and secure digital platforms such as WeChat. Collected follow-up data included the date of evaluation, adherence to prescribed medications, recurrence of symptoms, occurrence of adverse clinical events, and hospital readmissions.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS v26.0 (IBM, USA) and MedCalc v20.0 (MedCalc Software, Belgium). Normality of continuous data was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using t-tests; non-normal data were presented as median (IQR) and analyzed via Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Procedural metrics (e.g., stent number, burr size) were summarized as medians (Q1, Q3). The predictive performance of six risk scores for MACE was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, with area under the ROC curve (AUC) compared via DeLong’s test and optimal cutoffs identified by the Youden index. Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify independent predictors of MACE: variables with p < 0.10 in univariate analysis were entered into multivariate models. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. A simplified risk score was created by assigning weighted integer values to multivariate predictors based on normalized β coefficients. Model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 894 patients with angiographic or IVUS-confirmed severe CAC were initially screened. After applying the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 785 patients were included in the final analysis. The patient selection process is summarized in Fig. 1.

Baseline clinical characteristics

A total of 785 patients were enrolled in the study, among whom 121 (15.4%) experienced MACE during the follow-up period. As presented in Table 1, baseline characteristics—including age, sex, and conventional cardiovascular risk factors such as DM, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia—did not differ significantly between patients with and without MACE (all P > 0.05). However, individuals in the MACE group demonstrated significantly reduced eGFR (49.77 ± 27.81 vs. 70.67 ± 27.09 mL/min/1.73 m², P < 0.001) and lower LVEF (50.82 ± 14.00% vs. 58.31 ± 13.72%, P < 0.001) compared with those in the no-MACE group. In addition, serum creatinine levels were significantly elevated in the MACE group (P = 0.014).

Procedural and IVUS Characteristics

As shown in Table 2, there were no statistically significant differences between the MACE and no-MACE groups with respect to procedural success rates or the distribution of treated coronary vessels. However, patients who experienced MACE had significantly longer lesion lengths (33.63 ± 15.33 mm vs. 30.13 ± 18.00 mm, P = 0.026), a higher overall stent burden—reflected by both the number of stents implanted and the cumulative stent length (both P < 0.001)—as well as lower stent expansion percentages at both the MSA and the site of maximal calcification (P < 0.001 and P = 0.004, respectively). Notably, IVUS-based indicators of calcific plaque severity were more frequently observed in the MACE group. This included a significantly greater incidence of lesions with a maximum calcium arc of 360° and the presence of CN, occurring in 64.5% vs. 40.5% and 22.3% vs. 7.8% of patients, respectively (both P < 0.001).

Univariable and Multivariable Predictors of MACE

As presented in Table 3, univariable logistic regression analysis identified several factors significantly associated with the occurrence of MACE. These included reduced eGFR (HR = 0.114, P < 0.001), decreased LVEF(HR = 0.931, P < 0.001), the presence of a 360° maximum calcium arc (HR = 1.125, P < 0.001), and detection of CN (HR = 10.425, P = 0.031). In the subsequent multivariable regression model, four variables emerged as independent predictors of MACE: lower eGFR (HR = 0.932, P < 0.001), reduced LVEF (HR = 0.068, P = 0.002), the presence of a maximum calcium angle of 360° (HR = 1.626, P < 0.001), and CN (HR = 2.614, P < 0.001).

Construction of the mACEF-Ca model incorporating clinical and IVUS predictors

Building upon the multivariable analysis findings, our study identified maximum calcium angle of 360° and CN as strong and independent predictors of 2-year MACE (Table 4), alongside the established mACEF score. Based on these predictors, we developed a novel integrated risk model, termed the mACEF-Ca score, which combines the traditional mACEF score with two key IVUS-derived markers of calcific burden. To construct the model, a binary logistic regression was performed including all three variables: mACEF, maximum calcium angle of 360°, and presence of CN. All variables were retained in the final model due to their statistical significance (P < 0.05). Comparison of risk score distributions between MACE and non-MACE groups

To assess the discriminative power of various risk scores for MACE prediction, the distributions of seven scoring systems were compared between the MACE and non-MACE groups (Table 5). The ACEF, mACEF, and ACEF-MDRD scores showed no significant differences between groups (P > 0.05), suggesting limited utility in this cohort. In contrast, ACEF II, ACEF-STEMI, ACEF-rSS, and the novel mACEF-Ca model demonstrated significantly higher scores in the MACE group compared to the non-MACE group (all P < 0.001). Notably, the mACEF-Ca score exhibited the largest intergroup difference (5.30 [4.90–5.66] vs. 3.19 [2.81–3.58]), highlighting its strong discriminatory capability for identifying high-risk patients.

ROC curve analysis and discriminative performance of risk models

To compare the predictive performance of various risk scoring models for 2-year MACE, ROC curve analysis was performed across seven models. As shown in Fig. 3 and summarized in Table 6, the novel mACEF-Ca model achieved the highest area under the curve (AUC = 0.778, 95% CI: 0.733–0.823), followed by the ACEF-STEMI (AUC = 0.757), ACEF-rSS (AUC = 0.747), and ACEF II (AUC = 0.718) scores. The traditional ACEF score had the lowest AUC (0.664). DeLong’s test was used for pairwise comparison, and the mACEF-Ca model showed statistically significant improvement over the standard ACEF, mACEF, and ACEF-MDRD models (P < 0.05). Although ACEF-STEMI and ACEF-rSS demonstrated relatively good discrimination, their differences with mACEF-Ca were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Sensitivity and specificity analyses further confirmed that mACEF-Ca provided the best balance between detection and false-positive rates (sensitivity 72.7%, specificity 69.3%, Youden Index = 0.585), supporting its clinical value as a superior risk stratification tool.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and risk stratification using the mACEF-Ca score

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was conducted to evaluate the prognostic performance of the mACEF-Ca scoring system(Fig. 4). When applying the continuous mACEF-Ca model, patients with scores above the optimal cutoff value of 0.501 exhibited a significantly higher incidence of MACE during the 2-year follow-up period (log-rank P < 0.001). In contrast, the simplified mACEF-Ca score—calculated as age divided by LVEF plus one point for the presence of a 360° calcium arc and one point for a CN—demonstrated a similar trend in stratifying risk. Using an optimal cutoff of 1.961, the simplified model achieved a sensitivity of 47.1% and a specificity of 87.8%, with an AUC of 0.649. Despite its slightly reduced discriminative power compared to the continuous model (AUC = 0.778), the simplified score maintained a clear separation between risk groups and offers greater feasibility for clinical implementation. However, its relatively low sensitivity (47.1%) highlights a limitation in detecting all high-risk individuals, which should be considered when applying this model in clinical practice. Additionally, model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The mACEF-Ca model demonstrated good calibration, with no significant difference between observed and predicted MACE probabilities (χ² = 9.13, df = 8, P = 0.332), indicating an adequate model fit to the data.

Discussion

In this retrospective study of patients with severe CAC undergoing IVUS-guided PCI, we evaluated six ACEF-based models for predicting MACE. The newly developed mACEF-Ca score—combining age, LVEF, eGFR, and key IVUS-derived calcification markers—demonstrated superior predictive performance, with the highest AUC and better model calibration. Multivariate analysis confirmed CN and a 360° calcium arc as independent risk factors, highlighting the added value of incorporating lesion-specific IVUS features into clinical risk models. These results support a more integrated approach to risk assessment in high-risk PCI patients.

Comparative predictive performance of ACEF and its derivative scores

Originally introduced by Ranucci et al. in 2009 for preoperative risk stratification in patients undergoing elective coronary artery bypass grafting [10], the ACEF score has since been widely adopted in diverse cardiovascular clinical settings. Despite its straightforward design—based solely on patient age, serum creatinine levels, and LVEF—it has consistently shown prognostic accuracy comparable to that of more elaborate risk assessment tools, including EuroSCORE and the SYNTAX score [16, 25].

To enhance the relatively limited predictive performance of the original ACEF score, particularly in anatomically complex or high-risk patient subsets, several modified models have been proposed. Among these, Capodanno et al. developed the ACEF-MDRD model, which replaces serum creatinine with stratified steps of eGFR [14]. However, within STEMI populations, neither the use of serum creatinine ≥ 2 mg/dL in the ACEF model nor the eGFR decrement-based approach in ACEF-MDRD significantly improved risk discrimination for MACE when compared to the simpler age/LVEF ratio alone [15]. Consistent with these observations, our analysis demonstrated that the ACEF-MDRD model achieved only modest prognostic accuracy in our study population and did not surpass the predictive performance of the mACEF-Ca score.

In response to the recognized limitations of earlier models, Reindl et al. introduced the ACEF-STEMI score, incorporating four variables tailored to STEMI contexts: dynamic renal dysfunction (defined as a reduction in eGFR > 10 mL/min/1.73 m² within 3 days), anterior MI involving the LAD as the culprit lesion, elevated C-reactive protein (CRP > 1 mg/dL on Day 2), and the presence of DM [15]. This augmented model significantly enhanced predictive accuracy for MACE in STEMI patients, yielding an AUC of 0.75 (95% CI: 0.66–0.84, P = 0.03), with CRP emerging as the most potent individual prognostic indicator [15].

Further refinements to the ACEF scoring paradigm have led to the development of additional models such as the ACEF II score, which integrates emergency PCI and the presence of preprocedural anemia—defined as hemoglobin levels < 13 g/dL in males and < 12 g/dL in females [22]. Another notable extension is the ACEF-rSS, which incorporates post-PCI residual ischemic burden into risk assessment [13, 26]. These expanded models have demonstrated enhanced prognostic utility in selected patient subsets, particularly among those with multivessel CAD or anatomically complex lesions [13, 27].

Despite advancements in ACEF-based models, none of the previously described scoring systems incorporate intravascular imaging parameters—factors increasingly recognized as essential for prognostication in patients with severe CAC. In this context, our study introduces the mACEF-Ca model, which uniquely combines clinical components from the mACEF score with lesion-specific IVUS-derived features, namely the presence of a maximum calcium arc ≥ 360° and CN. This integrative approach yielded the highest predictive accuracy, with an AUC of 0.778, and effectively distinguished high-risk individuals, who exhibited a significantly elevated 2-year MACE rate compared to the low-score group. By comparison, established models such as ACEF-STEMI, ACEF-rSS, and ACEF-MDRD demonstrated only moderate discriminative ability (all AUCs < 0.70) and failed to show a statistically significant improvement over mACEF-Ca in head-to-head comparisons (P > 0.05) [17].

These findings underscore that although ACEF-based extensions such as ACEF-STEMI offer clinical utility in the stratification of STEMI patients, their prognostic performance may be limited in individuals with substantial coronary calcification. Our results suggest that the integration of IVUS-characterized plaque morphology into ACEF-derived models significantly improves risk prediction in the setting of severe CAC, providing enhanced discriminatory capability over models relying solely on clinical variables.

Pathophysiological rationale for IVUS-Derived predictors

The incorporation of IVUS-derived features—specifically maximum calcium arc and the presence of CN—into the mACEF-Ca scoring system is supported not only by statistical significance but also by underlying pathophysiological rationale. These two imaging biomarkers represent distinct phenotypes of advanced coronary calcification and are both independently associated with procedural complexity and unfavorable long-term clinical outcomes [28]. A maximum calcium arc of 360° signifies concentric calcification encompassing the entire circumference of the vessel wall, a morphology known to significantly hinder optimal stent expansion [29]. This structural rigidity contributes to increased resistance during balloon inflation, often necessitating high-pressure dilation and increasing the likelihood of complications such as stent underexpansion and edge dissection [5]. Previous research has established that calcium arcs exceeding 270° are independently correlated with suboptimal stent deployment and a heightened risk of long-term restenosis [29]. In our study cohort, the presence of a maximum calcium arc of 360° was significantly associated with increased MACE incidence and remained an independent predictor in multivariate analysis, reinforcing its prognostic relevance in patients with severe CAC. In contrast, CN represent a different morphological entity—characterized by protruding, irregular calcific masses often accompanied by fibrous cap disruption and localized thrombosis [5, 30]. Histopathological evidence indicates that CN may function as focal sites of atherosclerotic inflammation and plaque vulnerability, particularly in elderly and dialysis-dependent individuals [30]. From an interventional perspective, CN pose significant challenges, as they are frequently associated with suboptimal stent apposition and increased risk of stent thrombosis or restenosis. These outcomes are attributed to incomplete lesion coverage and persistent eccentric calcific burden following PCI [30]. Consistent with previous literature, our findings demonstrated that the presence of CN was significantly correlated with a higher incidence of MACE and independently predicted adverse outcomes in multivariate analysis, thereby supporting its integration into the modified risk model. This observation aligns with earlier intravascular imaging studies—including both IVUS and optical coherence tomography—which characterize CN as a biologically active form of calcification associated with increased procedural complexity and unfavorable clinical prognosis [30]. Moreover, the concurrent presence of concentric calcification and nodular calcification likely signifies a more advanced and metabolically active atherosclerotic state. Patients with this phenotype frequently exhibit widespread coronary involvement, endothelial dysfunction, and chronic vascular inflammation—factors known to complicate percutaneous intervention and contribute to poorer long-term outcomes [30]. Taken together, these results provide both mechanistic and clinical rationale for incorporating maximum calcium arc and CN into a unified clinical-anatomical scoring framework. The inclusion of such lesion-specific imaging features meaningfully enhances the risk stratification capacity of the mACEF-Ca model, moving beyond traditional clinical predictors and reflecting the growing value of imaging-guided precision in managing heavily calcified CAD. Furthermore, recent studies have demonstrated that severe CAC is independently associated with an increased risk of periprocedural MI, in addition to its known impact on stent deployment. This complication may result from impaired vascular compliance, distal embolization of calcific fragments, and heightened procedural trauma, all of which contribute to myocardial injury during PCI. Given its prognostic implications, these findings underscore the necessity for meticulous procedural planning and adequate lesion preparation in patients with extensive coronary calcification [31].

Clinical implications

The mACEF-Ca model demonstrates strong clinical utility for managing patients with severely calcified coronary lesions undergoing PCI. By integrating IVUS-derived anatomical markers with essential clinical parameters, the score enables real-time, procedure-tailored risk stratification and facilitates early identification of high-risk patients. For these individuals, clinicians may consider more aggressive lesion preparation strategies—such as early RA or newer calcium-modifying devices (e.g., intravascular lithotripsy [IVL])—which have shown procedural advantages in patients with severe CAC—and intensified adjunctive therapies, including extended DAPT and closer imaging follow-up [2, 32].

Moreover, growing evidence from randomized trials and meta-analyses supports the use of intravascular imaging to guide PCI in patients with complex and calcified lesions. Imaging-guided PCI has been shown to enhance stent expansion, reduce procedural complications, and improve clinical outcomes compared to angiography-guided strategies [33, 34]. The use of IVUS in this study not only enabled accurate quantification of calcific burden but also aligned with contemporary practices favoring precision-guided revascularization in high-risk anatomy.

Post-procedurally, the mACEF-Ca score may guide secondary prevention, informing lipid management, cardiac rehab referral, and follow-up planning. As it relies on widely available variables—age, LVEF, eGFR, and IVUS features—it is practical for routine use without increasing resource demands. Overall, the mACEF-Ca model offers a pragmatic and effective approach to improving outcomes in the evolving era of individualized interventional cardiology.

Limitation

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the retrospective and single-center nature of the investigation may introduce inherent selection and information bias. Although rigorous inclusion criteria and multivariable adjustments were applied, the potential for residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Second, the mACEF-Ca score was both developed and internally validated within the same patient cohort, and thus lacks external validation in independent or multicenter populations. As a result, its generalizability across broader clinical contexts remains uncertain. Third, although all participants underwent IVUS-guided PCI, the study did not perform stratified analyses based on the use of adjunctive plaque-modifying techniques such as RA, which could influence procedural complexity and clinical outcomes. Fourth, the maximum calcium arc was assessed as a binary variable (e.g., 360°), which, while practical, may oversimplify lesion complexity. Additionally, although the simplified mACEF-Ca model incorporating this binary measure offers ease of use, it demonstrated a relatively low sensitivity (47.1%) for predicting MACE. This may result in under-recognition of certain high-risk cases, suggesting the need for caution when relying solely on this simplified model in clinical settings. Fifth, while all participants had severe coronary artery calcification (CAC) confirmed by intravascular ultrasound (IVUS), the inclusion criterion of ≥ 180° calcium arc allowed for a wide range of calcification severity. Although the median arc length was 341°, patients with moderate calcification (180°–341°) were not excluded. We acknowledge, however, that the cohort tended to include more severe cases, potentially reflecting referral and imaging selection bias. This limitation has been addressed to enhance clarity and transparency. Finally, despite employing a standardized imaging protocol and blinded double-reading for IVUS analysis, an element of operator subjectivity persists—particularly in identifying CN and quantifying calcium arc, which may affect interobserver reproducibility. Future adoption of machine learning–based automated image analysis tools may help reduce interpretation variability and improve the robustness of intravascular imaging assessments.

Conclusion

In patients with severe CAC undergoing IVUS-guided PCI, the novel mACEF-Ca score—combining clinical and IVUS-derived parameters—demonstrated superior prognostic value for MACE compared to existing ACEF-based models. Its simplicity and integration of imaging biomarkers enhance its clinical utility for real-time risk stratification. Future prospective, multicenter validation is warranted to confirm its generalizability.
