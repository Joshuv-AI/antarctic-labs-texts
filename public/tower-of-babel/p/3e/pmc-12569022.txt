Molecularly imprinted electrochemical sensor based on copper 4-amino benzoic acid metal-organic framework for determination of pregabalin: electrochemical and DFT studies
Authors: M. Jelvehzadeh, Kh. Ghanbari, M. Zahedi-Tabrizi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study introduces a novel detection approach for determining trace levels of pregabalin (PGB), which utilizes the combination of two advanced metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) with a molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP) technique. For this purpose, we have developed a highly sensitive electrochemical sensor for measuring and determining PGB. The first step involved modifying a carbon paste electrode (CPE) with a Cu-MOF. PGB was employed as a template in the electropolymerization of poly-orthophenylenediamine (POPD) by cyclic voltammetry. The sensors’ electrochemical performance was evaluated using cyclic voltammetry (CV), differential pulse voltammetry (DPV), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). The results obtained from the DPV revealed three linear response ranges of 0.003–0.09 µM,0.1-1 µM, and 1–90 µM with a low detection limit of 1.2 nM (signal-to-noise ratio = 3) and sensitivity 17.20 µA/µM. Moreover, the built-in sensor was utilized to measure PGB levels in both blood and Tablet with recoveries ranging from 96.67 to 109.19%. A detailed analysis of molecular structures, binding energies, and binding thermodynamic parameters of PGB-POPD configurations was performed using density functional theory methods. The calculated standard enthalpy change of binding indicates an exothermic process for all dimer and trimer configurations.

Introduction

Ensuring the safety of medications is critically important for individuals relying on them. This involves strategies to minimize the risks associated with adverse drug reactions (ADRs), which can be influenced by factors such as drug dosage, metabolism, and individual patient differences1. Pregabalin (PGB), an efficacious pharmaceutical produced by Pfizer, has shown significant effectiveness in addressing several nervous system problems, such as epilepsy, post-herpetic neuralgia, and diabetic peripheral neuropathy. Its efficacy stems from its capacity to manage these illnesses without inducing significant adverse drug reactions in the majority of instances, rendering it a commonly recommended therapy2,3. PGB acts by binding to the alpha-2-delta subunit of voltage-dependent calcium channels, which lowers the release of excitatory neurotransmitters, helping to avoid hyperalgesia and central sensitization. Its ability to cross the blood-brain barrier and not bind to plasma proteins further contributes to its effectiveness. The recommended daily dosage of Pregabalin typically ranges from 150 to 600 mg, divided into 2 to 3 smaller doses4–6. Given these considerations, it is essential to adopt a rapid, straightforward, and cost-effective approach for detecting PGB in plasma and tablet samples. Over the years, several methods have been employed for this purpose, such as UV/Visible spectrophotometry5, liquid chromatography–high-resolution mass spectrometry7, LC-MS/MS8, HPLC9, and electrochemical detection via square wave voltammetry10. Although these methods offer excellent sensitivity, they are associated with significant drawbacks, including high costs, lengthy analysis times, and the need for extensive sample preparation. Moreover, many of these techniques require the use of organic solvents and sample pretreatment steps such as separation, extraction, and adsorption, complicating the process. Additionally, they often suffer from poor selectivity and intricate operational procedures11. In contrast, electrochemical sensors have attracted considerable attention due to their simplicity, efficiency, rapid response, and enhanced sensitivity12–18. Notable examples of these sensors include the GCE/Cu-BTC/ePDA-MIP19 and the CPE/PREG-Cu (II) complex20.

A practical approach to enhancing the selectivity of electrochemical sensors involves integrating them with molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs)21. MIPs are meticulously engineered synthetic polymers that can selectively bind to a specific target molecule. By imprinting the polymer with the precise molecular structure of the target molecule, MIPs create an intricate “lock and key” recognition mechanism. This unique property allows MIPs to demonstrate extraordinary selectivity and specificity towards the intended target molecule22. As synthetic receptors, MIPs offer numerous advantages, including high selectivity for target molecules, remarkable chemical and thermal stability, reproducibility in synthesis, and cost-efficiency23. Furthermore, electropolymerization enhances the application of MIPs by allowing for the precise deposition of imprinted polymer thin films directly onto transducer surfaces. This capability not only improves the integration of MIPs into electrochemical sensors but also ensures greater reproducibility in their application24. However, one challenge that MIPs face is their relatively high density, which can limit the creation of imprint sites. Therefore, the sensor may lose its selectivity and sensitivity. Adding nanoparticles can overcome this problem by increasing the surface area and the number of etched holes, thereby increasing the overall performance of the sensor22.

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are crystalline substances characterized by high porosity, composed of metal ions or clusters and organic ligands. These frameworks exhibit exceptional properties, such as large surface areas, flexibility in design, and high stability, making them ideal for a range of applications25,26. They are particularly valuable in fields such as drug delivery, gas capture, chemical sensing, catalysis, and electrochemical devices, where their unique characteristics can be fully exploited27,28. As a major class of porous materials, MOFs are increasingly utilized for sensor surface modification, owing to their remarkable ability to enhance catalytic activity through their metal centers, which promote redox reactions29. One example of such a framework is Cu-MOF, which stands out for its well-defined structure, exceptional electrical conductivity, and outstanding electrocatalytic properties. These features make Cu-MOF an ideal candidate for use as an electrocatalyst, electrode material, or scaffold in electrobiosensors, further enhancing the effectiveness of these devices30. On the other hand, the combination of molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs) with metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) enhances sensing and drug detection. MOFs improve MIPs’ performance due to their porous structure and tunable properties, leading to better drug sensing and electrochemical device results31. MIPs and MOFs both feature pores and cavities in their structure. Utilizing MOFs as the foundation for synthesizing MIPs offers substantial advantages, including an increased pore count, heightened sensitivity, and an enhanced number of recognition sites, all of which significantly elevate the performance of the sensing system32.

Gholivand et al. designed a pregabalin (PGB) impedance biosensor based on immobilization of bovine serum albumin (BSA) on a graphene/glassy carbon electrode (BSA/Gr/GCE) using initial characterization with computational methods and supplemented with experimental observations. The Faradaic impedance responses were linearly related to PGB concentration between 10.0 and 280.0 nM, and the detection limit was calculated to be 3.0 nM33. El-Naby et al. reported a selective sensor based on a neutral ionophore polymer membrane for the determination of pregabalin using the organic-mineral hybrid material β-cyclodextrin: phosphomolybdic acid with a linear response range of 0.1 M to 1.0 µM and a detection limit of 0.6 µM34. This work employed the electropolymerization molecular imprinting process to imprint PGB on CPE/Cu-MOF. The proposed sensor platform offers promising environmental and economic advantages, particularly for field and clinical deployment. The Cu-MOF and poly(o-phenylenediamine) (POPD) were synthesized using low-energy and efficient methods without the use of toxic reagents. The preparation process was carried out under mild conditions and using a small volume of reagent, thereby minimizing the generation of chemical waste and environmental burden. From an economic standpoint, the materials used are inexpensive, commercially available, and the modification steps were simple, reproducible, and compatible with scale-up using low-cost screen-printing or batch-fabrication techniques. This makes the sensor highly cost-effective compared to traditional methods like HPLC or LC-MS, which require expensive. Adding Cu-MOF enhances its efficiency by increasing the surface area and number of binding sites. The integration of Cu-MOF and POPD onto the electrode surface offers a highly effective and innovative sensing platform for the electrochemical detection of pregabalin. This modified interface demonstrates superior sensitivity, exceptional selectivity, and outstanding stability compared to conventional electrode materials. Furthermore, a computational study was conducted via density functional theory (DFT) calculations to investigate the interaction between pregabalin molecules with the modified nanostructured electrode. Such a design highlights the potential of MOF-polymer composites in developing sensors for pharmaceutical analysis and clinical diagnostics. To assess its applicability in practical situations, levels of PGB were measured in samples of Blood plasma and pregabalin tablets. The findings demonstrated that this budget-friendly and uncomplicated sensor effectively detected PGB. Figure 1 illustrates the preparation steps of the Cu-MOF/MIP-POPD for the determination of PGB.

Experimental

Chemicals and instruments

All materials used in this study were analytical reagent grade. Graphite powder, paraffin, Orthophenylenediamine (OPD), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sulfuric acid (H2SO4), Copper (II) acetate monohydrate (Cu(CH3CO2)2), ethanol, Pregabalin, phosphoric acid (H3PO4), Na2HPO4, and KH2PO4 from Sigma-Aldrich or Merck were obtained. Phosphate buffer solution (PBS, 0.1 M, pH 7.0) was used as a supporting electrolyte. All solutions were prepared with double-distilled water. All experiments were done at room temperature (22–25 °C). A Tensor 27 (Bruker) instrument was used for ATR-FTIR analysis. The surface properties and morphologies and energy dispersive X-ray analysis (EDS) were examined by a field emission scanning electron microscopy with an accelerating voltage of 20 kV (FE-SEM) (MIRA 3, TESCAN, Czech Republic). XPS data for the characterization of the chemical bonding state of the Ni-Bio-MOF was obtained from the XPS (FLEXPS) instrument (Berlin, Germany). A HR-TEM FEI TECNAI F20 instrument was used for HR-TEM images. An XRD XPert PRo MPD instrument was used to identify the compositions of Cu-MOF syntheses and the phases in the materials. BET data for Cu-MOF syntheses were obtained from the BET BELSORP Mini II instrument. nnnnnnnA Metrohm Autolab B. V.® Autolab PGSTAT 204 potentiostat/galvanostat (Utrecht, UT, the Netherlands) was used to carry out all electrochemical experiments. As a working electrode, a bare or modified carbon paste electrode (CPE, diameter = 3.5 mm), as a counter electrode, a platinum wire, and as a reference electrode an Ag/AgCl/saturated KCl was used as a three-electrode cell system.

Cu-MOF preparation

The Cu-MOF was synthesized via a previously reported protocol with slight modifications35. For the synthesis of Cu-MOF under health and safety considerations by the coprecipitation method, 249.5 mg Cu (CH3COO)2, 42.0 mg para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA), and 5.4 mg CTAB were dissolved in 25 ml acetate buffer solution (50 mM, pH 7.0), respectively. CTAB is a key component in the synthesis of Cu-MOF, acting as a cationic surfactant, controlling particle growth, preventing agglomeration, and promoting the formation of uniform nanoparticles. It also acts as a soft template, influencing the final structure and performance of the Cu-MOF material36. Afterward, the solution was placed in an ultrasonic bath for 20 min, and after that, it was kept at room temperature for 8 h. Subsequently, the green powder was obtained by centrifugation, washed three times with a buffer solution of acetic acid − sodium acetate (pH 5.0), and dried in an oven for 6 h at 60 ◦C.

Fabrication of CPE/ Cu-MOF /MIP-POPD and CPE/ Cu-MOF /NIP-POPD

Graphite powder and paraffin were carefully combined in a 70:30 (w/w) ratio to create a homogenous paste to make a carbon paste electrode. The homogenous paste was inserted into a polyethylene syringe (3.5 mm diameter) and well-packed. After that, an electrical connection was made by inserting a copper wire. The electrode surface was smoothed by dragging it on clean paper and readied for further application37. The first step was to modify the CPE with Cu-MOF nanostructures. The CPE modified with Cu-MOF was prepared the same as the unmodified CPE method, except that in addition to fine graphite powder and paraffin oil, green Cu-MOF powder (3.0 mg) was also added. The second modifying step is the electropolymerization of OPD using the CV technique. To fabricate the CPE/Cu-MOF/NIP-POPD sensor, the CPE/Cu-MOF electrode was initially cleaned by polishing it on a clean paper surface. Then, the electropolymerization of OPD (1.0 mM) in 0.1 M HCl was performed by using the cyclic voltammetry (CV) method in the potential range of -0.2 to 1.0 V on CPE/Cu-MOF with 10 successive cycles. To form imprinted POPD film on CPE/Cu-MOF, the electropolymerization of OPD was performed in the presence of 1.0 mM of OPD and 1.0 mM PGB in 0.1 M HCl38. The CPE/Cu-MOF/MIP-POPD was placed in a methanol/acetic acid/acetonitrile (2:2:3 V/V) solution for 5 min with stirring of the solution to remove trapped PGB molecules.

Cyclic voltammograms obtained during the electropolymerization of OPD, both without and with PGB on a CPE/Cu-MOF, over 10 cycles within the voltage range of -0.2 to 1.0 V, are illustrated in Fig. 2a and b, respectively. Figure 2a illustrates the appearance of two anodic peaks at 0.36 V (O1) and 0.72 V (O2) during the initial anodic sweep, aligning with previously published findings on the electropolymerization of pure OPD39. The O1 peak signifies the oxidation of OPD, resulting in the formation of monovalent cation radicals, while the O2 peak is ascribed to the oxidation of a dimer, generated by monovalent cation radicals on the CPE/Cu-MOF surface, leading to the formation of divalent cation radicals that subsequently polymerize into the linear chain structure of the POPD film40. No reduction peak was observed, suggesting that the electrochemical oxidation of OPD on CPE/Cu-MOF was irreversible. During the second anodic sweep, the peak current of O1 diminished by 60%, indicating that the POPD layer was formed rapidly, obstructing the diffusion of the OPD monomer to the CPE surface. The peak currents of O1 and O2 diminished with time with ongoing potential cycling, although they did not reach zero, suggesting that the POPD film was not entirely insulating and had a porous structure. Figure 2b illustrates the cyclic voltammogram for the oxidation of OPD monomer in the presence of PGB during the initial cycle, featuring two anodic peaks at 0.30 V (O1) and 0.70 V (O2) in the first anodic sweep. Also, these two oxidation peaks show a lower current from monomer oxidation in the absence of PGB, which indicates the rapid formation of OPD radical cation through one-electron oxidation of amino groups. During electropolymerization in the presence of pregabalin (as the template molecule), the monomer (o-phenylenediamine) polymerizes around the template, forming specific binding sites complementary in shape, size, and functional group orientation. The current diminished as the number of cycles increased, with the peak current obtained in the initial cycle41. The potential range ensures effective monomer oxidation, while the scan repetition builds up a stable matrix incorporating the template. After polymerization, the template molecules are extracted, leaving cavities with high affinity and selectivity toward pregabalin, which significantly enhances the recognition capability of the sensor.

Results and discussion

Characterization.

Characterization.

Based on FE-SEM and TEM characterizations, the morphologies of Cu-MOF were evaluated. According to Fig. 3a and b, the FE-SEM images of Cu-MOF are shown with two different magnifications. As can be seen, the FE-SEM image of Cu-MOF exhibits the microscopic morphology of densely packed Cu-MOF cubic nanoparticles whose length is 156 –124 nm (Fig. 3c). The TEM images of the Cu-MOF (Fig. 3(d-f), different magnifications also reveal the same morphology as observed in the FE-SEM. In Fig. 3g and h, the HRTEM image of the individual Cu-MOF can be seen with a lattice spacing of 0.293 nm. The selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) image (Fig. 3i) reveals a series of diffraction rings centered on the transmission point, indicating the polycrystalline nature of the Cu-MOF48. This finding further demonstrates that the Cu-MOF has been properly synthesized and is compatible with the XRD test results.

The chemical structures of Cu-MOF, Cu-MOF/MIP-POPD before and after PGB removal, and Cu-MOF/NIP-POPD were studied using ATR-FTIR spectra, with the findings presented in Fig. 5a. For Cu-MOF, C-O-Cu bond assignments are seen at 719 and 1153 cm− 149. A peak at 1458 cm− 1 is assigned to aromatic C = C stretching. The O-C-O asymmetric stretching vibration bands in PABA are located at 1375 and 1524 cm− 1. The symmetric C = O stretch of carboxylate groups is located at 1722 cm− 1. Also, broadband is attributed to -OH and -NH stretching at 3000–3600 cm− 150,51. The spectra of Cu-MOF/MIP-POPD and Cu-MOF/NIP-POPD exhibit peaks around 719 cm− 1, which corroborate the presence of C-O-Cu. The bands at 3300–3367 cm− 1 are attributable to the distinctive N–H stretching vibrations of the –NH– group. The physical properties of the Cu-MOF, such as specific surface area, pore volume, pore size distribution, and average pore diameter, were analyzed through Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) and Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) techniques, and the result is shown in Fig. 5c. The rapid nitrogen uptake at P/P0 > 0.9 for Cu-MOF might be due to the presence of secondary, much larger pores54. The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) model determined the surface area of Cu-MOF to be 5.36 m² g− 1. The Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) pore size distribution plot (inset of Fig. 5c) further substantiates the presence of substantial mesopores measuring less than 1.2 nm in Cu-MOF. The pore diameters and particular surface areas enhanced the rate of analytes, consequently enhancing the performance of the electrochemical sensor.

Electrochemical characterization of different modified electrodes

The performance of the produced materials was assessed by comparing the electrochemical behavior of modified electrodes with these materials using CV and EIS methods in a 0.1 M KCl solution containing 1.0 mM [Fe (CN)6] 3−/4−.

Figure 7a displays cyclic voltammograms for various electrodes submerged in a solution containing 1.0 mM [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− and 0.1 M PBS (pH = 7.0), with a scan rate of 50 mV/s. Figure 7a presents a pair of oxidation-reduction peaks for the bare carbon paste electrode (CPE). After modification with Cu-MOF, there is a notable increase in the redox peak currents. This improvement can be attributed to several factors: Cu-MOF increases the electrochemically active surface area, providing more electron transfer sites. Its high conductivity enhances electron transfer, reducing resistance and improving overall performance. Additionally, the porous structure of Cu-MOF promotes better interaction with reactants, while copper ions may function as catalytic centers, further boosting reaction efficiency. As a result, the Cu-MOF-modified CPE shows better electrochemical performance than the bare CPE. After electropolymerization of POPD on the CPE/Cu-MOF surface (CPE/Cu-MOF/NIP-POPD), a slight decrease in current is observed due to the polymer layer, which blocks some electroactive sites and introduces resistance, reducing electron transfer efficiency. Despite this, the polymer improves the electrode’s selectivity and stability. As shown in Fig. 7a, the peak current of CPE/Cu-MOF/MIP-POPD before PGB removal is higher than CPE/Cu-MOF/NIP-POPD due to the molecularly imprinted cavities, which enhance PGB adsorption and provide pathways for electron transfer. Before washing, residual PGB molecules in these cavities also contribute to the increased current, but this masks the electrode’s selectivity. After washing, the removal of residual PGB molecules clears the cavities, allowing for selective binding. This results in a 3.9-fold increase in current compared to the bare CPE. The enhanced current after washing is due to the selective binding sites in the imprinted cavities, which improve sensitivity and selectivity. The high conductivity of Cu-MOF and the MIP’s selective recognition further boost the electrochemical response, making the system highly effective for PGB detection.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was employed to examine electron transport during the surface modification process, therefore determining the interfacial behavior of modified electrodes. The EIS analysis was conducted in 0.1 M KCl solution containing 1.0 mM of [Fe (CN)6]3−/4− for the bare CPE, CPE/Cu-MOF, CPE/Cu-MOF/NIP-POPD, CPE/Cu-MOF/MIP-POPD before and after removal of the template, and the corresponding Nyquist plots are shown in Fig. 7b and c. Impedance responses were recorded at the formal potential of the redox probe within the frequency range from 0.1 Hz to 105 Hz, with an applied amplitude of 10 mV. The impedance spectrum exhibits a semicircular component at high frequencies, corresponding to the limiting process of electron transfer, and a linear component at low frequencies, resulting from the diffusion-limiting stage of the electrochemical process60. Inset of Fig. 7b, indicates a Randle’s equivalent circuit that was used to fit Nyquist plots. The equivalent circuit comprises Rs (solution resistance), marking the semicircle’s initiation, Rct (charge transfer resistance at the electrode-electrolyte interface), representing the semicircle’s diameter, CPE (constant phase element), indicative of the catalyst’s impedance, and W (Warburg impedance), which delineates mass transfer limitations. An obvious, well-defined semicircle was observed at the CPE, signifying a large electron transfer resistance of 8.0 kΩ. Upon the modification of the CPE with Cu-MOF, the CPE/Cu-MOF creates a conductive layer that diminishes charge transfer resistance, resulting in a reduced semicircle diameter (4.8 kΩ). Furthermore, following the electropolymerization of OPD on CPE/Cu-MOF, the CPE/Cu-MOF/NIP-POPD exhibited a significantly reduced semicircle (6.8 Ω), signifying a reduced electron transfer resistance to the redox probe and enhanced conductivity at the electrode surface. This improvement can be ascribed to the superior conductive properties of POPD and its synergistic effects with Cu-MOF, which enhanced the electroactive surface area and facilitated the diffusion of [Fe (CN)6]3−/4− towards the electrode surface61. The Rct of the CPE/Cu-MOF increased to 15.5 Ω when the POPD was synthesized in the presence of template molecules. The Rct value decreased following the elution of the template molecule (7.8 Ω) and then increased after the recombination of the template molecule, agreeing with the findings from the CV experiment. The variations in Rct during the modification and application of CPE/Cu-MOF/MIP-POPD were analyzed using EIS, and the findings were consistent with the CV experimental results.

Optimization of the experimental conditions

Optimization the metal-to-linker ratio in Cu-MOF

The metal-to-linker ratio in MOFs significantly affects their performance in electrochemical sensors. It influences the electronic structure, porosity, conductivity, stability, catalytic activity, defect formation, and selectivity. A higher ratio leads to improved sensor performance due to increased interaction with analytes. A balanced ratio leads to more robust MOF structures, increased sensitivity and response time, and improved catalytic efficiency. Furthermore, it can influence defect formation and potentially create additional active sites for sensing. Therefore, careful optimization is crucial for the development of high-performance electrochemical sensors. Thus, to achieve the most effective and optimal performance, seven electrodes with varying ratios of metal to linker (1:2, 1:1, 2:1, 3:1, 4:1, 5:1, and 6:1) were tested (Fig. 9a). As shown in Fig. 9a, the highest oxidation current was achieved at a ratio of 4. Therefore, for the synthesis of Cu-MOF, the ratio of 4:1 was chosen as the optimal value.

Optimization of the amount of Cu-MOF modifier in carbon paste electrode

The amount of modifier in carbon paste electrodes (CPEs) affects the performance of electrochemical sensors. Optimizing the amount of modifiers can enhance sensor performance. Thus, in this step, we optimized the amount of Cu-MOF in the carbon paste. As shown in Fig. 9b, by increasing the amount of Cu-MOF to 0.003 g, the anodic current related to ferrocyanide oxidation increased. The increase may result from the enhanced effective surface area and the electrocatalytic properties of the carbon paste electrode, which contributed to an elevated electron transfer rate and improved efficiency of the intended electrode. However, the studies showed that with a further increase in the amount of MOF, the current decreased. Further increase in the amount of MOF can create a dense layer that prevents the diffusion of analytes to the electrode surface and reduces the sensitivity and response time of the electrode. Also, an excessive amount of MOF can disrupt the conductive network formed by graphite particles in the carbon paste. This leads to a decrease in the electron transfer efficiency and the overall performance of the electrode63. Therefore, the amount of 0.003 g was determined as the optimal amount for the preparation of the modified electrode (CPE/Cu-MOF).

Optimization of the molar ratio of the template molecules to the monomer

To evaluate how the molar ratio of the template molecule to monomer influences the sensing performance of MIP sensors, five sensors were fabricated with varying ratios. As illustrated in Fig. 9c, the highest current response was observed at a molar ratio of 3 mM, which was determined to be the optimal condition. The reduced responses at other ratios can be attributed to the following reasons:(i) When insufficient amounts of a key component are present, the number of available bonding sites diminishes, as additional molecules of the same type are unable to interact effectively with one another. (ii) Conversely, an excessive monomer concentration decreases the number of specific binding sites, reducing the sensor’s selectivity and sensitivity. This highlights the importance of achieving a precise balance in the molar ratio to maximize sensor performance.

Optimization of the extraction time

A study was conducted on the extraction and removal step of PGB to improve the sensor’s sensitivity, selectivity, and reproducibility. This involved immersing the electrode in a mixture of Acetonitrile: Acetic acid: Methanol with a ratio of 3:2:2 for varying periods (2–20 min), followed by testing the sensor’s response to PGB. The findings shown in Fig. 9d indicate that as the extraction time increased, so did the PGB response. Nonetheless, there was no notable alteration in the PGB response following a 15-minute interval. Thus, an optimal extraction duration of 10 min was chosen.

Optimizing the number of electropolymerization cycles

The number of electropolymerization cycles significantly affects the performance of molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP) electrochemical sensors. The number of electropolymerization cycles determines the thickness of the printed film in the imprinting process. As the number of electropolymerization cycles increases, the thickness of the MIP film increases, which affects the sensitivity and response time of the sensor. Excessive cycles can create a tight, low-porous film that completely blocks the access of the redox probe to the electrode surface. It also reduces the number of accessible binding sites. With a few cycles, the MIP film becomes too thin or loose, allowing free access of the redox probe to the electrode surface regardless of the presence of the target analyte. To optimize the MIP sensor’s performance, finding the right balance in the number of electropolymerization cycles is crucial. This balance ensures sufficient film thickness for stable imprinting while maintaining porosity for analyte access and efficient electrochemical sensing64. As can be seen in Fig. 9e, the best performance is when the number of polymerization cycles is 10.

The pH effect

The adjustment of solution pH is crucial in the measurement of pharmaceutical compounds using electrochemical sensors, as it directly affects reaction dynamics, sensitivity, and overall measurement accuracy. Consequently, adjusting the pH of the solution is crucial for achieving ideal conditions for electrochemical analysis. The pH of a 0.1 M PBS containing 0.1 M KCl, 1.0 mM Fe(CN)63−/4− and 50.0 µM PGB was adjusted from 5.0 to 9.0 to examine the influence of pH on the potential and current of the oxidation of Fe(CN)63−/4− in CPE/Cu-MOF/MIP-POPD (Fig. 9f). As the pH increases from 5.0 to 6.0, the peak current increases, attaining its maximum at pH 7.0 (Fig. 9f). Subsequent increases in pH lead to a decrease in peak current value. The results showed that the optimal pH value was 7.0. The significance of pH value may be elucidated by examining two characteristics: Firstly, the interaction between pregabalin and orthophenylene diamine is optimally attained at pH 7.0. Secondly, given the pKa1 value of pregabalin, it has a negative charge at higher pH values, which leads to a repulsive effect and a decrease in peak current with increasing pH. Proper management of pH conditions is essential for achieving reliable and reproducible results in pharmaceutical analysis. PGB, like Gabapentin (GBP), is a pharmaceutical compound characterized by distinct Lewis basic and acidic sites. The basic sites arise from the lone electron pairs on nitrogen atoms in the amino groups, while the acidic sites are associated with the carboxylic acid (-COOH) groups. The dissociation constants (pKa1) of Pregabalin are approximately 4.2 for the carboxylic group and 10.6 for the amine group (pKa2). This indicates that Pregabalin achieves maximum solubility and stability at a neutral pH (approximately 7.0), where it adopts a zwitterionic form. This form balances its ionic interactions, enhancing solubility and stability in aqueous environments. The weaker acidic nature of the -COOH group can be attributed to potential intramolecular hydrogen bonding, which reduces the availability of acidic sites for interaction with polar probes. These bonds also influence the molecule’s conformational dynamics65,66.

Calibration curves

After examining the evidence, a quantitative analysis was conducted using the differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) technique to identify PGB (Fig. 12a). As PGB does not react within the desired potential range, a [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− probe was used to indirectly measure its interaction with CPE/Cu-MOF/MIP-POPD. The calibration curve for PGB (shown in Fig. 12b and c, inset) displayed three linear dynamic ranges: 0.003–0.09 µM, 0.1-1 μM, and 1–90 µM, with corresponding regression equations of Ip (µA) = 17.199 C (µM) + 0.0824 (R2 = 0.9981), Ip (µA) = 2.2668 C (µM) + 1.5231 (R2 = 0.9911), and Ip (µA) = 0.0272 C (µM) + 4.1188 (R2 = 0.9504), respectively. In the first range, there was a sharp decrease in current as the concentration of PGB increased; in the second and third ranges, the decrease was more gradual and gentler. The limit of detection was determined to be 1.2 nM with a signal-to-noise ratio of three. Additionally, we examined the correlation between PGB concentration and peak current using the NIP sensor under identical conditions; however, no significant linear relationship was found between them. This could be due to specific cavities present for PGB on the surface of CPE/Cu-MOF/MIP-POPD. The measure used to evaluate the ability of the template and corresponding functional monomer (POPD-PGB) to interact is known as the imprinting factor (α). This factor is determined by comparing the slopes of calibration curves for PGB in SMIP and SNIP, using the formula α = SMIP/SNIP. In this instance, the calculated value of α is 3.211. Selectivity, repeatability, and reproducibility of the modified electrode

One of the critical parameters in a method validation is selectivity, particularly when combined with high sensitivity for the target analyte. In this study, the efficacy of the developed sensor was evaluated in the presence of four potential interfering species that display structural or chemical similarities to PGB. The sensitivity of the CPE/Cu-MOF/MIP-POPD sensor was examined independently and in the presence of Phenobarbital, sodium valproate, gabapentin, and lamotrigine. This investigation was conducted in a solution containing [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− and a phosphate buffer (pH 7, 0.1 M), as these compounds are non-electroactive.

Calibration curves for these interfering species were constructed using various concentrations ranging from 0.003 µM to 0.1 µM, as illustrated in Fig. 13a. Additionally, the sensor’s response to each interfering species was quantified and is summarized in Table 3. The slopes of the calibration curves, which indicate sensitivity, were contrasted with the response curves of the molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP) electrode in the presence of different amounts of interferents. The regression equations for each interferent are presented in Table 3. In addition, in Fig. 13a, the slopes of the calibration curves for PGB against selected interferents are compared. As can be seen, the modified sensor shows higher sensitivity to PGB.

Selectivity coefficients, using the CPE/Cu-MOF/MIP-POPD electrode, were calculated as the ratios of the slopes of the calibration plots for PGB to those of the interfering species in the absence of PGB. The selectivity coefficients for phenobarbital, sodium valproate, gabapentin, and lamotrigine relative to PGB were determined to be 1, 11.49, 3.75, 2.62, and 3.32, respectively. These results indicate that the CPE/Cu-MOF/MIP-POPD sensor demonstrates exceptional selectivity and sensitivity towards PGB, attributed to the presence of molecular cavities with recognition sites specifically designed for the target analyte.

Also, the analysis of pregabalin in pharmaceutical and biological samples can be influenced by common interferents, including inorganic ions and organic compounds. Therefore, it is essential to evaluate the sensor’s selectivity for pregabalin under optimal conditions in the presence of these potential interferences. For this purpose, seven compounds, namely Sugars, KCl, NaCl, Mg (SO4), Urea, CaCl2, and Uric acid (each 0.5 mM). The differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) response of 0.5 µM PGB was recorded both in isolation and in the presence of these interferents. If the variation between the PGB response when these species are present and when they are not is less than ± 5%, the sensor’s anti-interference and selective detection capabilities are validated. As demonstrated in the inset of Fig. 13b, the proposed sensor exhibited a response deviation of less than ± 5%, affirming its acceptable selectivity.

To assess the reproducibility of the sensor, five CPE /Cu-MOF/ MIP-POPD electrodes were fabricated under identical conditions and utilized to measure the DPV response for 50.0 µM PGB. The calculated relative standard deviation (%RSD) of 0.41% demonstrates the high reproducibility of the sensor (Fig. 13c). For evaluating repeatability, the sensor’s DPV response to 50.0 µM PGB was recorded immediately after fabrication and then stored in a dry environment at room temperature. Subsequent measurements were conducted on 1, 5, 10, 20, and 30 min. All DPVs exhibited identical oxidation peak currents, with an RSD of 0.85%, confirming the excellent repeatability of the sensor (Fig. 13d).

For long-term stability investigation, after the fabrication of the sensor, the DPV response of 5 µM PGB was examined and then stored in a dry place at room temperature. The DPV response of 5 µM PGB was examined again after 5, 10, 15, and 30 days, and it was observed that the DPV responses were 96.26%, 96.59%, 95.00%, and 94.80% of the primary response. Therefore, the sensor’s good long-term stability is also confirmed. Finally, the reusability of CPE/Cu-MOF/MIP-POPD was evaluated, and it was concluded that the fabricated CPE/Cu-MOF/MIP-POPD is not a disposable electrode. It can be used at least 10 times by immersing the electrode in a methanol/acetic acid/acetonitrile (2:2:3 V/V) solution for 5 min with stirring of the solution.

Conclusion

This work presented a simple, sensitive, and selective MIP sensor that was developed using the electropolymerization method. The sensor was created by electrochemically polymerizing the O-phenylenediamine monomer on a CPE/Cu-MOF surface while PGB template molecules were present. The incorporation of Cu-MOF nanostructures increased the number of PGB template molecules per unit surface area, thereby enhancing the sensor’s sensitivity and selectivity. Upon removal of PGB templates, specific cavities were created, enabling selective detection of PGB even in the presence of other substances. The sensor exhibited three linear detection ranges with a low detection limit of 1.2 nM and was cost-effective, durable, and reproducible. The PGB and OPD geometrical parameters were studied using density functional theory calculations and molecular electrostatic potential maps. The optimized geometries of the most stable configurations confirmed the critical role of hydrogen bonds in the molecular orientation in PGB-OPD configurations.
