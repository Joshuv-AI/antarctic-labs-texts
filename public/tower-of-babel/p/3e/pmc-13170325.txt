CT predictors of visceral pleural invasion in subsolid nodular pulmonary adenocarcinoma: differences between direct and indirect tumor-pleura contact
Authors: Yun Wang, Deng Lyu, Xiaoli Deng, Lei Hu, Junhong Wu, Xiuxiu Zhou, Yi Xiao, Li Fan, Shiyuan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreoperative accurate prediction of visceral pleural invasion (VPI) in subsolid nodular pulmonary adenocarcinoma (PA) can provide guidance for the surgical method of early lung cancer. The aim of this study is to explore the value of CT features in predicting VPI in clinical stage IA subsolid nodular peripheral PA with contact with the pleura.MethodsA total of 464 patients across three hospitals were retrospectively analyzed. The internal dataset (n = 363) was divided into training cohort (n = 244) and internal validation cohort (n = 119) based on examination time, using a 7:3 ratio. Patients were categorized into VPI-positive and VPI-negative groups according to the pathological diagnosis. The CT features were analyzed, including pleural end characteristics, tumor signs, and tumor-pleural features. Multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to screen the optimal combination of variables for predicting VPI. Based on these variables, a predictive model and corresponding nomogram were developed, and their predictive performance was evaluated using external validation cohort (n = 101). According to the type of tumor-pleural contact, it was divided into indirect and direct contact types. Subgroup analyses were subsequently performed for each category to identify CT features indicative of VPI.ResultsMultivariable logistic regression analysis identified the optimal combination of predictor variables, including solid component diameter, tumor-pleura relationship, vacuole sign, vascular convergence sign, and soft tissue density shadow of pleural end. Among them, solid component diameter (OR = 1.17), the Type III classification of the tumor-pleura relationship (OR = 7.43), Type IV (OR = 37.22), and Type V (OR = 13.62) were independent risk factors. The AUC values of the model for predicting VPI in three cohorts were 0.833, 0.781, and 0.796, respectively. Subgroup analysis further revealed that solid component diameter (OR = 1.12), Type IV tumor-pleura relationship (OR = 27.63), vascular convergence sign (OR = 10.68) and soft tissue density shadow of pleural end (OR = 10.48) were independent risk factors for indirect contact type, whereas solid component diameter (OR = 1.15), Type V tumor-pleura relationship (OR = 4.44), and solid component contact pleura (OR = 6.23) were independent risk factors for direct contact type.ConclusionsThe model based on CT features demonstrated acceptable diagnostic efficacy in predicting VPI. The types of tumor-pleural contact are different, and there are differences in CT predictive variables.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40644-026-01029-5.

Introduction

According to the latest global cancer report statistics, lung cancer accounts for 11.4% of all malignancies, ranking second among malignant tumors worldwide, and remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality. Among its subtypes, pulmonary adenocarcinoma (PA) represents the most prevalent pathological type of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [1]. Visceral pleural invasion (VPI) of lung cancer is defined as tumor infiltration beyond the elastic fiber layer of the visceral pleura and is recognized as an adverse prognostic factor. The presence of VPI is associated with increased tumor recurrence and reduced overall survival of patients, even in lung tumors ≤ 2 cm or ground glass nodules (GGNs) [2–6]. Based on the 9th edition of TNM staging criteria, when PA with cT1N0M0 exhibits pathological VPI, the T stage is upgraded from T1 to T2, and consequently, the TNM stage is elevated from IA to IB [2]. For preoperative clinical T1 lung tumors (including T1a), lobectomy has been demonstrated to confer a better prognosis in cases with VPI [7]. Moreover, tumors with cT1/VPI (+) (stage IB) require more extensive lymph node dissection than tumors with cT1/VPI (-) (stage IA) [8]. Currently, postoperative pathology remains the gold standard for diagnosing VPI; however, it has a lag in the formulation of clinical treatment decisions. In addition, intraoperative pathological assessment often fails to definitely determine the presence of VPI. Therefore, accurate preoperative prediction of VPI is particularly important for the diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis of patients.

Previous studies have demonstrated that CT manifestations of pGGNs and tumors without pleural contact do not exhibit VPI [9–15]. Conversely, several CT features, including pleural indentation sign, pleural tag sign, and solid component contact pleura, have been identified as risk indicators for predicting VPI [9–15]. However, most of the studies have included patients with clinical stage IA PA or NSCLC, encompassing both solid nodules and subsolid nodules [11–15]. Only a limited number of investigations have specifically focused on subsolid nodules [9, 10]. Different categories of lesions that come into contact with the pleura require different indicators to be observed and measured, and their risk factors may also be different [14, 15]. Recent research by Sun et al. [15] reported that, in solid nodular NSCLC ≤ 30 mm, the jellyfish sign, pleural thickening, and contact surface area serve as CT predictors for pleural-attached nodules, whereas multiple pleural tag signs are predictive for pleural-tag nodules. However, for VPI predictors of subsolid nodular NSCLC, this study failed to further explore them due to the relatively small number of cases in this subgroup.

Although some studies have employed radiomics and deep learning approaches to predict VPI [16–22], these methods have notable limitations. Compared with CT features that radiologists are more familiar with and easier to master, radiomics and deep learning techniques often involve complex assessment processes and suffer from poor interpretability. Consequently, the predictive models constructed based on these techniques may face challenges in routine clinical implementation. This study aims to predict the VPI in clinical stage IA subsolid nodular PA based on CT features and to conduct subgroup analysis according to the type of tumor-pleural contact. These findings are expected to provide some valuable information for the formulation of individualized diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Patients

This research was designed as a multi-center, retrospective, and observational study. Ethical approval was obtained from the ethics committees of all three participating hospitals, and patients were exempted from signing informed consent forms. A retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical and imaging data of patients with peripheral lung cancer who underwent preoperative chest CT examination, surgical resection, and pathological confirmation at three hospitals from July 2020 to December 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Clinical stage IA (cT1N0M0) PA (maximum tumor diameter ≤ 3 cm); (2) presence of a subsolid nodule; (3) preoperative CT demonstrating that the tumor was located beneath the pleura and exhibited either direct or indirect contact with the pleura. The indirect contact type is defined as the presence of a certain distance between tumor and pleura, and tumor and pleura are connected by one or more linear or strip-like shadows, that is, the pleural tag sign [15]. The direct contact type is defined as the tumor in direct contact with the pleura [14]; (4) the minimum distance from the lesion to the pleura (DLP) ≤10 mm; (5) availability of thin-slice chest CT with slice thickness ≤ 2 mm obtained within one month prior to surgery and free of artifacts. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Maximum tumor diameter > 3 cm; (2) pathological diagnosis of glandular prodromal lesions (atypical adenomatous hyperplasia, adenocarcinoma in situ) or non-PA; (3) pGGNs or solid nodules; (4) absence of pleural contact; (5) receipt of preoperative neoadjuvant and chemotherapy, or biopsy puncture; (6) poor CT image quality.

If multiple lesions in the same patient met the inclusion criteria, the largest lesion was selected for retrospective analysis. Ultimately, hospital one included a total of 363 cases that met the requirements as the internal dataset. This dataset was further divided into the training cohort (n = 244) and the internal validation cohort (n = 119) based on the order of examination time, using a 7:3 ratio. An additional 101 cases from hospital two and hospital three were included as the external validation cohort. All cases were classified into VPI-positive and VPI-negative groups based on the pathological diagnosis, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Each group was further categorized into direct contact and indirect contact types according to the type of tumor-pleural contact. CT image measurement and evaluation

The DICOM images of the patient were imported into Radiant DICOM Viewer software (version 4.2.1, Medixant, Poland), and CT features of the lesions were assessed using multiplanar reconstruction (MPR), maximum intensity projection, and minimum intensity projection. Quantitative and qualitative evaluations of the lesions were conducted by two chest radiologists, each with 10 years of working experience. Prior to evaluation, both radiologists underwent systematic training under the guidance of a senior radiologist with 20 years of working experience, following the evaluation criteria reported in previous literature. For quantitative parameter evaluation, the mean value of measurements obtained by the two radiologists was recorded as the final result. For qualitative indicator assessment, the lesion signs were interpreted by two radiologists using a double-blind method, and any discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion.

CT qualitative indicator evaluation: (1) Assessment of the tumor-pleural relationship: Analyze the type of tumor-pleural contact (indirect or direct), pleural indentation sign (absent or present), and classify the morphology of tumor-pleural relationship into 5 types. Type I is indirect contact between tumor and pleura, without pleural indentation sign. Type II is direct contact between tumor and pleura, without pleural indentation sign. Type III is characterized by a thin line shadow connecting tumor to pleura, with pleural indentation sign. Type IV is characterized by a thick line shadow connecting tumor to pleura, with pleural indentation sign. Type V is characterized by direct contact between tumor and pleura, with pleural indentation sign, as illustrated in Fig. 2. In addition, the presence of solid component contact pleura was evaluated. (2) Evaluation of tumor signs: including tumor shape (round/nearly round or irregular), margin (lobulation sign and spiculation sign), boundary (clear or unclear), adjacent structure (vascular convergence sign and bronchial changes), internal morphology (vacuoles and cystic cavities/cavities), and emphysema background (normal or combined with emphysema). CT quantitative parameter measurement: On the MPR lung window image measuring tumor diameter, solid component diameter, calculation CTR (consolidation-to-tumor ratio), measure DLP, whole tumor contact length and solid component contact length. For tumors in direct contact with pleura, the DLP value was recorded as 0, and both the whole tumor contact length and the solid component contact length were measured. For tumors in indirect contact with pleura, the whole tumor contact length and the solid component contact length were both recorded as 0, while the DLP was measured.

Subgroup analysis: In addition to the common features described above, for tumors indirectly contacting the pleura, the presence of a bridge sign was assessed [23]. For tumors directly contacting the pleura, the presence of the pleural tag sign was evaluated [4]. The definitions of these CT features were based on descriptions provided in previous studies [14], see Supplementary material 2.

Pathological diagnosis

Hematoxylin-eosin staining was employed to assess the extent of tumor invasion into the pleura under a light microscope. In cases where the diagnosis was challenging, elastic fiber staining was performed to evaluate whether the tumor had invaded the elastic fiber layer. For detailed pathological diagnosis, see Supplemental material 1.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using the statistical package for the social sciences (SPSS, version 20.0) software and R statistical software (R version 4.2.2). The Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to analyze data normality. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using the Student’s t-test. Measurement data are expressed as medians (Q1, Q3) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data are expressed as case numbers and compared using Pearson’s chi-square test, Yate’s correction for continuity, or Fisher’s exact test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in the univariate analysis. Variables with p < 0.1 in univariate logistic regression were included in multivariate logistic regression using a backward stepwise selection approach. A multivariate logistic regression model was subsequently established, and its efficiency was tested on the independent internal and external validation cohorts. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and corresponding area under the curve (AUC) value were used to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the model. The goodness of fit of the model was evaluated by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. p > 0.05 was considered a good fit of the model. Multivariate logistic regression analysis and internal and external validation were conducted using the “rms” package of R software, while ROC curve analysis was performed using the “pROC” software package. The Kappa coefficient and intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) were calculated to assess the consistency of qualitative and quantitative parameters among observers. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Consistency analysis of qualitative and quantitative indicators

Regarding CT features, the interobserver consistency for quantitative parameters in both the internal and external datasets was high, with ICC ranging from 0.955 to 0.995. Similarly, the qualitative indicators assessments demonstrated strong consistency with Kappa values ranging from 0.815 to 1.000. Further details are provided in Supplementary material 3.

Univariate and multivariate analyses

In the training cohort, univariate analysis demonstrated statistically significant differences between patients with VPI-positive and VPI-negative PA in terms of tumor diameter, solid component diameter, CTR, solid component contact length, tumor-pleural relationship, soft tissue density shadow of pleural end, spiculation sign, vacuole sign, and vascular convergence sign (all p < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were observed for the remaining indicators (all p > 0.05), as demonstrated in Table 2. Considering the collinearity between solid component diameter and CTR, only the solid component diameter and other variables with a P value < 0.1 screened by univariate logistic analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. No evidence of multicollinearity was observed among the variables included in the analysis, as presented in Supplementary material 4. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was subsequently performed to select the optimal combination of predictor variables for model construction and to generate the corresponding nomogram, as presented in Fig. 3. The best predictive variables included: solid component diameter, tumor-pleura relationship, vacuole sign, vascular convergence sign, and soft tissue density shadow of pleural end, as presented in Table 3. The regression equation was formulated as follows: ln(P/1-P) = −4.656 + 0.155 × solid component diameter + 1.152 × tumor-pleural relationship (Type II) + 2.006 × tumor-pleural relationship (Type III) + 3.617 × tumor-pleural relationship (Type IV) + 2.612 × tumor-pleural relationship (Type V) + 0.556 × vacuole sign + 1.069 × vascular convergence sign + 1.032 × soft tissue density shadow of pleural end, where P represents the predicted probability of VPI occurrence. Among them, solid component diameter (OR = 1.17), tumor-pleura relationship (Type III OR = 7.43, Type IV OR = 37.22, Type V OR = 13.62) were identified as independent risk factors for predicting VPI in patients with subsolid nodular PA (Table 3). The AUC values of the prediction model in training, internal and external validation cohorts were 0.833, 0.781, and 0.796, respectively, with corresponding accuracy rates of 75.82%, 67.62%, and 77.23% (Table 4). The ROC curves for each cohort are presented in Fig. 4. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test demonstrated that the model fits exhibited satisfactory calibration across all three cohorts (all P values > 0.05). The calibration curve further confirmed a good agreement between the predicted probabilities and the observed outcomes (Fig. 5). Subgroup analysis

Lesions with indirect contact with the pleura-characterized by tumor-pleura relationships of type I, type III, or type IV-were only included in DLP for analysis, with additional assessment of the bridge sign. Univariate analysis revealed statistically significant differences in tumor diameter, solid component diameter, CTR, tumor-pleural relationship, soft tissue density shadow of pleural end, and vascular convergence signs between patients with VPI-positive and VPI-negative PA (p < 0.05), as detailed in Supplementary material 5. Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified solid component diameter (OR = 1.12), Type IV tumor-pleura relationship (OR = 27.63), vascular convergence sign (OR = 10.68) and soft tissue density shadow of pleural end (OR = 10.48) as independent risk factors for VPI in indirect contact lesions, as presented in Table 5. For lesions exhibiting direct contact with the pleura-characterized by the tumor-pleura relationship types II and types V-only the solid component contact pleura, whole tumor contact length, and solid component contact length were included in the analysis. Additionally, the combined pleural tag sign was analyzed. Univariate analysis revealed statistically significant differences in tumor diameter, solid component diameter, CTR, whole tumor contact length, and solid component contact length, solid component contact pleura, tumor-pleura relationship, pleural tag sign, spiculation sign, and bronchial changes between patients with VPI-positive and VPI-negative PA (p < 0.05), as presented in Supplementary material 6. Multivariate logistic regression analysis further identified solid component diameter (OR = 1.15), Type V tumor-pleura relationship (OR = 4.44), and solid component contact pleura (OR = 6.23) as independent risk factors for VPI in patients with direct contact PA, as presented in Table 6. Discussion

This study investigated the CT-based predictive factors of VPI in clinical stage IA subsolid nodular PA, specifically excluding lesions without pleural contact. For the first time, a classification system describing the tumor-pleura relationship was proposed. The pleural end characteristics, tumor signs, and tumor-pleural features were comprehensively analyzed to construct a prediction model, followed by subgroup analyses based on the type of pleural contact. Validation using both independent internal and external cohorts demonstrated AUC values ranging from 0.781 to 0.833, indicating favorable accuracy.

Regarding tumor-pleura relationship, this study, based on previous literature reports and actual observations, identified direct pleural contact and the presence of pleural indentation sign as two key factors associated with VPI [11, 24–26]. Based on these observations, we proposed a classification system for characterizing the tumor-pleura relationship, which may represent different developmental stages of tumor progression. Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that this classification (Type III/Type IV/Type V) served as an independent risk factor for predicting VPI and Type IV had the highest OR value. PA is characterized by intratumoral reactive fibrous hyperplasia accompanied by scar formation, and scar contraction can cause contraction of the surrounding fibrous framework, causing the connected visceral pleura to be pulled and shifted to the lesion [27], resulting in pleural indentation sign. In the early stages of tumor development, the tumor can connect with the pleura via the linear shadow or come into direct contact without inducing pleural traction or indentation, manifesting as type I and type II morphologies, respectively. As the tumor grows, reactive fibrous hyperplasia within the tumor intensifies, eventually generating sufficient traction to produce indentation of the adjacent pleura, corresponding to type III morphology. With further tumor invasion and expansion, the range of the adjacent pleural indentation expands, and the distance from the pleura further narrows or even disappears, leading to the development of type IV and type V morphologies [24–26]. The presence of soft tissue density shadows within the pleural indentation region provides additional evidence of tumor infiltration into the visceral pleura, consistent with findings reported in previous studies [28].

Previous studies have reported that the pathological basis of the pleural tag sign may involve compression and convergence of the visceral pleura resulting from traction and depression caused by intrapulmonary pressure, as well as localized interlobular septa edema, tumor extension within or beyond lymphatic vessels, inflammatory reactions or fibrosis, and focal atosis of the lobules [25, 26, 28]. For solid nodular PA directly contacting the pleura, the coexistence of multiple pleural tag sign types has been associated with an increased likelihood of VPI [4]. The present study demonstrates that a similar pattern applies to subsolid nodular PA. The presence of the pleural tag signs suggests more pronounced reactive fibrous hyperplasia or peritumoral fibrosis within the tumor, indicating a higher degree of invasiveness and a higher probability of adjacent visceral pleural involvement.

Regarding tumor signs, this study identified the spiculation sign, vacuole sign, and vascular convergence sign as significant factors associated with VPI in subsolid nodular PA, while bronchial changes were associated with VPI in lesions directly contacting the pleura. Spiculation sign is formed when tumor tissue invades adjacent structures along the surrounding bronchial vascular sheaths and lymphatic vessels, and it serves as an independent risk factor for predicting PA invasiveness [29]. Consistently, Zhang et al. [30] and Liang et al. [31] demonstrated that bronchial changes and vascular convergence signs in GGN are independent risk factors for predicting PA invasiveness. The appearance of these contractile signs reflects interstitial fibroblast infiltration and scar formation within PA. With increasing tumor aggressiveness, reactive fibrohyperplasia intensifies, resulting in greater traction on adjacent tissues and consequently inducing morphological distortion of bronchi and blood vessels surrounding or traversing the tumor.

Regarding quantitative indicators, this study found that regardless of whether the tumor directly contacts the pleura or not, the diameter of solid components is a key factor for VPI in subpleural clinical stage IA PA. Solid components usually represent the more aggressive part of PA, a finding consistent with the use of solid component diameter as the criterion for T staging in clinical staging [32]. The larger the maximum diameter of the solid component, the higher the internal invasiveness of the tumor and the greater the risk of VPI. For PA in direct contact with the pleura, this study found that whole tumor contact length and solid component contact length were larger in the VPI (+) group, which is consistent with previous studies [4, 9]. However, greater attention is warranted regarding the presence of solid component contact pleura, as ground-glass components mostly represent the areas of tumor adherent-like growth [32] and generally exhibit lower invasive potential, making them unlikely to penetrate the inner elastic layer of the visceral pleura.

This study has some limitations. First, it is a retrospective study, and there is some degree of selection bias in the case selection. Second, the CT images were obtained from different CT scanners, and the CT features were evaluated manually, which inevitably introduces potential bias due to subjectivity. To minimize such bias, the senior radiologists received systematic training before assessing the features, and the same image analysis software was used to display the lesions’ CT features. Third, the overall sample size and external validation sample were relatively small. In the future, we plan to continue collecting cases to increase the sample size and perform multicenter validation to enhance the model’s generalizability and robustness. Lastly, this study primarily relies on CT features to predict VPI, providing valuable information. In subsequent studies, we will use automated/semi-automated quantification and interpretable artificial intelligence methods to further validate these findings and explore more robust model optimization techniques to enhance the overall performance of the model while maintaining its clinical applicability.

In conclusion, for clinical stage IA subsolid nodular PA with pleural contact, the classification of tumor-pleura relationship (Type III/Type IV/Type V) is an independent risk factor for predicting VPI, indicating that the pleural indentation sign serves as a critical determinant in predicting VPI. Moreover, there is a need to carefully evaluate solid component diameter, the presence of soft tissue density shadows within the pleural indentation area. Notably, the predictive CT features associated with VPI vary depending on the type of tumor-pleura contact. Close attention to these CT features can facilitate more accurate preoperative assessment of VPI, thereby providing valuable guidance for clinical treatment decisions.
