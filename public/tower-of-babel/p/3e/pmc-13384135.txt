Local innovations and systemic barriers in stunting reduction: a qualitative analysis of food and social assistance programmes in Indonesia
Authors: Anissa Rizkianti, Juariah Juariah, Laili Rahayuwati, Mira Trisyani, Desy Nuri Fajarningtiyas, Andhika Ajie Baskoro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionReducing stunting remains a pressing global challenge, particularly in low and middle-income countries (LMICs), where nutrition insecurity and limited access to health services persist. With its large-scale national social assistance and health programmes, Indonesia offers valuable insights into implementing integrated strategies for stunting prevention. This study examines the substantive elements of food and social assistance interventions as well as the contextual and structural factors that influence their implementation. It also assesses how multisectoral collaboration contributes to the implementation process of nutrition-specific efforts aimed at reducing stunting.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative case study in two districts of West Java Province, representing contrasting urban and rural contexts. Data were collected through 78 in-depth interviews with policymakers, implementers and primary beneficiaries. The policy implementation framework developed by Edward was used as a guide for the thematic analysis.ResultsWe identified key factors influencing programme delivery, including communication, resource availability, commitment of implementers and bureaucratic structure. Urban settings demonstrated better outreach, service utilisation and intersectoral coordination while rural areas faced logistical and administrative barriers. Innovative local practices, such as digital health monitoring and women’s farmer groups helped bridge implementation gaps. However, structural constraints, such as inflexible eligibility criteria, poor data integration and fiscal dependency on the central government, limited the programme’s reach.ConclusionIndonesia’s experience demonstrates that effective stunting reduction requires national policy frameworks and locally adapted, multisectoral approaches that address system-level barriers. The practical lessons from this study can empower LMICs aiming to scale up integrated nutrition, health and social protection programmes, equipping them with the necessary knowledge and insights.

Introduction

Globally, stunting continues to be a prominent marker of disparities in children’s health. In low and middle-income countries (LMICs), where structural inequities and implementation bottlenecks are more prevalent, the rate of stunting reduction remains uneven, despite progress made by global nutrition initiatives. Stunting is a condition of impaired growth, resulting from chronic malnutrition, characterised by a Z-score of −2 SD or more below the median height-for-age.1 It is also closely linked to infections before and after the natal period. Stunting leads to long-term consequences for health and cognitive development. Children who experienced stunting in early life are also at higher risk of developing non-communicable diseases, including obesity, diabetes and cardiovascular conditions in later life.2 3 Moreover, persistent stunting rates at the population level may limit educational achievement, workforce productivity and national economic performance over time.3 4

Indonesia has committed to reducing its national prevalence of stunting to 14% by 2024, which is nearly 5% of the global burden.5 6 The current rate, however, shows a failure rate of 21.6% (in 2022), despite a 2.8-point decrease from the previous year.7 Socioeconomic, geographical and administrative variables primarily contribute to the significant gap between provinces and districts, which persists despite specific improvements.8 Cultural behaviours, communication systems, transportation, education and access to healthcare also contribute to the decline in stunting prevalence.9 10 A study in Kenya reported that the likelihood of stunting increased by 12% among individuals from households experiencing food insecurity.11 Households facing food insecurity, characterised by limited dietary intake and restricted access to nutritious food, struggle to maintain a healthy life, leading to stunted growth.12 In some cases, however, the relationship is indirect. For example, this contributes to the lower gestational age of newborns, which in turn affects child growth and development.13 14

Global evidence indicates that integrated approaches, including food assistance, conditional cash transfers and access to healthcare services, are crucial for reducing stunting.1517 However, the delivery of these programmes depends not only on their design but also on how well they are implemented locally.18 Indonesia’s expansive social assistance infrastructure, including the Family Hope Program (Programme Keluarga Harapan, PKH), Non-Cash Food Assistance (Bantuan Pangan Non-Tunai, BPNT) and subsidised national health insurance (Penerima Bantuan Iuran Jaminan Kesehatan; PBI-JK), provides a unique lens for examining the enablers and barriers of multisectoral stunting interventions in decentralised settings.19 Yet despite national coverage, implementation outcomes vary widely across districts, influenced by differences in local governance, infrastructure and cross-sector coordination.20 West Java, one of the most populous provinces, illustrates these disparities, with district-level stunting prevalence ranging from 6% to 27.6% in 2022.21

Given this variation, the present study focuses on two contrasting districts in West Java to investigate how food and social assistance programmes are operationalised at the subnational level. Applying George Edward III’s policy implementation framework, the study aims to explore the factors that influence the success or failure of these interventions, with particular attention to communication, resources, implementer commitment and bureaucratic structure. The study also examines the role of multisectoral collaboration and local innovations in driving these implementation processes and identify the systemic barriers and facilitators that influence the delivery of these interventions in different geographical settings. The findings offer practical insights to enhance nutrition-sensitive programme delivery in decentralised systems, with broader relevance for LMICs facing similar implementation challenges.

Methods

Study design and settings

The study employed a qualitative case study approach to conduct an in-depth assessment of nutrition-specific interventions aimed at reducing stunting in West Java. We assessed interventions or programmes that address the underlying determinants of stunting, as outlined in Presidential Regulation No. 72 of 2021, including food security, social safety, access to health services, adequate caregiving resources at the household and community levels and a safe and hygienic environment.22

Guided by the George Edward III theory of policy implementation (figure 1), this study aims to examine the content of the interventions and the contextual and structural factors that influence their implementation. This theory suggests that the relationship between contextual and structural factors, such as communication, resources, dispositions or attitudes and bureaucratic structure, directly or indirectly influences policy implementation.23 24 In particular, the research aims to identify the roles of actors, the availability of resources, interorganisational co-ordination and policy clarity that contributes to programme outcomes. Additionally, the study examines the impact of multisectoral collaboration among stakeholders on the implementation of nutrition-specific interventions for reducing stunting in the selected regions.

George Edward III’s theory of how interrelated factors affect the implementation of policy.

The study focused on two distinct locations, based on the prevalence of stunting in 2022: Sumedang Regency and Bekasi City. Sumedang had the highest stunting rate at 27.6%, whereas Bekasi had the lowest rate at 6.0%. Additionally, these two areas demonstrate distinct geographical characteristics, as Sumedang is predominantly rural, while Bekasi is primarily urban. In each location, we selected two subdistricts with the highest and lowest prevalence of stunting. Sumedang is a landlocked regency in West Java, with a population of 1 152 507 people, and 10.26% of the population is classified as poor. In contrast, Bekasi is a buffer area for the capital city of Jakarta, with a total population of 2 543 676 people, and the poverty rate was recorded at 5.27%. Detailed maps of the study locations are available in online supplemental file 1 provided.

The study was conducted from January to December 2024. Description of study participants

The main participants, comprising parents of children with stunting and pregnant women with chronic energy deficiency (CED), were recruited by the community health cadres. A total of 40 women were purposively selected, with 10 informants recruited from each subdistrict or village. Selection was based on the study criteria and participants’ willingness to take part.

The sample size was considered sufficient, as data saturation was reached and no new information emerged during the final interviews. Main participants received compensation to cover transportation costs and acknowledge the time they spent participating in the interviews. All selected participants agreed to take part in the study, although several interview schedules had to be adjusted to accommodate participants’ childcare responsibilities.

Since the participants in this study were policymakers, we were aware of the potential for social desirability bias due to the possibility that the researchers had prior relationships with some government informants as a result of their institutional affiliations. To mitigate this, the research team emphasized the study’s independent and research-oriented objectives, ensured strict confidentiality, and actively probed for answers during interviews. The inclusion of diverse perspectives from beneficiaries, who tend to lack institutional ties, also helped counterbalance this potential bias.

Data collection

Data were collected through interviews using semistructured guidelines developed in Bahasa Indonesia. The English version of the interview guidelines is found in online supplemental file 2. Interviews with stakeholders and programme implementers were conducted at their respective offices. In contrast, interviews with parents were held in private rooms at the initially prepared subdistrict offices. Informants were interviewed at prearranged times under the coordination of the health cadres, who served as liaisons. The research team conducted all interviews, and with participants’ consent, each session was audio-recorded and documented.

Before the interviews, participants signed an informed consent form after being informed about the study’s purpose and confirming their understanding and agreement. The interviews were conducted by researchers experienced in health and social policy research, enabling them to approach the interviews with a strong understanding of the study context. Each interview lasted approximately 45 to 60 min. All audio recordings were stored securely in a designated folder on the principal investigator’s password-protected laptop and were only shared with other researchers for data analysis.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s Thematic Analysis framework (2012), with an additional analytical lens provided by the theory of policy implementation, as illustrated in figures1 2. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim. The transcripts and field notes were read closely and carefully reviewed to gain a thorough understanding of the content and context. Thematic analysis was selected due to its flexibility and suitability for exploring complex implementation issues across diverse informants. Initial codes were developed based on the study objectives, field notes and interview guidelines. These codes were refined through iterative reading and constant comparison.

Steps in data analysis using a qualitative approach.

To ensure the rigour of the qualitative analysis, the data were independently coded by two researchers. The coding process, coding framework and development of themes were discussed regularly among the research team to resolve discrepancies and achieve consensus, thereby enhancing the credibility and dependability of the findings. An audit trail documenting coding decisions and theme development was maintained to support confirmability and transparency. Field notes and interview transcripts were also reviewed iteratively to strengthen data triangulation and ensure consistency between the data and the reported findings.

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients, programme beneficiaries and public stakeholders were not involved in the initial design or development of the study. However, their perspectives enriched the data and provided valuable inputs for understanding programme implementation. A broad range of stakeholders, including policymakers, programme implementers, health cadres and community members, were engaged to capture diverse insights into the implementation of food and social assistance programmes. Primary beneficiaries, including parents of children with stunting and pregnant women with CED, participated as informants to explore how social and food assistance programmes were experienced and accessed at the local level. Health cadres also played a vital role in facilitating access to participants and coordinating interviews. Participants were informed about the study’s objectives, provided written informed consent, and were interviewed in private settings to ensure confidentiality and comfort. While neither stakeholders nor beneficiaries were involved in the interpretation of findings, their insights significantly shaped the analysis and conclusions of this research.

Results

Overview of the food and social assistance programmes

Key government programmes, such as BPNT, PKH and PBI-JK, were implemented in both urban and rural areas. BPNT focused on improving food access through staple provision, PKH provided conditional transfers to promote healthcare and education services, and PBI-JK offered subsidised health insurance for vulnerable families. Description of food and social assistance programmes for stunting reduction in Bekasi City and Sumedang Regency

To better understand the factors influencing programme outcomes, the results are organised around four key domains derived from George Edward III’s policy implementation framework: communication, resources, implementer attitude and bureaucratic structure. These thematic areas reflect the primary determinants that shaped the implementation of food and social assistance programmes in the two study locations.

Effectiveness of programme communication and awareness

Programme awareness varied significantly. Bekasi City benefited from proactive outreach through Posyandu and local cadres. Health workers and local officials actively engage with families through Posyandu (integrated health posts), socialisation meetings and home visits, ensuring eligible beneficiaries receive the necessary information. In contrast, rural Sumedang experienced gaps in understanding due to limited outreach, leading to underutilisation of services. Health cadres were crucial intermediaries, but their impact depends on sustained support.

So, our focus is on reinforcing the integrated health post (posyandu) through lokmin (mini workshop) and regular meetings once a month and always discussing stunting. … We always go ‘door to door’ with the health workers, CHWs in the integrated health posts, family assistance team (TPK), and also all cooperation between each other (Community health worker, Bekasi city)

Just like the basic food program, the PKH program also does not specifically target stunting, but targets all beneficiaries of PKH. …. but constrained by quota. (Quota) does not exist, meaning someone must leave before others can enter. So far, it has been like that. Some still should have entered PKH but have not been included in the program participants. (Social service officer, Sumedang regency)

Resource allocation and nutritional impact of assistance programmes

The distribution and delivery of food and financial assistance programmes vary between Bekasi City and Sumedang Regency. BPNT improved food access but lacked dietary diversity, especially in rural Sumedang. Local initiatives, such as the Kelompok Wanita Tani (KWT) or Women Farmer’s Group, supported household nutrition through small-scale food production; however, these efforts remained limited in scale and sustainability. The removal of designated food vendors (e-warung) also reduced programme oversight, while irregular distributions undermined the consistency of food security.

We received assistance for the stunting program in the form of supplementary food, such as meat, eggs, and formula milk. … Currently receiving (supplementary food) PMT assistance once a day (since last year) from the village. The PMT menu usually consists of quail egg and tofu stew, baked potato, pudding and nuggets, sausages, and vegetable soup (quail egg and carrot filling). (Mother of stunted children, Bekasi city)

In terms of the effectiveness of distribution, it’s good, because of the use by the community, we don't know after the money is taken, whether it’s received, or what it’s bought for, we don't know, we can't monitor it. (Social service officer, Sumedang regency)

Meanwhile, the PBI-JK programme played a crucial role in improving healthcare access for low-income families in both regions. In Bekasi, stronger infrastructure and combined national and regional budget support (APBN and APBD) facilitated more consistent service delivery. However, Sumedang’s limited fiscal capacity and rural geography led to persistent gaps in coverage and access. Health officers reported difficulties in ensuring timely referrals and adequate maternal and childcare, particularly in remote areas.

In terms of budget, of course, the challenge is budgeting capability. (Apart from that,) the local original income has not contributed much, we only rely on the central government. (Health officer, Sumedang regency)

The misallocation of resources is further compounded by administrative barriers that vary across decentralised settings. While urban centres struggle with tracking highly mobile migrant populations, rural districts face an additional layer of low public awareness regarding vital civil registration requirements.

We were constrained by data on people who were moving and died. If I'm not mistaken, the local government was not provided with data on beneficiaries (by name or by address) from the central population and civil registration service (Dukcapil Pusat). So, the local population and civil registration service (Dukcapil kota Bekasi) could not compare which of its citizens have different addresses and died, while the data is dynamic. (Health officer, Bekasi city)

So, this data problem still exists, then yes, it is a population administration problem because not everyone is aware that this is important. Some people are poor and then miss out on not having health insurance due to the population administration (problem). (Health officer, Sumedang regency)

Commitment of programme implementers and service utilisation

Programme success in both districts reflected strong local commitment and coordination. Local governments ensured that health insurance support and social assistance reached high-risk families through efficient data management, streamlined verification processes and proactive allocation of funds. Local officers (LOs) played a crucial role in bridging the gap between provincial and district policies and community-level action, ensuring that stunting prevention received focused attention.

Local officer (LO) plays a crucial role in ensuring that programs at the sub-district level run smoothly. They ensure interventions are carried out and ensure that stunting-related issues receive serious attention. (Regional development planning officer, Sumedang regency)

Cross-sector collaboration was crucial, especially in Sumedang, where partnerships among government agencies, NGOs, academia and the private sector enabled more integrated responses.

We see the success of stunting prevention in Sumedang due to close cooperation between various sectors. We can work together to tackle this issue. (Regional development planning officer, Sumedang regency)

Technology innovation also supported implementation by improving data integration and targeting. Digital platforms like Simpati Jitu and monitoring systems such as Sinurmi enabled real-time analysis of maternal and child health indicators, enhancing the precision of interventions.

By using AI, we can deliver more precise interventions. Data-driven recommendations greatly help in preventing stunting among children…. Utilising technology to monitor and evaluate programs enables us to adjust strategies more quickly, based on real-time data. This is crucial for the program’s effectiveness. (Regional development planning officer, Sumedang regency)

Bureaucratic efficiency and programme accessibility

Bureaucratic and administrative challenges continued to be significant barriers to ensuring equitable access to health insurance for vulnerable populations. In Sumedang Regency, although the nominal coverage of Jaminan Kesehatan Nasional (JKN) membership is 95.4%, only approximately 75% of participants are active, resulting in a significant number of impoverished households remaining uninsured. Issues such as outdated population data, inconsistent premium payments and limited public awareness of JKN utilisation limit the programme’s reach.

Many low-income families have not registered as JKN participants because of problems with administrative data that has not been updated. This hinders their access to health services. (Health officer, Sumedang regency)

In Bekasi City, administrative challenges also arose, especially for migrants and mobile populations who faced difficulties with residency requirements and documentation. Local governments faced limited fiscal space to subsidise premiums, resulting in dependence on central government transfers. Moreover, JKN’s benefit package was often limited for the poorest groups, constraining coverage for specific diseases and specialised services.

We are very dependent on government funding. If the government does not pay the premium, we cannot get the health access we need. (Health officer, Sumedang regency)

Our findings highlighted the need for more flexible enrolment systems, improved interagency coordination and better targeting to accommodate dynamic populations. Public education was seen as essential to maintaining active membership and preventing dropouts among low-income families. Improving administrative efficiency and ensuring inclusive policies are crucial for maximising the impact of health insurance assistance, reducing financial barriers to care and supporting the prevention of stunting through accessible maternal and child health services.

Although the coverage of membership is already high, many are inactive because some feel they do not need or cannot pay premiums on time. We need to raise awareness so that they remain active. (Health officer, Sumedang regency)

Nevertheless, our findings suggest a more complex picture from the perspective of primary beneficiaries. Parents and caregivers described several practical benefits of joining the programmes, particularly access to updated information on child growth, development and appropriate parenting practices. Regular health gatherings also provided an important space for direct consultation, enabling mothers to discuss their infants’ and children’s health concerns with health workers and community health cadres.

Some families also mentioned that occasional charity-based activities within local programmes helped them manage unexpected household needs. However, the data also revealed signs of community resistance. In several cases, families refused to have their babies or children examined. This reluctance appeared to be closely related to anxiety about receiving a possible stunting diagnosis, which many parents perceived as socially unacceptable or stigmatising.

Discussion

This study emphasises the significant impact of local contextual factors on the delivery of food and social assistance programmes in addressing stunting in Indonesia. National initiatives, like BPNT (non-cash food assistance), PKH (conditional cash transfers) and PBI-JK (health insurance subsidies), have been widely implemented. However, our findings reveal that the effectiveness of these programmes varies significantly depending on how they are implemented at the subnational level.

Four critical factors, such as communication, resources, implementer disposition and bureaucratic structure, shape the success of policy implementation. Communication is found to be a decisive factor.25 Effective communication ensures stakeholders understand programme goals and reduces resistance.26 In Bekasi, proactive health cadres engaged communities through outreach and education, improving programme awareness. Conversely, in Sumedang, low awareness left many families vulnerable, reflecting insufficient information dissemination. This aligns with findings in Padang City, where limited campaigning due to high health worker workloads reduced programme reach,27 and with other studies emphasising the central role of communication in stunting reduction efforts.28 Technological tools, such as Zoom and WhatsApp groups, have also been noted to strengthen communication in programme delivery.28 However, the adoption of such tools varies across user groups, reflecting differing levels of digital literacy, access and openness to innovation.29 This variation demands adaptive communication strategies that accommodate diverse user capabilities within target communities.

Resources are equally crucial. It has been highlighted that staff, information, authority and facilities are key components, with staff being most pivotal. These findings are consistent with research showing that optimal stunting interventions depend not only on service availability but also on delivery quality and local context.30 31 The difference in programme performance between urban and rural areas suggests that the implementation of Edward III’s framework is context-dependent, indicating the necessity of contextual administrative capacity in shaping policy outcomes.

Food assistance programmes improved basic food access but struggled to ensure dietary diversity, especially in rural Sumedang. Bekasi beneficiaries could supplement BPNT allocations through diverse local markets, but Sumedang’s limited market access and infrequent distributions constrained dietary quality. The removal of designated food vendors (e-warung) complicated monitoring and increased the risk of misuse. Local empowerment initiatives, such as KWT, promoted food production and yard-based food programmes (Teras Hejo) but remained limited in scale and sustainability. The sustainability of Women Farmers Groups (KWT) and yard-based food programmes depends heavily on regional social characteristics. These observations align with international evidence indicating that food insecurity, inadequate infrastructure and limited market integration can undermine nutrition outcomes, even with assistance.30 31 Moreover, preventing chronic undernutrition requires more than just food supply; it also includes improved access to healthcare, maternal education and hygiene practices.32 33

Implementer disposition, as shown by their commitment and motivation, is another pivotal aspect. This study found strong commitment in both Bekasi and Sumedang, where local governments prioritised families at high risk of stunting and fostered cross-sector collaboration. Previous studies have similarly highlighted the intrinsic motivation, altruism, empathy and sense of empowerment of health cadres as critical for achieving policy goals.34 35

Bureaucratic structure also shaped programme implementation. Although PBI-JK was designed to reduce financial barriers to maternal and child healthcare, its impact was limited by administrative inefficiencies and weak data management, with outdated population data and migration-related errors, leaving many registered beneficiaries inactive or incorrectly recorded and causing some high-risk groups to remain uninsured or experience delays in accessing care. Such systemic weaknesses mirror broader issues in Indonesia’s social protection systems, where fragmented databases and top-down enrolment procedures hinder equitable service delivery.30 31 Strengthening civil registration and local-level data validation is as important as expanding coverage. Flexible, locally integrated enrolment and referral systems are essential for effectively reaching targeted populations.36

Although Edward III’s framework mainly focuses on internal bureaucratic structures, this study shows that stunting reduction efforts require a broader and more dynamic application of the model. In our study, we highlight the impact of local leadership and innovation in driving progress in resource-constrained settings. In Sumedang, digital tools such as Sinurmi and Simpati Jitu enabled real-time monitoring of maternal and child health, supporting early detection and targeted interventions. These efforts were strengthened through penta-helix collaboration among government, academia, private sector, media and communities, embodying global best practices for multisectoral, community-driven solutions.4 Sumedang’s example shows that decentralised, technology-driven and partnership-based governance can help overcome geographic, budgetary and human resource challenges. This pattern of innovation adoption in Sumedang reflects Rogers’ diffusion of innovations theory, which explains how communities adopt new ideas at different speeds depending on their readiness, resources and social influence. In this context, local health workers and village leaders may have acted as early adopters, helping to build trust and accelerate the acceptance of digital innovations for stunting prevention.37 38

Taken together, these findings suggest that Edward III’s framework should be complemented by broader socio-political and economic considerations. Our results indicate that social stigma, community-led innovation, and fiscal dependency shape implementation in ways not fully captured by the original model. Incorporating this contextual dimension may better explain variation in policy outcomes across decentralized settings. Importantly, the relationship between innovation and implementation barriers changed over time. Some stunting prevention innovations were not initially well received by communities, not because people rejected the programmes, but because they faced practical challenges such as long travel distances, limited time and restricted access to digital tools. Health cadres helped address these barriers through regular community outreach, which gradually strengthened participation and trust. Support from internet and network providers also helped health workers carry out digital-based early detection, while platforms such as Ogapudake digital education showed promise in improving knowledge about stunting prevention.39 However, the benefits of these innovations were not equally experienced, particularly in areas with poor signal coverage, indicating that infrastructural inequalities continue to influence programme implementation.

Despite broad programme coverage, several systemic bottlenecks limited impact. Many families remained uninsured due to outdated beneficiary data, lack of awareness or administrative hurdles, and misallocated funds to inactive participants further constrained resources for high-risk populations. While some innovative local practices on digital health monitoring, such as Simpati Jitu and Sinurmi, helped bridge implementation gaps, poor data integration was not solely a technical challenge but was also shaped by institutional silos, fragmented governance arrangements, and bureaucratic competition across sectors responsible for nutrition, health, and social protection programs. These factors constrained information sharing and coordinated service delivery.

Both areas also relied on central government funding, which limited their fiscal autonomy and prevented them from scaling or adapting services to local needs. This fiscal dependency reflected longstanding patterns of centralized decision-making, uneven fiscal decentralization, and limited local revenue-generation capacity, hindering local governments' ability to adapt interventions to community needs. This finding suggests that addressing stunting requires not only improvements in program implementation but also reforms that strengthen multisectoral governance, local institutional capacity, and fiscal autonomy. Moreover, administrative requirements, such as proof of residency, excluded vulnerable groups, including internal migrants and the urban poor. Programme eligibility rules, budget caps and inconsistent monitoring reduced flexibility in responding dynamically to stunting cases. Such challenges are widely documented in studies of Indonesia’s social assistance system.13 40 Without more responsive policy instruments and improved cross-sector data interoperability, achieving national stunting reduction targets will remain difficult.20 41

In addition, the social barrier also shows that improving programme targeting requires a stronger and more sensitive data system. In this regard, the government’s effort to unify social and economic data through the National Social and Economic Single Data System (DTSEN) is important for improving the accuracy and transparency of social assistance. However, data unification at the national level will only be meaningful if local records are regularly updated and verified, and if the use of data does not unintentionally reinforce stigma among targeted families.42

Moreover, our study found that stigma and shame influenced some families’ decisions to decline stunting-related assistance. Although the programme was intended to provide support, being identified as a recipient could be perceived as socially burdensome, particularly because stunting is often associated with poverty, poor caregiving or parental failure. The notion of ‘bureaucratised shame’, refusal may be understood not merely as resistance to the programme, but as a strategy to protect family dignity and avoid negative social labelling.43

Limitations

This study has some limitations. Conducted in two areas of West Java, the findings are context-specific and may not capture the diversity of experiences across Indonesia. Moreover, as a qualitative study, the findings are not designed to be statistically generalisable. Future research could adopt a mixed-methods approach to examine more precisely how differences in cross-sectoral coordination influence child growth outcomes across districts. Yet, the triangulation of perspectives and the focus on implementation processes provide valuable and transferable lessons for other settings in Indonesia and LMICs facing similar challenges.

Conclusion

In conclusion, stunting is not merely a technical or nutritional problem but a systemic challenge that demands strong local systems, effective communication, accurate data and cross-sector collaboration. The study underscores the complex interplay of factors influencing the implementation and effectiveness of food and social assistance programmes aimed at reducing stunting in Indonesia. While national initiatives such as BPNT, PKH and PBI-JK offer promising frameworks, their impact varies significantly at the local level due to differences in local capacities, data management and intersectoral co-ordination. The high dedication and altruism of community health workers in Bekasi and Sumedang illustrate effective strategies to overcome implementation barriers; however, persistent structural challenges, such as limited fiscal autonomy and administrative complexity, continue to hinder programme coverage and effectiveness.

To achieve Indonesia’s stunting reduction targets, investments are needed in local governance, regulatory flexibility and community-driven interventions that strengthen local capacity and adapt to the diverse needs of urban and rural areas. These findings not only inform national policy but also offer valuable lessons for other LMICs seeking to improve nutrition outcomes through more effective, decentralised and context-sensitive implementation strategies. Furthermore, future comparative studies across Indonesian provinces and other LMICs would be useful for ensuring the robustness of the identified barriers and innovations in implementing stunting reduction programmes.
