Body mass index and post-operative outcomes in older adults after major surgery
Authors: Jun Kiat Thaddaeus Tan, Wei Jing Fong, Sudha Harikrishnan, Ming Zhe Cai, Chin Jin Seo, Chin-Ann Johnny Ong, Chia Shulyn Claramae, Hairil Rizal Abdullah, Si Min Jolene Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUp to 40% of major elective surgery are performed in older adults. Various frailty assessments have been developed to risk stratify older adults undergoing surgery. However, these are often complex and time-consuming to administer. There is need for a convenient and pragmatic screening tool. We hypothesize that body mass index (BMI) can be used to identify older adults at risk of poorer outcomes following major elective surgery.MethodsWe queried an institutional Perioperative Anaesthesia Subject Area Registry to identify older adults (aged ≥ 65) who underwent major elective surgery between January 2020 and December 2021. A BMI of less than 18.5 kg/m2 was defined as underweight, 18.5–22.9 kg/m2 normal-weight, 23.0–27.4 kg/m2 overweight and ≥ 27.5 kg/m2 as obese. We built multivariable regression models to test associations between BMI and outcomes including overall length of stay(LOS), LOS in intensive care(ICU) and 30-day mortality.ResultsSix thousand five hundred forty-eight older adults underwent major elective surgery. Mean BMI was 25.8 kg/m2, mean hospital LOS was 9 days, ICU LOS 8.6 h, and 30-day mortality 0.1%. 244(4%) had low and 1057(16%) had very high BMI. Being underweight was associated with longer overall LOS (IRR 1.31, 95% CI 1.19–1.44). There was no difference in 30-day mortality between BMI groups.ConclusionExtremes of BMI was associated with an overall longer overall LOS in hospital in older adults undergoing major elective surgery. BMI can serve as a supplementary tool to identify at-risk older adults for targeted interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03673-x.Keywords: Surgery, Body mass index, Geriatric medicine, Frailty

Introduction

The global population is gradually ageing with a deleterious impact on healthy life expectancy [1]. Recent data suggests that up to 40% of major elective surgery is performed in older adults, and this includes hip or knee replacement, lower extremity revascularization, or visceral resection such as colectomy and cholecystectomy [2–4]. Aging and its pathophysiological consequence have been found to be associated with major post-operative complications and mortality [5, 6]. In a study of adults undergoing cardiac surgery, age of more than 80 years was associated with a 2.5-fold increase in odds of in-hospital mortality and a 3-fold increase in stroke risk when compared to younger adults [7], and a recent meta-analysis discovered that certain geriatric syndromes can portend poorer outcomes in older adults undergoing elective surgery [8].

Frailty is common in older adult populations [9], and mediates the effect between age and post-operative outcomes [10–13]. Among older adults undergoing non-cardiac surgery, increasing frailty scores was found to be an independent predictor of 30-day mortality and institutional care following surgery [14, 15]. A recent validation study by Chau et al. further suggested these scores potentially predict mortality and unplanned readmissions up to 270 days postoperatively [16]. Multivariable frailty assessment tools while useful, often include several measured clinical and biochemical variables that may not be readily available to assess in a preoperative setting, making them difficult to reliably incorporate to clinical practice [17]. Furthermore, in a review of 67 types of frailty instruments, Buta et al. had found that no single score was broadly applicable across all clinical contexts for uniform use across all surgery types [18]. As such, a pragmatic and convenient screening tool specifically catered towards preoperative risk stratification of older adults undergoing major elective surgery would be useful to supplement available tools in clinical practice.

Body mass index (BMI) is universally measured and is a surrogate for nutritional status and peri-operative fitness [19, 20], with the benefit of being simple to measure using readily available information. Adults with high BMI were found to have reduced mortality after hip fracture surgery [21, 22]. A similar association between high BMI and reduced 30-day mortality was also found in abdominal surgery across 1.8 million patients in 62 studies [23].

We sought to evaluate the association between BMI and early post-operative clinical quality indicators among older adults undergoing various types of major surgery. To do so, we performed a retrospective longitudinal analysis of older adult patients undergoing surgery in our institution, to characterize the types of major elective surgery performed among older adults and test the association between BMI and early post-operative outcomes in this population.

Methods

Study design and participants

We queried the Perioperative Anaesthesia Subject Area Registry (PASAR) registry between January 2020 and December 2021 and included older adults aged 65 years and above who underwent major elective surgery. The PASAR registry is an ongoing, prospective database containing more than 150,000 patient admissions for all surgeries performed at the largest academic medical centre in Singapore [24].

Major elective surgery was defined in accordance with the American Agency of Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) and was identified referencing International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Procedure Coding System (ICD-10-PCS) Medicare claims codes [25, 26]. Matching the Table of Surgical Procedures (TOSP) coding designated by the Singapore Ministry of Health and the American ICD-10- PCS codes, we identified codes corresponding to various major surgery types as shown in Supplementary Table S1 [27]. All definitions adopted were in accordance with the American Medicare Provider Analysis and Review (MEDPAR) [28]. To overcome differences in coding practices between ICD-10-PCS and our local TOSP coding system, we performed a pragmatic selection of the top five major surgery types performed in our study cohort, namely - colorectal resection, cholecystectomy, coronary artery bypass graft (CABG), lower extremity revascularization, and orthopaedic surgery. These five procedure types were selected because firstly, they were the most commonly performed major surgical procedures in our study cohort, and secondly, they were procedures that were most likely to be performed in isolation without an associated sub-procedure, to minimize the risk of analytical duplication due to coding errors. Older adults undergoing these surgeries were included in our study. Older adults undergoing emergency surgery or with missing body mass index, demographics, functional status, and post-operative outcomes data were excluded [29].

Exposure: body mass index

We operationalised Body Mass Index (BMI) as a categorical variable in accordance with the World Health Organization’s (WHO) international standards adjusted for an Asian population [30, 31]. A BMI of less than 18.5 kg/m2 was defined as underweight, 18.5–22.9 kg/m2 normal-weight, 23.0–27.4 kg/m2 overweight and 27.5 kg/m2 and above as obese [30, 32].

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes measured were overall hospital length of stay (LOS), LOS in the intensive care unit (ICU) and, and 30-day mortality following major elective surgery.

Admission and discharge criteria for the intensive care unit at our institution aligns to international standards, involving an individualized assessment of patients’ organ support requirements and hemodynamic stability [33].

Other covariates

Patient characteristics included age, gender, ethnicity, and American Society of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) status, cardiac risk index and comorbidities. Comorbidities included history of diabetes mellitus, cerebrovascular accidents or ischemic heart disease, hypertension, or hyperlipidaemia. Preoperative functional status operationalized as a three-category variable: partially dependent, totally dependent, and totally independent, in accordance to the Katz Index of activities of daily living (ADL) [34].

Statistical analysis

We performed descriptive analyses for patient characteristics based on the type of major surgery performed. The Pearson’s chi-square test was used for categorical variables and Student’s T test for continuous variables. We built negative binomial regression with a log link, zero-inflated negative binomial regression, and binomial regression to compare unadjusted and adjusted differences in overall LOS, LOS in ICU, and 30-day mortality, respectively, in the four BMI exposure groups. Reference category for all comparative analyses for BMI groups was the normal-weight category. Multivariable negative binomial regression was used to model LOS outcomes and logistic regression for binomial outcomes. Regression models were adjusted for age, gender, ASA status, comorbidities, cardiac risk index, functional status, and major surgery type. Coefficient estimates from all models were exponentiated and reported as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) for negative binomial models and odds ratios (ORs) for the binomial model. We also performed subgroup analysis of outcomes by major surgery types. Statistical analysis was performed using R (Version 4.3.1; R Core Team 2021). Two-sided p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Given the exploratory nature of the study, no correction was performed for multiple testing.

Results

Study participants

A total of 18,879 older adults underwent major elective surgery in the PASAR registry during the defined period. Of these, 11,124 patients were not categorized under the selected procedure groups, and a further 1207 patients had insufficient data for analysis and were excluded. The remaining 6548 were included in this study. Of these, 3848 (58.8%) older adults underwent orthopaedic procedures, including total hip and knee replacement (Fig. 1).

In comparison to patients with normal range BMI, overall inpatient length of stay of patients who were underweight was 1.3 times longer (13.4 vs. 10.2 days; IRR 1.31, 95% CI 1.19–1.44, p < 0.001). Adjusted prediction modelling of interactions between age, BMI and overall LOS demonstrated that being underweight was associated with a longer overall length of stay regardless of age (Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this longitudinal cohort study of older adults undergoing major elective surgery, there were 4% of older adults who were underweight and this was associated with a prolonged overall hospital length of stay (LOS).

Low BMI is indicative of a malnourished state which is known to adversely impacts post-surgical outcomes [23, 35–39]. In a cohort study of patients undergoing vascular procedures for peripheral vascular disease, a low BMI was associated with higher rates of reintervention (20% vs. 18% BMI, p < 0.001) and major adverse limb events (27% vs. 22%, p < 0.001) [40]. In other surgical populations undergoing cardiac procedures, a similar correlation between malnutrition and major adverse cardiac and cerebral events was found [41]. In our study, 8% of older adults underwent revascularization surgery for peripheral arterial disease, 11% cardiac, 13% colorectal, 59% orthopaedics, and 9% cholecystectomy. Regardless of major elective surgery types, being underweight demonstrated an independent association with prolonged overall hospital LOS. In one study, patients who were identified to be malnourished or at risk of malnutrition using the Malnutritional Universal Screening Tool (MUST) and received routine pre-operative nutritional optimization were shown to have better post-operative outcomes, including less major post-operative complications [42]. Though reliable, the administration of MUST is often time-consuming and requires dedicated manpower [43, 44]. Our findings suggest that being underweight is potentially associated with a longer postoperative length of stay, and there may be a role to consider routine pre-operative nutritional intervention in underweight older adult patients in order to improve surgical outcomes.

A notable finding of this study is the nonlinear relationship between BMI and postoperative LOS as well as 30-day mortality. Underweight patients in this study population tended to have longer overall LOS as well as a trend towards higher mortality rates, and this trend was similarly observed in patients with extremely high BMI (Fig. 3). The current literature describes an ‘obesity paradox’ which accounts for the trend towards better postoperative outcomes for patients with above-normal BMI [22, 23], whereupon retrospective cohort studies found that obese patients had a better overall postoperative recovery and reduced long-term postoperative morbidity, ultimately attributable to their well-nourished state [21, 45]. Our study did not demonstrate such an association, with obese and overweight patients having no significant difference in measured postoperative outcomes compared to the normal-BMI group, although it is notable that obese patients demonstrated comparatively favourable postoperative outcomes compared to patients in the other extreme BMI category. The findings from our study suggest that a low BMI in older adults planned for surgery is independently associated with a longer postoperative hospital stay, supplementing more complex frailty assessment tools and providing clinicians means to rapidly assess older adults planned for elective major surgery to optimize clinical resource allocation and enhance preoperative counselling.

The current study has several limitations. First, the study population was limited to a single academic centre limiting its generalizability across surgical populations worldwide. However, Singapore has a unique multi-racial society and various ethnicities are well-represented in our dataset [46]. The use of registry data is subject to coding errors and missingness, and this introduces bias to the study’s findings. The exclusion of 11,124 older adults undergoing surgical procedures outside of the selected five procedure groups, and further exclusion of 1206 patients in the registry with insufficient data for analysis potentially introduces selection bias and limits the generalizability of results across all surgical populations. Operationalizing BMI as a single categorical variable to predict postoperative outcomes potentially results in loss of information and also neglects current trends in nutritional assessment favouring the use of body composition assessment methods (e.g. bioelectrical impedance analysis), although this was intentionally done to assess the utility of standard BMI categories in predicting postoperative outcomes and simplifies its use as a convenient tool for preoperative risk stratification. This study also does not account for the impact of preoperative intervention, prehabilitation regimes or the effects of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways, nor does it report data on postoperative complications or long-term outcomes due to limitations with collected registry data. We also did not correct for nor report data on other intermediary nutritional factors influencing BMI, including body composition studies, sarcopenia, skinfold thickness, ASPEN nutritional scores, since the intent of the current study was to determine if BMI could be adopted as a convenient screening assessment tool to risk stratify older adults undergoing major surgery. The use of LOS in ICU as a clinical outcome also does not account for operational factors such as bed capacity limitations, patient and physician preferences which may confound the decision for discharge from these monitored care settings. Furthermore, outcomes pertaining LOS in ICU, were in the metric of hours as such the clinical implications of this difference may be limited. Mortality outcomes reported also bear limited clinical relevance given the overall low incidence of mortality in this population (1.0%).

Future studies should additionally evaluate the interactions between BMI and various other methods of estimating overall nutritional status (for instance, bioelectrical impedance studies, rectus femoris muscle ultrasound) on postoperative complications and long-term mortality. Specifically pertaining the use of BMI as a pragmatic tool for preoperative risk stratification of older patients, future studies may investigate the cost-effectiveness of this approach in perioperative management of the older adult surgical patient compared with more elaborate multicomponent frailty assessment tools to justify its use in a clinical setting.

Conclusion

In older adults, extremes of BMI were found to be associated with higher care requirements, and in particular being underweight was additionally associated with a longer overall hospital length of stay. BMI can serve as a convenient screening tool to supplement existing frailty assessment scores to identify at-risk older adults for targeted interventions to improve clinical outcomes.
