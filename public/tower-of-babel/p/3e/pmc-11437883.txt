Aberrant positivity for BCOR immunohistochemistry in merkel cell carcinoma - a potential diagnostic pitfall
Authors: Viola Katharina Vetter, Martina Haberecker, Florian Alexander Huber, Chantal Pauli
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackrgoundMerkel cell carcinoma (MCC) is a rare, aggressive primary cutaneous neuroendocrine carcinoma, frequently associated with clonal Merkel cell polyomavirus integration. MCC can pose significant diagnostic challenges due to its diverse clinical presentation and its broad histological differential diagnosis. Histologically, MCC presents as a small-round-blue cell neoplasm, where the differential diagnosis includes basal cell carcinoma, melanoma, hematologic malignancies, round cell sarcoma and metastatic small cell carcinoma of any site. We here report strong aberrant immunoreactivity for BCOR in MCC, a marker commonly used to identify round cell sarcomas and other neoplasms with BCOR alterations.MethodsBased on strong BCOR expression in three index cases of MCC, clinically mistaken as sarcoma, a retrospective analysis of three patient cohorts, comprising 31 MCC, 19 small cell lung carcinoma (SCLC) and 5 cases of neoplasms with molecularly confirmed BCOR alteration was conducted. Immunohistochemical staining intensity and localization for BCOR was semi-quantitatively analyzed.ResultsThree cases, clinically and radiologically mimicking a sarcoma were analyzed in our soft tissue and bone pathology service. Histologically, the cases showed sheets of a small round blue cell neoplasm. A broad panel of immunohistochemistry was used for lineage classification. Positivity for synaptophysin, CK20 and Merkel cell polyoma virus large T-antigen lead to the diagnosis of a MCC. Interestingly, all cases showed strong positive nuclear staining for BCOR, which was included for the initial work-up with the clinical differential of a round cell sarcoma. We analyzed a larger retrospective MCC cohort and found aberrant weak to strong BCOR positivity (nuclear and/or cytoplasmic) in up to 90% of the cases. As a positive control, we compared the expression to a small group of BCOR-altered neoplasms. Furthermore, we investigated a cohort of SCLC as another neuroendocrine neoplasm and found in all cases a diffuse moderate to strong BCOR positivity.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that neuroendocrine neoplasms, such as MCC and SCLC can express strong aberrant BCOR. This might represent a diagnostic challenge or pitfall, in particular when MCC is clinically mistaken as a soft tissue or a bone sarcoma.Keywords: Merkel cell carcinoma, BCOR, Immunohistochemistry, Neuroendocrine carcinoma, SCLC, Sarcoma

Introduction

Merkel cell carcinoma (MCC) is a primary neuroendocrine carcinoma of the skin. It typically presents as a rapidly growing nodule or plaque on sun-damaged skin of elderly, predominantly Caucasian patients and is associated with immunosuppression [1]. It is a very rare neoplasm with an incidence between 0.13 per 100.000 in Europe and 1.6 per 100.000 in Australia [2, 3]. The clinical course is often aggressive with early metastatic disease and high mortality rates [4].

In 2008 Feng et al. discovered that the majority of MCCs are associated with the clonal integration of Merkel cell polyoma virus (MCPyV) leading to the constitutive expression of the oncogenic viral large and small T-antigens [5, 6]. In contrast, virus-negative MCCs are associated with sun-damage and show a distinct mutational landscape from virus-positive tumors, including high mutational burden and a UV-signature mutational pattern [7].

Histologically, MCC presents as a small, round, blue-cell neoplasm that is classically positive for neuroendocrine markers and in ∼95% for CK20 by immunohistochemistry. In the skin the histological differential diagnosis of MCC include basal cell carcinoma, melanoma, hematologic malignancies, round cell sarcoma and metastatic small cell carcinoma of any site [8].

In this study we present three diagnostically challenging cases of MCC with an unusual clinical presentation, clinically mistaken as soft tissue and bone sarcomas. Due to the clinical suspicion of sarcoma and the round cell morphology, BCOR (BCL6 Corepressor) was included in the immunohistochemical work up. Strong aberrant positive staining for BCOR was found and further investigated in a larger cohort of MCC and small cell carcinoma of the lung (SCLC). BCOR overexpression has previously been reported as a highly sensitive marker for round cell sarcomas with BCOR genetic abnormalities and is routinely used in our soft tissue and bone pathology service [9]. Positivity for BCOR in clinically challenging small cell neoplasms such as MCC or SCLC might present an important diagnostic pitfall.

Materials and methods

Patient cohorts

In addition to the three index patient cases (Table 1), 31 tumor samples of 21 patients with MCC from primary (n = 13) and metastatic sides (n = 16), one local recurrence (n = 1) and one case presenting within a parotid lymph node with unknown primary (n = 1) were included in this retrospective study (Table 2). In addition we analyzed 19 patient cases with SCLC. Five cases with a molecularly confirmed BCOR-rearrangement were included as a positive control: two sarcomas with BCOR-ITD (internal tandem duplication), one sarcoma with a BCOR::CNNB3 fusion, one clear cell sarcoma of the kidney with BCOR-ITD and one high-grade endometrial stroma sarcoma with an EP300::BCOR fusion. Archival FFPE (formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded) material was available for all cases. The present study was conducted following regional / cantonal and institutional guidelines in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration and after approval by our cantonal ethical review board Zurich (BASEC- 2021 − 00417).

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical stains for the MCPyV large T-antigen (clone CM2B4, Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc., platform: Ventana, dilution 1:250) and BCOR (clone C-10, Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc., platform: Leica (BOND), dilution 1:100, antibody incubation: 30 min, pre-treatment: BOND Epitope Retrievel Solution 2 for 60 min, 100°) were performed on whole slide FFPE sections. BCOR immunohistochemistry was read by two pathologists (VKV, CP). Positive BCOR staining intensity was scored semi-quantitatively as negative (0), weak (1+), moderate (2+) or strong (3+). In cases of focal or heterogeneous staining intensity, the maximum staining intensity was registered. Moreover the percentage of tumor cells with any positivity (1+, 2 + or 3+) and the staining location (nuclear, cytoplasmic, peri-nuclear dot like) was reported.

Results

Three clinically challenging patient cases of Merkel cell carcinoma (MCC)

We present three challenging patient cases of MCC, clinically mistaken as soft tissue and bone sarcoma (Table 1).

The first case involved a 66-year-old male patient with a 12 cm soft tissue mass in the inguinal region and an 8 cm soft tissue mass in the gluteal region. MRI imaging showed two lesions that were T1 isointense to muscle and T2 hyper-intense (Fig. 1A-B). Sonography showed an increased vascularization (not shown). Radiologically and clinically these two masses were highly suspicious for a metastatic soft tissue sarcoma.

C, axial, and D, sagittal, T2-weighted MRI images of case 2 showing a slightly hypointense lesion in the second thoracic vertebra (arrow) in the transversal (C) and sagittal plane (D) and additional similar lesions in other thoracic vertebrae. (F) axial T1-weighted MRI image showing a mass (arrow) with isointensity to muscle

The second patient, a 79-year-old male patient, had a mass located in the second thoracic vertebra associated with an impression fracture (Fig. 1C-D). MRI imaging revealed additional similar lesions throughout the thoracic and lumbar vertebral columns. Clinical suspicion leaned towards metastatic neoplasia of unknown primary versus a primary metastatic bone neoplasm.

The third case was a 62-year-old female patient exhibiting a subcutaneous soft tissue lesion on the upper arm, which had grown progressively over the preceding year. The lesion was painless and immobile. Sonographic evaluation revealed a multilobulated, hypoechoic lesion (maximum diameter 19 mm) with increased vascularization and focally indistinct edges (Fig. 1E). Additionally, two separate smaller satellite nodules were identified. MRI (Fig. 1F) indicated lesions that were T1 isointense to muscle, T2 hyper-intense, exhibited contrast enhancement, and displayed restricted diffusion. These findings were suspicions of malignancy, specifically leaning towards a soft tissue sarcoma, with potential differential diagnoses including myxofibrosarcoma and dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans.

Histologic examination of biopsies from all three cases showed solid sheets of a small-round-blue cell neoplasm (Fig. 2A-C). The tumor cells were relatively uniform. The nuclei were oval to slightly irregular in shape and displayed a finely dispersed, occasionally mildly granular chromatin, with nuclear hyperchromasia (particularly in case 3). Nucleoli were absent or inconspicuous. In cases two and three the tumor cells exhibited scant cytoplasm with indistinct cell borders and areas of nuclear molding. The tumor cells in case one showed scant to moderate eosinophilic cytoplasm and no nuclear molding. In cases one and two foci of necrosis were visible. Mitoses and apoptotic figures were abundant. Overall, the tumor cells showed some cytomorphologic features that are consistent with a neuroendocrine neoplasm. Still, a broad panel of immunohistochemistry (including CD45, pan-cytokeratins (AE1/AE3), S100, SOX 10, desmin, synaptophysin, CD99 and BCOR) was used for the lineage classification. Positivity for synaptophysin and pan-cytokeratins guided further work-up using CK20 as well as MCPyV large T-antigen and Merkel cell carcinoma was diagnosed in all three cases (Fig. 2D-L). Ki67-staining showed a proliferation index exceeding 60%. Notably, all three cases showed strong positive nuclear staining for BCOR (Fig. 2M-O), a marker that was included in the immunohistochemistry panel for the initial work-up of a small-round-blue cell neoplasm with a clinical differential of a sarcoma.

BCOR expression in neoplasms with molecularly confirmed BCOR-rearrangement

BCOR overexpression by immunohistochemistry has previously been described to be a sensitive marker for detecting round cell sarcomas with BCOR alterations [9]. As a positive control for the BCOR immunohistochemical stain we included five cases of molecularly confirmed BCOR altered neoplasms (BCOR ITDs, BCOR::CNNB3- and EP300::BCOR-rearrangements). As expected, all cases presented a moderate (2+) to strong (3+) diffuse, nuclear staining in > 90% of tumor cells (Fig. 3).

Aberrant BCOR expression in MCC

To systematically investigate BCOR expression in MCC, we conducted immunohistochemical staining on tissue samples obtained from 31 MCC from 21 patients, encompassing both primary and metastatic sites. 18 tumor samples were confirmed positive for MCPyV by immunohistochemistry, 13 cases were negative or showed an inconclusive staining for MCPyV. All tumors were positive for CK20 and synaptophysin. Our analysis revealed that 90% of cases (n = 28) exhibited at least focal positivity for BCOR (either nuclear, cytoplasmic or peri-nuclear dot-like staining or a combination). Two cases showed a strong (3+) staining intensity. The majority of cases (n = 17; 55%) demonstrated a heterogeneous moderate (2+) staining intensity. 9 cases (29%) showed a weak (1+) staining intensity. 3 cases (10%) showed no BCOR staining. The average percentage of tumor cells with any staining (weak to strong) was 70% for cases with 3 + maximum staining intensity, 61,5% (range: 20–90%) for cases with 2 + maximum staining intensity and 40% (range: 10–70%) for cases with 1 + maximum staining intensity. Tumor cells predominantly showed a nuclear staining pattern (92% of positive cases) (Fig. 4A), often accompanied by an additional, weak, cytoplasmic staining (71% of positive cases) (Fig. 4B). Intriguingly, 9 cases (32% of positive cases) displayed a peri-nuclear dot-like staining pattern (Fig. 4C). The dot-like staining pattern is a well-established staining pattern in MCC, commonly observed in other immunohistochemical stainings such as CK20 [8]. These findings highlight that immunoreactivity for BCOR in MCC is a common phenomenon. However, in contrast to neoplasms with molecularly confirmed BCOR-rearrangements, where BCOR expression is diffuse and moderate to strong, MCC typically showed a more heterogeneous moderate to strong staining pattern.

BCOR staining patterns in MCC: (A) moderate nuclear staining (B) moderate nuclear staining accompanied by a weak cytoplasmic staining (C) peri-nuclear dot-like staining

BCOR expression in SCLC

To explore BCOR expression in other neuroendocrine neoplasms, we performed immunohistochemical BCOR stains on 19 SCLC biopsies. Remarkably, all cases showed BCOR positivity with moderate (n = 8, 42%) to strong (n = 11, 58%) staining intensities (Fig. 5). In cases with strong 3 + staining intensity, the percentage of tumor cells with any staining was on average 75% (range: 50–90%). In cases with moderate 2 + staining intensity, on average 52% of the tumor cells showed any positivity (range: 20-90%). Nuclear staining was observed in all cases, occasionally accompanied by weak cytoplasmic staining (n = 9, 47%). One case showed focal peri-nuclear dot-like staining. These results underline that aberrant BCOR immuoreactivity is common in neuroendocrine carcinomas.

Discussion

Merkel cell carcinoma (MCC) typically presents as an asymptomatic, rapidly growing pink or violaceous skin lesion e.g. nodule. However, the correct clinical identification can be challenging. Indeed, it has been shown that in over 50% of cases, the initial clinical impression favors a benign lesion such as a cyst [1]. Rarely, the preliminary diagnosis may lean towards a sarcoma as small blue round cell sarcomas can strongly stain for keratins (e.g. desmoplastic small round cell tumor). Indeed, rare case reports describe how Merkel cell carcinoma can mimic a soft tissue sarcoma radiologically and histologically [10, 11].

The three index patient cases documented in this study underscore the potential atypical clinical and radiologic presentation of Merkel cell carcinoma as a soft tissue or bone mass.

By immunohistochemistry, MCC are typically positive for epithelial markers (e.g. AE1 / AE3), CK20, often in a perinuclear dot-like staining pattern, neuroendocrine markers (synaptophysin, chromogranin, INSM1) and neurofilament (NF) [12–16]. Moreover, they also commonly express the B-cell-antigens BCL-2, PAX-5, TdT and KIT questioning the cell of origin and potentially challenging the diagnosis [17]. SATB2 has been found as a promising and reliable marker in combination with CKAE1/AE3 and synaptophysin for the detection of Merkel cell carcinoma, especially in difficult locations such as lymph node metastasis [18]. Interestingly, aberrant expression of ALK and EZH2 has also been reported in MCC [19]. NF and Merkel cell polyomavirus (by immunohistochemistry or qPCR) appear to be helpful markers in distinguishing Merkel cell carcinomas from extra-cutaneous neuroendocrine carcinomas [20].

In the skin the histological differential diagnosis of MCC encompasses basal cell carcinoma, melanoma, hematologic malignancies, small round blue sarcomas and metastatic small cell carcinoma of a different site [8]. However, when encountering a small-round-blue-cell neoplasm in soft tissue or bone the differential diagnosis is broader and should include entities such a round cell sarcoma with EWSR1-non-ETS fusions, CIC-rearranged sarcoma, sarcoma with BCOR genetic alteration, high-grade myxoid liposarcoma, desmoplastic small round cell tumor, mesenchymal chondrosarcoma, small cell osteosarcoma and rhabdomyosarcoma [21].

BCOR (BCL-6 interacting Corepressor) is an interactor of BCL6 [22] and takes part in a polycomb repressive complex that is involved in epigenetic histone modification [23]. In cancer, BCOR aberrations, in particular ITDs and fusions, are drivers of various sarcomas and central nervous system neoplasms. Other BCOR alterations commonly occur in hematolymphoid neoplasms [5]. BCOR overexpression detected by immunohistochemistry was previously established as a sensitive marker for detecting round cell sarcomas with BCOR genetic abnormalities. However, the stain is not entirely specific with a subset of synovial sarcomas also showing positive BCOR immunoreactivity. This is thought to be associated to transcriptional mRNA up-regulation of BCOR in synovial sarcomas [9]. BCOR immunoreactivity has also been reported in uterine fusion-negative adenosarcomas [24]. Our study highlights BCOR immunoreactivity in MCC and SCLC. Without considering the proper context and other immunohistochemical stains, this could potentially result in misinterpreting MCC as a sarcoma and lead to costly reflex testing for the detection of a BCOR genetic alteration. This pitfall might be particularly relevant in cases of MCC or SCLC which only exhibit a patchy cytokeratin expression, potentially obscuring their epithelial nature.

To our knowledge BCOR immunoreactivity in MCC and other neuroendocrine carcinomas has not been previously described. BCOR genetic alterations, in particular BCOR-ITD and BCOR-fusions, have not been reported in neuroendocrine neoplasms in the literature. MCC do not commonly harbor alterations of BCOR. Two whole exome sequencing studies of Merkel cell carcinomas for example did not show BCOR-ITD alterations or break points suggestive for a rearrangement and only single cases of Merkel cell carcinomas with BCOR mutations have been reported [7, 25, 26]. Similarly, two integrative genomic analyses of SCLC that included exome and transcriptome data did not report BCOR genomic alterations [27, 28]. While recurrent BCOR mutations have been observed in gastrointestinal neuroendocrine neoplasms [29, 30], these were mostly loss-of-function mutations [30].

Based on our data, BCOR positivity or negativity in MCC is independent of the MCPyV immunohistochemical status. Further research is needed to understand the mechanism of positive BCOR immunoreactivity. A hypothetical mechanism could be transcriptional up-regulation of BCOR as shown in synovial sarcoma [9]. Transcriptional up-regulation has also be suggested as the mechanism for false positive pan-TRK-immunohistochemistry in tumors without molecular NTRK1-3 rearrangements [31]. An alternative potential mechanism for aberrant positivity could be nonspecific antibody cross-reactivity. Moreover, further systematic research is needed to highlight BCOR expression in other types of neuroendocrine neoplasms in particular and carcinomas in general.

Our study further highlights the importance of cautious interpretation when utilizing BCOR immunohistochemistry, which should only be used as part of a comprehensive panel of immunohistochemical stains, including cytokeratins and neuroendocrine markers. Consistent with existing literature [9], our analysis of 5 cases with molecularly confirmed BCOR alterations revealed moderate to strong diffuse BCOR immunoreactivity in nearly all tumor cells. In contrast, the staining patterns in MCC often exhibited greater heterogeneity and weaker intensity, suggesting the potential absence of a molecular alteration. However, it is worth noting that diffuse positivity can be observed in some MCCs and small cell lung carcinomas (SCLC), challenging the reliability BCOR immunohistochemistry. Furthermore, in the evaluation of small biopsies, the extent of staining heterogeneity may not always be readily apparent. Interestingly, we noted a more consistent and stronger immunoreactivity in SCLC compared to MCC. A potential confounding factor could be the difference in tissue fixation, as predominantly small biopsies were analyzed in the SCLC cohort, while samples from both biopsies and excision specimens were evaluated in the MCC cohort.

Conclusions

In conclusion, MCC is a rare and aggressive primary cutaneous neuroendocrine carcinoma that can pose significant diagnostic challenges. In this retrospective study, we show that neuroendocrine neoplasms such as MCC and SCLC can show strong aberrant positivity for BCOR immunohistochemistry and therefore challenge the interpretation if not used in the context of a comprehensive marker panel.
