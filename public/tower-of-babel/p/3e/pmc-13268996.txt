Expanding the role of insulin resistance to the earliest phases of cognitive decline: Preliminary evidence
Authors: Salvatore Mazzeo, Arianna Frijo, Maria Vittoria Corbari, Elisa Bortolin, Giulia Bruschi, Alessandro Bombaci, Federico Emanuele Pozzi, Alberto Astengo, Francesca Magrì, Antonio Pettinato, Elena Costa, Massimo Filippi, Maria Salsone
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Insulin resistance (IR) is a pathological condition in which target tissues exhibit reduced responsiveness to insulin, leading to systemic metabolic dysfunction. 1 A growing body of evidence identifies brain IR as a key mechanistic link between metabolic dysfunction and neurodegeneration, particularly Alzheimer's disease (AD). 2 Experimental and clinical studies suggest that impaired insulin signaling may promote amyloid accumulation, tau phosphorylation, synaptic dysfunction, and neuroinflammation, thereby contributing to AD pathophysiology. 3 In a recent article published in your Journal, Padovani et al. demonstrated that IR, indexed by the triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index (defined as the natural logarithm of triglycerides × fasting glucose), interacts with Apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4/ε4 homozygosity in modulating blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity in individuals with AD dementia. 4 This finding is biologically supported by evidence that IR disrupts BBB integrity (compromising endothelial function and tight junction stability) 5 and that an increase in BBB permeability may contribute to and even precede cognitive decline. 6

Building on this evidence, we aimed to extend the investigation of the role of IR in AD to the earliest symptomatic stages by analyzing a cohort of 25 consecutive individuals with subjective cognitive decline (SCD, n = 9) or mild cognitive impairment (MCI, n = 16) referred to our center and enrolled in an ongoing longitudinal study (“The Signature of Alzheimer's Disease in Subjective Cognitive Decline ‐ SIGN‐AL” – NCT07402161). Participants were recruited in accordance with the 1994 Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the local review board (CET 25‐2025). Participants in this study provided informed consent to participate. All the participants underwent cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarker assessment, comprehensive neuropsychological evaluation, and brain magnetic resonance imaging. AD biomarker status was defined as A+ if at least one core biomarker (CSF amyloid beta [Aβ] 42/Aβ40 ratio, tau phosphorylated at threonine 181 /Aβ42 ratio, or total tau/Aβ42 ratio) was abnormal, and as A− if all biomarkers were within the normal range. 7

We observed an inverse correlation between TyG index and global cognitive performance, measured by the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) (r = −0.449, p = 0.024). This association remained significant in multivariable models adjusting for age, sex, clinical stage (SCD vs MCI), and AD biomarker status. Both TyG and AD biomarker status emerged as independent predictors of MMSE score (TyG: B = −5.17, 95% confidence interval [CI] [−9.33, −1.01], p = 0.018; AD biomarker status: B = −2.37, 95% CI [−4.25, −0.49], p = 0.016). As illustrated in Figure 1, the regression slope indicates a negative association between TyG and MMSE in both biomarker groups, while the regression line for A+ individuals is consistently shifted downward relative to A− individuals.

These findings suggest that, in the earliest stages of cognitive decline (SCD and MCI), global cognitive performance is influenced both by underlying AD pathology and by systemic IR. Notably, the effect of TyG was independent of clinical stage, indicating that the impact of IR on cognition operates similarly across the earliest phases of decline.

Although limited by the small sample size and the lack of APOE genotype data, this study provides novel preliminary evidence that IR interacts with AD pathology in shaping cognitive performance in SCD and MCI, extending prior observations to the earliest clinical stages of cognitive decline.

If confirmed in larger and longitudinal cohorts, these findings may have important implications. The TyG index represents a simple, inexpensive, and reproducible surrogate marker of systemic IR, relying solely on fasting glucose and triglyceride levels. 8 Compared with other indices requiring insulin measurements, TyG may be more readily implemented in routine clinical practice. Its inclusion alongside demographic, cognitive, and biomarker data could improve risk stratification and patient profiling in memory clinic settings.

Moreover, our results lend support to the growing interest in targeting metabolic dysfunction as a modifiable contributor to cognitive decline. While current evidence remains inconclusive and no such treatments are recommended for AD, emerging data suggest that modulation of metabolic pathways may influence disease progression. 9 Lifestyle interventions aimed at improving insulin sensitivity, as well as pharmacological approaches, may hold promise. Several antidiabetic agents (including metformin, thiazolidinediones, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists, and sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors) have been investigated for potential neuroprotective effects. 10 Our findings raise the possibility that such strategies could be explored not only in patients with established AD pathology, as an adjunct to disease‐modifying therapies, but also in biomarker‐negative individuals, in whom improving insulin sensitivity may directly enhance cognitive function and quality of life.

In conclusion, our results support a model in which subclinical IR contributes to early cognitive decline in synergy with AD pathology. These findings underscore the importance of integrating metabolic and neurodegenerative frameworks in the study of early cognitive decline and highlight IR as a potential target for early intervention strategies.
