Open vs Arthroscopic Tibiotalar Arthrodesis: A Comparative Cohort Study from an Asian Tertiary Center
Authors: Jeremy Tze En Lim, Yen Chin Jeremy Tan, Ramesh Radhakrishnan, Selvaraj Vijayaragavan, Rui Xiang Toh, Kizher Shajahan Mohamed Buhary, Zongxian Li, Kae Sian Tay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Tibiotalar arthrodesis remains the standard surgical treatment for end-stage ankle arthritis. Although arthroscopic techniques are increasingly used, differences in operative time, complication profiles, and union rates between arthroscopic and open techniques remain inconsistently reported. In Asian populations, degenerative ankle arthritis is frequently associated with varus coronal deformity, which may influence surgical approach selection and outcomes. This study compared perioperative outcomes, radiographic union, postoperative complications, and patient-reported outcomes between open and arthroscopic tibiotalar arthrodesis.Methods:This study included 83 adult patients who underwent isolated tibiotalar arthrodesis at a single tertiary institution between January 2004 and December 2024 using either an open (n = 30) or arthroscopic (n = 53) approach. Perioperative outcomes, radiographic time to union, postoperative complications, and 6-month patient-reported outcomes were compared.Results:With the numbers available, no significant difference could be detected in the primary outcome of time to radiographic union between the arthroscopic and open cohorts (120.0 ± 99.5 vs 113.1 ± 73.0 days, respectively; P = .734), and no significant difference could be detected in overall complication rates between groups (18.9% vs 23.3%, respectively; P = .778). Patients undergoing open arthrodesis demonstrated greater preoperative coronal deformity compared with those treated arthroscopically (15.6° ± 10.8 vs 8.2° ± 7.4; P = .002). Arthroscopic arthrodesis was associated with longer operative time (154.7 ± 44.0 vs 130.1 ± 49.5 minutes; P = .028), whereas fewer reoperations were observed during follow-up (3.8% vs 20.0%; P = .024).Conclusion:In this comparative cohort, with the numbers available, no significant differences were detected in radiographic union, early functional outcomes, or overall complication rates between arthroscopic and open tibiotalar arthrodesis. Fewer reoperations were observed in the arthroscopic cohort, which was generally composed of patients with less severe deformity. Both arthroscopic and open tibiotalar arthrodesis remain reasonable treatment options when selected according to patient characteristics, deformity severity, and surgical requirements.Level of Evidence:Level III, retrospective comparative cohort study.

Introduction

Tibiotalar arthritis is an uncommon but highly disabling condition that differs from osteoarthritis affecting the hip and knee. Unlike hip and knee osteoarthritis, ankle arthritis is frequently secondary to prior trauma, deformity, instability, inflammatory arthropathy, avascular necrosis, infection, or congenital conditions. 1 Patients with advanced ankle arthritis often experience substantial pain, stiffness, impaired push-off, and difficulty ambulating on uneven terrain, resulting in functional limitation that may be comparable to that observed in end-stage hip or knee osteoarthritis.1-3

When nonoperative management fails, surgical intervention is often required. Although total ankle replacement has emerged as an alternative treatment option for selected patients, tibiotalar arthrodesis remains a well-established procedure that provides reliable pain relief and restoration of stability.2,3 Traditionally, ankle arthrodesis has been performed using an open approach, which facilitates deformity correction, bone grafting, and management of bone loss or retained hardware.4-6

In appropriately selected patients, minimally invasive arthroscopic tibiotalar arthrodesis has emerged as an alternative technique that preserves the soft tissue envelope while enabling effective joint preparation and fixation. Prior comparative studies have suggested that arthroscopic tibiotalar arthrodesis may be associated with reduced wound complications, shorter hospitalisation, and faster postoperative recovery when performed in appropriately selected patients.7-10 However, comparative studies and systematic reviews have reported heterogeneous findings, and uncertainty remains regarding differences in radiographic union, perioperative efficiency, and complication profiles between arthroscopic and open techniques.4,7

Most comparative studies evaluating arthroscopic vs open ankle arthrodesis have been conducted in Western populations. Varus coronal deformity is a recognised pattern in degenerative ankle arthritis and has been frequently reported in East Asian clinical series.11,12 Because substantial coronal deformity may influence surgical approach selection, particularly when considering arthroscopic techniques with limited ability for deformity correction, comparative outcome data in Asian populations may provide additional insight into the generalisability of existing literature.

Accordingly, the purpose of this study was to compare perioperative outcomes, radiographic union, postoperative complications, and early patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) between open and arthroscopic tibiotalar arthrodesis in a single-center cohort managed with broadly standardised postoperative protocols.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a single tertiary health care institution using prospectively maintained registry data. Ethical approval was obtained from the SingHealth Centralised Institutional Review Board (CIRB), Singapore (reference no. 2023/2551). Adult patients who underwent isolated primary tibiotalar arthrodesis for end-stage ankle pathology between January 2004 and December 2024 were identified from institutional surgical databases.

Inclusion criteria comprised patients aged ≥18 years who underwent primary isolated tibiotalar fusion. Exclusion criteria included concomitant subtalar or midfoot arthrodesis, revision ankle fusion, and insufficient clinical or radiographic follow-up for assessment of fusion or postoperative complications. Eighty-three consecutive cases met inclusion criteria.

PROMs were collected during routine postoperative follow-up. Availability varied according to outcome domain and follow-up time point; analyses were therefore performed using available data with outcome-specific denominators reported where appropriate. Data collection was performed by independent physiotherapists not involved in patient care.

Surgical approach selection was based on surgeon preference and patient-specific factors, although open arthrodesis was generally favoured in cases with substantial deformity or bone loss.

Surgical Procedures and Postoperative Care

All patients underwent isolated tibiotalar arthrodesis via either an arthroscopic (n = 53) or open (n = 30) approach according to surgeon judgement and patient-specific factors. Procedures were performed by fellowship-trained orthopaedic foot and ankle surgeons under general or spinal anaesthesia with the patient positioned supine and a thigh tourniquet applied.

Arthroscopic arthrodesis was performed using standard anteromedial and anterolateral portals established at the ankle joint line. A 4-mm 30° arthroscope was used to debride residual cartilage, osteophytes, and loose bodies. Fusion surfaces were prepared using curettes and a high-speed burr until bleeding cancellous bone was exposed on both the tibial and talar surfaces. The ankle was positioned in neutral dorsiflexion, 0° to 5° of hindfoot valgus, and 0° to 5° of external rotation. Two to 3 tibiotalar guidewires were inserted percutaneously under fluoroscopic guidance, followed by definitive fixation using cannulated compression screws.

Open arthrodesis was performed through either an anterior or lateral transfibular approach depending on deformity pattern and exposure requirements. Joint preparation was performed under direct visualisation, with fixation achieved using compression screw constructs comparable to those used in the arthroscopic cohort. Screw number and configuration were determined intraoperatively based on bone quality and perceived stability, although compression screw fixation was used in all cases.

All patients followed a standardised rehabilitation protocol consisting of 6 weeks of immobilisation and non-weight-bearing, followed by progressive protected weight-bearing in a walking boot. Return to usual activities was permitted following radiographic evidence of fusion. In our institution, postoperative inpatient admission following tibiotalar arthrodesis is standard practice to facilitate pain management, wound monitoring, physiotherapy assessment, and mobilisation training prior to discharge. Routine follow-up occurred at regular postoperative intervals until fusion was achieved or revision surgery was indicated.

Outcome Measures

Perioperative outcomes included operative duration and postoperative length of stay. Operative duration was defined as the time from skin incision to wound closure as recorded in the operative record. Radiographic outcomes included time to union, defined as bridging trabeculae across the tibiotalar joint on standard radiographs with resolution of clinical symptoms. Nonunion was defined as persistent pain with absence of progressive radiographic fusion across serial postoperative imaging, with or without revision surgery. Delayed union was defined as absence of radiographic union by 6 months postoperatively. Time-to-union analysis was performed only in patients with confirmed radiographic union and available postoperative imaging. Fusion status was assessed on serial weight-bearing radiographs during routine follow-up by the treating foot and ankle team.

PROMs assessed at 6 months included the Numeric Pain Rating Scale (NPRS), American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS) Ankle-Hindfoot Score, and 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36). Expectation fulfilment and overall satisfaction were assessed using single-item questions adapted from the North American Spine Society (NASS) low back pain instrument. Expectation fulfilment was defined as an expectation score ≤4 and satisfaction as a satisfaction score ≤3. Outcome-specific denominators were reported where applicable.

Postoperative complications were categorised as wound-related, implant-related, or osseous complications. Reoperation was defined as any unplanned return to the operating theatre related to the index arthrodesis. Baseline radiographic alignment was assessed using preoperative weight-bearing ankle radiographs. Tibiotalar (TT) angle was measured between the tibial plafond and talar dome articular surfaces, with deformity magnitude defined as the absolute deviation from neutral alignment. Hindfoot alignment was categorised as neutral, varus, or valgus.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality and compared using independent samples t tests, with Welch correction applied when appropriate. Ordinal variables, including North American Spine Society expectation and satisfaction scores, were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analysed using Fisher exact or χ2 tests, as appropriate. Continuous data are presented as mean ± SD, and categorical data as frequencies with percentages. Effect sizes are reported as Cohen d and Cohen h, respectively. Radiographic union was designated as the principal outcome of interest. Given the retrospective design and absence of prospective outcome prespecification, all analyses were considered exploratory. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed, and statistically significant findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided P value <.05. Analyses were performed using R statistical software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline Patient Demographics

A total of 83 consecutive patients met inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis (open n = 30; arthroscopic n = 53). Baseline demographic characteristics were comparable between the open and arthroscopic cohorts, with no significant differences in age, sex, or body mass index (Table 1). Preoperative radiographic alignment differed between groups. Patients undergoing open arthrodesis demonstrated significantly greater coronal deformity magnitude compared with those treated arthroscopically (15.6° ± 10.8 vs 8.2° ± 7.4, P = .002). Talar declination angle also differed between groups (Table 1). The distribution of directional tibiotalar alignment (varus vs valgus) was similar between groups (P = .39), with varus alignment predominating in both cohorts. All patients had a minimum of 6 months of clinical and radiographic follow-up for assessment of fusion and complications. Median follow-up duration was 19 months (range, 6-38) in the open cohort and 19 months (range, 6-80) in the arthroscopic cohort.

Perioperative and Radiographic Outcomes

Operative duration was significantly longer in the arthroscopic cohort compared with the open group (154.7 ± 44.0 vs 130.1 ± 49.5 minutes; P = .028). Postoperative length of stay was shorter following arthroscopic tibiotalar arthrodesis (4.0 ± 4.4 vs 6.3 ± 6.0 days), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .074) (Table 2).

Mean time to union was 120.0 ± 99.5 days in the arthroscopic cohort and 113.1 ± 73.0 days in the open cohort (P = .734) (Table 2). Time-to-union analysis was available for 75 patients with confirmed radiographic union and adequate postoperative imaging. Eight patients were excluded, including 7 patients with nonunion and 1 patient with incomplete radiographic follow-up. Mean time to union did not differ significantly between groups.

Among patients with available 6-month radiographs, residual coronal deformity was comparable between groups (6.5° ± 5.1° vs 4.3° ± 3.9°, P = .056). Open arthrodesis demonstrated a trend toward greater absolute deformity correction (7.8° ± 11.0° vs 3.3° ± 5.8°), reflecting the greater baseline deformity present in this cohort. Neutral postoperative alignment (88°-92°) was achieved in 14.8% of open and 28.6% of arthroscopic cases.

Patient-Reported Outcomes

At 6 months postoperatively, pain and functional outcomes were comparable between surgical approaches (Tables 3-5). NPRS pain scores were 2.23 ± 2.68 in the arthroscopic group and 2.52 ± 2.61 in the open group (P = .673). Mean AOFAS ankle-hindfoot scores were 65.5 ± 23.0 and 59.7 ± 21.1 for the arthroscopic and open groups, respectively (P = .32). Expectation scores were lower in the arthroscopic cohort, indicating better expectation fulfilment (2.59 ± 1.38 vs 3.39 ± 1.33; P = .029). When dichotomised, the proportion of patients reporting fulfilled expectations was similar between groups (93.1% vs 88.9%; P = .631). Satisfaction scores were comparable between groups (2.48 ± 1.06 vs 3.06 ± 1.30; P = .163), although a higher proportion of patients in the arthroscopic group reported being satisfied when analysed dichotomously (93.1% vs 72.2%; P = .089).

Health-related quality-of-life outcomes assessed using the SF-36 at 6 months demonstrated largely comparable absolute scores and changes from baseline between surgical approaches (Table 4). The Social Functioning domain was higher in the arthroscopic cohort (66.7 ± 34.2 vs 46.7 ± 38.5; P = .044), whereas no other SF-36 domains showed significant between-group differences. Overall, no consistent between-group differences were observed across PROM domains. PROM availability at 6 months was incomplete. Pain scores were available for 23/30 (76.7%) open and 43/53 (81.1%) arthroscopic cases, function scores for 22/30 (73.3%) and 41/53 (77.4%), and expectation/satisfaction scores for 18/30 (60.0%) and 29/53 (54.7%), respectively.

Postoperative Complications and Reoperation

No significant difference in overall postoperative complication rates could be detected between surgical approaches, occurring in 10/53 patients (18.9%) in the arthroscopic cohort and 7/30 patients (23.3%) in the open cohort (P = .778; Cohen h = −0.11) (Table 6).

Reoperation rates were lower following arthroscopic arthrodesis (3.8% vs 20.0%; P = .024), representing an absolute risk reduction of 16.2%. Among the 6 reoperations in the open cohort, 4 were revision arthrodeses performed for symptomatic nonunion, and 2 were procedures for infection requiring wound debridement with implant removal. Both reoperations in the arthroscopic cohort were revision arthrodeses for symptomatic nonunion. None of the captured reoperations in either cohort represented elective hardware removal following successful fusion.

Rates of nonunion were lower in the arthroscopic cohort (5.7% vs 13.3%; P = .247), as were implant-related infections (1.9% vs 6.7%; P = .295). Wound-related complications were uncommon in both groups, and no cases of deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism were observed.

Discussion

In this cohort of 83 isolated tibiotalar arthrodeses, with the numbers available, no significant difference in the primary outcome of radiographic union was detected between approaches. Arthroscopic fusion was associated with a longer operative time, and fewer reoperations were observed in the arthroscopic cohort compared with the open cohort; postoperative length of stay was also shorter following arthroscopic arthrodesis, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. With the numbers available, no significant differences in overall complication rates or most patient-reported outcomes could be detected between groups, although the open cohort had greater baseline coronal deformity, reflecting likely selection of more complex cases for open surgery.

Our findings are consistent with prior literature reporting increased operative duration with arthroscopic fusion. 4 The longer operative duration observed in the arthroscopic cohort may reflect the technical demands and learning curve associated with arthroscopic joint preparation and fluoroscopic fixation, particularly during adoption of minimally invasive techniques within a tertiary referral practice. Several meta-analyses have also reported shorter length of stay following arthroscopic fusion.4,13-15

Time to union did not differ significantly between approaches. Arthroscopic techniques may theoretically preserve periosteal blood supply and reduce soft tissue disruption, potentially promoting fusion.14-16 Although nonunion was numerically less frequent in the arthroscopic cohort, this difference was not statistically significant.

Six-month postoperative coronal alignment did not differ significantly between groups. Although patients undergoing open arthrodesis had substantially greater preoperative deformity, both surgical approaches achieved acceptable postoperative alignment when selected according to deformity severity and surgical requirements.

Early PROMs at 6 months were comparable between groups, consistent with prior comparative series and longer-term studies demonstrating similar pain and functional outcomes following arthroscopic and open fusion.5–8 Although expectation scores were lower in the arthroscopic cohort, expectation fulfilment rates after dichotomisation were similar between groups. SF-36 outcomes were otherwise comparable between groups apart from higher Social Functioning scores in the arthroscopic cohort, an isolated finding of uncertain clinical significance. PROM findings should be interpreted in the context of incomplete follow-up and relatively short-term assessment. Given the exploratory nature of the analyses and the number of outcomes assessed, isolated statistically significant findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Arthroscopic arthrodesis demonstrated lower reoperation rates and numerically lower rates of nonunion, wound complications, and implant-related infection. Similar trends toward lower wound complication and reoperation rates have been reported previously and may reflect reduced soft tissue disruption associated with arthroscopic techniques. 7,14,17-19 In the present study, reoperation occurred in 3.8% of arthroscopic cases compared with 20.0% of open cases (P = .024). Although this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the retrospective design, small number of events, and baseline differences in deformity severity between cohorts, the magnitude of the observed difference may be clinically relevant. Larger comparative studies are warranted to determine whether arthroscopic arthrodesis confers a genuine reduction in reoperation risk after adjustment for baseline deformity and other potential confounders.

Surgical approach selection reflected real-world practice patterns, with arthroscopic arthrodesis generally reserved for patients with less severe deformity and limited bone loss, whereas open arthrodesis was preferentially utilised in patients requiring deformity correction or greater exposure. Varus coronal deformity is a recognised pattern in degenerative ankle arthritis and may influence surgical strategy, particularly when considering arthroscopic techniques with limited ability for deformity correction.11,12 The preferential use of open arthrodesis in patients with greater deformity in the present cohort is therefore consistent with these anatomical and technical considerations.

These findings support the use of both arthroscopic and open tibiotalar arthrodesis when selected according to patient characteristics, deformity severity, and surgical requirements. The present study also adds to the limited literature describing ankle arthrodesis outcomes in Asian populations, where greater coronal deformity may influence surgical approach selection. Despite greater deformity in the open cohort, clinical outcomes were broadly consistent with previously published international series and contemporary outcome studies of ankle and hindfoot arthrodesis. 20

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces potential selection bias, as arthroscopic fusion was generally performed in patients with less severe deformity, whereas open fusion was more commonly utilised in cases requiring deformity correction or structural reconstruction. Although baseline demographics were similar, preoperative radiographic alignment differed between cohorts and may have influenced outcomes. Although postoperative coronal alignment was assessed at 6 months, longer-term maintenance of alignment and its relationship to clinical outcomes were not evaluated. Residual deformity and deformity correction may represent important determinants of long-term function, patient satisfaction, and adjacent joint loading, and warrant further investigation in future studies.

Second, union was assessed using serial radiographs rather than routine computed tomography, which may underestimate incomplete fusion. Time-to-union analyses were also restricted to patients achieving radiographic union and therefore should be interpreted cautiously. Information regarding elective retained hardware removal performed outside the index episode was not consistently available throughout the 20-year study period. However, all reoperations included in the present analysis were individually reviewed and classified according to indication. These factors may have influenced surgical approach selection and postoperative outcomes. Third, PROM availability was incomplete and may introduce response bias. Additionally, the AOFAS score includes clinician-derived components and has recognised psychometric limitations despite its widespread use in foot and ankle literature. The duration of follow-up beyond the minimum 6-month inclusion requirement was variable and not uniformly available for all patients. As a result, cumulative outcomes such as complications and reoperations may have been influenced by differences in observation time between patients. This limitation should be considered when interpreting between-group event rates. Although reoperation rates were significantly lower in the arthroscopic cohort, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of events and the baseline differences in deformity severity between cohorts. Finally, the relatively small sample size may have limited the ability to detect clinically meaningful differences in other outcomes, including nonunion and overall complication rates. Accordingly, the absence of statistically significant differences should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence between surgical approaches.

Conclusion

In appropriately selected patients, with the numbers available in this retrospective study, no significant differences could be detected in radiographic union, early patient-reported pain and functional outcomes, or overall complication rates between arthroscopic and open tibiotalar arthrodesis. Postoperative length of stay was numerically lower following arthroscopic fusion, while reoperation rates were lower. Both arthroscopic and open tibiotalar arthrodesis remain reasonable treatment options when selected according to patient characteristics, deformity severity, and surgical requirements. These findings are broadly consistent with outcomes reported in previously published international series.
