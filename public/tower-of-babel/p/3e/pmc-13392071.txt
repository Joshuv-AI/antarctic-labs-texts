An LLM-based synthetic data generation approach for addressing class imbalance in malicious traffic detection
Authors: Krzysztof Przystupa, Michał Majka, Andrii Lutsiuk, Orest Lavriv, Mykola Beshley, Ihor Kahalo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid growth of telecommunication network traffic poses increasing challenges for reliable and timely detection of malicious activity. One of the key difficulties in this domain is class imbalance, where malicious events constitute only a minority portion of real-world datasets. Traditional oversampling techniques often struggle to capture the structural diversity of minority-class patterns in such settings. This paper investigates the use of large language models (LLMs) for synthetic data generation as a data-level approach to mitigating class imbalance in network traffic classification tasks. The proposed method leverages the generative capabilities of LLMs to produce diverse and statistically consistent synthetic samples that enhance the representation of minority classes while preserving the overall characteristics of the original data. Experimental results show that incorporating LLM-generated samples during training increases recall for the minority malicious class from approximately 0.78 to 0.84, while maintaining competitive precision. In addition, an analysis based on the coefficient of variation indicates that the proposed method exhibits lower variability of recall and F1-score compared to synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE), and demonstrates stability comparable to adaptive synthetic sampling (ADASYN) and table variational auto-encoder (TVAE) across repeated training runs. These results suggest that LLM-based synthetic data generation improves minority-class detection under constrained augmentation conditions while maintaining stable and reliable model behavior.Keywords: Large language models, Data security, Network monitoring, Proactive monitoring, Communication systems, Machine learning, Neural network, Anomaly detection, Malicious traffic, Synthetic minority over-sampling technique, Adaptive synthetic sampling, Table variational auto-encoderSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The proliferation of interconnected systems and the exponential increase in cyber threats have made malicious traffic detection a critical component of modern cybersecurity. Network-based intrusion detection systems (NIDS) heavily rely on supervised machine learning models to classify traffic flows into benign and malicious categories1–5. However, a fundamental challenge arises from the highly imbalanced nature of real-world datasets, where normal traffic constitutes the vast majority, while malicious events are rare but potentially devastating. This imbalance leads to biased models, reduced detection rates for minority classes, and a higher risk of undetected intrusions6–9.

Existing techniques, such as random oversampling, undersampling, or algorithm-level modifications, partially mitigate the imbalance problem but suffer from inherent drawbacks. Oversampling often introduces redundant patterns, undersampling discards valuable information, and cost-sensitive learning does not always capture the complex contextual dependencies of minority classes10–12. As a result, traditional solutions frequently fail to ensure reliable generalization in dynamic and heterogeneous network environments.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have opened new opportunities for data augmentation beyond natural language processing13. Thanks to their contextual reasoning and generative capabilities, LLMs can synthesize realistic and diverse samples that preserve statistical and semantic consistency with the original data14. In the domain of malicious traffic detection, this ability is particularly valuable, as it allows for the generation of rare attack patterns that are otherwise underrepresented in training datasets.

This paper proposes an LLM-based synthetic data generation framework designed to address class imbalance in malicious traffic detection. The approach focuses on enriching minority classes with representative synthetic samples to improve the discriminative performance of classification models. Unlike traditional resampling methods, the proposed solution enhances the diversity and contextual relevance of rare classes, leading to more robust detection outcomes. Performance evaluation is carried out using precision, recall, and F1-score metrics, which are more appropriate for imbalanced settings than overall accuracy.

Related works

Artificial intelligence for cyber resilience and intrusion detection

Artificial intelligence (AI) methods are a key tool for improving cyber resilience in general-purpose networks and IoT ecosystems, where they help both prevent attacks and reduce the risk of data leaks15. Intrusion detection systems (IDS) occupy a central place among solutions, having become the standard first line of defense in modern digital infrastructures16. Several studies have addressed the problem of detecting malicious network traffic using statistical and machine learning approaches. Hu et al.17 proposed statistical techniques for detecting cyberattacks by analyzing abnormal traffic behavior, identifying patterns of anomalies, and computing threshold-based metrics for classification. However, their approach does not leverage machine learning to model complex feature dependencies and does not address the class imbalance problem, which is crucial for detecting rare but critical attacks. The evolution of IDS from signature-based methods, which rely on known attack patterns, to anomaly-oriented and machine learning approaches is due to the emergence of new threats and traffic variability18. That is why machine learning and deep learning methods are increasingly used to detect deviations from the “norm”19–21. Zavushchak19 explored the impact of AI on cybersecurity and data protection, highlighting AI’s potential for threat detection, automated response, and attack prediction. This work, however, is largely theoretical and lacks practical implementation using real or synthetic data; it also does not consider synthetic data generation to balance underrepresented attack classes. Ndichu et al.20 applied supervised learning methods, including SVM, Random Forest, and KNN, for detecting remote access attacks. While these models demonstrated effective classification performance, the study did not address class imbalance or explore generative methods for augmenting rare attack samples. Li et al.21 used various types of machine learning algorithms, demonstrating an accuracy of over 90% in classifying incoming traffic, which is strong evidence in favor of the feasibility of this approach. But these systems often work with pre-prepared data that has a substantial number of instances of both normal and malicious traffic, which makes the learning process a bit easier and ultimately shows really good results. Khandare and Alvi22 analyzed an improved clustering algorithm on real and synthetic data for anomaly detection without labeled samples. Although effective for unsupervised anomaly identification, their method does not generate additional data to mitigate class imbalance and is not directly applicable to supervised learning scenarios. The predominance of legitimate traffic over rare malicious events leads to a significant imbalance in labeling, which complicates the training of classifiers and causes a systematic “drift” of decisions toward the majority. In such conditions, aggregate metrics such as accuracy become uninformative: the model can achieve high accuracy while almost completely failing to detect rare (but critical) classes. This necessitates a transition to balanced metrics (precision/recall/F1, PR-AUC) and special training procedures.

Historically, two basic approaches have been used: resampling (truncating the overrepresented class) and oversampling (replicating or synthetically augmenting the minority)23. The advantage of the former is simplicity and control over data size; the disadvantage is loss of information. The advantage of the second is increased sensitivity to the minority; the disadvantage is the risk of excessive correlation and “overfitting”24. Modern works increasingly combine both approaches or adapt the sample taking into account the complexity of features, the degree of class overlap, and target metrics25. Mounika et al.26 developed a modified CNN-based intrusion detection system using local outlier factor-based recursive feature elimination, enhancing feature selection and classification accuracy. Despite these improvements, the study does not employ synthetic data generation to address class imbalance, leaving rare but critical attack classes underrepresented.

Synthetic data generation for cybersecurity

Class imbalance is a fundamental challenge in cybersecurity datasets, where malicious traffic typically represents only a small fraction of all network flows. This imbalance significantly degrades the performance of intrusion detection systems, as machine learning models tend to be biased toward the majority (benign) class. To address this issue, synthetic data generation and oversampling techniques are widely used to increase the representation of attack samples while preserving the original dataset structure27. Among classical approaches, SMOTE and ADASYN are the most commonly applied in cybersecurity28–31. SMOTE generates new attack samples by interpolating between existing minority-class traffic flows, while ADASYN adaptively produces more synthetic samples in regions that are harder to classify, thereby focusing learning on difficult attack patterns32. These techniques have been successfully applied to network intrusion detection, malware classification, and traffic anomaly detection.

However, traditional oversampling methods rely entirely on the statistical structure of the available training data. As a result, they tend to reproduce local patterns of existing attacks and may fail to capture the full diversity and evolving nature of real-world cyber threats. This limitation can lead to biased synthetic data and increase the risk of overfitting, particularly in highly imbalanced and high-dimensional tabular datasets. Although oversampling is generally preferred over undersampling in cybersecurity applications, where losing benign traffic samples may degrade the model’s ability to characterize normal behavior33, there remains a need for more expressive synthetic data generation methods. Such methods should be capable of incorporating broader contextual information and generating more diverse and realistic malicious traffic patterns that better reflect the complexity of modern cyberattacks.

Against this backdrop, we explore LLM-driven synthetic data generation as an alternative data-level augmentation strategy for highly imbalanced multiclass cybersecurity datasets. The proposed approach leverages prompt-based generation to enrich minority attack classes while maintaining consistency with the statistical characteristics of the original traffic data. Through systematic comparison with classical oversampling methods, this study assesses whether LLM-generated samples can provide improved representation of rare malicious patterns and support more stable and effective model training.

Data preparation for network traffic classification

Dataset description and honeypot-based data collection

To address the challenge of accurate traffic classification in communication systems, this study adopts an approach grounded in the use of high-quality, well-structured datasets for model training. One of the primary datasets employed is the LUFlow Network Intrusion Detection Dataset34, which was specifically designed and labeled to support the development of models for intrusion detection. Unlike generic traffic datasets, LUFlow was collected using honeypots, i.e., decoy systems intentionally exposed to malicious activities in order to capture realistic attack traces. This strategy enables the collection of highly valuable samples that reflect genuine adversarial behaviors, thereby enhancing the robustness of subsequent analysis and detection tasks.

Honeypots serve as controlled resources within the network that deliberately attract unauthorized probing or attack attempts. By acting as vulnerable endpoints, they provide researchers with unique opportunities to study malicious activities in a safe and isolated environment. For telemetry extraction, each honeypot node was instrumented with the Cisco Joy tool — a BSD-licensed software package built on libpcap, capable of extracting rich statistical and flow-level features from both live traffic streams and packet capture (pcap) files. This allowed for the generation of feature-rich datasets suitable for training advanced machine learning models.

An important characteristic of LUFlow is that it does not limit traffic representation to strictly malicious or benign categories. In addition to clearly labeled attack flows, the dataset also contains outlier traffic, which represents flows that could not be conclusively classified as either malicious or normal. These ambiguous cases are deliberately preserved to stimulate further research, encouraging the exploration of whether such flows represent emerging threats, misclassified benign activities, or novel attack vectors.

Importantly, due to the autonomous labelling mechanism and reliance on correlation with external Cyber Threat Intelligence sources, LUFlow does not consistently provide fine-grained annotations regarding specific attack types. As a result, malicious traffic is primarily labeled at a behavioral level rather than by explicit attack category. This characteristic reflects realistic deployment scenarios, where the precise nature of an attack is often unknown at detection time, and further supports the use of LUFlow for evaluating detection models under conditions of uncertainty and evolving threat landscapes.

Figure 1 illustrates the generalized principle of honeypot integration into a communication system. In a conventional architecture (upper part of the figure), a request originating from the external network is directed to the internal network, and the corresponding response is returned. In the honeypot-enabled architecture (lower part of the figure), suspicious or unauthorized requests are redirected to a decoy system instead of reaching the internal infrastructure. The honeypot generates realistic responses, which are returned to the external entity, while simultaneously capturing detailed telemetry for analysis. This redirection mechanism provides two key advantages: it isolates the internal network from direct exposure to potential attacks, and it enables the systematic collection of malicious traffic traces that can be used for developing and validating intrusion detection models.

Through this mechanism, the LUFlow dataset integrates both benign and adversarial flows, offering a balanced foundation for advanced traffic classification research. Moreover, the incorporation of ambiguous outlier traffic extends the dataset’s utility by simulating real-world uncertainty, making it particularly valuable for building predictive and robust security systems.

Correlation and statistical analysis of features

To gain a deeper understanding of the data it is necessary to move from individual cases to a statistical examination of the relationships between features. For this purpose, pairwise correlation analysis was conducted using the Pearson coefficient, which quantitatively measures the degree of linear dependence between parameters. The correlation matrix obtained from this analysis (Fig. 2) provides insight into the internal structure of the dataset, highlighting which attributes contain unique discriminative information and which are redundant. This knowledge is essential for effective feature selection and for the construction of more accurate and reliable traffic classification models.

The results of the pairwise correlation analysis demonstrate that the label value has a strong association with several parameters, including bytes out, destination port, destination IP, source port, source IP, and total entropy. Nevertheless, the remaining parameters should not be disregarded, since their combined contribution forms an integral description of the system’s state at a given moment in time. In addition to correlation analysis, it is also essential to evaluate the degree of class imbalance, as noted earlier. This requires calculating the distribution of records across the known classes, which provides a quantitative measure of imbalance and forms the basis for selecting appropriate strategies to mitigate its impact on model training.

Although this range is relatively broad, the obtained value of 22.84% places the malicious class closer to the lower bound, which can reasonably be interpreted as an indicator of moderate imbalance.

The label field, as presented in Table 2 of the previous section, is defined in text format, where each class is represented by its corresponding textual name. Such a representation is not directly suitable for computational processing or model training. To overcome this limitation, the one-hot encoding technique was applied, whereby the label field is transformed into a binary vector. Each class is represented as a unique position within the vector, ensuring that the categorical labels can be effectively utilized in machine learning pipelines.

However, the dataset under study exhibits a pronounced class imbalance, particularly with respect to the malicious traffic class. Training on such imbalanced data without compensation may lead to models that disproportionately favor the majority class, thereby neglecting minority classes that are critical from a security perspective. One of the fundamental strategies to mitigate this problem is the class weighting approach. The underlying principle is to assign higher weights to underrepresented classes during training, thereby amplifying their impact on the optimization process. In practice, this means that misclassifications of the rare class (malicious flows) incur a higher penalty in the loss function compared to misclassifications of majority classes.

This strategy has been extensively studied in the literature9 and is widely used as a baseline method for handling class imbalance. Its application leads to three key benefits:

Improved recall and precision for the minority class, thereby enhancing detection of rare but critical events;

Reduction of class neglect, ensuring that the underrepresented class is not systematically ignored by the model;

Neural network architecture

Proceeding to the design of the neural network architecture, a linear feedforward structure with four layers was implemented. The three hidden layers employ the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function, which is widely recognized for its computational efficiency and ability to accelerate convergence during training, particularly when working with large-scale datasets. The use of ReLU also helps to mitigate the vanishing gradient problem, thereby ensuring stable optimization across multiple layers.

To reduce the risk of overfitting and enhance generalization capability, two Dropout layers were incorporated into the architecture. Dropout randomly deactivates a fraction of neurons during training, which prevents co-adaptation of features and improves the robustness of the model when applied to unseen data.

At the output layer, the Softmax activation function was applied. This function transforms the raw outputs into a normalized probability distribution over the target classes, enabling the network to correctly address the multi-class classification problem. By returning class probabilities that sum to one, Softmax allows for interpretable decision-making and facilitates the evaluation of model confidence for each prediction. Figure 3 shows the number of parameters for each layer and the dropout layer.

Number of parameters in neural network layers.

Concretely, the network consists of two fully connected hidden layers with 32 neurons each, followed by a third hidden layer with 16 neurons. Dropout regularization is applied after the second and third hidden layers. The final output layer contains three neurons corresponding to the target classes. All hidden layers use ReLU activation, while the output layer uses a softmax activation function. The total number of trainable parameters in the model is 2,083, as summarized in Fig. 3.

The network was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10⁻³ and the sparse categorical cross-entropy loss. Training was performed for up to 30 epochs with a batch size of 2048. To ensure comparable convergence behavior across all experiments and mitigate overfitting, EarlyStopping was applied based on the validation loss (monitor = val_loss, patience = 10) with restoration of the best weights. In addition, ModelCheckpoint was used to save the best-performing model according to the minimum validation loss.

All experiments were implemented using TensorFlow (version 2.20.0) with the integrated Keras API. The same implementation environment was preserved across all experimental configurations to ensure reproducibility.

LLM-based synthetic data approach for malicious traffic detection

A widely adopted strategy for addressing multiclass imbalance operates at the data level by augmenting the training set with additional samples from underrepresented classes. Classical techniques such as random oversampling, the SMOTE and ADASYN are commonly employed to increase the effective representation of minority classes in the feature space. While these methods have demonstrated practical utility, they primarily rely on local interpolation or density-based heuristics, which limits their ability to model complex, high-dimensional dependencies inherent to heterogeneous network traffic.

In this work, we introduce an alternative data-level augmentation approach based on LLMs for synthetic data generation. Unlike conventional oversampling techniques, LLMs leverage generative learning mechanisms to capture both statistical regularities and latent semantic relationships among features. This enables the generation of synthetic samples that exhibit greater structural diversity and realism, leading to a more faithful representation of complex minority traffic patterns, such as malicious or anomalous flows. Additionally, the scalability of LLM-based generation allows for efficient expansion of training datasets without extensive manual feature engineering. In this context, “latent semantic relationships” refer to structured inter-feature dependencies expressed through the textual representation of tabular data (e.g., CSV sequences augmented with statistical summaries and correlations), which allows the LLM to approximate joint feature distributions via sequence modeling.

The initial stage of generating synthetic samples for a selected data class, in this case malicious traffic, requires a comprehensive analysis of the available real-world data. This step is critical because it provides a reliable baseline for evaluating the quality of the generated records and enables fine-tuning of the synthesis process, including adjustments to the size of the input data, the configuration of the generative model, or the applied constraints. Such a preparatory analysis ensures that the synthetic data not only mimics the statistical properties of the original distribution but also preserves essential structural relationships among the variables.

To establish a reference framework, the study employs Pearson’s correlation coefficient, which quantitatively measures the linear dependence between different parameters in the dataset. This metric is particularly useful for identifying strongly correlated variables that play a crucial role in defining the behavioral patterns of network traffic. A visual representation of these dependencies is provided in the form of a heat map (Fig. 4), where the correlation structure of the malicious traffic class is highlighted. This graphical analysis illustrates the degree of association among features and serves as a benchmark for assessing whether the synthetic samples generated by the model successfully reproduce the intrinsic relationships observed in the original data.

Heat map of Pearson’s correlation coefficient for each pair of variables in the entire dataset.

In addition to the correlation analysis, an initial review of the dataset parameters was conducted using the describe function from the pandas library. This method provides comprehensive descriptive statistics, including measures of central tendency (mean, median), dispersion (standard deviation, minimum and maximum values, interquartile range), as well as indicators that indirectly characterize the overall shape of the data distribution. Such an analysis is essential for identifying anomalies, skewness, or irregularities in the dataset that may significantly affect both the training process and the quality of synthetic data generation.

Table 4 presents an illustrative example of the output of the describe method for the bytes_in parameter. The statistics obtained demonstrate not only the average and variation in the volume of incoming traffic but also reveal the presence of extreme values and sparsity of certain ranges. This insight provides a valuable reference point for evaluating whether the synthetic samples adequately capture the variability and distributional properties of the original dataset.

The descriptive statistics (Table 4) and Pearson’s correlation coefficient serve a dual purpose. First, they define the context for the LLM prompt and provide a baseline for comparison. Second, they act as a quality control mechanism: the recorded minimum and maximum values establish valid boundaries, allowing us to filter out any synthetic samples that fall outside the historical data range. In this study, the experiments were conducted using the GPT4o-mini model (gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18) provided by OpenAI through the OpenAI developer platform. According to the official documentation, this model supports a context window of 128,000 tokens, which enables the inclusion of a substantial volume of sample data together with detailed instructions in a single request. Such a capability is particularly valuable when working with large-scale, heterogeneous datasets, as it allows the model to account for both statistical properties and contextual dependencies during synthetic data generation.

Based on statistics obtained from the OpenAI console, the average number of tokens per request amounted to approximately 66,300 tokens for input and 1,400 tokens for output. According to the official OpenAI pricing at the time of experimentation ($0.15 per million input tokens and $0.60 per million output tokens), this corresponds to an estimated cost of approximately $0.011 per request. All requests were executed using the OpenAI batch API, which provides up to a 50% cost reduction, resulting in an effective cost of approximately $0.0055 per generation request.

The choice of 1,500 records was not arbitrary but determined iteratively. At each stage, the generated results were analyzed and the input adjusted. Too few records led to low variability in the synthetic data, essentially resulting in replicated samples, whereas providing more than 2,000 records often caused the model to lose contextual alignment, producing unrealistic outputs or failing to complete the task.

For efficient interaction with the model, all data transmitted to the LLM were formatted in CSV, and the outputs were also returned in CSV format. Compared to alternative formats such as JSON, CSV significantly reduced the number of input tokens and facilitated a clearer interpretation of the data structure by the model. This approach improved both computational efficiency and the fidelity of the generated synthetic records.

Figure 5 presents the overall workflow of synthetic data generation using an LLM. Since the available context window does not permit the transfer of the entire dataset in a single request, the generation process is organized in an iterative manner. This design ensures that the model can process large-scale data while retaining consistency in the statistical properties and structural dependencies of the records.

Overall workflow of synthetic data generation using an LLM.

The first stage of the process, illustrated in Fig. 6, involves the preparation of general information about the dataset together with a portion of representative records. As described previously, the general information includes Pearson’s correlation coefficients and descriptive statistics such as measures of central tendency, variance, and distributional characteristics. Importantly, this information is calculated not for the entire dataset but specifically for the target class subset, in this case malicious traffic. Such a focus allows the generative model to concentrate on replicating the patterns of minority or security-critical classes, which are often underrepresented in real-world network data.

Details of the generation process.

The sample records included in each request are drawn sequentially and consistently from the target subset, without preliminary shuffling or randomization, in order to preserve the natural temporal and structural order of the data. For this study, the number of records per request was fixed at 1,500, a value determined through iterative testing to ensure sufficient variability while maintaining model stability. This structured preparation step ensures that the LLM has access to both statistical summaries and concrete traffic instances, enabling the generation of synthetic data that aligns closely with the baseline distributions.

The second stage involves the construction of the final request to the LLM. This request consists of two principal components. The first is the system prompt, which defines the global task instructions and remains unchanged throughout all iterations of generation, ensuring consistency and reproducibility of the process. The second is the user prompt, which plays a central role in guiding the generation of synthetic data. Unlike the system prompt, the user prompt is dynamic: it is updated at each iteration as new data samples are introduced. Along with the samples, the user prompt incorporates metadata that conveys the broader task context, key parameter descriptions, and other relevant details. Metadata remains stable across iterations, which helps maintain the continuity of the generative process, while the integration of variable data ensures diversity and adaptability. The synergy of these two elements, stable metadata and evolving samples, improves both the accuracy and the consistency of the generated records.

An additional factor influencing the quality of the generated data is the temperature setting of the LLM. In this study, the temperature was set to 0.5, providing a balance between diversity in synthetic examples and adherence to the semantic and statistical boundaries defined by the input prompts. Lower temperature values (0.0–0.3) typically yield highly predictable and repetitive outputs, whereas higher values (0.7–1.0) introduce greater creativity and variability, sometimes at the expense of alignment with the training data distribution.

The final stage of the pipeline is the formation of the synthetic dataset. At each iteration, the LLM was instructed to generate a predefined number of records, which in this experiment was 50 samples. The outputs of individual iterations were stored in separate files, which were subsequently merged into a consolidated dataset. This dataset was then subjected to cleaning and validation procedures to eliminate inconsistencies, assess quality, and prepare the data for subsequent model training and evaluation.

In the base dataset, given that the size of the input sample for the LLM was fixed at 1,500 records, the total number of processed samples (chunks) amounted to 504. According to the experimental setup, each request was expected to generate 50 synthetic records, which should have resulted in more than 25,000 records in the aggregated synthetic dataset. However, during the generation process, difficulties were encountered with the model’s compliance to the specified instructions. None of the outputs precisely matched the required number of records: in some iterations, only 10 samples were generated, while in others 30 or even 60. As a consequence, the total number of synthetic records obtained after processing the entire dataset was approximately 30% lower than the expected volume. This inconsistency highlights a limitation in the stability of LLM-based generation and indicates the need for further research into mechanisms that ensure stricter adherence to predefined output constraints.

In addition to inconsistencies in the number of generated records, typical hallucination phenomena were observed during generation. In several instances, the model violated schema-level constraints by altering the expected data representation. For example, while the dest_ip field in the original dataset is encoded as a numeric identifier (e.g., 768), the model occasionally produced a textual IPv4 address (e.g., 192.168.0.1), thereby breaking the predefined data type specification. Such deviations required post-generation validation and filtering to ensure structural and statistical consistency of the synthetic dataset.

Since the volume of synthetic data obtained at this stage was insufficient to meet the target of producing 5% of the dataset size as synthetic samples, the dataset was reprocessed. To avoid generating redundant outputs caused by identical inputs, the sample overlap method was applied. Specifically, during the first iteration, records from positions 0 to 1,500 were processed, whereas in the next iteration, records from 500 to 2,000 were selected. This overlapping strategy mitigates the risk of producing near-duplicate synthetic records, as identical input conditions often result in repetitive outputs.

As a result of this iterative approach, a synthetic dataset of approximately 44,000 records was generated. To assess the quality of the produced data, the first step was to analyze differences in Pearson’s correlation coefficients between the original and synthetic datasets. Ideally, these differences should be close to zero, indicating that the correlation structure of the original data is preserved. Figure 7 presents a heat map visualizing these differences. The results demonstrate that deviations in correlation coefficients are minimal for the majority of variables, which suggests that the model adequately captured the contextual dependencies within the data and generally adhered to the instructions provided.

Heat map of Pearson’s correlation coefficients for the generated and baseline datasets.

To quantitatively assess the statistical fidelity of the generated synthetic datasets, an SDV-based quality evaluation was conducted for data produced using SMOTE, ADASYN, TVAE, and the proposed LLM-based generation approach. The comparison, illustrated in Fig. 8, reflects the overall similarity between the synthetic and original data distributions.

Statistical quality of synthetic data generated by SMOTE, ADASYN, and LLM.

As shown in the Fig. 8, SMOTE achieves the highest quality score (0.988), followed by both ADASYN and the LLM-based approach, which obtain identical scores of 0.938. In contrast, TVAE yields a noticeably lower quality score (0.851), indicating weaker statistical alignment with the original data distribution under the considered evaluation criteria.

Notably, despite relying on a fundamentally different generative mechanism, the LLM-based approach demonstrates a level of statistical consistency comparable to that of established interpolation-based methods such as ADASYN. This result suggests that the increased generative flexibility and structural diversity introduced by the LLM do not lead to a degradation in statistical fidelity, while remaining superior to the variational autoencoder–based approach in terms of SDV quality metrics.

Overall, the obtained results indicate that the proposed LLM-based generation approach achieves a level of statistical consistency comparable to classical data-level augmentation techniques, providing a sufficient methodological basis to proceed to the next stage of the study. Accordingly, the following section focuses on evaluating the practical impact of synthetic data on downstream classification performance under controlled experimental conditions.

Results and analysis

LUFlow network intrusion detection dataset

This section presents a comparative experimental analysis of synthetic data generation approaches based on LLM, SMOTE, ADASYN and TVAE in the context of imbalanced multiclass classification. The primary objective of this analysis is to evaluate how different data-level augmentation strategies influence model training stability and classification performance under controlled and unified experimental conditions.

All experiments were implemented in Python (version 3.12) using the following libraries: TensorFlow 2.20.0 (with the integrated Keras API), imbalanced-learn 0.14.1, SDV 1.26.0, scikit-learn 1.8.0, SciPy 1.16.1, and pandas 2.3.3. LLM-based synthetic sample generation was performed via the OpenAI API using the official openai Python library (version 1.99.9).

To ensure a fair comparison, all methods are assessed using identical data preprocessing pipelines, model architectures, training configurations, and evaluation protocols. The analysis focuses on two complementary aspects: the stability of the training process across multiple runs, and the impact of synthetic data on class-level classification metrics, with particular emphasis on recall for the critical minority class. This approach enables an objective assessment of whether LLM-based synthetic data generation provides tangible advantages over classical interpolation-based oversampling methods when applied under realistic augmentation constraints.

To enable a fair and reproducible comparison between the proposed LLM-based synthetic data generation approach and classical oversampling techniques, SMOTE and ADASYN were implemented using the imbalanced-learn library, which provides widely adopted reference implementations for interpolation-based augmentation methods.

To ensure consistency across all experiments, identical preprocessing pipelines, model architectures, and training configurations were applied to all evaluated approaches. A unified feature scaling procedure was employed to map all input variables to a common numerical range, and the same neural network architecture and hyperparameter settings were maintained throughout the experiments in order to isolate the data augmentation strategy as the only varying factor.

For all evaluated augmentation strategies (SMOTE, ADASYN, and the LLM-based approach), the number of synthetic samples added to the training data for the minority class (Class 1) was kept approximately equal (≈ 44,000 samples) to ensure a fair and controlled comparison.

In addition, the training process for all models incorporated two standard callbacks: EarlyStopping, configured to monitor the validation loss with a fixed patience parameter, and ModelCheckpoint, also based on validation loss. These mechanisms were applied consistently across all experiments to prevent overfitting and to ensure that performance comparisons reflect stable and well-converged model states.

The dataset was partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets. The test set accounted for 20% of the original data and remained unchanged across all experiments, while 20% of the remaining training data was reserved for validation. Synthetic samples were incorporated exclusively during model training, whereas both validation and test sets contained only real data, ensuring an unbiased evaluation and preventing information leakage.

No explicit global random seed was enforced across all stages of model initialization and training. However, controlled randomness was partially introduced through fixed random_state (value 42) parameters during dataset splitting and synthetic data shuffling procedures (LLM and TVAE-based augmentation). All experiments were repeated multiple times (10) to explicitly account for stochastic variability arising from random weight initialization and training dynamics. This experimental design enables a stability-oriented assessment of the compared augmentation strategies, rather than relying on single-run performance estimates. Figure 9 presents a stability analysis of the classification results based on the coefficient of variation (CV) computed over multiple independent training runs. Lower CV values indicate reduced variability and, consequently, more stable model behavior.

Comparison of classification stability based on the coefficient of variation of Precision, Recall, and F1-Score.

The results show that the use of synthetic data generally reduces performance variability compared to the baseline model trained on the original dataset. This effect is most pronounced for the Recall metric. The baseline configuration exhibits a Recall coefficient of variation of 4.17%, indicating a noticeable sensitivity to stochastic effects during training. When LLM-generated synthetic data are incorporated, the Recall variability decreases to 3.09%, reflecting a more stable learning process across repeated runs.

In comparison with classical oversampling techniques, the LLM-based approach demonstrates lower Recall variability than SMOTE (CV = 5.24%) and ADASYN (CV = 4.58%), while being comparable to the TVAE-based method (CV = 3.06%). Overall, the LLM-based approach achieves a balanced level of variability, providing improved training stability while preserving sufficient diversity in the augmented dataset.

To further investigate the observed differences in training stability, Figs. 10 and 11 present the mean learning dynamics of Precision and Recall for the minority class across training epochs, averaged over multiple independent runs. The LLM-based augmentation exhibits smoother and more consistent recall trajectories compared to SMOTE and ADASYN, with reduced oscillations throughout training, which is consistent with the lower recall variability observed in the CV analysis. While TVAE shows slightly more stable learning curves, its recall converges to a lower level, indicating that increased stability alone does not necessarily lead to improved minority class sensitivity. Overall, the learning dynamics confirm that the LLM-based approach achieves a balanced trade-off between training stability and effective recall optimization, which explains the superior average performance reported in Fig. 12.

Figure 12 summarizes the average classification performance for the minority class (Class 1) across different data augmentation strategies. In the context of malicious state detection, Recall is considered the primary evaluation metric, as the cost of missed detections is significantly higher than that of false positives.

As shown in Fig. 12, the use of LLM-generated synthetic data leads to a substantial improvement in recall for the minority class, increasing the score from 0.786 for the baseline model to 0.846. This improvement exceeds the gains obtained using classical oversampling techniques, with SMOTE and ADASYN achieving recall values of 0.781 and 0.805, respectively, while TVAE attains a recall of 0.796. These results indicate that LLM-based augmentation more effectively enhances the model’s sensitivity to minority-class instances under constrained augmentation conditions.

Importantly, the improvement in Recall achieved by the LLM-based approach is accompanied by only a minor reduction in Precision. Although the Precision score for the LLM-based model (0.684) is slightly lower than that of the baseline configuration (0.699), it remains comparable to the values obtained with SMOTE (0.700) and is only marginally lower than that of TVAE (0.698) and ADASYN (0.698). This indicates that the observed increase in Recall is not primarily driven by a disproportionate rise in false positive predictions.

As a consequence, the F1-score improves from 0.740 for the baseline model to 0.756 when LLM-generated synthetic data are used, outperforming SMOTE (0.738), ADASYN (0.747), and TVAE (0.743). Overall, these results demonstrate that LLM-based synthetic data generation provides a favorable balance between detection sensitivity and prediction reliability, yielding the strongest overall performance among the evaluated augmentation methods.

The confusion matrix analysis confirms that the improved Recall achieved by the LLM-based approach primarily stems from a reduction in false negatives for the minority (malicious) class, while maintaining a comparable level of false positives relative to SMOTE, ADASYN, and TVAE. This observation indicates that the observed performance gains are driven by more effective identification of malicious instances rather than by overly aggressive decision boundaries.

At the same time, a moderate increase in misclassification of outlier samples as malicious can be observed. This effect is likely associated with a shift in the decision boundary induced by the denser representation of the malicious class in feature space following LLM-based augmentation. Since outliers typically occupy borderline or low-density regions, they become more susceptible to reassignment toward the malicious class under a recall-oriented optimization regime. This behavior reflects a precision–recall trade-off rather than instability of the model.

Discussion

The experimental results demonstrate that LLM-based synthetic data generation can yield robust and statistically consistent improvements in minority class detection performance under constrained augmentation conditions. The observed gains are supported by stability analysis, learning dynamics, confusion matrix inspection, and non-parametric statistical testing. However, despite these promising results, several practical challenges and limitations remain that may affect the stability, predictability, and real-world applicability of LLM-based augmentation strategies.

One key challenge relates to the controllability of the generation process. In particular, the model exhibited limited consistency in adhering to explicit generation constraints. Although the prompt specified a fixed number of records per generation step, the actual output size varied considerably across iterations. Such variability complicates process automation and highlights the need for additional stabilization mechanisms or post-generation validation to ensure predictable and reproducible outcomes.

Another important limitation concerns the effective use of input tokens during synthetic data generation. High-fidelity generation requires not only representative input samples but also auxiliary information describing the statistical and structural properties of the dataset. However, increasing prompt length beyond a certain point does not necessarily improve output quality and may instead introduce instability or noise into the model’s responses. Conversely, overly concise prompts risk omitting critical contextual information. This trade-off suggests that LLM-based data synthesis requires careful prompt design to balance informational completeness, computational efficiency, and generation stability.

The augmentation ratio was initially set to 10% of the minority class size as a conservative configuration to evaluate the impact of LLM-based semantic augmentation under controlled conditions. This choice prioritized data quality and minimized the risk of synthetic dominance or distributional drift. To verify the stability of the observed improvement, an additional experiment with a 20% augmentation ratio was conducted (Figure 25). In this configuration, the effective ratio was increased by adjusting the proportion of real minority samples rather than proportionally increasing the number of generated samples. The results did not demonstrate a visible additional performance gain compared to the 10% configuration. This indicates that the primary effect is already achieved under moderate augmentation levels within the evaluated experimental setting.

Comparison of Precision, Recall, and F1-Score for Minority-class detection using different data augmentation methods. 20% of malicious class.

The issue of hallucinated samples represents an additional risk factor, particularly in security-sensitive applications. The generation of implausible or unrealistic records may distort the training distribution and bias downstream detection models. These observations emphasize the importance of incorporating robust quality control, filtering, and validation procedures prior to integrating synthetic samples into the training pipeline.

Finally, the use of proprietary large language models, such as GPT-4o-mini, raises practical concerns related to security, confidentiality, and deployment constraints. While such models provide strong generative capabilities, reliance on external infrastructure may be unsuitable for environments involving sensitive or classified data.

This limitation motivates future research into fully on-premise LLM-based generative alternatives. In particular, fine-tuned open-source language models deployed locally represent a promising direction, as they may retain the semantic modeling capacity observed in this study while enabling full control over data access and deployment. Another relevant direction is the integration of structured generation constraints into LLM-based pipelines, such as fixed schema outputs or constrained decoding strategies, to improve controllability and consistency of synthetic tabular data.

In addition, combining LLM-based generation with explicit statistical validation and refinement loops may further reduce hallucination risks and improve robustness. Systematic evaluation of such on-premise and constraint-aware LLM-based approaches, relative to the method proposed in this work, constitutes an important direction for future research.

From a practical perspective, the additional computational overhead of LLM-based augmentation is primarily justified in scenarios where accurate detection of rare but critical events is essential and where preservation of complex feature dependencies plays a significant role in classification performance. In such contexts, even moderate improvements in minority-class recall may outweigh the increased computational and implementation complexity. For simpler binary classification tasks or resource-constrained environments, classical oversampling techniques may remain more appropriate due to their lower cost and operational simplicity.

Conclusion

This study investigated the use of large language models for synthetic data generation as a data-level approach to mitigating class imbalance in network traffic classification tasks. The experimental results demonstrate that, under the evaluated conditions, LLM-based generation can enhance the representation of minority classes and lead to measurable improvements in downstream classification performance. In particular, incorporating a limited amount of LLM-generated synthetic data (approximately 10% relative to the minority class size) led to a substantial improvement in Recall for the malicious traffic class, increasing the average value from 0.786 in the baseline configuration to 0.846. This result consistently outperformed classical interpolation-based oversampling techniques, including SMOTE (0.781) and ADASYN (0.805), as well as the generative TVAE-based approach (0.796).

The observed recall gain was achieved at the cost of a moderate reduction in Precision (from 0.699 to 0.684). However, this decrease remained comparable to the precision levels obtained using SMOTE (0.700), ADASYN (0.698), and TVAE (0.698), indicating that the LLM-based augmentation does not introduce a disproportionate increase in false positives relative to other oversampling strategies. In addition to improving average performance, the LLM-based approach demonstrated enhanced training stability. Repeated experimental runs showed a reduction in performance variability, with the coefficient of variation for Recall decreasing from 4.17% in the baseline model to 3.09% when LLM-generated samples were incorporated. This variability was notably lower than that observed for SMOTE (5.24%) and ADASYN (4.58%), and was comparable to TVAE (3.06%).

Overall, these results indicate that LLM-generated synthetic data provides a favorable trade-off between detection performance and training stability, yielding both higher recall and reduced sensitivity to random initialization compared to classical oversampling methods. Unlike TVAE, which primarily improves stability without delivering a clear recall advantage, the proposed LLM-based augmentation achieves both objectives simultaneously. The results on the UNSW-NB15 dataset show that in a binary classification setting, classical oversampling methods, particularly SMOTE and ADASYN, are the most effective for improving malicious traffic detection in terms of recall. TVAE provides only a limited and non-significant improvement, while LLM-based augmentation does not increase recall but achieves higher precision and fewer false positives. Overall, for relatively simple binary tasks, traditional oversampling techniques offer clearer benefits than generative augmentation approaches. While the reported results demonstrate consistent trends across the evaluated datasets, they were obtained under a specific experimental configuration. Therefore, the observed performance gains should be interpreted with caution and not directly generalized to all network environments or security scenarios without further validation. The analysis also identified several limitations that must be addressed before LLM-based synthetic data generation can be reliably deployed in operational settings. These include limited controllability of the generation process, sensitivity to prompt formulation, constraints related to effective input token usage, and the risk of hallucinated samples introducing unrealistic patterns. Furthermore, the reliance on proprietary and externally hosted LLM solutions raises concerns related to security, confidentiality, and deployment feasibility in sensitive domains such as critical infrastructure monitoring. Future research directions include the development of more controllable and lightweight generative models suitable for local deployment, as well as improved strategies for stabilizing the generation process, validating synthetic samples, and ensuring data quality. Progress in these areas may further strengthen the applicability of generative approaches for addressing class imbalance in cybersecurity and telecommunication monitoring, where accurate detection of rare but critical events remains a central challenge.

Data availability

The repository further includes a dataset of synthetic samples generated using the proposed LLM-based method. In addition, a subset of the UNSW-NB15 dataset is provided for validation purposes to assess the generalizability of the proposed approach. To facilitate reproducibility, two Jupyter notebooks are provided, implementing the complete experimental pipeline, including synthetic data generation, model training, evaluation, and comparative analysis: https://zenodo.org/records/19467837.
