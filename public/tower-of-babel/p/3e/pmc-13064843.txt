Regulations for international non-proprietary name prescribing and substitution, relevant for cross-border ePrescribing and eDispensation services in the European Union
Authors: Robert Vander Stichele, Joseph Roumier, Dirk Van Nimwegen, Dipak Kalra, Argiris Gkogkidis, Nicole Vegiotti, Yuri Quintana, Petra Wilson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrescribing by international non-proprietary name (INN) and substitution rules are advocated in the education and practice of rational prescribing, and for cost containment policy. Regulations that restrict or foster INN prescribing and substitution exist in all member states. The aim of this study is to describe the national regulations in European Union (EU) countries, to nation-specific tools for ePrescribing and eDispensation systems based on the standards for Identification of Medicinal Products (IDMP) in the European Health Data Space. A survey was sent to the policy makers from national competent authorities, active in UNICOM, and responsible for writing and monitoring national regulations. Data were collected from 14 EU countries. INN prescribing is mandatory in Greece, Portugal, and Estonia. It is allowed in Germany, Belgium, Norway, Spain, the Netherlands, and Italy (seldom used). Substitution based on precise substance and dose form is mandatory in Finland and Sweden, and allowed in Ireland (with a positive list). In Austria and Croatia, only brand substitution is possible. Rules for a substitution module in the cross-border ePrescribing and eDispensing services can be set up, with three possible levels of equivalent lists: brand substitution, PhPID substitution, and INN substitution. Harmonization of national rules is needed, also for exemptions and the possibility of physician’s veto. This may be facilitated by the implementation of IDMP. The European Commission has the legal basis to foster this harmonization. It will have major implications for research, handling of drug shortages, and clinical care.

Introduction

Prescribing by international non-proprietary name (INN) prescribing and substitution rules are health policy measures, advocated in the education and practice of rational prescribing and dispensing, and for cost containment [1–5].

For the prescribing physician, it implies that the recipe line of the prescription (either on paper or electronic) is expressed by the generic name of the active substance(s) contained in the medicinal product, and by a specification of dose form and strength. The naming of the substances is governed by the nomenclature of the World Health Organization (WHO) INNs [6].

INN implies the aggregation of specified substances (with different modifiers) to a group of substances with the same active moiety. In addition, it also needs standardized aggregations of dose form groups [7–9].

When confronted with an INN prescription, the dispensing pharmacist can make a rational choice between the medicinal products that pertain to the substance(s), dosage, and strength of the prescription. Countries may formulate exemptions from INN prescribing or substitution for particular drug classes or allow the physician to explicitly veto substitution.

National regulations exist in all European countries, but may foster or restrict on INN prescribing and substitution. This variability of approaches may be an impediment for the correct and safe procedures in the Cross-border Services, as established by the Cross Border Care Directive (2011/24/EU). This directive created the legal basis for the dispensation in one European Union (EU) country of a prescription issued in another EU country. Directive 2012/52/EU lays down measures to facilitate the recognition of medical prescriptions issued in another member state (and the countries of the European Extended Area). These two directives set out the rules that apply to prescriptions which are issued specifically for dispensation in another EU member state. Despite the adoption of these two pieces of EU legislation, the use of cross-border ePrescribing is still not high.

New EU-level policy and the legislative proposals for a regulation on the European Health Data Space are seeking to support member state adoption of the building blocks for the implementation of a European Health Data Space. This includes the implementation of five ISO/CEN standards on Identification of Medicinal Products (IDMP) by the European Medicines Agency (EMA), the 27 national competent authorities (NCAs), and the national eHealth systems [10]. The implementation of these standards by EMA and across all member states of the EU has been fostered by a large Horizon 2020 research and innovation project UNICOM (https://unicom-project.eu/, last accessed 1 October 2024). This action program was initiated in 2019. Eleven NCAs for marketing authorization of medicinal products were involved, and 29 other partners.

The aim of this study is to describe and compare the national regulations for prescribing by INN and substitution that exist across the EU countries involved in the Horizon 2020 Research and Innovation Project UNICOM.

In addition, the consequences on the construction of nation-specific tools will be explored to help dispensing pharmacists in EU member states, to find suitable equivalents for medicinal products when presented with an ePrescription through the official eHealth Cross Border Services. In addition, dispensing pharmacists must be able to communicate the choice made during cross-border substitution or INN fulfilment through a feedback eDispensation message for the prescriber in the sending EU country, in a multilingual, standardized, and understandable way.

Finally, the legal basis for the EU to harmonize the national regulations will be explored.

Methods

A survey was sent to the representatives of the NCAs active in UNICOM, with the request to update information from a previous 2015 survey from Ghent University, Belgium [11].

This previous survey was constructed based on a literature search in PubMed and on the world wide web, using the European country names and search terms: ‘Drug Prescriptions/standards’ [MeSH], ‘Drugs, generic’ [MeSH], ‘Drug substitution’ [MeSH], ‘Health policy’ [MeSH], ‘health care system’, ‘prescribing regulation’, ‘medicines regulation’, ‘reference price system’. All relevant information was extracted and used as framework to draft a questionnaire, which was discussed and tested in several expert meetings with general practitioners, pharmacists, drug utilization researchers, and experts in pharmaceutical policies. The survey was sent in September 2013–21 EU countries. Given answers were validated with information available on the websites of national health insurance institutes, medicine agencies, and professional associations. Afterwards, the completed questionnaires were sent to a country representative of competent authorities for a second review. These representatives were contacted by the Head of the WHO Collaborating Centre for Pharmaceutical Pricing and Reimbursement Policies. Reviewed questionnaires were returned to the authors for analysis. In the 2023 round of this survey, the results of 2015 were presented to representatives of the 11 NCAs represented in UNICOM, with the request to update the information where needed. For an additional three countries (the Netherlands, Italy, and Greece), an expert respondent from an eHealth Organization or Drug Information Centre was identified.

Active substances (moieties and modifiers), dose forms, and strength were described according to the IDMP ISO/CEN standards [10], with the terminology of European Directorate for the Quality of Medicines (EDQM) Standard Terms [12].

The definition of the higher levels of abstraction for dose forms was based on an analysis performed within the UNICOM project [8, 9], and in accordance with the EDQM value set of the Characteristics of Intended Site for the Dose forms.

A scoping update of the literature on the subject was also performed, with a review of the definition of generic drugs [13], country policy updates [14–22], and attention to the reaction of patients [20, 23].

Results

Data were requested from 14 EU countries, with responses from all countries. A total of 11 countries responded through official representatives of the NCAs (Germany, Austria, Ireland, Croatia, Belgium, Finland, Sweden, Norway, Estonia, Spain, and Portugal) and three countries through expert respondents, involved in UNICOM (Greece, Italy, and the Netherlands).

In Germany, INN prescribing is permitted, and the pharmacist must dispense the cheaper(est) products when the physician uses this prescription method. For brand prescribing, the pharmacist can substitute a cheaper product, unless the physician issues a veto (aut- idem -regelung). Exempted medicinal products (Substitutionsausschlussliste) cannot be substituted.

In Austria, only brand prescribing (for originator products and generics) is permitted.

In Ireland, medicinal products on a positive list can be substituted by the pharmacist, unless vetoed by the physician. For medicines outside this list, substitution is not possible when the physician has prescribed by brand name. Apart from biological medicines, there is no exemption list.

In Croatia, only brand prescribing is permitted. INN prescriptions from foreign countries are admissible. Generic substitution is only permitted in case of shortages. New substitution rules (and exemption lists) are under development.

In Belgium, INN prescribing is permitted and implemented in IT systems. Substitution to cheaper products is mandatory for antibiotics. There is an exemption list, based mainly on the assessment of the narrowness of the therapeutic window (the relationship between dosage and blood concentration). There is also a list of medicinal products for which INN prescribing is allowed but only at initiation of a therapy, and not recommended during regular therapy (no-switch medicines).

In Finland, generic substitution by pharmacists is mandatory, with an extensive exemption list. INN prescribing is seldom used. New rules (for inhalation products) are under development.

In Estonia, INN prescribing is the default option in the IT prescribing systems. Deviations are possible but must be documented and justified.

In Sweden, generic substitution is mandatory, with an extensive exemption list. INN prescribing is not allowed.

In Norway, INN prescribing is allowed and voluntary, but has been recently incorporated in the prescribing systems, so that its share is increasing. Substitution by pharmacists is mandatory, with an extensive exemption list.

In Spain, INN prescribing is mandatory, including for biological medicines, for acute care, and the first prescription for chronic treatment, corresponding to the initiation of the first treatment. Generic substitution at the community pharmacy level is mandatory. Patients are always dispensed the lowest-priced alternative medicine even if they insist on a specific brand (included in the same reference price group) and would be willing to pay out-of-pocket the price difference. Biosimilar substitution is not allowed at the community pharmacy level.

In Portugal, INN prescribing has been made mandatory in principle, but the share of generics in drug utilization remains limited.

In Greece, INN prescribing is mandatory, but generic penetration remains low.

In Italy, INN prescribing is voluntary, but seldom used. Substitution by pharmacists is mandatory (unless exclusion of substitution is indicated by the prescribing doctor). Biosimilar substitution at the pharmacy level is not allowed.

In the Netherlands, voluntary substitution by the pharmacist and INN prescribing is allowed, on the basis of professional agreements.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to study of the consequences of national rules on substitution and INN prescribing in the context of the implementation of the Cross Border Care Directive (2011/24/EU), the implementation of INN prescribing, and the construction of the European Health Data Space, supporting wider use of ePrescribing and eDispensation within and across EU borders, as well as the reuse of health data for research and policy.

From the results of this study, it is clear that in most European countries, national rules allow for a great extent substitution and INN prescribing, albeit with subtle differences. These subtle differences might hamper and complicate the procedures for cross-border services, with the exchange of ePrescription and eDispensation records between 27 member states.

In addition, the European Health Data Space will require the standardized and precise collection of health data, including medication lists and current prescriptions, whether dispensed within the same member state from where the ePrescription originated or in another one. This also requires harmonized rules for INN prescribing and substitution.

Such harmonization effort will coincide with the general implementation of IDMP standards by EMA and the NCAs. This might facilitate the multilingual exchange of critical identifying characteristics (substance, dose form, unit of measurement), and standardized common identifiers at the level of the pharmaceutical and medicinal product, and provide a solid basis for harmonized rules on INN prescribing, based on a standardized ontology of dose forms.

Strength and limitation of this study

A limitation of the study is that it is not a full in-depth description of these regulations in the overall context of drug policy. The study should be extended to the remaining countries of the 27 EU member states, and to countries outside the EU, notably those in the European Economic Area, as well as those with frequent visits of EU citizens.

The strength of the study was the opportunity to reach out in this survey on policy regulations to the high-level officials of the NCAs, represented in the EU Horizon 2020 UNICOM project.

Implications for clinical care, public policy, and research

The results of this survey indicate that INN prescribing and substitution are possible in most of the EU countries. In some countries, substitution or INN prescribing is even mandatory, although not always embedded in the ePrescription system, and if so, not in an EU harmonized way.

It is possible to build tools to comply with national rules in cross-border services [24], but subtle differences remain, complicating smooth ePrescription and eDispensation.

National medicines agencies and eHealth organizations are responsible for national rules for substitution and INN prescribing, exemptions, and regulation of physician vetoing, and to facilitate the use of IDMP in medicinal product identification. Voluntary efforts to harmonize the regulations on INN prescribing and substitution are possible, as there is already a great deal of collaboration in the implementation of IDMP standards and pilot projects for cross-border services.

Nevertheless, the European Commission and the EMA may also play an active role in completing this harmonization.

Article 168 provides an exception to the rule of subsidiarity in health in paragraph (4), which allows, inter alia, ‘measures setting high standards of quality and safety for medicinal products and devices for medical use’, that are provided on the European market to people who are free to provide and consume services across borders.

The Cross Border Care Directive (2011/24/EU) makes clear that a patient has a right to seek dispensation of a Rx issued in their home member state is a member state he or she is visiting, or if he or she returns from a visited member state with a prescription that prescription should be dispensed at home to ensure continuity of care. This directive provides that implementing legislation shall be adopted to help health professionals verify the authenticity of Rx and allow safe dispensation, including developing a non-exhaustive list of elements to be included on a Rx (Article 11(2)(a)) [25], and may consider the use of INN as an appropriate tool to ensure as well as guidelines on the interoperability of eRx (Article 11(2)(c)).

Safe dispensation means that the dispensation must comply with the prescription even when it is issued in an aggregated way (INN prescribing). It also implies that the elements used for identification and aggregation are standardized and understandable for exchange in multilingual Europe. To achieve this, the implementation of the ISO/CEN IDMP standards and the harmonization of the INN prescribing and substitution rules will be necessary.

Directive 2012/52/EC—an implementing directive adopted pursuant to Article 11(2)(c)—sets out that for Rx that are intended to be dispensed in another member state, INN should be used, unless the medicine prescribed is a biological or the prescriber deems it medically necessary to use a brand name. National exemption lists and stipulations regarding the possibility of physician’s veto against substitution should be harmonized. In the EU regulation, there is an exception to the rule of using INN prescribing for cross-border services. The 2012 directive states that biologicals should be identified by brand name, not INN. This may however be an artefact of its time of adoption, given that in 2012, there were a limited number of biological medicines. Now, over a decade later, this might be revised, as numerous member states have developed a generic policy for biologicals.

In addition, the European Health Data Space regulations regarding ePrescription and eDispensation will become legal requirements in 2026: ‘Following dispensation of medicinal products based on an electronic prescription from another Member State, pharmacies shall report the dispensation to the Member State that issued the prescription, through MyHealth@EU’ (Article 12(6)). It is vital that this eDispensation record accurately describes what was actually delivered by the dispensing pharmacist in response to an INN ePrescription from another country. This is also true for INN ePrescriptions within a country, and for substitution of branded projects, originating from within or outside the country of the dispensing pharmacist.

Within the scope of the proposed new Pharmaceuticals Regulation, which will update the directive 2001/83/EC relating to medicinal products for human use, the use of standards such as IDMP may be implemented, which could make it easier to identify substitutions of such medicines in the case of cross-border prescription in the future.

Hence, the EU has the legal basis and a responsibility to harmonize the national regulations on INN prescribing and substitution, and to help coordinate the implementation of the ISO/CEN IDMP standards in national ePrescribing and eDispensation systems. The Heads of Medicines Agencies and the eHEALTH network can also play an additional, crucial role in this.

The implications for health policy, research, and clinical care can be multiple. While ePrescription can be aggregated at different levels for substance and dose form, the eDispensation record will need to be precise and universally understandable to permit correct drug utilization research and pharmaco-epidemiology research. Hence, if the national systems and cross-border services for ePrescription and eDispensation are well coordinated, based on standards and harmonized rules, it is possible to avoid the detrimental effects of intensified travel and lack of interoperability. Consistent European implementation of IDMP and harmonized INN prescribing and substitution rules can be of help in the internationalization of local expertise in computerized decision support. It will be helpful in collecting and analysing types and root causes of drug shortages, and in managing international solutions. It will facilitate precise medicinal product identification for pharmacovigilance. The vendors of physician and pharmacist systems will be helped to create and update performant prescription and medication modules, with bidirectional information exchange among the two pivotal disciplines of health. Finally, patient drug information can also be organized at different levels of relevance (group level and brand level).

Rules for a substitution module in the cross-border ePrescribing and eDispensing services can be set up, with three possible levels of equivalent lists: brand substitution, PhPID substitution, and INN substitution. Harmonization of national rules is needed, also for exemptions and the possibility of physician’s veto. This may be facilitated by the implementation of IDMP. The European Commission has the legal basis to foster this harmonization. It will have major implications for research, handling of drug shortages, and clinical care.
