Genomic characteristics, antimicrobial resistance profiles and virulence factors of Gallibacterium anatis isolates from layer chickens in Northern China
Authors: Fangfang Guo, Dan Wang, Hao Wu, Xin Zhao, Fuzhou Xu, Yifang Cui, Yuxin Shao, Yunpeng Zhang, Weidong Bu, Tong Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGallibacterium anatis is an emerging pathogen causing substantial economic losses in global poultry production. Despite its growing clinical significance, the genomic basis of pathogenicity and antimicrobial resistance in this species remains poorly understood, particularly in China.ResultsWe sequenced and analyzed five clinical G. anatis isolates from Chinese layer chickens alongside 31 global strains, their genomes ranged from 2.25 to 2.81 Mb with 39.8% average GC content. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that Chinese isolates cluster according to historical breeding stock importation patterns, reflecting international trade influences on pathogen distribution. Contemporary isolates showed extensive multidrug resistance compared to the antimicrobial-sensitive historical strain F149, with resistance profiles correlating directly with documented antibiotic usage in Chinese poultry production. Virulence analysis identified universal conservation of the RTX toxin system (97–100% prevalence) across all strains, contrasting sharply with variable distribution of other factors including fimbriae (30–35%) and secretion systems (50–60%). All strains harbored CRISPR-Cas systems, predominantly types I and III, indicating strain-specific phage defense adaptations.ConclusionsThe RTX toxin system represents a core virulence mechanism and potential universal vaccine target for G. anatis. The rapid evolution from antimicrobial-sensitive to extensively resistant phenotypes demonstrates how global poultry trade accelerates both pathogen spread and resistance development. These findings provide molecular insights for targeted interventions against an increasingly problematic poultry pathogen.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04440-3.

Background

Multiple international analyses demonstrate that global poultry meat consumption has increased by 86% over the past two decades (2001–2021), with developing countries experiencing the most rapid growth rates (2.8% annually vs. 0.4% in developed nations), establishing poultry as the dominant livestock product worldwide, accounting for 43–46% of total meat consumption [1–3]. Poultry diseases, particularly those caused by resident microbiota, severely affect animal welfare and lead to significant, hard-to-track losses in the industry. Septicemia is a major cause of mortality in poultry, notably chickens, resulting in substantial economic losses. Bacterial pathogens including Escherichia coli, Pasteurella multocida, and others are responsible for septicemia [4–6]. Moreover, recent studies have identified Gallibacterium anatis as a key pathogen in septicemia cases [7, 8].

Despite being identified in 1950, Gallibacterium anatis (G. anatis) was not formally classified as a unique genus in the Pasteurellaceae family until 2003 [9]. G. anatis is characterized by an exceptionally wide host range, being found not only in domestic poultry such as chickens, ducks, geese, and pigeons, but also in various wild bird species [10, 11]. Although G. anatis is infrequently isolated from mammals, particularly cattle, and is recognized as an opportunistic pathogen [12, 13], it has been implicated in several human clinical cases, including chronic bronchitis [14], lung abscesses [15], diarrhea [16], and mortality caused by pathogenic bacteria among immunocompromised patients [17].

Although G. anatis can be isolated from apparently healthy birds as a component of the normal microbiota in their upper respiratory and lower reproductive tracts, accumulating evidence suggests that it serves as a significant opportunistic pathogen [18–23]. G. anatis has been linked to a range of pathological conditions, including peritonitis, salpingitis, oophoritis, hepatitis, and respiratory syndrome [10, 13]. It is recognized as a major pathogen contributing to decreased egg production, with reported reductions in yield of 8–10% and mortality rates reaching up to 73% in experimentally immunosuppressed laying hens [24–26]. Furthermore, this bacterium can induce epididymitis in roosters, leading to compromised semen quality [7].

In the context of pathogenicity, G. anatis is equipped with a multitude of factors that facilitate its establishment within host organisms [18], thereby significantly impacting the virulence of various strains. These factors encompass the GtxA toxin, outer membrane vesicles, fimbriae, metalloproteases, biofilms, elongation factor-thermal unstable (EF-Tu) protein, clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR), and integrative and conjugative elements (ICE) [18, 27–32]. Of particular note is the bacterium’s capacity to form biofilms.

In recent years, the treatment of G. anatis infections with antibiotics has been increasingly complicated by the emergence of multidrug-resistant strains [33]. This bacterium has demonstrated resistance to a range of antibiotics, most notably ampicillin, clindamycin, doxycycline, penicillin, sulfamethoxazole, tetracycline, and tylosin [32, 34–39]. Furthermore, tetracycline resistance genes, including tetB and tetC, have been documented in strains originating from Mexico [40], Denmark [41], the United States [35] and Iran [39]. Notwithstanding these observations, the biological characteristics, virulence factors, antimicrobial resistance genotypes, and pathogenic mechanisms of G. anatis remain inadequately characterized.

This study aimed to determine and annotate the complete genome sequences of five clinical G. anatis strains isolated from chicken flocks that succumbed to an outbreak of diarrhea. To enhance our understanding, we performed a comparative genomic analysis of 36 strains, which included 31 publicly available strains obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database, along with the five clinical strains sequenced in this investigation. The analysis concentrated on critical aspects such as genomic features, phylogenetic relationships, CRISPR-Cas systems, potential virulence factors, and antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs). The overarching objective was to elucidate the phylogenetic relationships, biological characteristics, antibiotic resistance genotypes, and potential virulence mechanisms associated with G. anatis.

Materials and methods

Bacterial isolates

Five bacterial strains were obtained from layer hens and breeder chickens that had succumbed to pre-production diarrhea across four farms in suburban Beijing (Table 1). These isolates were collected between 2017 and 2022 by the Veterinary Laboratory of Beijing Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences, which conducted microbiological examinations on caecum and bursal samples from deceased poultry submitted by farmers. All isolates were inoculated on blood agar plates (Hopebio Technology, Qingdao, China) containing 5% sheep blood and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h, followed by confirmation via 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. For positive control purposes in PCR reactions, the reference strain Gallibacterium anatis F149 was procured from the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures at the Leibniz Institute. All bacterial strains were preserved for subsequent analysis in TSB broth (Oxoid, UK) supplemented with 35% glycerol and maintained at − 80 °C.

Library construction and whole-genome sequencing

Following identification and preservation of the bacterial isolates, preparation for whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was initiated to facilitate comprehensive genomic characterization. The isolates were revived by cultivation in TSB broth (Oxoid, UK) at 37 °C for 12 h with constant shaking at 200 rpm. Subsequently, extraction of genomic DNA was performed utilizing the FastPure Bacteria DNA Isolation Mini Kit (Vazyme DC103) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Complete genomic assemblies were generated through the integration of both short reads (150 bp × 2) from the Illumina HiSeq platform and long reads obtained via third-generation single-molecule sequencing technology using a PacBio RSII sequencer. These sequencing procedures were carried out by BIOZERON Technologies (Shanghai, China).

Genome assembly and annotation

Sequence data generated from the PacBio Sequel IIe and Illumina Novaseq 6000 platforms formed the basis for comprehensive bioinformatics analysis. Initially, raw Illumina sequencing reads from the paired-end library underwent quality filtration using fastp v0.23.0 with the following parameters: quality score threshold of Q30, minimum read length of 100 bp after trimming, automatic adapter detection and removal, and exclusion of reads containing more than 5% N bases. The resulting clean short reads, together with HiFi reads from the PacBio platform, were then assembled into complete genomes employing Unicycler v0.4.8 [42]. To minimize the occurrence of minor errors, the assembly was further refined through Pilon v1.22, which utilized short-read alignments for this purpose [43]. Subsequently, coding sequences on chromosomes and plasmids were identified using Glimmer 3.02 and GeneMarkS 4.3, respectively [44, 45]. Annotation of genes and open reading frames was accomplished via Prodigal and Prokka software with default parameters.

Comparative genomic analysis

To investigate phylogenetic relationships among Chinese isolates and those from other countries, we assembled a comprehensive dataset comprising 36 genomes through systematic curation from the NCBI database. Genome selection employed stringent quality criteria including reference genome status, NCBI RefSeq annotation, assembly completeness > 95% (BUSCO), contig N50 > 100 kb, and contamination < 2%, while excluding atypical genomes and metagenome-assembled sequences. This process yielded 31 high-quality complete G. anatis genome sequences available from NCBI (data collected through August 2023), which combined with our five newly sequenced isolates provided a final dataset representing all publicly available sequences suitable for robust comparative genomic analysis.

Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using core gene-based methodology following Treangen et al. (2014) [46]. Single-copy core genes were identified from comparative genomic analysis, aligned using MUSCLE software, and converted to coding sequences (CDS) for phylogenetic tree construction. Maximum likelihood phylogenetic trees were constructed using PhyML with 100 bootstrap replicates. The resulting phylogenetic tree in Newick format was visualized and annotated using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) platform [47]. Core gene sequences used in this analysis are provided in Supplementary Data S1. To gain insights into potential transmission patterns, we assessed pairwise SNP distances between all isolates. Furthermore, circular comparative maps of eight genome sequences were generated through BLAST-based comparisons using the BLAST Ring Image Generator (BRIG) [48]. To characterize the virulence and antimicrobial resistance profiles, we employed ABRicate v1.0.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate), scanning the genomes against the VFDB [49] and CARD [50] databases, respectively.

The pan-genome and core genome analyses followed the methodology described by Medini et al. [51]. Based on gff3 annotation files generated by Prokka, we employed Roary v3.13.0 for pan-genome analysis [52] using bidirectional best hit clustering with 95% sequence identity thresholds. Core genes were determined from gene presence-absence profiles in Roary’s output file gene_presence_absence.csv. Random sampling procedures with ten independent iterations per sample size calculated theoretical core genome convergence at 100%, 99%, and 95% presence thresholds. Genes were classified by frequency distribution: core genes (>99% strains), soft core genes (95–99% strains), shell genes (15–95% strains), and cloud genes (< 15% strains). These analyses involved sequential addition of genomes in a randomized order without replacement at each fixed number of genomes. This approach enabled us to calculate both the sizes and numbers of pan-genome and core genomes as functions of the number of genomes progressively included.

Additional genomic characterization was performed as previously described [53]. In brief, genome alignments were conducted using progressive Mauve v2.3.1 and LASTZ v1.02.00 within the Geneious Prime software v2021.1 (https://www.geneious.com). We also utilized the online platform CRISPRone to identify and characterize clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat arrays and CRISPR-associated genes (CRISPR/Cas) systems present in our isolates.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed by the disk diffusion method conducted on TSA agar plates (Oxoid, UK) in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute [CLSI] (2020) guidelines. The assessment was performed on five newly isolates and F149 strains. A panel of 29 antibiotics was employed, including β-lactams (ampicillin, cefazolin, cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, cefoperazone, cefuroxime, cefaclor, cefoperazone/sulbactam, cefixime, cephalexin, cefepime, imipenem), aminoglycosides (gentamicin, streptomycin), macrolides (erythromycin, azithromycin), tetracyclines (tetracycline), quinolones (norfloxacin, ciprofloxacin, nalidixic acid, ofloxacin, levofloxacin), amphenicols (florfenicol), lincosamides (lincomycin, clindamycin), polypeptides (vancomycin), polyenes (polymyxin B), sulfonamides (trimethoprim), and others (rifampicin). For validation purposes, Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 served as the control strain throughout all susceptibility evaluations.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted with a minimum of three biological replicates. Data analysis employed SPSS version 16.0 and GraphPad Prism version 7 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, United States), with results presented as mean ± SE or geometric mean. Differences between groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Results with p < 0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

Results

Genomic characteristics of G. anatis strain

This comparative genomic study analyzed 36 Gallibacterium anatis strains, including five isolates sequenced in this study and 31 publicly available strains retrieved from the NCBI database. These strains were isolated from diverse geographical locations worldwide. The strains predominantly originated from poultry samples, with others isolated from various avian species. The genomic features, including genome size, G + C content, coding sequences (CDSs), tRNA, and isolation sources, are summarized in Table S1. The genome sizes of the analyzed G. anatis strains exhibited notable variation, ranging from 2.26 to 2.81 Mb, with an average genome size of approximately 2.52 Mb. The number of CDSs varied between 2,215 and 2,732, whilst the G + C content ranged from 39.42% to 40.2%, with an average of 39.81%. The tRNA gene count remained relatively consistent across all strains, ranging from 42 to 76.

The comparative genomic analysis utilizing F149 as the reference genome (2.40863 Mb) reveals remarkable sequence conservation amongst Gallibacterium anatis strains isolated from diverse geographical origins. The concentric rings of the circular diagram illustrate that strains from Mexico (ESV200), Germany (IMT49310), the United States (UMN179), and the five newly sequenced strains (BJ1F2, BJM2, BJF12, YJ622 and DFSO2) demonstrate high levels of sequence similarity and structural homology throughout the majority of their genomes (Fig. 1). This pronounced conservation, particularly evident in regions exhibiting 100% identity (depicted in deeper colors), suggests significant genomic stability across G. anatis populations despite geographical separation, indicating potential global dissemination of highly conserved genetic lineages. Further examination of the genomic structure reveals localized variations, characterized by regions of reduced sequence identity (70% or 50%, shown in lighter colors), which frequently coincide with antimicrobial resistance determinants or mobile genetic elements. The distribution patterns of GC content demonstrate relative stability across the genome, with notable fluctuations adjacent to resistance genes. Most strikingly, the antibiotic resistance genes annotated around the perimeter—conferring resistance to fosfomycin, fluoroquinolones, vancomycin, and daptomycin—exhibit substantial conservation across geographically disparate strains. This consistent presence of resistance mechanisms, despite different environmental pressures, strongly suggests horizontal gene transfer has facilitated widespread antimicrobial resistance dissemination and contributed to genome homogenization in G. anatis across continental boundaries.

Phylogenetic construction of G. anatis

Pan-genomic analysis was performed to characterize the genomic diversity and gene distribution patterns within G. anatis, assess the degree of genomic stability across strains, and evaluate the genetic relationships among our newly sequenced isolates. This approach was essential to understand the evolutionary dynamics and adaptive potential of G. anatis populations, as traditional single-reference comparisons cannot capture the extensive presence-absence variation that drives phenotypic diversity in bacterial species.

Sequential genome addition analysis confirmed the open pangenome architecture of G. anatis, with core genome size declining rapidly before stabilizing at 179 genes, while the pangenome continued to expand linearly without saturation (Supplementary Fig. S2). This pattern demonstrates ongoing gene acquisition through horizontal transfer, consistent with the extensive accessory genome observed across the 36 analyzed strains. Pan-genomic statistics revealed significant variation in functional gene conservation among different strains. Through statistical analysis, it was found that the number of core genes (contained in > 99% of the strains) in the genome of 36 strains was 179, the number of soft core genes (contained in ≥ 95 to < 99% of the strains) was 1289, the number of shell genes (contained in ≥ 15 to < 95% of the strains) was 1321, and the number of cloud genes was 2307 (Fig. 2A). Venn analysis results further show that the number of specific genes of each strain is widely distributed, ranging from 6 to 160 (Fig. 2B). This intimated that the genome of G. anatis is relatively unstable, and there may be great phenotypic differences among different strains. Taking strain F149 as a reference, the pan-genome analysis of five strains sequenced in this study showed that there were 1640 genes shared by all strains, and the number of specific genes of each strain ranged from 8 to 225 (Fig. 2C). This phylogenetic distribution strongly correlates with documented international breeding stock movement patterns, particularly reflecting China’s shift from primarily US-sourced imports before 2014 to more diversified European sources thereafter, demonstrating how global poultry trade has influenced G. anatis population structure and antimicrobial resistanceevolution worldwide. Virulence genes

Although G. anatis is commonly found as a commensal bacterium in healthy birds, it remains an opportunistic pathogen with significant pathogenic potential. Previous studies have identified numerous virulence factors in G. anatis isolates from various sources, which play crucial roles in cell colonization, immune system escape, host recognition, and other processes of pathogenesis. In this study, genomic analysis of 36 G. anatis strains revealed that virulence-associated genes could be classified into 14 functional categories (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Data S2). Our findings demonstrated that the RTX toxin system components demonstrate the highest conservation, with gtxA present in 97–100% of isolates and its secretion components (gtxBD) in 90–95%, underscoring their fundamental importance in pathogenicity. Iron acquisition systems (present in 85–90% of strains) and stress adaptation mechanisms (found in 80–85% of strains) are similarly widespread, highlighting their crucial role in bacterial survival during infection. Virulence determinants distributed moderately include immune evasion factors (60–75%), secretion systems (50–60%), and non-RTX toxin genes (45–55%), exhibiting considerable strain-to-strain variation. In contrast, several virulence categories show notably limited distribution, including fimbrial genes (30–35%), adherence/invasion factors (25–30%), antiphagocytosis genes (20–25%), efflux pumps (30–35%), and quorum sensing systems (15–20%), many of which appear clustered in phylogenetically related strains. This differential distribution highlights the remarkable diversity in virulence strategies employed by G. anatis, potentially explaining variations in pathogenicity observed across different isolates and geographic regions. Resistance gene analysis and antibiotic susceptibility test

Analysis of antimicrobial susceptibility testing results (Table 2) in conjunction with resistance gene profiles (Fig. 5) reveals significant correlations between phenotypic resistance and genetic determinants in the five G. anatis isolates and reference strain F149. The heatmap comprehensively illustrates the distribution of approximately 23 distinct antibiotic class resistance genes operating through 7 major resistance mechanisms, including enzymatic inactivation (predominant in beta-lactams), target modification (prevalent in macrolides and fluoroquinolones), efflux pump systems, and membrane permeability alterations. The distribution of these resistance determinants across the strains strongly explains their observed resistance patterns, with aminoglycoside resistance genes (aac, aadA, aph) appearing most prevalent, closely matching the high phenotypic resistance to this class. Notable genotype-phenotype associations were observed with beta-lactam resistance genes (blaOXA, blaTEM, blaSHV) corresponding to ampicillin and amoxicillin resistance, tetracycline resistance genes (tetA-M) aligning with observed tetracycline resistance, and qnr and oqx genes correlating with fluoroquinolone resistance. This integrated analysis provides molecular validation for the phenotypic resistance patterns and offers valuable insights into the mechanistic basis of antimicrobial resistance in these isolates, with most strains exhibiting multi-drug resistance determinants against several antibiotic classes simultaneously through multiple pathways.

The similar resistance patterns observed in YJ922, BJM2, BJ1F2, and BJF12, especially their shared resistance to tetracyclines and quinolones (Table 2), is reflected in their comparable resistance gene profiles (Fig. 5). All these strains possess tetracycline resistance genes (tetB, tetR) and fluoroquinolone resistance determinants (gyrA mutations, parC), explaining their uniform resistance. The universal resistance to lincosamides and vancomycin across all tested strains is likely mediated by consistently present core resistance mechanisms, including lmrA, lmrB, and vanW.

Notably, strain F149’s exceptional susceptibility to 26 of 29 antibiotics tested (Table 2) represents a significant finding. As the earliest reported isolate of G. anatis, F149’s susceptibility profile reflects the antimicrobial sensitivity of the species prior to decades of selective pressure from antibiotic use in poultry production. F149 appears to lack many of the resistance genes present in more recent isolates (Fig. 5), particularly functional tetracycline resistance determinants (tetB, tetR) and quinolone resistance genes (gyrA mutations), demonstrating how G. anatis has evolved through horizontal gene acquisition to develop multidrug resistance over time.

The pleuromutilin, sulfonamide, and phenicol resistance genes (salA, sul1, sul2, catA, floR) present in several strains align with their observed resistance to corresponding antibiotics. Additionally, the universal presence of lipid A biosynthesis genes (lpxA, lpxC) is consistent with the sensitivity to polymyxin B observed in all tested strains.

CRISPR-Cas systems

A comprehensive analysis of CRISPR-Cas systems across 36 genomes of G. anatis has demonstrated a conserved presence of these systems, albeit with significant diversity in system types (Fig. 6). Every strain examined contained at least one kind of CRISPR-Cas system, with types I and III being the most prevalent across the species. The distribution pattern indicated that 14 strains contained a single system type, 20 strains exhibited two concurrent types, and only strains F-279 and 18102-2 possessed three different system types simultaneously. Notably, strain 1637, which has been previously identified as phylogenetically distinct based on its virulence and antimicrobial resistance profiles, uniquely contained a type IV CRISPR-Cas system, further substantiating its genetic divergence from other G. anatis isolates. Among the five strains characterized in this study (BJ1F2, BJF12, BJM2, DFSO2, and YJ922), strains BJ1F2 and YJ922 exclusively harbored type I systems, with 17 and 18 CRISPR array repeat units, respectively. The remaining three strains (DFSO2, BJM2, and BJF12) possessed both type I and type III systems, with CRISPR arrays containing between 6 and 31 repeat units (Figure S1).

Geographical clustering was noted among specific CRISPR-Cas variants, with type II systems identified solely in three Danish isolates (F-279, IPDH697-78, and 18102-2). The widespread conservation of multi-protein complex CRISPR-Cas systems (types I and III) across the majority of strains indicates their critical role in phage defense for G. anatis. In contrast, the strain-specific differences in array size and system types likely represent adaptations to unique environmental pressures and historical phage exposure.

Discussion

Gallibacterium anatis is likely an under-recognized or overlooked pathogen contributing to infections in Chinese poultry, despite its potential significance in the industry. The initial isolation and identification of Gallibacterium from layer chickens with reproductive tract issues in China was documented in 2010 [54]. In the current study, five strains of G. anatis were isolated from the caecum and bursal of chickens that had succumbed to chronic diarrhea resistant to antibiotic treatment. Whole genome sequencing was conducted, followed by comparative genomic analyses with strains isolated globally, to elucidate the phylogenetic relationships, antimicrobial resistance profiles, and potential vaccine candidates for these highly pathogenic G. anatis isolates. This research offers valuable insights into the molecular characteristics of G. anatis in Chinese poultry and enhances our understanding of its pathogenicity and potential control strategies.

The antimicrobial susceptibility testing of five G. anatis isolates from China indicates significant resistance to multiple antimicrobial classes, with one notable exception. Strain F149, one of the earliest isolates [9], exhibits susceptibility to most of the antibiotics tested. This pronounced contrast suggests that antimicrobial resistance in G. anatis has progressively evolved due to selective pressure from the prolonged use of antibiotics in poultry production. The transition in resistance profiles from the sensitive F149 isolate to the highly resistant recent strains provides compelling evidence for the evolutionary adaptation of these bacteria to antimicrobial challenges within the farming environment.

The observed resistance profiles directly correlate with documented antimicrobial usage in Chinese poultry production. Studies conducted in Shandong Province between 2018 and 2020 identified 24 types of antibiotic residues in poultry eggs, prominently including fluoroquinolones, sulfonamides, and macrolides [55]. Supporting evidence from Kunming City revealed that 18.13% of 513 poultry egg samples contained fluoroquinolone and sulfonamide residues, with concentrations reaching up to 2.48 µg/g [56]. These findings correspond precisely with the observed resistance patterns, notably the high levels of fluoroquinolone resistance (100% to enrofloxacin and 70.6% to ciprofloxacin) identified in our isolates.

The resistance profiles of the five isolated strains studied here show significant similarities with global trends, while also exhibiting some regional characteristics. Their high level of multi-drug resistance is consistent with previously reports from the United States, Austria, Egypt, Germany, and Poland [10, 36, 38, 57, 58]. Specifically, the fluoroquinolone resistance patterns in Chinese isolates closely resemble those observed in Polish isolates (91.2%) but contrast sharply with those from the USA (100% susceptible) and Austria (58.2% resistant) [38, 58, 59]. Additionally, resistance to macrolides and tetracyclines reflects high global rates (85.3–100% for tylosin and 88.2–100% for tetracycline in the USA and Austria) [59]. This discrepancy may be attributed to geographical variation or the limited sample size in our analysis.

The evolutionary progression from sensitive strains, such as F149, to extensively resistant isolates, alongside the significant overlap between Chinese and global resistance patterns, indicates the presence of common selective pressures worldwide. These pressures are likely driven by similar antimicrobial usage practices in poultry production across various regions. The phylogenetic distribution of Gallibacterium anatis isolates reveals a clear correlation with China’s historical patterns of breeding stock importation. Before 2014, over 90% of China’s white-feather broiler grandparent stock originated from the United States [60, 61], which is reflected in the close genetic relationship between early Chinese isolates and American strains. This relationship is particularly evident in the clustering of TS0001 (2014) with American isolates F0003406 and UMN179. Following the avian influenza outbreak in 2014 [62], China diversified its sources of breeding stock to include European countries such as the United Kingdom, France, Spain, Poland, and Denmark. Interestingly, among the limited Chinese isolates examined in this study, the more recent strains (2017–2023) appear to show closer genetic relationships with European strains, particularly those from Denmark and Germany, compared to earlier isolates. While this observation is consistent with the documented diversification of breeding stock sources following 2014, larger-scale genomic surveillance would be necessary to confirm whether this represents a broader temporal trend in Chinese G. anatis populations [63].

Furthermore, the temporal evolution of antimicrobial resistance profiles in Chinese isolates parallels the dramatic increase in breeding stock imports, from 522,900 sets in 2005 to a sustained annual average of over 1 million sets in recent years [64]. This expanded international trade has facilitated not only the transmission of diverse G. anatis strains but also the dissemination of antimicrobial resistance determinants, as evidenced by the stark contrast between the limited resistance in historical isolates like F149 (Denmark) and the extensive multi-drug resistance in contemporary Chinese strains.

In recent years, the virulence mechanisms of Gallibacterium anatis have been increasingly elucidated. Initial investigations identified a limited set of virulence factors, such as the F17-like fimbrial adhesin encoded by the flfA gene [18] and the DNA gyrase subunit B encoded by the gyrB gene [32]. However, our genomic analysis has unveiled a comprehensive virulence profile that spans multiple functional categories. These categories include genes related to adhesion, exotoxins, immune evasion factors, iron acquisition systems, efflux pumps, secretion systems, and stress adaptation mechanisms, all of which may contribute to the pathogenesis of G. anatis.

The RTX (Repeats in Toxin) toxin system is of particular significance, comprising the GtxA cytotoxin [29, 64], the GtxB and GtxD secretion components, and the TolC outer membrane protein, together constituting a complete type I secretion system [65]. Heatmap analysis reveals remarkable conservation of the entire RTX system across all examined isolates, with the gtxA, gtxB, and gtxD genes exhibiting nearly 100% prevalence. This high degree of conservation is correlated with the β-hemolytic phenotype characteristic of G. anatis isolates. The GtxA toxin shares structural and functional similarities with the Apx toxins of Actinobacillus pleuropneumoniae, characterized by glycine-rich nonapeptide repeats that bind calcium ions, which are essential for proper folding and functional activity. The mechanism of action of GtxA involves binding to target cell membranes, forming transmembrane pores, disrupting the host cell cytoskeleton, and interfering with signal transduction pathways, ultimately resulting in the cytolysis of target cells [65].

The ubiquitous presence of the complete RTX system contrasts significantly with the limited distribution of the flfA gene (22.7%), suggesting differential importance in pathogenesis. This disparity indicates that while adhesion mechanisms may vary among strains, the cytotoxic capability conferred by the RTX system represents a core virulence trait across the species. The high conservation of the RTX components positions them as promising vaccine candidates, although their complex immunomodulatory properties present challenges distinct from those encountered with Apx-based vaccines against porcine pleuropneumonia [66–68].

Our analysis revealed widespread antibiotic resistance determinants across G. anatis strains, complemented by diverse CRISPR-Cas systems that likely evolved in response to distinct bacteriophage populations. This adaptive immune diversity enables strain-specific protection against horizontal gene transfer while allowing beneficial genetic acquisition. The bacterium’s pathogenic strategy involves a coordinated sequence of adhesion (via strain-specific adhesins), tissue damage (through the highly conserved RTX toxin system), nutrient acquisition, immune evasion, and antimicrobial resistance (via efflux pumps). This multifaceted approach explains G. anatis’ remarkable persistence in avian hosts despite therapeutic interventions, suggesting that alternative control strategies targeting the conserved RTX system may prove more effective.

In conclusion, this study offers essential insights into Gallibacterium anatis as an increasingly significant pathogen in Chinese poultry production. Our extensive genomic analysis reveals that the phylogenetic distribution of isolates closely aligns with historical patterns of breeding stock importation, highlighting the influence of global trade on pathogen spread. Importantly, we observed significant evolution in antimicrobial resistance profiles due to selective pressures common in contemporary farming environments. The identification of the universally conserved RTX toxin system, in contrast to the variable distribution of other virulence factors, suggests a promising target for future intervention strategies.
