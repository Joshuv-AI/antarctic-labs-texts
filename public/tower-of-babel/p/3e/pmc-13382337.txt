Estimating soil organic carbon density in mangroves of different stand ages using multi-source remote sensing and machine learning
Authors: Zongzhu Chen, Tiezhu Shi, Yun Zeng, Zhensheng Wang, Xiaoyan Pan, Tingtian Wu, Xiaohua Chen, Yiqing Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMangrove ecosystems are highly efficient natural carbon sinks, yet quantifying soil organic carbon density (SOCD) across stand ages is critical for evaluating restoration benefits. This study integrated WorldView-2, LiDAR, and Sentinel-1 SAR data with machine learning to map SOCD in Qinglan Harbor, China. eXtreme Gradient Boosting achieved the highest accuracy (R2 = 0.72, RMSE = 2.87 kg m−2) among four regression methods. Lasso regression identified LiDAR-derived structural metrics and optical vegetation indices as key predictors, highlighting the importance of 3D canopy structure and spectral data. Results showed age-dependent SOCD accumulation: mangroves older than 15 years stored significantly more carbon (11.47 kg m−2) than 0–5 year stands (10.49 kg m−2). These findings underscore the value of long-term restoration for blue carbon sequestration and provide a scalable monitoring framework that integrates multi-sensor remote sensing and machine learning to support coastal management and climate mitigation.Subject areas: Soil science, Ecology, Aquatic science, Oceanography, Global carbon cycle, Remote sensing, Machine learning

Introduction

Mangrove forests are vital coastal wetland ecosystems in tropical and subtropical regions, providing ecological services such as shoreline stabilization, water quality regulation, and biodiversity conservation.1,2,3 Despite occupying less than 5% of global terrestrial area, mangroves are disproportionately important carbon sinks, storing carbon in both aboveground biomass and subsurface soil.4,5,6 Mangrove soils alone store approximately 10.2 Pg of carbon globally,7 with sequestration rates up to five times than those of tropical upland forests.8 These soils constitute the largest terrestrial carbon pool,7,9 critically influencing soil structure, hydrology, nutrient cycling, and ion exchange—processes essential to sustainable coastal management and climate mitigation.10,11 As soil organic carbon density (SOCD) serves as a key metric for evaluating carbon storage potential,12 understanding its spatiotemporal dynamics and controls is essential for optimizing the climate regulation capacity of mangrove ecosystems.

Historically, SOCD assessment has relied on labor-intensive field sampling and laboratory analysis,13,14 methods often impractical at large scales. Remote sensing has transformed this paradigm by enabling spatially continuous monitoring across extensive and inaccessible areas.15 While optical satellite imagery (e.g., Sentinel-2, Landsat-8) has been widely utilized for soil organic carbon (SOC) monitoring, its effectiveness is significantly constrained in tropical regions due to inherent limitations in cloud and precipitation penetration, resulting in substantial data gaps. These limitations have driven adoption of active remote sensing, particularly Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) and light detection and ranging (LiDAR). SAR platforms (e.g., Sentinel-1, Advanced Land Observing Satellite [ALOS]) operate in all weather and day-night conditions, with vegetation-penetrating signals that capture subsurface soil information. Similarly, LiDAR systems deliver highly precise three-dimensional (3D) data through laser pulse measurements, enabling detailed characterization of critical forest structural parameters such as canopy height, density, and leaf area distribution.16 Unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV)-based LiDAR platforms further enhance monitoring by combining high resolution with operational flexibility and cost-effectiveness,17 offering a practical alternative to satellite and aerial surveys.

The synergistic integration of optical imagery, SAR, and LiDAR data has emerged as a powerful approach for developing comprehensive SOC-mapping methodologies.18,19,20 This multi-sensor framework capitalizes on the complementary strengths of each technology, high-resolution optical sensors, such as WorldView-2 (WV-2) and Sentinel-2, provide critical spectral information through vegetation indices that correlate strongly with SOC by capturing vegetation health and organic matter inputs.21,22 SAR systems (particularly, Sentinel-1) overcome the cloud-cover limitations of optical sensors while capturing soil moisture and surface roughness data, key factors in SOC accumulation.23 LiDAR provides high-resolution 3D structural data, including canopy height and terrain elevation, the metrics help model microtopography and hydrological conditions that affect SOC distribution.24,25 LiDAR-derived canopy metrics also indicate biomass input, indirectly reflecting SOCD variations. By combining these datasets, machine learning (ML) models can account for spectral, structural, and moisture-related factors, leading to more accurate SOCD predictions than single-source approaches.23,26 Despite growing recognition of mangroves as vital carbon sinks, a critical gap remains in estimating SOCD from integrated high-resolution optical (WV-2), SAR (Sentinel-1), and LiDAR data, particularly across mangrove stands of varying ages.

SOC prediction methodologies generally fall into two categories: conventional statistical approaches and ML techniques.27,28 Traditional statistical methods, including multiple linear regression and regression kriging, offer computational efficiency and interpretability.29 However, their assumptions of linearity and predictor independence limit effectiveness,30 often failing to capture nonlinear interactions between remote sensing variables and SOC.31 In contrast, ML algorithms such as support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT) model these nonlinear dynamics more effectively.31,32 Deep learning has also been applied to SOCD prediction. For instance, Liu et al.33 utilized a deep neural network to conduct spatial monitoring of soil SOCD based on airborne hyperspectral images, achieving relatively high prediction accuracy. Similarly, Ni et al.34 integrated multi-source remote sensing data with deep learning methods to estimate SOCD on the Tibetan Plateau, and found that the approach achieved an improvement of 10.6%–22.9% in prediction accuracy compared to previous studies. However, based on the previous researches, we have observed that deep learning models often require long training times, involve a large number of parameters, exhibit high model complexity, and are prone to overfitting when training samples are limited.18,33,35 In practice, deep learning methods may not match the efficiency of decision tree-based models such as RF, GBDT, and XGBoost. While ML methods generally outperform traditional approaches in SOCD prediction, there remains a pressing need for systematic evaluation of different ML algorithms (particularly SVM, RF, GBDT, and XGBoost) when applied to multi-source data integration (WV-2, Sentinel-1 SAR, and LiDAR) across mangrove stands of varying ages. Such an assessment is crucial for optimizing SOCD prediction in these ecologically significant yet understudied ecosystems.

In recent decades, population growth, urbanization, and aquaculture expansion have severely degraded mangrove ecosystems worldwide.36 These disturbances have caused the loss of over 25% of global mangrove soil carbon stocks,37 with China losing over 40% of its original mangrove coverage.38 In response, China has launched large-scale restoration programs for these ecologically critical coastal systems.39 While numerous studies have examined SOC dynamics in restored mangroves, research has primarily addressed static factors such as species composition and edaphic conditions.

Several studies have examined carbon storage changes during mangrove restoration. Lunstrum and Chen,40 who combined 6 years of field monitoring with chronosequence analysis to demonstrate increasing soil carbon concentration with stand age. Zhang et al.41 found that soil carbon storage increases with age but that the fast-growing exotic Sonneratia apetala accumulated carbon less efficiently due to its easily decomposed fine roots, with mature native mangroves (e.g., 80 years old) storing significantly more carbon. Chen et al.42 compared mangroves of the same age but different growth forms and found that the shrub Aegiceras corniculatum accumulated soil carbon faster than the tree species Kandelia obovata, with storage continuing to increase with age. Further research is needed to understand how SOCD evolves with stand age during recovery,43,44 particularly regarding spatial distribution. We therefore proposed to use spatial SOCD mapping alongside the distribution of restored mangroves to evaluate the long-term sequestration potential of restoration efforts and optimize management strategies for climate mitigation.

This study presents an integrated approach for mangrove SOCD mapping in Qinglan Harbor, Hainan Province, China, by synergistically combining multi-source remote sensing data (WV-2, LiDAR, and SAR) with advanced ML techniques. The study addresses three objectives: (1) evaluate the effectiveness of multi-sensor data fusion for SOCD prediction and mapping; (2) identify the optimal modeling approach for SOCD prediction through comparative analysis of four ML algorithms (SVM, RF, GBDT, and XGBoost); (3) characterize SOCD variation across stand ages (0–5, 5–10, 10–15, and >15 years). This comprehensive approach not only enhances the accuracy of SOCD mapping but also provides critical insights into age-dependent carbon accumulation processes in restored mangrove ecosystems.

Results

Statistical analysis of SOCD content

Figure 1 presents the statistical distribution of SOCD measurements from 61 sampling sites in Qinglan Harbor mangrove ecosystem, revealing a right-skewed distribution ranging from 3.64 to 23.31 kg m−2. The dataset exhibits a mean SOCD of 10.92 and median of 9.03 kg m−2, with substantial variability (coefficient of variation, CV = 47%) reflecting the complex interplay of biotic and abiotic factors across different stand ages and microtopographic conditions. The histogram distribution shows distinct multimodal patterns suggesting SOCD subpopulations, including several outliers in the higher range (>20 kg m−2), with a standard deviation of ±5.12 kg m−2 indicating significant spatial heterogeneity. These characteristics directly informed our stratified sampling design for ML analysis, ensuring comprehensive representation of the observed SOCD spectrum while accounting for the ecosystem’s inherent variability in carbon accumulation patterns.

Changes of mangrove distribution

Using an object-oriented SVM classification approach, we quantified mangrove forest distribution dynamics across four time periods (2008, 2013, 2018, and 2023), revealing significant expansion patterns and restoration outcomes (Figures 2 and 3). The results reveal a significant acceleration in mangrove recovery, with total coverage expanding from 3.61 km2 in 2008 to 3.92 km2 in 2013, 4.05 km2 in 2018, and ultimately reaching 5.4 km2 by 2023 (Figure 2). This progression demonstrates a clear increase in both the rate and extent of mangrove restoration over the 15-year period. This dramatic acceleration coincided with the implementation of enhanced conservation policies in 2017. Spatial-temporal analysis of these distribution patterns (Figure 3) identified two primary expansion mechanisms of natural colonization along hydrologically connected riverbanks and targeted artificial planting in areas adjacent to existing stands. Land use transition analysis demonstrated that this expansion occurred predominantly through the conversion of aquaculture operations (68% of new mangrove area), abandoned agricultural land (22%), and degraded forests (10%). The expansion progressed through three distinct phases: (1) initial natural colonization (2008–2013) primarily along hydrologically connected riverbanks; (2) transitional mixed regeneration (2013–2018) combining natural spread with emerging planting clusters; and (3) intensive engineered restoration (2018–2023) featuring systematic rehabilitation of aquaculture operations. The spatial configuration evolved from scattered riparian colonization to organized geometric planting designs, while land conversion sources shifted from predominantly degraded forests (early period) to aquaculture ponds (recent period). These quantifiable patterns provide compelling evidence for the effectiveness of science-based restoration management, with the changing expansion rates and spatial patterns directly correlating with strengthened policy support and improved technical capacity in the region.

Selected features

The coefficient of the Lasso algorithm feature selection results (Figure 4) revealed that LiDAR-derived structural metrics (elev_variance, density_metrics2, density_metrics7, density_metrics5) are the most influential predictors, and may be sufficient to precisely characterize the vertical canopy structure and biomass distribution of mangrove ecosystems. Vegetation indices, notably Atmospherically Resistant Vegetation Index (ARVI) (Lasso’s coefficient = 0.81) and Green Leaf Index (GLI) (Lasso’s coefficient = 0.23), demonstrated strong predictive power as proxies for photosynthetic activity and chlorophyll content that influence litter production and root exudation rates. Selected spectral bands including the green (Lasso’s coefficient = 0.48), coastal blue (Lasso’s coefficient = 0.27), and yellow (Lasso’s coefficient = 0.09) bands could be used to estimate vegetation vigor, soil moisture, and senescent vegetation, while Sentinel-1 SAR data provided complementary information on surface roughness and subsurface moisture conditions (VV polarization Lasso’s coefficient = 0.31). This multi-sensor feature importance pattern ecologically aligns with known SOCD drivers, where canopy structure determines litterfall inputs, which in turn affects sediment trapping efficiency, meaning that vegetation health regulates root exudation, and soil moisture influences decomposition rates. This complex story is why the integration of structural (LiDAR), spectral (optical), and microwave (SAR) features provides a more comprehensive characterization of SOCD determinants than any single data source alone.

Model accuracy comparison analysis

ML models were implemented using Python’s scikit-learn library, with hyperparameters tuned as detailed in Table 1. The leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) results for the four machine learning models (Figure 5) revealed significant differences in predictive performance for SOCD prediction. The evaluation revealed distinct performance differences among the models. GBDT showed the lowest accuracy (R2 = 0.54, root-mean-square error (RMSE) = 3.50 kg m−2, relative percent deviation (RPD = 1.45), indicating limited reliability for spatial predictions. RF demonstrated better performance, with R2 = 0.57, RMSE = 3.35 kg m−2, RPD = 1.51. SVM achieved a relatively high R2 of 0.64 but was constrained by substantial bias, as indicated by a lower RPD. XGBoost demonstrated the best overall performance (R2 = 0.72, RMSE = 2.87 kg m−2, RPD = 1.77).

The superior performance of XGBoost stems from its algorithmic architecture, which effectively addresses complex prediction challenges in mangrove ecosystems. The model’s combination of regularization techniques and gradient boosting framework enables robust handling of multi-sensor data interactions while mitigating overfitting risks. The ensemble tree approach captures nonlinear relationships among optical, LiDAR, and SAR features, yielding more accurate predictions across the full SOCD range. Iterative residual correction and subsampling maintain prediction reliability even in data-sparse ranges, and the consensus-based approach generates spatially coherent SOCD patterns consistent with known ecological gradients. This balance of predictive accuracy and ecological plausibility makes XGBoost particularly suitable for mangrove carbon stock assessments requiring both statistical robustness and biological realism.

Stand age-dependent patterns in SOCD spatial distribution

Given XGBoost’s superior accuracy, we used its SOCD predictions to investigate the relationship between stand age and carbon storage. Figure 7 shows the frequency distribution of SOCD across different mangrove ages. Across all age classes, SOCD was primarily distributed between 7 and 17 kg m−2, with a peak frequency observed around 9–11 kg m−2. Mangroves over 15 years old showed a higher proportion in the upper SOCD range. In contrast, 0–5 year-old mangroves are more concentrated in the lower to intermediate SOCD ranges (7–13 kg·m−2), indicating a relatively early stage of carbon accumulation.

Comparative analysis of soil organic carbon density across mangrove stands of different age classes

Total SOC stock was calculated by multiplying SOCD by grid cell area. Mean SOCD and total SOC stock across age classes are compared in Table 2. Mean SOCD increased consistently with forest age. Specifically, the mean SOCD was 10.49 kg m−2 stands for aged 0–5 years, 10.55 kg·m−2 for 5–10 years, 10.96 kg·m−2 for 10–15 years, and 11.47 kg·m−2 stands for older than 15 years. Similarly, total SOC stock of 20 cm depth increased from 14,161 t (0–5 years) to 41,406 t (>15 years). Notably, the SOC stock for the 5–10 years (1,372 t) and 10–15 years (3,398 t) mangroves were significantly lower, likely reflecting their smaller areas. As shown in Figure 8, mangrove expansion in the past 5 years exceeded that of the previous decade and was concentrated at the periphery of existing stands. Overall, stand age plays a critical role in determining both SOCD and total SOC stock; long-term mangrove development enhances soil carbon storage and strengthens the carbon sink function.

Discussion

Remote sensing-based SOCD monitoring is typically conducted under bare soil conditions to capture soil optical properties directly.33 In mangrove forests, however, dense canopy cover precludes direct soil observation, posing challenges for SOCD estimation. Since changes in soil properties can affect vegetation physiological status and productivity, this study proposes an indirect approach to predict SOCD by extracting vegetation growth features from remote sensing imagery. Specifically, we utilized WV-2 optical imagery to derive various optical indicators and vegetation indices, characterizing two-dimensional (2D) optical features of the mangroves while SAR and LiDAR contributed three-dimensional structural information. Integrating these 2D and 3D features comprehensively characterizes mangrove conditions, enabling indirect SOCD estimation. This method has been validated as an effective approach for mapping soil properties in multiple studies.18,19,25

The interplay between feature variables and model performance in SOCD prediction underscores the complexity of carbon dynamics in mangrove ecosystems. Lasso analysis revealed that optical bands, vegetation indices, LiDAR features, and Sentinel-1 SAR data all contribute to SOCD prediction. Multi-source data integration significantly enhanced SOCD monitoring accuracy, highlighting the synergistic value of combining remote sensing technologies. This finding aligns with the work of Zhang et al.,20 who effectively predicted topsoil total carbon content in agricultural systems by integrating airborne imaging spectroscopy, LiDAR, and Sentinel-1 data.

LiDAR features were the most influential predictors, with three density metrics and elevation variance among the top ten selected variables. This dominance can be attributed to LiDAR’s unique capability to capture high-resolution 3D structural information, including canopy height, density, and terrain elevation. These metrics characterize microtopography and hydrology, which directly influence SOC distribution. Canopy density reflects biomass input, a key driver of SOC accumulation via litterfall and root exudation.45 Elevation variance captures terrain complexity, affecting water flow paths and sediment deposition rates—processes that govern organic carbon distribution.46

The importance of LiDAR data aligns with prior research on 3D structural information for modeling belowground carbon. For example, Chen et al.18 demonstrated that LiDAR-derived canopy metrics improved predictions of SOC in tropical forests by capturing subtle variations in vegetation architecture. Similarly, Hurtt et al.47 highlighted the value of LiDAR in quantifying forest structural complexity, which is closely linked to ecosystem productivity and carbon sequestration potential. Our findings extend this to mangrove ecosystems, emphasizing the need for high-resolution 3D data to resolve fine-scale SOC heterogeneity in dynamic coastal environments.

WV-2 multispectral bands also emerged as strong SOCD predictors, with specific wavelengths explaining substantial SOC variability. Previous research has shown demonstrated that visible and near-infrared wavelengths are sensitive to soil organic matter due to the optical properties of organic carbon compounds.21,48,49 Soils with elevated SOC typically display lower reflectance in the green, coastal blue, and yellow bands due to organic matter absorption.50 This darkening effect lowers albedo with increasing SOC, producing a measurable spectral response.51 WV-2’s high spatial resolution (2 m) and narrow bandwidths (20–40 nm) make it particularly effective at capturing this signal, even in partially vegetated areas where soil exposure is limited. ARVI and GLI, commonly used to assess vegetation health and density, showed strong correlations with SOCD, consistent with the organic matter inputs from litterfall and root decay in densely vegetated areas.52 These results confirm that vegetation indices can reliably track SOC dynamics, consistent with Taghizadeh-Mehrjardi et al.53 who identified vegetation indices as essential covariates for high-accuracy SOC spatial prediction.

Model performance ranked as XGBoost > GBDT > SVM > RF. XGBoost’s superior accuracy reflects its advanced algorithmic design. Although all three are ensemble methods, they differ in their optimization strategies. RF uses parallel bagging, GBDT optimizes sequentially with first-order derivatives and XGBoost incorporates a second-order Taylor expansion for more precise optimization. This advantage explains XGBoost’s outperformance, consistent with Sun et al.54 and Nguyen et al.55 Additional studies have confirmed XGBoost’s predictive superiority.26,56,57 SVM outperformed RF, likely because its epsilon-insensitive loss function reduces sensitivity to outliers and noise. These results underscore the importance of algorithm selection for complex environmental prediction tasks.

Our results reveal a clear positive relationship between stand age and SOCD, with values increasing from 10.49 kg m−2 in young (0–5 years) stands to 11.47 kg m−2 in >15 years forests. This pattern emerges through multiple interacting ecological processes. Older mangrove ecosystems support substantially greater biomass production compared to younger stands,58 with extensive root systems (especially pneumatophores and fine roots) contributing organic matter through exudation and turnover.6 The enhanced carbon storage capacity of longer-grown mangroves can be attributed to several mechanisms. Their denser vegetation reduces water velocity, promoting fine-grained sediment deposition.59 During tidal inundation, clay, and fine silt particles remain suspended due to turbulence but settle during slack high tide within the forest60 resulting in more efficient sediment trapping by mature stands. Previous studies indicate that soil bulk density (BD) changes little after restoration, with SOCD primarily determined by SOC concentration rather than BD,61 and other studies found that mangrove SOC content was negatively related to soil BD.62,63 Moreover, Lunstrum and Chen64 observed a positive correlation between SOC content and mangrove stand age, while soil BD generally decreased with increasing duration of mangrove restoration. These findings suggest that SOC accumulation over time is the primary driver of SOCD increase. Therefore, our study also observed the age-related SOCD increase primarily reflects greater SOC accumulation in >15 years stands. These results have implications for blue carbon strategies and coastal zone management. The correlation between stand age and SOCD supports more precise carbon stock assessments and demonstrates the need for 20–30-year planning horizons to realize substantial sequestration benefits. The dual capacity of mature stands for carbon storage and coastal protection through sediment trapping underscores their value in climate adaptation.60 Restoration planning must account for the decades-long timescales needed to realize full sequestration potential. Incorporating these age-specific carbon accumulation patterns into ecosystem management frameworks will be crucial for maximizing the global climate mitigation capacity of mangrove ecosystems, and developing science-based conservation policies that reflect the long-term nature of carbon sequestration processes.

Additionally, previous studies have indicated that SOCD in coastal zones is influenced by multiple environmental factors, including pH, salinity, tidal gradients, and groundwater table.4,42 For instance, Zhao et al.65 found that in coastal wetlands, the average SOCD within the top 0–30 cm of soil generally exhibited a decreasing trend with increasing salinity. This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that high-salt concentrations elevate soil pH, affect microbial activity, and nutrient availability, and consequently suppress the decomposition and accumulation of SOC.66 Elevated salinity and pH also promote carbonate formation and increase inorganic carbon content.67 Groundwater table fluctuations alter soil organic matter biogeochemistry by shifting redox conditions, further influencing carbon distribution.68 Therefore, the aforementioned environmental factors significantly influence the spatial distribution of SOCD and can effectively enhance the precision of its spatial prediction. However, due to limitations in data availability, this study did not incorporate these environmental variables into the SOCD prediction model. Future research should, where feasible, consider integrating relevant environmental data to further improve the accuracy and reliability of SOCD spatial mapping.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, due to data availability constraints, key environmental variables that influence SOCD, such as pH, salinity, tidal gradients, and groundwater table, were not incorporated into the prediction model, despite their known effects on carbon decomposition and accumulation. Second, the chronosequence approach used to determine stand ages assumed that mangrove establishment occurred near the beginning of each time period (e.g., 2008, 2013, and 2018), which may not capture fine-scale variations in actual stand age or potential disturbance history. Third, the relatively small sample size (n = 61) and the single-site focus on Qinglan Harbor may limit the generalizability of the findings to other mangrove regions with different environmental conditions, species compositions, and restoration histories. Finally, soil sampling was limited to the top 20 cm depth, which represents the primary carbon accumulation zone but does not capture deeper carbon stocks or the full ecosystem carbon profile. Future research should address these limitations by incorporating additional environmental covariates, expanding sampling across diverse mangrove regions, and extending soil sampling to greater depths.

Experimental model and study participant details

The study was conducted in Qinglan Harbor (19°34′N, 110°45′E), a nationally significant mangrove ecosystem located in Wenchang City, northeastern Hainan Island, China (Figure S2). This 2948-hectare mangrove forest, situated along extensive intertidal mudflats, is a typical tropical monsoon wetland with stable annual temperatures (24°C–26°C) and high precipitation (1,500–2,000 mm). The vegetation comprises diverse mangrove species, including Bruguiera sexangula, Rhizophora stylosa, Lumnitzera racemosa, Excoecaria agallocha, Avicennia marina, Ceriops tagal, Sonneratia spp., Kandelia candel and Rhizophora apiculata, forming complex ecological communities.70

Following extensive aquaculture development, the area was transformed by a government-led restoration program initiated in 2018. By 2023, all aquaculture ponds had been converted to functional mangrove wetlands, creating an ideal setting for studying SOCD dynamics across restoration ages (0–5, 5–10, 10–15, and >15 years). The site’s combination of restoration history and preserved natural stands provides an opportunity to examine age-dependent soil carbon accumulation.

Soil sampling was conducted across the study area in January 2023. Using a stratified random design accounting for stand age and hydrological gradients, we collected 61 intact soil cores (Figure S2). Undisturbed samples were extracted to 20 cm depth using a stainless-steel corer (5 cm diameter), targeting the primary carbon accumulation zone. Samples were sealed in airtight containers, transported to the laboratory, and oven-dried at 60°C to constant weight (typically 48–72 h) for bulk density determination.71

Dry bulk density (BD, g cm−3) was determined through gravimetric analysis, calculating the ratio of oven-dry mass to total sample volume (including pore spaces). Following drying, samples were processed through a standardized preparation protocol: (1) mechanical sieving through a 2 mm mesh to remove coarse fragments and macro-organic matter, followed by (2) fine grinding using an agate mortar to achieve a homogeneous particle size fraction of <150 μm. This two-stage preparation effectively eliminated residual root tissues and mineral particles that could interfere with subsequent chemical analyses.40 To ensure accurate organic carbon measurement, samples were treated with 1 M hydrochloric acid (HCl) for 24 h to remove inorganic carbon components. Method details

To reconstruct the mangrove restoration chronology, we employed a multi-temporal remote sensing analysis approach using Google Earth images (2008, 2013, 2018) and WV-2 imagery (2023). All images were first co-registered and geometrically corrected to ensure spatial consistency across different years. Mangrove distributions were classified using an object-oriented support vector machine algorithm,69 which effectively distinguishes mangrove vegetation from other land cover types. Through temporal comparison of the classified maps, we delineated newly restored mangrove areas for three distinct periods: 2018–2023 (0–5 years old), 2013–2018 (5–10 years old), and 2008–2013 (10–15 years old), and areas existing prior to 2008 were >15 years old stands. This chronosequence approach was adopted due to the practical limitations of direct age determination methods in mangrove ecosystems. The classification assumes that mangrove establishment occurred near the beginning of each period, providing a framework for analyzing age-dependent SOCD variations across successional stages.

We acquired WV-2 satellite imagery on January 29, 2023, featuring eight multispectral bands at 2 m resolution and a 0.5 m panchromatic band, whose superior spatial resolution enables more detailed vegetation and soil analysis.73 The imagery underwent comprehensive preprocessing in ENVI 5.3 software, beginning with geometric correction using Ground Control Points to WV-2, followed by radiometric calibration to convert digital numbers to radiance values, and concluding with FLAASH atmospheric correction to derive surface reflectance. From these processed data, we calculated 20 vegetation indices (Table S1) that are particularly sensitive to mangrove biophysical and biochemical properties, thereby facilitating accurate assessment of vegetation health and growth dynamics across different stand ages.74,75 This integrated approach of high-resolution imagery acquisition, rigorous preprocessing, and multi-index analysis provides a robust foundation for investigating age-dependent SOCD variations in mangrove ecosystems.

The LiDAR data acquisition was conducted in January 2023 using a DJI Matrice 600 Pro UAV platform equipped with an advanced ARS-100 LiDAR sensor. The system configuration featured: (1) a high pulse repetition frequency of 150 kHz, (2) short laser pulse duration of 3.5 ns for enhanced vertical resolution, and (3) a 14-degree scan angle with quad-return detection capability. Flight missions were carefully planned with >50% sidelap between adjacent swaths to ensure minimum point density of 8 pts/m2 across the study area. Raw laser returns were recorded in LAS 1.0 format, with intensity values (1556 nm wavelength) normalized to 8-bit digital numbers (0–255) for consistent radiometric calibration. The collected point cloud data were rigorously georeferenced to the WGS84 UTM Zone 49N coordinate reference system through tight integration of onboard GNSS receivers and inertial measurement units, achieving sub-decimeter positional accuracy (±5 cm), sufficient for high-precision canopy structure analysis and microtopographic characterization.

The raw LiDAR point clouds underwent comprehensive preprocessing using LiDAR360 software (GreenValley International, Beijing), following a workflow optimized for mangrove ecosystems. The processing pipeline consisted of four key stages: (1) initial data refinement through advanced denoising algorithms and outlier removal, followed by adaptive smoothing filters to enhance signal quality; (2) ground point classification using an improved progressive Triangulated Irregular Network densification algorithm76 specifically optimized for coastal wetland topography; (3) generation of high-resolution (0.5 m) Digital Elevation Model (DEM) and a Digital Surface Model (DSM) through ordinary kriging interpolation of classified ground and first returns, respectively; and (4) production of normalized height point clouds through precise subtraction of DEM elevations from each laser return. This rigorous processing chain ensured millimeter-level vertical accuracy in the derived aboveground height measurements, critical for detecting subtle structural variations across mangrove stands of different ages.

Recent advances in LiDAR analytics have yielded an extensive suite of structural metrics that can be systematically categorized into four functionally distinct groups70,77,78: (1) height metrics derived from vertical point distributions that characterize canopy architecture and tree dimensions; (2) density metrics quantifying return distributions through the vegetation profile; (3) canopy volume metrics capturing three-dimensional morphological features critical for ecosystem functional assessmen79; and (4) intensity metrics analyzing backscatter signal properties that reflect surface biochemical characteristics. This comprehensive feature set (totaling 99 metrics, including gap fraction analysis as detailed in Table S2) enables multi-dimensional characterization of mangrove structural complexity, providing the necessary inputs for robust SOCD modeling across different successional stages.

Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) plays a critical role in all-weather Earth observation, particularly valuable in tropical regions where persistent cloud cover frequently obstructs optical remote sensing. For this study, we utilized C-band SAR data from the Sentinel-1 satellite, which provides reliable imaging capability regardless of atmospheric conditions. Specifically, we acquired Ground Range Detected (GRD) products in Interferometric Wide (IW) swath mode through the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform (https://developers.google.com/earth-engine) on January 2, 2023. These pre-processed datasets, with vegetation and cloud interference minimized, contain backscattering coefficient and dual-polarization information (VV and VH channels) at 10-meter spatial resolution. Following acquisition, we generated a seamless mosaic covering the entire study area for subsequent geospatial analysis.

The integration of multiple remote sensing data sources (optical, SAR, and LiDAR) typically generates a high-dimensional feature space containing redundant information that may introduce noise and compromise model performance through overfitting.80 To address this challenge, we implemented a two-stage feature optimization approach. First, all input variables were standardized using the StandardScaler function to achieve zero mean and unit variance, a critical preprocessing step that enhances algorithm performance by ensuring comparability across different measurement scales while preventing numerical instability during training.
