Analysis of antibiotic resistance and cagPAI variability in Helicobacter pylori strains from Malaysian patients
Authors: Shaza Azlin Razak, Alfizah Hanafiah, Asif Sukri, Hui-min Neoh, Noraziah Mohamad Zin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibiotic resistance and virulence factor of Helicobacter pylori pose formidable challenge towards eradication of this pathogen. Heterogeneity of H. pylori cagPAI is essential in the emergence of antibiotic resistance. The objective of this study was to investigate the correlation between antibiotic resistance and cagPAI variability among H. pylori strains in multiethnic Malaysian patients. H. pylori antibiotic susceptibility profile to six antibiotic classes was determined using E-test and the presence of each cagPAI gene was determined using polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The highest resistance was observed against metronidazole (58.6%, 34/58), followed by clarithromycin (36.2%, 21/58), and levofloxacin (25.9%, 15/58); all strains were susceptible to amoxicillin, tetracycline and rifampicin. Clarithromycin-resistance in H. pylori was significantly higher among elderly patients (> 50 years old) (P = 0.040). Isolates harboring complete cagT4SS genes exhibited significantly higher resistance to antibiotic treatments, primarily towards a single antibiotic (P = 0.043). Resistance to a single antibiotic was significantly higher in East Asian strains (P = 0.027), of which majority of the infected patients were Chinese. Among the resistant isolates, 20.9% (9/43) exhibited multi-drug resistance (MDR), demonstrating resistance to metronidazole, clarithromycin, and levofloxacin concurrently. Female patients were notably at higher risk of being infected with MDR-H. pylori (P = 0.022) than males. Our findings offer insights into the association between variability of cagPAI and antibiotic resistance in H. pylori, of which further research is warranted to elucidate the mechanisms underlying cagPAI heterogeneity and antibiotic resistance in H. pylori for effective therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10123-025-00741-9.Keywords: Pathogenicity island, Antibiotic resistance, Multi-ethic population, Helicobacter pylori

Introduction

Helicobacter pylori is a gram-negative bacterium that colonizes human stomach and is a well-known causative agent for various gastrointestinal disorders including gastritis, peptic ulcers, mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma, and gastric cancer (Ansari et al. 2022). Variations in disease outcomes upon H. pylori infection can be attributed to several factors, including environmental factors, host’s immune response and pathogen’s virulence (Malfertheiner et al. 2023). H. pylori genome is highly diverse and this is attributed to high recombination rates compared to other bacteria (Ailloud et al. 2021). Its high genetic diversity contributes to the development of antibiotic resistance and diverse virulence factors (Linz et al. 2024).

The rise in antibiotic-resistant H. pylori has become a significant challenge for eradication this pathogen. Regimen selection should rely on local antibiotic resistance patterns of H. pylori and the individual's medication history. The main reason for eradication failure or a low eradication rate in treated patients is resistance of the bacterium to administered antibiotics (Aumpan et al. 2023). Antibiotic resistance mechanism in H. pylori is intricate and involves multiple factors, including gene mutations (Sukri et al. 2022), biofilm formation (Krzyżek et al. 2022) and efflux pump overexpression (Gong et al. 2024). In Malaysia, previous report showed that resistance rate for metronidazole was 59.3%, clarithromycin was 35.6%, and levofloxacin was 25.4% while there was no report on resistance to tetracycline and rifampicin (Hanafiah et al. 2019). Failure to eradicate H. pylori due to antibiotic resistance complicates prevention strategy of gastric cancer.

Cytotoxin-associated gene pathogenicity island (cagPAI) is a unique genetic cluster, with a size estimation of 40 kbp and containing ~ 30 genes (Tran et al. 2024), found in pathogenic strains of H. pylori. The cagPAI is known to play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of H.-related diseases, including triggering IL-8 production and morphological alteration of gastric cells, which leads to the development of gastric diseases (Hatakeyama 2014). It is a genetic cluster that contains several virulence factors, including the cagA and the type IV secretion system (T4SS) genes (Tran et al. 2024). cagA is responsible for the production of a protein called CagA, which has been shown to manipulate host cell signaling pathways and contribute to the development of gastric diseases (Keikha et al. 2022). This genetic diversity enables the bacterium to evolve rapidly in response to antibiotic exposure, leading to the emergence of resistant phenotypes. Understanding the mechanisms by which the cagPAI contributes to antibiotic resistance is critical for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies to combat the infections. Further research in this area is essential to elucidate the intricate interactions between the cagPAI and antibiotic resistance, paving the way for the development of novel treatment approaches. This study was conducted to investigate the correlation between antibiotic resistance and cagPAI variability among H. pylori strains in multiethnic Malaysian patients.

Materials and methods

H. pylori cultivation on culture media

A total of 59 H. pylori strains were isolated from patients previously recruited in other studies (Hanafiah et al. 2019; Fadilah et al. 2016), in which the isolates were kept in frozen stock culture until experimentation. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Medical Research Ethic Committee of Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (Approval No. UKM1.5.3.5/244/JEP-2016–095, dated 17 March 2016). Patient consent was waived as the study utilized H. pylori stock cultures; however, these isolates were previously obtained from patients in earlier studies (Research No. ETP-2013–042 and GUP-2011–307), where written informed consent had been obtained.

These isolates were cultivated on Columbia blood agar (Oxoid, Basingstoke, United Kingdom) supplemented with sheep blood (7%) and Dent’s supplement (Oxoid, Basingstoke, United Kingdom), and incubated at 37 °C for 3–5 days under microaerophilic environment. Identification of H. pylori culture was confirmed through biochemical tests and morphological observation. Successfully grown bacteria were subjected to cagPAI genes identification and antibiotic resistance profiling.

Characterization of cagPAI and cagA genes

Genomic DNA of cultured H. pylori strains were extracted, followed by cagPAI genes detection through polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using primers and protocols as described previously (Hanafiah et al. 2020). Each strain undergone detection of flanking regions to identify the existence of cagPAI as described previously using PCR and primers flanking the region of cagPAI (Olbermann et al. 2010), cagPAI-positive strains subsequently underwent individual cagPAI gene identification as described previously (Olbermann et al. 2010). Complete cagPAI is defined when all genes in cagPAI was present including cag2. As cag2 is a pseudogene, intact cagPAI is defined as the presence of all cagPAI genes in H. pylori strains, including without cag2 in its genome. Characterization of CagA EPIYA motifs was conducted as previously described (Razak et al. 2024). Amplified genes were viewed using gel electrophoresis. Intactness of cagPAI and the presence of genes that encode type IV secretion system (T4SS) were determined as described previously (Hanafiah et al. 2020). List of genes that encode T4SS include cag3, cag4, cag5, cagα, cag7, cag8, cag9, cag11, cag16, cag18, cag23, cag25, and cag26. The presence of genes in cagPAI responsible for IL-8 induction was determined as previously described (Razak et al. 2024). The list of genes that is responsible for IL-8 induction includes cag3, cag4, cagα, cag7, cag8, cag9, cag10, cag11, cag12, cag16, cag18, cag20, cag23, and cag25.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing (AST)

Antibiotic susceptibility profiling in H. pylori isolates were carried out using E-test to measure the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) against six antibiotics classes: metronidazole (MTZ), clarithromycin (CLA), amoxicillin (AMX), levofloxacin (LEV), tetracycline (TET) and rifampicin (RIF). Bacterial suspension of approximately 106 CFU/mL, equivalent to McFarland standard of 2.0, were aseptically inoculated onto Muller-Hinton agar supplemented with 5% sheep blood, to produce confluent growth of H. pylori on the surface of the media. On each plate, the E-test strip (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Etoile, France) was placed on the surface media for 72 h at 37 °C under microaerophilic conditions. H. pylori ATCC 43526 strain served as control. The resistance breakpoints for each drug were as follows: > 8 mg/mL for MTZ (Kim et al. 2001), ≥ 1 mg/mL for CLA (CLSI 2009), > 1 mg/mL for AMX (Francesco et al. 2011), LEV (Kim et al. 2005) and TET (Monno et al. 2015), whereas 4 mg/mL for RIF (Chisholm and Owen 2009).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS software version 23 (SPSS Inc, Illinois, USA).

The data comparing cagPAI integrity, antibiotic susceptibility profiles, cagA types, patient ethnicity, and disease severity were analyzed using either the Chi-square test or Fischer's exact test. Kruskal–Wallis test with multiple pairwise comparisons via Mann–Whitney U test were used to analyse the association of continuous variables including patient’s age and number of gene deletions with antibiotic resistance. Statistical significance was defined as a P value below 0.05.

Results

Patient’s demography features

In this study, 59 H. pylori strains were successfully revived and cultured from frozen stock. The strains were previously isolated from patients of different demographic features, including age range, gender, ethnicity and diagnosis. Most of the infected patients’ age were 50 years and older (38/59, 64.4%) and the remaining 35.6% (21/59) were younger than 50 years old. Patient’s distribution based on ethnicities were highest among Chinese (33/59, 55.9%), followed by Malays at 20.3% (12/59), Indian (16.9%, 10/59) and other ethnicities (4/59, 6.8%). The majority of these patients were diagnosed with chronic gastritis (53/59, 89.8%) whereas 6.8% (4/59) were with gastritis, and 1.7% (1/59) each for peptic ulcer and normal stomach, respectively. No gastric cancer among patients was recorded in this study.

Antibiotic susceptibility of H. pylori strains

A total of 58 strains underwent antibiotic susceptibility test using E-test. The profiling of antibiotic susceptibility test to six antibiotic classes is displayed in Table 1. As observed, the majority of the strains (43/58, 74.1%) were resistant to at least one antibiotic. Moreover, the highest percentage of resistance was detected in MTZ, followed by CLA and LEV. Nine strains (20.9%) were classified as MDR, in which all were resistant to metronidazole, clarithromycin, and levofloxacin. All strains were susceptible to amoxicillin, tetracycline and rifampicin. Interestingly, patients aged more than 50 years old demonstrated significantly higher risk of infection with clarithromycin-resistant H. pylori than patients aged less than 50 years old (P = 0.040). Furthermore, women had higher risk to be infected with MDR strains than men (P = 0.022).

cagPAI genes profiling in H. pylori strains and antibiotic susceptibility features

Most of the H. pylori strains in this study were found harbouring cagPAI (57/59, 96.6%), although 96.5% (55/57) of these isolates had incomplete cagPAI genes. The integrity of cagPAI was further narrowed according to the existence of genes responsible for T4SS and IL-8 secretion. Among the cagPAI-positive isolates, 71.9% (41/57) of the isolates contained all genes associated with cagT4SS whereas only 31.6% (18/57) had complete genes set for IL-8 induction. Statistical analysis showed that strains with intact or complete cagT4SS genes were significantly associated with antibiotic resistance (P = 0.043), as illustrated in Fig. 1. No significant difference was observed in antibiotic resistance based on cagPAI IL-8 genes set.

The presence and absence of cagPAI genes against the resistant and susceptible categories were analyzed to determine whether the presence of the virulence factor genes carried by H. pylori was related to antibiotic resistance. All 26 cagPAI genes were analyzed against their resistance and susceptibility against MTZ, CLA and LEV (Table S1). Among the detected cagPAI genes, strains which were cag2-positive recorded the highest prevalence in MTZ resistance (14/21, 66.7%) as well as in levofloxacin resistance (9/21, 42.9%), while cag19-positive strains recorded the highest percentage being resistant towards CLA treatment (41.2%). The existence of cag2 appeared to have significant impact towards the strains ability to resist LEV treatment (p= 0.034), whereas cag19-positive strains were more likely to be resistant towards CLA (p = 0.041). At the same time, strains with cag11 were found to be at higher risk of being antibiotic resistant (P = 0.014). There was no significant difference in the distribution of other cagPAI genes against the resistance and susceptible categories of the strains.

Distribution of cagA among H. pylori strains and its association with antibiotic susceptibility

Identification of cagA type in H. pylori strains was based on the EPIYA motifs. Most of the strains were cagA-positive (55/59, 94.9%), of which East Asian cagA had the highest prevalence (33/55, 60%), Western cagA at 29.1% (16/55) and undefined cagA (6/55, 10.9%). Repetition of EPIYA-C motifs were documented as well and majority did not have repetition (51/55, 92.7%). Upon observation, majority of the East Asian cagA strains were isolated from Chinese patients (27/33, 81.8%), whereas Western cagA strains were mostly came from Malay patients (8/16, 50%) (p = 0.010). Table 2 displayed the characterization of cagA and antibiotic susceptibility profiles, with apparent significant association shown between virulent East Asian cagA and antibiotic resistance (P = 0.027). Interestingly, this association was also correlated to the patient’s ethnicity with similar significance value. Despite the high percentage of antibiotic resistance among undefined cagA strains (83.3%), these strains were only resistant towards one type of antibiotic. There was no significant correlation observed upon EPIYA-C repetitions towards antibiotic treatment.

Multidrug resistance among H. pylori strains

As mentioned previously, only nine cases among antibiotic-resistant H. pylori strains categorized as MDR, particularly towards MTZ, CLA and LEV. Majority of the strains with complete cagT4SS genes were MDR (7/9, 77.8%), whereas strains with incomplete IL-8 genes set were MDR (6/9, 66.7%), although there was no statistical significance detected between cagPAI integrity and MDR. Observation on the existence of individual cagPAI genes was conducted as well to analyze its association towards strain’s MDR risks. Interestingly, MDR strains were observed to exhibit likelihood to have deletions in cag7 and cag16 genes. Despite there was no statistical significance detected, majority of the MDR strains were East Asian cagA type (7/9, 77.8%).

Discussion

With the scarcity of suitable antibiotics for eradicating H. pylori and the global rise in antibiotic resistance, there is a pressing demand for new efforts aimed in understanding the mechanisms behind H. pylori's resistance to current antibiotics. Furthermore, with the existence of virulence factor such as cagPAI this will impact the severity outcomes of infected patients (Farzi et al. 2019). The genetic variety within H. pylori shows an important role in establishing connections between genetical components and antibiotic resistance.

In this study, the antibiotic susceptibility and cagPAI profiling in H. pylori has been analysed in order to discover the association between antibiotic resistance and cagPAI heterogeneity. Majority of the strains were resistant to at least one antibiotic, with metronidazole having the highest percentage compared to other antibiotics, of which the pattern is similar pattern to another study (Sukri et al. 2022). From our observations, patients aged 50 and above were notably prone of being infected with clarithromycin-resistance H. pylori. Excessive usage of antibiotic in the past among elderlies could be the contributor to the resistance development (Nguyen et al. 2019). Moreover, elderly people often suffer from chronic diseases including diabetes and kidney failure, hence limiting the administration of certain drugs due to potential adverse reactions from drug interactions (Anrys et al. 2021). Interestingly, MDR cases in this study were found explicitly among women patients. This could be contributed to the MDR strains also being the East Asian cagA, impacting on its virulence. Despite no significant difference of MDR status between the cagA strains, yet it is notably higher among East Asian cagA H. pylori.

The association between the cagPAI and antibiotic resistance in H. pylori can be attributed to several mechanisms. Overall analysis had shown no association between cagPAI genes and antibiotic resistance in H. pylori. There were similarities in the findings as reported by other studies, of which no significant differences were observed between cagPAI and antibiotic resistance (Sukri et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2022). In contrast to cagT4SS genes set, H. pylori with intact or complete cagT4SS genes appeared to play an essential role in conferring resistance of antibiotic. T4SS is a set of genes responsible for formation molecular “needle-like” structure important in delivery of bacterial virulence factor(s) to host’s cell for normal cellular disturbance (Tran et al. 2024). In addition, it is also pertinent in bacterial horizontal gene transfer of essential antibiotic resistance gene and plasmid that confer phenotypic antibiotic resistance in bacteria (Roy et al. 2025). Recent study reveals the importance of T4SS in the emergence of antibiotic-resistant Acinetobacter, in which T4SS-encoding plasmid is important in horizontal gene transfer of resistance gene (Nasser et al. 2024). However, the role of T4SS in conferring resistance in H. pylori remains to be elucidated, particularly that the presence of plasmid as horizontal gene transfer vector in H. pylori genome is low (Bui et al. 2025). It is hypothesized that the presence of T4SS in H. pylori increases drug efflux pump in this bacterium, thereby confers resistance of this bacterium to antibiotic. This hypothesis should be investigated further in future research. Nevertheless, complete cagT4SS genes enable efficient functionality in translocating cagA into host cells, thus initiating pathogenesis in host (Tran et al. 2024). Recent studies have investigated the connection between H. pylori virulence factors and antibiotic resistance. These include exploring the relationship between antibiotic-resistant H. pylori strains and those lacking the cagA virulence factor (Fasciana et al. 2015). Our analysis showed significant association between the virulent East Asian cagA strain and the antibiotic susceptibility profile. Although our findings were consistent with previous studies on cagA (Liu et al. 2022; Fasciana et al. 2015), those studies only examine the presence of cagA without categorizing its EPIYA motifs. Explorations on individual cagPAI genes and EPIYA characterization are warranted for in depth understanding of the H. pylori’s virulence towards development of antibiotic resistance, especially those displaying variations in genetic structure.

Strength of this study includes genetic diversity in H. pylori strains and its reflections towards multiple ethnicities of hosts. To the best of our knowledge, this is first study that explored the connection between cagPAI integrity and antibiotic resistance of H. pylori among Malaysian patients. These new data are possible in providing insights of the potential treatment and management improvement in infected patients in the future.

However, limitations were also being acknowledged in this study. The number of samples should be larger for the findings to be applicable and presentable for overall population. Information on mutations that contribute to the antibiotic resistance were not included, hence data on specific genetic altercation were not applicable. Furthermore, input on individual cagPAI genes were not presented in this study that led towards lacking in interaction of individual cag gene towards outcomes in treatment resistance, instead the integrity of cagPAI gene sets were used. Moreover, these data solely relied on cultivable H. pylori obtained from gastric biopsies and stored frozen. There is limited understanding regarding whether bacteria that grow in vitro exhibit genetic distinctions from those cultured in vivo.

This study showed that H. pylori strains isolated from multiethnic-Malaysian patients exhibited variety in both antibiotic susceptibility and cagPAI. Profiling on H. pylori antibiotic susceptibility was established with metronidazole having the highest rate among the six antibiotics. There was an association between integrity in cagT4SS set towards resistance in antibiotics observed. H. pylori harboring East Asian cag are more prone in developing resistant towards antibiotic. Furthermore, correlation was shown upon infected patient’s age and gender in resistance profiling, and indirectly towards cagPAI variability. Observation on MDR were detected among strains with correlation to the virulent East Asian cagA strains. These results provide valuable insights into the relationship between cagPAI variability and antibiotic resistance in H. pylori, highlighting the need for additional research to uncover the mechanisms driving cagPAI diversity and antibiotic resistance in H. pylori, which can inform the development of more effective therapeutic approaches.
