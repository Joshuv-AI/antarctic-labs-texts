Validation of a health literacy assessment tool in a nationwide population: a measurement invariance study using confirmatory factor analysis and multiple-group latent class analysis
Authors: Hanna Lee, Jeongwon Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndividuals with lower health literacy are more vulnerable to misinformation and may face challenges in disease prevention, treatment, and healthcare utilization6. Accordingly, accurately assessing the level of individuals’ health literacy and implementing educational and policy-based interventions to improve it are essential public health goals. This study assessed the psychometric properties of a health literacy assessment tool, using data from the 2023 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES). Specifically, the study tested measurement invariance across sociodemographic groups and identified latent health literacy subgroups using multiple-group latent class analysis (LCA).MethodsData from 5,677 adults aged 19 years and older who participated in the 2023 KNHANES were analyzed. A confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to validate the factor structure of the tool, and measurement invariance was tested across gender, age, educational level, income, and spousal cohabitation using multiple-group analyses. In addition, an LCA was performed to classify subgroups based on health literacy levels and to identify item-level response differences.ResultsThe CFA results supported a unidimensional structure with excellent model fit. The measurement invariance testing confirmed configural, metric, scalar, and strict invariance across all of the sociodemographic groups. The LCA identified a two-class model as the best fit, with a significant between-group difference in the response to the item assessing confidence in knowing what to do first in an emergency.ConclusionThe findings reaffirm the validity and reliability of the health literacy tool across diverse subpopulations. Moreover, the LCA proved useful in identifying subpopulations with limited health information utilization. These results provide a valuable empirical basis for the development of targeted health literacy interventions and public health policy planning.Trial registrationClinical trial number: not applicable.

Introduction

The widespread adoption of digital technologies and smart devices over the past decade has substantially transformed how individuals access health information [1]. The development of online platforms, search engines, and social networking services has led to an exponential increase in the quantity of health-related information available to the public [2]. While these tools have enhanced accessibility, they have also resulted in the proliferation of unreliable and non-evidence-based information. Consequently, the ability to independently evaluate, interpret, and apply health information has become a critical competency. In this context, health literacy has emerged as a key public health competency [3, 4].

Sørensen et al. [5] define health literacy as the ability to access, understand, evaluate, and apply health information, make informed health decisions, and take appropriate actions. Individuals with lower health literacy are more vulnerable to misinformation and may face challenges in disease prevention, treatment, and healthcare utilization [6]. Accordingly, accurately assessing the level of individuals’ health literacy and implementing educational and policy-based interventions to improve it are essential public health goals.

Recognizing its importance, national-level efforts have been made to enhance health literacy. In South Korea, the 5th National Health Promotion Plan (Health Plan 2030) includes “enhancement of health information literacy” as a core indicator of health equity. To operate this, the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) developed a health literacy assessment tool and incorporated it into its 2023 cycle [7]. However, unless the tool’s measurement invariance is tested across diverse demographic groups, its validity for comparing health literacy levels may be limited.

Measurement invariance ensures that a measurement tool operates equivalently across different groups such as those defined by gender, age, education, or income and is necessary for meaningful group comparisons [8, 9]. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and measurement invariance testing are crucial for ensuring the structural validity and interpretability of measurement instruments, particularly those used in national surveillance initiatives.

Health literacy is a multidimensional and complex construct. Individuals with similar total scores may differ substantially in how they interpret or use health information [3]. This highlights the possible existence of unobserved heterogeneity among subgroups, which can undermine the effectiveness of standardized interventions. As a result, person-centered analytic approaches, such as latent class analysis (LCA), have gained traction. These methods allow researchers to identify subgroups of individuals who share similar response patterns and to analyze their unique characteristics and needs [10].

Therefore, this study evaluated the factorial structure of a health literacy assessment tool using CFA and examined its measurement invariance using multiple-group LCA (MGLCA), based on data from the 2023 KNHANES. The aim was to provide empirical evidence for the validity of the tool in population-based research and to support the identification of vulnerable subpopulations with low health literacy.

Study design and methods

Study design

This study conducted a secondary analysis of data drawn from the 2023 KNHANES, which is administered by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency. KNHANES is a nationally representative cross-sectional survey that uses probability sampling to collect comprehensive data on health status, health behaviors, nutritional intake, and socioeconomic indicators of the Korean population. This study used CFA and MGLCA to examine the factor structure of the survey’s health literacy measurement tool and to test measurement invariance across various demographic groups.

Study population

The participants in this study were adults aged 19 years and older who took part in the 2023 KNHANES and responded to all items related to health literacy. After surveys missing responses on any of the health literacy items were excluded, the final sample consisted of 5,677 respondents.

Measurement

Health literacy

In this study, health literacy was assessed using a 10-item instrument developed by Yoon et al. [7] for integration into the KNHANES as a national indicator for monitoring health literacy under the Health Plan 2030. The instrument was developed through a rigorous multi-step process, including literature review, expert consultation and Delphi surveys, qualitative interviews with the general population, cognitive interviews, and large-scale national survey validation (n = 1,041). The instrument is designed to assess individuals’ ability to understand, evaluate, and apply health information in everyday health-related contexts and consists of four domains: disease prevention (3 items), health promotion (1 item), healthcare management (4 items), and resource utilization (2 items). The instrument consists of 10 items, each rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = “Strongly disagree,” 2 = “Disagree,” 3 = “Agree,” and 4 = “Strongly agree”). At the time of its development, Cronbach’s value for the reliability of this tool was 0.87; in this study, the Cronbach’s α for internal consistency was 0.92.

Sociodemographic variables

The instrument’s measurement invariance was assessed across a range of sociodemographic variables. Gender was recorded as male or female. Based on research suggesting that health information needs and comprehension may vary across different life stages such as young adulthood, middle age, and older adulthood [11], age was categorized into three groups: under 40 years, 40 to 64 years, and 65 years and older. Educational attainment was classified into four levels: elementary school or less, middle school graduate, high school graduate, and college graduate or higher. Income level was measured using the annual personal income and grouped into low, lower-middle, upper-middle, and high according to the KNHANES classification criteria. Lastly, marital status was measured based on whether a participant was living with a spouse.

Data analysis

The descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were calculated according to the participants’ sociodemographic characteristics and responses to the health literacy instrument using SPSS 26.0 (Data Solution, Inc.). The KNHANES employs a complex sampling design incorporating stratification, clustering, and sampling weights. However, as the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the measurement properties and latent structure of the instrument rather than to estimate population-level parameters, sampling weights and design variables were not incorporated into the CFA and LCA analyses. This approach is commonly adopted in psychometric validation studies. To examine the measurement invariance of the health literacy instrument, the analysis was conducted in several stages. First, a CFA was conducted using JASP 0.16.4 [12] to evaluate the instrument’s factor structure and model fit. The model fit was assessed using multiple indices, including the χ² test, comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis’s index (TLI), Bentler–Bonett normed fit index (NFI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). Given that the scale uses a 4-point ordinal Likert response format, diagonally weighted least squares (DWLS) estimation was used. This estimation method is recommended for ordinal or categorical data as such variables often violate multivariate normality and yield non-Wishart distributed covariance matrices, which limits the applicability of a maximum likelihood estimation [13]. However, when utilizing the DWLS estimation method, the RMSEA can be biased depending on the number of items or categories. Particularly with large sample sizes, the RMSEA may respond more sensitively to the statistical characteristics of the data rather than the actual model fit, potentially leading to unacceptable levels [14]. Therefore, this study interpreted the model fit indices while carefully considering these statistical limitations.

Next, measurement invariance across sociodemographic groups was tested using a multiple-group CFA (MGCFA), in which the respondents were grouped by demographic characteristics. Following the framework proposed by Vandenberg and Lance [15], the analysis proceeded through three levels of invariance testing: configural, metric, and scalar invariance. Although strict invariance (which assumes the equivalence of error variances) is the fourth level, it was excluded in this study due to its highly restrictive assumptions and practical infeasibility [16]. For model comparisons, changes in fit indices (i.e., ΔCFI ≤ 0.01 and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015) were used as criteria for establishing invariance, in line with recommendations by Cheung and Rensvold [17] and Kline [18]. While χ² difference tests are widely used, recent research has highlighted their sensitivity to large sample sizes, which can lead to misleading assessments of significance [19]. Therefore, changes in model fit indices were considered more appropriate for this dataset and were used to determine the variables’ invariance.

Additionally, this study adopted the approach proposed by Kankaraš et al. [20] to verify measurement equivalence using a latent class modeling framework. This involved a three-step process. First, an LCA was conducted using the poLCA package in R (version 4.4.3) to determine the optimal number of latent classes within each sociodemographic subgroup. Given the survey’s 4-point ordinal response format, LCA with ordinal indicators was used in this step of the analysis. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), log-likelihood, χ², and likelihood ratio chi-square (G²), and the results were used to determine whether the number of latent classes remained consistent across demographic groups.

Once equivalence in the number of latent classes was confirmed, an MGLCA was conducted to examine scale-level measurement equivalence across groups. Three nested models’ non-invariant, partial invariant, and structurally invariant were sequentially compared using the AIC, BIC, model fit indices, and significance testing of the χ² differences.

Confirmatory factor analysis of health literacy

Prior to testing the measurement invariance of the items, a CFA was conducted to examine the factor structure of the health literacy instrument (Table 2). The health literacy assessment tool was originally developed by Yoon et al. [7] with a single-factor structure, and therefore we conducted CFA based on one latent factor in this study. Accordingly, this study applied a one-factor model in the analysis. The results demonstrated a model fit: χ² = 6662.74, df = 35, CFI = 0.985, TLI = 0.981, NFI = 0.985, RMSEA = 0.183, and SRMR = 0.008. The model demonstrated mixed fit indices with acceptable comparative fit. While CFI (0.985) and TLI (0.981) indicated an excellent fit, the RMSEA (0.183) was above the conventional threshold. However, it has been documented that RMSEA can be upwardly biased when employing DWLS estimation with large representative samples and ordinal data. Given the strong comparative fit indices and the known sensitivity of RMSEA in this specific analytical context, the model was deemed acceptable for further analysis.

Measurement invariance of the health literacy instrument

To examine whether the health literacy instrument would function equivalently across different sociodemographic groups, an MGCFA was conducted. The results, presented in Table 3, demonstrated acceptable model fit for the configural, metric, and scalar invariance models. Specifically, the configural model examines whether the same factor structure holds across groups, the metric model tests the equality of factor loadings, and the scalar model evaluates the equality of item intercepts. Furthermore, changes in the fit indices between nested models ΔCFI ≤ 0.010 and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015 met the established thresholds, upholding the baseline finding of measurement invariance. These findings suggest that the instrument performs consistently with respect to gender, age, education, income level, and spousal cohabitation status.

Multiple-group latent class analysis of health literacy

To explore patterns in the health literacy responses, an LCA was performed by incrementally increasing the number of latent classes from two to five. Model selection was based on the comparative evaluation of AIC, BIC, log-likelihood, χ², and G² values. As shown in Fig. 1; Table 4, the AIC and G² values steadily decreased, while the BIC, log-likelihood and χ² value values increased in the five-class model. Statistical criteria, changes in fit indices, and interpretability were used to assess the models, and the two-class model was identified as the most accurate for capturing adult health literacy patterns. The item with the greatest variability between the two groups was Item 6: “Are you able to judge what to do first in an emergency?” Group 1 exhibited high confidence in their responses to this item, whereas Group 2 expressed low confidence, indicating that this item played a pivotal role in differentiating the two groups. The findings of this study reveal that the two latent classes exhibit the most pronounced divergence in Item 6. This suggests that the health literacy measurement tool employed in this study may possess a structural characteristic where the ability to judge emergency situations plays a more dominant role in reflecting a participant’s relative level of health literacy compared to other items. However, these results also suggest that the identified latent classes may be characterized as groups representing relative levels of proficiency (high vs. low) centered around core competencies, rather than being defined as qualitatively distinct or unique types. Subsequently, the latent class structures were assessed across the sociodemographic variables (gender, age, education, income, and spousal cohabitation), and the results indicated that the two-class solution was consistently appropriate across all subgroups, satisfying the prerequisite for measurement equivalence. Furthermore, measurement invariance at the scalar level was supported for gender, age, educational level, and spousal cohabitation status (Table 5).

Discussion

This study used CFA and MGLCA to examine the measurement invariance of a health literacy assessment tool. The findings are discussed in this section.

First, the CFA results uphold the measurement invariance of the health literacy tool across various sociodemographic groups. These findings are consistent with those of Leitgöb et al. [8] and indicate that the instrument functions robustly across subgroups defined by age, gender, education, income, and spousal cohabitation status. Notably, unlike the European Health Literacy Survey Questionnaire, which failed to demonstrate full invariance across gender, age, and education groups [21], the tool assessed in this study appears to be well adapted to the Korean healthcare context. This result may be attributable to factors such as high-quality translation procedures, appropriate item construction, and a design incorporating item response theory (IRT) principles [22]. These results also align with Chen’s [19] recommendation to carefully interpret model fit indices due to their sensitivity. These results reaffirm the validity and reliability of the health literacy tool developed by the KNHANES team, which was designed to reflect the specific features of the Korean health system and population health information usage. In particular, the tool avoids knowledge-based items and instead focuses on general health information competencies, which likely contributed to the attainment of measurement invariance. Additionally, by applying both classical test theory (CTT) and IRT in the design, the tool effectively addresses limitations commonly associated with self-report measures, thus reducing measurement error and enhancing item-level reliability. Nevertheless, if new items are added or existing ones revised in future applications, measurement invariance testing should be conducted repeatedly to minimize the risk of misinterpretation and statistical bias in group comparisons.

Second, the results of the MGLCA confirm the existence of two latent classes of health literacy among adults. This result differs from the findings of Yekaninejad et al. [23], who identify five latent classes of self-reported health literacy levels among Iranian adults. In the Iranian study, multiple groups emerge from the analysis of varying response levels across all items. The results of this study indicate that the two latent classes exhibit the most pronounced difference on Item 6, which concerns the ability to judge what to do first in an emergency. This suggests that the health literacy instrument employed in the present study may possess a structural characteristic whereby the emergency decision-making item contributes more strongly than other items to reflect individuals’ relative levels of health literacy. However, these findings also imply that the identified latent classes may be more appropriately characterized not as qualitatively distinct, mutually exclusive types, but rather as latent groups representing relatively higher and lower levels of proficiency, centered around core competencies. This is a key component of critical health literacy, which goes beyond simply understanding and interpreting health information and involves the ability to assess the credibility of information and apply it to complex, real-life contexts [24, 25]. This finding underscores the notion that health literacy is not merely about comprehension but also requires sophisticated judgment and practical application [25]. It further suggests that LCA, as a person-centered analytic approach, is useful for identifying subgroups of individuals who may benefit from targeted policies or intervention strategies [26, 27]. However, the results of an LCA can vary with the characteristics of the study population and the interpretation framework adopted by the researchers, and they may also be influenced by cultural and healthcare system contexts. In Korea, the widespread accessibility of digital media may have enhanced surface-level skills in seeking health information. However, in high-stakes contexts like emergencies where advanced decision-making and action are required individual differences in capability may become more pronounced. The respondents in the second group could be understood to have low levels of judgment and application ability in terms of health information in emergencies. For these individuals, visual materials, action-oriented messaging, and repetitive practice-based education may be more effective than complex text-based content. Universal strategies such as visual aids, repeated education, and behavior-centered messages have been shown to improve health literacy among low-skilled populations [28]. Accordingly, these groups should be prioritized in future community-based health literacy interventions.

This study deepens our understanding of health literacy by verifying the measurement invariance of a health literacy assessment tool across various demographic characteristics and by identifying group-specific features using MGLCA. By using nationally representative survey data, the study empirically demonstrates the structural validity and stability of a well-established health literacy tool for the Korean population. These findings offer a robust foundation for future public health policies and the development of tailored intervention strategies. Furthermore, by using LCA to identify subgroups that have low levels of health literacy and proposing specific directions for intervention, the study highlights the potential for promoting universal health equity.

However, this study has several limitations. First, as it is based on cross-sectional secondary data, its ability to capture temporal changes in health literacy or identify causal relationships is limited, making it difficult to assess the long-term effectiveness of interventions. Future research using longitudinal designs is needed to examine the developmental trajectories of health literacy and the factors influencing them. Second, the latent classes identified in this study may vary depending on the items included and the sample composition; therefore, further validation in diverse populations and cultural contexts is warranted. Third, this study did not account for the complex sampling design of the KNHANES, including sampling weights, stratification, and clustering, in the CFA and LCA analyses. As the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the measurement properties and latent structure of the health literacy instrument rather than to estimate population parameters, unweighted analyses were conducted, which is a common approach in psychometric validation studies. Nevertheless, the findings should be interpreted with caution when generalizing to the broader national population.

Despite these limitations, the findings of this study have several implications for research, practice, and policy. First, the confirmation of measurement invariance affirms the structural validity and cross-group applicability of the health literacy tool, providing empirical support for its continued use in both research and public health practice. Second, the identification of latent subgroups, particularly those with lower confidence in emergency decision-making, highlights the need for targeted interventions that prioritize critical health literacy. Educational strategies should therefore move beyond information dissemination to experiential, scenario-based learning that cultivates higher-order cognitive skills. Third, additional testing of measurement invariance is recommended whenever the tool is modified to ensure its continued psychometric soundness. Collectively, these implications emphasize the necessity of differentiated, equity-focused interventions to enhance health decision-making capacity, reduce disparities, and promote public health equity.

Moreover, this study focused on the unidimensional construct of health literacy to examine its characteristics via measurement invariance and latent class analysis. However, there is a need for further analysis using multidimensional tools. Comparing the outcomes of different measurement scales would provide meaningful insights into how these tools function across different populations and study purposes. In the model selection process for the Latent Class Analysis (LCA), although statistical fit indices such as AIC and BIC favored a five-class solution, a two-class model was ultimately selected for its substantive interpretability and practical significance. However, it must be acknowledged that this approach carries the risk of oversimplifying the differences between groups and may not fully capture the multifaceted information inherent in the data. Consequently, future research should consider employing LCA with complex samples or conducting comparative analyses across diverse populations to validate and refine these latent structures.

Conclusion

This study confirmed the structural validity and measurement invariance of a health literacy assessment tool using CFA and MGLCA with data from the 2023 KNHANES. The findings demonstrate that the tool can be reliably applied across diverse demographic groups and that health literacy should be understood as a multidimensional, action-oriented competence.
