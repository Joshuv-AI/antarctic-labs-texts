Fully automated 3D MRI assessment of isolated chondral fracture of the knee in an adolescent athlete: A case report
Authors: Nobutake Ozeki, Hisako Katano, Tomomasa Nakamura, Yusuke Nakagawa, Takashi Hoshino, Mai Katakura, Masaki Amemiya, An Jae-Sung, Jun Masumoto, Hideyuki Koga, Ichiro Sekiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Isolated chondral fractures of the knee are rare injuries in pediatric patients and may be difficult to diagnose because of the absence of osseous attachment. We report an 11-year-old boy who sustained a right knee injury during soccer. Although radiographs and computed tomography showed no abnormalities, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed an isolated chondral fracture of the lateral femoral condyle. Fully automated 3-dimensional MRI visualized a full-thickness cartilage defect and enabled precise measurement of lesion morphology and area. The patient underwent anatomical reduction and fixation using bioabsorbable pins. Follow-up MRI at 5 months demonstrated complete cartilage healing, and the patient returned to competitive soccer 8 months postoperatively without symptoms. This case highlights the diagnostic and clinical utility of fully automated 3-dimensional MRI for preoperative assessment, surgical planning, and postoperative evaluation of isolated chondral fractures in pediatric patients, while demonstrating that timely fixation can achieve successful cartilage healing and return to sport.

Introduction

Isolated chondral fractures of the knee, though uncommon, pose a significant clinical challenge—particularly in pediatric and adolescent populations, where joint preservation and the return to high-level activity are essential goals [1]. These injuries, typically resulting from acute shearing trauma, lack osseous attachment, thereby complicating both diagnosis and surgical decision-making [2]. While such lesions were historically deemed unsalvageable, recent advances in fixation techniques—employing bioabsorbable implants, chondral darts, and fibrin glue—have demonstrated promising short- to mid-term outcomes, especially in appropriately selected young patients [2]. Systematic reviews now report low failure rates and high rates of return to sports when fixation is performed early [2,3].

Recent developments in fully automated 3D MRI analysis have enabled the objective and reproducible quantification of cartilage and meniscal morphology, including thickness [4,5], volume, and body size using independent indices such as the projected cartilage area ratio and the medial meniscus coverage ratio [6,7]. A cross-platform study using 3T MRI scanners from five major manufacturers demonstrated excellent interscanner reliability in cartilage thickness measurements (within 0.20 mm in 95% of cases) and identified clinically relevant correlations among projected cartilage area ratio, age, and meniscal extrusion, underscoring the utility of this technology for structural knee assessment [5].

In this case report, we describe the successful surgical fixation of an isolated chondral fracture in an adolescent patient and highlight the value of fully automated 3-dimensional (3D) MRI in both preoperative surgical planning and the postoperative evaluation of cartilage healing.

Case report

An 11-year-old boy presented to our institution with right knee pain following a sudden onset of severe pain during a competitive soccer match. His history revealed that he had first noted mild right knee discomfort the morning after a soccer practice session approximately 2 weeks prior, with no identifiable traumatic event at that time. He had been managed conservatively at a local clinic and had gradually returned to running. Upon resuming competitive play, he experienced a sudden exacerbation of pain during a match, was unable to continue playing, and presented with an antalgic gait. On initial examination at our institution, physical examination revealed slow but independent ambulation, mild joint effusion, and restricted range of motion due to pain. The affected right knee demonstrated a range of motion from 0° to 130°, whereas the contralateral left knee exhibited full motion from +5° to 155°. The patient had a medical history of C3 glomerulopathy (C3G) and was receiving oral prednisolone at a dose of 20 mg on alternate days at the time of presentation, under the care of a pediatric nephrologist. The duration of corticosteroid therapy prior to the injury was not available from the medical records. There was no prior surgical history. The patient had no remarkable family history. He had been playing soccer 5 days per week since kindergarten and was a member of a competitive club team. Preoperative laboratory investigations were performed to evaluate systemic inflammation and renal function in the context of the patient's history of C3G and chronic corticosteroid therapy. The results showed no evidence of active systemic inflammation, renal dysfunction, or coagulation abnormalities. Detailed laboratory data are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

MRI scanning

Magnetic resonance imaging was performed using a 3.0 T system (Achieva 3.0TX; Philips, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) equipped with a 16-channel coil. The knee was positioned at approximately 15° of flexion during image acquisition. Sagittal plane images of the knee joint were obtained using both a fat-suppressed spoiled gradient echo (SPGR) sequence and a proton density–weighted (PDW) sequence. For the SPGR sequence, the repetition time (TR) was 20 milliseconds, with echo times (TEs) of 7 milliseconds and 13.8 milliseconds for the first and second echoes, respectively. The flip angle was set at 35°. For the PDW sequence, the TR was 1000 milliseconds, TE was 35 milliseconds, and the flip angle was 90°. An echo train length of 32 was applied for the PDW sequence (not applicable to SPGR). In both sequences, the acquisition voxel size was 0.6 × 0.6 × 0.6 mm, reconstructed to an isotropic resolution of 0.3 × 0.3 × 0.3 mm. A total of 320 contiguous slices were obtained, with a slice thickness of 0.3 mm and no interslice gap. The field of view was 150 × 150 mm. The total scan durations were 7 minutes 30 seconds for the SPGR sequence and 7 minutes 10 seconds for the PDW sequence.

MRI 3D image analysis

Three-dimensional MRI analysis was performed using SYNAPSE Vincent (version 6.8; Fujifilm Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), an advanced image processing platform. The software automatically co-registers SPGR and PDW sequences, enabling integrated image reconstruction. Cartilage segmentation was automatically derived from the SPGR images, whereas bone and meniscal structures were segmented from the PDW images. The platform allows a multidirectional projection of reconstructed 3D models, facilitating the comprehensive visualization of joint morphology. The accuracy of segmentation using this software has been validated previously, demonstrating high concordance with manual segmentation as assessed by dice similarity coefficients. Mean dice similarity coefficients values were 0.985 for the femoral bone, 0.980 for the tibial bone, 0.911 for femoral cartilage, 0.892 for tibial cartilage, 0.916 for the medial meniscus, 0.891 for the lateral meniscus, 0.905 for the femoral subchondral bone region of interest, and 0.888 for the medial and lateral tibial plateau region of interests. These values reflect excellent agreement between automated and manual segmentation across all evaluated structures.

Discussion

This case highlights the usefulness of fully automated 3D MRI for surgical planning and the evaluation of fragment healing in isolated cartilage fractures, and represents an exceptionally rare clinical scenario: an isolated chondral fracture of the lateral femoral condyle in a corticosteroid-treated pediatric patient, evaluated and monitored using fully automated 3D MRI analysis.

Fully automated 3D MRI analysis differs from conventional 3D reconstruction in that it employs a deep neural network-based algorithm to automatically segment bone, cartilage, and the region of interest without any manual correction. Unlike standard clinical 3D MRI reconstructions, which typically require manual segmentation and are subject to operator-dependent variability, this software provides objective, quantitative measurements of cartilage thickness, volume, and projected cartilage area within approximately 10 minutes [4,6]. The Dice similarity coefficient for automatic segmentation accuracy has been validated at 0.911 for femoral cartilage and 0.892 for tibial cartilage, with an interscan measurement error ranging from 0.03 to 0.11 mm across subregions [5,7]. In the present case, this system enabled precise 3D visualization and accurate measurement of the chondral defect area (216.7 mm2) with vertical, horizontal, and diagonal dimensions of 13.8 mm, 16.2 mm, and 18.2 mm, respectively—measurements that would have been difficult to obtain reliably from conventional 2D or standard 3D MRI alone. Furthermore, postoperative 3D MRI at 5 months demonstrated near-complete restoration of the cartilage surface with an almost smooth cartilage interface, providing objective quantitative evidence of healing that corroborated the clinical recovery. This dual role of fully automated 3D MRI—in both preoperative planning and postoperative evaluation—represents a meaningful advancement over conventional imaging approaches in the management of isolated chondral fractures.

Displaced fragments composed purely of cartilage are rare, reported in only 0.9% of 5233 consecutive knee arthroscopies [3]. The present case is further distinguished by the patient's young age of 11 years, the location on the lateral femoral condyle, and the background of C3G—a combination of clinical factors not previously reported in the literature. Prior reports of isolated chondral fractures have been almost exclusively in otherwise healthy adolescents sustaining sports-related shear injuries [1,2]. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of an isolated chondral fracture evaluated and monitored using fully automated 3D MRI analysis in a pediatric patient receiving long-term corticosteroid therapy.

Isolated chondral fractures, when present, typically cause pain or mechanical symptoms that require surgical intervention to prevent further joint damage. For the diagnosis of this pathology, 2D MRI is routinely essential, allowing surgical planning. However, measuring the size of the cartilage defect is challenging, as it is difficult to accurately assess longitudinal and radial dimensions when the defect is not perfectly square and horizontal relative to the femoral bone. Using 3D MRI analysis software, the chondral defect could be visualized in a fully reconstructed spatial form, allowing its extent and morphology to be clearly appreciated at a glance. Additionally, the software enabled the straightforward and accurate measurement of the cartilage defect area, and the ability of 3D MRI to precisely quantify defect size makes it highly valuable for clinical assessment and optimal surgical planning.

The management of isolated chondral-only fragments has historically been controversial, as traditional biological principles suggest that cartilage-to-bone healing is unreliable due to the avascular nature of cartilage and the limited mitotic activity of chondrocytes. A systematic review demonstrated a favorable return-to-sports rate of 90% in patients with isolated chondral defects of the knee, with bioabsorbable implants being the most commonly employed fixation method [1]. In a case series of 10 young male patients (mean age 14.6 years), no clinical failures were reported at a mean follow-up of 56 months, and all 8 patients who underwent postoperative MRI demonstrated complete healing on MRI [8]. Similarly, healing was achieved in all 10 adolescent patients at a median follow-up of 5 years using bioabsorbable Meniscus Arrows, with all returning to their preinjury level of sport [9]. These findings support the viability of primary fixation of chondral-only fragments—particularly in young patients—as an effective treatment option with promising short- to mid-term outcomes and a relatively low failure rate. In our case, the postoperative course was uneventful; however, careful evaluation of preoperative imaging and clinical findings remains essential for optimal surgical indication and planning.

The successful healing in the present case is consistent with these prior reports and may be attributed to several favorable factors: the young age of the patient, which is associated with high chondrocyte viability and regenerative potential; the acute nature of the injury; anatomical reduction achieved through open surgery following arthroscopic fragment retrieval; and stable fixation using 6 bioabsorbable pins. Regarding fragment size, the defect area of 216.7 mm2 in this case is comparable to fragment sizes reported in prior case series, in which fixation has been shown to be feasible and effective. These studies have consistently demonstrated that larger fragments in young, skeletally immature patients are amenable to successful fixation and healing, largely because the incompletely formed calcified cartilage layer in adolescents creates a mechanically weak cartilage-bone interface prone to shear injury, yet one that retains the biological capacity for healing [8,9].

Regarding the influence of corticosteroid use on cartilage healing, intra-articular corticosteroid injections in juvenile idiopathic arthritis patients have been associated with an increased risk of osteochondral lesions, especially with repetitive use [10]. Nevertheless, osteonecrosis and isolated chondral fractures are distinct pathological entities. In the present case, the isolated chondral fracture was most likely induced by soccer-related trauma. Therefore, no clear association was identified between corticosteroid administration and the occurrence of the chondral injury. The uneventful postoperative course and near-complete restoration of the cartilage surface on MRI at 5 months suggest that the systemic corticosteroid therapy at the doses used in this patient did not adversely affect the healing process.

This case offers several important clinical lessons. First, primary fixation with bioabsorbable implants may be a viable option even in pediatric patients with underlying systemic conditions such as chronic corticosteroid use, provided that the fracture is acute, the fragment is anatomically reducible, and stable fixation is achievable. Second, this case underscores the importance of recognizing isolated chondral fractures as a distinct entity from osteochondral lesions, as their management and biological healing potential differ meaningfully.

This case has several limitations. It is a single case report. This case is limited by the short imaging follow-up period and the absence of functional outcome scores and histological confirmation. Although the patient remained asymptomatic at 3 years, long-term imaging outcomes and OA progression remain unknown. Further prospective studies with longer follow-up are necessary to establish the durability of fixation in pediatric patients with similar background conditions.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that the primary fixation of isolated chondral fractures using bioabsorbable implants can achieve successful healing and a return to sports in pediatric patients. Fully automated 3D MRI provided valuable support for surgical planning and postoperative evaluation.
