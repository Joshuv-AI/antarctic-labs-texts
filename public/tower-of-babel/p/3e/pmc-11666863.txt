Management of vancomycin‐resistant Enterococci and daptomycin‐resistant Enterococci infections in liver transplant recipients in a single academic center
Authors: Aldo Barajas‐Ochoa, Olivia Hess, Tucker Smith, Matthew Ambrosio, Megan Morales, Idris Yakubu, Lora Thomas, David Bruno, Nicole Vissichelli
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionVancomycin‐resistant Enterococci (VRE) infections cause significant morbidity and mortality in liver transplant (LT) recipients. Management is challenging, especially in the setting of daptomycin resistance (DR).MethodsSingle‐center retrospective review of patients who underwent LT between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2022, and developed VRE infections. Descriptive statistics were used and Kaplan‐Meier curves estimated freedom from treatment failure and survival.ResultsForty‐two patients (median age 58; 64% female; 67% white) were included. Alcohol‐related cirrhosis (48%) and metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (31%) were the most common indications for LT, and most were from deceased donors (86%). VRE infection occurred at a median of 21 days after LT, and 16% had known prior VRE colonization. Common infection sites were blood (45%, n = 19), intraabdominal (36%, n = 15), and urine (36%, n = 15). Most were initially treated with daptomycin alone (64%) or in combination with other agents (21%); 7% received linezolid alone. Twelve (29%) developed breakthrough infections during treatment and 11 (26%) had recurrent infections after discontinuation of treatment. All‐cause mortality was 36% (n = 15) at a median of 90 days after VRE infection diagnosis and was nearly twice as high in patients with DR (63%).ConclusionVRE infection in LT recipients relapsed or recurred in over 25%. Mortality was high, especially in cases with DR. More data is needed to establish an optimal treatment approach, particularly for relapse and DR. Keywords: eravacycline, liver transplant, vancomycin‐resistant Enterococci

Liver transplant (LT) recipients are at an increased risk for developing infections due to vancomycin‐resistant Enterococci (VRE). 1 These infections are associated with prolonged hospitalizations and significant morbidity and mortality in LT recipients. 2 VRE can colonize the urinary, gastrointestinal, and respiratory tracts and be part of polymicrobial infections. 2

The treatment of VRE infections is challenging and can be complicated by limited antimicrobial options and the development of antibiotic resistance due to a variety of intrinsic and acquired antibiotic resistance mechanisms, along with genomic plasticity 3 . There are no current guidelines for antibiotic selection and treatment duration for VRE infections except for endocarditis, in which linezolid and daptomycin are the recommended agents. 4 , 5 Linezolid is the only drug that is currently approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of VRE infections, based on a compassionate use program where there was a clinical cure in 63% of solid organ transplant recipients. 6 However, linezolid use is often not feasible due to drug‐drug interactions and the risk of myelosuppression particularly in LT recipients with baseline thrombocytopenia, as well as lactic acidosis and neuropathy with extended use. Daptomycin is used off‐label for the treatment of VRE infections and is considered standard of care 2 . High doses of daptomycin are associated with reduced mortality due to VRE infections and beta‐lactams and aminoglycosides may be used for synergy. 7 , 8 However, daptomycin resistance (DR) can develop which poses a treatment challenge with limited alternative antimicrobial options. 9 , 10 , 11 Eravacycline is a tetracycline derivative with higher serum levels than other tetracyclines that were FDA‐approved for complicated intraabdominal infections in 2018 after demonstrating non‐inferiority to meropenem. 12 Even though it has potent in‐vitro activity, there is a paucity of clinical data describing the use of eravacycline for VRE infections.

There is limited data to guide antibiotic management of VRE, particularly when DR develops or when standard therapy is not effective. It is unclear how novel antibiotics such as eravacycline should be incorporated into clinical practice. Here, we describe the clinical characteristics of LT recipients with VRE infections from a single academic center.

METHODS

This is a single‐center retrospective review study of adult patients who underwent LT at Virginia Commonwealth University Medical Center (VCUMC) between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2022, and developed VRE infections. VCUMC is an 820‐bed tertiary care hospital that performs approximately 160–170 LT and 12 simultaneous liver‐kidney (SLK) transplants per year. Piperacillin‐tazobactam is used as standard perioperative prophylaxis at the time of LT at this institution.

VRE infection was defined as having (1) VRE detected on clinical culture including blood, intraabdominal, peritoneal, deep wound, superficial wound, and urine culture, and (2) signs and symptoms of infection. This was determined by a review of the electronic health records of LT recipients for which a VRE‐positive culture was identified. Enterococci species were identified by matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization‐time of flight and VRE was defined as a species with a vancomycin minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of ≥32 µg/mL or a zone diameter ≤14 mm based on the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) breakpoints. 13

Symptoms that were considered consistent with infection included fever, tachycardia, hypotension, leukocytosis, abdominal pain (for intraabdominal infections), purulence at the wound site (for wound infections), and symptoms of dysuria, urinary frequency or urgency for urinary tract infections. Patients also were only considered to have VRE infection if they had signs and/or symptoms and required treatment of the VRE that was isolated. Patients who had VRE detected on culture but no signs of infection including asymptomatic VRE bacteriuria or positive screening rectal swab for VRE without evidence of an active VRE infection were excluded from the study. Patients with VRE colonization of the respiratory tract were excluded unless an additional infection due to VRE was identified. For patients with multiple VRE infections, the first infection identified within 6 months prior to LT or post‐LT until the end of the assessment period was included. Each record was reviewed by two separate authors (Aldo Barajas‐Ochoa, Olivia Hess, Tucker Smith, and Nicole Vissichelli) to determine study inclusion.

VCUMC clinical microbiology laboratory follows CLSI protocols for antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST). Enterococcus sensitivities were performed using Sensititre (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc). All daptomycin‐resistant results were reflexed to an Etest for final determination. According to CLSI guidelines, DR was defined as MIC ≥ 8 µg/mL and sensitive‐dose dependent for MIC ≤ 4 µg/mL for Enterococcus faecium. Eravacycline resistance was defined as a MIC > 0.125 µg/mL and linezolid resistance was defined as MIC ≥ 8 µg/mL. All AST was performed at VCUMC except eravacycline and omadacycline which were performed at a reference laboratory. 13 VRE screening is performed at the discretion of the treating physician at our institution and is identified by rectal culture using a Remel Spectra VRE medium. High‐dose daptomycin was defined as ≥10 mg/kg/dose)

Time to treatment failure was the primary outcome of this study. This was defined as the time elapsed to the development of breakthrough infection, recurrent infection, or death from the date of diagnosis of VRE infection. For patients with multiple of these outcomes, whichever occurred first was used to define time to treatment failure. Breakthrough infections were defined as worsening infection, as indicated by further positive cultures for VRE while on appropriate VRE‐directed therapy after initial improvement and/or culture clearance. Recurrent infections were defined as a new episode of VRE infection including a new positive culture that occurred after completion of treatment for the initial VRE infection.

Secondary outcomes included the development of the individual components of treatment failure. This included all‐cause mortality, and development of breakthrough and/or recurrent infections, as defined above. Additionally, the emergence of DR and allograft dysfunction were assessed. Allograft dysfunction was defined as an elevated total bilirubin at the end of antibiotic therapy or the development of graft rejection.

Patient data were collected from the electronic medical record using the Redcap Electronic Data Capture electronic data capture tool. Descriptive statistics were used. Categorical variables are described with counts and percentages, and continuous variables are described with medians and ranges. Kaplan Meier curves were used to estimate freedom from treatment failure and survival. R was used for all analyses (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Virginia Commonwealth University.

RESULTS

A total of 464 patients underwent LT during the study period, including 19 SLK transplants. Forty‐nine of these patients had cultures positive for VRE, 42 of which were treated for VRE infection and were included in the study (Table 1). The most common LT indications were alcohol‐related cirrhosis (48%) and metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (31%); the median Model for End‐Stage Liver Disease score at LT was 32. Most LT were from deceased donors (n = 36, 86%) and 25% of the cohort had SLK transplants. Reoperation after index transplant occurred in 18 (43%) patients, most commonly for bleeding (n = 13). Before patients were diagnosed with VRE infection, VRE screening was performed in seven (17%), and five of these patients were confirmed to have VRE colonization. In most cases, screening was performed in patients to help direct empiric antimicrobial therapy in patients with risk factors for VRE and persistent signs of infection with negative cultures and workups. Of those with positive results, four were given empiric treatment using VRE‐active antimicrobials while awaiting further workup. Three patients had VRE screens performed prior to LT and in the two patients with positive results, daptomycin was given perioperatively. Those with negative screens were not given empiric treatment. Two additional patients had a history of VRE colonization in urine that was not previously treated. Within 1 month preceding VRE infection, 27 (64%) were treated for other infections; 31(74%) received vancomycin and six (14%) received daptomycin. The median time from LT to VRE infection was 21 days, and most patients were hospitalized at the time of diagnosis.

The most common positive culture sites were blood (n = 19, 45%), intraabdominal (n = 15, 35%), and urine (n = 15, 35%) (Table 1). Thirteen (31%) patients had drainage for source control during initial infection including interventional radiology guided (n = 3) or surgical (n = 10) drainage and 5 required biliary interventions. Patients received a median of 19 days of treatment and those with breakthrough infections received a median of 63 days of treatment.

Description of management strategies

For the treatment of initial infections, daptomycin alone (64%) or in combination (21%) was the most common initial regimen, while linezolid alone was the initial regimen for 7% (Appendix 1). Initial daptomycin doses were at least 8 mg/kg in 89% of patients and 10 mg/kg in 33%. The management of breakthrough and recurrent infections is outlined below. Eravacycline was used in 16 (38%) patients mostly in the setting of breakthrough infection (n = 10) or daptomycin MIC ≥ 4 µg/mL (n = 15, 94%), including six patients with DR (Table 2). It was used as monotherapy (n = 5) or in combination with other antibiotics (n = 11) which were de‐escalated to eravacycline alone in five patients after a median of 17 days (Appendix 2). Fifty percent of patients were successfully treated. Eight patients who received eravacycline died; five were receiving eravacycline four had allograft dysfunction at the time of death, and two of these patients were unable to clear their cultures. Eravacycline was discontinued prior to treatment completion in two patients, either to convert to an oral regimen before discharge or to transition back to daptomycin once susceptibility was confirmed. Other agents that were used included fosfomycin (n = 2) and nitrofurantoin (n = 1) for the treatment of urinary tract infections. Omadacycline (n = 1) and tigecycline (n = 1) were also used to de‐escalate or simplify initial regimens (Appendix 2).

Time to treatment failure

Treatment failure occurred at a median of 24 days from diagnosis of initial infection (Table 3). Intraabdominal infections trended toward earlier treatment failure compared to other infection types, although comparative analysis could not be performed due to the small sample size (Figure 1A). Breakthrough infections occurred in 12 (29%) patients at a median of 17 days from VRE diagnosis and most occurred in patients with initial positive intraabdominal (n = 7) and/or blood cultures (n = 5). After completion of treatment, 11 (26%) had recurrent VRE isolated on culture. Seven of these patients had signs or symptoms of infection and were treated, most commonly in patients with initial positive urine (n = 6) cultures. Four of these infections required source control after recurrent infection was diagnosed including drainage of fluid collections (n = 2), reoperation (n = 1), and removal of urinary stent (n = 1).

Breakthrough and recurrent infections

Most breakthrough infections occurred in patients with intraabdominal sources of infection (n = 8) and/or bacteremia (n = 5). Most of these patients required interventions to address source control: three underwent percutaneous drainage of fluid collections and four had surgical interventions. Of the 12 patients who developed breakthrough infections, six received combination therapy when they were first diagnosed with VRE infections. Once breakthrough infections were diagnosed, they were initially treated with regimens including eravacycline (n = 12, nine as part of a combination) or daptomycin (n = 6, all as part of a combination). Some of these were changed to final regimens including eravacycline (n = 11, 4 as part of a combination) or daptomycin (n = 5, four as part of a combination). DR was identified in 33% of breakthrough cases and half subsequently died.

After treatment was completed, 11 subjects had recurrent cultures positive for VRE. However, only seven of these patients exhibited signs and/or symptoms of infections and were treated. There were three second recurrences that were treated. Of these recurrent infections, the majority were treated with regimens containing daptomycin (n = 8, two as part of a combination).

Daptomycin resistance

Daptomycin resistance (DR) developed in eight (19%) patients. Most patients with DR were admitted in the ICU (75%) at the time of VRE diagnosis with initial VRE diagnosis a median of 16 days from LT (range 1–90 days). Six (75%) required re‐operation after the index transplant surgery. All received vancomycin within the month preceding the initial VRE diagnosis, but only three (38%) received daptomycin (Table 1). Most patients had initial intraabdominal (n = 5) and/or bloodstream infections (n = 4) and 50% had surgical interventions for source control. All patients initially had daptomycin‐susceptible organisms and developed DR after a median of 34 days (range 4, 73) of daptomycin use, either prior to culture clearance (1/8), at breakthrough (4/8), or recurrence (3/8). Three patients did not have adequate source control at the time DR developed. All isolates were susceptible to linezolid, eravacycline, and omadacycline for those with AST available. Six were initially treated with eravacycline (5 as part of combination therapy). These treatments were ultimately adjusted due to breakthrough, recurrence, or simplification, resulting in final treatment regimens including eravacycline in six subjects (n = 6, one as part of combination therapy).

Mortality outcomes

All‐cause mortality was 36% (n = 15) with a median time to death from VRE diagnosis of 90 days (range 6–431 days) (Table 3). Fifty percent (n = 6) of patients with breakthrough infections died at a median of 58 days from initial VRE diagnosis and 45% (n = 5) of patients with recurrent infections died at a median of 135 days from initial VRE diagnosis. The highest mortality was seen in patients with DR (n = 5, 63%) although no statistical comparison was made due to small sample sizes (Figure 1B).

DISCUSSION

VRE infection is associated with high morbidity and mortality in LT recipients. DR enterococcal infections are becoming increasingly recognized, reported in up to 35% of LT recipients with VRE infection, and contribute to significant morbidity and mortality in this population. 9 , 10 , 14 In this cohort, 19% of patients developed DR after initially receiving daptomycin and commonly in the setting of inadequate source control. Treatment is challenging, especially for DR infections given the limited antimicrobial options and lack of data to guide management. Eravacycline is a novel antibiotic with activity against VRE, however, its optimal place in the clinical practice algorithm for the treatment of VRE is unclear. This study presents a single‐center experience of managing infections caused by VRE, including DR‐VRE in patients after LT. Additionally, it explores the use of eravacycline in this specific population.

In this cohort, most patients were treated with daptomycin alone, which is considered standard treatment for VRE infections. However, there was an overall high rate of breakthrough infections (29%) and development of DR despite the use of high doses of daptomycin and combination therapy. Identification of infection sources and attaining source control are vital for optimal resolution of infection. In this cohort, inadequate early source control likely played a key role in the development of resistance and progressive infections. Distinguishing between seromas or hematomas from abscesses or bilomas in the immediate post‐operative period may be challenging without drainage of the collection, which is not always feasible in the early post‐LT period.

Linezolid is currently the only FDA‐approved drug for the treatment of VRE infections, yet it was used to treat only a few patients in this cohort. It is difficult to retrospectively determine the rationale for the low use of linezolid, but it is suspected that this was due to the need for prolonged courses, particularly for intraabdominal infections, and the risk for side effects with prolonged use including myelosuppression in a population with baseline thrombocytopenia and drug‐drug interactions with other commonly used medications after LT. 2 , 15 While Linezolid was approved for the treatment of VRE based on its compassionate use programs, only 50 LT recipients were included in these programs. Among these, only 63% achieved clinical cure and 42% died from VRE infections. 6 Therefore, the optimal treatment strategies for VRE infections in this population remain unknown.

Eravacycline has potent in vitro activity against VRE and was FDA‐approved in 2018 for the treatment of complicated intraabdominal infections based on the results of the Investigating Gram‐Negative Infections Treated with Eravacycline (IGNITE)1 and IGNITE4 trials. 16 , 17 However, there is limited clinical data describing its clinical use for VRE infections. 12 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 In this cohort, eravacycline was used predominantly for patients with breakthrough infections, including bloodstream, endovascular, and DR infections. Almost all patients with breakthrough infections received initial and definitive antibiotic regimens that included eravacycline, either alone (25% initial regimens; 58% definitive) or in combination with other antibiotics (75% initial regimens; 33% definitive). In patients who received eravacycline, 50% clinical cure was achieved. Several confounding factors may have contributed to the suboptimal cure rates with eravacycline. First, eravacycline was predominantly used as second‐line therapy for patients who already developed progressive or breakthrough infections while on daptomycin, including those with DR infections. These patients likely had prolonged hospitalization and other surgical and medical complications that influenced success. Second, DR alone is known to be associated with higher mortality regardless of subsequent treatment. Finally, the patients with breakthrough infections who were treated with eravacycline also had inadequate source control and required subsequent interventions, which likely influenced the ability to achieve clinical cure. It is unclear how eravacycline would compare to daptomycin if it were used as initial therapy. In this cohort, of the patients who were initially treated with daptomycin only 53% achieved clinical success without breakthrough infection, recurrence, or death within 30 days of completing treatment. It is possible that given the high tissue penetration of eravacycline and the low barrier to resistance of daptomycin, there may be a role for eravacycline in initial treatment, particularly for intraabdominal VRE infections. Further studies will need to be performed to determine the role of eravacycline in the management of VRE infections.

Colonization and infection with VRE is associated with increased mortality in LT recipients ranging from 9% to 48%, with some studies reporting increased odds of death of 4.9 when compared to LT recipients without VRE and increased hazard ratio of death of 2.63 associated with detection of VRE prior to LT. 12 , 23 , 24 DR‐VRE has been reported to occur in about 35% of VRE infections and is associated with high mortality ranging from 43–71%. One study found that LT recipients with DR‐VRE bacteremia were 2.65 times more likely to die within one year than those with daptomycin‐susceptible isolates. 9 , 10 , 11 , 14 In this cohort, the all‐cause mortality was 36%, but 71% for DR‐VRE infections, which is consistent with what has been previously reported. Mortality was also particularly high in patients with breakthrough (50%) and recurrent (54%) infections, however, the small size of these subgroups precluded direct comparative analysis. There were likely other factors contributing to mortality. Most patients developed VRE infections early after LT (median of 21 days from LT to infection) with prolonged hospitalizations including intensive care stays, in addition to being immunosuppressed after transplant. This cohort also had relatively high complication rates with 43% requiring reoperation. At VCUMC, 22% of all LT had planned or unplanned reoperation within 90 days in 2022.

This study was limited to a retrospective study without a control group available to compare outcomes in patients without VRE infections. The small sample size and limited number of patients in subgroups hindered the ability to perform comparative analyses between groups. In addition, the CLSI changed the MIC breakpoints in 2020 which may have introduced variability in AST interpretation by treating clinicians. 13 The lack of established CLSI breakpoints to eravacycline and omadacycline (FDA only has a susceptible category for eravacycline if MIC ≤ 0.06) may make it difficult to standardize therapy approaches. 13 , 25 It is difficult to compare the efficacy of eravacycline to tigecycline or omadacycline given the limited use of these agents in this cohort. Despite these limitations, this study highlights the challenges in managing VRE in LT recipients, potential management strategies, and treatment‐related outcomes.

In summary, VRE infections in LT recipients are associated with high morbidity and mortality, with apparent high adverse effects with recurrent infection, high rates of breakthrough infections despite standard of care therapy, and substantial progression to DR‐VRE. The treatment of VRE‐related infections remains challenging due to the limited availability of therapeutic antibiotics and high rates of resistance. This study data supports eravacycline as a potential antibiotic for the treatment of VRE including those with DR, but further studies are needed to appropriately assess the efficacy and safety compared to other antimicrobials and its role as first‐line versus salvage therapy.
