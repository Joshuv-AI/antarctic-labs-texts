Structure–Property Relationships in Fluorine‐Free Ionic Liquids and Salts Utilising the Acesulfame Anion
Authors: Wern Huay Mah, Craig M Forsyth, Luke A O’Dell, Jennifer M Pringle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIonic liquids and salts are of interest as safer next‐generation electrolytes for energy storage applications due to beneficial properties that can include nonvolatility and nonflammability. However, to overcome environmental concerns associated with perfluorinated anions prevalent in these materials, it is important to develop alternative, fluorine‐free anions. Here, we report the synthesis and characterization of five nonfluorinated salts by pairing the acesulfame anion (ACE‐) with a range of cations: N‐methoxymethyl‐N,N,N‐trimethylammonium (N111(1O1) +), N‐methoxyethyl‐N,N,N‐trimethylammonium (N111(2O1) +), N‐ethyl‐N‐methyloxazolidinium (C2moxa+), N‐ethyl‐N‐methylmorpholinium (C2mmor+), and N‐methyl‐N‐propylpyrrolidinium (C3mpyr+). The thermal, structural, transport and electrical properties of these materials were studied using techniques including differential scanning calorimetry, single crystal X‐ray diffraction, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, pulsed‐field gradient nuclear magnetic resonance and cyclic voltammetry. Discussion of the structure–property relationships in acesulfame‐based salts and their comparison with fluorinated salts with the same cations is reported for the first time, revealing distinct trends that are concluded to be largely a result of the size, conformational freedom, charge delocalisation and the intermolecular interactions of the ACE‐ anion. All acesulfame‐based salts displayed thermal stabilities above battery operational range (>180°C) and wide electrochemical stability windows exceeding 4.9 V, demonstrating their potential as electrolytes for lithium and sodium batteries.

Introduction

The widespread use of energy storage devices with increasing energy demand necessitates the development of safer and more sustainable electrolytes. This is intensified by the use of volatile and flammable organic solvents in electrolytes for secondary batteries, which poses safety risks [1]. In contrast to conventional organic solvents, many ionic liquids (ILs) exhibit negligible vapor pressure and high thermal stability, improving operational safety [2]. Moreover, the chemical structure of ILs can be tuned to result in distinct properties [3], such as high ionic conductivity and wide electrochemical window [4], making them promising solvents for battery electrolytes.

Fluorinated anions such as hexafluorophosphate (PF6 ‐), tetrafluoroborate (BF4 ‐), bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (TFSI‐), and bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (FSI‐) are often used in IL‐based electrolytes due to the established performance of these fluorinated compounds in conventional electrolytes [5]. However, the TFSI‐ anion is listed as a perfluoroalkyl substance, also known as ‘forever chemicals’, due to their resistance against degradation, making them environmentally persistent [6]. As a consequence, these chemicals will accumulate in the environment and organisms, posing a threat to human health and the ecosystem [7]. In addition, the utilization of PF6 ‐ and BF4 ‐ anions is also problematic due to their hydrolytic instability and highly corrosive degradation product, HF, upon exposure to ambient humidity [8]. Hence, strict regulations are being imposed on the use of these fluorinated materials across several regions [9], prompting the search for alternatives.

Given the limitations associated with the use of fluorinated anions alongside increased restrictions on their use, fluorine‐free alternatives are growing more popular. These include borate‐based anions [10, 11], and cyano‐based pseudo‐halides [12]. These anions have shown great promise in electrochemical studies. For instance, Hernandez et al. found that Li‐NMC111 cells using electrolytes based on the lithium bis(oxalato)borate salt provided higher discharge capacity and cycling stability than equivalent electrolytes using the LiPF6 salt [11]. However, the cost and availability of these fluorine‐free anions remain a challenge.

The acesulfame anion (ACE‐), an anion originating from the artificial sweetener potassium acesulfame, is of interest as it is nonfluorinated, has low toxicity [13], and biodegrades in the environment [14]. Moreover, it is widely consumed across the globe, making it cheap and easily accessible. These properties make it a promising candidate for further investigations in electrochemical applications as a fluorine‐free alternative to conventional anions. For instance, the incorporation of the ACE‐ anion in zinc batteries as an additive facilitates the formation of a stable, organic‐rich solid electrolyte interphase that suppresses side reactions, regulates interfacial water structure, and enables uniform Zn2+ deposition, thereby significantly improving reversibility, cycling stability, and battery lifespan across various zinc battery systems [15].

ILs containing the ACE‐ anion have been reported [14, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22], but the study of their application in the electrochemical field is scarce. A wide cyclic voltammogram (≈5 V vs. steel pseudo‐reference) has been reported for butyltrihexylphosphonium acesulfame while investigating its potential as an electrolyte for solar cells [22]. In addition, four acesulfame‐based salts with ammonium, pyrrolidinium, and phosphonium cations were synthesized by our group as an initial evaluation of their potential applications in lithium and sodium batteries [23]. Amongst the salts synthesized, the [N1222][ACE] (where [N1222]+ is the N,N,N‐triethyl‐N‐methylammonium cation) exhibited the highest ionic conductivity of 1.4 × 10−4 S cm−1 at 30°C and an electrochemical window between −1.5 and 2.4 V (vs. Fc/Fc+, 60°C). Preliminary electrochemical studies also show that the acesulfame‐based salt could support lithium and sodium cycling at 0.1 mA cm−1 when immobilised on polyethylene oxide with the corresponding lithium or sodium salt at 70°C. These results highlight that acesulfame‐based ILs show promising electrochemical properties which are beneficial for energy storage applications. However, knowledge of the structure–property relationships in acesulfame‐based ILs, which can aid the design of high‐performance ionic fluorine‐free electrolytes, is scarce, highlighting the need to understand the influence of the ACE‐ anion on the structural, thermal, and transport properties of the materials and how they compare to fluorinated salts with the same cation.

This study reports the synthesis and characterisation of five novel salts utilising the [ACE]‐ anion paired with N‐methoxymethyl‐N,N,N‐trimethylammonium ([N111(1O1)]+), N‐methoxyethyl‐N,N,N‐trimethylammonium ([N111(2O1)]+), N‐ethyl‐N‐methyloxazolidinium ([C2moxa]+), N‐ethyl‐N‐methylmorpholinium ([C2mmor]+), and N‐methyl‐N‐propylpyrrolidinium ([C3mpyr]+) cations (Figure 1). These cations were chosen due to previously reported favourable performance when paired with fluorinated anions, discussed below. Where possible, a halide‐free synthetic procedure (using the tosylate intermediate) [24] was implemented to mitigate the adverse effects of halide impurities on the electrochemical stability [25]. Three solids and two room‐temperature ILs were obtained. The thermal and transport properties of all five salts were assessed and pulsed field gradient NMR was employed to probe the ionic mobilities in the materials. Single crystal and Hirshfield surface analyses were carried out on [C2mmor][ACE], [C3[ACE], and [N111(1O1)][ACE] to elucidate the ion packing and intermolecular interactions within the crystal lattice. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed to determine the electrochemical stability of the acesulfame‐based ILs. Detailed discussion on the structure–property relationships in acesulfame‐based salts and their comparison with fluorinated salts with the same cations is reported for the first time, revealing distinct trends largely dependent on the size, conformational freedom, charge delocalisation, and the intermolecular interactions of the ACE‐ anion. The effect of side chain length and ether functionalisation of the cation is also discussed. All acesulfame‐based salts show high thermal stability and wide electrochemical windows, demonstrating their potential for future application as electrolytes in batteries.

Methodology

Materials and Methods

Trimethylamine (31–35 wt. % in ethanol, 4.2 M, containing toluene as stabilizer, Sigma Aldrich), 2‐bromoethyl methyl ether (85%, Sigma Aldrich), 4‐methylmorpholine (99%, Sigma Aldrich), bromoethane (98%, Sigma Aldrich), N‐methylpyrrolidine (97%, Sigma Aldrich), 1‐bromopropane (99%, Sigma Aldrich), N‐methoxymethyl‐N,N,N‐trimethylammonium tosylate ([N111(1O1)][OTs], >97%, Boron Molecular), N‐ethyl‐N‐methyloxazolidinium tosylate ([C2moxa][OTs], >97%, Boron Molecular), potassium acesulfame (99%, Sigma Aldrich), acetone (>99.5%, CSA Scientific), acetonitrile (>99.5%, Sigma Aldrich), ethyl acetate (>99.5%, CSA Scientific), and diethyl ether (>99.5%, stabilized with BHT, RCI Labscan). Acetonitrile, diethyl ether, and ethyl acetate were dried with 3 Angstroms molecular sieves before using them to grow single crystals. All other chemicals were used as purchased.

Purity Analysis

Liquid‐state 1H and 13C NMR spectra were obtained using Bruker Avance Neo Nanobay spectrometer (400 and 100 MHz, respectively) using either deuterated water or dimethylsulfoxide, which showed no impurity peaks in the synthesized acesulfame‐based salts. Mass spectroscopy was performed on an Agilent 1200 series HPLC system.

The residual bromide content was quantified using the ion selective electrode. Ionode IJ‐Br electrode and SMARTCHEM‐ION3 analyser were used. Calibrations using standard solutions (1, 10, and 100 ppm) made using potassium bromide were done before measurement. 1 M KNO3 was added to the standard solutions and the sample to act as an ionic strength adjuster.

The amount of potassium was measured using an inductively coupled plasma‐mass spectrometer (NexION 350X, PerkinElmer, USA). Internal standards Sc (200 ppb) and Rh (20 ppb) in 1% aqua regia were used for correction of matrix effects. The internal standard solution was mixed prior to the nebulizer using a T‐piece in a 1:1 ratio. Calibration standards for potassium (PerkinElmer, potassium standard 1000 ppm in 2% HNO3) were prepared at 0.1, 1, 10, 50, 100, and 500 ppb with 2% suprapur HNO3 in each. The mass spectrometer was operated in kinetic energy discrimination mode with 50 ms dwell times, 20 sweeps, one reading and three replicates. The plasma source conditions were: nebulizer gas flow 1.02 L min−1, auxiliary gas flow 1.2 L min−1, plasma gas flow 15 L min−1, and ICP RF power 1500 W. Data analysis was carried out using Syngistix (PerkinElmer) software. Signal responses were normalised to the scandium internal standard.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry

The thermal behaviour of the samples was analysed using a NETZSCH 214 Polyma at a scan rate of 10°C min−1 for three heating and cooling cycles. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) samples were prepared by sealing 5–10 mg of sample in an aluminium pan in a glovebox. Data from the first heating cycle is reported. Prior to DSC measurements, the melting point of samples was determined visually using a Gallenkamp visual melting point device.

Thermogravimetric Analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) analysis was performed using a NETZSCH TG 209 F1 Libra between 30°C and 450°C under Argon (flow rate: 40 mL min−1), at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1. The samples were prepared under inert atmosphere using open‐lid platinum pans (sample size: 5–10 mg). The thermal decomposition temperature is defined as the temperature at which each sample displayed 5% weight loss after 100°C.

Viscosity and Density

The viscosity of [N111(2O1)][ACE] and [C2moxa][ACE] was examined using a rolling‐ball viscometer (Lovis 2000 M/ME, Anton Paar) at temperature range between 25°C and 80°C at 5°C intervals using a 10 mm long capillary tube (Ø 2.5 mm diameter) with automatic tilted angle. The densities of ILs [N111(2O1)][ACE] and [C2moxa][ACE] were determined using a density meter (DMA4500M, Anton Paar) at temperatures between 25°C and 80°C at 5°C intervals. The ILs were directly injected into the device using a 1 mL syringe. The error was estimated to be ±0.001 g cm−1.

Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy

The ionic conductivity was determined via electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) performed using a Biologic MTZ‐35 Solartron Modulab at temperatures between 30°C and 80°C for all samples except [C2mmor][ACE], which was measured between 30°C and 100°C. All samples were equilibrated for 20 min per temperature and three heating and cooling cycles were performed per sample. A Eurotherm 2204 temperature controller was connected to the MTZ‐35 to control sample temperature during measurements. The samples were measured in a dip‐cell, which measures the conductivity via two platinum wire electrodes, loaded into the cell in a glove box. The cell constant of the dip‐cell was determined using 0.01 M KCl solution at 30°C. Data were collected over frequency range between 1 and 1 × 107 Hz at a signal voltage of 0.1 V and analysed on the MT‐Lab software.

Solid‐State NMR

A Bruker Avance III 300 WB NMR spectrometer equipped with a 5 mm HF/X double resonance probe was used for variable temperature static solid‐state 1H NMR measurements at 300 MHz. Samples were prepared in an Ar‐filled glove box by packing them into a 4 mm magic angle spinning rotor then placing the rotor in a 5 mm glass tube. The 1H spectra were recorded with a single pulse excitation. The frequency scales are referenced to the 1H NMR signal of tetramethylsilane (0 kHz). Results were analysed using Topspin and DMfit software.

Pulsed Field Gradient NMR

The diffusivities of cations and anions of [C2moxa][ACE] and [N111(2O1)][ACE] were determined using 1H pulsed‐field gradient double‐stimulated echo (PFG‐DSTE) NMR while 1H pulsed‐field gradient stimulated echo (PFG‐STE) NMR was used for the remaining salts. Samples were prepared in an Ar‐filled glove box by packing them into a 4 mm magic angle spinning rotor then placing the rotor in a 5 mm glass tube. The measurements were performed using Bruker Avance III 300 WB NMR spectrometer equipped with a Diff50 probe. The data were recorded at 25°C and every 10°C between 30°C and 100°C (with a minimum equilibration time of 5 min before each measurement). The pulse sequence was utilised with 8 scans at each gradient strength. The diffusion time was 25 ms, gradient duration was 2 ms and the maximum gradient strength was 2800 G cm−1. The signal attenuation profiles were fitted to Stejskal–Tanner equation on Topspin to obtain diffusion coefficients.

X‐Ray Crystallography

Single crystals of [C3mpyr][ACE], [C2mmor][ACE], [N111(1O1)][ACE] were obtained using ≈15 mg of the samples. All salts were dissolved in a minimal amount of anhydrous acetonitrile in a test tube in a 50°C water bath. Then the test tubes were placed in a larger glass vessel filled with 1 mL of diethyl ether for [C3mpyr][ACE] and [C2mmor][ACE], while 1 mL of ethyl acetate was used for [N111(1O1)][ACE]. The glass vessels were filled with argon and sealed before placing them in the fridge. The single crystals were obtained within 2 weeks.

A representative colourless thin plate/needle crystal (dimensions [C3mpyr][ACE]: 0.097 × 0.054 × 0.050 mm; [C2mmor][ACE]: 0.254 × 0.114 × 0.098 mm; [N111(1O1)][ACE]: 0.300 × 0.200 × 0.030 mm) was selected and mounted on a nylon cryoloop. Diffraction data were collected at 123 K (−150°C) using CuKα radiation (λ = 1.54184 Å) on a Rigaku Synergy S diffractometer fitted with a HYPIX 6000 hybrid photon counting detector. Data were collected and processed, including an empirical (multiscan) absorption correction, with the CrysAlisPro software. The structure was solved and refined by standard methods using the SHELX software suite in conjunction with the Olex2 graphical interface [26, 27]. Nonhydrogen atoms were refined with anisotropic displacement ellipsoids and hydrogen atoms attached to carbon were placed in calculated positions using a riding model.

Cyclic Voltammetry

CV experiments were conducted using a 3‐electrode cell at 25°C in an Ar‐filled glovebox using a Biologic SP‐200 potentiostat. A 3 mm glassy carbon (GC) electrode, a platinum coil, and a platinum wire were used as the working, counter, and reference electrode, respectively. The GC electrode was polished with 0.3 m alumina and water before each scan in the cathodic or anodic direction. The platinum wires were cleaned with a flame. Ferrocene was used to normalise the reference potential versus Fc/Fc+.

Synthetic Procedures

N‐Methyl‐N‐Propylpyrrolidinium Bromide, [C3mpyr][Br]

N‐methylpyrrolidine (5.11 mL, 48.0 mmol) was dissolved in acetonitrile. Then, 1‐bromopropane (4.82 mL, 52.9 mmol) was mixed with acetonitrile and added into the solution dropwise at 0°C. After stirring the solution under argon at 30°C for 1 day then cooling to room temperature, ethyl acetate was added dropwise to precipitate the product. The solid product was separated by filtration under argon. The filtrate was recovered and concentrated using a rotary evaporator, followed by addition of ethyl acetate. The white solid formed was again isolated via filtration under argon. All solids obtained were dried overnight under vacuum on a Schlenk line. [C3mpyr][Br] forms as a white hygroscopic solid (Yield: 95%). 1H‐NMR (Solvent: D2O): 0.99 ppm (t, NCH2CH2CH3, 3H), 1.84 ppm (m, NCH2CH2CH3, 2H), 2.28 ppm (m, NCH2CH2, 4H), 3.06 ppm (s, NCH3, 3H), 3.31 ppm (m, NCH2CH2CH3, 2H), 3.52 ppm (m, NCH2CH2, 4H).

N‐Ethyl‐N‐Methylmorpholinium Bromide, [C2mmor][Br]

Bromoethane (3.88 mL, 52.9 mmol) was added dropwise into a round bottom flask containing 4‐methylmorpholine (5.23 mL, 48.1 mmol) and acetonitrile at 50°C. The mixture was refluxed at 75°C for 7 h, then continuously stirred at room temperature for another day. The supernatant was removed via filtration under argon, leaving a white solid. More product was recovered by adding ethyl acetate dropwise into supernatant, producing white solid that was also isolated by filtration under argon. Both portions of solids were combined and dried overnight at 50°C under vacuum on a Schlenk line. [C2mmor][Br] forms as a white solid (Yield: 66%). 1H‐NMR (Solvent: (CD3)2SO): 1.25 ppm (t, NCH2CH3, 3H), 3.08 ppm (s, NCH3, 3H), 3.38 ppm (t, NCH2CH2O, 4H), 3.50 ppm (q, NCH2CH3, 2H), 3.91 ppm (s, NCH2CH2O, 4H).

N‐Methoxyethyl‐N, N, N‐Trimethylammonium Bromide, [N111(2O1)][Br]

2‐bromoethyl methyl ether (2.37 mL, 25.2 mmol) was added dropwise into a solution containing trimethylamine (7.21 mL, 30.3 mmol) and ethanol. The solution was heated under reflux at 30°C. After 5 days, the solvent was removed using a rotary evaporator. The residual solid was purified via recrystallization using acetonitrile and ethyl acetate. The product was dried overnight under vacuum. [N111(2O1)][Br] forms as a white solid (Yield: 81%). 1H‐NMR (Solvent: (CD3)2SO): 3.10 ppm (s, N(CH3)3, 9H), 3.31 ppm (s, NCH2CH2OCH3, 3H), 3.53 ppm (m, NCH2CH2OCH3, 2H), 3.75 ppm (m, NCH2CH2OCH3, 2H).

N‐Methyl‐N‐Propylpyrrolidinium Acesulfame, [C3mpyr][ACE]

[C3mpyr][Br] (3.59 g, 17.2 mmol) was mixed into a solution containing potassium acesulfame (K‐ACE, 3.81 g, 19.0 mmol) and acetone. The mixture was stirred under argon at room temperature. After 2 days, the mixture was filtered to remove the solid byproduct, and solvent was removed from the filtrate under reduced pressure. The product was dried under vacuum overnight. [C3mpyr][ACE] forms as a hygroscopic, white solid (Yield: 95%). 1H‐NMR (Solvent: (CD3)2CO): 1.01 ppm (t, NCH2CH2CH3, 3H), 1.89 ppm (d, CH = CCH3, 3H), 1.94 ppm (m, NCH2CH2CH3, 2H), 2.30 ppm (t, NCH2CH2, 2H), 3.26 ppm (s, NCH3, 3H), 3.55 ppm (m, NCH2CH2CH3, 2H), 3.75 ppm (t, NCH2CH2, 2H), 5.25 ppm (d, CH = CCH3, 1H).

N‐Ethyl‐N‐Methylmorpholinium Acesulfame, [C2mmor][ACE]

[C2mmor][Br] (3.60 g, 17.1 mmol) was mixed into a solution containing K‐ACE (3.79 g, 18.8 mmol) and acetonitrile. The mixture was stirred under argon at room temperature. After 2 days, the mixture was filtered to remove the solid byproduct, and solvent was removed from the filtrate under reduced pressure. The product was dried under vacuum overnight. [C2mmor][ACE] forms as a hygroscopic transparent crystal (Yield: 66%). 1H‐NMR (Solvent: (CD3)2SO): 1.25 ppm (t, NCH2CH3, 3H), 1.89 ppm (d, CH = CCH3, 3H), 3.10 ppm (s, NCH3, 3H), 3.39 ppm (t, OCH2CH2, 2H), 3.51 ppm (m, NCH2CH3, 2H), 3.91 ppm (d, OCH2CH2, 2H), 5.26 ppm (d, CH = CCH3, 1H).

Results and Discussion

Cation Selection

As the acesulfame‐based salts in this study are investigated as potential electrolytes for lithium and sodium batteries, it is important to incorporate cations that possess favourable characteristics for electrochemical applications. The [C3mpyr]+ cation was chosen to serve as a reference to compare the acesulfame‐based salt with the well‐studied fluorinated IL, [C3mpyr][FSI], which possesses beneficial characteristics for electrochemical applications such as high metal salt solubility [31], and its ability to form stable solid–electrolyte interface that helps support stable lithium and sodium cycling [32, 33].

On the other hand, the highly flexible ether side chains in [N111(1O1)]+ and [N111(2O1)]+ are believed to contribute to increased conformational freedom, in turn reducing viscosity and enhancing ionic conductivity [30]. Furthermore, both methoxymethyl and methoxyethyl functionalities can help promote Li+ and/or Na+ ion dissociation and suppress formation of metal‐anion complexes [30, 34]. It is hypothesized that the lone pair on the oxygen atom in the alkoxy group can act as a Lewis base and compete with the anion to solvate Li+ [35]. As a result, the interaction between the anion and Li+ can be weakened (lowered average solvation number of anions per Li+ ion) [36]. This, coupled with reduced viscosity, can improve the mobility and transference number of Li+ ions, subsequently enhancing battery performance [37, 38]. Apart from that, ILs utilising cyclic ether cations [C2moxa]+ and [C2mmor]+ also exhibit improved transport properties, such as an order of magnitude increase in ionic conductivity for [C2moxa][TFSI] and [C2mmor][TFSI] (1.0 × 10−7 and 2.0 × 10−7 S cm−1, respectively) [39, 40], when compared to their pyrrolidinium and piperidinium analogues (3.6 × 10−8 and 2.0 × 10−8 S cm−1, respectively) [24, 41].

The thermal behaviour of neat ILs can be broadly classified into three types, depending on their kinetics under different measurement conditions (e.g., cooling and heating rate). The first type presents a cold crystallization before melt. This thermal behaviour was not observed by the acesulfame‐based ILs studied here but has been reported when ACE‐ was paired with other pyrrolidinium‐based cations [23]. The second type is characterized by having only a single glass transition at subambient temperatures, where ILs in the amorphous glassy state below T g turn into a liquid when the T g is exceeded. Two of the newly synthesized ILs, [N111(2O1)][ACE] and [C2moxa][ACE], fall into this category. The final type crystallizes upon cooling and melts upon heating, which is observed for the three remaining salts, [C2mmor][ACE], [C2mpyr][ACE], and [N111(1O1)][ACE]. However, the melting peak visible in the first heating trace for [C2mmor][ACE] was not reproducible on subsequent cycles, even when using a slower scan rate of 5°C min−1 and annealing at 0°C for 20 min. This indicates that the crystallization kinetics of [C2mmor][ACE] are severely hindered, leading to supercooling of the material in the liquid state. Thus, only a T g at −43°C was observed in subsequent cycles.

Despite having a melting transition, ILs exhibiting the third type of thermal behaviour are distinct from the solid‐state analogues of ILs, organic ionic plastic crystals (OIPCs). Solid–solid phase transitions often found in OIPCs are absent in [C2mmor][ACE], [C3mpyr][ACE], and [N111(1O1)][ACE], implying a lack of disorder in these salts, leading to ΔS f values between 54 and 75 J K−1 mol−1. Thus, these new salts cannot be classified as OIPCs. Interestingly, OIPCs with ΔS f values below 20 J K−1 mol−1 often form when [C2mmor]+ and [N111(1O1)]+ are paired with fluorinated anions such as TFSI‐, FSI‐, BF4 ‐, and/or PF6 ‐ [39, 42], while for [C3mpyr]+, room‐temperature ILs are typically observed [43]. This suggests that the interactions between the ACE‐ anion and the cations may have restricted the conformational freedom of the cations within the lattice. In addition, the conformational freedom of the ACE‐ anion itself could be more constrained as compared to the fluorinated anions, which might be attributed to the difference in anion structure: ACE‐ has a rigid cyclic structure, while FSI‐ and TFSI‐ have acyclic structures and can be disordered over different conformations [44, 45], and spherical PF6 ‐ and BF4 ‐ can exhibit rotational disorder [46].

A deviation from the usual trend in thermal properties with ion structure was observed in [C3mpyr][ACE]. Generally, side chain elongation from methyl to butyl in pyrrolidinium‐based cations leads to a decrease in melting point in ionic salts as the increasing chain flexibility leads to crystal lattice disruption [47, 48]. In contrast, the melting points of ACE‐ salts displayed the opposite trend, where the melting point of [C3mpyr][ACE] (T m: 63°C) with a longer side chain length was higher than the ethyl‐substituted [C2mpyr][ACE] (T m: 51°C) [23]. The impact of cation symmetry on their thermal properties was also different from expected: the melting point of the acesulfame salt containing the N, N‐diethylpyrrolidinium cation, [C2epyr][ACE], was 4°C lower than [C3mpyr][ACE], which has a less symmetrical cation. Conversely, the melting points of the analogues containing the FSI‐ anion, [C2epyr][FSI] and [C3mpyr][FSI], are 131°C and −9°C, respectively [49, 50]. This suggests that the thermal properties of these materials are not just influenced by the symmetry and side chain length of the cation, but also affected by the anion structure and their interactions with the cations, which results in a change in crystal lattice and melting temperatures.

[N111(1O1)][ACE] displayed the lowest melting point among the solid acesulfame‐based salts, at 51°C. This is lower than for [N1222][ACE] (Tm: 67°C) reported in previous studies [23]. The decrease in melting point moving from [N1222][ACE] to [N111(1O1)][ACE] was also observed in the FSI‐ analogues [N111(1O1)][FSI] (T m: 78°C) and [N1222][FSI] (T m: 133°C) [42, 46]. With the same cation, [N111(1O1)][TFSI] resulted in a room‐temperature IL (T m: 23°C) [42], possibly due to the higher degree of charge delocalisation in TFSI‐ than ACE‐ leading to decreased interionic interactions. On the other hand, the higher melting point of [N111(1O1)][FSI] (T m: 78°C) [42] compared to its acesulfame‐based analogue might be due to the smaller size of the FSI‐ anion, which could increase the crystal lattice packing.

Compared to [C2mmor][ACE], which has the highest T m of 79°C amongst the synthesized salts, a room‐temperature IL with T g of −67°C is formed when the ACE‐ anion is paired with [C2moxa]+, possibly due to the smaller cationic size of the latter. When paired with both the ACE‐ and fluorinated anions, it is observed that the presence of ether oxygen in the cyclic ring of [C2moxa]+ resulted in a reduction in or absence of melting point compared to their pyrrolidinium analogues. For instance, [C2moxa][ACE] is an IL with T g at −67°C, while its pyrrolidinium analogue, N‐ethyl‐N‐methylpyrrolidinium acesulfame, [C2mpyr][ACE] is a solid with T m at 38°C. This is also observed in fluorinated salts, for instance, the melting point of [C2moxa][FSI] (T m: 127°C) is lower than [C2mpyr][FSI] (T m: 205°C) [40, 51]. These suppressed melting points may arise from increased polarity due to the presence of the ether functionality in the cyclic ring, which increases the repulsive electrostatic interactions between the cations and anions, in turn reducing the melting point [44, 52].

[N111(2O1)][ACE] displays a glass transition temperature at −42°C, higher than another ether ammonium cation‐based salt N‐ethyl‐N‐[2‐(2‐hydroxyethoxy)ethyl]‐N, N‐dimethylammonium ([N112(1O1OH)]+), which possesses a T g at −57°C [14]. However, when the ACE‐ anion is paired with the hydroxylated N‐(2‐hydroxyethyl)‐N, N,N‐trimethylammonium cation ([N111(2OH)]+), a melting point at 25°C was observed instead, likely attributed to the presence of strong hydrogen bonding between the OH group and the ACE‐ [20]. On the other hand, the melt present in [N111(1O1)][ACE] at 51°C is lost when the side chain length is increased in [N111(2O1)][ACE] (T g: −42°C), attributed to the increased flexibility of side chain, which hinders the efficient packing.

The T g of [N111(2O1)][ACE] is 25°C higher than for [C2moxa][ACE], in contrast to the usual trend observed in fluorinated salts. For example, [C2moxa][TFSI] and [C2moxa][FSI] melt at 51°C and 127°C, respectively [40]. These melting points are significantly higher than their corresponding salts containing the [N111(2O1)]+ cation (T m [N111(2O1)][TFSI]: 38°C, [N111(2O1)][FSI]: 34°C) [30]. It is hypothesized that competing polarity and flexibility effects are present in ether‐functionalised cations. Usually flexibility prevails when the oxygen‐containing group is on the side chain, lowering the melting point, whereas the opposite is observed when ethoxy groups are located on the rigid cyclic ring as polarity effects are more dominant [53, 54]. The deviation of acesulfame‐based salts from this trend suggests that the nature of the anion, including its size, flexibility, and interactions with the cation, alters the thermal properties of the salts on top of influences arising from the cation, highlighting the complexity of structure–property relationships in ILs.

The thermal stabilities of the acesulfame‐based ILs were assessed using TGA with a heating rate of 10°C min−1. The decomposition temperatures (T d) at which 5% weight loss is detected are shown in Table 1, while the thermogravimetric traces are given in Figure S2. The T d values increased in the order of [N111(1O1)]+ < [C2moxa]+ < [N111(2O1)]+ < [C2mmor]+ < [C3mpyr]+ in the range of 180°C–250°C. This is within the range of acesulfame‐based salts from other cation families such as ammonium [14, 21], phosphonium [22], and imidazolium [19], suggesting a strong influence of the ACE‐ anion.

The acesulfame‐based ILs synthesized in this study displayed lower thermal stabilities compared to their fluorinated counterparts. Generally, anions that are less nucleophilic and weakly coordinating would improve the thermal stability of ILs [55]. The charge in the ACE‐ is largely localized on the nitrogen atom [20], whereas in fluorinated salts, effective charge delocalisation is achieved through fluorine atoms for the anions such as BF4 ‐ and PF6 ‐ [56], or through the ‐SO2–N–SO2‐ functionality in anions such as TFSI‐ and FSI‐ [35, 57]. Thus, it is expected that the acesulfame‐based ILs will decompose at a lower temperature than ILs with fluorinated anions and the same cation. As an example, [C3mpyr][TFSI] decomposes at 426°C, compared to [C3mpyr][ACE] at 249°C. The same applies for the ether‐functionalised cations as well, where the T d of fluorinated salts with the [C2mmor]+, [C2moxa]+, [N111(2O1)]+, and [N111(1O1)]+ cation lies between 230°C and 380°C [24, 39, 40, 42, 53], compared to their acesulfame‐based counterparts which decompose between 180°C and 235°C.

Amongst the synthesized ILs, [C3mpyr][ACE] with a T d of 249°C displayed the highest thermal stability. This is expected, as [C3mpyr][ACE] is the only cation without an ether functionality, and is consistent with previous reports; for the same TFSI‐ anion, the T d of salts with the [C3mpyr]+ cation was the highest at 426°C [58], compared to salts with ether functionalized [C2mmor]+, [C2moxa]+, [N111(2O1)]+, and [N111(1O1)]+ cations with T d ranging between 291°C and 362°C [39, 40, 42, 53]. On the other hand, the thermal stability of ILs containing ethoxy group is dependent on the proximity of ether oxygen to the charged nitrogen centre. For example, [N111(1O1)][ACE] (Td: 182°C) containing the methoxymethyl (1O1) side chain is more susceptible towards thermal decomposition than [N111(2O1)][ACE] (T d: 226°C) with the longer methoxyethyl (2O1) side chain. This is in line with reported results, where the T d of [N111(2O1)][TFSI] and [N111(2O1)][BF4] were reported to be higher than [N111(1O1)][TFSI] and [N111(1O1)][BF4] (TFSI‐: 382°C vs. 291°C, BF4 ‐: 376°C vs. 270°C) [42, 53]. The low electron density on the H atoms in the ‐CH2‐ spacer of the 1O1 chain, which arises from the synergistic electron‐withdrawing effect of neighbouring electronegative O and N+ atoms, has been suggested to be the cause of the higher susceptibility of cations containing the 1O1 side chain towards thermolysis through nucleophilic attack than the 2O1 side chain [59]. A similar concept may also be applied for cyclic ether cations [C2moxa]+ and [C2mmor]+. Nevertheless, the new acesulfame‐based ILs are thermally stable above 180°C, which is sufficient for most energy applications, including as battery electrolytes.

[C3mpyr][ACE] has a triclinic structure with a space group of P−1. The asymmetric unit is made up of two very similar but distinct ion pairs. The torsion angles found in [C3mpyr]+ cation in both ion pairs were −63.5(2)° and −64.2(2)° for C5‐N2‐C10‐C11 and C28‐N12‐C30‐C31, respectively. At least one hydrogen atom on each carbon atom along the propyl chain in both types of cations is interacting with an ACE‐ anion. These contacts are a mixture of C—H···O (2.450 to 2.540 Å), and C—H···N (2.576 Å). This suggests that the mobility of the propyl side chain in the [C3mpyr]+ cation is constrained. Hough–Troutman et al. have analysed the crystal structure of the long chain hexyldecylpyridinium acesulfame IL, reporting a triclinic crystal structure with space group of P−1, similar to the crystal structure obtained for [C3mpyr][ACE] [18]. The melting point of hexyldecylpyridinium acesulfame (57°C) is comparable with [C3mpyr][ACE] (63°C), though longer alkyl chains, π–π interactions, and shorter C—H···O (< 2.4 Å) interactions may also play a role in the former. On the other hand, the ACE‐ anions form a 1D chain propagating along the a‐axis, stabilised through short C—H···O potential contacts ranging between 2.472 and 2.490 Å. Additional cation–anion interactions result in a 2D network, which presumably stabilizes the structure of [C3mpyr][ACE], contributing to the formation of a solid salt despite having a long propyl side chain, while fluorinated [C3mpyr][FSI] and [C3mpyr][TFSI] are room‐temperature ILs [48].

A triclinic crystal structure with space group P−1 was also observed for [N111(1O1)][ACE], with two unique ion pairs present in an asymmetric unit. The main difference between the ion pairs lies in the cyclic ring of the anion (torsion angle: C11‐O6A‐S2‐N1A: −35.4(9)°, C2‐O3‐S1‐N1: −43.36(16)°). Notably, the anion containing the N1 atom exhibits static disorder at the measured temperature, 123 K. Both the ordered and disordered anions have a total of 8 potential contacts, with the majority being C—H···O contacts. The range of C—H···O bond lengths is similar in both the ordered (2.357 to 2.530 Å) and disordered anion (2.316 to 2.570 Å). Most of the C—H···O contacts of the ordered anion are shorter than 2.5 Å in contrast to the disordered anion, suggesting that the ordered anion interacts more strongly with its neighbouring ions, compared with the disordered anion, discouraging conformational disorder. It should also be noted that there are a large number of cation–anion contacts, but no anion–anion contacts. The single crystal structure of [N111(1O1)][PF6] organic ionic plastic crystal in phase II has been analysed by Warrington et al., reporting a triclinic crystal system and P−1 space group similar to [N111(1O1)][ACE] [42]. However, unlike [N111(1O1)][ACE] which was disordered in the anion, they found disorder in the 1O1 ether side chain of the [N111(1O1)]+ cation of [N111(1O1)][PF6] in the form of bent and linear conformations. In [N111(1O1)][ACE], only the bent conformation is present in the cation, with N‐CH2‐O‐CH3 torsion angles of 106.7(2)° and 102.4(2)° for N4‐C15‐O10‐C14 and N2‐C6‐O5‐C5, respectively. Additionally, the ether oxygen in the methoxylmethyl chain of [N111(1O1)][ACE] interacts with neighbouring anions through C—H···O contact (2.442 Å), while the ether oxygen in [N111(1O1)][PF6] is not bound by any interactions. These observations imply a more restricted conformational freedom in [N111(1O1)][ACE] compared to [N111(1O1)][PF6], resulting in a lack of short‐range disorder in the former.

The structure of [C2mmor][ACE] is monoclinic with space group P21/c. Like [C3mpyr][ACE] and [N111(1O1)][ACE], there are two unique ion pairs in the asymmetric unit. Although all anions adopt a half‐chair conformation, the orientation of the ‐SO2‐ group in space differs, with torsion angles C14‐O8‐S2‐N3 at 39.9(2)° for one anion conformer and C3‐O3‐S1‐N1 at −33.0(1)° for another, leading to the formation of two ion pairs. Only the N3 anion displayed static disorder. Both the ordered and disordered anions have a total of 9 and 8 potential contacts, respectively. These contacts are a mixture of C—H···O (2.350 to 2.580 Å), C—H···N (2.370 to 2.620 Å). A series of iodide crystals have been prepared by Reichert et al. using morpholinium and piperidinium cations, in which stronger cation–cation and cation–anion interactions due to the presence of ether oxygen on the cyclic ring of in [C2mmor]+ as compared to its piperidinium analogue were observed [60]. However, no significant hydrogen bonding is found in [C2mmor][ACE] and its C—H···O contact lengths are comparable to that of [N111(1O1)][ACE] and [C3mpyr][ACE]. Thus, Hirshfield analysis was conducted to provide insights into its higher melting point than the two acesulfame salts.

Hirshfield Surface Analysis

Hirshfield surface analysis enables the visualisation and quantification of the intermolecular interactions within a crystal structure, which is a useful tool for elucidating the thermal behaviour in the acesulfame‐based salts. Generated using the CrystalExplorer software, the Hirshfield surface represents the region where the electron density of a molecule is equal to that of its surrounding environment [61]. A red–white–blue colour scheme is used to indicate the proximity of neighbouring atoms, with red denoting shorter contacts and blue indicating longer distances. The Hirshfield surfaces for acesulfame‐based salts in this study and the OIPC [N111(1O1)][PF6] [42] for comparison, were calculated for two ion pairs to allow good approximation of the overall structure (Figure 6). These show that both the cations and anions in all acesulfame‐based salts have a significant contribution to the intermolecular interactions as the red dots are distributed across both ion species. The effect of the incorporation of the ACE‐ anion may be more evident by comparing salts of the same cation, [N111(1O1)][ACE] and [N111(1O1)][PF6]. More red areas are present in the Hirshfield surface of [N111(1O1)][ACE] than [N111(1O1)][PF6], suggesting closer ion packing in the acesulfame‐based salt, which may lead to a reduction in the rotational and/or translational freedom of ions present in [N111(1O1)][ACE] compared to [N111(1O1)][PF6].

3D Hirshfield surface and fingerprint plots for (A) [C3mpyr][ACE], (B) [C2mmor][ACE], (C) [N111(1O1)][ACE], and (D) [N111(1O1)][PF6].

The Hirshfield surface can be translated into a 2D fingerprint plot with axes representing the distance of internal nuclei (d i) and external nuclei (d e) to the surface (Figure 6). The fingerprint plot is also colour coded depending on the frequency of occurrence of specific contacts, from blue (less frequent) through green and red (more frequent). Thus, this plot can provide information on the fraction of the Hirshfield surface area due to any potential contact and the distances of atoms involved in these contacts from the Hirshfield surface, allowing a rapid comparison between structures. In Figure 6, two light blue spikes representing C—H···O interactions are visible in all the studied acesulfame‐based salts. It is worth noting that the spikes are lighter blue in [C2mmor][ACE] compared to the two other salts, indicating that the C—H···O interactions at a distance shorter than the van der Waals radii are more frequent in [C2mmor][ACE], in line with its higher melting point than the other acesulfame‐based salts. A third, dark blue spike is evident for [N111(1O1)][ACE], resulting from C—H···H interactions between the ‐OCH3 groups of the cation and the ‐CH3 groups of the anion. A greater spread in the range of d i occurs in [N111(1O1)][ACE] compared to [C3mpyr][ACE] and [C2mmor][ACE], suggesting a less dense packing in [N111(1O1)][ACE]. On the other hand, three spikes are displayed in the fingerprint plot of [N111(1O1)][PF6], with the green spikes mainly comprised of C—H···F interactions, and the blue spike made up of C—H···H interactions. The C—H···F interactions occur more frequently in [N111(1O1)][PF6], indicated by the green colour, compared to the C—H···O interactions in [N111(1O1)][ACE]. Moreover, the fluorinated salt exhibits a narrower spread in bond distances than the corresponding acesulfame‐based salt. These observations indicate stronger intermolecular interactions within the [N111(1O1)][PF6] as compared to [N111(1O1)][ACE], which is consistent with the higher melting point of the former (T m: 204°C vs. 51°C).

The fingerprint plots can be decomposed to show which type of atoms are involved in the interactions and their relative percentage contributions. The quantified specific intermolecular interactions are given in Figures S4, S5, S6, and S7. Higher contributions of O···H interactions (total of Hin···Oout and Hout···Oin) were found in salts containing ether functionality; 45% for [N111(1O1)][ACE], 43% for [C2mmor][ACE], and 35% for [C3mpyr][ACE]. On the other hand, the percentage of H···H interactions is the largest in [C3mpyr][ACE] (55%) as compared to [C2mmor][ACE] and [N111(1O1)][ACE] (both at 45%), due to the contribution of nonpolar interactions in the propyl side chain of [C3mpyr][ACE]. The large contribution of O···H interactions in [N111(1O1)][ACE] and H···H interactions in [C3mpyr][ACE] suggests the possible involvement of the side chains of both the [N111(1O1)]+ and [C3mpyr]+ cations in the intermolecular interactions, consequently, a reduction in cation flexibility, consistent with crystallographic data. On the other hand, the fingerprint plot in [N111(1O1)][PF6] is mainly contributed by H···F interactions (68%), followed by H···H interactions (27%).

Combining crystallographic data and Hirshfield surface analysis, it can be concluded that intermolecular interactions are stronger and present at a higher frequency in [N111(1O1)][PF6] compared to [N111(1O1)][ACE], leading to a higher melting point in the former. However, the ions in [N111(1O1)][ACE] are more closely packed than [N111(1O1)][PF6], which may have restricted the motion of the methoxymethyl side chain in the acesulfame‐based salt, leading to a lack of short‐range disorder in [N111(1O1)][ACE] compared to [N111(1O1)][PF6] which is a plastic crystal.

Viscosity and Density

Viscosity is an important parameter for IL electrolytes as it can have a major impact on the diffusion of charged species, in turn limiting the charge–discharge rate in batteries [36, 38, 62]. To study the effect of anion and cation structure on this property, the viscosities of [C2moxa][ACE] and [N111(2O1)][ACE] were measured between 25°C and 80°C (Figure 7A and Table S1).

Temperature‐dependant (A) viscosity and (B) density plots for [N111(2O1)][ACE] and [C2moxa][ACE]. Solid lines represent the fit of data to the VFT equation (Equation (1)).

Compared to analogous ILs with fluorinated anions, the viscosity of [N111(2O1)][ACE] at 30°C (872 cP) is significantly higher than [N111(2O1)][TFSI] (48 cP) and [N111(2O1)][FSI] (33 cP) at the same temperature [30]. The viscosity of other fluorinated ILs containing the 2O1 functionality is also below 300 cP, despite the larger cations. For instance, the viscosities of pyrrolidinium ([C2O1mpyr][TFSI]), piperidinium ([C2O1mpip][TFSI]), and morpholinium ([C2O1mmor][TFSI]) ILs are 52, 70, and 258 cP, respectively [63, 64]. The higher viscosity in [N111(2O1)][ACE] is likely a result of increased rigidity of the anion, coupled with increased intermolecular forces when the ACE‐ anion is used. A four‐fold increase in viscosity was also reported when switching the linear TFSI‐ anion to a more rigid, six‐membered cyclic sulfonylimide anion in imidazolium‐based ILs [65]. Similarly, the viscosity of an oxazolidinium‐based IL containing the larger 4,5‐dicyano‐2‐(trifluoromethyl)imidazolide (TDI‐) anion ([C2moxa][TDI]) is 75 cP higher than [C2moxa][ACE] [66]. However, with the 4,5‐dicyano‐2‐(pentafluoroethyl)imidazolide (PDI‐) anion, the viscosity of [C2moxa][PDI] decreased to 54 cP at 30°C [66], significantly lower than [C2moxa][ACE] (239 cP at 30°C). Lower viscosities with larger anions have been previously reported [67, 68]. This phenomenon was hypothesized to be a result of less electrostatic interactions that are stronger and more associative, and increased dispersive forces that are weaker and nonassociative, as the anion size increases. Thus, the viscosity can decrease upon lengthening the side chain of cations or anions [67, 68]. In addition, the presence of ‐CF2CF3 in PDI‐ may lead to greater conformational freedom compared to ACE‐, in turn reducing the viscosity.

At 30°C, the viscosity of [N111(2O1)][ACE] is 872 cP, while the viscosity of [C2moxa][ACE] is nearly 4 times lower, at 239 cP, despite both ILs having a similar molecular weight (280.1 g/mol for [N111(2O1)][ACE] and 278.1 g/mol for [C2moxa][ACE]). The presence of the ether group in the cation can induce both flexibility and polarity effects, with contributions differing depending on the position of the ether functionality. Increased rotational flexibility has been proposed to lead to less effective assembly of bulky ions and larger free volume for mass transport, in turn lowering the viscosity [35, 53, 69]. In contrast, polarity effects are hypothesized to dominate when the conformational freedom of ether functionalities is restricted, resulting in higher viscosities instead [34, 54]. Philippi et al. [70] have probed the effects of anion rigidity on the viscosity of ILs containing ether on the side chain, revealing that conformational freedom in the anions can significantly affect the structural relaxation of ILs, as well as cation flexibility. These findings suggest that the difference in the effect of cation ethoxy group positions between the ACE‐ ILs and fluorinated ILs may be attributed to differences in structural relaxation and interionic interactions arising from anion rigidity. Further investigations using computer simulations may be helpful to elucidate these competing effects.

As the temperature‐dependant viscosity plot shows a deviation from linear Arrhenius behaviour, the data were fitted with the VFT equation (Equation (1)) [28]. The best fit parameters are listed in Table 3. B 0, η (K) is the temperature dependent pseudo‐activation energy for mobility, which is related to the energy barrier that needs to be overcome for ions to move past each other. T 0 (K) represents the ideal glass transition temperature. The T 0 values are all lower than experimental T g listed in Table 1, which is expected as T 0 is the temperature at which ions start to show mobility. Moreover, plotting log η against T g/T (Figure S9) reveals a similar trend between [N111(2O1)][ACE] and [C2moxa][ACE]. The Angell strength parameter (δ η ), a measure of liquid fragility, is 4.9 and 4.1 for [N111(2O1)][ACE] and [C2moxa][ACE], respectively. These values are comparable to the δ η values reported for other fragile fluorinated and nonfluorinated ILs, which lie between 3.1 and 5.8 [71, 72]. In particular, the δ η value of [N111(2O1)][ACE] (4.9) is similar to [N111(2O1)][TFSI] and [N111(2O1)][FSI] with δ η values reported as 3.5 and 5.5, respectively [30]. A straight line with R 2 exceeding 0.99 can be obtained within a narrow temperature range of 30°C–60°C, allowing fitting to the Arrhenius equation (Figure S8), obtaining the activation energy for ion migration, E a, η within this temperature range (Table 3). The E a,η calculated for both [N111(2O1)][ACE] and [C2moxa][ACE] (52 and 43 kJ mol−1, respectively) were higher than their fluorinated analogues, [N111(2O1)][TFSI] (29 kJ mol−1, temperature range: 25°C–60°C) [30], [N111(2O1)][FSI] (23 kJ mol−1, temperature range: 25°C–60°C) [30], [C2moxa][TDI] (42 kJ mol−1, temperature range: 25°C–50°C) [66], and [C2moxa][PDI] (30 kJ mol−1, temperature range: 25°C–50°C) [66]. This indicates that a higher energy barrier has to be overcome for viscous flow in acesulfame‐based ILs than fluorinated ILs.

At 30°C, the density of [C2moxa][ACE] is 1.27 g cm−3, which is slightly higher than [N111(2O1)][ACE] at 1.25 g cm−3. Prior work has shown that ILs containing cyclic ether cations tend to have higher densities than those with ethoxy groups on the side chain. For instance, TFSI‐based ILs using the N‐butyl‐N‐methylmorpholinium cation and N‐methoxyethyl‐N‐methylpiperidinium cation exhibit densities of 1.439 and 1.420 g cm−3, respectively, at 40°C [64, 73]. The density of [C2moxa][ACE] is similar to other ILs such as [C2moxa][TDI] and [C2moxa][PDI] (both 1.27 g cm−3 at 30°C) [66]. On the other hand, [N111(2O1)][ACE] is generally less dense compared to other fluorinated ILs; at 50°C, the density of [N111(2O1)][ACE] is 1.23 g cm−3, compared to [N111(2O1)][TFSI] 1.43 g cm−3 and [N111(2O1)][FSI] 1.35 g cm−3 [30].

[C2moxa][ACE] and [N111(2O1)][ACE] exhibit high ionic conductivities as they exist in the liquid‐state over the entire measured temperature range. [N111(1O1)][ACE], [C2mmor][ACE] and [C3mpyr][ACE], on the other hand, displayed a premelting behaviour where a significant increase in ionic conductivity is observed near the melting point of the solid salts as they transition from a solid to a liquid.

The highest ionic conductivity of 3.5 × 10−4 S cm−1 at 30°C was displayed by [C2moxa][ACE], closely followed by [N111(2O1)][ACE], which had an ionic conductivity of 3.0 × 10−4 S cm−1 at the same temperature. This corroborates well with the viscosity data, where [N111(2O1)][ACE] with a higher viscosity was less conductive. Ionic conductivities of salts based on the [C2moxa]+ cation have been reported [24, 40, 66], but only those present in the liquid‐state will be used for comparison. The ionic conductivity of fluorinated [C2moxa][FTFSI] (FTFSI‐: trifluoromethanesulfonyl (fluorosulfonyl)imide) is 5.0 × 10−3 S cm−1 at 30°C [40], an order of magnitude higher than [C2moxa][ACE], attributed to the lower T g of [C2moxa][FTFSI] (T g: −103°C) compared to [C2moxa][ACE] (T g: −67°C). When fluorinated Hückel anions are used, the conductivity of [C2moxa][ACE] is in the same order of magnitude as [C2moxa][TDI] with ionic conductivity of 6.3 × 10−4 S cm−1 at 30°C, but lower than [C2moxa][PDI] (1.8 × 10−3 S cm−1, 30°C) [66]. This trend suggests a combined effect of viscosity and more effective charge delocalisation in the Hückel anions than the ACE‐ anion, as the viscosity of these materials decreased in the order of [C2moxa][TDI] > [C2moxa][ACE] > [C2moxa][PDI].

The ionic conductivity of [N111(2O1)][ACE] is also lower than ILs such as [N111(2O1)][TFSI] (3.9 × 10−3 S cm−1, 30°C) and [N111(2O1)][FSI] (7.5 × 10−3 S cm−1, 30°C) [30], [N112(2O1)][BF4] (1.7 × 10−3 S cm−1, 25°C) [53], and [N112(2O1)][TFSI] (3.1 × 10−3 S cm−1, 25°C) [53]. This is likely because of the significantly higher viscosity of [N111(2O1)][ACE] (872 cP) than its fluorinated counterparts for which viscosities mostly lie below 100 cP, except [N112(2O1)][BF4] at 335 cP (all measured at 30°C) [30, 53].

Amongst the solid salts, [N111(1O1)][ACE] exhibits the highest ionic conductivity at 6.1 × 10−6 S cm−1. This is closely followed by [C3mpyr][ACE] with 4.1 × 10−6 S cm−1 at 30°C, while [C2mmor][ACE] displays the lowest ionic conductivity at 4.4 × 10−9 S cm−1 at 30°C. This is likely a result of cation size, where in the same physical state, smaller cations are beneficial for ion transport. For instance, the ionic conductivity of [C4mpyr][TFSI] (2.7 × 10−3 S cm−1, 25°C) with a five‐membered ring is higher than [C4mpip][TFSI] (1.0 × 10−3 S cm−1, 25°C) and [C4maze][TFSI] (0.6 × 10−3 S cm−1, 25°C, aze: azepanium) with a six‐ and seven‐membered ring, respectively [35]. Additionally, decreasing ionic conductivity across [C2moxa][FSI], [C2mpyr][FSI], [C2mmor][FSI], and [C2mpip][FSI] indicates that a larger ring size adversely impacts conductivity despite the presence of ether functionality, possibly due to increasing van der Waals interactions between the bulky ions [24, 35, 39].

A comparison of the ionic conductivities of solid acesulfame‐based salts with those using fluorinated anions in the same physical state reveals notable differences. At 30°C, the ionic conductivity of [N111(1O1)][BF4] (5.0 × 10−6 S cm−1) [42], a solid salt that does not exhibit plasticity, was slightly lower than [N111(1O1)][ACE] (6.1 × 10−6 S cm−1). In contrast, the fluorinated OIPC, [N111(1O1)][PF6] shows significantly lower conductivity (1.4 × 10−8 S cm−1), while [N111(1O1)][FSI] demonstrates higher conductivity (2.1 × 10−5 S cm−1) than [N111(1O1)][ACE] at 30°C [42]. These differences are attributed to their respective thermal phase states: at 30°C, [N111(1O1)][PF6] exists in phase II, characterized by a more ordered lattice structure, whereas [N111(1O1)][FSI] is in phase I, exhibiting greater disorder and enhanced conductivity. A similar trend is observed for [C3mpyr][ACE] and [C3mpyr][BF4] at 40°C. In phase II, [C3mpyr][BF4] has a lower ionic conductivity (8.8 × 10−7 S cm−1) compared to [C3mpyr][ACE] (9.6 × 10−6 S cm−1) [75]. On the other hand, [C2mmor][ACE] (4.4 × 10−9 S cm−1 at 30°C) exhibits lower conductivity than [C2mmor][FSI] and [C2mmor][TFSI], which show conductivities of 6.0 × 10−7 S cm−1 and 1.0 × 10−7 S cm−1 at 30°C, respectively [39]. Both fluorinated salts are in phase I at the measured temperature, contributing to their enhanced ionic conductivity.

Self‐Diffusion Coefficients

Pulsed‐field gradient (PFG) NMR was performed to assess the mobility, or self‐diffusion coefficients, D s, of the individual ionic species present in the new acesulfame‐based salts. This is useful for evaluating the individual contribution of the cationic and anionic species in the salts on the transport properties. The measurements were conducted between 25°C and 80°C for all samples except [C2mmor][ACE], which was measured from 60°C to 80°C due to its comparatively low ionic conductivity below 60°C. The data is presented in Figure 9, while detailed self‐diffusion coefficient values of individual ions and their sum can be found in Table S5.

Comparison of temperature dependant self‐diffusion coefficients between acesulfame‐based salts (A) in the solid state ([N111(1O1)][ACE], [C3mpyr][ACE], [C2mmor][ACE]), and (B) in the liquid state ([N111(2O1)][ACE] and [C2moxa][ACE]) at room temperature.

PFG NMR data (Figure 9) shows that cations diffuse faster than anions in most of the salts, in contrast to many other reported ionic salts [40, 62]. A significant increase in anion mobility is observed above the melting point of the salts that are solid at room temperature. The sum of cationic and anionic diffusion coefficient for the solid acesulfame‐based salts at 60°C decreased in the order of [N111(1O1)][ACE] (2.8 × 10−11 m2 s−1) > [C3mpyr][ACE] (1.9 × 10−11 m2 s−1) > [C2mmor][ACE] (6.7 × 10−13 m2 s−1), consistent with the trend in ionic conductivity. Single crystal XRD measurements also revealed that [C2mmor][ACE] was the only solid acesulfame‐salt within the studied materials containing hydrogen bonding, whilst the ions in [C3mpyr][ACE] and [N111(1O1)][ACE] mainly interact through weaker, dispersive forces. Thus, the stronger intermolecular interactions in [C2mmor][ACE] may hinder the translational ion motions in the salt, leading to lower cationic and anionic diffusion coefficients. Similarly, the slightly higher cationic diffusivity of [C3mpyr][ACE] than [N111(1O1)][ACE] at low temperatures could be related to the abundance of stronger C—H···O interactions between the ion pairs in [N111(1O1)][ACE] compared to in [C3mpyr][ACE], where they are mainly comprised of weaker C—H···H interactions.

The diffusivity of cations and anions in [N111(2O1)][ACE] is very similar to those of [C2moxa][ACE] at each measured temperature. This is consistent with the similar ionic conductivities. Like the viscosity and ionic conductivity data, the data also presented a curved profile (Figure S12), thus, the VFT equation for diffusion coefficient was applied (Equation (1)), where D 0 (m2 s−1) is the diffusion coefficient at infinite temperature, B 0,D (K) is the pseudo‐activation energy, and T 0,D (K) is the ideal glass transition temperature. The best fit values for D 0 (m2 s−1), B 0,D (K), and T 0,D (K) are provided in Table S6. The activation energy for diffusion, E a,Ds for [N111(2O1)][ACE] and [C2moxa][ACE] were calculated by fitting the diffusivity data to the Arrhenius equation in a narrow temperature range of 30°C–60°C (Equation (2) and Figure S13). Comparison between the E a, Ds values for cations and anions in [N111(2O1)][ACE] and [C2moxa][ACE] revealed that the ion species have a larger energy barrier for diffusion in [N111(2O1)][ACE] than [C2moxa][ACE], consistent with higher viscosity and lower ionic conductivity in the former.

Ionicity

An inverse relationship between viscosity and ionic conductivity is often observed when characterising ILs, as viscosity limits the ion mobility, which directly impacts the ionic conductivity. However, lower viscosities do not always translate to higher ionic conductivities, as seen in the liquid‐state materials studied here, [N111(2O1)][ACE] and [C2moxa][ACE], where the ionic conductivities are similar despite [N111(2O1)][ACE] being nearly four times more viscous than [C2moxa][ACE] at 30°C. The discrepancy between viscosity and ionic conductivity suggests that other factors intrinsic to these ILs, such as the degree of ion association/ion aggregation, may be contributing to their transport properties, which can be probed by analysing the ionicity.

At 30°C, the ΔW of [N111(2O1)][ACE] is 0.24, similar to those reported for [N111(2O1)][FSI] and [N111(2O1)][TFSI] (0.27 and 0.29, respectively) [30]. The degree of deviation from the ideal KCl line, or the ionicity, is related to the ion structure and coulombic interactions between ions [35, 76]. Thus, the much larger ΔW and lower ionicity of [C2moxa][ACE] than the other fluorinated ILs implies stronger intermolecular attractive forces between its constituent ions, leading to the formation of neutral long‐lived ion pairs, aggregates or ion networks that reduce the concentration of effective charge carriers for ion conduction.

The ratio of Λimp to ΛNMR (also known as the inverse Haven ratio, I HR) can be used as a measure of ionicity as it correlates to the proportion of ions available for ion conduction amongst all diffusing species within the ILs [76]. The I HR of both [N111(2O1)][ACE] and [C2moxa][ACE] lies below unity, indicating that not all diffusing species contribute to ion conduction. The I HR decreases with temperature from 0.79 and 0.75 at 30°C, to 0.71 and 0.69 at 80°C for [N111(2O1)][ACE] and [C2moxa][ACE], respectively. The decrease in I HR with temperature can be attributed to the formation of aggregates or ion pairing at higher temperatures.

The trend in I HR and those obtained through the Walden plot are consistent; [N111(2O1)][ACE] is more dissociated than [C2moxa][ACE]. The lower ΔW (0.24 vs. 0.73) and higher I HR (0.79 vs. 0.71) of [N111(2O1)][ACE] than [C2moxa][ACE] at 30°C suggest reduced ion pairing or aggregate formation and a greater proportion of ions available for ion conduction in [N111(2O1)][ACE]. This accounts for the comparable ionic conductivities of [N111(2O1)][ACE] and [C2moxa][ACE], despite the significantly higher viscosity of [N111(2O1)][ACE] at 30°C (872 cP as compared to 239 cP for [C2moxa][ACE]). The acesulfame‐based ILs exhibit a greater degree of aggregate formation or ion pairing as compared to their ILs containing fluorinated anions with the same cations, possibly due to the less delocalised charge of the ACE‐ anion as compared to fluorinated anions, thereby reducing the availability of free ions for charge transport. However, the incorporation of ether side chains may be a possible strategy to overcome this limitation, as observed in the comparable ionicity of [N111(2O1)][ACE] and fluorinated [N111(2O1)][TFSI] and [N111(2O1)][FSI].

Electrochemical Stability

The electrochemical stability of ILs can be expressed by the electrochemical window (EW), which is the potential range within which ILs remain stable without undergoing oxidation or reduction. A wide EW is a desirable quality in ILs for electrochemical applications, as it enables their use with high voltage cathodes for high energy density batteries [77]. Figure 11 shows the EWs of the acesulfame‐based salts obtained using CV, while Table S9 provides the values for the reduction (E red) and oxidation potentials (E ox) that define the limits of the EW. All acesulfame salts were tested in acetonitrile (1 g L−1) to allow accurate comparison as the high melting point of some salts prohibited measurements in the molten state. The values E ox and E red were determined based on a cut‐off current density calculated from three times the background current of each material. For [N111(2O1)][ACE], a small anodic peak is observed at ≈1 V versus Fc/Fc+, however, the E ox of this material was recorded at 2.3 V versus Fc/Fc+ as significant decomposition only occurs above this voltage.

The anodic scan (brown) and the cathodic scan (orange) were separately measured, with the working electrode (WE) cleaned and polished between scans. All measurements were conducted at 25°C, at scan rate of 10 mV s−1, with a GC WE, platinum coil counter electrode (CE), and platinum wire reference electrode (RE). All acesulfame‐based salts exhibited wide EWs exceeding 4.9 V under the measured conditions. This range is close to or higher than the upper limit of EWs previously reported for other acesulfame‐based salts [N1222][ACE] (3.9 V) and [P4666][ACE] (5.0 V) [22, 23]. Slightly higher anodic potentials were found for all five acesulfame‐based salts (2.2 to 3.2 V vs. Fc/Fc+) than those previously recorded by pairing the ACE‐ with pyrrolidinium‐ and ammonium‐based cations, which lie between 2.1 and 2.5 V versus Fc/Fc+ [23]. A direct comparison of anodic potentials was possible as all experimental conditions were the same. Particularly, the E ox of [C3mpyr][ACE] was 2.4 V versus Fc/Fc+, which was 0.2 and 0.4 V more positive than [C2mpyr][ACE] and [C2epyr][ACE], respectively [23]. This displays the effect of side chain modification in the cation: increased side chain length could enhance the shielding on the positively‐charge nitrogen centre in the pyrrolidinium cation, reducing the susceptibility of the [C3mpyr]+ cation towards electrochemical degradation. On the other hand, the stability of free radicals generated during the decomposition process also increases upon side chain elongation, making the ethyl side chain a better leaving group than the methyl group [41], which may reduce the electrochemical stability of [C2epyr]+ as compared to [C2mpyr]+. On the other hand, the EWs of [N111(1O1)][ACE] and [N111(2O1)][ACE] were EW: 5.1 and 4.9 V, respectively, which are a bit lower than for [N1222][ACE] (≈5.5 V) [23]. The slightly higher oxidative stability of [N1222][ACE] (E ox: 2.5 V vs. Fc/Fc+) [23] than the ether functionalised [N111(1O1)][ACE] and [N111(2O1)][ACE] (E ox: 2.3 and 2.2 V vs. Fc/Fc+, respectively) could be due to the presence of ether oxygen on the side chains of [N111(1O1)]+ and [N111(2O1)]+ cations in close proximity to the nitrogen centre, which can destabilise the ether‐functionalised cations towards electrochemical degradation [53].

One challenge in the use of fluorine‐free electrolytes could lie in the narrower electrochemical window, as compared to fluorinated systems [78], which would limit their use with high voltage electrode. Interestingly, the electrochemical stability of the acesulfame salts in this study was on par with most fluorinated ILs using the TFSI‐, PF6 ‐, and BF4 ‐ anions, which have EWs lying between 3.0 and 6.5 V [79]. The EWs of the acesulfame‐based salts studied herein were compared with fluorinated salts of the same cation, though varying experimental conditions should be accounted for. [C2mmor][ACE] exhibited the widest EW at 5.5 V amongst the synthesized salts, with an E red of –2.3 V and E ox of 3.2 V, which is ≈1.5 V wider than [C2mmor][TFSI] [39]. However, this could be due to the higher experimental temperature and scan rate used for [C2mmor][TFSI]. On the other hand, [C2moxa][ACE] experiences significant oxidative decomposition from 2.8 V, leading to a slightly narrowed EW of 5.1 V. Nonetheless, its EW was still comparable to [C2moxa][BF4] (4.8 V) [40], and larger than other fluorinated salts with the same cation; [C2moxa][TFSI] (4.3 V) [40], [C2moxa][PDI] (4.2 V) [66], [C2moxa][TDI] (4.0 V) [66], [C2moxa][PF6] (3.7 V) [40], and [C2moxa][FTFSI] (3.7 V) [40].

Initial demonstration of the electrochemical performance of different ACE‐ salts was demonstrated in our prior work using lithium and sodium symmetric cells incorporating solid polymer electrolytes containing the [N1222][ACE] with either lithium or sodium acesulfame immobilised on polyethylene oxide [23]. At 70°C, the lithium cells display a lower overpotential than for sodium at current densities below 0.1 mA cm−2, while sodium cells were more stable than lithium at current densities exceeding 0.1 mA cm−2. Good compatibility of the ACE‐ anion with Li and Na anodes was demonstrated. Coupled with the promising electrochemical stability of the new acesulfame‐based salts in this study, this highlights the potential of these salts for supporting Li and Na electrochemistry and encourages their development as electrolytes for battery applications.

Conclusion

In this work, five new fluorine‐free acesulfame‐based salts were synthesized by pairing the ACE‐ with cations that have shown favourable electrochemical properties when used with fluorinated anions. The thermal properties, ion dynamics, structural, and electrochemical properties of these new salts were characterized to understand the influence of the nonfluorinated ACE‐ alongside the influence of the cation structure and functionalisation.

[C2moxa][ACE] and [N111(2O1)][ACE] formed as room‐temperature ILs while [C2mmor][ACE], [C3mpyr][ACE], and [N111(1O1)][ACE] are solids at room temperature. The trend in melting points across the solid acesulfame salts is consistent with the crystallographic data and Hirshfield surface analysis, both revealing a larger number of short contacts at a shorter intermolecular distance in [C2mmor][ACE] than [C3mpyr][ACE] and [N111(1O1)][ACE]. This is also reflected in the ionic conductivity of these materials, where [N111(1O1)][ACE] (6.1 × 10−6 S cm−1, 30°C) is more conductive than [C3mpyr][ACE] and [C2mmor][ACE] (4.1 × 10−6 S cm−1 and 4.4 × 10−9 S cm−1, respectively, at 30°C) in the solid state. The lack of disorder in the crystal lattice of acesulfame‐based salts, compared with fluorinated salts of the same cations, is due to the closer ion packing and participation of cation side chains in intermolecular interactions in the former, demonstrating the effect of the anion on the structural and thermal properties.

The highest ionic conductivity of 3.5 × 10−4 S cm−1 at 30°C was displayed by [C2moxa][ACE], closely followed by [N111(2O1)][ACE] which had an ionic conductivity of 3.0 × 10−4 S cm−1 at the same temperature, in line with the similar cationic and anionic diffusivities of both ILs measured through PFG NMR, despite the former being four times less viscous possibly due to the higher ionicity of [N111(2O1)][ACE] than [C2moxa][ACE]. Comparable ionicities are found between [N111(2O1)][ACE] and fluorinated ILs such as [N111(2O1)][TFSI] and [N111(2O1)][FSI], demonstrating the favourable influence of ether functionalisation on the cation side chain on the transport properties.

The thermal stabilities of the acesulfame salts, though lower than fluorinated salts with the same cation, are well above standard battery operating temperature: the highest T d is observed for [C3mpyr][ACE] (249°C) and the lowest T d for the [N111(1O1)][ACE] (182°C). The electrochemical windows of all the acesulfame‐based salts exceed 4.9 V in the neat state. This shows that the acesulfame‐based salts in this study exhibit promising thermal and electrochemical stability to encourage further electrochemical investigations for the future use of these salts as electrolytes.

In conclusion, this study reports an initial demonstration of the structure–property relationships in fluorine‐free acesulfame‐based salts, which result in distinct thermal, structural, transport and electrochemical properties, such as increased intermolecular interactions and improved electrochemical stability depending on the salt, compared to when fluorinated anions are used. These findings expand the range of potential fluorine‐free anions for future battery electrolyte applications.
