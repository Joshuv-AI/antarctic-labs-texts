Maternal multimorbidity, lifestyle behaviours during pregnancy, and early-childhood growth trajectories: a register-based cohort study
Authors: Yue Zhang, Xiaoqin Zhu, Gita D. Mishra, Mary McCauley, Parul Puri, Hui Wang, Yangyang Cheng, Zifan Zhang, Yaguan Zhou, Sunyi Wang, Qingyong Zeng, Hongyu Cai, Yipei Zhao, Junjie Lin, Zhiran Guo, Weijie Ding, Xiaolin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe burden of chronic conditions is increasing in women during pregnancy, but maternal multimorbidity and associated long-term effects on children have not been well-studied. This study examines the associations of maternal multimorbidity and lifestyle behaviours with growth trajectories in children.MethodsThis longitudinal study used data of women who gave live birth between July 2020 and June 2021 in Huai'an, China. Information of 15 maternal long-term conditions were extracted from electronic maternity information system, and multimorbidity was defined as presence of two or more maternal conditions. Maternal lifestyle score was constructed based on physical activity, sleep duration and quality, regularity of daily meals, and dietary preference collected at the second trimester of pregnancy. Children's body mass index (BMI) was measured ten times, from birth to 36 months. Latent class growth mixed model was conducted to identify growth trajectories in children using age- and sex-specific BMI z-scores. Adjusted multinomial logistic regression models were used to assess the associations.FindingsAmong 22,440 mother–child pairs, 1288 (5.7%) mothers were screened for multimorbidity during pregnancy. Five growth trajectories were identified during children early-childhood. Children born to mothers with multimorbidity had 79% increased relative risks of experiencing low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain (relative risk ratio [RRR] = 1.79, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.39–2.31). The significantly rapid rate of BMI gain among these children occurred within the first eight months. Certain multimorbidity patterns were particularly associated with a higher relative risk of this growth trajectory, for example, the Cardiometabolic pattern (RRR = 2.63, 95% CI = 2.03–3.40) and the Hepatic-haematological pattern (RRR = 1.79, 95% CI = 1.18–2.71) were associated with a higher relative risk of offspring experiencing low birth weight with accelerate BMI gain. Among 7061 mothers who reported their lifestyle behaviours during pregnancy, 14.6% had unfavourable lifestyles. Children born to mothers with multimorbidity and unfavourable lifestyles were associated with 4.13-fold (1.65–10.3) increased relative risk of low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain.InterpretationMaternal multimorbidity and unfavourable lifestyles during pregnancy are associated with higher risks of children experiencing low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain. The provision of high quality comprehensive integrated antenatal care with healthy lifestyle education and interventions are required for all women and especially for women with multimorbidity, to improve their health and wellbeing during pregnancy and to contribute to health benefits for their infants.FundingChina Medical Board (21-416), 10.13039/501100001809Natural Science Foundation of China (72474197), Zhejiang University Fundamental Research Fund for the Central Universities, Huai’an Science and Technology Program (HAB2024039), Key Medical Program of Jiangsu Health Commission (ZD2021044).

Research in context

Evidence before this study

The burden of long-term health conditions is increasing in women during pregnancy. We searched PubMed and Google Scholar from inception to March 27, 2025 using the terms “maternal multimorbidity” and “maternal chronic conditions”. We found a few studies examined maternal multimorbidity in women of the reproductive age, but most of these studies were conducted in women living in high-income countries, such as the USA and the UK. The burden of maternal multimorbidity for women living in low- and middle-income countries is currently less studied. Moreover, the investigation of health impacts of maternal multimorbidity was confined to short-term birth outcomes, such as preterm birth and low birth weight. Little is known about the long-term health trajectories following maternal multimorbidity for both women and their children.

Added value of this study

Using data of 22,440 mother–child pairs, we examined the burden of maternal multimorbidity in a Chinese population and explored its association with children's growth trajectory tracing from birth to 36 months. 5.7% of women were screened for multimorbidity during pregnancy, and this prevalence is comparable to studies in other Asian countries, including Japan (6.3%) and India (3.5%). Children born to mothers with multimorbidity during pregnancy had 79% increased relative risks of experiencing low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain, and the significantly rapid BMI gain occurred within the first 8 months. We also found that multimorbidity patterns involving hypertensive disorders were particularly associated with a higher relative risk of this growth trajectory. When combining with unfavourable lifestyles during pregnancy, the relative risk of children experiencing the trajectory of low birth weight with accelerate BMI gain further increased.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our study demonstrates evidence on long-term outcomes for children born to mothers with multimorbidity and poor lifestyles. The results implies that there is a need for current research to shift from single maternal chronic condition to their coexistence and to explore their interrelationships and impacts. In clinical practice, holistic comprehensive integrated pre-conception and antenatal care with lifestyle promotion is needed to manage maternal multimorbidity effectively, from which the health benefits will expand to the next generation.

Introduction

Globally, the burden of chronic conditions among women is rising and women during pregnancy are increasingly affected by pre-existing medical conditions that contribute to severe maternal morbidity and mortality.1 In addition, pregnancy-related conditions, such as gestational diabetes mellitus and preeclampsia, have also become more prevalent.2,3 Multimorbidity, defined as the co-existence of two or more chronic conditions,4 has been widely explored in general adult populations and middle- and older-aged populations.4,5 However, current maternal health research often focuses on single medical conditions rather than their combinations.6 Emerging studies have linked pregnancy with multiple health problems with higher risks of adverse obstetric outcomes in short-term, including preterm birth,7, 8, 9 low birth weight,8,9 and maternal mortality.10 Despite this, evidence on the epidemiological patterns of multimorbidity for women during pregnancy remain limited. Until recently, a lack of awareness of maternal multimorbidity has hindered understanding of the impact of multiple long-term conditions on maternal and children's short and long-term health outcomes, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.11,12

Children's growth trajectories are established in utero, persist through early childhood and track into adulthood.13 Previous evidence suggested that rapid postnatal growth following low birth weight, rather than low birth weight itself, is associated with higher risks of obesity, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease in later life,14 highlighting the need of continuous tracing of childhood weight. Associations between specific pregnancy-related conditions and children growth trajectories have been studied,15,16 for example, medical conditions such as hypertensive disorders15 and gestational diabetes,16 are associated with offspring rapid body mass index (BMI) growth and obesity. In addition, several pre-existing chronic conditions, such as immune diseases, have also been linked to increased risk of offspring obesity.17 Beyond examining maternal conditions separately, many pre-existing and pregnancy-related conditions may be correlated and are commonly co-occur, which may amplify the health effects of any single diseases alone.18 However, evidence on how different combinations of maternal conditions shape children's growth trajectories is still lacking.

Maternal lifestyle behaviours during pregnancy, such as physical activity, sleep, and dietary factors, are known to influence women and children's health outcomes, including early-life growth.19, 20, 21 Furthermore, maternal medical conditions and lifestyles have been hypothesised to be interrelated. Previous studies suggested, for example, engaging in leisure-time physical activity and having a healthy diet were associated with a lower risk of gestational diabetes.22 Another clinic-based cohort study revealed that both short and long sleep duration were associated with increased risk of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy.23 In contrast, maternal medical problems may also shape lifestyle behaviours during pregnancy, as an example, less physical activity were observed among women with gestational diabetes.24 Even so, there is no research to date examining the combined associations of maternal multimorbidity and lifestyle behaviours with offspring growth. As the early-life growth trajectories have life-long health effects, it is important to identify modifiable maternal risk factors in utero for informing early prevention strategies.

This study therefore will assess the burden of multimorbidity among women during pregnancy in Huai'an, China between 2020 and 2021, and examine the associations between maternal multimorbidity and early-childhood growth trajectories. As a secondary objective, we will explore the role of maternal lifestyle behaviours during pregnancy on early-childhood growth trajectories also.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was conducted using data from the Grandmothers, Mothers, And Their Children's Health (GMATCH) study, an ongoing cohort study of three generations women's health conducted in Huai'an city, Jiangsu province, China.9,25 Huai'an is a city located in eastern China, with a predominant population of Han Chinese. The economic level and average years of educational attainment in Huai'an are close to the national middle levels in China. In this study, all women during pregnancy who gave live birth during 1st July 2020 to 30th June 2021 were enrolled as the study population (n = 27,923). Their sociodemographic and medical information were linked to the electronic maternity information system. Among these women, over 8000 agreed to undergo an electronic questionnaire interview at the second trimester antenatal visit, which collected maternal information on lifestyle behaviours during pregnancy.25 Children's growth outcomes were followed through the linkage to routine health examination system.

Finally, a total of 22,440 mother–child pairs were included in the analyses. The exclusion criteria were: (1) children died during the perinatal or neonatal periods (within the first 28 days after deliver, n = 202); (2) children's growth information cannot be merged to mothers' perinatal informational system due to the lack of personal identity information or move to other cities (n = 2797); (3) twin or multiple pregnancies (n = 328); (4) children with birth defects (n = 321); and (5) children's information on height or weight were less than four times (n = 1835). Among these participants, 7061 mothers completed the lifestyle behaviour questionnaire interviews (Figure S1). Assessment of maternal multimorbidity

We explored the electronic maternity information system, which records maternal clinical diagnoses based on the “Five Colour Management” framework for high-risk pregnancy issued by the National Health Commision,9 to identify all possible chronic long-term conditions in women prior to or during pregnancy. Maternal conditions that have ongoing significant impact on women, including conditions that are relapsing and remitted in nature, were selected. With the reference to previous studies,1,8 fourteen physical and one psychological conditions were finally chosen, including uterine fibroids or ovarian cysts ≥5 cm, heart disease, respiratory disease, digestive disease, chronic kidney disease, anaemia, haematological disease, thyroid disease, psychiatric disorders or neurodevelopmental disorders, epilepsy, immune disease, cancer, diabetes mellitus in pregnancy (DMP), hypertensive disorders in pregnancy (HDP), and intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy. Detailed categories of these conditions were presented in Table S1. Maternal multimorbidity was assessed in three dimensions: (1) co-existence of two or more of the 15 maternal conditions; (2) combinations of specific maternal conditions; and (3) multimorbidity patterns identified using exploratory factor analysis (Supplementary Methods).

Assessment of early childhood growth trajectories

Ten times measurements of children's length/height and weight from birth to 36 months (0, 1, 3, 6, 8, 12, 18, 24, 30, and 36 months) were conducted by paediatricians using standardised protocols and entered into the routine health examination system. Weight and recumbent length (0–24 month) or standing height (30–36 months) were measured with children in lightweight clothing and without shoes. Age- and sex-specific BMI z-scores were calculated according to the World Health Organization (WHO) standards.26 Macros in SAS were also provided by WHO and used to calculate BMI z-scores in our study (https://www.who.int/tools/child-growth-standards/software).

We conducted latent class growth mixed model to identify potential trajectories of early childhood BMI z-scores specified as functions of age using R package “lcmm”. Four repeated BMI measurements were required for proper estimation of complex models involving trajectories following non-linear trends.27 The latent class parameters were determined by fixed effect coefficients for each group and random effect coefficients for each individual. Repeated trajectory analyses were conducted by changing number of groups from 2 to 10 based on the 1-group model. The following criteria for the choice of a best fit model together with the study-specific requirements were used28: (1) a reduction of Bayesian information criterion of at least 10 points; (2) high mean posterior class membership probabilities ≥0.65; and (3) theoretical interpretability. Estimated parameters of models were shown in Table S2.

Assessment of lifestyle behaviours during pregnancy

A five-component maternal lifestyle behaviours score was constructed based on the items collected during the survey, including physical activity (less than or more than once a week moderate-vigorous exercise), sleep duration (less than 7 h per day or more than 9 h per day/7–9 h per day), sleep quality (bad/good), regularity of daily three meals (irregular/regular), and dietary preference (preference for vegetarian food or preference for meat/balance portion of vegetables and meat). These maternal lifestyles have been associated with offspring growth previously.19, 20, 21 Detailed classification of maternal lifestyle behaviours can be seen in Table S3. Participants with favourable lifestyles were assigned one point for each component. We used a summed score (ranging 0–5) of five single behaviours to make the classification. As for the summed score of lifestyle behaviours, the higher score indicated more favourable lifestyles which was further classified into three groups of unfavourable (0–1), intermediate (2–3), and favourable (4–5).

Covariates

Covariates assessed in this study were extracted from the electronic maternity information and routine health examination system. We used the directed acyclic graph to select the minimally sufficient adjustment set (Figure S2). According to the graph, baseline covariates included maternal age at childbirth, maternal educational level (middle school or below/high school or equivalent/junior college or above), maternal occupation (enterprise or public institution/business or service sector/workers or famers/unemployed or others), pre-pregnancy BMI, parity before this pregnancy (0/1/≥2), adverse pregnancy history (yes/no), gestational weight gain (GWG, inadequate/adequate/excessive), and child sex (male/female). Adverse pregnancy history included maternal histories of recurrent miscarriage, preterm birth, still birth, neonatal death within 28 days, birth defect, ectopic pregnancy, trophoblastic disease, and pregnancy complications or comorbidities (including intrauterine growth restriction of the foetus, gestational diabetes mellitus, HDP, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, and placenta previa). GWG was calculated as the differential between pre-pregnancy weight and weight at birth, and was categorised according to the Chinese Nutrition Society guidelines.29

Statistical analysis

Maternal and children's characteristics were presented by maternal multimorbidity and children childhood growth trajectory as mean (standard deviations [SDs]) and number (percentage) for continuous variables and categorical variables, and were compared using Students' t tests, analysis of variance, or χ2 tests when appropriate.

In the primary analysis, we firstly used linear mixed-effects models to examine offspring's longitudinal BMI z-score trajectories from birth to 36 months stratified by maternal multimorbidity. Child age (months) was modelled as a quartic polynomial and interaction terms with maternal multimorbidity (age, age,2 age3 and age4 × multimorbidity). Each child had a random intercept and random linear slope for age to account for within-child correlation of repeated measurements. Next, we used multinomial logistics regression models to assess associations of maternal multimorbidity and number of conditions with children's early childhood growth trajectories by estimating relative risk ratios (RRR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model 1 was adjusted for demographic characteristics (maternal age and child sex); Model 2 was additionally adjusted for socioeconomic factors (maternal educational level and occupation); Model 3 was additionally adjusted for anthropometry and reproductive factors (pre-pregnancy BMI, GWG, parity, and adverse pregnancy history). Adjusted absolute risk differences were obtained from multinomial logistic models using marginal standardisation, by contrasting model-based predicted probabilities between exposure groups; 95% CIs were derived using the delta method.30 The top five frequent maternal condition combinations were presented using the upset plot, and multinomial logistic regression models were conducted to assess associations of these combinations with offspring trajectories. Multimorbidity patterns were identified using exploratory factor analysis and multinomial logistic regression models were conducted to assess the association.

In the secondary analysis, we examined combined associations of maternal multimorbidity, multimorbidity combinations, and multimorbidity patterns with lifestyle behaviours during pregnancy on childhood growth trajectory using multinomial logistics regression models among 7061 mother–child pairs whose information on lifestyle behaviours were available. Moreover, the model-estimated linear slopes of growth trajectories of childhood BMI z-scores were calculated at each age point in 1-months intervals according to the model parameters and their first derivatives. Linear regression models were conducted to examine the associations of maternal multimorbidity as well as the combinations of maternal multimorbidity and lifestyle behaviours with linear slopes of BMI z-scores.

Several additional analyses were performed to test the robustness of our results. First, the association between maternal multimorbidity and offspring accelerated or non-accelerated growth trajectories was assessed among children with BMI z-score at birth of < −1.5 SD. A series of sensitivity analyses were further conducted by: (1) excluding participants with missing covariates; (2) imputing missing covariates with multiple imputation by generating five imputed datasets and combining estimates using Rubin's rules to obtain pooled estimates and 95% confidence intervals31; (3) excluding participants with assisted reproductive technology; (4) excluding children died during follow-up; and (5) including maternal medication for hypertension or diabetes as a covariate in the model. Mediation analyses were conducted to assess the roles of preterm birth and gestational week on the association between maternal multimorbidity and offspring growth trajectory. Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the association between maternal multimorbidity and the trajectory of low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain stratified by maternal age, child sex, maternal educational level, maternal occupation, pre-pregnancy BMI, and GWG. For the combined association of maternal multimorbidity and lifestyle behaviours during pregnancy with growth trajectory, we additionally used a weighted standardised lifestyle behaviours score, which was derived from the beta coefficients of each component with the low birth weight with accelerate BMI gain trajectory in the logistics regression model adjusted for covariates (maternal age, maternal educational level, maternal occupation, pre-pregnancy BMI, GWG, and child sex). The points for each lifestyle component were multiplied by the beta coefficients and thereafter summed and standardised. The standardised sum score was categorised as unfavourable, intermediate, and favourable lifestyles according to tertiles.32 In addition, to account for the potential selection bias due to the lack of lifestyle information, we conducted an inverse probability weight sensitivity analysis for the assessment of combined maternal multimorbidity and lifestyles through weighting participants by the inverse of their predicted probability of whether having lifestyle questionnaire data.

Significance tests were evaluated at the 0.05 level, using two-sided tests. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., NC, USA) and R (version 4.3.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Associations between maternal multimorbidity and children growth trajectories

Estimated by the linear mixed-effects model (Fig. 2), children born to mothers with multimorbidity had lower BMI z-scores at the first few months, but higher BMI z-scores consistently throughout the 36-month follow-up. Table 3 shows significant association between maternal multimorbidity and higher relative risk of children belonging to a growth trajectory characterised by low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain. In the fully adjusted model, children born to mothers with ≥1 condition and multimorbidity were associated with 1.21-fold (95% CI = 1.03–1.42) and 1.79-fold (95% CI = 1.39–2.31) increased relative risk of experiencing low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain, respectively. For maternal multimorbidity, the relative risk corresponded to an absolute risk difference of 2.26% (95% CI = 0.99–3.52%). There is also a dose–response relationship between the number of conditions and children growth trajectory (Table 3 & Table S5). With a higher number of maternal conditions, the relative risk of children experiencing low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain increased, ranging from 1.07 (0.89–1.28) for one condition to 1.83 (0.91–3.67) for three or more conditions (P for trend <0.001). When we limited the study population to children with BMI z-score at birth of < −1.5 SD (Table S6), maternal multimorbidity remained associated with children's accelerated trajectory (adjusted OR = 1.53, 95% CI = 1.03–2.27), compared to non-accelerated trajectory. Subgroup analyses show similar association between maternal multimorbidity and the low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain trajectory in different subpopulation (Figure S5). Sensitivity analyses also show similar results to the main findings (Table S7).

Multimorbidity combinations involving HDP were both associated with higher relative risk of offspring experiencing low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain (Figure S6). The relative risk for the combination of DMP & HDP was 2.93 (95% CI: 1.70–5.04); while that for the combination of anaemia & HDP was 3.30 (95% CI: 1.37–7.93).

Using the exploratory factor analysis (Figure S7), we identified four maternal multimorbidity patterns: Immuno-inflammatory pattern (with higher loadings on respiratory disease, immune disease, and chronic kidney disease), Cardiometabolic pattern (with higher loadings on heart disease, HDP, and DMP), Hepatic-haematological pattern (with higher loadings on intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, haematological disease, and anaemia), and Endocrine-metabolic pattern (with higher loadings on thyroid disease, DMP and cancer). Maternal higher adherences to the Cardiometabolic pattern (RRR = 2.63, 95% CI = 2.03–3.40) and the Hepatic-haematological pattern (RRR = 1.79, 95% CI = 1.18–2.71) were associated with higher relative risk of offspring experiencing low birth weight with accelerate BMI gain.

The results for individual maternal chronic conditions revealed that uterine fibroids or ovarian cysts, digestive disease, chronic kidney disease, psychiatric or neurodevelopmental disorders, immune disease, and HDP was associated with higher relative risk of offspring experiencing low birth weight and accelerated BMI gain (Table S8). However, after controlling for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate, only HDP remained statistically significant.

Maternal multimorbidity, lifestyle behaviours, and children growth trajectories

Among the 7061 mother–child pairs, 29.8% (n = 2103), 55.6% (n = 3928), and 14.6% (n = 1030) women reported favourable, intermediate and unfavourable lifestyles, respectively (Table S9). Women with unfavourable lifestyles were more likely to have children with low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain (RRR = 1.62, 95% CI = 1.06–2.49) and high birth weight with slower BMI gain (RRR = 1.36, 95% CI = 1.03–1.79) (Table S10). Compared to children born to mothers with no multimorbidity and favourable lifestyles, those born to mothers with multimorbidity combined with unfavourable lifestyles were associated with 4.13-fold (95% CI = 1.65–10.3) increased relative risk of experiencing low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain (Table 4). However, the confidence interval was wide, which was mainly caused by the small sample size in this group, suggesting that this estimate may be unstable and should be interpreted with caution. Specifically, maternal multimorbidity combined with unfavourable physical activity (RRR = 2.12, 95% CI = 1.16–3.88), sleep duration (RRR = 2.50, 95% CI = 1.12–5.62), and sleep quality (RRR = 2.30, 95% CI = 1.27–4.15) were associated with a higher relative risk of the low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain trajectory group. However, we observed no interaction effects between maternal multimorbidity and lifestyle behaviours on offspring growth trajectories. According to the subgroup analysis, the combined association of maternal multimorbidity and unfavourable lifestyles with offspring adverse growth trajectories were more pronounced among female offsprings (Table S11). When we used the classification of lifestyle behaviours by weighted scores, the association of combined maternal multimorbidity and unfavourable lifestyles with the low birth size with accelerated BMI gain trajectory remained (Table S12). Discussion

A total of 5.7% of women were screened for multimorbidity during pregnancy, and children born to these mothers were more likely to experience low birth weight with accelerate BMI gain from birth to 36 months. The significantly rapid rate of BMI gain among these children occurred within the first 8 months after birth, suggesting the specific need to manage children's weight during this period. Maternal unfavourable lifestyles during pregnancy increased the burden of maternal multimorbidity on children growth trajectory. The highest relative risk of children with a growth trajectory of low birth weight with accelerate BMI gain were observed for those born to women with multimorbidity combined with lack of physical activity, short or long sleep duration, and bad sleep quality during pregnancy.

The observed prevalence of maternal multimorbidity in our study is 5.7%, which is relatively lower than the prevalence noted in previous studies based on populations from the UK (16.8%)7 and the US (21% for black women; 13% for white women),33 but is similar to that reported in studies based on populations from Asian countries, including India (3.5%)34 and Japan (6.3%).8 The major reason for the different prevalence may be the lack of a standardised definition of maternal multimorbidity. In our study, the electronic maternity information system covered limited number of maternal conditions, which would lead to the underestimation of the prevalence. Previous studies reporting a high prevalence included more conditions in the measurement of maternal multimorbidity (e.g., 79 conditions used by Amaya A.L. and colleagues,7 30 conditions used by Clare C.B. and colleagues33). In addition, pre-pregnancy obesity was analysed as a covariate in this study based on previous studies,18,35 because the effect of pre-pregnancy BMI may be non-linear and dichotomising BMI into a binary variable may underestimate its contribution to child growth trajectories throughout its range of values. The prevalence would be higher if obesity was included. Considering the variations in the definition, there is a need of collaborative work to inform a standardised measurement framework for maternal multimorbidity, including number of conditions to be used, names of the conditions including body systems, reason for choosing these conditions, like the impact, severity, and linkages with adverse events, which will allow future studies to build upon this definition and to compare and validate results across different backgrounds. Besides, selection bias may be another reason, as we excluded twin or multiple pregnancies, children died during the perinatal or neonatal periods, and children with birth defects. These groups were more likely to expose to maternal multimorbidity, which may lead to an underestimation of the prevalence.

We identified five patterns of children's early-childhood growth trajectory, with children born to mothers with multimorbidity in pregnancy more likely to follow a pattern of low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain. This growth trajectory was associated with 2.69-fold increased relative risk of childhood overweight or obesity in our study population, supporting its classification as an adverse growth pattern. Although few studies have used latent class trajectory models to define this pattern, a previous systematic review using simpler definitions of “catch-up growth” after low birth weight, based on BMI changes or gains, suggested that this growth pattern was associated with greater risks of higher BMI or cholesterol in long-term.36 These findings collectively suggest the importance of early identification of modifiable factors that may shape children's growth trajectories following low birth weight.

The intrauterine environment plays a crucial role in shaping offspring growth patterns.37 Previous studies have linked maternal health problems, including uterine fibroids,38 hypertensive disorders,39 thyroid disease,40 kidney disease,41 autoimmune disease,42 and multimorbidity,8 to increased risks of low birth weight in children. Our findings align with these associations, reinforcing the impact of maternal multimorbidity on birth weight and subsequent growth patterns. However, not all infants born with low birth weight would experience catch-up growth. When we limited the study population to those with BMI z-score at birth < −1.5 SD, only 22% were categorised into the accelerated BMI gain group. Among these participants, maternal multimorbidity remained associated with the accelerated growth group, compared to the non-accelerated growth group. This suggests that children's accelerated growth cannot be fully explained by low birth weight, but that maternal multimorbidity may also contribute to this growth trajectory. This rapid growth rate occurred within the first eight months in these children, highlighting the importance of weight management and regular assessment within the first eight months after childbirth, in particular for children born to mothers with multimorbidity, to promote life-long health benefits for children.

In addition to the impact of birth weight, we also found that preterm birth may partly mediate the association between maternal multimorbidity and offspring experiencing low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain. This pathway is biologically plausible, as previous studies showed that maternal multimorbidity is an important predictor of preterm birth7,8 as well as preterm birth comorbid with low birth weight.43 Infants with preterm birth, in turn, are also more likely to undergo rapid or excessive catch-up growth.44 Although residual confounding and other unmeasured mechanisms remain, the mediating role of preterm birth highlights the need to strengthen the routine surveillance of childhood BMI and early intervention to prevent unhealthy BMI gain among children born preterm.

In terms of specific multimorbidity combinations and patterns, we observed that combinations involving HDP were highly prevalent and associated with offspring trajectory characterised by low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain. This aligns with our previous network analysis, indicating that HDP was the central factor with the greatest connection to other maternal conditions among pregnant women in Huai'an.9 In both observational and Mendelian randomisation studies, maternal HDP has been associated with a higher risk of low birth weight.39,45 Furthermore, infants exposed to HDP also had significantly rapid weight gain from birth to 2 years.46 These growth patterns are also consistent with our findings. Together with previous studies, our study suggests that HDP should be particularly noted in maternal multimorbidity patterns.

In addition to maternal long-term conditions, our study shows that children born to mothers with unfavourable lifestyles, including physical inactivity, short or long sleep duration, and bad sleep quality, are at higher relative risk of experiencing low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain. Such results were consistent with previous studies revealing the adverse effects of maternal unhealthy lifestyles on pregnancy outcomes and children growth.47 Importantly, our study provides novel evidence of combined associations between maternal multimorbidity and unfavourable lifestyles, showing a stronger combined effect. However, there was no evidence of interaction effects, indicating that neither maternal multimorbidity nor lifestyles would obscure each other's influence on growth trajectories. Potential biological mechanisms underlying the combined effects of maternal multimorbidity and unfavourable lifestyles can be hypothesised. Maternal multimorbidity, particularly for Immuno-inflammatory and Cardiometabolic patterns, may reflect maternal conditions of chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction.48,49 Unfavourable lifestyles during pregnancy may further exacerbate these pathophysiological processes by worsening inflammation signalling and stress-hormone regulation,50,51 amplifying the adverse intrauterine environment among mothers with multimorbidity. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine these combined associations, suggesting that unfavourable physical activity and sleep behaviours may exacerbate the burden of multimorbidity on child growth. Future lifestyle-based intervention strategies should be considered in maternal and child health promotion programmes, with special focus on those with maternal multimorbidity.

Strength and limitations

The strengths of this study include the measurement of multiple maternal long-term conditions and the linkage to maternal and child healthcare systems. The subgroup and sensitivity analyses further confirm the robustness of our results. Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, multimorbidity was measured based on 15 maternal conditions available in the maternity information system, which were collected based on the Chinese high-risk pregnancy management framework during the study period and did not capture conditions not listed in that framework (e.g., anxiety, depression). This may have led to an underestimation of the prevalence of maternal multimorbidity, and the low prevalence of several conditions may also resulted in unstable effect estimates. In addition, the dataset lacked complete information on the onset time of maternal conditions, thus we could not distinguish pre-existing conditions from those newly diagnosed during pregnancy. For example, anaemia may be pre-existing or first identified during antenatal screening. Considering this limitation, our findings cannot directly inform timing-specific intervention strategies. Second, maternal medication use during pregnancy may also influence offspring growth. In sensitivity analysis, we additionally adjusted for medication use for hypertension or diabetes as a covariate in the model and the results were consistent with the main findings. However, detailed medication data were not available for all conditions in our study and residual confounding by unmeasured medication usage cannot be avoided. Third, the growth trajectories were not validated in external datasets, which may limit the ability to determine their true clinical relevance. Nonetheless, we have linked the trajectory of low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain with a higher risk of childhood obesity. Unfortunately, good quality hospital inpatient data and primary care data covering children's health outcomes were not available when we conducted the analyses, and we were unable to further explore other outcomes, such as cardiovascular or metabolic disorders. Forth, maternal lifestyle score was constructed based on several crude variables, and we did not verify the reliability and validity of the lifestyle questionnaire, which may introduce measurement error and potential misclassification of lifestyle behaviours. In addition, some other lifestyles that were predictors of child health, such as tobacco and alcohol use, sedentary behaviour, and dietary patterns, were not available in this study. Therefore, the full impact of maternal lifestyle on child growth may be underestimated. Future study with more detailed maternal lifestyle information is needed to verify our results. Fifth, selection bias may exist in this study. In the main analysis of maternal multimorbidity, we excluded twin or multiple pregnancies, children died during the perinatal or neonatal periods, and children with birth defects. These groups were more likely to expose to maternal multimorbidity, which may lead to an underestimation of multimorbidity prevalence. In the secondary analysis of maternal lifestyles, only 31.5% participants provided lifestyle information. Participants who reported lifestyle behaviours differ in sociodemographic characteristics from those without the data, which could limit the generalisability of lifestyle-related findings to the total population. Sixth, growth trajectories were derived using a data-driven latent class model, and individuals were assigned to classes based on posterior probability. Hence, not every child within a given class followed the corresponding trajectory exactly, and misclassification remained a possibility. Seventh, this is an observational study and we cannot eliminate residual confounding although we have adjusted for known covariates. Also, the causality relationship cannot be inferred. Lastly, this study was conducted based on a population from the east part of China, limiting the representative of our results to other settings. Although the socioeconomic status of Huai'an is close to the national average level, it does not fully represent populations living in other regions of China, particularly for less developed western regions. In addition, our results were obtained within a specific Chinese social context and maternal and child healthcare system, and may not generalise to population in other countries.

Implications

Our study showed that children born to mothers with maternal multimorbidity would have 79% increased relative risks of experiencing low birth weight and accelerated BMI gain. The relative risk corresponded to an absolute risk difference of 2.26%, suggesting an approximately 2–3 additional children per 100 following this adverse trajectory among those of mothers with multimorbidity. This result implies that there is a need for current research to shift from single maternal conditions to their coexistence and explore their cross-associations. In addition, due to the lack of a consensus on the definition of maternal multimorbidity, it is difficult for researchers to compare and contrast findings across different settings. A standard measurement methodology is warranted to inform future studies to build upon such definition.

For current maternal and child healthcare system, our results indicate that the prevention and intervention strategies should start before pregnancy to avert the progression from one condition to multimorbidity, and such health surveillance should go beyond shortly after childbirth. Due to the complex interplays of different maternal conditions, there is also an urgent need of a more comprehensive, integrated and holistic approach to manage women with multimorbidity during pregnancy.7

Conclusion

Maternal multimorbidity is associated with higher relative risks of children experiencing low birth weight with accelerated BMI gain. The independent association was strengthened in those with combined unfavourable lifestyle behaviours during pregnancy. Our findings indicate that a holistic approach is needed to manage maternal multimorbidity, which would ultimately improve the health outcomes for the next generation.
