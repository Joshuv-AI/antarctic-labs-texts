A systematic review focusing on how medical researchers report variables in risk scores or models to predict prognosis of patients after percutaneous coronary intervention
Authors: Hong-Liang Zhao, Jing Shi, Guo-Qing Qi, Ming-Qi Zheng, Gang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVariability in risk variable selection for prognostic scores/models after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is poorly characterized. This systematic review examined how researchers report such variables and compared selection between Caucasian and non-Caucasian populations.MethodsWe searched PubMed/MEDLINE (inception to December 2025) for English-language studies developing, validating, or comparing risk scores/models for post-PCI prognosis. Data were synthesized descriptively. Risk of bias was assessed using PROBAST on a random sample.ResultsFrom 981 records, 212 articles (180 distinct scores/models) were included. Most frequent variables: sex/gender, age, diabetes, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral vascular disease, acute coronary syndrome, heart failure, Scr/eGFR, LVEF, and culprit artery. Significant differences between Caucasian (n = 94) and non-Caucasian (n = 86) scores/models were found for SBP, heart rate, Scr/eGFR, ACS presentation, ST-segment deviation, and culprit artery. PROBAST (20-model sample) showed high risk of bias in 70%.ConclusionsRisk variable selection varies substantially across post-PCI prognostic scores/models, with significant ethnic differences. High risk of bias in existing studies highlights the need for more rigorous methodology.Keywords: PCI, Scores, Models, Variable, Prognosis

Background

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is one of the most common types of coronary heart disease (CHD) worldwide [1]. At present, significant progress has been made in the development of related technologies and devices. Despite this, there is still a significant increase in major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) after PCI [2–5]. Further improving the prognosis of patients after PCI remains a major challenge for clinicians. Therefore, it is necessary to evaluate the prognostic risk of patients after PCI and to take appropriate measures to guide clinical treatment.

Several risk scores/models can be used to predict the short- and/or long-term prognosis after PCI, such as the GRACE risk score [5, 6], TIMI risk score [7], SYNTAX score [8], EuroSCORE [8, 9], Mayo Clinic Risk Score [10], etc. Nevertheless, clinicians and researchers face the basic question of selecting variables when developing scores/models. Are the variables in one score or model appropriate for another patient population? What are the generalities and particularities of the variable characteristics in these risk scores/models?

Researchers parse diversity differently with different expectations and requirements for variables in scores/models. To clarify these issues, we conducted a detailed survey of articles related to the prognostic assessment of risk scores/models for patients after PCI in various professional journals, paying particular attention to the application of variables in relevant scores/models under different scenarios, which is a novel contribution to the literature.

Methods

A protocol was not prepared or registered for this systematic review. The methods reported herein follow the PRISMA 2020 guidelines for systematic reviews.

Search strategy and selection criteria

We performed a retrospective analysis of articles published between inception and December 2025 in the PubMed/MEDLINE database. Articles were retrieved using a combination of the keywords “Risk score” or “Risk model” AND “Percutaneous coronary intervention.”

All English language articles involving risk scores/models development, validation, and/or comparison for the assessment of patient prognosis after PCI were included. The type of CHD was not specified, and included stable angina pectoris, unstable angina pectoris, non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). Prognostic outcomes were defined as (1) unplanned readmission, (2) mortality, (3) major adverse cardiac events (MACE), or (4) MACCE. The duration of follow-up was not limited, including short-term (in-hospital, 30-day, or 6 months) and long-term (≥ 1 year). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) duplicate literature; (2) abstracts, editorial comments, letters, corrections, expert consensus or guidelines, meta-analyses, and reviews; (3) the study population was mixed with patients without PCI; and (4) irrelevant to the study purpose.

Data extraction

Two researchers independently performed the literature search, screened the full-text articles, selected the risk scores/models, and extracted variables and other data. There was a good concordance coefficient between researchers (kappa index = 0.91). Discrepancies were discussed independently with the corresponding author until a consensus was reached.

We extracted risk scores or models from the included articles. For each risk score/model, we analyzed what date about age, sex/gender, smoking, body mass index (BMI), heart rate, systolic blood pressure (SBP), hypertension, chronic lung disease (CLD, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)), diabetes, heart failure (HF), peripheral vascular disease (PVD), cerebrovascular disease (CVD, including transient ischemic attack (TIA)), prior PCI, prior coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), Killip class, serum creatinine (Scr) or estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) (including chronic kidney disease (CKD), acute kidney injury (AKI), kidney insufficiency, and dialysis)), acute coronary syndrome (ACS, including acute myocardial infarction (AMI), unstable angina) presentation, cardiogenic shock, cardiac arrest, lymphocyte, glucose, hemoglobin (including anemia), elevated cardiac markers (myocardial enzyme and/or cardiac troponin I/T), brain natriuretic peptide (BNP)/N-terminal proBNP (NT-proBNP), ST-segment deviation on electrocardiogram, TIMI flow grade, and/or culprit coronary artery (including left main (LM) coronary artery, multivessel disease, chronic total occlusion (CTO)), and/or intra-aortic balloon pumping (IABP). Each article was coded to indicate whether the variables were reported. We also collected information on the year of publication, journal, country, or region of the study population, and names of risk scores/models for each article. If a study presents several scores/models, then the data for each score/model should be extracted separately.

Variables assessment and statistical analysis

We calculated the percentage of articles that reported or did not report the names of the risk scores/models, the proportions of variables used in the prognostic risk scores/models of patients after PCI based on race (Caucasian participants or not), and the percentage of time each variable (age, sex/gender, BMI, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, CVD, PVD, CLD, prior PCI, previous CABG, heart rate, SBP, ACS presentation, Killip class, HF, cardiogenic shock, cardiac arrest, Scr or eGFR, BNP/NT-proBNP, elevated cardiac markers, ST-segment deviation, LVEF, culprit coronary artery, and TIMI flow grade were used. We also counted the number of scores/models constructed based on geography and the countries in which study participants were located.

Quality and risk of bias assessment

The quality and risk of bias of the included prediction model studies were assessed using the Prediction model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST). Two reviewers independently assessed each included study across four domains: participants, predictors, outcomes, and analysis. Each domain was rated as “low risk of bias”, “high risk of bias”, or “unclear”.

Results

The initial literature search yielded a total of 981 articles. After removing 57 duplicates and 17 non-full-text articles, 907 titles and abstracts were screened. After a careful review, 695 articles were excluded. Finally, 212 articles were included in the analysis. The literature search process and the reasons for exclusion are shown in Fig. 1.

Over the past two decades, the largest number of related articles were published in 2021 (24 articles, 11.3%). Of the 212 included articles, 84 (39.6%) were registry studies, and the study population covered more than 27 countries and regions (Fig. 3). Among them, the largest number of related articles were published with the Chinese study population (58 articles, 27.4%), followed by articles with the USA study population (44 articles, 20.8%), the Japanese study population (17 articles, 8.0%), and Turkey (10 articles, 4.7%). The study populations of the Italy, Netherlands, and UK all published 9 articles, South Korea published 6 articles, and Australia published 5 articles. Moreover, except for seven international study populations involving multiple countries, other countries and regions published fewer than five articles each.

Of the 186 risk scores/models, six studies were based on international populations, involving multiple countries and ethnicities. The remaining 180 scores/models were divided into two groups according to the study population: Caucasian participant group (n = 94) and non-Caucasian participant group (n = 86). Variables such as age (77.7% vs. 62.8%), sex/gender (24.5% vs. 11.6%), BMI (7.4% vs. 5.8%), smoking (11.7% vs. 11.6%), hypertension (16.0% vs. 9.3%), diabetes (36.2% vs. 24.4%), CVD (18.1% vs. 14.0%), PVD (17.0% vs. 10.5%), CLD (13.8% vs. 4.7%), prior PCI (11.7% vs. 7.0%), ACS presentation (39.4% vs. 14.0%), cardiogenic shock (16.0% vs. 5.8%), cardiac arrest (16.0% vs. 7.0%), Scr or eGFR (52.1% vs. 29.1%), LVEF (38.3% vs. 19.8%), culprit coronary artery (36.2% vs. 16.3%), and TIMI flow grade (10.6% vs. 5.8%) were more commonly used in the Caucasian group. Variables such as prior CABG (14.0% vs. 9.6%), heart rate (31.4% vs. 17.0%), SBP (29.1% vs. 12.8%), Killip class (17.4% vs. 11.7%), HF (24.4% vs. 19.1%), BNP/NT-proBNP ratio (7.0% vs. 6.4%), elevated cardiac markers (9.1% vs. 5.3%), and ST-segment deviation (20.9% vs. 8.5%) were more commonly used in the non-Caucasian group (Table 2).

Regarding methodological rigor, among the 180 risk scores/models, 110 (61.1%) reported discrimination (using either the C-index or area under the curve [AUC]). Notably, neither measure was reported for 32 scores/models (17.8%). Calibration was reported in only 18 scores/models (10.0%), with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test being the most common method. To assess risk of bias, a random sample of 20 scores/models (10 from Caucasian participant group and 10 from non-Caucasian participant group) was selected for full PROBAST assessment. The overall risk of bias was judged as high for 14 models (70.0%), low for 3 models (15.0%), and unclear for 3 models (15.0%). The primary sources of bias were in the analysis domain (e.g., inadequate handling of missing data, insufficient sample sizes, lack of internal validation). The PROBAST results were generally consistent across the two participant groups.

Discussion

Age, sex/gender, BMI, smoking, heart rate, hypertension, CVD, PVD, diabetes, CLD, prior CABG, prior PCI, heart failure, SBP, Killip class, ACS presentation, cardiogenic shock, cardiac arrest, hemoglobin, Scr or eGFR, LVEF, culprit coronary artery, ST-segment deviation, elevated cardiac markers, TIMI flow grade, BNP/NT-proBNP, lymphocyte, glucose, and IABP were the most widely applied variables in the risk prediction scores/models for patients after PCI. Moreover, our results show that variables are often used variously by researchers and participants, which suggests that ethnicity or geographical considerations influence how these variables are used. These results complement and expand on recent guidelines [1, 36] that prognostic risk assessment may contribute to further precise secondary prevention after PCI.

Even after PCI, patients with CHD require precise risk assessment and secondary prevention, which can significantly improve patient outcomes. Guidelines recommend risk scores/models used to identify risk stratification to guide therapeutic decision-making and help physicians improve the prognosis of patients [1, 36, 37]. Several scoring systems have been developed to evaluate the prognosis of patients with CHD within the last two decades [5–11, 27, 28, 32], but few have been unanimously recognized as being suitable specifically for prognosis assessment of patients after PCI. Risk scores/models use covariates (each variable is assigned relative weights) to estimate the probability that a certain outcome (e.g., death or readmission) will occur in the future [38–40]. Therefore, variables are the basic elements of risk scores/models, and different variables may play different roles in different risk scores/models. Hence, it is necessary to conduct a detailed analysis of the variables contained in the risk scores/models to identify suitable candidates for further study and discussion.

In risk prediction scores/models, some variables, such as demographics, personal history, comorbidities, physical examination, and test results, have relatively stable predictive performance and universal applicability [39, 40]. Our results showed that age, sex/gender, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, smoking, and SBP are representative of these variables (Table 2). We found that age, as a well-known prognostic predictor, was treated as a continuous variable (e.g., per year, per 10 years, or 30 years) in some scores/models [5, 6, 10, 11], whereas in other scores/models, it was applied as a categorical variable (e.g., > 40 years, > 50 years, > 60 years, > 65 years, > 70 years, > 75 years, or > 80 years) [7, 9, 16, 21, 24, 41–45]. Similarly, SBP and some scores/models regard per mmHg, per 10 mmHg, or per 20 mmHg as a continuous variable [5, 6, 13, 19], while more scores/models used it as a categorical variable based on < 100 mmHg or not [7, 12, 18, 46]. At present, there is no uniform regulation on whether it is better to define such variables as continuous or categorical predictors, and researchers tend to favor their own needs for building models (e.g., increasing the discrimination power), which will cause a certain degree of selection bias. However, the guidelines [1, 36, 47] and several well-known risk scores [7, 9, 17] seem to favor age and SBP as categorical variables to assess risk and assist in the treatment of patients with CHD, which is also preferred by our panel to construct a score/model for patient prognosis assessment after PCI.

Diabetes, hypertension, and smoking have long been established as risk factors for CHD and used as predictors to assess patient outcomes after PCI [16, 21, 25, 36, 45, 48]. Similar to CHD, CVD, and PVD are both arterial diseases, and the pathogenesis is both atherosclerosis, which leads to vascular stenosis or blockage and, in turn, causes ischemia of the target organ [49]. Therefore, CVD and PVD are also often included as variables in the risk scores/models of patients [15, 50] CLD (including COPD) is also a common concomitant disease of CHD [15]. CLD is closely related to the prognosis of patients with CHD, and is often selected as an important variable in the prognostic scoring system of PCI patients [16, 23, 43, 51]. Perhaps it would be a good idea to construct a scoring system where all risk variables are comorbidities.

Regarding sex/gender in predicting the prognosis of patients after PCI, there is still a matter of controversy among different scores/models. In general, if other factors are not considered, women with CHD have worse prognosis than men. However, there is now growing evidence that differences in adverse outcomes for women after PCI in different situations, compared to men, either no longer present or are significantly reduced after adjusting for confounding factors [52]. Specifically, some scores/models took female as the risk variable of the prognosis score after PCI (e.g. ATRIA risk score, EuroSCORE, EuroSCORE II, SYNTAX score II, and STS score) [2, 9, 53, 54], while others took male as the risk variable of the prognosis score after PCI (e.g. CHIP score, COAP risk model, and Framingham Risk Score) [16, 25, 30]. Sex/gender, in any case, is a very important variable in the risk assessment scores/models for patients after PCI, and whether men or women are more at risk depends on the situation.

Obesity is an independent risk factor for CHD onset and development. Interestingly, a large body of literature has shown that overweight and obese patients with CHD have a better prognosis than the lean patients, which is known as the “obesity paradox” [29]. For example, in models such as KORMI score [26], MVD score [14], and TIMI score [7], body weight/BMI less than a certain cut-off value is considered to be a risk factor for poor prognosis of patients after PCI, while in models such as NCDR CathPCI risk score [50] and Logistic Clinical SYNTAX score [35], BMI higher than a certain cutoff value is a predictor of poor prognosis in patients after PCI. In addition, some researchers have suggested that either a higher or lower BMI is a risk variable for long-term mortality after PCI [15], with a U-shaped association. We are in favor of the last view; however, the conclusion needs to be verified by more high-quality, multicenter, and large-sample risk score/model studies with long-term follow-up.

Some indicators related to cardiac function, such as variables in the risk scores/models, also influence the prognosis of patients after PCI. Cardiogenic condition is a serious condition. In Mayo Clinic Risk Score [10], NERS score II [55], NCDR CathPCI risk score [15], PPCI risk score [53], and Toronto score [56], cardiogenic shock was used to predict the in-hospital and 30-day mortality of AMI patients after PCI (patients with AMI are more prone to cardiogenic shock). More risk scores/models are needed to explore the impact of cardiogenic shock on long-term prognosis after PCI. As we all know, LVEF and Killip class are the criteria used to evaluate cardiac function, BNP and NT-proBNP are important biomarker for diagnosis and evaluation of HF in many clinical settings, and their level represents whether HF has occurred and the severity of HF [22, 57]. For patients with CHD, the level of cardiac function indicates the clinical consequences of coronary ischemia. Therefore, cardiac function-related indicators can be used as indispensable variables in the construction of prognosis scores for post-PCI patients.

Additionally, prior PCI [50, 58, 59], prior CABG [58, 59], ACS presentation [31], ST-segment deviation [5–7, 60], elevated cardiac markers [5–7], culprit coronary artery [10, 16, 24, 34, 55], and TIMI flow grade [24, 31, 60, 61] are directly related to an increased risk of poor prognosis after PCI. Studies have shown that previous revascularization (PCI or CABG) is associated with adverse cardiac events of patients with repeat PCI [62, 63] ACS presentation, ST-segment deviation, and elevated cardiac markers represent an unstable state of CHD, even AMI, which means greater and more variable risk [64]. As variables, they occupy an important place in the prognostic scoring model of patients after PCI. The culprit coronary artery and TIMI flow grade are two factors that are directly related to PCI. Left main, multi-vessel, and chronic occlusive diseases are more complex and high-risk stratified coronary vascular lesions. They not only pose risks to PCI itself but also to the prognosis after PCI [10, 16, 20, 24, 34, 55]. The TIMI flow grade indicates whether the coronary blood supply is adequate, and pro- or post-procedural TIMI flow grade can affect the clinical outcome of PCI patients [24, 31, 33, 61, 62, 65]. Therefore, this type of risk variable is essential for creating a score/model to predict the outcome of patients after PCI.

Another variable worth discussing when constructing prognostic scores for patients after PCI is Scr or eGFR. Scr or eGFR, includes relevant indicators such as AKI [66], CKD [21, 67], eGFR [45, 55, 67], kidney insufficiency [68, 69], and dialysis [16, 44], excluding contrast-induced nephropathy, and has different degrees of influence on the clinical outcomes of patients with stable CHD or ACS after PCI. The heart and kidney are both important organs in the human body, and their simultaneous onset of the two organs can affect each other, resulting in a series of pathophysiological changes known as cardiorenal syndrome, which increases the incidence of adverse prognosis. For AKI or CKD, mild kidney insufficiency, or end-stage kidney disease requiring dialysis, the serum creatinine level was the final evaluation criterion. Similarly, serum creatinine level was used as either a categorical variable [16, 21, 55, 67–69] or a continuous variable [8, 9, 11, 19, 24] in the risk score/model after PCI.

However, a critical issue with significant clinical implications is the ethnic variability observed in the risk scores/models themselves. As shown in Table 2, not only did the specific variables included differ between models developed for Caucasian versus non-Caucasian populations (e.g., Scr/eGFR and ACS presentation weighting more heavily for Caucasians, while SBP and heart rate were more prominent for non-Caucasians), but the cut-off values for even shared variables also varied substantially across ethnic groups (e.g., generally lower BMI thresholds for Asians compared to Caucasians) [70]. This inherent variability underscores a crucial clinical relevance: risk prediction tools optimized for one ethnic group may perform suboptimally or even be misleading when applied to another. Failure to account for these ethnic-specific differences can lead to inaccurate risk stratification, potentially resulting in inappropriate clinical decisions regarding prevention strategies, treatment intensity, or resource allocation for diverse patient groups. Consequently, when developing or applying such predictive models, it is imperative to explicitly consider and incorporate the ethnic background of the target population to ensure clinical validity, equity, and ultimately, improved patient outcomes.

Limitations

The limitations of this study include the fact that it was based on a literature analysis of previous studies. Although we thoroughly analyzed the high-frequency application variables in the scores/models of prognostic evaluation after PCI, it is still necessary to verify whether these variables can construct a high-performance score/model to predict the prognosis of patients after PCI in a real cohort. Additionally, applying keywords to filter articles may result in missing scores/models. Furthermore, we believe that other scores such as the CoronaryArtery Calcium score [71], MESA risk score [71], and Gensini score [72], which have not been specifically used for prognosis assessment of patients after PCI, can also be employed in clinical research.

Conclusions

In the risk scores/models for prognostic assessment after PCI, the variables applied vary. When constructing scores/models, the variable selection should fully consider the ethnic background of the study population. Analyzing articles by variables in risk scores/models may improve the predictive power of related scores/models after PCI in follow-up studies. Clinicians and researchers need to critically consider the variables selected to construct a “super” risk model or score that can be widely used in the prognostic assessment of patients after PCI.
