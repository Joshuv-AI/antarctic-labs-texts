Accuracy of combined fetal lung volume and pulmonary artery doppler in predicting respiratory distress syndrome after elective cesarean section in diabetic pregnancies
Authors: Marwan Osama El-Kady, Ahmed Sherif Abdel Hamid, Salah Taha Fayed, Ahmed Salah Ahmed, Amr Saad Mahmoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimNeonatal respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) remains a significant cause of morbidity, particularly in infants of diabetic mothers. This study aimed to evaluate the predictive accuracy of non-invasive prenatal ultrasound parameters—Fetal Lung Volume (FLV) and Pulmonary Artery Resistance Index (PA-RI)—for anticipating RDS in this high-risk population.Patients and MethodsThis prospective cohort study was conducted at Ain Shams University Maternity Hospital over 18 months. 123 pregnant women with Diabetes scheduled for elective cesarean delivery were enrolled. Within 72 h of pre-delivery, FLV was measured by 3D ultrasonography with VOCAL analysis, and PA-RI was assessed by pulsed-wave Doppler. Neonatal RDS was diagnosed by a blinded pediatrician according to the Vermont Oxford Network criteria. Statistical analysis included ROC curves to determine optimal cut-offs and predictive values.ResultsThe incidence of RDS was 13.0% (n = 16). Affected neonates had significantly lower median FLV (29.8 vs. 38.2 cm³, p < 0.001) and higher median PA-RI (0.88 vs. 0.68, p < 0.001). ROC analysis revealed that both parameters were excellent predictors. FLV ≤ 33.5 cm³ showed 93.8% Sensitivity, 87.9% Specificity, 53.6% Positive Predictive Value, and 99.0% Negative Predictive Value (AUC 0.941). PA-RI > 0.75 demonstrated 100% Sensitivity, 90.7% Specificity, 64.0% Positive Predictive Value, and 100% Negative Predictive Value (AUC 0.963). The combined model achieved the highest accuracy (AUC 0.981). Both parameters showed strong correlations with RDS severity (FLV: P = -0.80; PA-RI: P = + 0.77) and NICU stay duration (FLV: P = -0.77; PA-RI: P = + 0.75).ConclusionFLV and PA-RI are highly accurate, non-invasive tools for predicting neonatal RDS in Diabetic pregnancies. PA-RI, with its perfect Sensitivity and NPV in our cohort, is particularly effective for ruling out the condition, enabling improved perinatal risk stratification and management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-08916-6.

Introduction

Neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS) continues to be a major cause of illness and death among newborns globally, mainly due to inadequate production of pulmonary surfactant, which results in alveolar collapse and compromised gas exchange [1]. Although it has been primarily associated with premature infants, RDS can also occur in full-term babies, especially when maternal conditions such as Diabetes disrupt normal lung development [2]. The effects of RDS go beyond immediate respiratory issues, often necessitating intensive care and potentially resulting in long-lasting complications. Therefore, accurately predicting RDS before delivery is a critical focus in perinatal medicine [1, 2].

The traditional method for evaluating fetal lung maturity relied on biochemical analysis of amniotic fluid obtained by invasive amniocentesis [3]. Although this approach yields valuable information, it poses significant risks, such as Preterm Labor, Premature Rupture of Membranes, Placental Abruption and Fetomaternal Hemorrhage [3, 4]. These risks have greatly restricted its regular use in clinical settings and highlighted the urgent need for safer, non-invasive alternatives [4]. The decline in the use of invasive procedures has prompted intensified efforts to identify reliable ultrasound-based techniques that can offer similar predictive insights without subjecting the mother and fetus to procedural hazards [4, 5].

Recent advancements in prenatal ultrasound technology have shown that fetal lung development follows discernible patterns that can be quantified using specific metrics [5, 6]. As gestational age increases, both structural and functional maturation align with observable changes in lung volume and pulmonary blood flow dynamics [6, 7]. Notably, the Pulmonary Artery Resistance Index (PA-RI) declines as vascular resistance decreases during lung development [7, 8]. These physiological transformations serve as a basis for creating non-invasive predictive instruments. Earlier studies by Laban et al. have indicated the potential benefits of integrating measurements of Fetal Lung Volume with pulmonary artery Doppler assessments; however, there is still a significant need for a validated, comprehensive methodology in clinical settings [8, 9].

This research aimed to assess the predictive validity of two non-invasive ultrasound metrics—Fetal Lung Volume (FLV) and Pulmonary Artery Resistance Index (PA-RI)—in forecasting neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS), building on earlier studies by Laban et al. [8, 9]. The study specifically targeted pregnant women with Diabetes with pregnancy who were scheduled for elective cesarean deliveries at term, as this group is considered at high risk for neonatal respiratory issues.

Patients and methods

This prospective cohort study was conducted at the Obstetrics and Gynecology Department of Ain Shams University Maternity Hospital over 18 months, from December 2023 to May 2025. The study protocol received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Ain Shams University (FMASU MS 695/2023). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. A total of 123 pregnant women with Diabetes during pregnancy and scheduled for elective CS were enrolled.

Eligibility criteria

The study included pregnant women aged 20 to 45 years with Pregestational or Gestational Diabetes mellitus who were carrying a singleton pregnancy and had a gestational age of at least 37 weeks. Participants were scheduled for an elective cesarean section due to reasons such as a previous cesarean delivery, breech presentation, or suspected fetal macrosomia (with an estimated fetal weight over 4000 g), and were required to agree to take part in the study. Conversely, women were excluded if they had other maternal health issues, such as Preeclampsia, if their fetus had structural or chromosomal anomalies, if they experienced preterm Premature Rupture of Membranes (PPROM), if they were carrying multiple fetuses or were post-term, if they opted for vaginal delivery or Trial of Labor After Cesarean (TOLAC), or if they had previously received antenatal corticosteroids for lung maturity.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the prediction of neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS) using antenatal measurements of Fetal Lung Volume (FLV), Pulmonary Artery Resistance Index (PA-RI), or a combination of both.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes included Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min, the rate of Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) admission, the number of neonates requiring surfactant therapy, and the incidence of other neonatal complications, such as sepsis, within the first 28 days of life, as well as mortality within the first 28 days of life.

Sample size justification

The sample size was calculated using the PASS 15 program. Based on the findings of Laban et al. [7], which reported a Sensitivity of 81.8% and a Specificity of 97.1% for the combined use of FLV and PA-RI in predicting RDS, with an expected RDS incidence of 13.8%, a sample size of 123 women was determined to be necessary to achieve a study power of 80% with an alpha error of 0.05.

Fetal Lung Volume (FLV) Measurement: Using the three-dimensional ultrasonography function, a longitudinal section of the fetal thorax was first obtained. The 3D window was activated, followed by a complete electronic sweep. If the image quality was compromised by fetal movements or excessive maternal respiratory motion, the sweep was repeated. The acquired volume dataset was then analyzed offline using the Virtual Organ Computer-Aided Analysis (VOCAL) software. The manual option was selected with a rotational step of 30°. Using the multiplanar view, the lung parenchyma contour was carefully traced from its apex to its base across six sequential rotational steps. The software automatically calculated the volume for each lung. The entire process was repeated three times by the same examiner for each lung, and the final FLV value used for analysis was the average of the three mean measurements (from both lungs) (Fig. 1).

Pulmonary Artery Resistance Index (PA-RI) Measurement: Utilizing the pulsed-wave Doppler function, the examination began by obtaining a standard four-chamber view of the fetal heart. The probe was then adjusted to identify the three-vessel view. The Doppler sample gate was placed on the main Pulmonary Artery, midway between the Pulmonary valve and the bifurcation, with careful attention to maintain an insonation angle ≤ 15° to ensure accuracy. The machine’s software then automatically calculated the resistance index from the acquired spectral Doppler waveform (Fig. 1). All ultrasound scans and measurements were performed by a single consultant fetal medicine specialist with over a decade of experience in obstetric imaging and certified expertise in Doppler and 3D volume sonography. The reliability of the ultrasound measurements was assessed to ensure consistency. The protocol itself incorporated measures to minimize variability: FLV was measured three times per lung by the examiner, with the mean used, and the mean of three PA-RI Doppler recordings was taken. A formal analysis on a subset of 20 cases confirmed excellent intra-observer (ICC: 0.92–0.94) and very good inter-observer (ICC: 0.89–0.91) reliability for both parameters.

Neonatal Assessment: Finally, a pediatrician blinded to all antenatal FLV and PA-RI results conducted the neonatal assessment immediately after delivery. The pediatrician recorded the neonatal sex, birth weight, and Apgar scores at both 1 and 5 min. The diagnosis of Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS) was made strictly according to the Vermont Oxford Neonatal Network criteria. This required the presence of clinical signs—such as tachypnea (respiratory rate > 60 breaths per minute), intercostal retractions, nasal flaring, expiratory grunting, and cyanosis—in conjunction with a partial arterial oxygen pressure (PaO₂) of ≤ 50 mmHg while the neonate was breathing room air. For any neonate suspected of having RDS, a chest radiograph was performed. The radiographic findings were classified into four grades: Grade I (fine granular densities), Grade II (air bronchograms), Grade III (ground-glass appearance), and Grade IV (white lung pattern).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0. Normally distributed quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed data are presented as median and inter-quartile range (IQR). Categorical data are presented as numbers and percentages. The Mann-Whitney U test was employed to compare Fetal Lung Volume (FLV) and Pulmonary Artery Resistance Index (PA-RI) between neonates with and without Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the predictive performance of FLV and PA-RI for RDS and to determine optimal cutoff values, along with Sensitivity, Specificity, Positive Predictive Value (PPV), Negative Predictive Value (NPV), and the area under the curve (AUC). The DeLong test was used to compare the AUCs of different models. Binary logistic regression was utilized to create a combined predictive model incorporating both FLV and PA-RI. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was used to assess the relationship between the ultrasound parameters and the severity of RDS (grade) and the duration of NICU stay. The Kruskal-Wallis H test, followed by post-hoc analysis, was used to compare FLV and PA-RI across the different RDS severity grades. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The study included a cohort of 123 pregnant women diagnosed with Diabetes during pregnancy (Pregestational and Gestational DM), with an average age of 33.4 ± 5.7 years. A significant majority (78.9%) lived in urban environments. The participants displayed glycemic levels typical of their diabetic condition and had a high incidence of anemia, affecting 68.3% of them. Obstetric records indicated that most participants (92.7%) were multigravidas, all of whom had undergone at least one previous cesarean delivery. The smaller group of Primigravidas (7.3%) was scheduled for an elective cesarean section due to complications related to Diabetes with pregnancy, such as suspected fetal macrosomia in 55.6% of cases and breech presentation in 44.4%. This profile highlights the effective recruitment of a specific high-risk group for elective cesarean delivery (Table 1).

A prenatal ultrasound assessment showed an average Fetal Lung Volume (FLV) of 36.6 ± 4.9 cm³ and a mean Pulmonary Artery Resistance Index (PA-RI) of 0.73 ± 0.09, indicating a range of fetal lung development and vascular resistance. Upon delivery, the neonates had an average birth weight of 3330 ± 558 g, with 8.9% classified as macrosomic. Initial Apgar scores suggested moderate depression at one minute (average 6.2 ± 1.5), but significant improvement was noted by the five-minute mark (average 8.5 ± 1.1). The main outcome, neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS), was identified in 16 infants (13.0%), with most cases being moderate in severity. Among these, 68.75% were categorized as Grade 2, while severe cases (Grade 3–4) represented 31.25% on chest radiographs. All RDS cases required respiratory assistance, with 8.9% managed by CPAP and 4.1% requiring mechanical ventilation. One infant with Grade 4 RDS received surfactant therapy. The median length of NICU stay for affected neonates was 4.5 days (IQR: 4-6.5 days). Importantly, there were no cases of neonatal sepsis or mortality reported within the first 28 days of life (Fig. 2; Table 2).

Discussion

Our results have important implications for clinical practice, as they demonstrate that antenatal ultrasound assessment of Fetal Lung Volume (FLV) and Pulmonary Artery Resistance Index (PA-RI) provides a highly accurate, non-invasive method for predicting neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS) in term diabetic pregnancies. Specifically, a PA-RI value ≤ 0.75 was highly effective in ruling out RDS in our cohort. offering a good tool for risk stratification and perinatal management. These findings directly translate into a practical clinical algorithm: patients with low PA-RI may avoid unnecessary NICU preparation, while those with elevated values warrant targeted neonatal vigilance and resource allocation, thereby optimizing care pathways for this high-risk obstetric population.

In the studied group, 13.0% experienced RDS, with lower median FLV (29.8 cm³ vs. 38.2 cm³) and higher median PA-RI (0.88 vs. 0.68) in those affected. The strong correlation (p < 0.001) underscores the importance of these metrics in RDS development. PA-RI demonstrated 100% Sensitivity in our cohort for excluding RDS risk, while FLV had 93.8%. Significant negative correlations were found between FLV and RDS severity (P = -0.80) and NICU stay duration (P = -0.77); PA-RI correlated positively with both outcomes (P = + 0.77, + 0.75). Our findings strongly suggest that impaired fetal lung development and elevated pulmonary vascular resistance are associated with the onset of neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome in this population. The observed decline in FLV (33.1 → 29.8 → 26.2 cm³) and the rise in PA-RI (0.80 → 0.86 → 0.92) across increasing RDS severity grades (Grade 2 → Grade 3 → Grade 4) indicate a clear dose-response relationship, further supporting the use of these measurements to forecast respiratory outcomes in neonates.

The combined model of FLV and PA-RI achieved the highest predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.981), though the improvement over PA-RI alone (AUC = 0.963) wasn’t statistically significant (p = 0.28), suggesting PA-RI may be a stronger standalone predictor. The refined equation Logit(P) = 24.8 - (0.63 × FLV) + (17.9 × PA-RI) is practical for assessing RDS risk. With a 13.0% RDS prevalence in our cohort, this model maintains excellent discriminatory ability for this high-risk population.

The strong inverse correlation between Fetal Lung Volume (FLV) and Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS) is linked to the impact of a diabetic intrauterine environment on lung development. Maternal hyperglycemia causes fetal hyperinsulinemia, hindering cortisol-mediated maturation of alveolar type II cells and impairing surfactant synthesis. A reduced FLV indicates parenchymal immaturity, with fewer, smaller alveoli, and is directly correlated with pulmonary immaturity, which increases the risk of RDS in neonates.

Comparison of our results to similar studies

Our research closely aligns with the findings of Laban et al. [7], emphasizing the effectiveness of Fetal Lung Volume (FLV) and Pulmonary Artery Resistance Index (PA-RI) as strong predictors of neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS). In both studies, similar RDS rates were observed: 13.0% in our cohort and 14% in Laban’s. The optimal cutoff values for FLV were comparable—≤33.5 cm³ in our study and ≤ 32 cm³ in Laban’s—both showing high Sensitivity and Specificity. We found that while the combination of FLV and PA-RI provided the highest predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.981), PA-RI alone (AUC = 0.963) achieved good Sensitivity and a Negative Predictive Value of 100%, highlighting its strength as an individual predictor. Conversely, Laban’s research suggested that their combined model was superior to either predictor alone, which may stem from differences in sample sizes and populations, as our study targeted pregnancies affected by Diabetes .

Our results, derived from a term diabetic population, contrast with those of Laban et al. [8], who focused on a preterm group. The predictive performance of the parameters evaluated differed notably. Laban’s study reported lower predictive power (AUCs of 0.83 for both PA-RI and FLV) and reduced Sensitivity (83.3% and 79.17%, respectively). This discrepancy can be attributed to the distinct characteristics of our study populations. In our diabetic cohort, term infants experienced RDS primarily due to impaired maturation, making both structural (FLV, AUC = 0.941) and hemodynamic (PA-RI, AUC = 0.963) indicators highly effective. In Laban’s preterm cohort, the higher incidence of RDS (46%) was primarily due to extreme physiological immaturity, which increased variability and reduced predictive accuracy. This pathophysiological difference underlines the distinctive risk profile created by maternal Diabetes, although both studies reinforce that a combination of low FLV and high PA-RI is a reliable antenatal predictor of RDS across different groups.

Compared with Morshedy et al. [10], our study demonstrated superior predictive performance. Morshedy et al. assessed pulmonary artery Doppler indices for predicting RDS in term pregnancies, reporting an AUC of 0.91, Sensitivity of 95%, and Specificity of 90% for the Acceleration Time/Ejection Time (At/Et) ratio. In contrast, our study achieved a higher AUC of 0.963 for PA-RI, with good Sensitivity and Negative Predictive Value, making it more reliable for ruling out RDS. We provided a comprehensive assessment of fetal lung maturity by evaluating both vascular resistance and structural development, whereas Morshedy et al. focused on a single parameter. The performance of our predictive markers in this diabetic cohort indicates their significant value in managing high-risk populations, where accurate RDS prediction is crucial.

Additionally, our findings in a term diabetic population showed stronger predictive values than those reported by Eldeeb et al. [9] in their preterm cohort. Although Eldeeb et al. achieved good Sensitivity and NPV by combining FLV and PA-RI—mirroring our findings for PA-RI alone (100% Sensitivity, 100% NPV)—their Specificity was lower. This highlights the influence of our cohort characteristics; Eldeeb et al. studied a preterm cohort with a higher incidence of RDS (40.14%) and mortality, while our diabetic term cohort demonstrated a 13.0% RDS incidence without mortality. Their lower FLV cutoff reflects physiological differences between preterm and term fetal lungs. Both studies support the notion that the combination of FLV and PA-RI provides superior predictive capability over either parameter alone. The NPV (100%) identified in both studies is immensely beneficial for clinical decision-making, particularly in our diabetic population, as it enables high confidence in ruling out RDS, optimizing resource allocation, and reducing unnecessary interventions.

Strength and limitations of our study

Our study possesses a notable methodological strength that enhances the validity and clinical applicability of our findings. The inclusion of a well-defined, high-risk population—women with Diabetes with pregnancy undergoing elective cesarean section—allowed us to study a clinically relevant group where accurate RDS prediction is particularly valuable.

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The subgroup analysis of RDS severity was severely limited by the very small number of cases in each grade (especially Grades 3 and 4), precluding robust statistical inference. The correlations and trends observed, while biologically plausible, must be interpreted with extreme caution and require validation in much larger cohorts.

The absence of Grade 1 RDS cases may reflect stringent Vermont Oxford diagnostic criteria requiring both clinical and laboratory confirmation, potentially leading to under-ascertainment of milder respiratory distress. This limits generalizability to all RDS severity spectra.

As a single-center study conducted at a tertiary referral hospital, our patient demographics, sonographic expertise, and neonatal care standards may not be fully representative of primary or secondary care settings. This limits the immediate generalizability of our proposed cut-off values, which require external validation in broader, more diverse populations.

Recommendation for further studies

Future research should validate the cut-off values (FLV ≤ 33.5 cm³ and PA-RI > 0.75) in large-scale, multicenter studies. It should include high-risk groups, particularly women with Diabetes and varied gestational ages, and examine FLV and PA-RI changes in the third trimester.

Conclusion

In conclusion, antenatal assessment of Fetal Lung Volume and the Pulmonary Artery Resistance Index provides an excellent, non-invasive predictor of neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome in diabetic pregnancies.
