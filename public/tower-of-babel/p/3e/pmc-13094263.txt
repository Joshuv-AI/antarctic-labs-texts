Daily dynamics between self-perceptions of ageing and self-rated health: a lifespan perspective
Authors: Manon Royer, Aurélie Goncalves, Nicolas Pellerin, Sarah Le Vigouroux, Maxime Deshayes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHow would you rate your health today? Self-rated health is a key indicator of overall well-being, yet little is known about how daily fluctuations in self-perceptions of ageing relate to day-to-day variations in health. This study investigated whether daily fluctuations in subjective age and awareness of age-related change are associated with variations in self-rated health across adulthood.MethodsFor 10 consecutive days, participants (N = 78, age range 20 to 81) completed daily questionnaires, via the m-path application, assessing their daily subjective age, awareness of age-related gains and losses, and self-rated health. Multilevel models were used to examine associations at both the between- and within-person levels, as well as moderation by chronological age.ResultsBoth average and daily subjective age were significant associated with self-rated health across the lifespan, such that feeling younger was related to better self-rated health. At the between-person level, higher awareness of age-related losses, but not gains, was associated with poorer self-rated health. In addition, chronological age moderated the associations between subjective age and self-rated health at both the between- and within-person levels. In particular, on days when they felt older than usual, younger participants reported worse self-rated health than older participants. challenging the notion that subjective age is only associated with outcomes in later life.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the importance of considering self-perceptions of ageing as dynamic processes that shape daily health evaluations across the adult lifespan, including early and mid-adulthood.

Background

Self-rated health (SRH)

How would you rate your health today? This seemingly straightforward question lies at the heart of understanding one individual’s general health [1]. This question is known to request self-rated health (SRH) and refers to the subjective evaluation of one’s own health and can be used as a substitute for clinical measurement [2]. However, this subjective assessment does not always correspond to objective measures of physical, emotional, or cognitive functioning [3]. Rather, SRH integrates multiple facets of well-being, including physical, psychological, and social dimensions [4], and may capture predispositions toward better or worse health [5]. Evidence is mounting that poorer SRH is associated with increased risk of chronic diseases [6], declines in physical and cognitive functioning [7], greater health service use [8] and poorer clinical biomarkers [3]. A meta-analysis further demonstrated that SRH predicts mortality, even after controlling for demographic, biophysical, and behavioural risk factors [9]. Most studies rely on global retrospective item such as “In general, how would you rate your current health status?”, which invites participants to provide broad evaluations that may reflect health over several days, weeks, or months. Such global assessments risk overlooking meaningful within-person fluctuations in subjective health [10], potentially limiting our understanding of the psychological processes that shape SRH.

More recently, daily diary and ESM (Experience sampling method) approaches have shown that SRH fluctuates from day to day and covaries with behavioural factors such as sleep [11], but also to psychosocial factors including social support or coping strategies [12]. More frequent assessment of subjective health makes it possible to explore intraindividual variations [13] and examine how they relate to behavioural, emotional, and contextual factors over time [14]. From a lifespan developmental perspective [15], such daily health evaluations can be understood as dynamic self-appraisals shaped by age-related expectations, personal resources, and situational demands. This perspective highlights the importance of identifying psychological processes that influence the interpretation of health on a daily basis. Besides age, which may moderate these associations [16], SRH may be affected by several factors from biological [17], to environmental [18], to psychological [19]. Among these processes, self-perceptions of ageing (SPA) may play a particularly important role [20] and have been under-investigated.

Self-perceptions of ageing (SPA)

From a theoretical standpoint, the link between SRH and perceptions of ageing is well grounded in lifespan developmental models. According to the Integrated Model of Subjective Ageing (IMSA) [21], SPA influence health via interconnected psychological, behavioural, and physiological pathways. They shape expectations, coping responses, motivation and engagement in health-related behaviours, ultimately influencing daily experiences of functioning and well-being. Within the lifespan developmental framework [15], SPA can be understood as self-regulatory processes through which individuals interpret and respond to age-related changes. They reflect the dynamic integration of normative expectations, personal experiences, and age-related beliefs that accumulate across adulthood. SPA are therefore defined as « an integral psychological process or condition of the ageing self » and encompass multiple constructs that reflect how individuals make sense of their own ageing [22].

This study focuses on two key constructs: subjective age, and awareness of age-related change (AARC) [23]. This exploration of the two constructs is based on studies that have demonstrated their daily variability [24]. Subjective age refers to one’s individual perception of their age and may differ from their chronological age [25]. Individuals may perceive themselves as younger or older than they actually are. Feeling younger can help individuals distance themselves from the perceived inevitable decline associated with ageing [26]. Subjective age is a strong and reliable predictor of various health outcomes including psychological well-being, depressive symptoms, and cognitive functioning [27–30],. A meta-analysis further demonstrated that perceiving oneself as younger than one’s chronological age was associated with better mental health and improved cognitive performance, regardless of individuals’ age and sex [31]. However, it does not specify the concrete experiences underlying these feelings [22].

AARC, on the other hand, captures the specific experiences and changes that contribute to these perceptions, making individuals aware of how ageing influences their behaviour, performance, and way of experiencing life [32]. AARC includes both positive (age-related gains) and negative aspects (age-related losses) of the ageing process. These two subdimensions are based on well-defined expectations and beliefs about age-related change by adults of different ages [33]. More importantly, AARC is investigated within and across five behavioural domains related to health, physical, cognitive and social functioning, interpersonal relations and lifestyle. AARC-losses have been linked to both physical and mental health, whereas AARC-gains have only been associated with mental health [34]. Together, subjective age and AARC provide complementary perspectives: subjective age reflects a global appraisal, whereas AARC identifies the concrete experiences that shape this appraisal.

Understanding SPA is essential, given their important impact on individuals’ health. For individuals, negative SPA have been associated with poorer physical and cognitive functioning, increased risk of chronic disease, and higher mortality rates as demonstrated by a meta-analysis [35]. Various constructs of SPA have been related to better SRH. For example, identifying with a younger age group is positively associated with SRH [20], and positive attitudes toward ageing were related to better SRH over time [36]. Although studies focusing on subjective age and AARC are limited, both constructs show significant associations. Cross-sectional and longitudinal researches have shown that people who feel younger than their chronological age also tend to feel healthier and conversely [37, 38]. AARC is associated with SRH particularly in elderly people suffering from arthritis [39]. Despite this central role, most research has focused on older or middle-aged adults [24, 40], leaving the influence of SPA on health in younger adults relatively understudied.

Age-related differences in SPA

From Baltes lifespan developmental perspective, SPA are expected to evolve across adulthood because individuals’ experiences, goals, and interpretations of ageing shift as they progress through different life stages [15]. Although ageing is often stereotypically linked to later life, younger adults may also be influenced by perceptions of ageing [41], as SPA begin to form early and continue to shape functioning and behaviour across the lifespan. The developmental context in which individuals grow, their accumulated experiences, and their exposure to age-related expectations all contribute to how they construct the meaning of “being” or “becoming” older. Empirical work supports the idea that SPA change and diversify with age. With increasing age, the experience of ageing becomes less positive and more negative [42]. SPA reflect different representations and life experiences over the years [43]. Moreover, domains of changes can follow a linear age-related direction with gradual changes while others follow non-linear change trajectories. For example, people can perceive age related gains or losses in some domains when they are middle-aged, while changes in others may not become apparent until old age [44]. Studies examining AARC illustrate these age-related complexities. Moderate/high AARC-gains and low AARC-losses are more closely linked to better physical health in young adulthood and midlife, whereas it is more associated with better mood and mental health in old age [45]. This suggests that both how individuals perceive ageing and how these perceptions relate to functioning change across the lifespan. However, this process remains unclear. The association between AARC-losses and health indicators is less evident among younger individuals [45] suggesting that ageing perceptions may have a stronger impact on health outcomes in older populations. A study assert that effects of SPA do not differ between age groups [37] while a meta-analysis suggests that studies with younger samples showed stronger effects, although it remains unclear whether this reflects sample characteristics or specific health outcomes [46].

Taken together, research on age-related differences in SPA yields mixed results. What is clear, however, is that SPA are not uniform across adulthood and that their associations with health may vary by age. This underscores the importance of adopting a lifespan perspective to better understand how SPA influence individuals’ health at different ages. It also highlights a significant gap: few studies have examined these associations across a broad adult age range, and even fewer have done so using intensive daily methods that capture short-term dynamics.

Daily variability of SPA

Although research has traditionally conceptualised SPA as relatively stable trait-like constructs, recent theoretical and empirical work demonstrates that they fluctuate meaningfully from day to day [47]. From a lifespan developmental perspective, such fluctuations reflect the ongoing process through which individuals reinterpret age-related experiences in light of their goals, context, and personal resources [15]. SPA are influenced not only by long-term developmental factors but also by everyday events, including social interactions, performance feedback, contextual stressors, and bodily sensations [48]. These experiences can modify ageing-related beliefs, resulting in short-term changes in how individuals perceive themselves as ageing.

Empirical evidence supports this dynamic process. Both subjective age and AARC fluctuate from day to day [49]. Within-person variability in SPA have been linked to fluctuations in inductive reasoning performance [50], stress reactivity [51], negative affect [49], control beliefs [52], cognitive interference [53] and pain [54]. For example, on days when individuals report higher age-related gains and lower age-related losses, they tend to experience decreased negative affect and increased vitality [49]. Conversely, on days when older adults perceive higher age-related losses or lower age-related gains, and when they feel older than usual, they report lower perceived control [52]. These findings indicate that SPA respond to daily experiences and, in turn, shape daily lives.

Accordingly, daily SPA should contribute to the interpretation of daily health, particularly SRH, which relies heavily on subjective appraisal processes. Although recent work has demonstrated meaningful daily fluctuations in SPA, as well as in SRH [55, 56], the dynamic interplay between these two constructs remains poorly understood. Moreover, existing work has focused primarily on older adults, limiting our understanding of how these processes unfold across the adult lifespan [49, 50, 52, 53].

These gaps underscore the need to investigate SPA within intensive longitudinal designs, which are uniquely suited to capturing the dynamic interplay between daily SPA and daily health evaluations. Understanding daily variability is essential for advancing lifespan models of subjective ageing.

Objectives and hypotheses

The present study investigated how daily SPA were related to daily SRH across adulthood using an experience-sampling design. We examined both between-person associations: how individuals’ SPA were related to their SRH, and within-person associations: how day-to-day deviations in SPA covaried with same-day SRH. In addition, given theoretical and empirical indications that SPA may function differently across the lifespan, we tested whether chronological age moderated these associations.H1: (Between-person level). Individuals’ person-mean SPA scores would be associated with their SRH scores. Higher person-mean AARC-losses, lower person-mean AARC-gains, and a more negative subjective-age discrepancy would predict lower average SRH.H2: (Within-person level): Within-person deviations from each participant’s average SPA would covary with same-day SRH. On days when participants report higher AARC-losses, lower AARC-gains, or feel older than their personal average, they would also report lower SRH; conversely, on days when SPA are more positive than usual, SRH would be higher.H3: (Age moderation). Chronological age would moderate the within- and between-person association between SPA and SRH. In line with previous research [57], the relationship between SPA and SRH would be stronger among older adults.

Methods

Participants and procedure

Participants were from France and recruited through distribution of a study advertisement, posters and social networks in March-April 2023. Each of the media featured a QR code. When participants scanned the QR-code, they were redirected to a page requesting their availability for a phone call. Any French citizen aged over 18 with regular access to a smartphone and the internet was allowed to participate. Participants matching the inclusion criteria were invited to continue the study providing informed consent. They were then programmed to fill out daily diary questionnaires. Of the 84 initial participants, 78 were retained for the main analyses. Six participants were excluded due to incorrect response times. Participants were 20 to 81 years (M = 41.70, SD = 17.2) and 56.4% of them were women. Most of participants reported high level of education (M = 5.78, SD = 1.32). A sensitivity power analysis was conducted using R (version 4.3.1) and the {simr} package [58] to determine the minimum effect size detectable given our sample size (N = 78 participants, 10 daily observations per participant). A linear mixed-effects model (lmer) with random intercepts for participants was fitted, with day as a fixed predictor. Power simulations (100 iterations) using the Kenward-Roger test (package {pbkrtest}) indicated that our design had 100% power (95% CI: 96.38% – 100%) to detect an effect size of β = 0.30 at α = 0.05.

This study is conducted using the Experience Sampling Method (ESM) to assess daily variability of subjective age, AARC and SRH. Over 10 consecutive days [49], participants completed online surveys using m-Path’s software platform [59]. On Day 1, survey measured socio-demographic information (age, gender, socio-economic status, education level). On days 2 to 10, participants were notified in the evening to collect daily awareness of age-related change, subjective age and SRH. Across the 10-day assessment period, the 78 participants provided a total of 632 valid daily observations, corresponding to an average of 8.10 completed daily questionnaires per participant (SD = 1.58; range = 3–9 days), reflecting an overall compliance rate of approximately 90%. This study obtained ethical approval from the ethics committee at the University of Nîmes (ref. number 2023-0019) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study.

Measures

Awareness of age-related gains and losses

Daily AARC was assessed using the short version of the AARC questionnaire: the AARC-10 [60]. This scale identifies age-related changes across five domains: cognition (COG) health and physical functioning (PHYS), socio-emotional functioning (SC/SE), interpersonal relations (INT), and lifestyle and engagement (LIFE) [22]. The item stem was modified for daily use, « With my increasing age… » was adapted into « With my awareness of ageing today … » [49]. Five of the 10 items evaluate awareness of age-related gains (positive age-related changes), while the other five evaluate awareness of age-related losses (negative age-related changes). For example, concerning cognitive functioning domain, “I have more experience and knowledge to evaluate things and people” and “My mental capacity is declining” are used to assess age related gains and losses respectively. The items assessing negative and positive age-related changes are summed independently.

Subjective age

Daily subjective age was measured with the single question « Some people feel younger or older than their real age. How old do you feel today? » [61]. Participants responded by indicating how old they felt each day. The analyses were performed by subtracting chronological age from subjective age [62]. The difference between one’s chronological and subjective age is usually used as a precise measure of subjective age [63]. A negative value corresponds to a younger self-representation and a positive value to an older self-representation. The absolute discrepancy score was retained here because the sample consisted primarily of young and middle-aged adults. In such samples, absolute discrepancy scores provide a more stable and interpretable metric of subjective age differences, whereas proportional scores may inflate deviations at younger ages and compress variability at older ages [64]. Absolute discrepancy scores have also been widely used in prior research examining associations between subjective age and health outcomes across adulthood [37].

Self-rated health

Daily SRH was assessed with the following question: « Today, how would you rate your health? ». Participants rated their health on a visual analogue scale from 1 to 100 [65]. This question is commonly used to assess subjective health [66], providing a valid measure of individuals’ overall health perception [1].

Data analysis

Given the nested structure of our data inherent in diary studies [67], we used generalized linear mixed models (GLMM). Before conducting the multilevel analyses, the reliability of the AARC scales was examined at both the between- and within-person levels. McDonald’s omega (ω) coefficients were estimated using the omegaSEM package following the multilevel approach [68, 69]. In addition, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were computed for each AARC item using random-intercept mixed models in Jamovi to quantify the proportion of variance attributable to between-person differences. Using GLMM to analyze repeated measures data leads to more accurate estimation than using traditional statistical methods (e.g. linear regression) [70]. Statistical analyses were conducted using jamovi Version 2.3 sofware [71]. Fully unconditional models (i.e., intercept-only models; Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002) were first estimated to verify that sufficient between- and within-person variance existed to justify multilevel analyses. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were then computed for each main variable (SRH, subjective age, AARC-gains, and AARC-losses). This procedure is consistent with the approach used by other researchers [50].

To test our hypothesis, predictor variables of interest (AARC-losses, AARC-gains, subjective age) were disaggregated into between-person and within-person components [49]. Between-person components refer to the person-mean score across all available assessments and capture interindividual variability. Within-person components capture intraindividual variability which means people’s fluctuations around their own mean [72]. Those daily measures were person-mean-centred by subtracting the person-mean score to each observation across study days. All within-person slopes were specified as fixed effects, as models allowing random slopes for daily SPA predictors failed to converge given the limited number of observations per participant.

Significant effects of person-mean AARC-gains (or AARC-losses or subjective age) on SRH score would indicate that AARC-gains (or AARC-losses or subjective age) is associated with SRH at the interindividual level. Any significant effect of within-person AARC-gains (or AARC-losses or subjective age) on SRH indicates that temporary changes of AARC-gains (or AARC-losses or subjective age) below or above the individual’s average are associated with temporary changes in SRH. The total sample was analyzed according to our inclusion and exclusion criteria. Three multilevel models served to test our hypotheses, to examine between-person effects (Model 1), within person effects (Model 2) and the moderating effect of age (Model 3). For all models, the intercepts were the only random parameter; marginal R² is used to report the proportion of variance explained by fixed effect and conditional R² is used to explain the proportion of variance explained by fixed and random effects. Gender, education and socio-economic status were included as control variables. Significant effects of person-mean SPA variables on SRH in model 1 would indicate that inter-individual differences in SPA variables is associated with SRH (H1). Any effect of within-person SPA variables changes on SRH indicates that temporary changes of SPA variables below or above the individual’s average are associated with temporary changes in SRH (H2). Significant interaction effect of age would indicate a differentiated effect of SPA on SRH depending on chronological age (H3).

Intraclass correlation coefficients indicated that approximately half of the variance in SRH was between individuals (ICC = 0.50), whereas subjective age (ICC = 0.74), AARC-gains (ICC = 0.82), and AARC-losses (ICC = 0.80) showed higher between-person consistency. These ICCs are somewhat lower than those reported by similar studies [50] (ICCs = 0.90 for AARC-gains and 0.91 for AARC-losses), indicating a larger proportion of within-person variance (18–20%) in the present data.

Correlations between age and SPA variables suggest a differentiated effect depending on the type of SPA. Age is negatively correlated with AARC-gains (r = − .20 ; p < .001) and positively correlated with AARC-losses (r = .23 ; p < .001). These results suggest that ageing is negatively associated with awareness of age-related change. Age was also negatively correlated with subjective age (r = − .54 ; p < .001), indicating that older participants tended to feel younger relative to their chronological age. Subjective age was negatively correlated with SRH (r = − .29 ; p < .001). It would seem that older individuals manage to feel younger than they actually are, despite being more aware of age-related losses. This interpretation is supported by the weak but significant correlation observed between AARC-losses and subjective age (r = .13 ; p = .001). SRH is, as expected, negatively correlated with AARC-losses (r = .43 ; p < .001).

Internal consistency was evaluated at both the between-person and within-person levels using McDonald’s omega (ω) [69]. For the AARC-gains subscale, reliability was good at the between-person level (ω_between = 0.81) and moderate at the within-person level (ω_within = 0.42). For the AARC-losses subscale, reliability was excellent between-persons (ω_between = 0.90) but lower within-persons (ω_within = 0.35). Item-level ICCs ranged from 0.53 to 0.74 (M = 0.67), with mean ICCs of 0.69 for AARC-gains (items 1–5) and 0.64 for AARC-losses (items 6–10), indicating that most variance occurred between individuals. This aligns with the relatively low within-person ω values and suggests limited intraindividual variability in AARC across days.

Then, we ran a conditional analysis predicting SRH. Results of the linear mixed model that regressed our variables on SRH at both within and between levels are presented in Table 2 (Model 1 and Model 2). Model 1 tested our first hypothesis (H1), which predicted that between-person differences in SPA would be associated with between-person differences in SRH. In line with expectations, results showed significant effects associated with average AARC-losses and average SA. Average SRH was worse for people who reported more AARC-losses compared to other people. Individuals who felt older, reported significantly worse SRH than individuals who felt younger. No significant effect was found for AARC-gains. Model 1 explained 53.1% (conditional R²) and 21.8% (marginal R²) of the between-person variance in SRH score. Model 2 tested our second hypothesis (H2), which stated that daily fluctuations in SPA would covary with daily fluctuations in SRH. On days individuals felt older than usual, they also reported worse SRH. None of the daily AARC-losses and gains effect were significant. Model 2 explained 59.3% (conditional R²) and 9.5% (marginal R²) of the within-person variance in SRH score.

We proceeded with further analysis to test the moderating effect of age (see Model 3 in Table 2). Model 3 tested our third hypothesis (H3), which predicted that chronological age would moderate the association between SPA and SRH at the within- and between-person levels. Results showed significant interactions between chronological age and both average subjective age (p = .02) and daily subjective age (p = .02), indicating that the association between subjective age and SRH varied as a function of chronological age. To examine how average subjective age and daily subjective interact with age to affect SRH, we used simple slopes analysis to understand the specific conditions under which the effect on SRH is heightened. This involved estimating the predicted values of SRH on days people feel older or younger than usual separately for younger (mean − 1 SD) and older (mean + 1 SD) people. As shown in Fig. 1, for average subjective age, younger individuals who felt younger relative to their chronological age reported better overall SRH than older individuals with comparable subjective-age discrepancies. Conversely, younger individuals who felt older on average reported poorer SRH than older individuals with similar average subjective age discrepancies. Similarly, on days when people felt older than usual, younger people reported worse SRH compared to older people. Conversely, on days when people felt younger than usual, younger people reported significantly greater SRH compared to older people (Fig. 2). The effect of daily variations in subjective age appears to be stronger in younger people than in older people.

Discussion

The present study aimed to explore the association between SPA (i.e., AARC and subjective age) and SRH across adulthood, using an experience sampling approach. Consistent with our hypotheses, SPA emerged as important correlates of SRH at both the between- and within-person levels, with particularly strong associations observed for subjective age, and these associations varied by chronological age.

As predicted in H1, individuals who reported higher between-level AARC-losses also reported poorer SRH, extending previous work extending previous work showing that a broad range of perceptions of ageing (e.g., ageing attitudes, age stereotypes) are significant predictors of SRH [73]. Negative attitudes are correlated with poorer SRH. This expected relationship is particularly interesting given the population studied. Previous studies that identified a link between AARC-losses and health outcomes has mainly focused on individuals aged 50 and above [74, 75]. Our findings show that these associations are not restricted to late life, but are also observable in young and midlife adults. This aligns with prior findings showing that positive personal experiences of ageing predict better SRH in middle-aged adults [76], and with lifespan perspectives suggesting that SPA [15], even when still developing in early adulthood, can already shape individuals’ interpretations of their health and functioning. These results suggest that AARC influences health across the lifespan, from young adults to older populations.

In line with earlier work, AARC-gains did not show any significant association with SRH. This is consistent with findings reporting no significant effect of AARC-gains on reasoning performance, memory failures, and physical symptoms [50, 52]. As stereotype embodiment theory points out [77], negative SPA may have a more immediate and powerful impact than positive SPA on health. This potential asymmetry in how AARC-losses and AARC-gains influence health is also consistent with Sabatini’s work [74], which indicates that SRH is affected when individuals experience high levels of AARC-losses, even when they also perceive high levels of AARC-gains. However, another study showed a protective effect of AARC-gains in the context of negative affect and stress reactivity. In fact, this correlation was weaker when people perceived more AARC-gains than usual even if they perceived also more AARC-losses [51].

Concerning average subjective age, the results showed that individuals who felt older relative to their chronological age reported poorer overall SRH. This finding is consistent with previous research linking subjective age to health outcomes, including SRH and psychological well-being, in middle-aged and older adults [37, 78–80]. Importantly, the present results extend this evidence by demonstrating that this association is already observable across a broader adult age range. From a theoretical perspective, this association can be understood within lifespan developmental and subjective ageing frameworks, which conceptualise subjective age as a global self-evaluative marker integrating accumulated experiences, expectations, and perceived age-related changes [15, 39]. Feeling older than one’s chronological age may reflect heightened awareness of functional limitations, reduced perceived resources, or more negative interpretations of bodily and psychological signals, all of which are central components of self-rated health judgments [21, 36]. Notably, the fact that this association emerged in a sample characterised by generally positive ageing perceptions and relatively low levels of AARC-losses compared to European data [81], suggests that the observed effects may represent conservative estimates. Previous research indicates that when ageing attitudes are more favourable, the link between subjective age and health-related outcomes can be attenuated or even absent [82, 83]. In this context, the present findings underscore the robustness and relevance of subjective age as a correlate of SRH, even in relatively positively selected adult samples.

As hypothesised in H2, daily deviations in subjective age covaried with daily SRH. On days when individuals felt older than usual, they also evaluated their health more negatively. These findings are consistent with the IMSA, SPA influence subsequent emotional, cognitive, and behavioural responses, thereby shaping how individuals evaluate their daily functioning and health. They also align with prior daily-diary studies showing that subjective age fluctuates in response to daily depressive symptoms [84], negative affect, stressors [24] and physical symptoms such as pain [85], underscoring its role as a particularly sensitive indicator of everyday well-being.

In contrast, daily AARC-losses were not robustly associated with daily SRH. Although previous studies have linked AARC-losses to control beliefs [52], negative affect [49], and memory [50]. The present findings suggest that AARC-losses may be less sensitive to short-term fluctuations in self-rated health than subjective age. This pattern is consistent with the conceptual structure of AARC-losses as a cumulative construct reflecting awareness of age-related changes across multiple life domains (e.g., health, cognition, interpersonal relations, lifestyle), rather than a momentary self-appraisal. Supporting this interpretation, item-level intraclass correlations (ICCs) indicated substantial between-person consistency (range = 0.53–0.74), suggesting that a large proportion of variance in AARC-losses reflects stable interindividual differences. At the same time, some domains—particularly items related to interpersonal relations—showed lower ICCs, indicating that certain aspects of AARC may be more responsive to daily social experiences than others. Compared with previous diary research reporting very high ICCs for AARC-gains and losses [50], the present study revealed a similar proportion of within-person variability (approximately 18–20%), highlighting that AARC is neither purely trait-like nor uniformly dynamic. Rather, these findings underscore the importance of assessing AARC at both the between- and within-person levels, while recognising that daily effects may be more difficult to detect for constructs that integrate experiences across domains and time. This may partly reflect the fact that AARC relies on more explicit and reflective appraisals of age-related change, which are less immediately responsive to transient daily states [22]. In contrast, subjective age represents a more global and immediately accessible self-evaluation, likely operating at a more intuitive level of processing, which may explain its stronger and more consistent day-to-day association with SRH in the present study [22]. Consistent with the between-person findings, daily AARC-gains did not show reliable associations with daily SRH, suggesting that positive age-related changes may function more as longer-term resources than as immediate drivers of daily health evaluations.

Contrary to our hypothesis (H3), the association between subjective age and SRH was stronger among younger adults. In concrete terms, this indicates that on days when younger adults felt older than usual, they also evaluated their health more negatively, whereas an identical absolute deviation in subjective age was less strongly associated with health evaluations among older adults. A similar pattern emerged at the between-person level: younger adults who felt older relative to their chronological age reported poorer overall SRH than older adults with comparable average subjective-age discrepancies. These results challenge the notion that subjective age is only associated with late-life outcomes [86]. Indeed, several studies suggest report stronger effects of subjective age among older adults, for example on sleep quality [57] or on depressive symptoms [84]. However, an impact of subjective age on depressive symptoms has been identified in young adults, although it is less pronounced [84]. This could be explained by the fact that younger individuals have lower expectations regarding ageing compared to older adults [87]. Although initially unexpected, this pattern becomes coherent when viewed through a lifespan perspective. First, subjective age is more labile in young adulthood, when age-related identities are still forming and views of ageing tend to be more negative [82]. Recent lifespan research shows that subjective age fluctuates most strongly in early adulthood, whereas proportional age discrepancies become more stable only later in life [88]. Consequently, feeling older than usual may be particularly salient for younger adults, as it resonates more strongly with their age-related expectations and internalised views of ageing [89]. Second, older adults may be less affected by transient negative perceptions because they have developed more effective coping strategies and greater experience managing age-related changes over time, which could make us less susceptible to the potential effects of negative perceptions [90]. Finally, using the absolute discrepancy score may contribute to stronger associations among younger adults, as this metric is more linearly related to chronological age than proportional alternatives [64]. However, the age moderation observed here is theoretically interpretable within lifespan perspectives and provides valuable insight into how subjective age functions differently across adulthood.

Importantly, the directionality of the associations observed in this study should be interpreted with care, as subjective age and self-rated health are embedded in dynamic, reciprocal processes. Empirical evidence shows that poorer self-rated health can lead individuals to feel older [91], a pattern that has also been documented in non-Western contexts [92]. This aligns with IMSA [21], which conceptualises subjective age both as an antecedent and a consequence of health-related experiences. From this perspective, daily subjective age may partly reflect momentary fluctuations in perceived health. Beyond this bidirectional influence, theoretical models have proposed that ageing perceptions and health may form ongoing feedback loops, in which functional decline contributes to more negative ageing perceptions that subsequently reduce behavioural engagement and further accelerate decline [93]. However, while these short-term reciprocal influences are well documented, a substantial body of longitudinal work indicates that the pathway from SPA to later health is typically stronger and more stable than the reverse: negative SPA predict functional decline, disease incidence, and even mortality years later [63, 94, 95], whereas the effects of health on subsequent SPA tend to be weaker and more transient [96]. Bringing these strands together, the short-term reciprocal processes highlighted above do not contradict, but rather complement, the more robust long-term pathway from SPA to health. These findings also reinforce the concept of subjective ageing as “a biopsychosocial marker of ageing” affecting SRH [97], not only among older adults, but also among young people.

This study has several strengths. By using an intensive daily diary design, we were able to capture the dynamic nature of self-perceptions of ageing and, to our knowledge, to examine for the first time their association with daily self-rated health across adulthood. In addition, adopting a lifespan perspective provided new insights into how SPA–SRH associations vary as a function of chronological age. However, there are limitations to consider. The study sample was modest in size, composed mainly of highly educated adults and included few older-old participants, which limits the generalisability of the findings. Moreover, most participants reported predominantly positive SPA, with relatively low levels of AARC, resulting in an underrepresentation of AARC profiles typically observed in broader populations [74]. This restricted variability may have attenuated some between-person associations. In addition, while subjective age was reliably assessed, the French version of the AARC questionnaire has not yet been validated, which may introduce interpretation biases.

From a methodological perspective, the 10-day observation window restricts conclusions about longer-term developmental processes. While daily diary designs are well suited for capturing short-term within-person dynamics, extended or multi-wave ESM designs will be needed to determine whether the patterns observed here persist and accumulate over longer timescales. Furthermore, because the present analyses relied on same moment associations, they do not allow conclusions about temporal ordering or causality. More broadly, the present findings should be interpreted within a dynamic framework. The observed associations likely reflect short-term interactions embedded in a complex system in which subjective age and health mutually influence one another on a daily basis, while also contributing to longer-term developmental pathways.

Taken together, these limitations point to several promising directions for future research. Larger and more diverse samples are needed to examine age-related mechanisms more precisely and to identify both shared and age-specific pathways through which SPA influence daily health. Given findings by Hicks et al. [64], showing that positive affect mediates the association between favourable ageing perceptions and better SRH, future studies should test whether negative affect could similarly mediate the link between subjective age and poorer daily health. Additional work is also needed to better understand how AARC-gains and losses interact, as some evidence suggests that positive attitudes may buffer the detrimental effects of losses under certain conditions [49]. More broadly, assessing multiple SPA constructs simultaneously, including attitudes toward ageing, which have been shown to moderate the daily association between subjective age and depressive symptoms [49, 53], may help clarify how different ageing-related processes jointly shape daily well-being. Methodologically, future research should incorporate lagged associations, multi-wave ESM approaches, or longer measurement periods to determine how short-term fluctuations accumulate into longer-term health trajectories [22]. Finally, the heightened sensitivity to subjective age observed among younger adults in this study underscores the importance of investigating SPA dynamics earlier in adulthood, where they may represent meaningful targets for early intervention and prevention [98].

Conclusions

The present research demonstrates that SPA are dynamic daily processes that meaningfully relate to how individuals evaluate their health across adulthood. By combining a lifespan perspective with an intensive daily design, this study shows that between-person differences in subjective age and AARC-losses and within-person fluctuations in subjective age contribute to daily SRH, and that associations between subjective age and SRH vary by age. These findings underscore the importance of moving beyond static conceptualisations of SPA and highlight the value of examining ageing perceptions in everyday life. Overall, the results suggest that fostering more adaptive SPA throughout adulthood may play a role in supporting better subjective health [99].
