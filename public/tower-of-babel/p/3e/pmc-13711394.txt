Targeting the SMG9–Wnt/β-catenin axis: A novel mechanism by which ginsenoside Rh2 suppresses triple-negative breast cancer progression
Authors: Shuang Liu, Yueqi Wang, Ye Zhang, Kun Chen, Yi-Nan Wang, Qiaoxuan Zhang, Xing Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a highly aggressive malignancy with poor prognosis and limited therapeutic options, underscoring the need for new treatment approaches. Ginsenoside Rh2 (G-Rh2) has demonstrated antitumor effects across multiple cancer types; however, its effects and underlying molecular mechanisms in TNBC remain poorly understood.MethodsTNBC cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 assay.Flow cytometry was used to evaluate cell cycle distribution and cell apoptosis. Proteomic analysis was performed to identify potential downstream targets of G-Rh2. The functional roles of downstream targets were validated by western blotting, flow cytometry, and luciferase reporter assays. Furthermore, G-Rh2 suppresses tumor progression by targeting the SMG9-Wnt/β-catenin axis. Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), defined by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), is clinically recognized as a breast cancer subtype with an unfavorable prognosis [1,2]. TNBC accounts for approximately 10%–15% of all breast cancer cases and is characterized by highly aggressive biological behavior, including a high risk of early recurrence and distant metastasis [3,4]. Given the absence of well-established therapeutic targets, chemotherapy remains the primary treatment strategy. However, its clinical benefit is often compromised by the rapid emergence of chemoresistance and subsequent disease progression [5,6]. Although recent therapeutic advances, including immune checkpoint inhibitors, have shown encouraging results in selected patients with advanced TNBC [7], their overall clinical impact remains limited. These challenges highlight the urgent need to develop safer and more broadly applicable therapeutic agents and rational combination strategies for TNBC management [8,9].

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) and plant-derived natural products have been extensively used in China and other parts of Asia for centuries. Advances in modern pharmacological and analytical techniques have facilitated the systematic isolation and characterization of active compounds from medicinal herbs [10,11]. Among these compounds, phytochemicals with anticancer properties have attracted considerable attention. Ginseng Radix et Rhizoma, a fundamental component of TCM, is well known for its adaptogenic and immunomodulatory effects, which are largely attributed to triterpenoid saponins known as ginsenosides [12]. Ginsenoside Rh2 (G-Rh2), a major bioactive constituent of ginseng, possesses broad pharmacological activities and has shown significant antitumor potential. Accumulating evidence suggests that G-Rh2 inhibits proliferation, migration, and invasion while promoting apoptosis in various cancers, including hepatocellular, lung, colorectal, cervical, and breast cancers [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. Furthermore, the combined use of G-Rh2 and anti-PD-L1 antibody has been shown to enhance CD8+ T-cell infiltration, proliferation, and activation within the tumor microenvironment [18]. Another study reported that combining G-Rh2 with EGFR-targeted liposomes markedly suppressed TNBC cell proliferation and metastasis [19]. Despite these promising findings, the therapeutic role of G-Rh2 in TNBC remains incompletely defined, and the underlying molecular mechanisms have not been fully clarified, limiting its potential application in TNBC management.

This study aimed to elucidate the inhibitory effects of G-Rh2 on TNBC progression and to provide a scientific basis for its potential clinical application. To this end, we employed multiple experimental approaches, with particular emphasis on proteomic analysis, luciferase reporter assays and molecular docking. We further sought to define the molecular mechanisms and signaling pathways regulated by G-Rh2, thereby providing insight into novel therapeutic strategies for TNBC and advancing the understanding of the anticancer properties of natural compounds.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and reagents

TNBC cell lines MDA-MB-468 and BT-549 were obtained from the ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were maintained in DMEM/F-12 (1:1) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin and incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Routine screening for mycoplasma contamination was performed, and cells were seeded as required for each experiment. Ginsenoside Rh2 (HY-N0605; purity, 98.87%) was obtained from MedChemExpress (MCE, USA).

CCK8 assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, Tabaru, Japan). TNBC cells were seeded into 96-well plates (5000 cells/well) and allowed to adhere for 18 h, followed by treatment with 0-80 μM G-Rh2 for 48 h. The culture medium was then replaced with medium containing 10% CCK-8 solution and incubated for 2 h. Absorbance at 450/650 nm was measured using a BioTek EPOCH microplate reader. All CCK-8 assays were performed in three independent biological replicates, with five technical replicates for each treatment condition. Half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were calculated by fitting dose-response curves using the four-parameter log-logistic model implemented in the R package drc (version 3.0-1).

Cell cycle analysis

Cell cycle analysis was performed as described previously [20]. MDA-MB-468 and BT-549 cells were seeded in six-well plates at 1 × 105 cells/mL and allowed to attach for 24 h. MDA-MB-468 cells were then treated with G-Rh2 at 0, 5, 10, and 20 μM, whereas BT-549 cells were treated with 0, 10, 20, and 40 μM G-Rh2 for 48 h under the same experimental conditions. After treatment, cells were collected by trypsinization (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), centrifuged, washed with PBS, and fixed in 70% ice-cold ethanol overnight at 4°C. Cells were stained with 500 μL PI/RNase A solution (9:1, v/v) for 30 min at 4°C in the dark, and cell cycle profiles were analyzed using a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments (n = 3).

Cell apoptosis assays

Apoptotic cell death was assessed using the Annexin V–FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (KeyGen Biotech, Nanjing, China). Briefly, MDA-MB-468 and BT-549 cells treated with the indicated concentrations of G-Rh2 were harvested with EDTA-free trypsin, centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 5 min, and washed twice with cold PBS before being resuspended in 500 μL of binding buffer. The cells were then incubated with 5 μL Annexin V–FITC and 5 μL PI for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Apoptosis was analyzed using a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments (n = 3).

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing PMSF and protease/phosphatase inhibitors (Fdbio Science, Hangzhou, China). Equal amounts of protein (30 μg) were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Roche, Switzerland). The membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies included PARP, caspase-3, β-catenin, c-Myc, Cyclin D1 (1:1000, CST), SMG9 (1:1000, Abcam), and β-actin (1:5000, Bioss). Detailed information on antibody sources, catalog numbers, dilution ratios, and clone numbers, when applicable, is provided in Table S1. Protein signals were detected using Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Millipore, MA, USA). Automatic exposure settings were used to avoid signal saturation, and all bands included in densitometric analysis were within the linear detection range. Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software (version 1.52a; National Institutes of Health, MD, USA); target protein levels were normalized to β-actin and relative expression levels were calculated against the control group. Western blotting was independently performed three times using separate biological samples (n = 3).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, NY, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The extracted RNA was subsequently reverse-transcribed into cDNA, followed by quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Takara, Kyoto, Japan). The specific primers used for SMG9 amplification were as follows: forward, 5′-GATGTGTTGGTGGTTGGTGTC-3′; reverse, 5′-CCCTCGTTCCTTCATTTCAGC-3′ (Servicebio Technology, Wuhan, China). GAPDH was used as the endogenous control, with the following primers: forward, 5′-GAAGGTGAAGGTCGGAGTC-3′; reverse, 5′-GAAGATGGTGATGGGATTTC-3′ (Servicebio Technology, Wuhan, China). Relative SMG9 mRNA expression levels were calculated using the comparative Ct (2−ΔΔCt) method. All RT-qPCR assays were performed in triplicate and independently repeated three times.

Proteomic analysis

Proteomic analysis was performed using three independent biological replicates per group (n = 3). MDA-MB-468 cells were seeded into 60-mm culture dishes (Corning) and incubated for 12–18 h to allow cell adhesion. Cells were then treated with 20 μM G-Rh2 or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as a control for 48 h. Subsequently, cells were harvested, and total proteins was extracted according to established protocols for immunoblot analysis. Proteins were precipitated with acetone, and protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo, Waltham, MA, USA). For each sample, 100 μg of protein was denatured in 8 M urea containing 0.1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.5). The denatured proteins were reduced with 5 mM Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP) and alkylated with 20 mM chloroacetamide (CAA), both from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Proteins were then digested overnight at 37°C with trypsin at a 1:50 enzyme-to-protein ratio in 0.1 M ammonium bicarbonate (ABC, Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA). The resulting peptides were purified using C18 pipette tips (GL Sciences, Japan), collected by centrifugation, and dried under vacuum. Quantitative proteomic profiling was performed on a nano-RPLC-MS/MS system equipped with a Q Exactive mass spectrometer and an Ultimate™ 3000 RSLC nano HPLC system. The raw MS/MS data were analyzed using MaxQuant (version 1.5.1.6) with default parameters.

Cell transfection

MDA-MB-468 and BT-549 cells were seeded in 60-mm dishes with antibiotic-free medium for 24 h. Upon reaching 50-60% confluence, the medium was replaced with serum-free Opti-Minimal Essential Medium (Opti-MEM). At approximately 70% confluence, cells were transfected with 2 μg of either the pCMV5-SMG9 plasmid or the control plasmid pCMV5-VEC using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Invitrogen, California, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were then incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 for 8 h, after which the medium was replaced with complete medium containing 10% serum. Transfection efficiency was assessed by Western blot analysis.

Luciferase reporter assay

TCF/LEF transcriptional activity was evaluated using the TOP/FOP-FLASH luciferase reporter assay. MDA-MB-468 and BT-549 cells were transiently transfected with 0.5 μg of either the TOP-FLASH or FOP-FLASH plasmid, together with 12.5 ng of Renilla plasmid, using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Invitrogen). After transfection, cells were treated with the indicated concentrations of G-Rh2 for 24 h. The luciferase activity was quantified using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega), and firefly luminescence was normalized to Renilla luminescence. All luciferase reporter assays were performed in three independent biological replicates (n = 3), with five technical replicates for each experimental condition in each experiment.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was conducted using AutoDock4 [21]. The chemical structure of G-Rh2 was generated using ChemDraw 14.0 and used as the ligand. The crystal structure of SMG9 (PDB ID: 5NKM) was obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. All water molecules were removed, and the protein structure was optimized using MGTools (version 1.5.6). AutoGrid4 and AutoDock4 were run with the Lamarckian genetic algorithm. The ligand was modeled with flexible conformations, and the docking procedure was repeated 100 times. The binding affinity between G-Rh2 and SMG9 was assessed using the calculated binding free energy, with lower values indicating stronger binding tendency.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and visualization were performed in R (version 4.2.1). Clustering analysis of the proteomic data was conducted using the stats package, and heatmaps were generated using the ComplexHeatmap package (version 2.13.1). The drc package (version 3.0-1) was used to model and estimate the IC50 values of G-Rh2 in TNBC cell lines. Comparisons between groups were performed using two-tailed Student's t-test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical significance is indicated as ns, not significant; ∗P < 0.05; ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗P < 0.001; and ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001 throughout the manuscript and figure legends.

Results

G-Rh2 reduced the viability of TNBC cell lines

The cytotoxic effects of G-Rh2 on TNBC cells were examined by treating MDA-MB-468 and BT-549 cells with increasing concentrations of G-Rh2, with blank and vehicle control groups included. Cell viability was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. G-Rh2 markedly reduced the viability of both cell lines in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1A and B). Nonlinear regression analysis showed that the IC50 was 31.74 μM (95% CI: 29.33-34.43 μM) for MDA-MB-468 cells (Fig. 1A) and 49.63 μM (95% CI: 45.88-54.01 μM) for BT-549 cells (Fig. 1B). These findings demonstrate that G-Rh2 significantly reduces TNBC cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner.

The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of G-Rh2 in TNBC cells. Dose-response curves for MDA-MB-468 cells (A) and BT-549 cells (B) following 48 h of treatment with different concentrations of G-Rh2, as assessed by the CCK-8 assay. The IC50 values were derived by nonlinear regression analysis.

G-Rh2 induced G0/G1 phase arrest in TNBC cells

Uncontrolled cell proliferation, driven in part by dysregulated cell cycle progression, is a hallmark of malignant tumors and a key contributor to the initiation and progression of TNBC [22,23]. To assess the impact of G-Rh2 on cell cycle regulation, TNBC cells were exposed to different concentrations of G-Rh2, with a control group included. Flow cytometry revealed a dose-dependent increase in the proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase compared with controls (Fig. 2A and B). Treatment with 10 μM G-Rh2 significantly induced G0/G1 phase arrest in MDA-MB-468 cells (Fig. 2C; ns, not significant; ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001), whereas BT-549 cells required 20 μM G-Rh2 to achieve a similar effect (Fig. 2D; ns, not significant; ∗∗∗P < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001). The findings indicate that G-Rh2 effectively inhibits the G1/S phase transition in TNBC cells in a dose-dependent manner.

(C, D) The proportions of cells in the G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases were quantified for both MDA-MB-468 and BT-549 cells. Ns indicates not significant; ∗P < 0.05; ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗P < 0.001; and ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.

G-Rh2 promoted dose-dependent apoptosis in TNBC cells

Given the close association between apoptosis and cell proliferation [24], we next investigated whether G-Rh2 modulated apoptosis in TNBC cells. Flow cytometry demonstrated a dose-dependent increase in the proportion of apoptotic cells after G-Rh2 treatment (Fig. 3A). Significant apoptosis was observed at 10 μM in MDA-MB-468 cells and 20 μM in BT-549 cells (Fig. 3B; ∗P < 0.05; ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001). Consistently, western blotting showed significant increases in cleaved PARP and cleaved caspase-3, both of which are well-established markers of apoptosis activation (Fig. 3C). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that G-Rh2 effectively induces apoptosis in TNBC cells.

G-Rh2 suppressed TNBC progression via downregulation of SMG9

To investigate the molecular mechanism underlying the antitumor effects of G-Rh2, MDA-MB-468 cells were treated with 20 μM G-Rh2 for 48 h and subjected to proteomic analysis to identify potential downstream targets. Differential expression profiling revealed a panel of significantly downregulated proteins, among which SMG9 showed a marked reduction (Fig. 4A). Given our previous findings that SMG9 functions as an oncogene by promoting cell proliferation, apoptosis resistance, migration, and invasion [20], its downregulation suggested that SMG9 might mediate the inhibitory effects of G-Rh2 on TNBC progression. To validate these findings, SMG9 expression was evaluated by western blotting in MDA-MB-468 and BT-549 cells following G-Rh2 treatment. Consistent with the proteomic results, G-Rh2 significantly decreased SMG9 protein levels in both MDA-MB-468 (Fig. 4B; ∗∗P < 0.01) and BT-549 cells (Fig. 4C; ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001) compared with the control groups. To determine whether SMG9 downregulation occurred at the transcriptional level, SMG9 mRNA expression was assessed by RT-qPCR in MDA-MB-468 and BT-549 cells under the same G-Rh2 treatment conditions used for protein analysis. In contrast to the marked reduction in SMG9 protein abundance, G-Rh2 treatment did not significantly alter SMG9 mRNA levels in either cell line, suggesting that the decrease in SMG9 protein is unlikely to result from transcriptional repression (Fig. 4D). Collectively, these findings suggest that G-Rh2 exerts its antitumor effects in TNBC, at least in part, by reducing SMG9 protein abundance through a post-transcriptional mechanism, rather than by suppressing SMG9 mRNA expression.

G-Rh2 inhibited activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway

Aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is strongly linked to tumor initiation, progression, and malignant transformation [25,26]. Notably, our previous study demonstrated that Wnt/β-catenin signaling is a key mediator of SMG9-driven hepatocarcinogenesis [20], linking SMG9 function to this oncogenic pathway. Given the established involvement of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in cancer and its association with SMG9, we explored the potential modulation of this pathway by G-Rh2 in TNBC. The expression of key pathway components, including β-catenin, c-Myc, and Cyclin D1, was assessed by western blotting in MDA-MB-468 and BT-549 cells following G-Rh2 treatment. G-Rh2 significantly reduced the protein levels of β-catenin, c-Myc, and Cyclin D1 in MDA-MB-468 (Fig. 4E; ∗∗P < 0.01) and BT-549 cells (Fig. 4F; ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗P < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001). Collectively, these data indicate that G-Rh2 inhibits the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in TNBC cells and, together with our previous observation implicating SMG9 in Wnt-driven tumorigenesis, raise the possibility that G-Rh2 exerts antitumor effects partly by disrupting the SMG9–Wnt/β-catenin regulatory axis.

Overexpression of SMG9 attenuated the inhibitory effects of G-Rh2 on the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in TNBC cells

To investigate whether the inhibitory effect of G-Rh2 on the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is mediated by SMG9, two complementary experimental strategies were employed. First, MDA-MB-468 and BT-549 cells were transfected with either a SMG9-overexpressing plasmid (OE-SMG9) or an empty vector, followed by treatment with or without G-Rh2. Western blotting analysis demonstrated that SMG9 overexpression markedly increased β-catenin, c-Myc, and Cyclin D1 levels in both MDA-MB-468 (Fig. 5A) and BT-549 (Fig. 5B) cells. Notably, SMG9 overexpression substantially mitigated the G-Rh2-induced downregulation of these proteins (Fig. 5A and B). To further confirm these findings, TOP/FOP Flash luciferase reporter assays were conducted. This assay evaluated Wnt/β-catenin signaling activity by comparing the transcriptional activity of the β-catenin/TCF-responsive TOP reporter with that of the mutant FOP reporter, which served as a negative control. In both cell lines, G-Rh2 treatment suppressed TOP transcriptional activity, and this effect was effectively reversed by SMG9 overexpression (Fig. 5C and D; ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001). Taken together, these data indicate that SMG9 plays a crucial role in mediating the inhibitory effects of G-Rh2 on the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in TNBC cells.

SMG9 overexpression attenuated G-Rh2–induced cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in TNBC cells

To explore the role of SMG9 in mediating the biological effects of G-Rh2, MDA-MB-468 and BT-549 cells overexpressing SMG9 were treated with or without G-Rh2 at the indicated concentration. Flow cytometry revealed that SMG9 overexpression significantly attenuated G-Rh2–induced cell cycle arrest in MDA-MB-468 and BT-549 cells (Fig. 6A and B; ns, not significant; ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗P < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001). To assess the impact of SMG9 on G-Rh2–induced apoptosis, SMG9-overexpressing cells were exposed to G-Rh2 and then analyzed by flow cytometry. The results demonstrated that SMG9 overexpression markedly reduced G-Rh2–induced apoptosis in MDA-MB-468 and BT-549 cells (Fig. 6C and D; ∗∗∗P < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001). Overall, these findings suggest that SMG9 overexpression counteracts G-Rh2–induced cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in TNBC cells.

Molecular docking revealed the direct binding of G-Rh2 to SMG9

To determine whether G-Rh2 directly binds to and regulates SMG9, molecular docking analysis was conducted to characterize protein–ligand interactions at the atomic level. Among 100 predicted docking conformations, the lowest-energy complex was selected, with a minimum binding energy of −3.4 kcal/mol. The docking model revealed several stabilizing hydrogen bonds: a hydrogen atom on one six-membered ring of G-Rh2 formed a hydrogen bond with the oxygen atom of Val83 (2.8 Å). Additionally, the oxygen atom on the same ring formed a hydrogen bond with the amino hydrogen of Arg87 (2.6 Å), and a hydrogen atom on another six-membered ring formed a hydrogen bond with the oxygen atom of Asp203 (2.7 Å) (Fig. S1A and B). These interactions contributed to the stable association between G-Rh2 and SMG9, thereby supporting the inhibitory potential of G-Rh2 against SMG9 activity. In addition, G-Rh2 primarily engaged the active site of SMG9 through hydrophobic interactions and van der Waals forces, involving key residues including Val83, Arg87, Leu90, Leu136, Phe139, Lys204, Val205, Ile207, Leu259, Leu261, and Ser262 (Fig. S1C). Collectively, these molecular docking results provide structural evidence that G-Rh2 directly targetes and binds SMG9, supporting a potential mechanism of SMG9 regulation by G-Rh2.

Discussion

Ginseng, a traditional Chinese medicinal herb native to East Asia, has been widely used for centuries because of its therapeutic benefits. Ginsenosides, the principal bioactive constituents of ginseng, comprise more than 40 identified types and are categorized into four major classes: protopanaxatriol, protopanaxadiol, oleanolic acid, and pseudo-ginsenoside aglycones [27]. Ginsenosides exert anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, vasodilatory, and antitumor effects, supporting their potential therapeutic applications in metabolic disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and oncological diseases [[28], [29], [30]]. Among them, G-Rh2, a protopanaxatriol-type saponin, exhibits potent antitumor activity across diverse cancer types, making it a compelling candidate for further pharmacological and therapeutic research [14,31]. Although G-Rh2 has been reported to exert cytotoxic effects in multiple malignancies, including breast cancer, its precise role in TNBC has remained largely unclear. In the present study, G-Rh2 induced dose-dependent G0/G1 phase arrest and apoptosis in TNBC cells. Given the aggressive phenotype and limited therapeutic options of TNBC, these results identify G-Rh2 as a potential therapeutic candidate. Consistent with its pro-apoptotic role, G-Rh2 treatment increased the levels of cleaved PARP and cleaved caspase-3, confirming activation of apoptotic cascades. These observations align with the concept that restoring apoptosis is a critical therapeutic strategy in cancers such as TNBC, which are characterized by apoptotic evasion.

The mechanism by which G-Rh2 regulates TNBC progression remains poorly understood, with only one report by Ding et al. suggesting that G-Rh2 may modulate proliferation and apoptosis in TNBC cells through the IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway [32]. In this study, proteomic analyses identified SMG9 as a key downstream target downregulated by G-Rh2. As a core component of the nonsense-mediated mRNA decay complex, SMG9 was previously identified by our group as an oncogene [20,33]. Rescue experiments showed that SMG9 overexpression substantially mitigated G-Rh2-mediated cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, underscoring its pivotal role in the antitumor effects of G-Rh2. Furthermore, molecular docking analysis revealed a stable interaction between G-Rh2 and SMG9, suggesting that SMG9 may serve not only as a downstream effector but also as a direct molecular target of G-Rh2. The docking-predicted G-Rh2-binding pocket was located within the SMG9 G-domain region. Comparison with published structural information on the SMG8-SMG9 and SMG1-SMG8-SMG9 complexes suggests that this predicted binding region does not fully overlap with the major SMG8-or SMG1-associated interaction interfaces on SMG9 [34,35]. Therefore, the present data do not support a direct competitive model in which G-Rh2 occupies the same SMG9 binding site used by SMG8 or SMG1. Nevertheless, indirect effects on SMG9 conformation, SMG9 protein stability, or SMG1C complex stability cannot be excluded.

Consistent with the RT-qPCR results described above, G-Rh2 reduced SMG9 protein abundance without significantly decreasing SMG9 mRNA expression, indicating RNA-protein discordance. This finding supports a post-transcriptional mechanism in which G-Rh2 may regulate SMG9 primarily by altering protein stability and/or turnover rather than by suppressing transcription. Previous studies have shown that small-molecule binding can induce conformational changes or perturb protein-protein interaction surfaces, thereby exposing destabilizing regions or weakening the stability of protein complexes [36,37]. In this context, the predicted interaction between G-Rh2 and SMG9 may facilitate SMG9 clearance through ubiquitin-proteasome-dependent degradation and/or autophagy-lysosome-associated proteostasis pathways [38]. This interpretation is also consistent with previous reports showing that G-Rh2 can promote degradation of oncogenic proteins and modulate autophagy-related processes in tumor cells [[39], [40], [41], [42], [43]]. Further studies using proteasome and lysosome inhibitors, ubiquitination assays, and SMG9 protein half-life analyses are required to define the predominant pathway responsible for G-Rh2-induced SMG9 reduction in TNBC cells. Collectively, these findings revealed a previously unrecognized antitumor mechanism of G-Rh2 and underscored SMG9 as a promising therapeutic target in TNBC.

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway plays a crucial role in cancer development by promoting genomic instability, uncontrolled proliferation, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. Aberrant activation of this pathway is strongly associated with poor prognosis in various cancers, including TNBC [[44], [45], [46]]. In this study, G-Rh2 treatment markedly suppressed Wnt/β-catenin signaling, as evidenced by reduced TOP/FOP Flash reporter activity and decreased expression of β-catenin, c-Myc, and Cyclin D1. Mechanistically, SMG9 overexpression reversed the inhibitory effects of G-Rh2 on β-catenin transcriptional activity and downstream target expression, supporting SMG9 as a functional mediator of Wnt/β-catenin regulation. Although these rescue experiments indicate that SMG9 contributes to G-Rh2-induced cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis, and Wnt/β-catenin inhibition, they do not fully determine whether SMG9 loss alone is sufficient to reproduce the complete G-Rh2 phenotype in TNBC cells. Notably, our previous study showed that SMG9 knockdown induced G0/G1 arrest and apoptosis and inhibited β-catenin signaling in hepatocellular carcinoma [20], suggesting that SMG9 depletion may partially phenocopy G-Rh2-induced effects. Future siRNA-, shRNA-, or CRISPR-based SMG9 loss-of-function studies in TNBC models are needed to clarify the necessity of SMG9 in the antitumor mechanism of G-Rh2. Moreover, the precise mechanism by which SMG9 regulates Wnt/β-catenin signaling remains unclear. As a component of the SMG1–SMG8–SMG9 complex, SMG9 may influence this pathway through NMD-dependent regulation of Wnt-related transcripts [35,47,48]. However, emerging evidence also suggests potential NMD-independent functions of SMG9, including transcriptional dysregulation in SMG9-related disorders without clear impairment of PTC-induced NMD [49,50] and direct regulation of GPX4 degradation during ferroptosis [51]. Therefore, SMG9 may regulate Wnt/β-catenin signaling through both transcript surveillance and protein-level mechanisms. Importantly, the present study does not demonstrate that G-Rh2 directly disrupts SMG1C assembly or inhibits SMG1 kinase activity. Rather, the G-Rh2–SMG9 interaction should be interpreted as a potential upstream event associated with SMG9 downregulation and subsequent Wnt/β-catenin pathway inhibition. Future studies examining SMG1C assembly, SMG1 kinase activity, UPF1 phosphorylation, NMD activity, Wnt-related transcript stability, and SMG9-interacting proteins are needed to address the unresolved mechanistic questions raised by the present study. Collectively, these findings suggest that G-Rh2 exerts antitumor activity, at least in part, through the SMG9–Wnt/β-catenin signaling axis, highlighting a potential therapeutic strategy for TNBC.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study establishes G-Rh2 as a potent suppressor of TNBC progression through targeted inhibition of the SMG9–Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Beyond uncovering a novel molecular mechanism, our findings highlight SMG9 as a previously unrecognized therapeutic target in TNBC. Given the scarcity of effective treatments and the poor clinical outcomes associated with this aggressive subtype, the ability of G-Rh2 to attenuate key oncogenic pathways underscores its translational potential as a candidate for targeted therapy. Further investigations are warranted to define the precise molecular sites through which G-Rh2 regulates SMG9 and to evaluate the inhibitory effects of G-Rh2 on TNBC in vivo.
