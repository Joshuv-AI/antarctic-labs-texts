Tissue-specific partitioning of flavonoids and phenolic acids coordinates bioactivities in Ormosia henryi Prain
Authors: Ruyi Zhang, Lufeng Dan, Li Su, Xiaoli Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The accumulation patterns of bioactive compounds in Ormosia henryi Prain, a traditional Chinese medicinal herb, remain poorly understood, limiting its potential for development. In this study, LC–MS/MS-based untargeted metabolomics was used to profile metabolic patterns across six tissues: new leaves (NL), old leaves (OL), stem bark (SB), stem xylem (SX), root bark (RB), and root xylem (RX). Multivariate analyses (PCA, OPLS-DA) identified key differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs), including 34 phenolic compounds: 29 flavonoids and 5 phenolic acids. The total flavonoid content (TFC) was highest in the old leaves, while the total phenolic content (TPC) peaked in the root bark. Tissue extracts demonstrated strong antioxidant and hypoglycemic activities, with the old leaves showing the most significant bioactivity. Integrated correlation analysis further revealed significant relationships between TFC/TPC, core metabolites, and biological activities, highlighting potential biomarkers for functional evaluation. This study reveals the tissue-specific accumulation of bioactive phenolics in O. henryi Prain, providing valuable insights for the development of natural antioxidants, hypoglycemic drugs, and functional foods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at10.1186/s12870-025-07390-0.

Introduction

Given the increasing global burden of chronic diseases and concerns over the toxicity of the toxicity of synthetic drugs [1], natural plant-based medicines are gaining prominence in modern drug research due to their multi-target synergistic therapeutic effects and reduced toxicity risk [2]. Indeed, traditional Herbal medicines remain the primary Healthcare resource for approximately 80% of the global population [3]. A significant portion of this bioactivity is attributed to secondary metabolites, with flavonoids and phenolic acids being particularly noted for their potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and hypoglycemic properties [4]. Importantly, the accumulation of these chemicals varies significantly across different plant organs [5, 6]. Although studies have characterized the chemistry of entire plants or individual organs [7, 8], systematic quantitative mapping of key constituents (e.g., specific flavonoids/phenolic acids) throughout the plant, as well as an understanding of how their distribution correlates with major biological activities (e.g., anti-inflammatory or hypoglycemic effects), is significantly lacking [9]. Therefore, comprehensive investigations into the distribution of bioactive chemical across the entire plant and their associated functional processes in medicinal species are urgently needed.

Ormosia henryi Prain is an evergreen broad-leaved tree found in subtropical evergreen forests, primarily distributed across southern China (e.g., Jiangxi, Hunan, Fujian, Guizhou) [10]. O. henryi Prain has been as a traditional Chinese medicinal herb, with references dating back to the Tang Dynasty’s Bencao Shiyi (Supplement to Materia Medica). Various plant parts, including roots, leaves, stems, and wood, are utilized in traditional medicine to relieve wind-dampness, promote blood circulation, eliminate stasis, cleanse, and reduce edema [11]. Modern pharmacological research confirms that O. henryi Prain and related species are rich in flavonoids and phenolic acids. These compounds exhibit significant antioxidant, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties [12]. Increasing evidence suggests that flavonoid extracts from O. henryi Prain leaves enhance cognitive performance by regulating neuroinflammatory factor expression [13]. UPLC-QTOF-MS/MS analysis of the leaves identified 46 flavonoids, eight of which alleviate depression-like behaviors in mice exposed to chronic mild stress [14]. Similarly, flavonoids derived from root bark exhibit significant free radical scavenging activity and inhibit the growth of cancer cell lines (A549, LAC, and HepG2) [15]. Despite these reported bioactivities, research on O. henryi Prain lags behind that on well-studied medicinal species such as Ginkgo biloba[16] and Taxus spp[17]. This discrepancy is evident in the predominant focus on isolated tissues or chemicals. Notably, a comprehensive metabolome profiling of the entire plant that quantifies key bioactive constituents (especially flavonoids) across major organs (roots, stems, and leaves), and exploring the quantitative relationships between tissue-specific metabolite accumulation and differential bioactivities (e.g., antioxidative capacity, hypoglycemic potency), has not yet been conducted. This significant knowledge gap hinders the systematic evaluation of tissue-specific functional properties and the evidence-based selection of appropriate materials for high-efficiency antioxidant or antidiabetic compositions.

This study conducted a comprehensive mapping of phenolic bioactive compounds, primarily flavonoids and phenolic acids, across multiple tissues of O. henryi Prain and assessed their correlation with corresponding bioactivities. Building on this foundation, an innovative multi-omics approach was developed. Specifically, liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) was utilized for non-targeted metabolomic profiling of six distinct tissues of O. henryi Prain. The resulting datasets were integrated with multivariate statistical analyses to identify tissue-specific distribution patterns of flavonoids and phenolic acids and to screen for potential biomarkers based on differential metabolite profiles. An activity evaluation system was further developed to systematically assess antioxidant capacity and the inhibitory effects of tissue extracts on α-glucosidase and α-amylase. Concurrently, the system quantified total phenols, total flavonoids, and other key biochemical indices. To further elucidate the functional relationships between metabolites and bioactivities, a compound–bioactivity association model was constructed using Pearson correlation analysis. Overall, the findings offer new insights into the metabolic characteristics and pharmacological mechanisms of O. henryi Prain, providing a theoretical foundation for the development of novel medicinal resources and functional foods.

Materials and methods

Plant material

The plant materials consisted of three-year-old, container-grown seedlings of O. henryi Prain, sourced from the experimental nursery at Guizhou University’s College of Forestry (26°34’N, 106°42’E; elevation 1100 m). The seedlings were cultivated without the application of fertilizers, pesticides, or other human interventions, ensuring that metabolite accumulation reflected natural physiological processes. On May 20, 2024, 10 healthy, vigorously growing plants, free of disease and mechanical damage, were selected.

The plants were categorized into six tissue types: new leaves (NL, defined as apical shoots harvested ≤ 7 days post-expansion), old leaves (OL, mature leaves harvested ≥ 90 days post-expansion), stem bark (SB), stem xylem (SX), root bark (RB), and root xylem (RX) (Fig.1A-C). After thorough rinsing with deionized water, the samples were processed using two independent protocols: (1) Fresh tissue aliquots were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analyses. (2) Separate subsamples were enzymatically deactivated (105 °C, 3 min) and subsequently oven-dried to a constant weight at 60 °C, achieved when the mass changes were less than 0.2%. The dried materials were finely homogenized with a ball mill and kept under desiccation.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Untargeted metabolomics was conducted by MetWare Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Briefly, freeze-dried samples were ground, extracted with 70% methanol, and analyzed using LC–MS/MS. Metabolites were separated on a C18 column and detected in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes. Data processing involved feature extraction, retention time correction, peak alignment, and imputation of missing values. Metabolite identification was performed by comparing data with the MetWare Database, HMDB, and KEGG, followed by met-DNA prediction. Features were retained based on confidence scores and QC reproducibility, and relative abundances were calculated. Detailed instrument parameters and extraction conditions are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

In vitro bioactivity evaluation

Preparation of extract

Extracts were produced following a modified procedure adopted from Hao et al. [18]. In this process, 30 mg of the sample was weighed into 2 mL centrifuge tubes, combined with 1.5 mL of 60% (v/v) ethanol, and extracted for 2 h at 60 °C using an ultrasonic bath at 300 W (40 kHz). After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min, the supernatant was collected, filtered through a 0.45 μm membranes, aliquoted, and stored at 4 °C to quantification to total flavonoid content (TFC), total phenolic content (TPC), and bioactivity evaluation.

Determination of total flavonoid content (TFC) and total phenolic content (TPC)

The total flavonoid content (TFC) was determined using aluminum nitrate colorimetry [19]. Briefly, 40 µL of sample aliquots and rutin standards (0–100 µg/mL) were sequentially mixed with 20 µL of 3% NaNO₂ and incubated for 6 min under Light-protected conditions, followed by the addition of 20 µL of 6% Al(NO₃)₃ and another 6 min incubation under the same conditions. The reaction was terminated by adding 140 µL of 4% NaOH and 60 µL of 60% ethanol. Absorbance was measured at 510 nm using a Multiskan GO microplate reader (Thermo), and TFC was reported as milligrams of rutin equivalent per gram of dry weight (mg RE/g DW).

The total phenolic content (TPC) was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu method [18]. In this assay, 20 µL of samples or gallic acid standards (0–200 µg/mL) were mixed with 20 µL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and incubated for 5 min. Subsequently, 160 µL of 7.5% Na₂CO₃ was added and the mixture was incubated for 25 min under Light-protected conditions to allow color development. The absorbance was measured at 765 nm, and TPC was expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalent per gram of dry weight (mg GAE/g DW).

Determination of antioxidant and hypoglycemic activities

The antioxidant activities, including DPPH and ABTS⁺ radical scavenging capabilities, were evaluated using a modified method from Bowen et al. [20], with sample concentrations ranging from 3.125 to 100 µg/mL. The IC₅₀ values were determined with vitamin C and BHT as positive controls. FRAP activity was assessed using an FeSO₄ standard curve (0-1000 µM) following the protocol described by Chen et al. [21]. Results were expressed as µmol Fe²⁺ equivalents per gram dry weight (µmol/g DW), with the identical positive controls used in the radical scavenging assays.

The inhibition of α-glucosidase (α-GC) and α-amylase (α-AMS) was measured using the modified methods outlined by Chen et al. [21]. With acarbose as the positive control. The inhibitory potency was calculated and expressed as IC₅₀. IC₅₀, defined as the half-maximal inhibitory concentration, represents the concentration required to inhibit 50% of the target enzyme activity. Lower IC₅₀ values indicate higher bioactivity [22].

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate using independent biological replicates, and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD;n= 3). Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (v21.0, IBM, USA). Nonlinear regression analyses for IC₅₀ determination and graph generation were performed with OriginPro 2023 (OriginLab, USA). Multivariate metabolomics analyses, including Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Hierarchical Clustering Analysis (HCA), and Orthogonal Partial Least Squares–Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA), were conducted using the MetWare Cloud Platform. Differential metabolites accumulated among O. henryi Prain tissues was identified based on |fold change| ≥ 2.0 and variable importance in projection (VIP) ≥ 1.0. Functional annotation and classification of differential metabolites were performed with the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database (KEGG;https://www.kegg.jp/). Pearson correlation analysis was applied to examine the relationships between bioactive components and bioactivities activities, and interaction networks were visualized using Cytoscape 3.8.0.

Results and analysis

Metabolic profiles of different tissues of O. henryi Prain

To evaluate variations in the metabolic profiles of O. henryi Prain tissues (stems, leaves, and roots), the reliability of the experimental system was first assessed using quality control (QC) samples. Overlaid total ion current (TIC) and base peak chromatograms (BPCs) from QC samples in both positive and negative ion modes (Fig. S1A-B) demonstrated high reproducibility of data acquisition. Analysis of extracted ion chromatograms (EIC) from blank samples revealed no detectable residual peaks of internal standards (Fig. S1C-D), indicating minimal contamination during sample preparation. Pearson correlation coefficient analysis confirmed strong intra-group correlation among biological replicates (Fig. S1E-F), indicating a high degree of data consistency.

Following QC, 4093 metabolites were identified, with the principal groups being amino acids and derivatives (20.45%), organic acids (14.63%), benzene derivatives (13.51%), flavonoids (5.42%), and phenolic acids (2.37%) (Fig.1E). Analysis of inter-tissue variations showed strong intra-group correlations (r> 0.9), as indicated by the correlation heatmap (Fig.1D). Hierarchical clustering further revealed distinct metabolic profiles among the six tissue types (Fig.1F), with leaf (NL, OL) and root (RB, RX) profiles clustering separately from those of the stem tissues (SB, SX). These results highlight the robustness of the QC system and significant metabolic differences across O. henryi Prain tissues.

Multivariate statistical analysis of O. henryi Prain tissues

Multivariate statistical methods were employed to examine variations in the metabolic profiles of O. henryi Prain tissues. PCA revealed that the first three principal components accounted for 64.90% of the variance in positive ion mode (PC1: 27.46%; PC2: 19.34%; PC3: 18.10%) and 64.46% in negative ion mode (PC1: 27.80%; PC2: 20.29%; PC3: 16.37%) (Fig. S2A-B). The spatial distribution of sample points exhibited clear differentiation among the six tissue types (NL, OL, SB, SX, RB, and RX), indicating distinct metabolic compositions.

Furthermore, the tight clustering of biological replicates within each tissue group demonstrated strong experimental repeatability, validating the data for further analysis. CV analysis of QC samples revealed that 85% of metabolites exhibited CV values below 0.3 (Fig. S2C-D), indicating minimal analytical variability and robust technique performance. OPLS-DA was employed to identify tissue-specific metabolites. The key model parameters were as follows: NL vs. OL: Q² = 0.993 (R²X = 0.619, R²Y = 1); SX vs. SB: Q² = 0.990 (R²X = 0.582, R²Y = 1); RX vs. RB: Q² = 0.990 (R²X = 0.572, R²Y = 1) (Fig. S3A–F). All comparison groups demonstrated Q² values exceeding 0.9, confirming the strong stability and predictive power of the model for differential metabolite.

Differential metabolite identification and tissue-specific expression patterns

A total of 3368 differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were identified across O. henryi Prain tissues using rigorous criteria (|FC| ≥ 2, VIP ≥ 1)(Table. S1). Pairwise tissue comparisons revealed the following distributions (Fig.2A–C): OL vs. NL: 2034 DAMs (1030 upregulated, 1004 downregulated); SX vs. SB: 1703 DAMs (1215 upregulated, 488 downregulated); and RX vs. RB: 1658 DAMs (684 upregulated, 974 downregulated).

K-means clustering divided all DAMs into eight expression pattern subclasses (Fig.2D), revealing pronounced tissue-specific accumulation trends. Subclass 1 (570 DAMs) and 4 (218 DAMs) showed the highest abundance in RB, with subclass 1 containing the lowest percentage of flavonoids (3.1%) and subclass 4 containing the lowest percentage of phenolic acids (0.9%). Subclass 2 (246 DAMs) was most enriched in RX, accounting for 10.1% of total flavonoids. Subclass 3 (660 DAMs) was dominant in OL, with phenolic acids comprising only 1.5%. Subclass 5 (331 DAMs) accumulated predominantly in SX, containing 5.7% flavonoids and 2.7% phenolic acids. Subclass 6 (468 DAMs) was most abundant in SB, with flavonoids and phenolic acids accounting for 5.7% and 3.2%. Subclasses 7 (110 DAMs) and 8 (765 DAMs) were substantially higher in NL, with subclass 7 having the highest phenolic acid content (4.5%). Overall, DAMs texhibited a distinct tissue-specific clustering pattern.

Flavonoids and phenolic acids accumulate differently in different tissues

HCA revealed the distribution of flavonoids and phenolic acids across various tissues of O. henryi Prain (Fig.3). The HCA results categorized the 222 flavonoids into five groups based on their accumulation patterns (Fig.3A, Table S2): class I (NL-specific enrichment), class II (OL-specific enrichment), class III (SB-dominant accumulation), class IV (high abundance in SX and RX), and class V (RB-dominant accumulation). The 97 phenolic acids were divided into four categories (Fig.3B, Table S3): class I (high abundance in SX), class II (shared accumulation in SB, RB, and RX), class III (OL-specific enrichment), and class IV (NL-dominant accumulation).

To gain a deeper understanding of the differential expression profile across tissues, the study employed bar charts to analyze the top ten up-regulated and down-regulated flavonoids and phenolic acids in OL vs. NL, SX vs. SB, and RX vs. RB(Fig.4). The flavonoid analysis revealed 124 differentially expressed isoflavones (54 up-regulated and 70 down-regulated in OL) between the OL and NL groups, with fold change (FC) ranging from 0.004 to 114.02. The compounds substantially up-regulated in OL were phloretin (MEDL02408, FC = 114.02), isoorientin 2’’-O-rhamnoside (MW0151848, FC = 74.70), nepitrin (MW0139148, FC = 48.02), isoginkgetin (MW0138479, FC = 40.99), and ophiopogonone B (MW0154754, FC = 34.40)(Fig.4A-C). In the comparison between the SB and SX groups, 96 isoflavones were differentially expressed (40 up-regulated and 56 down-regulated in SX), with FC values ranging from 0.07 to 59.48. Epimedin B (MW0138084) was the most significantly up-regulated in the SX group (FC = 59.48). In the comparison between the RB and RX groups, 87 isoflavones were differentially expressed (45 up-regulated and 42 down-regulated in RX), with an FC values ranging from 0.04 to 167.65. Naringenin (pme0376) was the most significantly up-regulated in the RX group (FC = 167.65).

In phenolic acid analyses (Fig.4D-F), 46 phenolic acids were significantly differentially expressed between the OL and NL groups (23 up-regulated and 23 down-regulated in OL), with FC values ranging from 0.007 to 120.5. Vanilloloside (MEDL02757, FC = 120.50) exhibited the greatest up-regulation. Furthermore, 41 phenolic acids were significantly differentially expressed between the SX and SB groups (7 up-regulated and 34 down-regulated in SX), with FC values ranging from 0.024 to 19.67. Agnuside (MW0015605, FC = 19.67) was the most significantly up-regulated component in SX. Finally, 33 phenolic acids exhibited significant differences between the RX and RB groups (16 up-regulated and 17 down-regulated in RX), with FC values ranging from 0.05 to 16.34. Butylparaben (MEDN0746, FC = 16.34) exhibited the greatest up-regulation magnitude. The study revealed substantial spatial distribution specificities (FC > 2 or < 0.5) for flavonoids and phenolic acids across tissues, providing a molecular foundation for understanding their tissue-dependent bioactivities.

Differences in key co-differentiated flavonoids and phenolic acids​

To examine metabolic variations in O. henryi Prain tissues, 363 co-differentiated metabolites (CDMs) were identified through comparative analysis of OL vs. NL, SX vs. SB, and RX vs. RB (Table S4). The CDMs included 29 flavonoids and 5 phenolic acids (Table S5). The analysis of metabolite peak regions revealed distinct accumulation patterns of these chemicals across different tissues (Fig.5). Leaf tissues (NL, OL) exhibited unique distribution patterns for flavonoids. NL accumulated significantly higher quantities of chemicals 1, 3, 6, 8, 9, 11, 12, 15, and 16 compared to OL. For instance, the peak area of compound 8 was 16.07 times greater in NL compared to OL. SB accumulated chemicals 2, 4, and 20 in stem tissues at higher levels than SX, which accumulated compounds 13, 23, 27, and 29. In root tissues, RB accumulated chemicals 7, 14, 17, 22, and 28, while RX accumulated compounds 5, 10, 18, 21, 24, 25, and 26. Phenolic acids also exhibited distinct tissue-specific accumulation patterns: OL collected substantially more compound 31, SB accumulated compounds 30 and 33, and compound 34 accumulated extremely well in RB (peak area 5458.18-fold greater than in OL). These findings confirmed that tissue location significantly influenced flavonoid and phenolic acid composition and accumulation levels in O. henryi Prain, with the most pronounced tissue-specific accumulation patterns observed in the leaf (NL, OL) and root (RB, RX) tissues.

Correlation analysis of key co-differentiated flavonoids and phenolic acids​

Pearson correlation analysis was employed to extensively examine the interactions of 34 phenolic co-differentiated metabolites (29 flavonoids and 5 phenolic acids) in O. henryi Prain. Correlations between 1156 metabolite pairs were analyzed, of which 150 pairs exhibited strong significant correlations (|r| >0.80,p< 0.001) (Table S6). Butrin exhibited a significant negative correlation (r= −0.811,p< 0.001) with 5,7-Dihydroxy-3,6,8,3’,4’-pentamethoxyflavone, while the other significant correlations were positive.

Heatmap clustering revealed three distinct functional modules that share molecular features (Fig.6A). A molecular network was constructed, comprising 32 nodes and 149 edges. Its topological characteristics (average degree = 4.8, network width = 6, clustering coefficient = 0.787, and network density = 0.343) demonstrated a highly connected and organized structure (Fig.6B). Modularity analysis revealed that the network comprised three basic modules, which closely aligned with the heatmap clustering results. Notably, these three primary modules were grouped by flavonoid and phenolic acid metabolites, with most showing positive intercorrelations. Additionally, core regulatory nodes were identified: neohesperidin was the central molecule with the highest degree (degree = 16), followed by diosmetin, luteolin-7-O-glucuronide, (+)-usnic acid, and 3-hydroxyflavone (degree = 14), and 6-geranylchrysin had the lowest connectivity (degree = 2) (Table S7). Notably, flavonoids accounted for all five of the most highly connected metabolites, suggesting that they play critical regulatory roles in the phenolic metabolic network of O. henryi Prain.

Metabolic pathway analysis of differential metabolites

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed to examine the functional properties of DAMs in various tissues of O. henryi Prain. The analysis indicated that DAMs were associated with 89, 74, and 73 metabolic pathways in the OL vs. NL, SX vs. SB, and RX vs. RB comparative groups, respectively (Table S8). Among them, the top 20 enriched pathways were primarily associated with key biosynthetic activities. Significantly enriched pathways (P< 0.05) included metabolic pathways, amino acids biosynthesis, secondary metabolites biosynthesis, lysine biosynthesis, glucosinolate biosynthesis, and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis (Fig.7B-D), highlighting their key regulatory roles in the differential accumulation of metabolites between groups. Enrichment analysis of differential isoflavonoid and phenolic acid metabolites (Fig.8A-E) underscored the importance of well-annotated pathways, such as metabolic pathways, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, and isoflavonoid biosynthesis.

Determination of total bioactive components

This study quantified the TFC and TPC in the roots (RB/RX), stems (SB/SX), and leaves (OL/NL) of O. henryi Prain. Significant differences (P< 0.05) were observed among different tissues (Fig.9). OL exhibited the highest TFC concentration (16.19 mg/g DW), which was 4.36-fold higher than of SX (3.71 mg/g DW). The TFC content decreased in the order: OL > NL > RB > SB > RX > SX (16.19, 11.85, 8.95, 7.95, 5.19, and 3.71 mg/g DW, respectively). Similarly, the TPC was enriched in the roots, with the highest level detected in RB (21.59 mg/g DW), which was 3.48-fold higher than that in SX (6.21 mg/g DW). The TPC content decreased in the order: RB > RX > OL > SB > NL > SX (21.59, 14.80, 14.09, 10.09, 9.38, and 6.21 mg/g DW). In summary, SX exhibited the lowest TFC and TPC values among all tissues, suggesting a limited capacity to accumulate pharmaceutically active compounds. Leaf tissues, particularly OL, contained the highest flavonoid levels, whereas root tissues, especially RB, contained the highest phenolic levels. These findings were consistent with the results of our previous metabolome clustering analysis.

In vitro bioactivity evaluation

In vitro antioxidant capacity assay

The antioxidant potential of O. henryi Prain tissue extracts was assessed using DPPH, ABTS⁺ radical scavenging, and ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assays. All extracts demonstrated dose-dependent activity within the concentration range of 3.125–100 µg/mL (P< 0.05; Fig.10A, C, E). In the ABTS⁺ test, the OL extract exhibited the strongest activity, with the lowest IC₅₀ value (0.07 µg/mL), corresponding 29.9-fold and 132.9-fold greater efficacy than vitamin C (Vc; IC₅₀ = 2.09 µg/mL) and BHT (IC₅₀ = 9.30 µg/mL), respectively. By contrast, SX displayed the weakest effect (IC₅₀ = 18.2 mg/mL) (Fig.10B). For DPPH scavenging, RB extract showed the best performance among tissues (IC₅₀ = 97.97 µg/mL), although it remained less potent than BHT, which exhibited 2.7-fold greater activity (IC₅₀ = 35.98 µg/mL) (Fig.10D). In the FRAP assay, OL extract again demonstrated the strongest reducing power, reaching 0.1857 mg TE/g at 100 µg/mL—equivalent to 93% of BHT activity (0.1992 mg TE/g)—and significantly surpassing the other extracts(Fig.10F). Overall, OL displayed superior ABTS⁺ scavenging and FRAP reduction capabilities, while RB showed notable selectivity in DPPH scavenging. These findings highlight the tissue-specific antioxidant properties of O. henryi Prain, supporting their potential for future applications in antioxidant research.

In vitro hypoglycaemic activity assay

The inhibitory effects of O. henryi Prain tissue extracts on α-AMS and α-GC were evaluated. All extracts significantly inhibited both enzymes in a dose-dependent manner across the concentrations range of 3.125 to 100 µg/mL (Fig.11A, C). For α-AMS inhibition, OL extract demonstrated the greatest potency, with the lowest IC₅₀ value (2.90 µg/mL), corresponding to a 58.9-fold stronger effect than the positive control acarbose (IC₅₀ = 171.23 µg/mL). NL exhibited the second-highest activity (IC₅₀ = 3.42 µg/mL), 50.0-fold more potent than acarbose. The inhibitory activities of the other tissues followed the order: RB (IC₅₀ = 5.40 µg/mL) > RX (IC₅₀ = 5.57 µg/mL) > SX (IC₅₀ = 7.72 µg/mL) > SB (IC₅₀ = 8.85 µg/mL) (Fig.11B). For α-GC inhibition, NL had the highest inhibitory activity (IC₅₀ = 1.88 µg/mL), with 88.3-fold more potency than acarbose (IC₅₀ = 165.99 µg/mL). OL ranked second (IC₅₀ = 2.05 µg/mL), followed by RB (3.41 µg/mL) > RX (4.07 µg/mL) > SX (5.51 µg/mL) > SB (5.76 µg/mL) (Fig.11D). Notably, all tissue extracts exhibited significantly stronger inhibitory activity than acarbose (p< 0.01). In summary, leaf tissues, notably NL and OL, exhibited superior inhibition of both α-AMS and α-GC, highlighting their strong potential as natural hypoglycemic agents.

Correlation analysis of bioactive constituents with biological activities

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to investigate the relationships between the TFC and TPC and the biological activities of O. henryi Prain extracts(Fig.12A; Table S4). TFC showed a significant negative correlation with DPPH radical scavenging ability (higher IC₅₀ values indicate weaker activity;r= −0.525,P< 0.05) and a strong positive correlation with FRAP reducing capacity (r= 0.950,P< 0.01), indicating that flavonoids enhance antioxidant potential. For hypoglycaemic activity, TFC was significantly correlated with inhibition of both α-GC (r= −0.798,P< 0.01) and α-AMS (r= −0.732,P< 0.01), suggesting that flavonoids play an important role in glycemic metabolism. FRAP showed a significant negative connection with the IC50 value of α-AMS (r= −0.613,P< 0.01) and α-GC (r= −0.536,P< 0.05). Additionally, α-AMS inhibition was strongly positive connection with α-GC inhibitory activity (r= 0.939,P< 0.01). FRAP reducing ability also showed significant negative connection with α-AMS (r= −0.613,P< 0.01) and α-GC inhibition (r= −0.536,P< 0.05). Furthermore, α-AMS inhibition was strongly positively connection with α-GC inhibition (r= 0.939,P< 0.01).

The correlation between 34 phenolic co-differentiated metabolites and TFC, TPC, antioxidant, and hypoglycemic activities was investigated(Table S9). A significant correlation criterion (|r| >0.5,P< 0.01) led to the identification of 24 phenolic compounds strongly correlated with TFC and TPC (Fig.12B). Four metabolites showed substantial positive correlations with TFC: p-hydroxyphenylacetothiohydroximate (r= 0.773), butrin (r= 0.635), peonidin pentose (r= 0.634), and 3-hydroxyphloretin-4’-glucoside (r= 0.517). Nine distinct metabolites were associated with TPC. Among them, the phenolic acid 2-[3-(4-hydroxyphenyl)oxirane-2-carbonyl]−4-(3-methylbut-2-en-1-yl)benzene-1,3,5-triol (r= 0.884) and the flavonoid 5,7,2’,4’-tetrahydroxy-3-geranylflavone (r= 0.842) exhibited the highest correlations. A network analysis of these 34 co-varied phenolic compounds (29 flavonoids and 5 phenolic acids) identified 10 metabolites as significant antioxidant indicators (Fig.12C). Notably, 3-hydroxyphloretin-4’-glucoside showed the strongest negative correlation with DPPH scavenging activity (r= −0.699), while p-hydroxyphenylacetothiohydroximate had the highest positive correlation with FRAP reducing power (r= 0.832). In the hypoglycemic group (Fig.12D), the flavonoid diosmetin exhibited a substantial negative association with α-AMS inhibition (r= −0.676,P< 0.01) and a moderate negative correlation with α-GC inhibition (r= −0.582,P< 0.05). In conclusion, 3-hydroxyphloretin-4’-glucoside, p-hydroxyphenylacetothiohydroximate, and diosmetin were identified as key functional indicators that coordinate antioxidant and hypoglycemic actions in O. henryi Prain.

Discussion

Tissue metabolic profile differentiation provides the basis for focused resource development

O. henryi Prain, a valuable tree species with timber, ornamental, and medicinal values, is currently facing survival challenges, including population decline and reproductive barriers [23]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for comprehensive exploration of its high-value medicinal resources to enhance its overall benefits. The primary bioactive compounds derived from O. henryi Prain are phenolic acids and flavonoids [11]. However, detailed investigations into the phenolic content (mainly phenolic acids and flavonoids) and their biological activities across various tissues of O. henryi Prain remain limited, hindering their effective utilization. For the first time, non-targeted metabolomics technology was employed to construct a comprehensive metabolic profile of the six major tissues of O. henryi Prain, including roots, stems, and leaves. A total of 4093 metabolites were identified, of which 3368 DAMs were screened, including 363 cross-tissue-shared DAMs (comprising 29 flavonoids and 5 phenolic acids). Volcano plots and K-mean clustering analyses revealed that DAMs exhibited a clear tissue-specific clustering pattern, indicating functional differentiation in secondary metabolite production across tissues [24, 25]. The tissue-specific accumulation was confirmed by HCA of flavonoids and phenolic acids. Flavonoids were predominantly enriched in leaves (especially in OL), whereas phenolic acids were specifically concentrated in the RB, a distribution pattern similar to that observed in citrus species [26]. Metabolic pathway enrichment analysis revealed that secondary metabolite biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, and isoflavonoid biosynthesis were the major pathways driving phenolic compound accumulation in O. henryi Prain. The importance of flavonoid biosynthesis pathways in regulating phenolic compound production is well documented in the literature [27]. In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive examination of the tissue-specific distribution and regulatory pathways of key phenolic compounds (phenolic acids and flavonoids) in O. henryi Prain. This study not only advances our understanding of its metabolic foundation but also offers a theoretical basis for the targeted exploitation of its medicinal tissue parts.

Tissue-specific accumulation of phenolic metabolites reveals functional synergy

This study identified significant differences in the content and abundance of 34 common phenolic metabolites across six tissues of O. henryi Prain (NL, OL, SX, SB, RB, and RX). Specifically, OL contained high levels of the major flavonoid metabolite peonidin pentose, which is well known for its antioxidant and anti-tumor activities [28]. NL was enriched with nine flavonoid metabolites, including neohesperidin and naringin, which are widely used as natural food additives and exhibit antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities [29, 30]. RB selectively accumulated the primary phenolic acid metabolite (+)-Usnic acid, a naturally occurring phenolic acid with antibacterial, antiviral, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant activities [31]. Each tissue contained 8, 2, 4, 7, 8, and 7 distinct major metabolites, highlighting the functional variety of secondary metabolism in plant tissues [32]. Pearson correlation and network topology analyses revealed strong positive correlation and co-regulation among 29 flavonoids and 5 phenolic acids, suggesting synergistic interactions between these groups [33]. This synergy is likely attributable to phenylalanine, which serves as a common upstream precursor for both pathways [34], potentially enhancing overall antioxidant capacity and pathogen resistance in plants [35]. Notably, flavonoids served as key regulators within the metabolic network, with neohesperidin identified as the central hub metabolite (highest node degree = 16). Its structural properties features, such as the flavonoid ring with multiple glycosyl groups, confer multi-target regulatory capabilities [36], including enhancement of antioxidant activity through Nrf2/HO-1 pathway activation [37] and suppression of inflammation via NF-κB signaling inhibition [38].

Correlations between core metabolites and bioactivities confirm functional value

Quantitative analyses revealed distinct patterns of metabolite distribution, with flavonoids predominantly accumulating in leaves (particularly OL) and phenolics enriched in root tissues (mainly RB), consistent with multi-species metabolic partitioning studies [39, 40]. The accumulation of flavonoids in leaves is often linked to their “chemical sunscreen” function against ultraviolet (UV) radiation. Studies have demonstrated that flavonoids effectively absorb UV radiation, thereby reducing the potential damage to plant cells from UV exposure [41]. For example, flavonoids in the leaves of Garcinia subelliptica function as natural sunscreens by absorbing UV radiation and reducing its penetration through the leaf surface, offering protective effects [42]. In contrast, the phenolic enrichment in RB appears to be associated with rhizosphere interactions with root-associated bacteria. Previous studies have shown that phenolic acids in root exudates significantly influence the growth, diversity, and structure of rhizosphere microbial communities [43]. Specifically, phenolic acids can modify soil physicochemical properties (e.g., pH and nutrient availability) and may also regulate microbial metabolic pathways, indirectly influencing their growth and function [44]. Furthermore, phenolic acids exhibit selective effects on the growth of different microbes. For instance, some phenolic acids may promote pathogen growth while inhibiting beneficial microbes [45]. This selective effect may result in an imbalance in the rhizosphere microbial community, ultimately affecting plant health and soil ecosystem functions [46]. Therefore, the accumulation of phenolic acids in roots is not only closely related to the composition and function of rhizosphere microbial communities but may also significantly affect plant health and soil ecosystem stability through the regulation of plant–microbe interactions [47].

In vitro bioassays demonstrated that OL extracts exhibited significantly stronger ABTS⁺ scavenging ability (p< 0.05) and higher FRAP values than other tissues, while RB showed the highest DPPH radical scavenging capacity. Leaf tissues inhibited α-AMS and α-GC more effectively than roots or stems, which is consistent with previous findings in Michelia macclurei[21]. Correlation analyses revealed significant associations between TFC, TPC, and bioactivities in O. henryi Prain. TFC exhibited strong negative correlations with DPPH IC50values and strong positive correlations with FRAP, indicating that flavonoids are the primary antioxidants. TFC displayed significant negative correlations with α-GC/α-AMS IC50values, confirming their hypoglycemic relevance. Notably, FRAP values were negatively correlated with α-AMS and α-GC IC50 values, suggesting that antioxidant compounds exert dual functions in iron reduction and carbohydrate-digestion inhibition, thereby emphasizing their synergistic potential in functional food development [48, 49]. The strong connection between α-AMS and α-GC inhibition suggests potential synergistic effects in glycemic regulation [50]. Finally, this study establishes clear links between tissue-specific metabolite profiles and medicinal capabilities in O. henryi Prain hrough integrated compositional and functional validation, providing a basis for tailored harvesting strategies to promote sustainable resource utilization.

The identification of core signature markers reveals key bioactive metabolites for activity regulation

In this study, 34 frequently occurring differential phenolic compounds and their bioactive characteristics were identified and analyzed, enabling the precise screening of three key phenolic markers: 3-Hydroxyphloretin-4’-glucoside, p-hydroxyphenylacetothiohydroximate, and diosmetin. Comprehensive analysis revealed structure-activity relationships between their molecular features and specific bioactivities. With respect to antioxidant activity, 3-Hydroxyphtoretin-4′-glucoside exhibited strong DPPH radical scavenging ability, suggesting that glycosylation modifications may enhance this property [51]. p-Hydroxyphenylacetothiohydroximate showed a significant positive association with FRAP, indicating antioxidant activity mainly mediated by its multiple phenolic hydroxyl groups [52]. Diosmetin exhibited dual inhibitory effects relevant to hypoglycemia regulation. It effectively inhibited α-amylase, and molecular docking analysis revealed seven hydrogen bonds that stabilized the binding interaction [53]. Moreover, diosmetin effectively inhibits α-glucosidase, and mechanistic analysis demonstrated direct interactions with catalytic residues [53]. The combined effects of these inhibitory mechanisms reduced the catalytic efficiency of both enzymes. Three key functional indicators were identified in O. henryi Prain, and the molecular mechanisms underlying its dual antioxidant (radical scavenging and iron reduction) and hypoglycemic activities were elucidated. These findings not only identify molecular markers but also provide a theoretical foundation for targeted extraction, selective breeding, and the development of functional food and pharmaceuticals.

Conclusion

In this study, the tissue-specific accumulation patterns of flavonoids and phenolic acids in O. henryi Prain were systematically characterized by integrating non-targeted metabolomics, quantitative analysis, and in vitro activity evaluation. Leaves were predominantly enriched in flavonoids, while root bark contained higher levels of phenolic acids, and these compositional differences were strongly associated with distinct antioxidant and hypoglycemic activities. Correlation analyses established quantitative links between key metabolites and biofunctions, and several landmark bioactive compounds were identified, thereby clarifying the intrinsic mechanisms through which metabolite profiles regulate biological activity in O. henryi Prain. These findings extend beyond academic value by offering a theoretical foundation for practical applications. The identified molecular markers and functional associations can guide the targeted extraction of bioactive constituents, support selective breeding of metabolite-enriched germplasm, and inform the development of functional foods, nutraceuticals, and phytopharmaceuticals. The dual antioxidant and hypoglycemic effects also highlight the potential of O. henryi Prain as a sustainable resource for chronic disease prevention and healthcare innovation. While this study focused on single-source seedlings to minimize environmental variability, future research incorporating multiple provenances and growth environments will be crucial to assess ecological plasticity in metabolite profiles. As metabolite accumulation may vary across developmental stages, future studies on different ages and life stages will help clarify the distinction between developmental and tissue-specific metabolite allocation. Additionally, validation of these findings in animal and clinical models, alongside exploration of dose–response relationships and multi-omics approaches, will refine our understanding of the mechanisms involved. Long-term ecological and cultivation studies will be essential to ensure the sustainable utilization and broaden the translational potential of O. henryi Prain.
