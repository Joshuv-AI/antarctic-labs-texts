Effects of an arms-crossing technique added to Lamaze breathing on labour pain: a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Kehan Huang, Yahui Zhou, Yaxin Ye, Qiaoling Li, Xinchen Chen, Ruying Lin, Pingping Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEffective non-pharmacological options for labour analgesia remain limited. Lamaze breathing may provide modest analgesic benefits. The crossed-hands effect, associated with altered somatosensory processing, may modulate pain through sensorimotor mechanisms. This trial evaluated whether adding an arms-crossing technique to Lamaze breathing reduces labour pain during the latent phase of labour.MethodsThis single-centre, single-blinded, parallel-group randomised controlled trial was conducted in the obstetric unit of a hospital in China between April and June 2024. Sixty women in the latent phase of labour were randomly assigned (1:1) to either Lamaze breathing plus an arms-crossing technique (intervention group, n = 30) or Lamaze breathing alone (control group, n = 30). The arms-crossing technique involved repeatedly crossing both arms across the body midline synchronised with rhythmic breathing during uterine contractions. Outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation. The primary outcome was labour pain intensity measured using a numerical rating scale across five consecutive contraction cycles. Secondary outcomes included heart rate and pulse oxygen saturation (SpO₂) during contractions and contraction intervals. Analyses were conducted on an intention-to-treat basis.ResultsAll 60 participants completed the study and were included in the analysis. Baseline characteristics were comparable between groups. Pain scores decreased over time in both groups; however, the intervention group showed significantly greater reductions in pain scores than the control group. Repeated-measures analysis demonstrated a significant time-by-group interaction for pain scores (F = 6.109, P < 0.001, partial η² = 0.095), with significant between-group differences observed at the fifth contraction cycle (F = 4.742, P = 0.034, partial η² = 0.076). No significant group differences were observed for heart rate or SpO₂ during contractions. SpO₂ during contraction intervals differed significantly between groups (F = 6.824, P = 0.011, partial η² = 0.105), although values remained within normal physiological ranges. No adverse events occurred.ConclusionsAdding an arms-crossing technique to Lamaze breathing significantly reduced labour pain without observable adverse effects. The arms-crossing technique may represent a simple and feasible non-pharmacological strategy for labour pain management.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2400083201. Registered 18 April 2024.

Background

Labour pain, though physiological, can become so intense that it disrupts the normal progression of childbirth. Excessive pain may contribute to abnormal uterine contractions, prolonged labour, and disturbances in maternal acid–base balance, increasing the risk of instrumental delivery, episiotomy, or even emergency caesarean Sect [1]. These outcomes not only affect maternal and neonatal health but can also undermine women’s birth experiences and lead to complications such as perinatal depression and delayed lactation [2, 3]. Therefore, providing safe and effective labour analgesia is a critical component of obstetric care.

Currently, labour pain management strategies fall into two main categories: pharmacological and non-pharmacological. While pharmacological methods, such as epidural anaesthesia, are highly effective, they are not without controversy. Adverse effects—such as prolonged labour, impaired maternal mobility, and neonatal respiratory depression [4]—may limit their universal use, particularly in low-resource or out-of-hospital settings. Non-pharmacological methods, in contrast, are generally safe, cause minimal physiological disruption, and avoid pharmacological side effects [5]. However, these methods often have limited analgesic effects [6, 7].

Many non-pharmacological analgesic interventions are multimodal, making it difficult to distinguish the contribution of individual components or clarify their underlying mechanisms [8]. This issue is particularly relevant in labour pain management, where breathing, movement, attention modulation, and emotional support are often combined. Examining specific techniques with plausible neurocognitive mechanisms may therefore help refine and optimise non-pharmacological approaches to labour analgesia.

One such technique draws on the “crossed-hands effect”, a phenomenon well-documented in somatosensory research [9]. The crossed-hand paradigm can be considered a visual-proprioceptive illusion. Experimental research demonstrates that this posture impairs the localisation of tactile stimuli, an effect thought to arise from a conflict between the brain’s somatotopic (body-based) and spatiotopic (external space-based) representations. This conflict increases cognitive load and interferes with the normal processing of sensory input in cortical areas involved in pain perception [10]. The effectiveness of arms-crossing analgesia in reducing spontaneous pain in patients with unilateral upper limb complex regional pain syndrome has been confirmed [11].

During the first stage of labour, pain is primarily visceral, arising from uterine and cervical distension. Although these signals do not originate from peripheral receptors in the limbs, first-order somatic and visceral afferent neurons synapse onto the same second-order neurons. These signals ascend via the second-order neurons to the thalamus and are then relayed to the sensory cortex. Ultimately, the perception of pain is shaped by cognitive and emotional modulation within the brain’s pain matrix [8]. Neuroimaging evidence from functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) [12, 13] and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) [14] studies confirms that crossing the arms activates the prefrontal cortex—an area known to play a critical role in pain modulation. Furthermore, research indicates that women tend to rely more heavily on visual cues and exhibit greater left–right confusion than men, potentially heightening their susceptibility to the crossed-hands effect [15]. These factors suggest that the crossed-hands effect may exert an analgesic influence on labour pain.

Despite its established efficacy in experimental pain contexts, this phenomenon has not yet been investigated in the context of labour. Given its plausible neurocognitive basis, the arms-crossing technique merits empirical evaluation as a potential non-pharmacological approach to support women during labour.

Methods

Study design

This arms-crossing trial was a single-blind (participants-blinded), randomised controlled trial designed to investigate the effect of the arms-crossing technique on labour pain during vaginal birth. The allocation ratio between the intervention group and control group was 1:1. The trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration Number: ChiCTR2400083201) on 18 April 2024. The study procedures and reporting strictly followed the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) 2010 statement [16] and the TIDieR (Template for Intervention Description and Replication) checklist [17].

Study settings and participants

Participants were recruited from the birth room of a tertiary hospital in southeast China between 18 April and 30 April 2024. This hospital handles approximately 6, 000 births annually, including about 4, 000 vaginal births.

Inclusion Criteria: (i) Age ≥ 18 years; (ii) Literacy in written and oral Chinese; (iii) In the stage of vaginal trial birth; (iv) Cervical dilation ≤ 3 cm; (v) Having opted for but not yet received epidural analgesia. Rationale: To eliminate the influence of analgesic drugs on pain perception and minimize differences in pain management.

Exclusion Criteria: (i) Indications for caesarean section, as per the Expert Consensus on Caesarean Section by the Obstetrics Group of the Chinese Medical Association [18]; (ii) History of mental illness.

Withdrawal Criteria: (i) Emergency caesarean section; (ii) Voluntary withdrawal of consent; (iii) Administration of pharmacological analgesia during the trial; (iv) Inadequate adherence to the assigned intervention protocol, defined as ≥ 3 incorrect performances of Lamaze breathing or arms-crossing during any one of the five observed uterine contraction cycles (intervention group), or ≥ 3 incorrect performances of Lamaze breathing during any one cycle (control group).

Recruitment and sample size

Upon admitting a parturient into the birth room, a midwife performed a routine vaginal examination to confirm cervical dilation and promptly recorded the relevant information, which typically took about 3 min. Following this, the midwife notified the study team’s midwife (the interventionist), who screened the new admission’s medical records to determine if she was eligible to participate in the study. The interventionist then provided the potential participant with standardised information about the study, including its significance, purpose, procedures, methods, potential risks, and benefits, without mentioning specific interventions for different groups. After a thorough explanation and addressing any questions, willing participants signed the informed consent form.

Sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1 software, setting test power at 0.95, significance level at 0.05, and effect size at 0.25. The minimum sample size calculated was 28 participants, with at least 14 in each group.

Randomisation and allocation

Since the final number of participants was undetermined, randomisation followed a predefined process. An independent individual generated 100 two-digit random numbers using an online generator (https://www.randomizer.org). Each number was placed into identical, opaque, sealed, and sequentially numbered envelopes, stored securely in the head nurse’s office. After obtaining informed consent from eligible participants, a study midwife opened the next available envelope to retrieve the random number. The participant was assigned to either the control or intervention group based on whether the random number was odd (control) or even (intervention). Only the midwife knew the group assignments, ensuring participants remained blinded.

Interventions

The research team comprised a supervisor of a master’s programme in maternal and infant care, three postgraduate students, an obstetric nursing expert with 31 years of experience, and three midwives, each with a bachelor’s degree and 8–10 years of clinical experience. To ensure consistency in implementation, the team standardised the arms-crossing technique after conducting a comprehensive literature review and a series of collaborative discussions. The initial protocol was simulated by the three midwives on interns acting as parturients, with other team members evaluating the entire process. Feedback from these simulations was used to refine and finalise the standardised intervention protocol for the trial.

The three midwives continued acting as the interventionists for this study. Both the intervention group and the control group received standardised Lamaze breathing interventions, with the intervention group additionally adopting the arms-crossing analgesia. Protocol-related issues were resolved promptly during daily online research team meetings to maintain uniformity.

During each trial, which lasted approximately 20 to 40 min, the participant was continuously monitored for intrauterine pressure and contraction frequency using a Philips fetal heart monitor (Model FM20, manufactured by Shanghai Hanfei Medical Devices Co., Ltd., China), provided free of charge.

Intervention group

Participants were expected to perform both the Lamaze breathing and arms-crossing techniques as analgesia. The interventionist first ensured that the parturient was familiar with the Lamaze breathing technique, providing individualised instruction as necessary. Once the parturient was proficient in Lamaze breathing, the arms-crossing technique was introduced and taught through demonstrations.

The Lamaze breathing technique in this trial was guided as follows: (i) Cleansing Breathing: The parturient takes a deep inspiration slowly through the nose, allowing the abdomen to expand, and then exhales slowly through the mouth as if blowing out a candle. The abdomen returns to its original position while the chest remains relaxed, promoting deep, slow, and rhythmic breathing; (ii) Chest Breathing: The parturient inhales slowly through the nose, expanding the chest, and then exhales slowly through the mouth, allowing the chest to return to its original position while the abdomen remains relaxed. Each chest breathing sequence began and ended with a cleansing breath. During the trial, breathing rate was regulated by the interventionist, who gave verbal cues of “inhale” and “exhale.” at a frequency of 6–10 repetitions per minute, adjusted according to the intensity of uterine contractions. Incorrect performance was defined as failing to follow the cues or terminating inhalation or exhalation more than two seconds early.

The arms-crossing technique in this trial required participants: during inhalation, moving both arms across the body’s midline, allowing the fingertips to touch the opposite arm or shoulder naturally (Fig. 1-A); during exhalation, the arms returned to their natural sides (Fig. 1-B). Each cycle lasted approximately 6–10 s and was performed 6–10 times per minute, synchronised with Lamaze breathing. Incorrect performance was defined as: (i) Movements were not synchronised with breathing; (ii) Crossings were omitted; (iii) Wrists did not cross the midline during inhalation; or (iv) Fingertips failed to return across the midline during exhalation.

Demonstration of crossed-arms and uncrossed-arms

The interventionist provided continuous instruction until the parturient was able to simultaneously perform the arms-crossing technique with Lamaze breathing. Once competent, the parturient maintained a 45°–50° semi-recumbent position during the next five contraction cycles (20–30 min) to perform both techniques. During contraction intervals, the parturient breathed naturally and paused the arms-crossing movements.

Protocol adherence was monitored in real time by the interventionist using a binary checklist, and any improper performance was corrected immediately. No other non-pharmacological labour analgesia measures were provided, and there were no interruptions or interferences from other medical interventions during the intervention.

Control group

Participants received only the Lamaze breathing technique intervention. The guidance provided and the criteria for assessing correct performance of Lamaze breathing were identical to those used in the intervention group. All other procedures and care were the same as in the intervention group.

Data collection

Data were collected by the three members of the research team using a worksheet consisting of participants’ basic information and outcome indicators. Information such as age and gestational weeks was collected from medical records, while other data were gathered through face-to-face communication. Outcome indicators were assessed at six points: baseline (pre-intervention, Time 0) and five post-intervention contraction cycles (respectively from Time 1 to Time 5).

Basic information

The basic information of participants includes age, pre-intervention dilation of the cervix, gestational age, education level, employment status, marital status, current residence type, parity, faith, family income satisfaction, whether it is a planned pregnancy, whether there are family members accompanying, and whether they choose paid doula services provided by the hospital. The questionnaire also includes whether there is a history of dysmenorrhoea, as it may affect the perception and tolerance of participants towards uterine contraction pain.

Primary outcome

Pain intensity was measured using the Numerical Rating Scale, ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (severe pain). The scale is a valid measure for assessing labour pain intensity, suitable for verbal administration, with good compliance and ease of scoring [19]. The maximum pain during one contraction period was asked for assessment orally and recorded 15–20 s after uterine pressure returned to baseline.

Secondary outcomes

Heart rate (HR) and pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2) levels during contractions and intervals were measured using a pulse oximeter (Model PC-66, manufactured by Shenzhen Creative Industry Co., Ltd., China). Participants wore the oximeter on their left index fingers throughout the entire process. Data were recorded at the peak of each contraction (5 s after the peak uterine pressure) and during intervals (15–20 s after the uterine pressure returned to baseline).

Blinding

Participants were aware they were part of a labour non-pharmacological analgesia study but were not informed of the specific measures and group allocations. Due to the differing interventions and data collection methods, blinding of the interventionists was not feasible.

Reporting of adverse events

Adverse events will be reported immediately, reviewed by the research team, and escalated to the principal investigator and ethics committee for severe cases.

Statistical methods

Data were dual-entered into a database by two postgraduate students from the research team to ensure accuracy and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0. Categorical data were described using frequency and percentage, and group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test. Normally distributed continuous data were described using mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent samples t-tests. Non-normally distributed continuous data were described using median and interquartile range, and group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for outcome measures such as Numerical Rating Scale pain scores, SpO2, and heart rate. Partial η² values were interpreted using Cohen’s conventional benchmarks: small (≥ 0.01), medium (≥ 0.06), and large (≥ 0.14) [20]. A two-sided test with p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

Out of 95 potential participants, 29 did not meet the inclusion criteria, and 6 declined participation, leaving 60 women who qualified and agreed to join the study. The sample size more than doubled the minimum requirement determined by G*Power. These participants were randomly assigned to either the control group (n = 30) or the intervention group (n = 30), with no dropouts or exclusions during the intervention. All participants completed the study, and none opted for the paid doula services offered by the hospital. Labour took place in a quiet environment for all participants. No adverse events occurred. The flow from participants’ enrolment to intervention and analysis is shown in Fig. 2. Baseline characteristics were well-balanced between the two groups (p > 0.05), as detailed in Table 1. All participants in both groups adhered to the assigned intervention protocols as verified by the fidelity checklist. No deviations requiring exclusion were observed.

SpO2 during intervals

A significant main effect of time was observed (F = 2.556, p = 0.028, partial η² = 0.042), indicating fluctuations in SpO₂ levels over time. The effect size falls between small and medium, suggesting that time had a modest influence on oxygen saturation during the intervals, although its explanatory power was limited.

A significant main effect of group was also found (F = 6.824, p = 0.011, partial η² = 0.105), indicating that SpO₂ levels differed between groups. The effect size, falling between medium and large, suggests that the arms-crossing intervention had a meaningful impact on interval SpO₂ stability, despite all values remaining within normal physiological limits.

No significant time-by-group interaction effect was found (F = 0.587, p = 0.710, partial η² = 0.010).

Discussion

This study innovatively introduced the arms-crossing technique as a pain relief measure for women undergoing vaginal birth during the latent phase of labour in a hospital in southeastern China. Our findings indicate that the arms-crossing technique may significantly reduce subjective pain scores. However, it has no notable effect on heart rate or SpO2 levels, though it may potentially stabilise SpO2 levels during intervals between contractions.

How the arms-crossing technique may modulate labour pain

Both groups in the present study received standardised Lamaze breathing intervention, which may itself contribute to labour pain relief through rhythmic breathing–related analgesic mechanisms. Supporting this possibility, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported moderate evidence that breathing exercises during the first stage of labour may reduce pain intensity [21]. Additional support comes from a non-obstetric clinical study showing that rhythmic breathing alone reduced postoperative acute pain [22]. Consistent with these findings, pain scores also decreased over time in the Lamaze-only control group in the present study.

Importantly, however, the intervention group demonstrated significantly greater reductions in pain scores under otherwise comparable breathing conditions. Because the Lamaze intervention procedures, implementation protocols, and interventionists were standardised across both groups, the superior analgesic outcome is likely attributable to the additional arms-crossing component beyond the shared effects of rhythmic breathing alone. These findings therefore support an independent analgesic contribution of the arms-crossing technique during labour.

One possible explanation is that repeated arms-crossing movements may introduce additional sensorimotor and attentional modulation during painful uterine contractions. Consistent with the crossed-hands literature, crossing the body midline may alter multisensory processing and influence the cognitive perception of pain [10]. Neuroimaging studies have also suggested that arms-crossing postures may influence activity in cortical regions involved in sensory integration and pain modulation [12–14]. The enhanced analgesic effect observed in the intervention group may additionally reflect a degree of additive or potentially synergistic interaction between rhythmic breathing and the sensorimotor modulation introduced by the arms-crossing movements. However, these mechanistic interpretations remain hypothetical and require confirmation through future neurophysiological and neuroimaging research.

Physiological trends in heart rate and SpO₂

Pain activates a network of brain regions including the anterior cingulate cortex, which projects to the amygdala and hypothalamus. In turn, the hypothalamus modulates autonomic responses such as elevated heart rate and sweating [8]. Based on this mechanism, we initially hypothesised that the arms-crossing technique might influence autonomic indicators such as heart rate and SpO₂. However, our findings did not show clear support for this.

In our analysis, only the SpO₂ levels during intervals between contractions were significantly more stable in the intervention group than in the control group (p = 0.011). However, SpO₂ remained within normal physiological limits in both groups throughout the study. The observed difference, though statistically significant, may reflect a subtle physiological trend rather than a clinically meaningful effect. A likely explanation is that the pain experienced during contractions—despite being mitigated by the intervention—remained in the moderate range (4–6 on the numerical rating scale), and therefore may not have been sufficient to provoke distinct or sustained autonomic changes in either group.

Strength and limitations of the work and recommendations for further research

Strengths: (i) This study is the first to explore the use of the arms-crossing technique in labour pain management, demonstrating its effectiveness in alleviating pain with high participant compliance and no reported side effects. These findings suggest significant potential for the technique’s widespread application; (ii) This randomised and blinded study design minimised biases from known and unknown confounding factors; (iii) Both the intervention and control groups had the same interventionist, with both groups practicing Lamaze breathing under their guidance and avoiding other pain relief methods. This controlled for external factors, enabling a more accurate assessment of the intervention’s effectiveness and enhancing the reliability of the results; (iv) Outcome data were collected with precise timing, as indicated by fetal heart rate monitoring.

Limitations: (i) Due to ethical concerns, a blank control group without any pain support for women in the latent phase of labour was not feasible. Standardised rhythmic breathing was therefore provided as homogeneous background care for both groups to ensure that the arms-crossing technique remained the primary intervention difference between groups. Despite this control of confounding factors, the study could not fully isolate the absolute independent analgesic effect of the arms-crossing technique in the absence of rhythmic breathing. (ii) Pain assessment relied primarily on subjective and indirect physiological indicators. Objective biomarkers such as blood pressure, stress-related salivary or plasma components (e.g., cortisol), and neural activity (e.g., EEG or fMRI) could offer more comprehensive evaluations in future studies. The absence of such measures limits the strength of conclusions regarding physiological analgesia. (iii) The homogeneity of the study population—limited to women of Chinese ethnicity from a single hospital—constrains the generalisability of the findings. Cultural, genetic, and contextual factors can influence both pain perception and responses to non-pharmacological interventions. Multi-centre trials involving more ethnically and regionally diverse populations are needed to determine the broader applicability of the arms-crossing technique.

Implications for policy and practice

This study suggests that the arms-crossing technique is a viable and effective option for labour pain management, particularly in settings where pharmacological options are limited or contraindicated. Policymakers and healthcare providers should consider incorporating this technique into routine labour pain management protocols. Additionally, incorporating movements of arms crossing the body’s midline into existing non-pharmacological strategies—such as dancing and changing positions—may further enhance the effectiveness of these methods in alleviating labour pain.

Conclusion

Adding an arms-crossing technique to Lamaze breathing significantly reduced labour pain without observable adverse effects. These findings support the potential value of the arms-crossing technique as a simple non-pharmacological strategy for labour pain management.
