Partial cardiopulmonary bypass in thoracoabdominal aorta repair: a safe alternative in a low-volume center
Authors: Nicolas Nunez-Ordonez, Julian Senosiain, Juan Andres Sarmiento, Carlos A Villa, Carlos Obando, Tomas Chalela, Mauricio Palomino, Sergio A Ortigoza-Espitia, Ivonne Pineda-Rodriguez, Nestor Sandoval, Jaime Camacho-Mackenzie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe postoperative outcomes with the use of partial CPB for thoracoabdominal aortic repair.MethodsWe included all the patients who underwent programmed thoracoabdominal aortic procedures in which partial cardiopulmonary bypass was used at a single institution. Long term follow-up was collected by review of health records, national databases and via telephone.ResultsThe study involved 134 patients. Hypertension was present in 78% of patients. The primary surgical indication was an aortic aneurysm (67%), though aortic dissection was also common (33%). Extensive repairs and postoperative complications, such as acute kidney injury and RRT requirement, were significantly associated with increased mortality (p < 0.05). Patients with aortic dissection had worse outcomes, including higher rates of hypertension, stroke, and other complications (p < 0.05). Early mortality was notably higher in extent and II and IV repairs.ConclusionsThoracoabdominal aorta repair can be safely performed with the use of partial CPB in a low-volume setting when undertaken by a dedicated, experienced Aorta team.Keywords: Thoracoabdominal aortic repair, Partial cardiopulmonary bypass

Background

Thoracoabdominal aortic pathologies are still a challenging disease. Despite significant advances in endovascular therapies, open surgery remains the cornerstone for managing this group of patients. Current guidelines indicate treatment for patients with asymptomatic aneurysms larger than 55 mm (class Ic) as the probability of complications such as rupture or death grows exponentially beyond this measure. The repair should also be considered in high-risk patients with smaller diameters [1].

A successful open repair depends on the combination of a timely intervention and an adequate surgical technique that provides end-organ protection [1, 2]. Different adjunctive measures have been implemented in this regard, including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage to improve spinal perfusion [3], a sequential clamping strategy with selective visceral perfusion or intercostal and lumbar artery reimplantation. Several options are available as well for extracorporeal circulation which include cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), ECMO, left-heart bypass (LHB), or deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA).

Left heart bypass (LHB) has become the mainstay for surgical repair as some of the highest volume centers have reported improved outcomes in terms of a reduced incidence of paraplegia and paraparesis, over 2 decades ago [4]. The evidence, however, has been contradictory in other studies in smaller volume centers [5].

Implementing LHB can be challenging in a low-volume center as it requires specialized training with a different perfusion technique that is not commonly used in other settings of cardiovascular surgery; a special configuration with cell-saving devices allowing rapid reinfusion are frequently required, and these technologies are not widely available in resource limited settings. Therefore, at our hospital, we have continued to use CPB for thoracoabdominal aortic repair, aiming to improve operative team coordination. It was believed that this could provide the whole team (surgeon-anesthesiologist-perfusionist) with a familiar CPB setting that is used in day-to-day cardiac surgical practice.

The aim of the study was to describe postoperative outcomes with the use of CPB for thoracoabdominal aortic repair during the last two decades at a referral center for aortic surgery in Colombia with a relatively low volume of thoracoabdominal aortic repairs.

Methods

This was a retrospective cohort study. We included all the patients that underwent a programmed open thoracoabdominal aortic repair (according to the classification that was used by [6] from 2002- February 2024. Surgical procedures performed in an emergency setting due to aortic rupture or trauma were excluded. Partial Cardiopulmonary Bypass technique for the lower body (below the aortic clamp) and viscera was established via femoro-femoral cannulation. We use 3/8 tubing lines to reduce the priming volume. Visceral perfusion is provided with blood from the pump through an independent roller pump. Menasche cannulae are used in the celiac trunk and Superior mesenteric artery. Cold renoplegia with a 4 °C solution made with Ringer Lactate, Mannitol and Methylprednisolone is provided through an independent circuit. Three pump suctions are used to retrieve volume. And additional suction from a cell saving device is used in all cases.

Postoperatively, patients were managed with mean arterial pressure goals of 80–100 mmHg. In the cases in which CSF drainage was used, we limit drainage to 10-15 ml/h and in cases in which neurological symptoms develop, up to 20 ml/h can be gathered, aiming to improve perfusion pressure.

Baseline characteristics, intraoperative and postoperative data was gathered from the cardiac surgery database at Fundación Cardioinfantil-Instituto de Cardiologia (Bogotá, Colombia), which is compliant with the STS Database standards and definitions. Follow-up was performed by review of medical records, national databases, and updated phone calls.

Main outcomes included 30-day mortality, neurological complications (Stroke or TIA), postoperative spinal injury and kidney injury. Information on other key variables such as hospital length of stay, renal replacement therapy requirement, cardiac/respiratory/abdominal complications was also compiled as secondary outcomes.

A standard descriptive analysis was performed. We provide information on absolute and relative frequency for categorical variables, and continuous variables are presented according to their distribution. The Student’s t-test, Mann-Whitney, ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used for bivariate analysis according to the nature and distribution of the variables. The Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test were used for categorical variables. A standard level of significance (p = 0.05) was considered for determining statistically significant differences. Post-hoc corrections for multiple comparisons with the Bonferroni method were used. For the main variables a survival analysis using the Kaplan Meier method was created with a Cox’s proportional hazard model to assess differences between groups. Data were analyzed using STATA 15.

The study protocol was in accordance with the standards of the Helsinki Declaration and with national regulations; it was approved by the IRB and the ethics committee with the code CEIC–160–2024. Given the retrospective, no-risk nature of the study, the need for patient consent was waived in accordance with national and international regulations. Verbal consent was obtained and recorded prior to telephonic follow-up.

Results

The screening algorithm is shown in Fig. 1. A total of 134 patients with a median age of 58 years were included in the final analysis. Patient baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. The most common comorbidities were hypertension (78%) and dyslipidemia (27%). About one third of the patients had a history of prior cardiac interventions. Most patients presented on NYHA functional class I-II/IV (91%) and with normal or near-normal LVEF. The most frequent primary indication for the procedure was the presence of an aortic aneurysm (67%) however aortic dissection was also frequently encountered, in around one third of the patients.

Information on 100% of patients was gathered for the main outcome. A stratified analysis according to surgical indication was performed, to determine associations with a risk of mortality, it is shown in Table 1. Among the preoperative variables, only the extension of the repair was significantly associated with a higher mortality (p = 0.003). Predictably, the onset of complications in the early postoperative period such as Acute Kidney Injury, RRT requirement or gastrointestinal ischemic complication, showed a statistically significant association with the risk of mortality (p-0.003, 0.001 and 0.005 respectively).

Table 2 shows the analysis of outcomes according to the primary indication for surgery (aneurysm vs. dissection). Patients with aortic dissection were more frequently hypertensive upon admission however hypertension was also frequent in the aneurysm group (91% vs. 72%, p = 0.014). A history of stroke or myocardial infarction was more common in the group of patients with dissection (11%, p = 0.038 and 9%, p = 0.09 respectively). Although no statistically significant differences were found between groups, 20% of patients with aortic dissection had a prior diagnosis of Marfan syndrome, as compared to 13% of patients in the aneurysm group (p = 0.07). Postoperative outcomes were significantly different between groups. Patients presenting with dissection more commonly required reoperation for bleeding (p = 0.04) and developed transient ischemic spinal cord injury (p = 0.016), stroke (p = 0.04), lower extremity paralysis (p = 0.04) and acute kidney injury (p = 0.019) in the early postoperative period.

Discussion

Choosing an adequate perfusion technique is a critical step in aortic surgery. Several adjunctive techniques have been implemented so far as an effort to reduce the rate of postoperative ischemic complications and are almost universally used; these include interventions such as selective visceral perfusion, CSF drainage, cold renal perfusion, moderate heparinization, hypothermia, sequential aortic clamping or intercostal artery reattachment [4, 7].

However, the ideal cannulation and perfusion strategy has not been clearly established. It is dependent on different factors such as the extent of the repair, the presence of aortic dissection and familiarity of the surgical team with each method. In a study in 1999 Coselli et al. showed that the use of left heart bypass (LHB) significantly reduced the rate of paraplegia or paraparesis in patients undergoing extent II TAA repair (13.1% vs. 4.9%, p = 0.007) [4]. This became a standardized approach particularly for complex TAA surgery (extents I and II) under the premise that it reduced spinal cord ischemia by guaranteeing an improved intercostal artery flow, even despite longer aortic clamping times. Other large volume centers have implemented it with varying results [8–10].

Although ideal, in low-volume centers, the learning curve for the adequate use of LHB by the whole operative team might be long and difficult to achieve as it requires a different setup and perfusion technique as compared to what the cardiovascular team might be more familiar with (such as the requirement of rapid blood re-infusion), thus compromising patient safety. Therefore, we have consistently used partial Cardiopulmonary bypass as our standardized perfusion technique in TAA surgery for the past two decades.

Our results showed a postoperative mortality risk (11.9% overall) which is still far from the results of large-volume centers as Coselli’s (7.5%) [7]. Nevertheless, other large studies from centers that have been routinely implementing LHB have reported mortality rates that are at least comparable with our results [11] (Tanaka et al. – 16% overall mortality [12], Rocha et al. – 17.4% [13]). In a similar way, other centers using CPB as a standard perfusion technique have reported an in-hospital mortality around 5% and 85% 3-year survival [14, 15]. In our series, the extent of the repair (Crawford II) and visceral branch involvement (IV) seem to be the main variables associated with an increased mortality rate. Even if it was not possible to find associations with the increasing learning curve due to the limited sample size, our experience suggests that both hard and soft clinical outcomes have been progressively improving as the core aortic team (dedicated aortic surgeon, anesthesiologist, perfusionist and ICU staff) has been consolidated.

The rationale for the use of LHB from Coselli’s studies is an improved rate of SCI [7]. In our study using CPB, the rate of paraparesis > 24 h was 4.5% which is also at least comparable to the reported rate of complications in other studies using LHB [11, 16]. (Tanaka et al. – 8% [12], Rocha et al. – 4.1% [13]) and similar to other centers primarily using CPB [14] (Adam et al. – 3% [15]).

The most frequent postoperative complication was kidney injury (19,4%) however less than 7% required permanent postoperative kidney replacement therapy, as compared to rates ranging from 11 to 21% that have been reported in centers using LHB [10, 11, 12, 13, 14].

Conclusions

Thoracoabdominal aorta repair could be safely performed with the use of CPB in a low-volume setting when undertaken by a dedicated, experienced cardiovascular surgery team including a surgeon, anesthesiologist, perfusionist as well as a specially trained postoperative ICU care. It seems to generate a comparable mortality to other centers with similar or higher operative volume with low rates of ischemic complications.

Limitations

This was a retrospective study with a period of interest spanning over 2 decades, a time during which significant advances have been made in Aortic surgery. During this time the technique that we currently perform was developed and standardized at our center. We also implemented throughout the years novel adjuvant techniques to improve ischemic protection which might have guaranteed adequate outcomes despite the use of CPB. The current study is subject to inherent limitations due to its observational nature which were accounted for by using a comprehensive database and appropriate statistical analysis.
