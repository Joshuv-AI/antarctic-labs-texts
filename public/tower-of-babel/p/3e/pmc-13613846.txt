Artificial intelligence in urological education: a systematic review
Authors: Guanchen Liu, Long Xu, Jue Gong, Guangming Yin, Peng Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) is currently widely applied across various fields, especially in the field of education where it holds significant implications. This systematic review aims to explore the specific role of AI in urological education.MethodsFollowing the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, we searched the PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases for articles related to AI in urological education published up to February 10, 2026. The quality of included studies was assessed using the Medical Education Research Study Quality Instrument (MERSQI), and educational outcomes were classified based on the modified Kirkpatrick framework.ResultsA total of 15 eligible study records were finally included. The use of AI in urology education can be categorized into two groups: interventions that are used directly in the educational process, and enabling technologies that provide support for education. It was showed that combining AI in urology education had a positive impact on both learners and teachers. For learners such as medical students, urology residents, or interns, the AI-based hybrid teaching model increased learning efficiency and improved students’ theoretical exam scores. Meanwhile, the combination of high-fidelity virtual reality simulation training and intelligent AI feedback showed potential in facilitating the acquisition of clinical skills, in which simulation scores were positively correlated with surgical potential. On the other hand, as the supporting or enabling technology, AI assisted teachers in designing teaching materials and developing assessment programs. In terms of evaluating learners’ performance, AI provided fair and objective evaluation results. In addition, machine learning and computer vision algorithms incorporating AI could automatically recognize surgical steps with high precision.ConclusionThe application of AI possesses the capacity to drive transformative changes in urological education. AI can serve as an effective tool to improve students’ learning efficiency, theoretical knowledge level and clinical skills through assisted teaching. Moreover, as the enabling technology, the application of AI may be potential in providing support during the teaching process. But AI should be utilized in a responsible and ethical manner.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09687-6.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is currently receiving an increasing amount of attention and is widely applied across numerous fields [1]. Simply put, AI refers to the technology that enables computer systems to simulate human intelligence processes. It encompasses four core branches that are interconnected yet each with its own distinct focus: machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), computer vision (CV), and natural language processing (NLP) [2]. In this study, we define AI as computer systems or algorithms capable of performing tasks that typically require human cognitive functions, such as learning, recognition, and decision-making. The technologies discussed in this paper primarily include generative large language models (LLMs) for interactive teaching and assessment, as well as machine learning (ML) and computer vision (CV) algorithms for surgical video analysis and skill assessment. Although this review includes studies on virtual reality (VR) simulations, we have excluded traditional VR systems that merely provide digital immersive environments; the VR simulators included in this analysis are all AI-assisted.

Currently, AI has been applied in various domains of surgical education. For instance, in the field of neurosurgery, Winkler-Schwartz et al. utilized machine learning algorithms to conduct an in-depth analysis of trainees’ micromechanical and instrument kinematics data on a virtual reality (VR) brain tumor resection simulator [3], successfully establishing an automated objective skill grading model with an accuracy of up to 90%. In the context of postoperative debriefing, recent advancements in CV have enabled the automated recognition of surgical phases and tracking of instrument kinematics from video recordings. Such intelligent systems can now automatically segment core procedural steps, allowing medical students and surgical trainees to efficiently review complex anatomical maneuvers without the need for manual video editing [4].

However, in the field of urological education, research exploring the application and efficacy of AI in teaching remains extremely scarce. The traditional lecture-based learning (LBL) method in urology is teacher-centered. It adopts a “spoon-feeding” teaching method, wherein students passively listen to theoretical knowledge. This approach lacks teacher-student interaction and neglects students’ self-directed learning abilities [5]. Consequently, it possesses obvious limitations: it overemphasizes theory over practice, restricts students’ thinking, and makes it difficult for them to translate textbook knowledge into genuine clinical practical skills [6]. An increasing number of novel teaching methods are being attempted, such as problem-based learning (PBL) and case-based learning (CBL) [7]. These methods have been proven to enhance students’ interest, self-learning ability, clinical analytical skills, comprehension, and memory, while simultaneously making the classroom more dynamic [8]. Currently, preliminary research indicates that AI is beneficial to teaching, but its specific role in urological education is not yet fully elucidated [9].

Therefore, this paper aims to systematically review and evaluate the application and effects of AI in urological teaching. Within the framework of competency-based medical education (CBME), objective and validated assessment tools are considered an essential component of educational interventions. Therefore, in order to address the conceptual heterogeneity of AI applications, this review explicitly categorizes the included articles into two distinct areas: direct educational interventions applied to learners, and enabling technologies focusing on technology validation. This will lay the foundation for the future application and promotion of AI in the field of urological education.

Methods

The search strategy and selection criteria

The implementation and reporting of this systematic review were conducted entirely following Preferred Reporting Items on Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 recommendations [10]. The PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases were searched comprehensively from January 1, 2016, to February 10, 2026. No automatic database filters (such as language or article type filters) were used during the search. Scientifically, the search strategy incorporated controlled vocabularies (Medical Subject Headings, MeSH) and free-text keywords. The main search concepts tended to be very close to the notions of AI, Machine Learning, Large Language Models, Virtual Reality, Urological Education and the related synonyms. All the electronic search queries with every combination of Boolean operators and particular MeSH terms are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Prior to the literature screening, our research team had internally developed a standardized review protocol; however, this protocol was not prospectively registered in a public database. This decision was made to avoid lengthy registration wait times, given the rapid pace of iteration in AI technology. Nevertheless, all procedures strictly adhered to the PRISMA guidelines to ensure transparency in our research methodology.

Eligibility criteria

To ensure that literature which is strongly aligned to the goals of this work is filtered effectively, inclusion and exclusion criteria were set prior to the screening process. Inclusion criteria stipulated that articles must simultaneously include all four elements below: (1) Study population: the population encompasses both ends of the medical education spectrum. This includes trainees (medical students, urology interns, trained residents), fellows and educators (attending physicians, clinical faculty, surgical instructors), and human evaluators involved in skill assessment. (2) Interventions: use of AI-based large language models, machine learning algorithms, computer vision systems or intelligent VR simulators (Eligible articles that include virtual reality must integrate AI components or algorithms. These AI components primarily involve computer vision algorithms for automated extraction and analysis of kinematic data and for real-time surgical task recognition.) These studies fall into two main categories: educational interventions in which AI is involved in the “teaching and learning” or “feedback” aspects of teaching. The other is AI enabling technologies that test the intelligence of AI and the accuracy of the data measured to provide a technological foundation for future teaching and learning. (3) Comparators: For direct educational interventions, traditional clinical teaching or no intervention was used as a control. For the AI technology validation and feasibility study, traditional manual scoring or assessment by a senior human expert was defined as the standard control group. (4) Outcomes: quantifying educational assessment measures (e.g. objective exam scores, OSATS/GOALS ratings, operation time or validated satisfaction scores). Non-English literature, review papers, case reports, and multidisciplinary medical journals that exclusively addressed AI-assisted clinical condition diagnosis but did not provide a clear pedagogy aim were presented in the exclusion criteria, as well as traditional virtual reality (VR) or computer-aided simulation platforms that lack clear AI integration.

Selection process

Each reference identified during the search was imported into the EndNote citation management software, and duplicates were automatically removed by the software. In the first stage of screening, two reviewers independently assessed the titles and abstracts of all identified records based on predefined eligibility criteria. In the second stage, potentially relevant articles were retrieved in full text and re-evaluated independently by the same two reviewers. Any disagreements or inconsistencies arising at either screening stage were resolved through detailed discussion to reach consensus, or by consulting a third senior and experienced researcher for final arbitration. Following the screening, the two reviewers independently and in duplicate extracted data using Microsoft Excel. To best adhere to the PRISMA recommendations, we systematically extracted the following key data elements: (1) Initial study features (e.g. lead author, year of publication, country of origin and detailed experimental design, e.g. randomized controlled or prospective educational studies); (2) Characteristics of the participants (e.g. overall sample size, clinical training level, and previous surgical or AI experience); (3) Intervention and control information (including the exact type of AI technology, particular applications in cognitive/ psychomotor or assessment domains, and the pedagogical structure of the control group); (4) Key educational results (obtaining their respective specific Kirkpatrick evaluation, main outcome measure devices like the objective time metric on simulator, and overall statistics like mean differences and p-values).

Synthesis of results

Considering the extreme heterogeneity of the literature in terms of methods of design and clinical application settings- as is especially evident in the variety of AI technology types (as in the use of both LLMs and VR alongside computer vision) and large variation in target population, highly inconsistent measures of educational outcomes - the forced aggregation of such data into a meta-analysis of quantitative data was considered as not methodologically sound. However, the review used a narrative synthesis approach that was informed by reflexive thematic analysis [11]. We were able to present the shared value of AI technologies in the various aspects of education in urology through systematic synthesis of the study findings in a logical flow. In order to avoid overestimating the reliability of the various pieces of evidence, we have divided our analysis into two parts: direct educational interventions, and feasibility validation of the relevant technologies. Reporting practices as suggested by PRISMA and the latest recommendation on reporting of systematic reviews of medical education were used. Duplicate screening and extraction were done to reduce errors. Structured thematic synthesis was used to synthesize findings. Two reviewers (G.C.L. and L.X.) independently applied the Medical Education Research Study Quality Instrument (MERSQI) in the quality methodological control of quantitative studies [12]. Any discrepancies in scoring were resolved through discussion; if no consensus was reached, a senior reviewer (P.Y.) made the final decision. The MERSQI is a multidimensional scale that examines the literature in six main areas: study designs, sampling methods, types of data (subjective or objective), validity of assessment measures, and complexity of data analysis, and outcome measures, and it has a maximum score of 18 [13]. MERSQI is essentially an educational research quality indicator rather than a formal risk of bias (RoB) assessment tool. However, due to the methodological heterogeneity of the included studies—ranging from randomized controlled trials to AI technology validation studies— Applying a single standard RoB framework (such as Cochrane RoB 2 or ROBINS-I) uniformly across all studies is methodologically inappropriate. Therefore, MERSQI provides a framework capable of standardizing the quality of these studies, which is used in this article to encompass these diverse study designs. Also, in order to stratify the strength of evidence of these educational interventions in a more orderly and standardized manner, we adopted an innovative approach to apply a modified Kirkpatrick structure [14]. This theoretical framework strictly divides the effects of pedagogical results into four levels as follows: Level I (reaction and satisfaction of trainees); Level II (performance of learning which is further divided into level IIa, changes in attitude and level IIb, acquisition of knowledge/skills); Level III (transfer of skills to the clinical setting and behavior change); Level IV (results, i.e., improvement in real post-operative health care of patients or health indicators) [15].

Results

After searching the PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science databases systematically, we have found a cumulative number of 1,199 articles published as of February 10, 2026. Automated removal of duplicates left us with 1,171 unique citations. Eventually, there were 15 quantitative studies that met all the criteria and incorporated into the last synthesis (Fig. 1).

In the field of urology education, the use of AI and its impacts can be summarized in five main areas: The enhancement of student learning efficiency through AI-assisted teaching, the enhancement of medical trainees’ theoretical knowledge and feedback behavior, the feasibility of enhancing clinical skills, the performance validation of AI models in professional examinations, and the optimization of competency assessment and the potential to alleviate faculty workload. To ensure conceptual clarity, these findings were synthesized into two overarching domains: educational interventions and learner outcomes which synthesize the direct impacts on trainees and encompassing concepts as well as enabling technologies and performance validation which evaluate the basic functionality and automated assessment capabilities of AI systems, and encompassing concepts.

Educational interventions and learner outcomes.

The enhancement of student learning efficiency through. AI-assisted teaching. A randomized controlled trial by Digiacomo et al. demonstrated that a blended teaching model combining ChatGPT with traditional lectures can improve learning outcomes [17]. The median test score of the blended group (12 points) was higher than that of the pure ChatGPT group (10 points, p = 0.007). In addition, Hui et al. found that ChatGPT-assisted PBL (Problem-Based Learning) teaching enabled students to reach a theoretical score of 93.90 ± 3.65 at the end of the course [18], which was superior to the traditional teaching group’s 90.33 ± 4.08 (p < 0.01).Enhance medical trainees’ theoretical knowledge and feedback behavior. Performance validation of AI models in professional examinations. Hetz et al. confirmed that the dedicated language model UroBot-4o [23], optimized through Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) combined with the European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines, achieved an average accuracy rate of 88.4% when answering urology board exam questions, surpassing the average level of human urologists (68.7%). A study by Başaranoğlu et al. pointed out that traditional test question development typically requires 45–60 min per question [16], whereas using AI-assisted development can shorten this to 15–20 min, reducing the time consumption and cost per validated question by 65%. Similarly, Henning et al. observed that AI video platforms (e.g., Theator) can automate the tedious task of surgical video annotation [22]. Discussion

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the impact of introducing AI into medical education on both students and clinical faculty. It should be emphasized that AI in urology teaching can act as a direct educational intervention for the learner to improve the educational effectiveness. Our research results indicate that AI-assisted teaching tools have the potential to enhance students’ learning efficiency, mastery of theoretical knowledge, and acquisition of clinical skills [31]. The improvement in student learning efficiency and theoretical knowledge observed in this study may be theoretically attributed to the personalized features of AI algorithms. Unlike traditional large-class lectures, intelligent tutoring systems are designed to analyze students’ individual performance in real-time, push targeted learning resources, and provide immediate feedback [32]. In principle, AI has the capacity to meet the needs of different learning styles by generating multiple types of learning methods, such as combining charts, videos, and text. Furthermore, theoretical models suggest that the spaced repetition algorithm underlying the system could combat the knowledge forgetting curve, which also aligns with the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve to help students better remember knowledge [33]. If fully realized, these technical features offer the potential to enhance students’ learning efficiency, enabling them to focus their energy on filling specific knowledge blind spots, thereby accelerating the learning process [34].

In terms of learning efficiency and theoretical knowledge, our conclusions align with one previous study [35] and the results of multiple recent systematic reviews. They similarly found that AI-driven adaptive learning platforms can improve medical students’ learning efficiency and knowledge retention rates through personalized feedback. In addition, urology trainers using an AI-assisted virtual reality training system showed improved clinical skills. By providing an immersive, high-fidelity simulated environment, students can safely engage in repeated hands-on practice under the premise of reduced ethical burden and patient risk [36].

In terms of clinical skill training, the positive impact observed in this study corroborates research conclusions regarding AI combined with virtual reality (VR) simulators, such as the recently published article by De Luca et al. [37], which explored how immersive environments hold the potential to promote the standardization of clinical procedures and the formation of muscle memory. However, although this study observed positive effects within a short-term intervention, some existing literature has also proposed findings that differ from our results. This study emphasized the advantages of AI in improving clinical operational skills, but some scholars have raised opposite concerns. For example, a recent survey targeting clinicians highlighted that if there is an over-reliance on AI’s diagnostic recommendations and automated guidance during early training, it may weaken young doctors’ independent clinical reasoning abilities and basic physical examination skills [38]. Furthermore, as pointed out by scholars such as Sriram, AI simulators excel in enhancing procedural operations, but they cannot completely replicate real doctor-patient communication, empathy, and the adaptability to face complex clinical environments. These are blind spots that AI-assisted teaching currently cannot reach [39].

Besides direct educational impact and benefit (e.g., improvement in knowledge, skills, or clinical competence), AI may be also potential in utilized as an enabling technology that provides technical support and infrastructure for the educational process. Regarding technical capabilities in surgical environments, the AI system possesses the capability to identify surgical steps and provide automated micro-skill feedback. In future applications, this technology has the potential to play a crucial role in perfecting operational standards before trainees truly enter the operating room. Traditional clinical assessments are inherently subjective, time-consuming, and prone to scoring inconsistencies. By utilizing AI to generate theoretical test questions and relying on machine learning models for objective clinical skill evaluation, the reliance on continuous, direct observation by teachers could be minimized to the greatest extent [40].

In addition, in terms of optimizing assessment and reducing teacher burden, our research results resonate with the latest studies exploring AI-assisted scoring for Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCE), such as the latest research in JMIR by Yokose et al. [41]. These validation studies confirm that utilizing Natural Language Processing (NLP) and machine learning algorithms for real-time objective scoring shows promise for enhancing the standardization degree of assessment and potentially cutting the administrative and grading burden of clinical educators. At the same time, the future implementation of automated, AI-driven assessment methods offers a pathway to optimize the evaluation process, thereby holding the potential to reduce the administrative and routine teaching workload of clinical teachers [42]. From the perspective of educators, the potential reduction in workload is a projected result of the optimization of assessment methods. This envisioned shift would enable clinical faculty to reallocate their precious time to higher-order clinical mentoring and complex case discussions, rather than being bogged down in administrative work such as routine grading.

Furthermore, regarding technical accuracy, it is also important to note that although some studies, such as Hetz et al. have reported “superhuman” performance in professional exams, a recent study by Katsimperis et al. showed that the accuracy of a large-scale language model on urology board-style questions are currently limited to 63% to 77% accuracy. These validation metrics demonstrate that while large language models may be useful as a quick reference tool for doctors in training, their error rates are still too high to replace traditional learning methods or well-validated educational resources, and they are still a long way from being fully reliable [43]. And their study also mentions that the problem of AI ‘illusion’ also poses a serious threat to academic integrity. AI will falsify citations that seem plausible but don’t actually exist. While AI can significantly improve writing efficiency, its tendency to invent citations poses a threat to research rigor. For trainees, if used uncritically, it can undermine trust in scientific work and damage developing academic reputations. Thus, AI is an excellent assistant, but it is not yet the primary source of clinical evidence. Rigorous manual verification remains the most preferred option to ensure the authenticity and reliability of all cited literature in medical education.

The core findings of this study largely remain consistent with recent literature exploring the application of AI in medical education. The positive results of this study confirm the potential of AI as a medical education tool, consistent with the optimistic expectations of mainstream literature [44]. However, comparing the prudent findings of other scholars also reminds us: AI improves learning efficiency and holds the potential to enhance the degree of standardization, but it cannot completely replace the teaching of human experts and real doctor-patient interactions [45].

This study also provides new supplements to several core theories in the field of medical education: First, it promotes the theoretical implementation of “Competency-Based Medical Education (CBME)”: For a long time, the medical education community has continuously called for a transition from being “time-oriented” to “competency-oriented,” but the theoretical bottleneck lies in the lack of objective, continuous competency assessment indicators [46]. This study demonstrates the technical capacity of AI and machine learning models to quantify clinical skills and provide automated assessments. This theoretically indicates how AI could fill the gap in the CBME assessment system, suggesting that “data-driven competency evaluation” is technically feasible for future applications.

Despite achieving the above positive results, this study still has certain limitations. First, the sample size is relatively limited, and the research subjects are confined to a specific trainee group, which may limit the universal applicability of the conclusions of this study in broader medical disciplines. Second, although AI tools demonstrated high technical accuracy in validation studies, and showed positive educational effects on short-term skill acquisition, this study did not evaluate students’ long-term knowledge retention rate, nor did it track whether these skills obtained on the simulator can be fully translated into patient benefits in real clinical environments. Third, there may be uncontrolled potential confounding variables that could influence their interactions with AI platforms. Finally, a more fundamental issue lies in the heterogeneity of the evidence and the fact that its applicability to the field of education remains unclear. Some of the included studies evaluated the performance of AI rather than that of learners. Therefore, while these studies demonstrate the technical feasibility of AI tools, the lack of direct learner data prevents them from fully substantiating the long-term impact of these tools on trainees’ actual clinical behavior. It is also important to note that the MERSQI is designed to assess the overall methodological rigor of the included studies, rather than to formally quantify the risk of bias. This limitation suggests that, while the included studies are of acceptable methodological quality, the underlying evidence regarding specific experimental biases should be interpreted with caution.

Furthermore, while the future of AI in urological education looks bright, greater attention must be paid to ethical considerations. Since AI technology is still in its early stages of development, we need to devote more effort to ensuring that the technology is safer and more robust. Regarding ethics, the first point of concern is algorithmic bias. If the datasets used to train AI-driven surgical simulators or automated assessment tools lack diversity or are insufficient in volume, the resulting models may produce biased assessment results or reinforce existing clinical biases. Second, data security and privacy must be strengthened, particularly when AI platforms handle sensitive information such as intraoperative videos of urological surgeries, patient personal information, or trainees’ surgical performance metrics. Strict data encryption and access controls must be ensured, as these are crucial for protecting intellectual property and personal data security. Third, the “black box” nature of many complex AI models leads to a lack of transparency in the results produced, which may hinder learning efficiency. For AI-driven feedback to have educational value, the feedback must be interpretable and reproducible. Fourth, learner surveillance is a double-edged sword: while it allows for more detailed recording and evaluation of trainees’ procedures, it also places psychological pressure on those being observed and restricts their autonomy. Fifth, accountability for AI-generated educational content must be addressed as soon as possible. Questions such as who should bear responsibility when AI provides incorrect information or guidance to urology trainees, leading to adverse consequences, and who should oversee the accuracy of the generated content, must be clarified in the near future. Addressing these ethical issues is crucial for building trust between educators and learners, as well as for ensuring the feasibility and sustainability of AI-assisted urology training.

In the future, it is recommended to conduct large-sample, multi-center longitudinal cohort studies to further verify whether AI-trained students can more effectively learn theoretical knowledge and improve clinical skills, and whether teachers (clinical instructors and professors teaching theory) have reduced their workload and improved teaching efficiency. At the same time, researchers should explore the effectiveness of AI assessment tools’ evaluation capabilities at different stages of standardized residency training. On a practical level, medical school administrators and curriculum planners should no longer merely view AI as an option; they should formulate clear institutional guidelines to evaluate and thoughtfully incorporate validated AI-assisted simulators and automated feedback mechanisms into the core curriculum framework of clinical clerkships and residency training, and ensure fair access rights and comprehensive technical training are provided for all teachers and students.

Conclusion

In summary, this systematic review clarifies the role of AI in urology education. AI can be used as an adjunct tool to improve students’ learning efficiency, theoretical knowledge level and clinical skills through assisted teaching. Moreover, as the enabling technology, the application of AI may be potential in providing support during the teaching process. But AI should be utilized in a responsible and ethical manner, and further studies are needed to determine these educational outcomes.
