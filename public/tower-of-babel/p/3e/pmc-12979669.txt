VolE: A point-cloud framework for food 3D reconstruction and volume estimation
Authors: Umair Haroon, Ahmad AlMughrabi, Thanasis Zoumpekas, Ricardo Marques, Petia Radeva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate food volume estimation is crucial for medical nutrition management and health monitoring applications. However, existing methods for estimating food volume are often constrained by the use of monocular data. They typically rely on specialised hardware such as 3D scanners, gather sensor-specific information such as depth data, or depend on camera calibration with reference objects. In this paper, we present VolE, a novel framework that leverages mobile device-driven 3D reconstruction to estimate food volume. VolE captures images and camera locations in free motion to generate precise 3D models, thanks to AR-capable mobile devices. To achieve real-world measurement, VolE is a reference- and depth-free framework that leverages food video segmentation for food mask generation. We also introduce a new food dataset encompassing the challenging scenarios absent in the previous benchmarks. Our experiments demonstrate that VolE outperforms the existing volume estimation techniques across multiple datasets by achieving 2.22% MAPE, highlighting its superior performance in food volume estimation. The source code is available at https://umairharon.github.io/VolE.

Introduction

Dietary monitoring and nutritional analysis are essential to understanding health requirements and trends at the individual and population levels1. However, these processes encounter significant obstacles in obtaining data that is accurate, scalable, and efficiently collected2. Traditional diet assessment methods3, while widely used, are often time-consuming, costly, and error-prone because they rely on human expertise rather than objective measurements. Recent advancements in machine learning and computer vision offer promising solutions to these challenges, particularly by utilising the information contained in captured images. Hence, image-based intelligent dietary assessment provides a promising solution by automating tasks such as food segmentation, recognition, and volume estimation, using computational image analysis4. Although progress has been made in food segmentation5,6 and recognition7,8, the automatic estimation of food volume remains a complex issue and is usually prone to high measurement errors9. Food volume estimation is particularly crucial for smartphone-based dietary monitoring, where users capture free-motion multi-view images of meals without the impracticality of carrying calibrated scales for every dish. While nutritional databases express energy and nutrient content per weight, direct weighing is rarely feasible in real-world settings (restaurants, cafeterias, home kitchens). The automatic estimation of food volume is essential to determine the nutritional or macronutrient content directly from captured images11,12, yet achieving accurate results remains a significant challenge.

Furthermore, volume estimation is fundamentally dependent on 3D information, as understanding the dimensions and shape of an object in three-dimensional space is crucial for determining its volume13. This is particularly relevant in dietary assessment, where precise measurement of food volume is essential for estimating nutritional intake3. However, accurately estimating food volume remains an open problem due to the need for 3D data and physical references in images, which are necessary for inferring real-world sizes14. Additionally, extracting volume and density information from two-dimensional images is inherently challenging, highlighting the importance of advanced 3D reconstruction methods.

Recent advancements in 3D reconstruction, such as Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)15 and Gaussian splatting16, have shown significant advantages in estimating 3D object shapes from multi-view images17. NeRFs leverage neural networks to create detailed volumetric representations of 3D scenes, while Gaussian splatting models 3D shapes use Gaussian primitives for smooth and efficient reconstructions18. Both techniques rely on accurate 3D camera poses, often obtained through Structure from Motion (SfM) tools (e.g., COLMAP)19. However, these methods are inherently metric-less, as SfM alone cannot recover the real-world scale of a scene due to the inherent ambiguity of 3D reconstruction from images, as multiple scene scales can produce identical image projections20. As a result, prior information about scene scale, such as object dimensions or camera distances, is essential to derive precise metric measurements13. This scale ambiguity creates a critical barrier for mobile dietary monitoring, where users cannot practically carry calibrated weighing scales, specialised hardware (e.g., 3D scanners), depth sensors, fixed environments10, or reference objects21 (e.g., chessboards) for every meal in real-world scenarios. VolE eliminates these barriers by leveraging ARCore/ARKit metric poses from everyday smartphones. This enables accurate volume-to-nutrient conversion using unconstrained, reference-free, scale-free smartphone videos, without the need for scales, references, or specialized equipment. Moreover, the challenges are further aggravated in food volume estimation due to low-textured food surfaces that hinder accurate 3D reconstruction, as traditional Multi-View Stereo methods struggle in such scenarios. Recent innovations, such as SA-MVSNet22, utilise self-attention mechanisms to enhance feature matching and depth estimation in weakly textured scenes. Other approaches23 focus on improving dense alignment and reconstruction, demonstrating considerable promise for food-related applications. Thus, these limitations and advancements present substantial challenges and opportunities in achieving precise and reliable measurements, which are critical for food applications.

On the other hand, current food volume estimation techniques 24,25 face several hurdles that limit their effectiveness in real-world applications. For instance, they rely on single-purpose hardware and sensors, such as 3D scanners, depth sensors, or multiple cameras, which can be costly, inaccessible, and difficult to deploy in everyday settings24. Others depend on predefined models or templates, which struggle to account for the wide variability in food shapes and textures, often resulting in inaccurate volume estimations25. While supervised learning methods26,27 can provide high accuracy, they often require large amounts of annotated data and significant computational resources, limiting their practicality for widespread use. Motivated by the aforementioned challenges, we propose a novel framework, entitled VolE, that addresses the limitations of existing methods in estimating the volume of objects, especially in scenarios involving free-motion scenarios, which are known to pose difficulties for accurate 3D reconstruction28. VolE leverages images and camera locations captured by AR-capable mobile devices that leverage ARCore29 or ARKit30 frameworks. Obtaining images and camera locations is now readily achievable with most modern smartphones, as these frameworks seamlessly integrate sensor data to provide accurate location and orientation information, simplifying the data acquisition process in real-time29,30. By combining this spatial awareness with the captured images, we reconstruct a scaled, dense 3D point cloud of the scene. Our approach enables free-motion and reference-free approaches. Related work

Food volume estimation has evolved significantly, addressing the challenges of accurate volume measurement from visual data. SfM techniques exemplified by COLMAP20 laid the groundwork for reconstructing 3D scene geometry and camera locations from multiple images. Furthermore, NeRF-based methods leverage COLMAP20 to estimate the camera locations as an input with images. For instance, InstantNGP 31 is focused on rapid neural graphics primitive training to enhance reconstruction speed. Moreover, NeuS/232,33 offered improvements in reconstruction quality and efficiency but continued to face challenges related to adapting to the variability of shapes and textures found in food volume estimation. NeuS/2 continues to face difficulties adapting to food items’ diverse shapes and textures32,33, as shown in Fig. 11. Despite all of their SfM- and NeRF-based methods benefits, they struggle with scale ambiguity that necessitates careful calibration or depth information for accurate volume estimation20,31–33.

To apply NeRF on food volume estimation, MetaFood CVPR challenge13 introduced new food volume estimation novels based on 3D reconstruction and NeRF methods, where VolETA 21, the winning approach, leverages SfM20 and NeuS/233 to create detailed food meshes and calculate precise volumes. Notably, the need for reference objects in some methods, such as VolETA21, can also limit their practicality in specific real-world scenarios. ININ-VIAUN13 integrated deep learning with traditional computer vision techniques, utilising Multi-View Stereo for depth information. FoodRiddle13 employed 3D Gaussian splatting to estimate food volumes, addressing challenges of data scarcity and complex food geometries.

Additionally, many datasets provided the object masks13,34, making the 3D reconstruction easier. However, mask generation must be leveraged for real-world scenarios. Yet, FoodMem5 is a good candidate to be integrated within the 3D reconstruction process as it is a near-real-time food video segmentation model leveraging SeTR35 and XMem++36 to track food objects in a food scene video. Its memory mechanism enhances segmentation consistency in dynamic scenes and occlusions5. In contrast, some approaches require single-purpose hardware24 or fixed environments10, limiting their practical application37.

In response to these challenges, we introduce VolE, a novel reference- and depth-free framework that significantly improves 3D reconstruction and volume estimation for food scenes, as shown in Fig. 1. Unlike VolETA21, ININ-VIAUN13, and FoodRiddle13, our framework does not rely on reference objects, depth information, or single-purpose hardware to scale reconstructed 3D models. Instead, it leverages standard mobile device capabilities, utilising ARCore29 for Android and ARKit30 for iOS to capture real-world measurements through device location and IMU data during video recording or image capture. By leveraging advanced segmentation techniques such as FoodMem5 and robust 3D reconstruction methods, our framework aims to provide an automated and accurate volume estimation across diverse capture conditions and food types. Our framework addresses the scale ambiguity issue by leveraging AR-based spatial mapping, ensuring adaptability to various food shapes through advanced segmentation, and maintaining efficiency without requiring extensive training data or complex calibration procedures. Proposed methodology

Our framework leverages images and camera locations from AR-capable mobile devices to accurately estimate food volumes of individual food items, assuming each food scene contains only one prominent food object. The methodology is structured as follows: we begin with an overview of the process, followed by a discussion on data acquisition. Next, we delve into food video segmentation, point cloud masking, and outline our approach to mesh reconstruction. We then explain the mesh refinement phase before concluding with our method for volume estimation.

Point cloud masking

Point cloud masking41 isolates specific objects within a 3D point cloud of a scene using 3D point clouds and camera poses generated from SfM. While this intersection-based approach robustly handles segmentation false positives (FPs) by requiring multi-view consensus, it is sensitive to false negatives (FNs) that could carve away from the object geometry. Our framework mitigates this through three strategies: (1) 360\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phantom{0}^{\circ }$$\end{document} multi-view capture ensures each surface point is observed across multiple frames, minimising per-frame FN impact; (2) downstream mesh refinement fills FN-induced holes; and (3) geometric cleanup removes residual artefacts.

To extract the point cloud of the object of interest, we employ segmented masks \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {S} = \{S_j\}_{j=1}^{N_I}$$\end{document} for each image along with the corresponding refined camera poses \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C = \{(R_i, t_i) | i = 1, 2, \ldots , N_I\}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_i$$\end{document} is the rotation matrix and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_i$$\end{document} is the translation vector for the i-th camera pose. Let P denote the complete point cloud of the scene: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P = \{(x_i, y_i, z_i) | i = 1, 2, \ldots , N_P\}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(x_i, y_i, z_i)$$\end{document} are the 3D coordinates of the points, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_P$$\end{document} is the total number of points. To perform point cloud masking, we project the 3D points from the scene point cloud onto the 2D segmented masks \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {S}$$\end{document} using the camera intrinsic matrix K and poses C. For each point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_i$$\end{document}, its projection onto image j is computed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(u, v, w)^\top = K \cdot [R_j | t_j] \cdot P_i$$\end{document}, followed by homogenization to obtain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{ij} = \left( \frac{u}{w}, \frac{v}{w}\right)$$\end{document}. We then identify the points that fall within the segmented masks \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {S}_j$$\end{document} for each image, defining a set of valid points \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_j = \{ P_i \mid p_{ij} \in \mathcal {S}_j \}$$\end{document} across all visible views. The final segmented object point cloud \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {P}$$\end{document} is the intersection of all these valid points across all images: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {P} = \bigcap _{j=1}^{N_I} M_j$$\end{document}. This ensures that only points visible and masked in all views are retained, leading to the final segmented point cloud of the required object, given by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {P} = \{ (x_i, y_i, z_i) \in P \mid \forall j \, (m_{ij} = 1) \}$$\end{document}. Points that do not fall within every mask are considered part of the background and are discarded, efficiently isolating the object of interest.Fig. 4The illustration of the VolE framework output as a 3D reconstruction pipeline, covering each step from SfM to mesh refinement. Mesh reconstruction

Mesh reconstruction41 involves creating a watertight mesh from the segmented point cloud \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {P}'$$\end{document} using OpenMVS Multi-View Stereo (MVS)42 with key parameters: close-holes=50 (for occlusion filling) and a target resolution 0.5mm (for Foodkit/MTF). First, the point cloud \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {P}'$$\end{document} is first transformed into a tetrahedral mesh \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {T}$$\end{document} using the CGAL Delaunay triangulation function41 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{D}$$\end{document}: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {T} = f_{D}(\mathcal {P}')$$\end{document}. Next, a graph-cut optimisation function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{G}$$\end{document} determines whether each tetrahedron is inside or outside the object: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {L} = f_G(\mathcal {T})$$\end{document}. Finally, the marching cubes algorithm41 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{M}$$\end{document} extracts the mesh surface from the labeled tetrahedra: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {M} = \textbf{M}(\mathcal {T}, \mathcal {L})$$\end{document}. The resulting mesh \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {M}$$\end{document} is a seamless and accurate representation of the object’s geometry based on the original point cloud data.

Mesh refinement

Mesh refinement41 applies OpenMVS tools sequentially to improve geometric quality while preserving watertight topology for volume computation: volume is calculated on pre-smoothing mesh \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {M}$$\end{document} using divergence theorem, unaffected by subsequent surface smoothing (<0.5% impact validated by 1.22% Foodkit MAPE). In our framework, mesh refinement plays a crucial role in addressing holes caused by segmentation errors (FN) and removing artefacts (FP) from point cloud masking. This ensures that the reconstructed meshes accurately reflect the object’s geometry while eliminating inaccuracies from previous steps. High-quality meshes are vital for downstream applications like visualisation, simulation, or analysis, where inaccuracies can significantly impact results. While mesh simplification41 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_S$$\end{document} might seem counterintuitive in this context, it helps manage computational resources by reducing the number of vertices \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {M}_{s} = f_{S}(\mathcal {M})$$\end{document}, making subsequent refinement steps more efficient. Following simplification, mesh smoothing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{\bar{S}}$$\end{document} uses Laplacian or bilateral filtering to eliminate noise and outliers \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {M}_{\bar{s}} = f_{\bar{S}}(\mathcal {M}_{s})$$\end{document}, creating a more uniform surface. Mesh denoising \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_D$$\end{document}41 further enhances quality by removing remaining noise through standard voting tensor filtering \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {M}_{d} = f_D (\mathcal {M}_{\bar{s}})$$\end{document}. Finally, mesh optimisation41 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{f}$$\end{document}, such as vertex relaxation and edge flipping, refines triangle quality to produce a clean and precise mesh \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\mathcal {M}} = \hat{f}(\mathcal {M}_{d})$$\end{document}. By integrating these simplification, smoothing, denoising, and optimisation processes, our framework achieves high-quality meshes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\mathcal {M}}$$\end{document} that balance computational efficiency with geometric accuracy.

Volume estimation

The volume estimation of a 3D mesh is based on the divergence theorem, which connects volume integrals to surface integrals. For a closed triangular mesh \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\mathcal {M}}$$\end{document} consisting of N faces, the volume V is computed by summing the signed volumes of tetrahedra formed by each triangle face and the origin: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V = \frac{1}{6} \sum _{k=1}^{N} (v_1^k \cdot (v_2^k \times v_3^k))$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_1^k, v_2^k, v_3^k \in \mathbb {R}^3$$\end{document} are the vertices of the k-th triangle. Each tetrahedron is formed using the vertices of a triangle, along with the origin as the fourth vertex. The volume of each tetrahedron is calculated using the scalar triple product; the cross product (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}) determines the orientation and area of the triangle, while the dot product (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\cdot$$\end{document}) gives the volume of the tetrahedron. The factor of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{1}{6}$$\end{document} comes from the volume formula for tetrahedra. By summing these individual tetrahedron volumes, the total mesh volume is obtained efficiently in a single pass through all triangles, making this method computationally effective for complex 3D meshes.

Evaluation protocol

In order to assess the accuracy of our proposed framework in volume estimation, we employ the Mean Average Percentage Error (MAPE) metric. MAPE is calculated as:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {MAPE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum _{i=1}^n \left| \frac{V_{est,i} - V_{gt,i}}{V_{gt,i}}\right| \times 100\% \, , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_{est,i}$$\end{document} is the estimated volume, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_{gt,i}$$\end{document} is the ground truth volume, and n is the number of objects. For evaluating 3D reconstruction accuracy, we utilise the Chamfer distance metric44, which measures the average distance between the reconstructed and ground truth point cloud sampled from the surface of the mesh. For 3D reconstruction accuracy, we utilise the Chamfer Distance (CD) metric, computing symmetric nearest-neighbour distances between reconstructed and GT point clouds (sampled from meshes) after ICP alignment:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {CD}(P, Q) = \frac{1}{|P|} \sum _{p \in P} \min _{q \in Q} \Vert p - q\Vert _2 + \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum _{q \in Q} \min _{p \in P} \Vert q - p\Vert _2 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where the first term averages the distance from each reconstructed point p to its nearest GT point q, and the second term averages the distance from each GT point q to its nearest reconstructed point p. CD is sensitive to surface detail and alignment; our masking+refinement prioritises watertight volume over per-vertex precision, as validated by leading DTU CD (0.68, Table 6) and superior MAPE.

Datasets

We evaluate our method on the proposed Foodkit dataset, as well as MTF13, and DTU34 datasets. Below, we present a detailed explanation of each dataset

Foodkit: Our proposed dataset

We introduce the foodkit dataset, a new and comprehensive dataset designed for estimating food volume. This dataset was created to address the limitations of existing datasets, which often include only a limited number of scenes, depend on reference information or CAD models, or have fixed capturing environments10. We aimed to develop a dataset that closely resembles free-motion real-world scenarios, allowing for volume estimation using smartphone cameras without needing reference objects. The Foodkit dataset comprises 21 diverse food objects, each captured in isolation (single food item per scene) to enable reliable mask generation and accurate 3D reconstruction without multi-object ambiguity. This design choice reflects our target application of smartphone-based dietary monitoring for individual dishes, carefully selected to encompass a wide range of shapes, sizes, and textures commonly encountered in dietary assessment applications, as shown in Fig. 5. Each food item in the dataset is accompanied by ground truth measurements of volume and mass, providing a robust foundation for validating and benchmarking volume estimation techniques.Fig. 5Foodkit dataset visual examples and their labels.

To overcome the limitations of existing datasets, we developed an innovative approach combining AR technology with traditional 3D reconstruction techniques. The data collection process involved capturing each food object in a 360-degree view using an ARKit-based mobile application. This application captured dense sets of images extracted from the video along with ARKit-estimated 3D coordinates for each frame, providing crucial spatial information, as shown in Fig. 6. Additionally, segmented masks were created for each image to isolate the food object from the background using FoodMem5. A key advantage of using ARKit-based capturing is that it eliminates the need for reference objects to scale the reconstructed model to real-world dimensions. The 3D coordinates provided by ARKit can be used to accurately refine the camera poses and subsequently scale both the camera poses and the output 3D reconstruction to real-world measurements. For each food item, we obtained precise ground truth measurements using the Archimedes water displacement principle with a calibrated 500mL graduated cylinder (±5mL error margin from instrument resolution). MTF dataset

The MTF dataset13 is designed to evaluate food volume estimation techniques across varying levels of complexity. It comprises 20 food scenes strategically categorised into three difficulty tiers: easy, medium, and hard. The easy category encompasses the first eight scenes, each containing approximately 200 images, providing a rich set of multi-view data for each food item. The medium category consists of seven scenes, each offering around 30 images, presenting a more challenging scenario with fewer viewpoints. The hard category, representing the most demanding test cases, includes the remaining scenes, each containing only a single image. Each image in the dataset is accompanied by corresponding food masks and depth images, enhancing the dataset’s utility for various computer vision tasks. The scenes are carefully constructed to include a single food object, a reference board (such as a chessboard), and QR code papers surrounding the object. Although VolE is fundamentally reference-free (using ARCore/ARKit poses for scale), we followed the standard evaluation protocol established by the dataset owners and challenge organizers13, as well as prior state-of-the-art methods including VolETA21. The MTF dataset provides 3D meshes acquired via physical 3D scanning with corresponding images, but accurate registration of the provided camera poses to the scanner coordinate system requires additional calibration beyond the standard evaluation scope. Thus, all competing methods (including ours for fair comparison) use the reference board to scale reconstructions to ground truth dimensions. DTU Dataset

The DTU dataset34 is a comprehensive benchmark for multi-view stereo reconstruction, consisting of 128 scenes captured in controlled laboratory settings. Each scene was scanned from 49 or 64 fixed camera positions under seven lighting conditions, using structured light scanners to generate ground truth point clouds. While not specifically designed for food items, the dataset offers various objects and scenes to evaluate 3D reconstruction methods. The official evaluation uses the Chamfer distance metric to assess point cloud accuracy and completeness. Unlike our Foodkit and MTF evaluations, which utilise FoodMem for automatic segmentation, VolE does not require segmentation masks for the DTU evaluation. We selected the same 15 diverse scenes used in recent studies33, which come with provided ground truth (GT) segmentation masks specifically designed for point cloud masking in our pipeline. This selection ensures methodological consistency across datasets and allows for a fair comparison with state-of-the-art reconstruction methods (COLMAP20, NeuS233, InstantNGP31), which also use these GT masks for object isolation. Observation

Our ablation study reveals that both frame skipping and Hamming distance-based image selection can significantly reduce computational time while maintaining high accuracy. However, the Hamming distance approach demonstrates superior performance, allowing for more intelligent frame selection based on visual content rather than fixed intervals. The optimal configuration depends on the specific requirements of the application. For scenarios demanding the highest accuracy, using all available frames (1005 images) provides the most reliable results. In contrast, applications prioritising speed might opt for a Hamming distance threshold of 10 or 11, which reduces processing time by over 90% while maintaining accuracy above 97%. These findings underscore the flexibility of our framework, enabling users to tailor the image selection process to their specific needs, balancing accuracy and computational efficiency.

Discussion

The experimental results provide strong evidence for the effectiveness of VolE in accurately estimating object volumes across various scenarios. VolE’s ability to handle low-textured objects and unbounded scenes contributes to its superior performance across diverse food items. Leveraging the AR-capable devices to gather the images and camera locations and FoodMem for food video segmentation is a crucial factor in achieving high accuracy, as shown in Fig. 2(a). Frame sampling experiments reveal that our framework maintains robust performance even with fewer input frames, indicating its efficiency in real-world applications. The Hamming distance analysis provides insights into the optimal threshold for feature matching, balancing accuracy and computational cost. Moreover, our framework consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods in both volume estimation accuracy (lower MAPE) and 3D reconstruction quality (lower CD), as shown in Table 5 and 6. The framework’s strong performance across multiple datasets (Foodkit, MTF, DTU) demonstrates its versatility and potential for broad applicability in various domains requiring accurate volume estimation and 3D reconstruction. These findings underscore our framework’s potential as a robust, efficient, and accurate solution for volume estimation and 3D reconstruction tasks, particularly in the context of food volume estimation for dietary assessment applications.

Limitations

The VolE framework, despite its promising results, faces limitations primarily regarding real-time speed, which is crucial for real-time volume estimation applications. Additionally, our current framework assumes a single prominent food item per scene, which enables reliable FoodMem segmentation and point cloud masking but limits applicability to complex meals with multiple dishes. While this aligns with our focus on individual food volume estimation for dietary tracking apps, future extensions will incorporate user-specified object selection (e.g., via text or bounding box) or multi-object segmentation models to handle full plates. We also aim to integrate segmentation confidence metrics with 3D reconstruction, weighting points by confidence scores to reduce FN sensitivity, leveraging advanced segmentation models. Currently, the framework’s runtimes take 8 to 9 minutes on an RTX 3090, exceeding real-time needs for mobile devices. However, its modular design allows for optimisations, such as frame subsampling and mobile GPU/NPU acceleration. Adapting the framework for edge devices, such as mobile phones, will enable broader accessibility and usability, allowing users to leverage real-time volume estimation capabilities directly on their personal devices.

Conclusion

This paper introduces VolE, a reference- and depth-free framework for precise volume estimation of free-motion food scenes using images and camera locations from mobile devices. VolE leverages ARCore/ARKit and SfM to reconstruct accurately scaled 3D point clouds without reference objects or single-purpose hardware. We also present the Foodkit dataset, a benchmark of 21 food objects with ground truth volumes and masses, addressing the lack of high-quality benchmarks in food volume estimation. The experimental results emphasise that our framework shows superior performance, positioning VolE as a state-of-the-art approach for food volume estimation, offering practical solutions for real-world applications. Future work includes extending VolE to multi-object scenes via user-guided selection or advanced multi-instance segmentation of complex food scenarios, integrating with nutritional databases, optimising the speed of camera pose estimation and 3D reconstruction for real-time applications, and developing algorithms to improve the detection and segmentation of transparent and reflective food items.
