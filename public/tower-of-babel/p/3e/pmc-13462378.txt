Sex hormones and their receptors in osteoarthritis: current research progress
Authors: Ziwei Wang, Xueqing Shi, Ge Yang, Kai Zheng, Ren Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoarthritis (OA) is the most prevalent degenerative joint disease worldwide and a leading cause of pain, disability, and reduced quality of life, with pronounced sex-specific differences in incidence and clinical presentation. Epidemiological evidence shows a sharp increase in OA prevalence in women after menopause, whereas in men, the elevated risk at older age is more closely associated with declines in bone mass, muscle strength, and biomechanical stability. Increasing experimental and clinical studies indicate that sex hormones—including estrogen, androgens, and progesterone—and their respective receptors [estrogen receptor (ER), androgen receptor (AR), and progesterone receptor (PR)] play essential roles in maintaining joint homeostasis and modulating OA pathogenesis. At the molecular level, these hormones regulate chondrocyte proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, extracellular matrix (ECM) metabolism, synovial inflammation, and subchondral bone remodeling through diverse genomic and non-genomic signaling pathways. Dysregulation of hormone signaling, particularly estrogen deficiency or receptor imbalance, contributes to cartilage degeneration, aberrant bone remodeling, and inflammatory activation, thereby promoting OA progression. Notably, hormone-related effects appear to be tissue-specific, receptor subtype-dependent, and stage-dependent, underscoring the complexity of endocrine regulation in joint diseases. This review summarizes recent advances in the roles of sex hormones and their receptors in OA, integrating evidence from molecular mechanisms, experimental studies, and clinical research. It aims to provide a comprehensive framework for understanding sex differences in OA and to support the development of sex-specific and mechanism-based therapeutic strategies.Keywords: Osteoarthritis (OA), sex hormones, sex-specific differences, joint homeostasis

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a comprehensive joint disorder characterized by structural alterations in hyaline articular cartilage, subchondral bone, ligaments, joint capsule, synovium and periarticular muscles (1). The pathogenesis of OA involves complex interactions among mechanical, inflammatory, and metabolic factors that ultimately lead to progressive structural damage and functional failure of synovial joints. Rather than a passive “wear-and-tear” condition, OA is now recognized as an active and dynamic process resulting from an imbalance between tissue repair and degradation within the joint (2,3). As one of the most prevalent musculoskeletal disorders worldwide, OA affects more than 300 million individuals and represents a major cause of pain, disability, and reduced quality of life in aging populations (4).

Epidemiological studies consistently demonstrate a pronounced sex difference in OA prevalence and progression. For example, the prevalence of symptomatic knee OA in China is approximately 8.1%, with a substantially higher rate in women (10.3%) than in men (5.7%) (5). This sex disparity has long attracted attention and is widely believed to be associated with hormonal changes, particularly the sharp decline in estrogen levels after menopause (6). Accumulating experimental and clinical evidence suggests that estrogen plays an important role in maintaining cartilage homeostasis, regulating subchondral bone remodeling, and modulating joint inflammation. Consequently, estrogen deficiency has been proposed as a key endocrine factor contributing to the increased susceptibility to OA in women (7).

In addition to estrogen, other sex hormones, including androgens and progesterone, have also been implicated in the regulation of joint homeostasis. Increasing evidence indicates that these hormones influence joint tissues through their respective receptors and downstream signaling pathways. Therefore, elucidating the roles of sex hormones and their receptor-mediated signaling in joint tissues is essential for understanding sex-specific differences in OA and its underlying mechanisms. Such insights may provide a theoretical basis for improved risk assessment and the development of targeted therapeutic strategies for OA.

Pathological changes and the comprehensive role of sex hormones in OA

During the progression of OA, alterations in cartilage composition lead to the loss of structural integrity and increased susceptibility to mechanical stress (8). Initially, damage is limited to superficial erosions, but as the disease advances, deeper fissures develop along with expansion of the calcified cartilage zone (9). Hypertrophic chondrocytes attempt to repair the tissue by increasing synthetic activity. However, this response paradoxically promotes the production of matrix-degrading enzymes and proinflammatory mediators, disrupting chondrocyte homeostasis and inducing synovial inflammation. Activated synoviocytes further exacerbate this process by releasing inflammatory factors, resulting in synovial hyperplasia and increased vascularization (10). At the same time, subchondral bone undergoes increased remodeling, characterized by elevated bone turnover and vascular invasion extending into the cartilage, contributing to bone marrow lesion formation (11). Osteophyte development at joint margins, driven by endochondral ossification, is influenced by both inflammation and abnormal mechanical loading (12). Together, these changes create a mechanically compromised and proinflammatory joint microenvironment that promotes OA progression.

These structural and inflammatory alterations are closely linked to pain development. The joint is highly innervated for proprioception and nociception, and during OA progression, nociceptors become activated and sensitized by biochemical changes in the joint (13). Inflammation elevates nerve growth factor (NGF), which enhances transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) activity via TrkA signaling and promotes peripheral sensitization (14), while also activating p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling in the dorsal root ganglion and increasing pronociceptive mediators (15). In parallel, central sensitization further amplifies pain through increased neuronal excitability and reduced activation thresholds (16). Clinical evidence, including reduced pain thresholds and widespread allodynia, supports the role of central sensitization in OA pain (17-20). Collectively, these findings help explain the discordance between structural damage and pain severity in OA and suggest a modulatory role for sex hormones.

Sex hormones exert broad regulatory effects across multiple tissues and organs, primarily through interactions with their cognate receptors (21). As early as 1926, Cecil et al. first proposed a link between OA and sex hormones (22), observing that obese postmenopausal women were more susceptible to joint pain and stiffness, a condition they termed “postmenopausal arthritis”. This concept has since been widely supported, with accumulating evidence indicating that postmenopausal women not only exhibit a markedly increased prevalence of OA but also experience more severe disease manifestations (23). These findings suggest that the decline in endogenous estrogen levels may represent a critical risk factor for OA development. Consistently, recent studies have identified the expression of sex hormone receptors in key joint-related cells and tissues, including articular chondrocytes, synovium, and osteocytes—sites central to OA pathology (24-28). Together, these observations underscore a close association between sex hormones and OA pathogenesis.

The role of sex hormones in OA has therefore attracted increasing attention. Ettinger et al. demonstrated that estrogen exerts protective effects on bone through multiple mechanisms (29), while androgens have been shown to suppress bone resorption and promote bone formation (30). Experimental studies further indicate that orchiectomy induces cartilage degeneration, whereas androgen replacement mitigates this effect (31); similarly, ovariectomy leads to cartilage deterioration, which can be reversed by both estrogen and androgen supplementation (32). At the cellular level, estrogen deficiency has been linked to osteocyte apoptosis (33), potentially impairing mechanosensation and microdamage repair, thereby contributing to aberrant subchondral bone remodeling in OA. In addition, estrogen promotes angiogenesis and protects against vascular injury, facilitating subchondral bone repair (34). Collectively, these findings highlight that sex hormones regulate cartilage and bone metabolism through complex and interconnected mechanisms, with both shared and distinct effects, which will be further discussed in the following sections (Figure 1).

Estrogen and OA

The relationship between estrogen and OA

Estrogen comprises a group of steroid hormones predominantly produced by the ovaries and serves as a key regulator of physiological processes across multiple tissues and organ systems (35). Beyond its indispensable role in the development and maintenance of the female reproductive system, estrogen exerts profound effects on musculoskeletal metabolism and homeostasis (36-38). Four endogenous estrogens have been identified in humans—estrone (E1), estradiol (E2; 17β-estradiol), estriol (E3), and estetrol (E4) (39,40). Among these, E1, E2, and E3 represent the principal circulating forms, with E2 exhibiting the greatest biological potency, whereas E4 is predominantly detected during pregnancy and is synthesized by the fetal liver (40). Accumulating experimental and clinical evidence demonstrates that estrogen is essential for skeletal integrity in both sexes, as it promotes osteoblast differentiation and activity, enhances bone matrix synthesis and mineralization, and thereby maintains bone mass homeostasis while limiting excessive bone resorption. Accordingly, declines in estrogen levels associated with aging or endocrine dysregulation are widely recognized as critical contributors to bone loss and impaired skeletal health (41,42).

Beyond its role in bone, estrogen is increasingly recognized as a critical regulator of metabolic homeostasis within joint tissues, including articular cartilage, synovium, and subchondral bone (43-45). Estrogen signaling modulates chondrocyte metabolism, influencing both extracellular matrix (ECM) synthesis and inflammatory responses; for instance, it can stimulate prostaglandin production while, under certain conditions, suppressing proteoglycan synthesis (46). In animal models, ovariectomized (OVX) animals consistently exhibit exacerbated joint degeneration compared with controls, characterized by cartilage erosion, fissure formation, and pronounced alterations in subchondral bone architecture (47-50). In post-traumatic OA models, OVX further aggravates cartilage destruction and joint pathology (51), while also inducing dysregulated bone remodeling, as evidenced by increased expression of early osteogenic markers such as type I collagen and Runx2 (50). At the matrix level, glycosaminoglycans—key components of the cartilage ECM—are essential for maintaining biomechanical integrity, and their synthesis is enhanced by E2 in chondrocytes (52). Moreover, in bovine articular chondrocytes, E2 suppresses cyclooxygenase-2 expression, thereby mitigating oxidative stress–induced cellular injury (53). Given that articular cartilage is avascular and relies on synovial fluid for nutrient exchange, the hormonal composition of synovial fluid is particularly important. Notably, estrogen levels in synovial fluid correlate positively with circulating levels; thus, systemic estrogen deficiency leads to reduced intra-articular estrogen availability, weakening its inhibitory effects on proinflammatory and catabolic mediators such as interleukin (IL)-1β and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), thereby accelerating cartilage degeneration (54,55). Nevertheless, the protective role of estrogen in OA remains context-dependent. While estrogen supplementation attenuates cartilage damage in some OVX models by reducing excessive matrix turnover (47,48), other studies report persistent degeneration despite estrogen administration (56,57). Collectively, these findings highlight the complex, multifaceted, and context-specific actions of estrogen in joint homeostasis, underscoring the need for further investigation into the differential roles of individual estrogen subtypes and their receptor-mediated signaling pathways.

The relationship between estrogen receptors (ERs) and OA

The classical estrogen signaling pathway is initiated by the passive diffusion of estrogen across the cell membrane, facilitated by its lipophilic nature, followed by binding to intracellular ERs and activation of target gene transcription (58,59). The principal ER subtypes include ERα, ERβ, and the G protein-coupled ER (GPER) (60). These receptors are widely expressed in joint tissues, including articular cartilage, synovium, subchondral bone, and ligaments (43,44). ERα and ERβ are primarily localized in articular chondrocytes and synovial fibroblast-like cells (61), whereas GPER, a membrane-associated receptor, mediates rapid non-genomic signaling and modulates intracellular signaling cascades (62). Through these receptor subtypes, estrogen regulates chondrocyte proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis; promotes the synthesis of ECM components such as type II collagen and proteoglycans; and suppresses matrix-degrading enzymes, including matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs (ADAMTS), thereby maintaining ECM homeostasis and attenuating cartilage degeneration (63-66).

Disruption of estrogen signaling is increasingly recognized as a critical endocrine factor in OA pathogenesis. Epidemiological and clinical studies consistently report increased incidence and severity of OA in postmenopausal women, implicating estrogen deficiency in accelerated joint degeneration (6). Loss of estrogen signaling weakens ER-mediated anti-inflammatory and anti-catabolic effects, leading to elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α, heightened chondrocyte sensitivity to mechanical and inflammatory stimuli, and subsequent cartilage breakdown and abnormal subchondral bone remodeling (67,68). Interestingly, increased ER expression in the cartilage of small joints in postmenopausal women has been associated with greater severity of facet joint OA (FJOA) (69). Moreover, distinct ER subtypes may exert differential or even opposing roles: ERα, highly expressed in bone-related tissues, is mainly involved in regulating cartilage metabolism and inflammatory responses, whereas ERβ appears to exert protective effects by maintaining joint homeostasis and limiting degeneration (70,71). GPER-mediated rapid signaling contributes to chondrocyte survival and modulation of the inflammatory microenvironment (62,72). Collectively, subtype-specific estrogen signaling likely underlies sex-related differences and inter-individual heterogeneity in OA progression.

Molecular mechanisms of estrogen in alleviating OA

Although numerous animal studies and clinical investigations have demonstrated that estrogen supplementation can, to some extent, ameliorate OA, the precise molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying the protective effects of estrogen in OA remain largely unclear. Current evidence indicates that estrogen regulates chondrocyte survival, inflammatory responses, and cartilage homeostasis within the joint microenvironment through the activation of multiple downstream signaling pathways. Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) signaling pathway

The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is a central regulator of cellular survival, proliferation, and apoptosis, acting through multiple downstream effectors, including caspase-3, glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β), nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), and the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) (73-76). Accumulating evidence suggests that E2 attenuates OA-associated chondrocyte apoptosis, promotes chondrocyte proliferation, and increases the expression and phosphorylation of AKT (77). Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of phosphorylated- (p-)AKT partially abolishes these effects, supporting a causal role for the PI3K/AKT pathway in mediating the anti-apoptotic and pro-proliferative actions of E2 in OA chondrocytes (77). In addition, estrogen may modulate the PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis by suppressing IL-1β signaling, downregulating acid-sensing ion channel 1a (ASIC1a) expression, and reducing autophagic activity, thereby lowering inflammatory mediator levels and conferring further protection against articular cartilage degeneration (45).

ERα-mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase (MEK)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling pathway

Recent evidence indicates that E2 suppresses NGF expression in chondrocytes from rat knee articular cartilage as well as in transplanted cartilage tissue. This effect was abolished by the selective ERα antagonist methylpiperidinopyrazole (MPP), indicating that estrogen downregulates NGF expression through an ERα-dependent mechanism. Furthermore, estrogen attenuated transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1)- or IL-1β-induced NGF expression in rat chondrocytes, whereas inhibition of the MEK-ERK pathway with PD98059 led to increased NGF expression. These findings suggest that the regulatory effects of E2 on NGF expression may involve modulation of the MEK-ERK signaling pathway in non-hematopoietic fibroblast-like cells (78).

AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)/mTOR signaling pathway

A recent study reported that E2 treatment significantly upregulated mitophagy-associated proteins, including sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) and p-AMPK, while markedly downregulating p-mTOR in rat chondrocytes (79). Consistently, mitochondrial autophagosome formation was increased, accompanied by activation of the AMPK/mTOR signaling axis. Collectively, these findings suggest that E2 may enhance mitophagy through a SIRT1-dependent AMPK/mTOR pathway, thereby maintaining chondrocyte homeostasis and protecting against degenerative stress (79).

NF-κB signaling pathway

NF-κB signaling pathway plays a central role in mediating inflammatory responses during the development of OA. In osteoarthritic synoviocytes, estrogen has been shown to suppress NF-κB activation through interaction with ERβ, leading to reduced expression of inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and decreased phosphorylation of IκBα, thereby attenuating synovial inflammation and contributing to joint protection (80). Mechanistically, ERβ signaling can inhibit NF-κB activity by increasing the cellular levels of the inhibitory protein IκBα and preventing the binding of the NF-κB p65 subunit to the promoters of pro-inflammatory genes, ultimately suppressing inflammatory gene transcription (81). Collectively, these findings suggest that estrogen-ERβ signaling negatively regulates NF-κB-mediated inflammatory responses and may represent an important molecular mechanism through which estrogen mitigates inflammatory processes associated with OA.

Notch signaling pathway

The Notch signaling pathway is involved in regulating the differentiation and maturation. Notch1 and Notch2 are preferentially expressed in the deep and middle zones of articular cartilage, respectively, and numerous Notch signaling components have been detected in damaged cartilage, suggesting a close association between Notch activity and cartilage repair processes (82). Using a rat model of estrogen deficiency–induced OA, ovariectomy-mediated estrogen depletion was observed to significantly reduce Notch2 expression in the cartilage tissue of OA rats compared with control animals. These findings indicate that estrogen deficiency can downregulate Notch signaling in osteoarthritic cartilage. Accordingly, estrogen may participate in the maintenance of cartilage metabolic homeostasis by sustaining normal Notch pathway activity, whereas its deficiency may accelerate the onset and progression of OA through suppression of Notch signaling (83).

GPER-YAP-RhoA/Piezo1 signaling pathway

Studies investigating ER-mediated responses to mechanical stress in chondrocytes have shown that GPER suppresses the activation of the RhoA/LIMK/cofilin pathway, actin polymerization, and Piezo1 by promoting the expression of YAP and ARH-GAP29 as well as facilitating YAP nuclear localization. Through these mechanisms, GPER reduces chondrocyte apoptosis and modulates the progression of OA (62). This signaling pathway is distinct from those mediated by the aforementioned cytokines and related interventions, providing effective support for the mechanical stress–based theory of OA pathogenesis and offering a potential mechanistic basis for future studies on OA progression and therapeutic strategies.

These signaling pathways collectively correspond to distinct OA phenotypes, including inflammatory OA (e.g., NF-κB, ERα-MEK-ERK, and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways), metabolic OA (e.g., AMPK/mTOR and Notch signaling pathways), and mechanically driven OA associated with mechanotransduction pathways (e.g., GPER-YAP-RhoA/Piezo1 signaling pathways). Together, they highlight a complex and interconnected regulatory network through which estrogen and its receptors modulate joint homeostasis and influence the initiation and progression of OA.

Other sex hormones and OA

The relationship between androgens and their receptors and OA

Androgen biology and androgen receptor (AR) signaling

Androgens are a class of steroid hormones that exert their effects by activating the AR. Endogenous androgens are predominantly testosterone. In plasma, most circulating testosterone is bound to sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) (84). Bound and free testosterone exist in a dynamic equilibrium; only the free fraction can enter cells to produce biological effects. Within target tissues, testosterone can be converted by the enzyme 5α-reductase into the more potent dihydrotestosterone (DHT) or by aromatase into estrogens. Furthermore, dehydroepiandrosterone and androstenedione, primarily derived from the adrenal glands, serve as important precursor hormones. These androgens are important signaling molecules in the human body, exerting regulatory effects on a variety of tissues, including adipose tissue, muscle, liver, brain, bone, and cartilage.

Experimental evidence for androgen signaling in joint tissues

ARs are widely expressed in bone cells, including osteoclasts, osteoblasts, and their precursor cells. Androgens, by binding to either ARs or ERs, help maintain bone density and muscle strength, and influence the homeostasis of bone calcium metabolism (85,86). Research indicates that testosterone delays the deterioration of bone microstructure through osteoblast AR-mediated upregulation of Tenascin-C (87). Furthermore, targeted activation of AR signaling in the periosteum can enhance bone fracture repair (88). Experimental models indicate that both genetic ablation of AR and castration lead to a significant reduction in cortical bone area, thickness, and periosteal bone formation rate (89). Beyond their roles in bone tissue, androgens and their receptors may also influence chondrocyte phenotype. Liu et al. further showed that lentiviral overexpression of AR in rabbit articular chondrocytes reduced apoptosis and enhanced the expression of SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX-9), type II collagen, and aggrecan, accompanied by decreased expression of MMP-13 (27). Research published in 2025 revealed that DHT regulates chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation through the growth hormone and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) axis in temporomandibular joint (TMJ) OA, and its deficiency leads to a reduction in chondrocyte numbers and a decline in cartilage repair capacity (90). These findings provide direct evidence for the chondroprotective role of androgens and AR (27). The homeostasis of joint tissue is closely associated with inflammation and the balance between the catabolism and anabolism of the ECM. Previous research employing a kinetic computational model has elucidated the complex regulation of sex hormones on post-traumatic joint inflammation and cartilage degradation. The model revealed that elevated testosterone levels in males can significantly suppress the expression of IL-1β, TNF-α, and MMP-3, while promoting the production of IL-10, demonstrating a clear anti-inflammatory and cartilage-protective effect (91). Collectively, androgens and AR regulate joint tissue homeostasis by influencing bone and cartilage phenotypes, as well as inflammatory responses and metabolic processes.

Clinical and epidemiological evidence

Accumulating evidence indicates that androgens and AR are crucial regulatory factors in maintaining joint health, and their alterations or loss may influence disease progression. A longitudinal study published by Jin et al. in 2017 indicated that among female OA patients, testosterone levels were inversely correlated with knee pain and the volume of effusion-synovitis, suggesting that endogenous testosterone serves as an independent protective factor in suppressing synovial inflammation and alleviating joint pain (92). However, a Mendelian randomization study published in 2021 demonstrated a significant joint-specific nature in the effects of androgens on OA, indicating that they might be a clear pathogenic risk factor for hip OA, while their impact on knee OA remains inconclusive (93). This challenges the traditional view of androgens having a simplistic “protective” role and suggests a far more complex underlying mechanism. In addition, new insights were provided by a large-scale prospective cohort analysis based on the Rotterdam Study in 2025. This study found that in women, higher serum free testosterone levels were significantly associated with a reduced risk of incident chronic knee pain, and both total and free testosterone were linked to a lower prevalence of radiographic knee OA. Whereas, an opposite trend was observed in men: higher testosterone levels were associated with more severe radiographic hand OA, but no significant association was found with knee outcomes (94). These results underscore the distinct sexual dimorphism and joint-specific nature of testosterone’s impact on OA, highlighting the urgent need to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms. A recent prospective study leveraging the UK Biobank large cohort with over 13 years of follow-up further revealed that, among middle-aged and older women, excluding the influence of exogenous hormones, higher baseline serum testosterone levels were independently associated with a lower risk of knee OA prevalence and a significantly delayed time to knee OA onset. Nevertheless, this study did not observe significant associations between E2 or SHBG and knee OA risk. Notably, when all three hormones were included simultaneously in the analytical model, the protective association of testosterone disappeared. This suggests that the influence of endogenous sex hormones on OA likely stems from complex network regulation rather than the isolated action of any single hormone (86).

The relationship between progesterone and its receptors and OA

Progesterone, a steroid hormone primarily produced by the ovarian corpus luteum, placenta, and adrenal cortex, mediates its physiological functions through activation of specific intracellular progesterone receptors (PRs). The two principal isoforms of this receptor are PR-A and PR-B (95). Previous studies have demonstrated PR expression in cultured osteoblasts and fibrochondrocytes from the TMJ disc, knee meniscus, and pubic symphysis (96,97). The use of PR-Cre reporter mice further confirmed PR presence in the subchondral bone region and growth plate chondrocytes. Genetic models, including both global and osteocyte-specific PR knockout mice, exhibit a high bone mass phenotype, with notable sexual dimorphism observed in this effect (98). Dai et al. further discovered that PR deficiency in osteochondral progenitor cells leads to a marked phenotype of subchondral bone sclerosis, whereas its deletion in mature chondrocytes has minimal impact. More importantly, PR knockout within the chondrocyte lineage accelerates chondrocyte senescence and upregulates the expression of the matrix-degrading enzyme MMP-13 (99). Collectively, these findings suggest that PR plays a crucial role in maintaining joint homeostasis by regulating progenitor cell fate and suppressing chondrocyte senescence, and its dysfunction may represent an early event in OA pathogenesis. Additionally, conditional knockout of PR in osteochondral progenitor cells has been shown to significantly impair the activity of multiple immunomodulatory pathways implicated in the pathogenesis of OA (100).

In addition to the actions mediated by its receptor, progesterone itself is increasingly recognized as a modulator of bone and joint tissue homeostasis with potential therapeutic implications for OA. Studies indicate that fluctuations in estrogen and progesterone levels during menopause play a pivotal role in musculoskeletal pain and joint stiffness, and increase the risk of developing conditions such as OA (101). In a chemically induced mouse model of menopause, the postmenopausal deficiency of E2 and progesterone increases susceptibility to aging and ECM degradation. In vivo experiments have demonstrated that restoring both E2 and progesterone in these menopausal mice protects cartilage from degeneration compared to untreated control groups (7). In a longitudinal clinical study of patients with symptomatic knee OA, Jin et al. found that in female patients, higher serum progesterone levels were independently associated with greater tibial cartilage volume and less effusion-synovitis. This finding indicates that endogenous progesterone may not only contribute to maintaining cartilage integrity but also influence the OA process by suppressing intra-articular inflammation, and this effect exhibits distinct sexual dimorphism (92). Furthermore, a 2025 study identified three genes, IRAK3, ITGBL1, and RHOU, as key targets for OA through bioinformatics analysis. Subsequent molecular docking analysis demonstrated that progesterone exhibits strong binding affinity to all three target proteins. Cellular assays further verified that progesterone exerts chondro-protective effects under inflammatory conditions. Via Mendelian randomization analysis, this study revealed a significant negative correlation between higher circulating progesterone levels and multiple OA phenotypes, uncovering the genetic-level association between progesterone and all OA subtypes and further supporting its potential role in OA treatment (102).

Estrogen replacement therapy (ERT)

ERT serves as a therapeutic intervention designed to mitigate health issues associated with estrogen deficiency by administering exogenous estrogen (110). Its core goal is to compensate for the deficiency of endogenous estrogen and maintain the homeostasis of physiological functions. ERT has emerged as a significant area of investigation for the treatment of OA in postmenopausal women, owing to estrogen’s joint-protective mechanisms, while also needing to take into account the principles of safety and individualized application (111). The main targets for ERT are postmenopausal women with OA, especially those with menopausal symptoms such as hot flashes and osteoporosis.

The therapeutic efficacy of ERT for OA in postmenopausal women is mainly reflected in two aspects, including symptom alleviation and structural preservation. ERT may alleviate joint pain to a certain extent, as evidenced by a reduction in pain scores in some patients. It may also decrease stiffness, enhance joint mobility to some degree, and improve overall quality of life for patients (103,112). In addition, the long-term and standardized administration of ERT may help decelerate the pathological progression of OA in selected cases, mitigate articular cartilage degradation, and reduce the rate of joint space narrowing to a certain extent (104,113). This protective effect on cartilage may be more pronounced in women who are in the early postmenopausal phase, but its clinical significance needs to be further verified by large-sample, long-term follow-up studies. The mechanism underlying its efficacy is closely associated with estrogen’s role in regulating cartilage, synovial, and subchondral bone homeostasis, thereby potentially targeting and interrupting the core pathological processes of OA.

The clinical application of ERT warrants careful consideration of several key factors, with safety evaluation being a top priority. Firstly, ERT is not recommended as a first-line routine treatment for OA in postmenopausal women. It should be considered only when treatments such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and cartilage protectants demonstrate limited efficacy, or when distinct menopausal symptoms are present. Before initiating ERT, a comprehensive safety assessment is required, including evaluations of the patient’s cardiovascular status (such as blood pressure, lipid levels, and history of thrombosis), breast health (screening for breast cancer), endometrial condition (to rule out endometrial hyperplasia or cancer), and liver and kidney function, as these are key indicators for determining the suitability of ERT administration. Secondly, transdermal administration is preferred to mitigate the risk of systemic side effects. Furthermore, an individualized treatment plan should be developed based on factors such as the patient’s age, duration since menopause, estrogen levels, genetic polymorphisms (e.g., ERα, ERβ genotype), and comorbidities, in order to avoid indiscriminate use of medication (114). Lastly, incorporating non-pharmacological interventions, such as weight management and quadriceps strengthening exercises, can enhance the therapeutic effects on OA and reduce joint stress. In conclusion, ERT represents one of the personalized treatment options for OA in postmenopausal women. Its potential value lies in its dual role of symptom improvement and structural protection, but the efficacy is individual-dependent. However, it is crucial to carefully select the appropriate patient population, administration method, and safety monitoring protocols to optimize efficacy while minimizing potential risks.

Androgen replacement therapy (ART)

ART is a therapeutic approach that involves the administration of exogenous androgens, such as testosterone and DHT, to compensate for insufficient endogenous androgen levels (115). This therapy aims to maintain the homeostasis of the musculoskeletal system and regulate metabolic functions (116). Within the context of OA, ART primarily focuses on mitigating the onset and progression of OA associated with androgen deficiency in middle-aged and elderly males (117). By directly influencing cartilage-bone metabolism and indirectly enhancing muscle function, ART may serve as a potential represents a significant avenue for the individualized treatment of male OA. However, its clinical application necessitates a careful balance between efficacy and safety.

ART may directly modulate the homeostasis of cartilage by promoting chondrocyte proliferation and the synthesis of type II collagen and proteoglycans, inhibiting chondrocyte apoptosis and the expression of MMPs, and mitigating cartilage degradation (105). Furthermore, it down-regulates the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β in synovial tissue, thereby potentially reducing synovial inflammation and alleviating joint pain and swelling (118). Additionally, ART may indirectly diminish joint load by significantly enhancing the strength of skeletal muscles, such as the distal biceps, with clinical studies indicating a potential increase in muscle strength in some patients, though the reported 200% increase is an outlier and not representative of the general population (106,119). This potential improvement in muscle strength may contribute to enhanced joint stability and reduce stress-induced damage to articular cartilage during physical activity in select cases. In addition, ART may positively influence bone metabolism, maintain subchondral bone density, prevent abnormal cartilage support due to bone loss, and delay the structural progression of OA in patients with androgen deficiency (120). Importantly, ART carries notable safety concerns. long-term administration may increase the risk of cardiovascular events (e.g., hypertension, myocardial infarction), promote the progression of benign prostatic hyperplasia and prostate cancer in high-risk populations, and cause adverse effects such as acne, hair loss, and fluid retention. Regular monitoring of testosterone levels, prostate-specific antigen (PSA), and cardiovascular parameters is therefore essential during ART.

Small-scale prospective cohort studies have shown that a 12-month regimen of ART may reduce the VAS score for joint pain in some patients with OA, slightly enhance joint function scores, such as the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) scores, and improve overall quality of life in a subset of participants, but no consistent efficacy has been demonstrated across all studies (121,122). When ART is administered in conjunction with NSAIDs, it may modestly augment analgesic effects and allow for a slight reduction in NSAID dosages (123). Currently, the majority of clinical investigations into the application of ART for OA are characterized by small sample sizes and short- to medium-term follow-up periods, limiting the generalizability of their findings. The long-term safety profile, particularly beyond 5 years, requires validation through studies with larger sample sizes, and long-term use without clear indications is not advisable. In addition to the aforementioned cardiovascular and prostate-related risks, long-term ART may also disrupt lipid metabolism and increase the risk of insulin resistance. In conclusion, ART represents a conditional personalized therapeutic strategy for androgen deficiency-related OA in middle-aged and elderly males. Its potential benefit lies in its ability to balance cartilage protection with the enhancement of muscle function in select patients, but this must be weighed against the associated safety risks through careful patient selection and regular monitoring.

Selective ER modulators (SERMs) and selective AR modulators (SARMs)

SERMs and SARMs represent two distinct classes of hormone-related pharmacological agents characterized by their tissue-specific targeting capabilities (124,125). These compounds exhibit a high affinity for binding to sex hormone receptors, specifically the ER and AR, which may confer protective effects on target tissues such as joints and bones. Importantly, they may mitigate the adverse effects typically associated with conventional hormone therapies, such as ERT and ART, on non-target tissues, including the breast, uterus, and prostate. Consequently, SERMs and SARMs have emerged as pivotal areas of investigation in the personalized therapeutic management of OA, with ongoing research to clarify their efficacy and safety boundaries.

SERMs constitute a class of non-steroidal compounds that exhibit the ability to selectively bind to ERs, including ERα, ERβ, and GPR30, across various tissues (126). This binding induces conformational changes that modulate downstream signaling pathways. In specific tissues such as joints and bones, SERMs may exhibit estrogen-like agonistic effects, which include potential protection of cartilage, inhibition of inflammation, and regulation of bone metabolism, though the magnitude of these effects varies among individuals and subtypes of SERMs (127). Conversely, in tissues such as the breast and uterus, they exert estrogen-like antagonistic effects, thereby potentially preventing hormone-dependent tissue hyperplasia. This dual action aims to maximize potential therapeutic benefits while minimizing systemic risks, but safety concerns such as thromboembolic events, hot flashes, and gastrointestinal discomfort have been reported in clinical use, especially with long-term administration. In the context of OA, SERMs preferentially bind to ERβ, which is abundantly expressed in articular cartilage. This binding may promote the synthesis of type II collagen and proteoglycans, inhibit the release of MMPs and pro-inflammatory cytokines, and consequently delay cartilage degradation in some patients (128). Furthermore, SERMs may regulate the balance between osteoblasts and osteoclasts in subchondral bone, thereby potentially reducing osteophyte formation and bone loss, and maintaining joint stability (129). Additionally, they may inhibit the infiltration of inflammatory cells into the synovium, thereby alleviating joint swelling and pain in a subset of patients. The appropriate population comprises postmenopausal women diagnosed with OA, particularly those who also have osteoporosis, exhibit intolerance to ERT, or have contraindications to ERT. Priority should be given to patients demonstrating high expression of ERβ or possessing a favorable genotype of the ERβ gene, such as the rs1256049 genotype (130), and regular monitoring of breast, uterine, and lipid profiles is recommended during treatment to assess safety.

Currently, SERMs that have been implemented in clinical practice include Raloxifene and Bazedoxifene. Raloxifene is distinguished by its high affinity for ERβ and its pronounced targeting of bone and joint tissues (131). Several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have demonstrated that Raloxifene may moderately improve joint pain scores in postmenopausal women with OA and achieve a modest reduction in pain scores (107,132). However, Raloxifene is associated with an increased risk of venous thromboembolism, hot flashes, and leg cramps, and it is contraindicated in patients with a history of thromboembolic disease. In contrast, Bazedoxifene is noted for its balanced binding affinity to both ERα and ERβ, exhibiting potential anti-inflammatory and cartilage-protective properties (108). A clinical trial revealed that bazedoxifene may retard the structural progression of hand OA in some postmenopausal women (133). Its safety profile is generally comparable to that of a placebo, with common adverse events including mild gastrointestinal discomfort and headache, and it has been granted the “Fast Track” designation by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). In addition, emerging SERMs, such as lasofoxifene, are currently under investigation as novel, highly selective ERβ agonists that may demonstrate superior joint targeting capabilities compared to earlier compounds (134). Several of these agents have progressed to phase II clinical trials, with preliminary findings indicating potential efficacy in ameliorating OA symptoms and decelerating cartilage degeneration in selected patient groups, though their long-term safety and consistent efficacy remain to be confirmed (135).

SARMs represent a class of synthetic non-steroidal compounds that exhibit selective binding affinity to AR (136). These compounds may exert androgen-like agonistic effects specifically in target tissues such as muscles, bones, and joints, while demonstrating minimal activity in tissues such as the prostate and liver. This selective action is intended to mitigate the adverse effects commonly associated with traditional ART, including prostate hyperplasia, liver function impairment, and cardiovascular risks, but emerging evidence suggests potential safety concerns such as dyslipidemia, acne, and alterations in liver enzymes with some SARMs. SARMs are particularly suitable for elderly male patients with OA, especially those presenting with low serum testosterone levels and concurrent reduced muscle mass or quadriceps atrophy. Additionally, SARMs offer a potential therapeutic alternative for patients who are either intolerant to ART or have contraindications to ART, such as prostate hyperplasia. Notable examples of SARMs include MK-0773, enzalutamide analogs, and RAD140, many of which are currently undergoing phase II clinical trials (109,137,138). Preliminary clinical data indicate potential benefits in improving joint function and reducing pain, but their long-term safety, including effects on cardiovascular health and prostate function, requires further investigation before widespread clinical application.

Others

Innovative pharmaceutical formulations and targeted delivery strategies are transforming disease treatment paradigms, shifting from broad-spectrum systemic administration to precision-guided targeted therapies (139). The chondrocyte membrane biomimetic strategy and CD44 targeting system facilitate targeted delivery to cartilage (140,141). And the macrophage targeting strategy enables precise therapy for synovial inflammatory lesions (142). Additionally, the surface modification of engineered extracellular vesicles with targeting peptides allows for targeted enrichment in the subchondral bone (143). Integrating these targeting strategies with novel formulations of sex hormones can enhance the precision treatment of OA, offering improved efficacy, reduced toxicity, and more convenient administration.

Conclusions

Overall, research to date has yielded increasingly systematic and substantive insights into the roles of sex hormones and their receptors in the initiation and progression of OA, highlighting their pivotal involvement in maintaining joint tissue homeostasis and driving pathological remodeling. Despite these advances, significant research gaps persist in the field of sex hormones and their receptors in OA treatment. For estrogen and ERs, the inconsistent therapeutic effects of ERT across different OA stages and patient populations remain unresolved, and the distinct roles of ERα, ERβ, and GPER in regulating joint inflammation and cartilage metabolism in different OA subtypes (e.g., hip OA, knee OA, and hand OA) have not been fully elucidated. Additionally, the long-term safety of estrogen-based therapies, particularly the risk of cardiovascular events and hormone-dependent tumors, requires further long-term cohort studies to clarify. For androgens and ARs, the joint-specific and sex-dimorphic effects of androgens on OA (e.g., protective effects on knee OA in women vs. potential pathogenic effects on hand OA in men) lack clear molecular explanations, and the optimal dosage and administration route of ART for OA patients with androgen deficiency remain to be standardized. For progesterone and PRs, research lags far behind that of estrogen and androgens, the specific molecular mechanisms by which progesterone and PRs regulate chondrocyte senescence and subchondral bone remodeling are not fully understood, and there is a lack of large-scale clinical trials to verify the therapeutic efficacy and safety of progesterone-based interventions in OA patients. Moreover, the crosstalk between estrogen, androgen, and progesterone signaling pathways in OA pathogenesis, and how their mutual regulation affects joint homeostasis, remains a key research gap that needs to be addressed.

Looking forward, the future development direction of this field should focus on several key aspects. First, basic research should prioritize clarifying the subtype-specific and context-dependent mechanisms of ER, AR, and PR in OA, particularly exploring the non-genomic signaling pathways of these receptors and their interaction with mechanical stress and inflammatory microenvironment. Second, all sex hormone-related drugs used for the treatment of OA are still in the experimental stage so far. Clinical research should focus on conducting large-sample, long-term RCTs to evaluate the efficacy and safety of hormone-based therapies (including ERT, ART, SERMs, SARMs, and progesterone derivatives) in different OA subtypes and patient groups, and establish personalized treatment criteria based on sex, age, specific joints, hormone levels, and receptor expression profiles. Third, the development of novel targeted drugs, such as highly selective ERβ agonists, tissue-specific AR modulators, and PR-targeted small-molecule inhibitors, should be accelerated to improve therapeutic efficacy while reducing off-target side effects. Fourth, integrating multi-omics technologies (genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics) to identify potential biomarkers for predicting the response to hormone-based therapies in OA patients, enabling precise patient stratification and treatment guidance. Finally, further research on progesterone and PR is urgently required to clarify their potential role in OA pathogenesis and therapy, complementing the current focus on estrogen and androgens, and providing a more comprehensive hormonal therapeutic strategy for OA.
