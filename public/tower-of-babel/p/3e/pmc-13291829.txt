The upper airway in obstructive sleep apnoea: state of the art
Authors: Johan Verbraecken, Sara Op de Beeck, Alan R. Schwartz, Olivier M. Vanderveken
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The upper airway is a complex structure, prone to repetitive collapse in obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA). This review discusses the pathophysiological aspects of OSA, with a focus on the anatomy and pathophysiology of upper airway obstruction. This disorder is characterised by different mechanisms that constitute its pathology, which may all contribute to apnoea presence and severity. These include pharyngeal anatomy, collapsibility, upper airway muscle responsiveness, loop gain and arousal threshold. Identification of interindividual differences in the underlying mechanisms of OSA offers promise for a more targeted approach to OSA management.

Introduction

In the past decades, our understanding of the mechanisms leading to obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) has been steadily improved, with most studies being focused on upper airway patency and respiratory control mechanisms during sleep. An unstable breathing pattern can be associated with an increase in upper airway resistance, increased collapsibility of the upper airway and discoordination of local reflex mechanisms and can finally result into OSA [1]. The development of OSA may be determined by anatomical and nonanatomical characteristics of the upper airway and by the synchrony between upper airway muscles and respiratory muscles [1, 2]. In this review we discuss upper airway anatomy and the pathophysiological traits leading to OSA.

Upper airway structure and function in healthy subjects

The upper airway is a multipurpose anatomical region, transmitting air, liquid and solids, serving respiration, vocalisation and deglutition [3–6]. Structures surrounding the pharyngeal airway valve shape the airway during phonation and close the airway during deglutition [7, 8]. In normal conditions, the pharynx always remains open, except during speech, swallowing, regurgitations and eructation. During wakefulness, upper airway patency is secured by neuromuscular control mechanisms in the higher nervous system, coordinating the need for a patent airway with the need for phonation and swallowing in close contact with the respiratory pump [1]. Changes in upper airway geometry occur between upright and supine position during tidal nasal breathing, with a decrease in cross-sectional area of the region between the hard palate and the bottom of the uvula [9]. Airway resistance also rises more in men than in women [9]. The sleep state is characterised by a lack of conscious control and absence of the wakefulness drive, leading to a decrease in muscle tone.

The upper airway can be separated into different anatomic subsegments that can contribute to collapse in OSA subjects, including the palate, oropharynx and hypopharynx, including the lateral pharyngeal walls, tongue base and epiglottis [3–6, 10–14] (table 1).

During each inspiration, the diaphragm generates a negative intraluminal pressure in the upper airway that could reduce the upper airway diameter, depending on the compliance of the airway walls and opposing dilating forces. Hence, during each inspiration, the diaphragmatically generated negative pressure tends to reduce upper airway size [15]. During wakefulness, pharyngeal patency is preserved by pharyngeal dilator muscle activation, which counteracts the collapsing forces of the upper airway [16]. Negative intraluminal airway pressure is the most important local stimulus to activate the genioglossus muscle [14]. The phasic contraction of the pharyngeal dilator muscles is co-ordinated with inspiration and precedes contraction of the diaphragm by milliseconds [17]. Deformation of the human upper airway during breathing can be complex and is usually associated with heterogeneous changes in airway cross-section along the length of the conduit, due to different patterns of airway soft tissue movement during the respiratory cycle [18–20]. The average change in airway cross-sectional area at the soft palate region during tidal breathing is considerable and close to 32% [21].

Upper airway structure and function in OSA

Narrowing or occlusion of the upper airway during sleep results from several factors (table 2). An abnormal anatomy of the upper airway, increased collapsibility of the passive upper airway, dysfunctional upper airway dilator muscles, respiratory control instability referred to as an increased loop gain (LG) and a lower arousal threshold have all been shown to occur and contribute to pharyngeal collapse [22] and are currently described as the pathophysiological traits. Abnormal anatomy of the upper airway

Anomalies in the anatomy of the upper airway seem to be a plausible explanation for the occurrence of OSA. A smaller pharyngeal cross-sectional area can be observed in OSA patients in comparison with matched controls in studies during wakefulness and sleep, predisposing the subjects towards collapse. Moreover, in OSA patients the configuration of the airway is different from normal controls with an anterior–posterior configuration [23, 24]. During the inspiratory phase, a more pronounced enlargement of the airway can occur, possibly due to additional upper airway muscle dilator activity. During expiration, upper airway calibre initially increases, due to the positive intraluminal pressure, which is more pronounced in patients with OSA. On the other hand, at end-expiration the upper airway is significantly narrowed in OSA patients. Interestingly, an increased upper airway length (i.e. distance between the hyoid bone and the mandibular plane) results in a longer collapsible portion of the lumen and has been linked to OSA severity [25–27].

Most patients with OSA have an anatomically small pharyngeal airway (figure 1). This can be due to a small bony compartment in which the airway is enclosed, because of a retroposition of the mandible due to a reduction in mandibular body length (retrognathia) and/or the maxilla, usually with micrognathia (hypoplasia) and an inferiorly positioned hyoid bone [28]. Even in the absence of clear anomalies, presence of an overbite can be suggestive for an anatomical abnormality of the upper or lower jaw. The presence of skeletal anomalies and OSA is most common in nonobese patients and in patients with syndromal conditions. Reduced lower facial height and caudal position of the hyoid bone are features commonly associated with OSA [29]. Altogether, these anatomic particularities result in a reduced posterior airway space [30]. Differences in craniofacial indices may contribute to ethnic and racial differences in the prevalence of OSA. For example, compared to Caucasian people, Chinese patients have smaller soft tissue volume and smaller mandibular and maxillary structures [31].

Upper airway patency in normal, obese and overweight subjects, and in people with a small maxilla and mandible. Most patients with obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) are obese and overweight and have an anatomically small pharyngeal airway due to abnormalities of soft tissues in the upper airway (crowding, hypertrophy, accumulation of fat, infiltration and oedema) in combination with normal skeletal structures, leading to a higher critical closing pressure (Pcrit). Some OSA patients without obesity and overweight develop increased collapsibility of the upper airway due to skeletal structure anomalies in the absence of soft tissue hypertrophy.

Different abnormalities of soft tissues in the upper airway are associated with constriction and increased collapsibility [32] and include crowding, hypertrophy, accumulation of fat, infiltration and oedema of the pharyngeal walls (figure 1). For example, tonsillar hypertrophy and enlargement of the uvula and tongue contribute to the occlusion of the upper airway during sleep, which can be assessed by the Mallampati index [33]. Oedema in the soft tissue structures surrounding the upper airway may follow trauma from repeated apnoeic events [34]. Moreover, a rostral fluid shift from the lower part of the body into the head and neck region has been reported [35]. Due to gravity, fluid accumulates in the intravascular and interstitial spaces of the lower part of the body during daytime, while at night redistribution takes place rostrally upon lying down (figure 2). Accumulation of interstitial fluid might enlarge the pharyngeal tissue volume and hence reduce the upper airway lumen [36–38]. With sophisticated magnetic resonance imaging techniques, it could be demonstrated that the variation in the retropalatal area during the respiratory cycle was greater in apnoeic patients than in controls and this was even more clear during sleep, suggesting an increased compliance of the velopharynx in OSA [39, 40].

It has also been shown that patients with OSA have a relatively larger tongue for a specific maxillomandibular configuration than controls [41]. Macroglossia is not common and is part of congenital growth, amyloidosis and hypothyroidism [42, 43].

Mucosal hypertrophy of the nasal concha inferior can narrow the nasal cavity to such a degree that nasal resistance increases, contributing to the total resistance of the upper airway. Septum deviation and collapsing nostrils can also play a role. Inflammatory/vasomotor causes (acute and/or chronic rhinitis) have also been proposed as contributors to OSA [44, 45]. Nasal obstruction increases the likelihood that open mouth breathing will develop [46–49]. Mouth breathing results in posterior and caudal displacement of the genu of the mandible, as well as the hyoid bone, through the hyomandibular attachments. Mouth breathing causes the anterior pharyngeal wall structures, such as tongue and epiglottis, to move in the posterior direction, lowering pharyngeal airway size. Increases in nasal resistance will also produce a greater negative pharyngeal intraluminal pressure and reduces pharyngeal cross-sectional area [46–49].

Increased upper airway collapsibility

Increased upper airway collapsibility significantly contributes to the upper airway collapse in OSA patients [22]. Higher levels of upper airway collapsibility lead to a greater degree of airflow obstruction [50]. Upper airway collapsibility can be extrapolated from pressure–flow curves; the nasal mask pressure below which the upper airway closes can be considered as the critical closing pressure (Pcrit). The pressure–flow relationship is dependent on body position and sleep stage [51, 52]. Over the years, abbreviated methods have been developed to measure Pcrit more conveniently during sleep (figure 3) [51, 53]. Previous studies used a lengthy protocol to establish a pressure–flow relationship from multiple measurements of tidal airflow taken over a wide range of nasal pressures during prolonged periods of stable sleep. However, this protocol did not allow the determination of Pcrit and nasal resistance repeatedly under various test conditions within a single night. An abbreviated method was proposed that involved assessing responses in tidal airflow to brief rather than prolonged perturbations in nasal pressure [51]. Since airflow did not reach a steady-state level within the first three breaths after an abrupt reduction in nasal pressure, more time was allowed to lower nasal pressure for up to six breaths. Active as well as passive Pcrit can be assessed (table 3) [52, 53]. Alternative methods have been described as well (table 3) [54–57]. Ventilation parameters obtained from routine polysomnography (PSG) can also be used to define collapsibility using validated algorithms [58, 59]. Most commonly reported parameters from routine PSG include V′eupnoea, V′passive, V′active, muscle compensation, V′arousal and V′min (inversely related to Pcrit) [52, 53, 60, 61] (table 3). Interestingly, upper airway collapsibility is influenced by abnormal craniofacial and soft tissue structures. Significant correlations were found between Pcrit and soft palate length, the distance from the hyoid bone to the posterior pharyngeal wall and the distance from the hyoid bone to the posterior nasal space [62]. In nonobese patients, Pcrit was related to the distance of the hyoid bone to the mandibular plane, while in obese patients, Pcrit was related to the soft palate length. These findings suggest that the anatomy of the upper airway determines Pcrit. However, there are also other indications that the cross-sectional area of the upper airway, especially during inspiration, influences Pcrit and upper airway collapsibility; for example, motor stimulation of the hypoglossal nerve during inspiration significantly decreases Pcrit [63], which accounts for reductions in sleep apnoea severity. These observations are significant since they imply that local interventions that increase cross-sectional area of the upper airway at the end of inspiration have the potential to improve OSA by lowering Pcrit. It has also been shown that Pcrit may be dependent on lung volumes, the so-called thoracic–upper airway link. Upper airway size may expand at higher lung volumes, across the range from residual volume to total lung capacity [64–66]. The lung volume dependence of the upper airway size seems also to be greater in OSA patients. Inspiratory activity displaces the trachea caudally and stretches the connective tissues linking the trachea to the upper airway, stiffening the pharyngeal wall by passive axial tension. A reduction in functional residual capacity (FRC) may increase pharyngeal collapsibility through reductions in tracheal traction force (also called “tracheal tug”) on the pharyngeal segment [66]. Hence, increases in lung volume can contribute to improvements in upper airway patency in OSA [67]. Interestingly, Kairos positive airway pressure (KPAP) is able to stabilise the upper airway, with an algorithm that drops inspiratory positive airway pressure (IPAP) by 2 cmH2O at the start of inspiration and by an additional 3 cmH2O at peak inspiratory flow, yielding a pressure 5 cmH2O below the previously determined optimal pressure. Moreover, this reduced pressure is maintained into expiration, only returning to the optimal level at the end of expiration. Since pharyngeal narrowing primarily occurs at end-expiration, KPAP acts as a splint when the airway is most likely to close, before the next inhalation. Therefore, a lower IPAP should not compromise upper airway patency [68]. This is explained by the fact that the pressure required to open a collapsed airway (opening pressure) is typically higher than the pressure required to maintain an already open airway (closing pressure). This difference is known as hysteresis, related to muscle passive properties and viscoeleastic properties of the surrounding tissues in response to stretch, with different loading and unloading curves [69]. Also, KPAP could work by the creation of a slightly higher positive pressure during expiration, which leads to a sustained positive end-expiratory pressure at inspiration, a physiologic increase in FRC, elongation and stretching of the upper airway by the lung volume increase, and therefore a more stable upper airway [68–70]. Finally, significant lung volume reductions are observed at sleep onset, which are compensated by expiratory positive airway pressure (EPAP) and not IPAP [71, 72], emphasising a more prominent role for EPAP.

Dysfunction of upper airway dilator muscles

Pharyngeal muscle relaxation during sleep and lack of sufficient reactivation are key pathophysiological events leading to OSA. To preserve upper airway patency, the human pharynx is largely dependent on muscle activity. The human upper airway can be seen as a flexible tube, whose opening is determined by a balance of forces. Within this concept, the diameter of the upper airway depends on the balance between the forces that foster collapse, such as negative intraluminal pressure and increased extraluminal pressure, and the forces that promote patency of the upper airway, including pharyngeal dilator muscles [73, 74].

Activity of the pharyngeal dilators responds to many stimuli, including negative pressure in the upper airway and stimuli from the chemoreceptors [14]. During wakefulness, upper airway dilator muscle activation in OSA patients is quite adequate and even intensified, but substantially declines during sleep [75, 76]. This activation results from reflex activation by the negative intrapharyngeal pressures that fall to a greater extent in OSA patients due to the structurally narrower upper airway. This reflexive activation is locally mediated, since topical anaesthesia reduces this reflex. During nonrapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, this reflex activation of the upper airway dilators is significantly diminished or even lost completely [77].

Techniques for measuring pharyngeal muscle activity are described in table 3 [52, 60, 78]. Studies of genioglossal muscle activity during sleep suggest that patients with OSA have a more pronounced reduction in the genioglossal activity on the electromyographic (EMG) signal compared to normal subjects [79]. These findings suggest that an insufficient response by these muscles to the increased upper airway negative pressure during inspiration while asleep represents the key factor predisposing to occlusion rather than a primary abnormality in muscle function itself [80]. One potential mechanism responsible for this loss of reflex activation could be impaired upper airway afferent neural function, which attenuates transmission of information related to intraluminal pressure [81, 82]. The sensation of cold air and airway negative pressure are important, since they are triggers for contraction of the dilating muscles during inspiration [81]. Studies have reported not only impaired thermal sensitivity, but also mechanosensitivity at the level of the oropharynx [83, 84]. However, cold detection thresholds gave more discriminative results than vibration detection thresholds [85]. Interestingly, efficient treatment of OSA seems to protect the sensory innervation [86].

Upper airway neuropathy in OSA could be explained by mechanical trauma related to snoring vibrations, inflammation and oxidative stress [87–89]. Moreover, atrophied and hypertrophied muscle fibres have been demonstrated in sleep apnoeics in addition to frequent focal degeneration of myelinated nerve fibres in sleep apnoeics [90–96]. In OSA patients with an already narrower and more flexible upper airway, the loss of this compensatory reflex activation of the pharyngeal dilators will finally lead to pharyngeal collapse.

Recent studies show a large interindividual variability in the capacity of the pharyngeal dilators to compensate for a deficient upper airway during sleep and hence will partly determine who will or will not develop the disease. Interestingly, the interindividual dependency on this dilating activity will be the product of intrinsic upper airway anatomy and the collapsibility of the upper airway, where subjects with anatomically wider upper airways will be less dependent on muscle activity to keep the upper airway open than those with a more narrowed upper airway [97]. Reflex neuromuscular responses to upper airway obstruction can either mitigate or aggravate the obstruction, depending on the “balance of forces” across the pharyngeal airway (figure 4). Obstruction can activate accessory muscles of respiration that decrease pharyngeal collapsibility (Pcrit) by dilating and/or stiffening the pharyngeal airway with radial and caudal traction, respectively. If these responses are inadequate or poorly coordinated, airflow obstruction results in marked increases in inspiratory negative pressure swings, causing dynamic pharyngeal collapse and flow limitation when negative downstream pressure no longer generates any further increase in inspiratory airflow [98, 99]. Under flow-limited conditions, Pcrit becomes the effective back-pressure to inspiratory flow as flow becomes independent of downstream pressure and is largely determined by the pressure gradient between the nares (atmospheric) and the pharyngeal collapsing pressure (Pcrit). Once the airway becomes flow-limited, further decreases in downstream pressure can generate variable decreases in inspiratory flow, as the airway exhibits the phenomenon of negative effort dependence (table 3). Negative intraluminal pressures can also collapse pharyngeal segments downstream from the initial flow-limiting site [100]. The primary flow-limiting site is often located in the velopharynx with secondary sites involving the lateral pharyngeal walls, base of tongue and/or epiglottis. These secondary sites do not generally limit airflow and do not necessarily need to be addressed therapeutically when they only develop at markedly rather than minimally negative downstream pressures [101, 102].

Upper airway patency during sleep, presented as a result of negative pressures acting on the upper airway during inspiration and reflex neuromuscular responses to upper airway obstruction mitigating or aggravating the obstruction, depending on the “balance of forces” across the pharyngeal airway. An imbalance of structural and neuromuscular forces can increase pharyngeal collapsibility during sleep. Airway obstruction increases inspiratory negative pressure swings, which cause inspiratory flow limitation and variable degrees of negative effort dependence (NED).

Site and pattern of upper airway collapse

Partial or complete collapse of the upper airway can occur in the anteroposterior or laterolateral direction or can be concentric [103]. To assess the site of upper airway collapse, different techniques have been introduced (table 3) [103–107]. Drug-induced sleep endoscopy (DISE) is used to determine the site of collapse in routine clinical practice [108]. In a large cohort of patients with OSA, single-level collapse was seen most frequently at the level of the palate (12.6%), followed by base of tongue (9.4%), hypopharynx (3.6%) and oropharynx (0.9%). Most patients showed multilevel collapse, in which palatal collapse was involved most frequently (81%), followed by tongue base collapse (46.6%), hypopharyngeal collapse (38.7%) and oropharyngeal collapse (21.9%). The most frequently observed multilevel collapse pattern was a combination of palatal and tongue base collapse in up to 25% of the examined patients [109]. In the same study, the prevalence of complete collapse, multilevel collapse and hypopharyngeal collapse increased with increasing OSA severity. Multilevel and complete collapse patterns were more prevalent in obese patients and in those with more severe OSA, whereas the prevalence of tongue base collapse was lower in overweight and obese patients. Both increasing apnoea–hypopnoea index and body mass index (BMI) values corresponded with a higher probability of complete concentric collapse at the level of the palate (CCCp), partial lateral oropharyngeal collapse and partial lateral hypopharyngeal collapse [110]. Complete anteroposterior tongue base collapse was found with a higher probability in patients with lower BMI. Interestingly, CCCp was reported more frequently during DISE compared to natural sleep endoscopy (NSE) [111]. Regarding the extent of obstruction, higher obstruction grades were present during DISE observations, in comparison with NSE. Neuromuscular factors, soft tissue and skeletal characteristics contribute in the sense that the extent of upper airway collapse varies with sleep stage, with more extensive caudal collapse occurring in rapid eye movement (REM) sleep compared with NREM sleep [112]. An innovative, noninvasive approach to determine the site of upper airway collapse is airflow shape analysis (table 3) [105–107, 113].

Interestingly, it has recently been shown that the site of collapse during DISE is associated with all endotypic traits, except for LG [114]. Concretely, CCCp was associated with higher collapsibility but a lower arousal threshold while tongue base collapse showed an opposite pattern. Lateral wall collapse was associated with reduced muscle compensation.

Airway reopening at resumption of ventilation may be either abrupt or more gradual, with reopening extending from the caudal to the cranial direction [114]. Endoscopic studies looking at upper airway size also demonstrated that complete collapse of the upper airway in OSA occurs at the end of expiration preceding the apnoeic event [115]. This finding was confirmed in imaging studies and in studies assessing airway impedance with the forced oscillation technique [23, 116, 117].

Loop gain

Oropharyngeal dilator muscle contractility and upper airway patency may be adversely affected by disturbances in the chemoreflex feedback control loop, primarily related to fluctuations in CO2. Moreover, cyclic changes in arterial CO2 around the CO2 threshold for activation of upper airway motor neuron activity could lead to an imbalance of forces acting on the pharyngeal airway and favour closure. The sensitivity of this feedback system can be quantified using the engineering term loop gain (LG), which is the ratio of ventilatory response to a ventilatory disturbance [118]. LG represents an important predisposing factor to ventilatory instability. The principal components of LG are plant gain (i.e. effectiveness of the lungs to excrete CO2) and controller gain (i.e. the sensitivity of the chemoreceptors), and both are important in ventilatory stability. An LG >1, indicating that the ventilatory response is larger than the ventilatory disturbance, reflects an oversensitive ventilatory control system, which leads to breathing oscillations and sleep apnoea. Different techniques have been developed to assess LG (table 3) [52, 119, 120].

Younes et al. [121] found that LG was greater in patients with severe OSA than in those with mild OSA. Wellman et al. [122] reported that LG measured during NREM sleep was an important predictor of OSA severity, but only in patients with intermediate upper airway collapsibility. Subjects with extreme pharyngeal collapsibility have obstructive apnoeas regardless of LG. On the other hand, an LG closer to zero will lead to a faster return to the initial ventilation level, indicating an inherently stable ventilatory control. If LG is higher, the ventilatory system cannot regain its stability during sleep and the system needs to be restarted via arousal from sleep to restore stability [121, 122]. LG predisposes to OSA when a high LG (associated with a larger increase in ventilation following apnoea termination) increases the degree of ventilatory instability, resulting in a greater subsequent reduction in ventilation and in dilatory muscle contraction force. Hence, the way in which individuals respond to fluctuations in CO2 is crucial in the pathogenesis of OSA [123]. We previously could show that OSA patients may have a higher chemical drive (hypercapnic ventilatory response) that can contribute to the increased LG [124–127]. It must be emphasised that LG plays a contributing role, but that patients must have an underlying predisposition to upper airway collapse to develop OSA. A high LG alone can produce central but less easily obstructive events. It follows that LG has a lower effect size than anatomical traits but is nevertheless important to consider [127]. Presence of high LG in combination with a modestly collapsible upper airway (Pcrit <−2 cmH2O) is sufficient to result into relevant OSA [22]. Interestingly, evidence points at a circadian rhythm in LG, with peak values observed in the early morning hours [128].

Arousal threshold

The termination of an obstructive respiratory event is typically associated with a cortical microarousal. In response to upper airway obstruction, respiratory drive increases and with each consecutive breath the intrathoracic pressures become progressively more negative. At a certain pressure level, a respiratory-induced arousal occurs and ventilation resumes. An arousal is considered a crucial factor in the restoration of ventilation. Arousals in themselves may result in hyperventilation following apnoea termination, promoting ventilatory instability and further promoting pharyngeal collapsibility [129]. Cortical arousals related to respiratory events vary in degree, which may be quantified by the arousal threshold, and are nowadays considered as a distinct pathophysiologic feature [130]. Different assessment techniques have been reported (table 3) [52, 120, 131].

A low arousal threshold is associated with repetitive apnoeas and could also represent a therapeutic target in selective cases [132]. Most respiratory events are associated with a microarousal. This has led to the traditional belief that arousals are critical to overcome upper airway obstruction in patients with OSA [133]. However, currently, arousals are not absolutely required to restore ventilation. Interestingly, other restoration mechanisms such as the ventilatory control response to hypercapnia or negative pressure reflex arc of the dilator muscles might also be able to restore airway patency without the need for an arousal if the threshold is not too low [2]. Arousal threshold is then defined as the ventilatory drive causing arousal. Moreover, in many cases, arousals may even actually perpetuate the cyclical breathing pattern observed in OSA [129]. Indeed, recurrent arousals prevent deeper, more stable sleep stages and hinder the build-up of stimuli that are required to recruit enough upper airway muscles to reopen the upper airway [134, 135]. It is assumed that approximately 30–50% of OSA patients wake up to very small changes in respiratory effort [22]. These patients exhibit a low arousal threshold, which may also lead to sleep fragmentation and sleepiness during the daytime. Conversely, patients with a high arousal threshold may suffer from longer apnoeas and deeper oxygen desaturations, particularly when they have a poor muscle responsiveness [136, 137].

Relationship of LG and arousal threshold to the upper airways

High LG and a low arousal threshold interact to cause upper airway obstruction by promoting rapid shifts between over-breathing and apnoea. High LG causes ventilatory overshoots, while low arousal thresholds cause premature awakenings, resulting in hypocapnia that reduces upper airway muscle activity, leading to collapse. Hence, these frequent, premature awakenings break the muscle-stabilising effect of sleep, resulting in unstable breathing and further airway collapse. An airway closure causes an arousal, followed by a high LG (overshoot in breathing), which causes a drop in CO2 (hypocapnia). This hypocapnia removes the drive for upper airway muscles to dilate, causing another obstruction. High LG and a low arousal threshold often coexist, creating a synergistic effect that significantly increases the severity of upper airway obstructions. Eckert et al. [22] have demonstrated that up to 19% of all patients have the same degree of airway collapsibility as control participants without OSA. However, in these patients, the presence of an elevated LG and/or low arousal threshold is the primary effect modifier predisposing them to OSA. In a later study from the same group, it was shown that overweight/obese individuals without apnoea have a moderately compromised upper airway structure that is mitigated by highly responsive upper-airway dilator muscles to avoid OSA [138]. By contrast, there was no evidence of lowered LG or elevated arousal threshold compared with patients with OSA. Recently, it was shown that an increased LG predicts residual sleep apnoea among people using CPAP, with threefold odds of residual events [139].

Positional OSA

In the last decades it has become clear that the role of body position and gravity are important factors to consider, certainly in milder cases [140, 141]. When OSA patients shift from the supine to lateral position, they experience a substantial increase in awake end-expiratory lung volume, passive V′0, active V′0 and a significant decrease in Pcrit, while LG and arousal threshold are not influenced by changes in body position [142]. Moreover, patients with positional OSA have a significantly more favourable upper airway gain (ability to stiffen and dilate the airway more effectively) and Pcrit, which helps to almost completely avoid OSA in the lateral position [142]. The folding configuration of the lateral wall is also crucial in determining collapsibility of the airway. When a patient with positional OSA lies in the lateral direction, there is an opening up of the lateral parts of the airway, so that the velopharynx turns into a circular shape [143]. Moreover, the tongue muscle and soft palate now lie perpendicular to the gravitational pull (and now constitute the lateral wall of a 90° rotated upper airway), resulting in an airway that is less likely to collapse when passive and in the lateral position [144].

REM-OSA

REM sleep-related OSA (REM-OSA) is a distinct, often severe, phenotype characterised by upper airway collapse primarily during REM sleep. Its occurrence is driven by decreased muscle tone and reduced reflex muscle responsiveness (lowered motor neuron activity, decreased respiratory motor drive) [145, 146]. Krieger et al. [147] reported that indices of respiratory effort were significantly lower in REM than in NREM sleep, demonstrated by the values of peak negative intrathoracic pressure (39.6±1.9 mbar in REM sleep versus 50.9±2.5 mbar in NREM sleep). Also, contrary to NREM-predominant, REM-OSA is associated with lower LG (due to reduced controller gain and chemoreflex sensitivity), namely 0.365 (REM) versus 0.546 (NREM) [148]. This predisposition to pharyngeal collapse is most pronounced in those individuals who are critically dependent on upper airway dilator muscle activity for maintaining upper airway patency [149]. In REM-OSA, Joosten et al. [148] reported a significantly more collapsible airway (lower V′passive) in REM sleep (95.9% V′eupnoea) compared with NREM (98.4% V′eupnoea). Most recent evidence indicates that pharyngeal collapsibility is largely explained by ventilatory drive withdrawal rather than by particular decrements in muscle activity or responsiveness [150]. While REM sleep generally features higher arousal threshold (requiring more effort to wake), individuals with REM-OSA often have a paradoxical low arousal threshold, causing them to wake up frequently [151]. Altogether, REM-OSA is more prone to passive upper airway collapsibility as compared with the NREM-OSA phenotype [134, 145, 146, 148–150].

The role of the upper airways in obstructive as compared to central apnoea

Central and obstructive apnoeas are rarely seen in isolation. The vast majority of patients with OSA also have some central apnoeas and vice versa [152]. This observation indicates that the mechanisms responsible for the different types of apnoea must overlap. Önal et al. [153] showed that sleep-induced periodic breathing is primary to the development of OSA, while obstruction is only manifested in subjects susceptible to upper airway narrowing [154]. A causal relationship between central and obstructive apnoeas is given by the (frequent) occurrence of mixed apnoeas, characterised by a period of decreased central drive followed by an obstructed breath. In daily practice, central apnoeas without subsequent obstruction are less common than mixed apnoeas in referred populations with suspicion of OSA. A high index for central apnoeas, with a low index for obstructive apnoeas, is encountered in only a minority of subjects. This probably reflects the broad range in upper airway collapsibility and the tendency to collapse in most (even healthy) subjects when the drive to the inspiratory muscles, especially the upper airway muscles, is reduced at the nadir of periodic breathing [138, 155]. Upper airway muscle activation due to increasing arterial carbon dioxide tension (PaCO2) may be delayed relative to activation of the diaphragm. This represents a reasonable cause of upper airway collapse [156]. During unstable breathing with crescendo-decrescendo patterns of respiration, continuous changes in PaCO2 may trigger obstructive events by this particular type of mechanism [157]. Indeed, decreased ventilation, as it occurs during periodic breathing, is associated with less stimulation of the genioglossal muscle, since the corresponding negative pressure stimulus is equally reduced [158, 159]. Also, increased pharyngeal compliance and lung volume dependence may play a role in the pathogenesis of central apnoeas [160]. In recent decades, upper airway patency has been studied during central respiratory events using fibre-optic nasopharyngoscopy [161, 162]. During induced hypocapnic central apnoea, progressive pharyngeal narrowing occurred. Indications for airway closure during central apnoea can also be obtained based on measurements of critical closure pressure [163] and with the forced oscillation technique [117]. It can be speculated that an “early” arousal will terminate central apnoea, while a “delayed” arousal will allow the central apnoea to develop into a mixed apnoea. Chemical drives may play a role as well in this interaction [124]. Evidence has shown that CO2 drive correlates with event time in central apnoea and with apnoea duration in OSA [125]. These findings indicate that high chemical drives promote instability in the breathing pattern, but also suggest that high drives may shorten the apnoea duration in case of upper airway collapse [125, 164].

LG was initially recognised as a key mechanism in central sleep apnoea before its role in OSA was confirmed. Nowadays, LG is recognised as an important contributor to the pathophysiology of apnoeas of various origin. OSA patients with a high LG present severe disease with central and mixed events occurring more often in the last part of the night. Such a presentation is related to changes in LG, with a progressive increase in plant gain during the night and a physiological increase of LG in the morning [123, 165, 166].

A progressive fluid shift may also contribute to the pathogenesis of obstructive as well as central apnoeas. Intermittent hypoxia could be itself a trigger for an increase in LG; this mechanism has been postulated to be responsible for the increase of controller gain during the night with a progressive increase in LG [165, 167, 168]. A recent study reported that LG was significantly lower in REM (indicating more stable ventilatory control) compared to N2 sleep and that modest increases over the course of sleep take place [166]. This may explain the reduced propensity for central sleep apnoea in REM sleep. Also, in patients with predominant OSA versus pure OSA, no difference was found in Pcrit between the two groups, while a significantly lower CO2 reserve was reported in predominant OSA, indicating that breathing instability rather than upper airway collapsibility distinguishes OSA patients with a combination of obstructive and central events from those with pure OSA [152].

Mixed sleep apnoea

Mixed apnoeas are technically considered as part of the obstructive spectrum and are associated with more severe oxygen desaturation and fragmented sleep [169]. About 33% of patients with OSA present with mixed sleep apnoea [170]. Iber et al. [171] were the first to suggest that the nonobstructive component of a mixed apnoea may be the result of hyperventilation following the previous obstructive apnoea, rather than the cause of the subsequent obstructive apnoea. Nowadays, the occurrence of mixed events in OSA is explained by instability of the ventilatory control system and high airway collapsibility. Again, patients with OSA with obstructive and coexisting central/mixed respiratory events have been shown to exhibit smaller CO2 reserve, indicating greater respiratory control instability with elevated CO2 chemosensitivity driving the interplay of central apnoea and hyperventilation [166, 172]. It has also been shown that OSA patients with mixed apnoeas more often have NREM-dominant sleep apnoea, pointing again at high LG [173]. This concept suggests that the three types of apnoea represent a continuum of destabilised ventilatory control [152, 174]. Because of the underlying central component, these patients may sometimes show reduced response to traditional upper airway treatment such as CPAP. Recent studies have demonstrated that OSA with mixed apnoeas is associated with more treatment-emergent central sleep apnoea (TECSA), less optimal therapy, higher titrated CPAP pressure and lower compliance to CPAP [175, 176]. TECSA was most likely if mixed apnoeas had a long apnoea duration, shorter ventilatory period and a smaller duty ratio [175]. Finally, patients with mixed apnoeas also have a higher BMI, pointing at an increased collapsibility of the upper airway as well [170].

Sex differences in the upper airway obstruction

OSA exhibits significant sex-based differences in pathophysiology, related to anatomical and physiological features of the upper airway, crucial disparities in physiology, hormone regulation, neural control, and adipose tissue distribution. Upper airway dimensions are normally larger in men than in women, but similar when normalised for body weight [177]. Females generally have smaller, but less collapsible, upper airways and shorter upper airway lengths [26, 178, 179]. The airway is “stiffer” and more resistant to total collapse. On the other hand, males have longer upper airways, which are more susceptible to collapse, demonstrated consistently by increased upper airway collapsibility under passive conditions (Pcrit) [26]. A higher Pcrit could also be explained by differences in fat distribution, especially in the premenopausal stage, with a peripheral rather than the central pattern, characterised by less fat deposition around the neck [180, 181]. In postmenopausal women, the upper airway tends to lengthen and becomes more collapsible. Since differences between sexes in airway length do not occur in the prepubertal period, this suggests a major effect of sex hormones [182]. Females also have better upper airway neuromuscular compensation, especially in the premenopausal phase, due to the protective effects of progesterone and oestrogen on the genioglossal muscle activity [183, 184]. Males show lower neuromuscular compensation, which combined with higher collapsibility, results in more severe disease. Men also have a higher ventilatory response to spontaneous arousal from sleep, but also a greater “undershoot” (hypoventilation) upon returning to sleep and a higher chemoresponsiveness, which makes their breathing more unstable [185, 186].

Females also exhibit a significantly lower arousal threshold, leading to increased sleep fragmentation, higher frequencies of respiratory-effort related arousals and more disturbed sleep [187]. Finally, insignificant sex differences in LG were reported after adjustment for collapsibility, suggesting that LG does not independently contribute to the increased OSA risk in men compared to women [188, 189]. Interestingly, females show a higher proportion of apnoeas during REM sleep, leading to significant REM-specific, severe or long-lasting events. On the other hand, females less often show positional, supine-predominant OSA [190].

Therapeutical consequences in OSA

Overall, treatment of OSA is empirical in nature, implying a trial-and-error approach. However, assessment of pathophysiological endotype may be efficient in selecting upfront a therapeutical intervention that probably will sort beneficial results (figure 5). Moreover, given that many patients with OSA demonstrate a combination of multiple traits, it implies that a single treatment option may not be optimal. CPAP or an oral device should be offered as a first-line when collapse is purely structural. However, it has been shown that outcome of mandibular advancement device (MAD) therapy is strongly modulated by nonanatomical traits; patients exhibiting low LG, reflecting stable ventilatory control and moderate collapsibility, are significantly more likely to respond to an MAD [191, 192]. The presence of collapse at base of tongue and the absence of CCCp and lateral wall collapse were positively associated with success [193]. It was also found that positive outcome of hypoglossal nerve stimulation is related to the presence of high arousal threshold, low LG (particularly in mild collapsibility) and greater muscle compensation [194]. As in MAD therapy, the presence of collapse at the tongue base and the absence of CCCp and lateral wall collapse were associated with a positive treatment outcome [194–196]. Moreover, recently, Vena et al. [196] could show that lateral collapse as predicted using a noninvasive model and lateral wall collapse as assessed during DISE was associated with reduced odds for hypoglossal nerve stimulation therapy response [197]. Surgical procedures primarily target the extreme anatomical endotypes, where structural compromise predominate. It has also been shown that upper airway surgery most effectively resolves OSA in patients with low LG [198]. For CPAP, a high arousal threshold was associated with favourable long-term adherence [199]. Predicting success, failure and adherence of OSA therapies may prove to become more important in future assessment of OSA patients [80, 200].

Conclusions

The pathophysiology of OSA varies greatly. Pharyngeal anatomy, collapsibility, upper airway muscle responsiveness, LG and arousal threshold may all contribute to apnoea presence and severity. As outlined in this review, many factors, alone or in combination, can contribute to the development of OSA and suggest a heterogenous pallet of clinical presentations, rather than a single disease entity. The choice of therapy and the introduction of new therapies depend on understanding the pathophysiological traits of the OSA patient under consideration.
