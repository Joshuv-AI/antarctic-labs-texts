Integrated analysis of phenotypes and whole-transcriptome data for the preliminary exploration of molecular regulatory networks influencing beef quality
Authors: Mengli Yang, Xue Bai, Yuan Liu, Hongen Chu, Bei Cai, Runjun Yang, Lupei Zhang, Fen Li, Yun Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Guyuan cattle are a unique indigenous genetic resource in Northwest China, characterized by favorable roughage utilization efficiency and meat production potential. However, the molecular mechanisms governing their meat quality traits remain poorly elucidated. In this exploratory study, we characterized meat quality phenotypes and transcriptomic profiles in Guyuan cattle and Wagyu cattle, which differ significantly in intramuscular fat (IMF) content. Phenotypic comparisons (n = 6) revealed significant interbreed divergences in slaughter performance, IMF content, drip loss, moisture content, shear force, fatty acid composition, amino acid profile, and muscle fiber type. Through integrated whole-transcriptome sequencing (n = 3) and phenotypic analysis, and based on differential expression analysis coupled with phenotype correlation screening, we preliminarily identified 37 differentially genes associated with fat deposition (16), shear force (11), and muscle development (10). Based on seed region complementarity, phenotypic correlation, and signaling pathway enrichment analysis, we predicted potential target genes and constructed 16 co-expression networks. It should be noted that this study is limited by a small sample size, and the multi-omics data are associative in nature, which does not allow for causal inference. Therefore, the above genes and networks are predictive and have not undergone independent functional validation. These findings offer preliminary, exploratory insights into the potential molecular mechanisms associated with key meat quality traits in Guyuan cattle, and serve as a reference for future functional gene validation, genetic improvement of indigenous yellow cattle, and related industrial applications.

Introduction

Guyuan cattle are a unique indigenous genetic resource among yellow cattle in Northwest China. Owing to their outstanding climbing capacity, strong adaptability to roughage feeding conditions, excellent disease resistance, and good reproductive performance, they occupy an important position in the local livestock industry (Liu, Yan, Feng, et al., 2025). However, compared with specialized beef cattle breeds that have undergone long-term directional selection, such as Wagyu and Angus cattle, the meat quality of Guyuan cattle—particularly the key traits determining flavour, juiciness, and tenderness, including IMF content and shear force—exhibits significant deficiencies (Liu, Yan, Liu, et al., 2025). This contradiction between ‘high adaptability and low meat quality’ not only limits the full exploitation of Guyuan cattle's economic value but also renders the elucidation of the molecular mechanisms underlying Guyuan cattle's meat quality traits a critical issue with both theoretical significance and practical industrial value.

Meat quality is a complex quantitative trait governed by highly networked post-transcriptional regulation. Its core components—muscle fibres and IMF—are co-regulated by multiple genes. The ceRNA mechanism provides a molecular basis for this coordination: lncRNAs and circRNAs act as molecular sponges by competitively binding shared miRNAs, thereby relieving miRNA-induced suppression of target mRNAs and enabling the coordinated expression of multiple genes. For instance, at the circRNA regulatory level, key molecules such as bta_circ_00497 and bta_circ_034497 have been identified. These molecules regulate the expression of functional genes including MyH6, MyH7, and NEB, thereby influencing muscle fiber diameter and density, ultimately reducing shear force and improving meat tenderness (Yang et al., 2023). Regarding lncRNA regulation, Choi et al. have utilized quantitative trait locus (QTL) data to identify 12 lncRNAs associated with shear force and 6 associated with body weight (Choi et al., 2018). Salatta et al. have screened bovine muscle tissues with different fatty acid phenotypes and identified five key lncRNAs that regulate fatty acid metabolism (Salatta et al., 2025). At the miRNA level, molecules such as bta-miR-182, bta-miR-183, and bta-miR-338 have been shown to influence muscle protein hydrolysis and meat tenderness by binding to target genes including MEF2C, MAP3K2, MTDH, and TNRC6B (Kappeler et al., 2019). Although these findings provide important insights into the molecular regulation of beef quality, existing ceRNA networks remain oversimplified. Meat quality is inherently a complex trait shaped by multi-tissue interactions, where signal communication between different tissues (e.g., crosstalk between adipose and muscle tissues) directly influences the formation of meat quality-related phenotypes. However, current ceRNA studies in cattle have mostly been limited to single-muscle tissue sampling, neglecting the impact of inter-tissue communication on regulatory networks. As a result, the constructed ceRNA networks often fail to reflect the true in vivo landscape of meat quality regulation, thereby constraining a comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms (Wang et al., 2020). Therefore, systematically dissecting the role of ceRNA networks in beef quality regulation from a multi-tissue perspective holds important theoretical significance.

Based on these considerations, this study selected Guyuan cattle and Wagyu cattle (as a high-quality meat control) as experimental models, given their significant differences in key meat quality traits such as IMF content. Phenotypic evaluations of meat quality were conducted, and whole-transcriptome sequencing was performed on the longissimus dorsi muscle and subcutaneous fat tissue. Through this extreme phenotypic comparison design, this study aims to preliminarily screen mRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs, and miRNAs that regulate meat quality traits, delineate a preliminary landscape of ceRNA regulatory networks closely associated with meat quality traits, and conduct association analyses between the identified candidate regulatory modules and specific measured phenotypes (e.g., IMF content and shear force), thereby highlighting core molecular pathways and candidate genes potentially involved in meat quality regulation. This study is expected to provide initial insights into the post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms underlying meat quality traits in indigenous cattle, offering a theoretical foundation and a set of candidate molecular targets for future in-depth mechanistic studies on meat quality formation in Guyuan cattle and other related indigenous yellow cattle breeds.

Materials and methods

Animals and sample collection

All cattle in this study were raised at Guyuan Fumin Technology Development Co., Ltd. (dietary nutrient composition as detailed in Supplementary Table S1) and were slaughtered at 30 months of age. To maximize the detection of transcriptomic differences associated with IMF content, an extreme phenotypic sampling strategy was employed in this study. The experimental subjects consisted of 18 Guyuan bulls (GY) and 19 Wagyu bulls (WA). Using random numbers generated by the RAND function in Microsoft Excel, combined with electronic ear tag identification numbers, six GY (low IMF) and six WA (high IMF) were randomly selected from the respective groups. All experimental cattle were fasted for 24 h and deprived of water for 8 h before slaughter. At the slaughterhouse, the cattle were stunned by electric shock for 5 s, followed by exsanguination, skinning, evisceration, and splitting along the midline. The entire process of rearing, transportation, and slaughter complied with relevant animal welfare regulations and was supervised by Guyuan Fumin Technology Development Co., Ltd. Sample collection comprised three stages: (1) Within 30 min of slaughter, the longissimus dorsi muscle (LDM) was collected from between the 12th and 13th ribs of the left carcass. Tissue samples measuring 3–5 mm in diameter and weighing approximately 50–100 mg were placed into centrifuge tubes, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for RNA extraction and tissue sectioning. Additionally, tissue pieces of 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm × 0.2–0.3 cm were placed in tissue fixative solution for tissue sectioning. (2) Within 30 min of slaughter, subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) was collected from between the 12th and 13th ribs of the left carcass. The samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for RNA extraction. (3) Following 48 h of carcass precooling and maturation, approximately 500 g of LDM was transported to the laboratory in an ice bath and stored at −20 °C for meat quality assessment. The meat quality analysis included six biological replicates. For transcriptomic sequencing, three biological replicates were randomly selected from the six animals used for meat quality analysis. All omics layers (mRNA, lncRNA, circRNA, and miRNA) were derived from the same tissue sample and the same individual animal for each of the three RNA-seq replicates. This study and all experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Ningxia University (approval no. NXU-2023-091), and permission was obtained from Guyuan Fumin Technology Development Co., Ltd.

Slaughter performance assessment

The indicators and methods for determining slaughter performance are as follows: (1) Before slaughter, the cattle were fasted for 24 h and deprived of water for 8 h. Their live weight was then measured and recorded as the slaughter weight (kg). (2) Following slaughter, the carcass was weighed after removal of the head, limbs, hide, and internal organs (kidneys retained), and this weight was recorded as the carcass weight (kg). (3) Slaughter rate (%) was calculated using the following formula:(1)Slaughter rate%=carcass weight/slaughter weight×100%

Determination of meat quality indicators

Each experimental group consisted of six biological replicates. All meat quality parameters for each sample were measured in triplicate, and the average of the three measurements was used for subsequent analysis.

Meat color was measured using a CR-400 colorimeter (Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an 8 mm aperture. The instrument was calibrated against a white calibration plate before each measurement session. The colorimeter had a measurement time of 1 s per reading, with a minimum interval of 3 s between successive readings. Lightness (L*), redness (a*), and yellowness (b*) values were recorded.

The meat was cut into strips of 2 cm × 3 cm × 5 cm and then weighed (W1). The strips were suspended vertically within a plastic bag (without touching the bag walls), labele, and refrigerated at (4 ± 1)°C for 24 h. After removal, the surface moisture was blotted with filter paper, and the samples were weighed again (W2). The drip loss was calculated using the following formula:(2)Drip loss%=W1−W2/W1×100%

The surface fat was removed from the meat, which was then cut into 6 cm × 6 cm × 4 cm cubes. The cubes were weighed (W1). The cubes were placed in a resealable bag (opening facing upwards), heated in a water bath at a specified constant temperature until the center of the meat sample reached 70 °C, held for 30 min, cooled to room temperature, and weighed again (W2). The cooking loss was calculated via the following formula:(3)Cooking loss%=W1−W2/W1×100%

Shear force was measured using a C-LM4 digital muscle tenderness meter (Tenovo, Beijing, China). Muscle samples were cut into 1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm strips parallel to the myofibrils. Each strip was sheared three times at a shear speed of ≤5 mm/s, and the results were expressed in Newtons (N).

IMF content was determined using the Soxhlet extraction method (Buchi Labortechnik AG, Flawil, Switzerland) according to the Chinese national standard GB/T 5009.6-2016. Petroleum ether was used as the solvent, and the mixture was refluxed continuously for 8 h at a reflux rate of 6–8 cycles per hour.

Moisture content was determined using the direct drying method according to the Chinese national standard GB 18394-2020. A fine glass rod and 10 g of quartz sand were placed in a weighing bottle. The bottle with its contents was dried to constant weight in an electric forced-air constant-temperature drying oven (Boxun, Shanghai, China) at 103 °C, and the mass was recorded. A homogenized muscle sample was weighed into the pre-dried weighing bottle, was thoroughly mixed with the sand and glass rod, and was dried at 103 °C for 4 h. The bottle was then removed, cooled in a desiccator, and weighed. The drying, cooling, and weighing procedure was repeated at 103 °C at 1 h intervals until the difference between two consecutive weighings was less than 2 mg, at which point constant weight was considered to have been achieved.

Crude protein content was determined using the Kjeldahl method according to the Chinese national standard GB 5009.5-2016. Briefly, the sample was weighed and digested with 0.4 g of copper sulfate, 6 g of potassium sulfate, and 20 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid until the solution became clear and blue-green, and then heated for a further 0.5 h. The digest was distilled using a distillation apparatus. A receiving flask containing 20 mL of boric acid solution and two drops of methyl red–bromocresol green indicator was placed under the condenser. Sodium hydroxide solution was added to the digestion tube, and distillation was carried out for 10 min. The receiving flask was then moved away so that the liquid surface no longer touched the tip of the condenser, and distillation continued for an additional 1 min. The tip of the condenser was rinsed with a small amount of water, and the receiving flask was removed. The distillate was titrated with standard hydrochloric acid (HCl) solution until a grayish-red color appeared. A blank test was performed in parallel.

Total ash content was determined according to Method 1 of the Chinese national standard GB 5009.4-2016. A clean porcelain crucible was placed in an energy-saving box-type electric furnace (Tianjin Zhonghuan Experimental Electric Furnace Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China) and calcined at 550 °C for 30 min. After the crucible had cooled to 200 °C in the furnace, it was transferred to a desiccator, cooled for 30 min, and accurately weighed. The calcination process was repeated until constant weight was achieved (i.e., the difference between two consecutive weighings was ≤0.5 mg). A homogenized muscle sample was weighed into the pre-weighed crucible, carbonized over low heat on the electric furnace until no smoke was emitted, and then transferred to the energy-saving box-type electric furnace for calcination at 550 °C for 4 h until it was completely ash-free. The crucible was then cooled and weighed following the same procedure as described above, and calcination was repeated until constant weight was achieved.

Determination of amino acid and fatty acid content

Amino acid content was determined according to the Chinese national standard GB 5009.124-2016. An appropriate amount of sample was weighed into a hydrolysis tube, mixed with 10 mL of 6 mol/L hydrochloric acid, purged with nitrogen, and hydrolyzed at 110 °C for 22 h. After cooling, the hydrolysate was filtered and made up to volume with distilled water. An aliquot of 1.0 mL of the filtrate was accurately pipetted, dried under a nitrogen stream at 40 °C, redissolved in sodium citrate buffer, and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane. The sample was then analyzed using a fully automated amino acid analyzer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). The chromatographic column was packed with a sulfonate-type cation exchange resin. The analytical conditions were as follows: pump 1 flow rate, 0.40 mL/min; pump 2 flow rate, 0.35 mL/min; injection volume, 20 μL; separation column temperature, 57 °C; derivatization column temperature, 135 °C; detection was performed using a visible spectrophotometer at wavelengths of 570 nm and 440 nm. The mixed amino acid standard working solutions and sample test solutions were injected into the amino acid analyzer separately at equal injection volumes. Quantification was performed using an external standard method, and the amino acid concentrations in the sample test solutions were calculated from their peak areas. Under repeatability conditions, the absolute difference between two independent determination results obtained from the same sample shall not exceed 12% of the arithmetic mean of the two results. The instrument precision, repeatability, stability, and recovery of the proposed method were systematically evaluated. The instrument precision was satisfactory, with relative standard deviations (RSDs) ranging from 0.15%–0.82% (n = 6). The repeatability experiment yielded RSDs ranging from 1.34%–2.35% (n = 6), and the stability experiment produced RSDs ranging from 0.32%–0.77%. The spike-and-recovery experiment showed recovery rates ranging from 95.83%–100.04%, with all RSDs below 3%. The ratios of delicious amino acids (aspartic acid (Asp) and glutamic acid (Glu)), sweet amino acids (threonine (Thr), serine (Ser), proline (Pro), glycine (Gly) and alanine (Ala)) and bitter amino acids (valine (Val), methionine (Met), isoleucine (Ile), leucine (Leu), phenylalanine (Phe), lysine (Lys), histidine (His) and arginine (Arg)) to total amino acids were calculated.

Fatty acid content in muscle was determined using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). An accurately weighed sample was placed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube, mixed with 1 mL of chloroform–methanol (2:1, v/v) and 100 mg of glass beads, and ground at 60 Hz for 1 min. This step was performed three times. The mixture was then sonicated at room temperature for 30 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 5 min. The supernatant was collected. Subsequently, 2 mL of 1% methanolic sulfuric acid was added, and the mixture was shaken for 1 min. Esterification was carried out in a water bath at 80 °C for 30 min. After cooling, the fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) were extracted with 1 mL of n-hexane, washed with water, and centrifuged. The supernatant was then dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate. The extract was diluted 10-fold and thoroughly mixed with methyl salicylate as an internal standard. GC–MS analysis was performed using a gas chromatograph (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with a Thermo TG-FAME capillary column (50 m × 0.25 mm × 0.20 μm). The injector temperature was set to 250 °C, with a split ratio of 8:1 and an injection volume of 1 μL. The temperature program was as follows: 80 °C held for 1 min, ramped at 20 °C/min to 160 °C and held for 1.5 min, ramped at 3 °C/min to 196 °C and held for 8.5 min, then ramped at 20 °C/min to 250 °C and held for 3 min. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 0.63 mL/min. The mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was operated in electron impact (EI) mode with an electron energy of 70 eV, and detection was performed in selected ion monitoring (SIM) mode. Calibration was performed using a mixed standard solution of 51 FAMEs (total concentration: 4000 μg/mL). The stock solution was diluted with n-hexane to prepare ten working standard solutions at concentrations of 1, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 250, 500, 1000, and 2000 μg/mL (total concentration). Each working standard solution was analyzed to construct standard curves and evaluate linear ranges. Linear regression analysis was performed for each fatty acid, and all standard curves showed correlation coefficients (r) > 0.99. The concentration of each fatty acid in the samples was calculated from the corresponding standard curve and expressed as absolute content (μg/g).

Tissue section staining

For hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining, fixed LDM samples were paraffin-embedded, sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm (3 slides per sample), dehydrated using a gradient ethanol solution, cleared with xylene, and stained using a standard HE staining protocol. The prepared sections were photographed under a microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), with 5 non-overlapping fields (center and four quadrants) of view randomly selected from each section for imaging. ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to measure muscle fiber diameter and cross-sectional area. Only muscle fibres with clear boundaries and intact morphology were included in the measurements.

For ATPase staining, sections were prepared at 8 μm thickness (3 sections per sample) using a cryostat and air-dried at room temperature. Staining was performed using the standard ATPase calcium‑cobalt method (alkaline incubation solution staining). The prepared sections were photographed under a microscope, with 5 non-overlapping fields (center and four quadrants) of view randomly selected from each section. The inclusion criteria were the same as those for HE staining.

All image acquisition and analysis for both HE staining and ATPase staining were performed by personnel blinded to the sample information. Measurements from individual muscle fibres were used only for descriptive visualization (violin plots), whereas all statistical comparisons were performed based on the mean values per sample.

For Oil Red O staining, tissue samples were sectioned into 6–10 μm thick slices (3 sections per sample) using a cryostat microtome. The sections were stained using a standard Oil Red O staining protocol. The prepared sections were photographed under a microscope, with 5 non-overlapping fields (center and four quadrants) of view randomly selected from each section. Image acquisition and analysis were performed by personnel blinded to the sample information.

Differential expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from Guyuan cattle subcutaneous fat (GY_SAT), Guyuan cattle longissimus dorsi muscle (GY_LDM), Wagyu subcutaneous fat (WA_SAT), and Wagyu longissimus dorsi muscle (WA_LDM) using the Trizol method (TaKaRa, Kyoto, Japan). The integrity and concentration of RNA samples were assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop measurement (A260/280 ≥ 1.8, RQN/RIN ≥ 7, distinct bands on gel electrophoresis). The whole transcriptome sequencing library was prepared, and Illumina HiSeq sequencing was carried out with the assistance of Kangce Technology Co., Ltd. After sequencing was completed, the raw sequencing data were first filtered to obtain high-quality sequencing data (clean data). Subsequently, the clean data were mapped to the reference genome (GCF_002263795.2_ARS-UCD1.3) to further obtain comprehensive transcript information, and the summary results of sequencing data quality control are shown in Tables S2 and S3. Differential expression analysis was performed on the count matrices for mRNA, lncRNA, circRNA, and miRNA using the DESeq2 R package (v1.40.2). The analysis was based on a negative binomial generalized linear model. Raw counts were normalized using the median-of-ratios method implemented in DESeq2. Differential expression thresholds were set based on the actual data distribution characteristics of each RNA type. Specifically, at the initial threshold of |log2FC| ≥ 1, we observed excessively large numbers of differentially expressed mRNAs and lncRNAs, whereas very few differentially expressed circRNAs and miRNAs were identified. To balance sensitivity and specificity, a less stringent log2FC cutoff (|log2FC| ≥ 1) was used for miRNAs and circRNAs, as even modest expression changes of these stable regulatory RNAs are often biologically meaningful and their numbers were already limited. A more stringent cutoff (|log2FC| ≥ 1.5) was applied to mRNAs and lncRNAs to prioritize stronger signals amid their wider expression ranges and to reduce false positives given their abundant differential counts. A false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 was used uniformly to ensure consistent statistical confidence.

Construction of the co-expression network

Phenotypic data selected for trait-module association analysis included: (1) core quality traits that determine beef flavour, tenderness, and juiciness (IMF content and shear force); (2) production performance and meat efficiency indicators (pre-slaughter live weight, carcass weight, and dressing percentage); and (3) data from different tissue regions (SAT and LDM). Based on the gene expression matrix, a weighted gene co-expression network was constructed using the WGCNA package in R (Pan et al., 2022). Hierarchical clustering was performed on all samples based on genome-wide expression levels to assess outliers. The topological overlap matrix (TOM) was used to calculate gene co-expression similarity coefficients, and the optimal soft threshold (β) was determined using the pickSoftThreshold function to ensure that the constructed gene network conformed to a scale-free topological distribution. Using the aforementioned parameters, a weighted gene co-expression network was constructed, and genes with similar expression patterns were grouped into distinct co-expression modules (Zhang, Sheng, Pan, et al., 2023). The module eigengene (ME) of each module was calculated, and the correlation between these MEs and each phenotypic trait was assessed using Pearson correlation analysis. Modules significantly associated with the target trait were selected as key modules for subsequent analysis using the following criteria: for lncRNA/mRNA, |r| > 0.8 and P < 0.05; for circRNA/miRNA, |r| > 0.7 and P < 0.05 (as no module reached |r| > 0.8). Within these key modules, mRNAs/ncRNAs with module membership (MM) > 0.8 and gene significance (GS) > 0.8 were further selected as trait-associated core mRNAs/ncRNAs. A module-phenotype heatmap was used to visually illustrate the relationships between traits and gene co-expression modules.

Candidate gene identification and target gene prediction

SAT was used as a representative indicator of lipid metabolism and systemic energy homeostasis, while LDM directly reflects intramuscular fat deposition, muscle fiber structure, and meat tenderness. Both intramuscular and subcutaneous adipocytes belong to the white adipocyte lineage and exhibit highly similar processes of differentiation and metabolism. To reduce tissue-specific interference and identify core non-coding RNA regulatory modules with potential cross-tissue relevance, differential expression analysis was performed separately on SAT and LDM, followed by an intersection analysis of the two tissues. Specifically, non-coding RNAs that were significantly differentially expressed in both SAT and LDM were defined as candidate non-coding RNAs involved in the regulation of intramuscular fat deposition.

The overlap between differential expression analysis and WGCNA analysis was used to identify candidate mRNAs and ncRNAs (including miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs) associated with target traits (SAT content, IMF content, shear force, and muscle development). Pearson correlation analysis was performed to explore the associations between the expression levels of the screened mRNAs/ncRNAs and the morphological parameters of muscle fibres, differential amino acid contents, and differential fatty acid contents. Correlation coefficients with |r| > 0.8 and P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Subsequently, for each trait, 2–3 mRNA and ncRNA transcripts were selected from the identified hub RNAs, and the RNA-seq results were validated using real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR).

The STRING database (https://string-db.org/, version 11.5) was used to perform functional protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis, and then Cytoscape 3.7.2 was used to identify the top 10 core proteins based on degree centrality, which were then defined as candidate genes.

Candidate target miRNAs of lncRNAs were predicted through RNAhybrid. Parameters were set such that the binding score was ≥140 and the free energy was ≤−20 kcal/mol, and strict complementarity was also required in the seed region (positions 2–8). The predicted candidate miRNAs were intersected with the trait-related miRNAs from the WGCNA results, and the intersection was taken as the final target miRNAs. Candidate target miRNAs of circRNAs were predicted through miRanda. The parameters were set to a binding score ≥ 140 and free energy ≤−20 kcal/mol, with the additional requirement that the seed region (positions 2–8) be strictly complementary. The predicted candidate miRNAs were intersected with the trait-related miRNAs from WGCNA, and the intersection was taken as the final target miRNAs. Candidate target mRNAs of miRNAs were predicted using RNAhybrid and miRanda and then intersected with trait-related mRNAs from the WGCNA results. The intersecting genes were further analyzed for KEGG pathways using the bioinformatics platform OmicShare Tools (https://www.omicshare.com/tools/). Based on literature reports, genes that have been reported or are enriched in pathways related to fat deposition, lipid metabolism and muscle development were selected as the final target mRNAs.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues using the TRIzol method according to the instructions. The concentration and integrity of RNA were assessed using a full-wavelength microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA) and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. According to the instructions of the reverse transcription kit (TaKaRa, Kyoto, Japan), RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and the expression of mRNA/lncRNA/circRNA was detected by qRT-PCR. circRNA PCR amplification products were analyzed by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis and sent to Sangon Biotech for Sanger sequencing. According to the instructions of the MicroRNA first-strand synthesis and miRNA quantitation kits (TaKaRa, Kyoto, Japan), RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and miRNA expression was measured by qRT-PCR. GAPDH was used as an internal control for mRNA, lncRNA, and circRNA, while U6 was used as an internal control for miRNA. Fat deposition marker genes (lipoprotein lipase (LPL), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4), CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein alpha (C/EBPα), fatty acid synthase (FASN), acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), and stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD)) were analyzed with 6 biological replicates per group and 3 technical replicates per sample. All other mRNAs, miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs were analyzed with 3 biological replicates per group and 3 technical replicates per sample. Each reaction included a no-template control (NTC) and a no-reverse transcriptase control (NRC). Each sample was run in triplicate with a coefficient of variation (CV) for Ct values <1%. Melt curve analysis confirmed a single peak for all amplicons. Primers were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 software with the following parameters: Tm 58–62 °C, GC content 40%–60%, amplicon length 80–200 bp, and absence of non-specific structures. Primer specificity was verified using NCBI Primer BLAST. Primer information is provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Data processing and statistical analysis

All bar charts and box plots were analyzed and generated using GraphPad Prism 7 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Comparisons of single meat quality traits between the two groups were performed using independent samples t-tests. For multiple indicators in the amino acid and fatty acid analyses, multiple t-tests were conducted, and the significance levels were adjusted using the Holm–Šídák method to control for multiple comparisons. Comparisons of myofiber subtype-related genes between the two groups, as well as the validation of differentially expressed mRNAs and ncRNAs, were carried out using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test. All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). *P < 0.05 indicates a statistically significant difference, and **P < 0.01 indicates a highly significant difference.

Results

Analysis of slaughter performance and meat quality traits

To explore the differences in slaughter performance and meat quality between GY and WA, we compared the slaughter weight (SW), carcass weight (CW), and slaughter rate (SR) of the two types of cattle. Additionally, the longissimus dorsi muscle was collected from both breeds for meat quality analysis. As shown in Fig. 1A, the SW, CW, and SR of GY were all lower than those of WA. The meat quality test results showed that GY had higher moisture content and shear force than WA, while it had lower IMF deposition, lower expression levels of fat deposition marker genes (LPL, PPARγ, FABP4, C/EBPα, FASN, ACC, and SCD), and lower drip loss than WA (Fig. 1B–D). Fatty acid analysis was performed using GC–MS on the longissimus dorsi muscles of GY and WA, and a total of 50 fatty acids were identified (20 MUFA, 14 PUFA, and 16 SFA) (Fig. S1). GY showed a lower MUFA/SFA ratio but a higher PUFA/SFA ratio than WA. Among individual fatty acids, GY exhibited lower levels of major saturated (C10:0, C12:00, C14:0, C16:0) and monounsaturated (C16:1, C17:1, C18:1n9c) fatty acids, but higher levels of selected polyunsaturated fatty acids, including C18:3n6, C20:2, 20:3 (n-3), C20:5n3 (EPA), C22:6n3 (DHA), and C22:2, compared to WA. A total of 16 amino acids (7 EAA and 9 NEAA) were identified in the longissimus dorsi muscles of GY and WA. Among EAAs, Lys was the most abundant in GY, followed by Leu, while Met was the lowest. Discussion

Slaughter performance and meat quality jointly determine the economic value of meat products. The WA group exhibited significantly higher pre-slaughter live weight, carcass weight, and dressing percentage than the GY group, indicating superior growth and meat production performance (Wang et al., 2021). The WA group exhibited significantly higher IMF content and lower moisture content than the GY group. This result aligns with the classic principle of the opposing relationship between fat and moisture content within muscle tissue (Hunt et al., 2014; Legako et al., 2015). The molecular basis for this phenotypic change is directly associated with the high expression levels of lipid marker genes in the WA group. Studies in beef cattle indicate that the upregulation of lipid marker genes (such as PPARγ, FABP4, C/EBPα, LPL, FASN, and SCD) promotes IMF deposition by activating lipid synthesis pathways (Moisá et al., 2014; Song et al., 2025). This observation is in line with the higher expression of lipid marker genes in the WA group, suggesting a potential concordance between molecular and phenotypic levels. SF directly impacts meat tenderness. The low SF of the WA group suggests superior tenderness, consistent with the pattern of a positive correlation between IMF content and tenderness in muscle tissue (Hirai et al., 2023). Muscle fibres of the WA group exhibit a smaller diameter and cross-sectional area, resulting in superior tenderness (Chriki et al., 2012). The WA group has a higher proportion of Type I fibres and a lower proportion of Type II fibres, reflecting a predominance of oxidative metabolism. Correspondingly, MYH I gene expression in the WA group is higher, while Type II-related gene expression is lower. This further supports the metabolic characteristics of muscle in the WA group, that is, favouring oxidative metabolism, which promotes meat tenderness and flavour development (Bai et al., 2022). Overall, the WA group exhibits superior growth performance and meat production efficiency, coupled with higher IMF content and a more tender meat texture.

In this study, both groups exhibited significant differences in the fatty acid and amino acid compositions. Regarding meat flavour, the WA group had a higher MUFA/SFA ratio than the GY group. Meat from the WA group contains more medium- and short-chain saturated fatty acids (e.g., C10:0, C12:0) and monounsaturated fatty acids (e.g., C16:1, C18:1 N9C), which serve as precursors for meat aroma compounds (Tan et al., 2025). Meat from the GY group exhibits higher levels of flavour-enhancing amino acids (DAA, SAA) and a greater DAA/TAA ratio, with DAA contributing to umami (Zhu et al., 2025). Conversely, the higher SAA/TAA ratio in meat from the WA group imparts a sweeter taste (Kong et al., 2024). It is noteworthy that no significant differences were observed in the (DAA + SAA)/TAA ratio between the two groups, suggesting comparable contributions from total flavour-enhancing amino acids.

Meat texture is regulated by multiple genes, and the precise identification of candidate genes related to meat texture is crucial. Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis struggles to reveal gene interactions, while WGCNA, although it is capable of constructing regulatory networks, remains susceptible to interference from background genes. The integration of DEGs with WGCNA core genes allows for simultaneous consideration of gene expression differences and collaborative regulation, significantly enhancing screening accuracy (Monterde et al., 2023; Wang, Wang, et al., 2024). In this study, the DEGs+WGCNA approach identified potential mRNAs and ncRNAs influencing SAT, IMF, SF, and muscle development. Notably, STC1, ADAMTS15, and TNNI2 participate in the regulation of adipocyte differentiation and lipid accumulation (Choi et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2023). CRYAB correlates with meat tenderness and marbling characteristics (Picard et al., 2023). MYLPF, TNNI2, and MYH2 play crucial roles in muscle development (Bataille et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2023; Wang, Zhou, et al., 2024. bta-miR-206 promotes muscle differentiation and inhibits lipid accumulation through distinct mechanisms (Zhang, Sheng, Zhang, et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024). miR-196a participates in the regulation of preadipocyte differentiation and adipogenesis (Yang et al., 2024). bta-miR-486 promotes myocyte proliferation and differentiation (Samani et al., 2022). miR-504 inhibits smooth muscle cell contraction (Reddy et al., 2016). VCAN is the host gene of circRNA chr7_83426962_83432176_+. VCAN may participate in the physiological processes associated with lipid metabolism and myofibrillar organization (Piórkowska et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2025). LMO7 is the host gene of circRNA chr12_50842014_50873294_+. circLMO7 plays a key role in muscle development (Wei et al., 2017). Based on the reported functions of the aforementioned genes and ncRNAs, we can further infer that the screened genes and ncRNAs may influence meat quality.

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying meat quality formation, this study systematically predicted the target miRNAs of circRNAs and lncRNAs, as well as the target mRNAs of downstream miRNAs, using sequence complementarity as the core criterion. In the prediction of target miRNAs for circRNAs and lncRNAs, multiple miRNAs are reported to participate in fat deposition. For instance, bta-miR-193b targets the ACSS2/AKT axis, thereby regulating bovine adipocyte differentiation (Kang et al., 2020). bta-miR-10167-3p can inhibit bovine adipocyte differentiation (Hu et al., 2023). bta-miR-877 targets PEG10, thereby inhibiting triglyceride synthesis in mammary epithelial cells (Xia et al., 2021). In the prediction of miRNA target mRNAs, functional enrichment analysis was performed on these candidate mRNAs. The results showed that these candidate target mRNAs were enriched in pathways related to fat deposition, lipid metabolism, and muscle development, such as the MAPK signaling pathway and the Wnt signaling pathway (Wang, Zhou, et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). This further suggests that the screened lncRNAs/circRNAs/miRNAs and their target mRNAs are extensively involved in the core biological processes of meat quality formation. In summary, through target gene prediction, this study preliminarily predicted a framework of ceRNA regulatory networks, providing a clear direction for subsequent functional validation of key regulatory axes.

This study has provided a preliminary screening of the meat quality differences between WA and GY cattle and the associated ceRNA regulatory networks, but several limitations remain. The relatively small sample size constitutes the most significant limitation of this study. This limitation not only restricts statistical power and the generalizability of the findings but also makes it difficult to capture natural biological fluctuations within cattle populations, thereby affecting the accuracy of candidate gene screening, regulatory network construction, and association analyses between phenotypes and molecular mechanisms. Compared with those of similar studies (Bai et al., 2022; Nanaie et al., 2025), the robustness and representativeness of the present findings require further validation. The selection of extreme phenotypes—specifically low IMF content in the GY group and high IMF content in the WA group—may have exaggerated the observed differences between the two groups and introduced potential bias into the study results. Consequently, these findings need to be validated in independent cattle populations with random sampling to ensure their generalizability and reliability. Furthermore, candidate mRNAs were selected solely based on degree centrality within the STRING/Cytoscape network, without biological validation. Moreover, the observed associations between differentially expressed RNAs and meat quality traits are merely statistical correlations rather than direct causal relationships. Given these limitations, these findings require further validation through functional experiments, such as gene overexpression and knockout studies. The ceRNA network's cross-breed universality remains unknown. Future studies should expand sample sizes, include multiple breeds, and perform functional validations.

Conclusions

In summary, GY and WA differed in both slaughter performance and meat quality traits. By integrating whole-transcriptome sequencing with phenotypic data, this study screened out a set of potential candidate mRNAs and ncRNAs associated with SAT deposition, IMF deposition, SF, and muscle development. Additionally, several putative regulatory networks were predicted, including miRNA-mRNA, lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA, and circRNA-miRNA-mRNA. It is important to emphasize that these findings are exploratory and based entirely on computational predictions. The proposed networks and candidate RNAs should be interpreted as testable hypotheses that provide a foundational basis for future experimental validation.
