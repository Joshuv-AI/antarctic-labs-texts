Preoperative loop length determination for mitral valve repair by 4-dimensional computed tomography
Authors: Kazumasa Tsuda, Naoki Washiyama, Masahiro Hirano, Yuko Ohashi, Ken Yamanaka, Yuki Takeuchi, Kenji Kishita, Norihiko Shiiya
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIn the loop technique for mitral valve repair, the loop bundles are usually created during cardiac arrest after chordal length measurements, which seems time-consuming and less reproducible. To address this issue, we determined the loop length preoperatively using 4-dimensional computed tomography.MethodsThe loop length was determined on the basis of the distance from the papillary muscle head to the free margin of nonprolapsing leaflet corresponding to the prolapsed leaflet, to which the loops would be secured. Measurements were made on the commissural and long-axis views created by a medical image post-processor in the late systolic phase. This technique was used in consecutive 45 patients undergoing mitral valve repair with the loop technique since April 2021.ResultsA total of 55 loop bundles were created in 45 patients; in 10 cases loop bundles were fixed to both anterior and posterior papillary muscles. There were 31 posterior, 6 anterior, and 8 bileaflet prolapse. The loop length was set at 16 to 26 mm (median 19 mm). Mitral valve repair was successfully completed in all patients, and the loop bundles of predetermined length were used successfully in 42 patients (93.3%). Postoperative echocardiography revealed none/trace regurgitation in 41 and mild regurgitation in 4. There was no hospital mortality or major postoperative complication. During 4 to 35 months follow-up (median 10 months), no case required reintervention for the mitral valve.ConclusionsPreoperative measurements using 4-dimensional computed tomography can accurately and reproducibly predict the required loop length for mitral valve repair.

Perspective

In the MICS era, shortening crossclamp time becomes increasingly important because MICS is reportedly associated with prolonged crossclamp time. This is especially true for trainee surgeons and small-volume institutions. In this regard, our novel method for preoperative loop length measurement offers specific advantage to improve the outcomes of MICS mitral valve repair.

Mitral valve (MV) repair is the gold standard treatment for degenerative mitral regurgitation (MR). Although traditional MV repair techniques relied mostly on resecting diseased leaflet tissue, neochordal repair has recently been described because of its versatility and widespread applicability to multisegmental posterior, anterior, and bileaflet prolapse.1, 2, 3 These procedures provide better leaflet mobility and coaptation of the MV leaflet than resection and suturing, which is associated with a low MV pressure gradient.4

The most crucial point of neochordal repair is determination and adjustment of the appropriate loop length. Because adjusting the slippery expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE) neochordae to the desired length during knot-tying may be difficult, the loop technique has gained attention.5 In this technique, the chordal length after repair was adjusted by the loop length and the point of loop fixation. Opell and Mohr6 have reported that, although the loop technique with ePTFE is easy and reproducible, the problem of determining appropriate length remains. In addition, the loop length is traditionally determined intraoperatively, which requires extra time of cardiac arrest.

To address these issues, we have introduced 4-dimensional computed tomography (4D-CT) for the preoperative measurement of the loop length. We report the technique and efficacy of our novel measuring method of the loop length for MV repair using 4D-CT.

Methods

Among the 82 patients who underwent MV repair for severe degenerative MR between April 2021 and March 2024, 45 patients underwent preoperative 4D-CT for comprehensive MV and papillary muscle assessment and determination of the loop length. In the remaining 37 patients, preoperative 4D-CT loop length evaluation was not employed because resection-suture repair was planned in 28 and contraindications for 4DCT were present in 9.

We measured the loop length using the images of preoperative 4D-CT. A 256-slice CT scanner (Revolution CT; GE HealthCare) and the medium smooth convolution algorithm were used to acquire data sets with a slice thickness of 0.625 mm (reconstruction increment 0.625 mm) in every 10% step of the R-R interval (from 0% to 90%) and an image matrix of 512 × 512 pixels. The CT protocol included heart rate control by a short-acting oral b-blocker to minimize possible artefacts, and bolus injection of 35 to 70 mL of iomeprol (Iomeron 350; Bracco) at a flow rate of 2.5 to 5.0 mL/s followed by a 40- to 45-mL saline flush. Dedicated multiplanar planes were reconstructed by medical image post-processor (Synapse Vincent; Fujifilm).

Steps to Determine the Loop Length

First, 2-dimensional commissural and long-axis views of the MV combined with a 3-dimensional image of the left ventricle were created (Figure 1, A, and Figure 2, A). Then, leaflet and papillary muscle morphologies were assessed (Video 1). The repair technique was planned, including the number of loop bundles and the papillary muscle head to which the bundle would be placed. The center of the segment of prolapsing leaflet edge was determined. On the long-axis view crossing the papillary muscle tip and the edge of nonprolapsed leaflet, the distance between these 2 points was measured at the systolic phase (mostly 30% of cardiac cycle) (Figure 1, B and C, and Figure 2, B). Loop bundles of the determined length were created before surgery. Loop Technique for MV Repair

Using the Shibata Chordae System (Geister Inc), we prepared loop bundles from a CV-4 ePTFE suture (Gore-Tex; W. L. Gore & Associates) and a felt pledget. The number of loops per bundle depended on the extent of prolapsed leaflet. We usually use 2 loops per each of the following segments: A1, A2 lateral, A2 medial, A3, P1 (including anterior commissural leaflet), P2 lateral, P2 medial, and P3 (including posterior commissural leaflet). Eventually, the number of loops was more than 2 for each loop bundle.

The loop bundles were fixed 3 to 5 mm below the tip of the corresponding papillary muscle. For the anterior leaflet, the loops were fixed on the edge of the prolapsed leaflet, whereas they were fixed at approximately 1 to 3 mm away from the edge for the posterior leaflet. When the prolapsed posterior leaflet was large, the loops were fixed on the atrial aspect of the leaflet at 15 mm away from the posterior annulus to avoid the systole anterior motion (SAM).7 The loops were fixed to avoid crossing the midline5; the loops from the posteromedial papillary muscle were not fixed to the anterolateral side of the leaflet, and vice versa. We used a 5-0 monofilament figure-of-eight suture for loop fixation.

Statistical Analysis

All data were prospectively collected in an institutional database and were analyzed with the SPSS statistical software (version 20.0; SPSS Inc). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables are presented as proportions. The paired t test was used to evaluate the differences between preoperative and follow-up data. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to analyze the time-to-event data.

Postoperative Complications and Outcomes

There was no operative mortality. One patient who underwent right minithoracotomy approach developed surgical-site infection. There were no other postoperative complications including re-exploration for bleeding, permanent pacemaker implantation, myocardial infarction, respiratory failure, renal failure requiring dialysis, or neurologic complications. Median length of intensive care unit and hospital stay were 1 and 8 days, respectively. No patient had more than mild MR at the time of discharge. At a median follow-up of 10 months (range, 4-35 months), no patients underwent reoperations, and aggravation to moderate MR was noted 30 months postoperatively in 1 patient. Therefore, the freedom from greater than mild MR was 100% at 24 months. There was a significant reduction in both the left ventricular end-diastolic volume and the left atrial volume postoperatively (Table 3). Comment

The neochordae reconstruction for MV repair for degenerative disease had gained attention because it is reportedly associated with a low MV pressure gradient thanks to the better leaflet mobility.1, 2, 3, 4 Among the techniques of neochordae reconstruction, the loop technique has an advantage of the ease of adjusting the neochordal length because shortening of slippery ePTFE neochordae during knot-tying can be avoided.

Determination of the loop length is a crucial point in the loop technique. Opell and Mohr6 developed a caliper device for measuring the distance between the papillary muscle and the nonprolapsing leaflet edge intraoperatively. Shibata and colleagues8 reported that the length of normal chordae adjacent to the torn or elongated chordae was measured as a reference for the loop length. When the entire leaflet is prolapsing and there is no normal segment to be used as a reference, the distance from the papillary muscle head to the annulus can be used as an alternative.9 However, these techniques seem to be less reproducible in the arrested flaccid heart and prolong cardiac arrest time. In addition, agreement between the length measured during cardiac arrest and the length in systole remains undetermined.

To address these issues, we introduced preoperative measurement of the loop length using 4D-CT. Our technique allows reproducible measurements in systole and does not prolong myocardial ischemia. Among the 45 patients who underwent preoperative 4D-CT, the loop bundles with predetermined length were successfully used in 93%. In the minimally invasive cardiac surgery (MICS) era, shortening crossclamp time seems more important because MICS is reportedly associated with prolonged crossclamp time compared with conventional median sternotomy approach.10,11 This is especially true for trainee surgeons and small-volume institutions12 and to improve the outcomes of MICS MV repair.13 In these aspects, our novel measurement method offers specific advantage.

Another advantage of our technique is that it allows to evaluate the papillary muscle morphology. Neochordal fixation to the left ventricle is also crucial for successful MV repair. Using 4D-CT, we can preoperatively determine the papillary muscle head to which the loop bundle should be fixed. Such information is useful for every technique of MV repair.

Makhdom and colleagues14 reported the use of preoperative transesophageal echo for pre-measuring the expected loop length, and concluded that measurements can be reproducible and standardized. However, its quality depends on the skill of echocardiologists. In addition, transesophageal echo is usually stressful to the patients, although this should be balanced against the risk of radiation exposure from CT.

The loop length determined in this way may be short in the actual practice because we fix the loop bundle at 3 to 5 mm below the tip of papillary muscle. In addition, the loop is fixed 1 to 3 mm away from the leaflet edge for the posterior leaflet. Park and colleagues15,16 showed in the ex vivo experiment of posterior leaflet repair that, compared with the optimal length neochordae, regurgitation fraction remained similar when neochordae became shorter, whereas regurgitation was increased as they became longer. Therefore, shorter neochordae seem permissible at least for posterior leaflet repair.

The predetermined loop length does not take into account the changes in MV geometry by ring annuloplasty, which may result in suboptimal outcomes including SAM when excessive leaflet tissue is present. Therefore, this issue needs to be addressed separately. Moving the point of loop fixation away from the leaflet edge seems to be a promising solution, which has been successful in our functional leaflet height reduction technique.7

This study has several limitations inherent to the single-center retrospective observational study. Because we did not compare outcomes of this novel method with those of other techniques, we could not evaluate the impact of this technique on cardiac arrest time, early outcomes, and durability of MV repair. In addition, the median follow-up was only 10 months. We could not identify the risk factors for failure because of small sample size and a small number of events. 4D-CT assessment was performed by a single attending surgeon who analyzed numerous CT findings before applying this technique to clinical practice. Therefore, the learning curve remains unclear. Further studies are necessary to evaluate the reproducibility of this novel technique.

In conclusion, our novel method for loop length determination using 4D-CT is reproducible, accurate, and applicable to multisegmental MR (Figure 3).
