Value of implantable loop recorders in detecting atrial fibrillation for stroke prevention: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mahrokh Janmohamadi, Amirreza Peyrovinasab, Seyedhesamoddin Khatami, Sepehr Moghaddam, Seyed Mohammad Forouzannia, Seyed Ali Forouzannia, Mohammadsadegh Faghihi, Arash Sarveazad, Seyedeh Romina Rafiei Alavi, Mostafa Hosseini, Mahmoud Yousefifard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThough undiagnosed in many patients, especially those with cryptogenic stroke, atrial fibrillation (AF) is a key cause of ischaemic stroke. By allowing extended cardiac monitoring, implantable loop recorders (ILRs) may help enhance AF identification and secondary stroke prevention. Compared with standard therapy, this meta-analysis and systematic review assesses the effectiveness of ILRs in identifying AF and preventing recurrent stroke.MethodsWe searched Web of Science, Scopus, Embase and PubMed up to September 2024 following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses recommendations. Included were observational studies comparing ILR with conventional therapy in stroke or high-risk patients as well as randomised controlled trials (RCTs). AF detection, recurrent stroke and mortality were primary outcomes. Random-effects models were used to compute pooled HRs with 95% CIs. We looked at publication bias and heterogeneity.ResultsIncluded were 12 studies with four RCTs and eight cohorts. Compared with controls, ILR use was linked to a much greater AF detection rate (HR=3.13, 95% CI 1.97 to 4.97). Embolic events fell insignificantly (HR=0.81, 95% CI 0.65 to 1.02) and mortality showed no change (HR=1.01, 95% CI 0.77 to 1.31). Subgroup analysis showed more AF detection in RCTs than in cohort studies (p=0.035). Although no major publishing bias was found, heterogeneity was considerable.ConclusionBy greatly enhancing AF detection in patients who had a stroke and at high risk, ILRs did not help to prevent secondary strokes. Therefore, they did not have enough efficacy in lowering recurrent stroke and death. Larger, more comprehensive studies are still required to clarify long-term therapeutic advantages and maximise patient selection.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251051946.

Introduction

Stroke, a significant global health issue, is recognised as the second leading cause of permanent neurological impairments and mortality.1 2 Atrial fibrillation (AF) is responsible for about 20% of ischaemic strokes. However, the aetiology of approximately 20–40% of ischaemic strokes and transient ischaemic attacks (TIA) remains unexplained and is classified as cryptogenic stroke.1 3 Currently, undiagnosed AF is considered one of the potential aetiologies of cryptogenic strokes. Occurrence of new AF in patients with stroke history is correlated with an increased risk of subsequent strokes.4 Furthermore, a high CHA2DS2-VASc score is an important risk factor for the occurrence of secondary stroke in individuals diagnosed with AF.5 Therefore, AF monitoring after stroke with undetermined sources or suspected cardioembolic strokes is critical for recurrent stroke prevention.6 According to a recent hypothesis, detecting AF through monitoring and initiating anticoagulant therapy may decrease the risk of recurrent ischaemic strokes.3 4 7 AF can remain undetected and subsequently untreated in a significant proportion of patients who had a poststroke during short-term cardiac monitoring as a result of its lack of symptoms.2 3 Prolonged cardiac monitoring strategies include insertable cardiac monitors, such as implantable loop recorder (ILR), and continuous external monitoring methods.

Designed for long-term electrocardiographic (ECG) recording, ILRs are tiny, subcutaneous cardiac monitoring tools. Patients with unexplained syncope and cryptogenic stroke, especially AF, are mostly those who use them.8 ILRs allow earlier and more dependable AF detection than traditional short-term monitoring techniques by constantly tracking heart rhythms.9 Early detection of AF is essential since it enables quick start of anticoagulant treatment, hence lowering the likelihood of recurrent stroke and related death.10 These devices automatically record and retain ECG data, therefore capturing arrhythmic episodes—including asymptomatic AF—which could otherwise go unnoticed.11

Continuous cardiac monitoring is regarded as an appropriate strategy for diagnosing AF and preventing recurrent strokes, as advocated in the guidelines of the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association.3 4 6 One of the major challenges in continuous cardiac monitoring is determining the optimal duration for rhythm monitoring.5 Recent studies have demonstrated that implantable cardiac monitors have a greater impact on the detection of AF compared with short-term cardiac monitoring in individuals with a history of stroke.4 6 However, some of the disadvantages of insertable cardiac monitors include the high cost and their invasive nature, which faced its implementation with some limitations, and it is not obvious whether they can impact positively their related outcomes.7 The objective of the current systematic review and meta-analysis is to compare the detection rate of AF, its impact on mortality reduction and prevention of secondary stroke in individuals with a history of stroke or at high risk, using ILRs versus usual care.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis was reported following the guidelines set forth by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) criteria12 and registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251051946). This investigation aimed to evaluate the current evidence involving human subjects regarding the efficacy of ILRs in identifying AF for the purpose of stroke prevention. The PICO framework was organised in the following manner: the population (P) consisted of studies involving patients with AF; the intervention (I) entailed the implantation of an ILR; the comparison (C) was made against a control group that either did not receive the intervention or underwent standard treatment; and the outcomes (O) evaluated included the incidence of stroke.

Search strategy

A thorough search was performed across the Medline (via PubMed), Embase, Scopus and Web of Science databases up to 30 April 2025. The search terms were derived from MeSH and Emtree phrases linked to ‘implantable loop recorder’ and ‘atrial fibrillation’ along with their synonyms. The search technique was improved through expert consultation. A thorough search was conducted using Google and Google Scholar, along with an examination of citations from relevant papers to uncover any potentially overlooked studies. Details pertaining to the inquiries can be found in online supplemental material 1. Furthermore, grey literature, citation and reference tracking from the identified articles were scrutinised to identify any potentially overlooked studies.

Eligibility criteria

All clinical trials and observational studies evaluating the diagnostic value of ILR in patients with AF for stroke prevention and its effects were included, irrespective of the subject’s sex, age and country. As a result, we eliminated studies that did not use ILR as the intervention. In addition, case reports, case series, abstracts, comments, studies that lacked a control group, animal studies, studies that did not report stroke as an outcome model, protocols, studies that failed to report a desired outcome or lacked adequate data, duplicate studies, meta-analyses and reviews were excluded.

Screening and data collection

The collected records from the systematic search were imported into Endnote V.X9 software (Thomson Reuters, Toronto, Ontario, Canada). In the initial screening stage, after eliminating duplicate reports, two independent evaluators assessed the titles and abstracts of the collected papers to pinpoint potentially relevant studies. The complete texts of the chosen articles were analysed, the inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied and those articles that met the criteria were included. An examination of the grey literature (Google and Google Scholar) was conducted to guarantee that no papers were missed. The data were organised into a checklist following the PRISMA guidelines.12 Throughout all phases, the two reviewers remained unaware of each other’s evaluations. The collected data included information regarding the study design, sample size and characteristics, AF therapy regimen, ILR device, control method, follow-up duration and outcome measurement tests for AF, as well as details on embolic events, mortality and major bleeding incidents. In instances where multiple papers employed the same dataset, we opted for the article that featured the largest sample size or the longest follow-up duration. We contacted the corresponding author to obtain data that were not presented in the article. All disagreements were resolved through discussions with the third reviewer.

Risk of bias assessment and the level of evidence

The evaluation of bias risk in human research was conducted independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool for randomised clinical trials and the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies.13 14 The two authors engaged in discussions to address and resolve any disagreements that arose. Furthermore, the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) method was employed to evaluate the quality of evidence.15 Disputes were addressed through the involvement of an additional reviewer.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA V.18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). The investigations were organised and distilled according to the categorisation of documented results. The HR, along with their 95% CI, was calculated for each sample and was subsequently aggregated when feasible, including the count of intervention and control arms. A random-effects model meta-analysis was performed only when data were available from at least three separate analyses. Subgroup analyses and meta-regression were performed to determine the probable source of heterogeneity. A priori, subgroup analyses and meta-regression were decided to perform, but were not applicable for variables except study design, incorporating a robust number of trials that covered various study designs, patient settings, ILR devices, control methods and outcomes. Subsequent meta-regressions were performed to identify potential sources of heterogeneity. The I2 statistic and χ2 test were employed to evaluate statistical heterogeneity, where p<0.1 or I2>50% signified notable heterogeneity. The sensitivity analysis of the patient setting was also implemented by eliminating two different studies to assess the robustness of the model and examine its impact on the effect sizes. The assessment of publication bias was conducted through the utilisation of funnel plots and the modified Egger’s test as suggested by Doleman et al.16

Results

Study selection

As illustrated by the PRISMA flow diagram (figure 1), the initial search yielded a total of 5296 records (1799 from Scopus, 690 from Web of Science, 1970 from Embase and 837 from PubMed). After eliminating duplicates and screening articles based on titles and abstracts, 76 potentially relevant studies were assessed for full-text review. Among these, 64 articles met the exclusion criteria and were removed. Eventually, 12 full-text articles met the inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-analysis.1717

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram of the screening process.

Study characteristics

Out of 12 studies, four were randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and eight were cohort studies. The earliest study was published in 2014, with the remaining studies being published between 2019 and 2024. The majority of studies included patients with a history of stroke or TIA, with the exception of two studies that enrolled high-risk or postoperative AF populations and were included only in the sensitivity analysis. A total of 72 846 patients were included in the final analysis, of whom 34 516 (47%) were male. The overall mean age of patients ranged from 59.53 years to 75.64 years. The ILR arms comprised 14 249 patients (19.5% of the pooled cohort), of whom 7169 (50.3%) were male. The mean age of patients in ILR groups ranged from 61 years to 76.06 years. In the control groups, a total of 47 748 patients (65.5%) were included and 22 380 (46%) of them were male. The mean age in control groups ranged from 59 to 75.27.

The outcomes were assessed according to the following: all 12 studies reported AF detection, 11 mortality, 11 embolic events and 4 bleeding. In all 12 studies, participants diagnosed with AF received oral anticoagulant therapy. Follow-up duration ranged from 12 months to 60 months.

Six studies used short-term monitoring as the control group, while four studies employed extended cardiac monitoring. In two studies, both short-term and extended monitoring were used as control groups. The summary of baseline characteristics is presented in table 1.

Risk of bias in studies

The quality of cohort studies was assessed using NHLBI tool in table 2. All studies demonstrated a low risk of bias in most domains regarding well-defined study objectives, clear definition of the study population, adequate participation rates, clearly defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, measurement of the exposure prior to the outcome, sufficient follow-up period, well-defined outcome measurement, loss to follow-up of less than 20% and adjustment for confounders. However, three domains of exposure levels, exposure assessment and blinding were not applicable to all of the studies. Sample size justification was not reported in any of the studies, and one study did not define exposure measures. Finally, the overall risk of bias assessment was considered high risk in one study and low risk in the remainder.

Meta-analysis results

Loop recorder efficacy in AF detection

In a pooled analysis of seven studies, the HR for AF detection in patients who underwent ILR compared with the control group was 3.13 (95% CI 1.97 to 4.97). This indicates a 213% increase in AF detection among patients with a history of stroke who underwent ILR (figure 2).

In the case of subgroup analysis, we found the source of its heterogeneity related to study design which is available in table 4. The subgroup analysis and meta-regression were performed due to considerable heterogeneity. The sources of heterogeneity were determined through a sufficient number of studies. The subgroup analysis and meta-regression based on study design revealed that cohort studies (HR=1.98, 95% CI 1.15 to 3.43, p=0.014; I²=92.38%) resulted in a lower detection rate of AF compared with RCTs (HR=4.45, 95% CI 2.64 to 7.48, p<0.0001; I²=56.94%) (meta-regression, p=0.035).

Loop recorder efficacy in embolic events

Increased use of ILR was correlated with a non-significant reduction in risk of embolic event occurrence. The pooled HR for the occurrence of all embolic events in patients who underwent ILR compared with those in control groups was 0.81 (95% CI 0.65 to 1.02, p=0.07; I2=0) (figure 2).

Loop recorder efficacy in mortality

There was no significant difference in the risk of all-cause mortality between patients who underwent ILR and those in the control groups. The pooled HR for all-cause mortality in patients with ILR compared with the control group was 1.01 (95% CI 0.77 to 1.31), showing no significant increase or decrease in the risk of mortality associated with ILR implementation (figure 2).

The studies were categorised and compared based on the follow-up duration for ILR and non-ILR groups; in addition, the monitoring methods used in each group and the AF detection results at each follow-up interval were reviewed, all of which are presented in table 5. Grouping the 12 studies by follow-up length highlights a clear time-dependent gradient in AF yield: short-term investigations (≤12 months) already show a twofold to fourfold higher detection with ILRs, but this disparity widens in mid-term studies—reaching roughly threefold by 24–36 months—and becomes most pronounced in long-term cohorts, where continuous implantable monitoring uncovers more than twice the AF burden observed with conventional strategies even after 5–6 years. This pattern underscores that the incremental benefit of ILRs is modest in the first months but accumulates progressively, suggesting that study-level heterogeneity in follow-up duration is a major driver of between-trial differences in AF detection rates and should be accounted for in pooled analyses.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis, as shown in figure 3, was conducted by omitting the two studies that involved high-risk patients for AF or postoperative AF rather than stroke/TIA groups in order to assess the robustness of main results. The practically same impact sizes following reanalysis confirmed the robustness of the findings. With overlapping CIs, the HRs for AF detection (3.49), embolic events (0.86) and death (1.10) matched the first analysis. Exclusion produced no notable changes in point estimates or heterogeneity metrics, as shown by the forest plots. This consistency supports the validity of findings on the function of ILRs in secondary stroke prevention by implying that the observed treatment effects are dependable and not significantly affected by changes in patient selection criteria.

Publication bias

Funnel plots were constructed for outcomes of AF, mortality, embolic events and bleeding to evaluate potential publication bias. Egger’s test revealed no significant publication bias for studies reporting HR for outcomes of AF (p=0.18), death (p=0.07) or embolic events (p=0.78). Certainty of evidence

The level of evidence was assessed using GRADE criteria. There was moderate certainty regarding the effect of ILR on AF detection and preventing embolic events due to serious imprecision. However, the level of evidence was rated as low due to notable heterogeneity and serious imprecision found in the effect of ILR on mortality rates. In summary, the overall level of evidence ranged from low to moderate. Table 6 displays the certainty of evidence regarding the effect of ILR in stroke prevention.

Discussion

The current systematic review and meta-analysis comprised eight cohort studies and four RCT studies in which the participants were assigned to ILR groups and non-ILR groups. According to the findings, the pooled HR for diagnosis of AF in ILR groups compared with non-ILR groups is about 3.13 times greater, respectively. This indicates the superiority of ILR in AF detection. However, ILR did not improve the management of the post-AF stroke or reduce the recurrent stroke compared with usual care. In addition, there was no significant difference in mortality rates between ILR and usual care.

A recent meta-analysis by Kiriaki Mavromoustakou et al22 also focused specifically on cryptogenic stroke and showed that ILR-based monitoring progressively increased AF detection up to 26% at 3 years, with older age and higher CHA₂DS₂-VASc scores predicting detection; however, that study lacked a parallel control arm and did not examine mortality, so it could not fully clarify whether increased detection translated into tangible clinical benefit.17 In contrast, our systematic review and meta-analysis included both cryptogenic and non-cryptogenic stroke populations, incorporated four randomised trials alongside cohort studies, directly compared ILRs with external monitoring and demonstrated that although ILRs nearly triple AF detection, this higher diagnostic yield alone has not yet resulted in significant reductions in recurrent embolic events or mortality.

However, most of the studies do not have adequate power to consider recurrent stroke as a primary outcome. Additionally, eight studies did not report any OR, HR orRR for the secondary outcome of preventing recurrent strokes. Therefore, further RCTs are required to accurately assess the effect of ILR on secondary stroke prevention.

Although ILR is indicated for many clinical conditions, there are some disadvantages including its invasive procedure and expensive cost.7 Eligible patients for implementation of ILR may have concerns about complications of the procedure which might be minor or serious adverse effects. Recent studies reported incision site haemorrhages, pain, site infection,5 incision site irritation and inflammation as minor adverse effects of ILR procedure.17 For instance, the PER DIEM study conducted by Buck et al reported that 14 patients (9.3%) experienced more than one serious adverse event.17

Although ILR has a significant effect on AF detection, its effectiveness in improving the management of post-AF strokes remains unclear. Moreover, further investigation is required for the assessment of the correlation between AF detection and secondary stroke prevention.

Recently, two meta-analyses were published that included only three RCT studies. These meta-analyses differ from our meta-analysis in some significant aspects. First, despite previous meta-analyses being conducted only in RCT studies, our meta-analysis merged eight cohort studies and four RCT studies that met inclusion criteria. Second, we used HR instead of RR, because for time series data HR is more informative than RR/OR.

The meta-analysis conducted with Ko et al on three RCTs demonstrated that AF detection was significantly higher in ILR groups compared with usual care groups (pooled OR=5.8, 95% CI 3.2 to 10.2, p<0.001). Additionally, the study reported that the reduction in secondary stroke did not significantly differ between ILR and non-ILR groups (pooled OR=0.9), which was aligned with our results, too.23

In contrast to our meta-analysis and that of Ko et al, the meta-analysis by Huang et al (which included the three aforementioned RCTs) reported RRs for AF detection and recurrent stroke. Compared with non-ILR groups, the ILR groups slightly reduced the risk of recurrent stroke (pooled RR=0.72), which is consistent with our findings.24

In comparison to ILRs, recent studies suggest that smartwatches and smartphone-based single-lead ECGs can detect atrial arrhythmias with reasonable effectiveness and without procedural risks; for example, in patients with congenital heart disease, weekly smartwatch ECG recordings detected arrhythmias almost as early as ILRs but with a lower cumulative detection rate (24% vs 48%) and no implantation-related complications.25 Similarly, while ILRs continue to offer the highest AF detection yield due to continuous, long-term monitoring, smartwatches provide a scalable, patient-friendly alternative that shows promising diagnostic accuracy but still requires more evidence to confirm its impact on long-term stroke prevention.26

Our meta-analysis has several limitations. First, there was considerable variability in study design, stroke subtypes and the method of rhythm monitoring used for control groups. In terms of study design, eight studies were cohort studies, while four were RCTs, which led to stratified analysis to investigate potential differences of risk of bias between RCTs and cohort studies. In terms of stroke history, each study included different stroke subtypes as follows: four studies included patients with a history of stroke, three studies included participants with a history of cryptogenic stroke or TIA and two studies included patients with a history of ischaemic stroke or cryptogenic stroke. In addition, two studies recruited patients at high risk of stroke. The sensitivity analysis, therefore, verified that these variations in patient settings did not noticeably change the combined effect sizes, hence validating the robustness of the findings. The methods used for control groups varied in studies as follows: some studies used ECG, some used Holter monitoring and the others used external continuous monitoring. Second, some studies reported adjusted ratios, while others reported unadjusted ratios; consequently, we pooled adjusted HRs with no meaningful unadjusted HRs. Third, high heterogeneities were found for pooled HR of AF detection, recurrent stroke and death. However, due to the limited number of studies, we were unable to perform a subgroup analysis and determine the source of heterogeneities, except for AF detection, which was related to its study design. Fourth, the causes of death were not categorised in the studies. Fifth, each study reported only some adverse effects related to ILR implementation. Therefore, due to the insufficient number of studies on each adverse effect, we could not combine the HR of each side effect.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis reveals that ILRs greatly improve AF diagnosis in individuals with past stroke or high-risk profiles, exhibiting a threefold gain over conventional therapy. Although ILRs were linked to a non-significant decrease in recurrent embolic events and no effect on mortality, their better diagnostic capacity emphasises their possible use in directing anticoagulant therapy for secondary stroke prevention. The clinical translation of enhanced AF detection into real stroke recurrence reductions, however, is still in doubt, thereby stressing the necessity of more large-scale randomised trials with long-term follow-up to assess long-term results, cost-effectiveness and best patient selection. Refining guidelines and optimising the therapeutic advantages of ILRs in stroke preventive efforts will depend on how well we address the shortcomings of present evidence, including heterogeneity in trial designs, stroke subtypes and monitoring methods.
