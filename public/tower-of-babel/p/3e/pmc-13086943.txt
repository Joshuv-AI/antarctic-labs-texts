Accelerating the learning process of deep reinforcement learning algorithms in distribution network reconfiguration using an innovative replay method
Authors: Amirhossein Ghaemipour, Habib Rajabi Mashhadi, Seyed Hossein Mostafavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDistribution network reconfiguration (DNR) is one of the most widely employed methods for minimizing distribution network power losses with minimal investment. Most methods used for DNR are based on an accurate model. However, this study employs deep reinforcement learning (DRL), a model-free approach. This study proposes a loop-based method for effectively managing the large action space, which simultaneously utilizes the modified Q-learning algorithm to account for inter-loop coupling effects. Additionally, an innovative replay method is utilized to enhance convergence speed. Our approach has been tested on the IEEE 33-, 69-, and 119-bus distribution networks. The simulation results indicate that the proposed approach achieves significant superiority compared to DNR’s previous methods, such as metaheuristic and mathematical techniques, in terms of computational time, as well as final distribution network power loss and voltage deviation.Keywords: Distribution network reconfiguration, Deep reinforcement learning, Experience replaySubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Given the growing population and human needs, meeting those needs is one of the most critical responsibilities. One of their most essential needs is electrical energy. The sectors that supply this need include generation, transmission, and distribution. The distribution sector is the closest to the consumer, which is very sensitive. Consequently, delivering uninterrupted power to consumers is one of the most crucial tasks of the distribution network (DN). Numerous studies have been conducted on this sector. Electricity distribution companies strive to minimize power losses, ensuring a reliable electricity supply to consumers without interruptions. Various plans are made to minimize DN loss. However, distribution network reconfiguration (DNR) is one of the most effective methods for reducing DN loss at a significantly lower investment.

There are two types of switches in the DN: sectionalizing switches and tie switches. These are utilized for DNR so that the final structure should be radial when operating the DN. The primary challenge in DNR is the large action space, as noted in reference31. The DNR problem aims to open a line in each loop so that the DN does not suffer from issues such as islanding or supply shortages. We also must maximize the discounted reward. In this paper, we use a loop-based method to solve the DNR problem. Furthermore, it is assumed that the control is centralized, where decisions regarding the opening of lines are made by a single entity, like a DN operator, overseeing the process. Since a line must be opened in each loop, a network must be assigned to each loop to estimate the value of opening each line. Initially, all lines are closed, and in each loop, according to the switch values, one switch is opened, and then we proceed to the next loop. This continues until the number of loops is exhausted. The general process of this design is illustrated in Fig. 2. In summary, the proposed method considers a separate DQN with its own buffer for each loop. This approach helps solve the problem of high dimensionality, as each network only deals with a limited number of actions. However, two issues remain: shared actions between loops and the long simulation time. Solutions to these problems are provided in the following sections.

Modified Q-learning for DNR

In standard Q-learning, the target value is calculated using the immediate reward and the maximum state-action value in the next state. This definition is not suitable for a loop-based problem because, as some actions are shared between different loops, standard Q-learning does not account for this overlap. Therefore, to select an action, the maximum allowed state-action value in the next state is used. For example, in the 33-bus DN shown in Fig. 2, line S8 is shared between loop 1 and loop 3. As a result, if the agent in loop 1 selects line S8, it cannot be selected in loop 3. Therefore, during the algorithm’s training and target calculation, the impossibility of selecting this line must also be taken into account. Loss-based experience replay buffer

Although the introduced loop-based modeling and modified Q-learning algorithm can facilitate the convergence to suitable solutions, convergence is slow. Therefore, in this section, we develop a mechanism, the LBER buffer, which, when combined with the aforementioned algorithms, can improve convergence speed.

One disadvantage of the DQN algorithm is that it gives equal importance to all experiences in the replay buffer for training. This problem can be particularly problematic when the action space is big, as high-value experiences may occur only once during the agent’s interaction with the environment and are quickly forgotten. One of the algorithms proposed to overcome this problem is the PER algorithm42. The idea behind PER is to use the TD error for sampling, giving higher priority to experiences with larger TD errors, allowing them to be more likely selected for training. However, reference31 has shown that the performance of PER in DNR can be less effective, and in some cases, its performance is even worse than that of regular DQN. The reason behind this is that since PER, like regular DQN, uses a single buffer and stores all experiences within it, the difference is that it gives a higher chance of selection to experiences with higher TD errors. However, in high-dimensional environments like DNR, this doesn’t have a significant impact, and a new algorithm needs to be proposed.

As mentioned earlier, each time step in the loop-based model uses an independent DQN and its own experience replay buffer. At the last time step, our algorithm checks the final configuration and compares its power losses with those from the last time step buffer. If it is among the best experiences based on the desired criterion, the entire trajectory of the completed episode will be stored in a buffer called the LBER buffer. The LBER buffer has a separate buffer for each time step. Therefore, when training our DQN at each time step, we can use it alongside the main replay buffer for that time step. Essentially, the introduced method shifts the criterion for valuing an experience to the DN’s final structure, which, unlike the PER method, can have a much more significant impact. As a criterion, we utilize the concept of percentile. At the end of each episode, if the final power loss is less than the 5th percentile of losses in the last time-step replay buffer, the entire trajectory will be stored in the LBER buffer. Simulation and results

The proposed method has been evaluated on three standard DNs, namely the IEEE 33-, 69-, and 119-bus DNs, using an Intel i5-14600K CPU, an NVIDIA RTX 2060 Super GPU, and 32 GB of RAM. All proposed methods in this study were executed under identical experimental configurations and hardware settings. In contrast, the computational times reported for other approaches were directly adopted from their respective references and may therefore be influenced by differences in hardware platforms and implementation details. Accordingly, the reported computational-time comparisons are intended to reflect relative efficiency trends rather than absolute runtime superiority.

Due to the inherent stochastic nature of DRL, all reported results in this study were obtained by running each algorithm ten times with different random seeds. The final performance metrics and graphical results presented in this section represent the average values over these ten runs, aiming to enhance the reliability and statistical robustness of the reported outcomes. In the implementation, the DN is modeled as a DRL environment, while the PandaPower package43 is employed for load modeling and power flow analysis. All simulations were conducted in Python, and the proposed method was implemented using the PyTorch framework. All algorithms considered in this study were implemented following Algorithm 2. The primary differences among the evaluated methods lie in the neural network loss function (DQN or DDQN), the network architecture (Dueling or non-Dueling), and the presence or absence of the LBER mechanism.

Discussion

This paper demonstrates the applicability of deep reinforcement learning (DRL) to distribution network reconfiguration (DNR). The distribution network (DN) has a large dimension. Consequently, finding its optimal configuration is a challenge that subjects algorithms to the curse of dimensionality. As a result, Reinforcement learning (RL) alone cannot solve this problem. By combining RL and deep learning, a practical method called DRL is created. The type of modeling and training of the DRL agent is essential in this problem. These are, namely, loop-based action selection by using modified Q-learning and a loss-based experience replay buffer. The achievements of this paper are as follows:

Solving the DNR problem without dimensionality reduction.

Solving the DNR problem without dimensionality reduction.

Testing the proposed method on three well-known DNs, i.e., IEEE 33-, 69-, and 119-bus DNs, and achieving impressive results compared to prior research.

Testing the proposed method on three well-known DNs, i.e., IEEE 33-, 69-, and 119-bus DNs, and achieving impressive results compared to prior research.

The flexibility of the proposed method for DNs with small and large dimensions.

The flexibility of the proposed method for DNs with small and large dimensions.

The computational and training time of the proposed method are better than those of previous research, especially DRL research.

The computational and training time of the proposed method are better than those of previous research, especially DRL research.

Analyzing the results in this article suggests that the proposed method is a practical way to DNR. This can be applied with various objective functions or alongside other modeling techniques.

This paper, like previous research, has limitations. One limitation is that, as DNs’ dimensions increase, the learning process becomes time-consuming. The reason is that this method does not apply dimension reduction. Another limitation is that the loop-based method cannot achieve an optimal policy in a small number of episodes.

In future studies, we will explore different approaches to finding the optimal DN configuration without reducing the problem’s dimensions using DRL algorithms. We will also explore various methods to increase learning speed in DRL algorithms in DNR. Finally, we will also investigate the impact of electric vehicles and their charging and discharging management on the DNR. In general, we aim to accelerate the DRL agent’s learning speed using various modeling techniques, which we will implement under different conditions and reward functions.

Conclusion

This study modeled the DNR as a DRL problem. Furthermore, a loop-based method that utilizes the modified Q-learning algorithm was introduced to open and close the lines. Significantly impacting the algorithms’ faster convergence and making it unnecessary to reduce the action space to solve the problem. Also, to accelerate the agent’s learning, a loss-based experience replay buffer was introduced. The proposed method was applied to IEEE 33-, 69-, and 119-bus DNs. The results showed that the proposed method effectively reduces power loss and decreases the voltage deviation. Compared to previous DNR approaches, the proposed method offers a better computational and training time while maintaining competitiveness in terms of power loss and voltage deviation.
