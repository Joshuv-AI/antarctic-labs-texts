The PTHR1/PKA/CREB1 axis promotes osteosarcoma progression by activating the PVT1/miR-590-3p/AXIN2 ceRNA network to induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition
Authors: Jiaming Zhang, Weishang Li, Fei Liu, Hui Zhang, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteosarcoma (OS) remains a clinically challenging primary bone tumor because of its strong metastatic potential and unsatisfactory outcomes in advanced cases. Parathyroid hormone receptor 1 (PTHR1), a receptor closely associated with bone-related signaling, has not been fully characterized in OS. This study explored whether PTHR1 contributes to OS progression and clarified the downstream regulatory mechanism involved.MethodsPublic GEO datasets, clinical OS specimens, and cultured OS cells were used to assess PTHR1 expression. Stable PTHR1 knockdown and overexpression OS cell models were established using lentiviral vectors. Cell growth, apoptosis, motility, invasiveness, EMT, and tumor growth were evaluated by CCK-8, flow cytometry, Transwell, Western blot, and xenograft assays. The underlying mechanism was explored using dual-luciferase reporter assays, ChIP-qPCR, pathway intervention, and rescue experiments.ResultsPTHR1 was significantly upregulated in OS tissues and cells. PTHR1 overexpression promoted OS cell proliferation, migration, invasion, EMT, and xenograft tumor growth while suppressing apoptosis, whereas PTHR1 knockdown exerted the opposite effects. Mechanistically, PTHR1 activated the cAMP/PKA/CREB1 pathway, leading to transcriptional upregulation of lncRNA PVT1. PVT1 functioned as a competing endogenous RNA by sponging miR-590-3p, thereby relieving miR-590-3p-mediated repression of AXIN2. AXIN2 further promoted EMT and malignant progression, and AXIN2 overexpression partially reversed the inhibitory effects of PTHR1 knockdown.ConclusionPTHR1 promotes OS progression by activating the CREB1/PVT1/miR-590-3p/AXIN2 regulatory axis and enhancing EMT. This five-component signaling cascade may provide potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for OS.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00849-6.Keywords: Osteosarcoma, Parathyroid hormone receptor 1, PVT1, miR-590-3p, AXIN2, Competing endogenous RNA, CREB1, Epithelial-mesenchymal transition

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS) represents a major primary bone malignancy predominantly affecting children and adolescents. Owing to its aggressive biological behavior, OS frequently spreads to distant organs, with pulmonary metastasis being the most common pattern of dissemination [1, 2]. Although surgery combined with chemotherapy has become the mainstay of clinical management, outcomes remain unsatisfactory for patients who develop metastasis or recurrence, and long-term survival has improved only modestly over recent decades [3, 4]. The molecular mechanisms underlying OS progression remain incompletely understood, particularly those associated with tumor heterogeneity, stemness, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), invasion, and metastasis [5, 6]. Current therapeutic strategies have limited efficacy against EMT-driven metastatic OS, and effective molecular interventions targeting this process are still lacking [7]. Recent targeted therapy studies have identified several key signaling pathways involved in OS progression, highlighting potential directions for molecular intervention [8]. In addition, emerging evidence suggests that specific microRNAs and their downstream targets, such as the miR-605-3p/RAF1 axis, may regulate OS growth and chemoresistance [9]. Therefore, identifying critical regulatory factors and elucidating their molecular mechanisms are essential for improving diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis in OS.

PTHR1 is a G protein-coupled receptor involved in skeletal development and calcium-phosphate homeostasis [10]. Recent evidence indicates that abnormal PTHR1 expression occurs in multiple cancers and may facilitate tumor progression by activating downstream pathways, including cAMP/PKA/CREB1 signaling [11, 12]. In osteogenic tumors, dysregulated PTHR1 signaling has been associated with abnormal proliferation, metastasis, and chemoresistance [13–15]. However, the precise function of PTHR1 in OS remains unclear. In particular, whether PTHR1 regulates CREB1 activation and subsequently modulates non-coding RNA expression to promote EMT and metastasis has not been fully elucidated.

Among non-coding RNAs, lncRNAs are increasingly implicated in regulating malignant tumor behavior [16, 17]. PVT1 is a well-characterized oncogenic lncRNA that is upregulated in multiple cancers and can function as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) by sequestering specific microRNAs, thereby relieving repression of their downstream target genes [18–20]. miR-590-3p has been linked to tumor suppression in multiple cancers, while AXIN2, a Wnt pathway component, is associated with EMT regulation [13, 21–23]. Nevertheless, whether PVT1, miR-590-3p, and AXIN2 constitute a functional ceRNA network in OS, and whether this network is regulated by PTHR1-mediated CREB1 activation, remains unknown.

This study investigated whether PTHR1 contributes to OS progression by activating the PKA/CREB1/PVT1/miR-590-3p/AXIN2 pathway to enhance EMT and invasion. Validation of this regulatory cascade offers a new mechanistic perspective on OS metastasis. These findings may further expand current knowledge of EMT-related regulatory networks in OS and identify potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for high-risk patients, thereby offering a theoretical basis for molecular classification and personalized treatment.

Materials and methods

Differential expression analysis

Three OS-related microarray datasets were obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo): GSE12865 (12 OS tissue samples and 2 normal human osteoblasts), GSE126209 (12 OS tissues and 11 adjacent normal tissues), and GSE225588 (6 OS tissues and 6 matched adjacent tissues). All datasets were processed in R software (v.4.3.2) using the Bioconductor limma package (v.3.58.1). Before differential analysis, expression matrices were log₂-transformed and quantile-normalized, and no obvious batch effect was observed. Genes with |log2FC| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Volcano plots and heatmaps were produced using ggplot2 (v.3.4.4) and pheatmap (v.1.0.12), respectively. To further screen core regulatory genes, an additional threshold of |log2FC| ≥ 2 was applied.

ceRNA interaction prediction

Potential regulatory interactions among miRNAs, lncRNAs, and mRNAs were predicted and cross-validated using TargetScan Release 8.0, miRDB version 6.0, miRTarBase version 9.0, and starBase version 3.0. The screening criteria were as follows: context + + score ≤ − 0.2 for TargetScan, score ≥ 80 for miRDB, experimentally validated interactions for miRTarBase, and CLIP-supported interactions with clipExpNum ≥ 1 for starBase. To improve reliability, only interactions supported by at least two databases were retained. The prediction process was performed using standardized R scripts with custom functions.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction and visualization

Based on the STRING database (version 11.5, species: Homo sapiens, minimum interaction score ≥ 0.4), a PPI network of differentially expressed proteins was constructed and visualized using Cytoscape software (version 3.10.0). To identify potential hub genes, the cytoHubba plugin was applied, and the maximal clique centrality (MCC) algorithm was applied to construct the core regulatory network of OS.

Tissue sample collection

A total of 25 patients with OS who underwent surgical treatment at our hospital between January 2022 and June 2023 were enrolled. Paired tumor specimens and adjacent non-tumorous bone tissues were collected for molecular analyses. All cases were confirmed as OS based on clinical imaging and histopathological examination. The inclusion criteria were: (1) no prior chemotherapy or radiotherapy before surgery; (2) sufficient and intact tissue for molecular assays; and (3) signed informed consent permitting the use of samples for research purposes. The exclusion criteria were: (1) recurrent or metastatic disease; (2) presence of other malignant tumors; and (3) inadequate tissue preservation or quality.

Fresh specimens were separated into two parts immediately after collection. One part was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored for RT-qPCR and Western blot (WB) analyses, while the other was fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and used for immunohistochemistry, TUNEL staining, and immunofluorescence assays.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Liaoning Cancer Hospital & Inst (Approval No. 202205K9). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and all procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

RT-qPCR

OS tissue specimens were ground in liquid nitrogen and homogenized on ice using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Cat# 15596026). Cells were washed with PBS and lysed directly in TRIzol. Total RNA was extracted according to the TRIzol protocol and measured with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA purity was assessed using the OD260/280 ratio, and samples with values between 1.8 and 2.0 were used for subsequent analysis.

For reverse transcription, 1 µg of total RNA was used with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Cat#RR047A). For miRNA reverse transcription, the Mir-X miRNA First-Strand Synthesis Kit (Takara, Cat#638313) was employed with stem-loop-specific primers.

qPCR was carried out in a 20 µL reaction system containing SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara, Cat# R420A), gene-specific primers at 10 µM, and diluted cDNA templates. Amplification was performed on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) under the following program: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 34 s. GAPDH and U6 served as endogenous references for mRNA/lncRNA and miRNA quantification, respectively. All reactions were run in triplicate, and each experiment was repeated independently at least three times. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1. Relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot analysis

Tissue samples and treated cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Tissue specimens were homogenized with a glass homogenizer, whereas cells were disrupted by sonication. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for protein analysis. Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit and normalized to 2 µg/µL.

For each sample, equal amounts of protein, approximately 20–30 µg, were combined with 5× SDS loading buffer (Beyotime, Cat# P0015L) and denatured at 100 °C for 5 min. Proteins were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and then transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Cat# IPVH00010) at a constant voltage of 100 V for 60 min.

Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST buffer (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20, pH 7.6) for 1 h at ambient temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies (see Table 1 for details). The next day, membranes were washed three times with TBST for 10 min each and incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse secondary antibodies (ZSGB-BIO, Cat# ZB-2301; 1:5000) for 1 h at ambient temperature.

Protein signals were developed using ECL detection reagent (Thermo, Cat# 32106) and captured with a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP system. Band intensities were analyzed using ImageJ software version 1.8.0. Each experiment was independently repeated three times.

Cell culture and transfection

The human osteoblast cell line hFOB1.19 and the human OS cell lines Saos2, U2OS, HOS, MG63, and SJSA1 were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Shanghai Institute of Life Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM containing 4.5 g/L glucose (Gibco, Cat# 11965092), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Cat# 16000-044), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution, consisting of 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2, and fresh medium was supplied every 72 h.

Stable transfection (Lentiviral Infection)

Lentiviruses were packaged by GeneChem (Shanghai, China) using the pLenti-CMV-Puro vector. The following stable cell lines were generated for in vitro and partially in vivo experiments: ov-PTHR1, ov-NC, sh-PTHR1, sh-NC, sh-PTHR1 + ov-AXIN2, and sh-PTHR1 + ov-NC. Briefly, cells were plated in 6-well plates and transduced at approximately 50% confluence with lentiviral particles in the presence of 8 µg/mL Polybrene. A multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 was used for routine transduction, whereas MOIs of 2, 5, and 10 were tested for dose-response experiments. After incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h, the viral medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. When cell confluence reached approximately 70%, stable clones were selected with 5 µg/mL puromycin for 3–5 days. Transfection efficiency was confirmed by RT-qPCR and WB analysis. Target and control sequences are listed in Table S2.

Transient transfection

Transient transfection was carried out with Lipofectamine™ 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Cells were plated in 6-well plates and transfected when they reached approximately 50% confluence.

For miRNA intervention, 50 pmol of miR-590-3p mimics or inhibitors was transfected using Lipofectamine™ 3000 reagent (2 µL) and P3000 reagent (2 µL). Equivalent amounts of negative control oligonucleotides, including mimics-NC or inhibitor-NC, were used as controls. All oligonucleotides were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China).

For lncRNA or gene overexpression, cells received 2 µg/well pcDNA3.1-CMV plasmids carrying PVT1 or AXIN2, with the empty vector used as the corresponding control. The transfection mixtures were prepared in serum-free Opti-MEM medium (Gibco, USA), and subsequent assays were performed 48 h after transfection.

For rescue experiments, ov-AXIN2 was transiently transfected into stable sh-PTHR1 cells to assess the role of AXIN2 in the PTHR1-mediated regulatory pathway. In addition, miR-590-3p mimics were co-transfected into PVT1-overexpressing cells to validate the functionality of the ceRNA axis. For AXIN2 loss-of-function assays, sh-AXIN2 was transfected into PTHR1-overexpressing cells, with sh-NC used as the corresponding control. For PTHR1 dose-gradient experiments, cells were transfected with 0.5, 1.0, or 2.0 µg/well of PTHR1 expression plasmid to simulate low, moderate, and high expression levels. Expression gradients were verified by RT-qPCR and WB analysis, followed by CCK-8 and Transwell assays.

All transfections were conducted at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Functional and molecular assays were conducted 48 h post-transfection. Each experiment included three independent biological replicates. Sequences of oligonucleotides, plasmid information, and target sites are provided in Table S2.

Cell counting Kit-8 cell proliferation assay

Cell viability was determined using the CCK-8 kit (Dojindo, Japan, Cat# CK04). Stably transfected Saos2 cells were counted by trypan blue exclusion and then plated into 96-well plates at 2 × 103 cells/well in 100 µL medium. Five technical replicate wells were included for each group, and the same well layout was maintained at all detection points. After overnight attachment at 37 °C with 5% CO2, this point was recorded as 0 h. At 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, the culture medium was removed and replaced with 110 µL serum-free DMEM containing 10 µL CCK-8 reagent. After incubation for 1 h at 37 °C, absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a Bio-Rad iMark™ microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA). The assay was performed independently three times, with five technical replicates in each experiment.

Flow cytometry for apoptosis detection

Apoptosis was measured by Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining. Log-phase cells were collected, and 1 × 105 cells from each group were prepared for analysis. After PBS washing, cells were centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was removed. The pellet was then resuspended in 195 µL Annexin V binding buffer (Beyotime, Cat# C1062M), followed by staining with 5 µL Annexin V-FITC (Beyotime, Cat# C1062M) and 10 µL propidium iodide. The mixture was gently mixed and incubated for 20 min at ambient temperature in the dark. Samples were analyzed immediately without additional washing.

For each sample, 10,000 events were recorded using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). FlowJo software version 10.6.2 (TreeStar) was used for data analysis. Cell populations were classified by quadrant gating as follows: Q1, necrotic cells; Q2, late apoptotic cells; Q3, early apoptotic cells; and Q4, viable cells. PBS-treated cells served as negative controls. Each experiment included three biological and three technical replicates.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Cell motility and invasive ability were examined using Transwell inserts containing 8 μm pore-size polycarbonate membranes (Corning, Cat# 3422). Uncoated inserts were used for migration assays. For invasion assays, the upper surface of each membrane was covered with 100 µL Matrigel at a final concentration of 1 mg/mL (Corning, Cat# 356234), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30–45 min to allow gel formation.

Cells were rinsed with PBS, counted, and prepared in serum-free DMEM at 5 × 104 cells/200 µL. The cell suspension was then placed into the upper chamber, and 600 µL complete medium supplemented with 20% FBS was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 48 h, cells remaining on the upper membrane surface were gently removed. The membranes were washed twice with PBS, fixed with anhydrous methanol for 30 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (Solarbio, Cat# G1063) for 20 min, washed three times with PBS, and air-dried.

Five random non-overlapping high-power fields per well were imaged under an inverted microscope at ×200 magnification. Migrated or invaded cells were counted and averaged for statistical analysis. Each condition included three technical and three biological replicates.

ChIP-qPCR assay

Saos2 cell lines carrying the wild-type PVT1 promoter, HRE1, HRE2, HRE3, HRE4, or HRE-mutant PVT1 promoter were established for ChIP-qPCR analysis. Cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 15 min, followed by glycine quenching. Chromatin was sheared by sonication to obtain DNA fragments of 300–500 bp. Immunoprecipitation was performed using an anti-CREB1 antibody (12208-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:500), with control IgG (98136-1-RR, Proteintech, 1:500) as the negative control. Protein A-Sepharose (NCTP58765, Saier) was added, and samples were incubated at ambient temperature for 1 h. After reversal of cross-linking, DNA was purified for qRT-PCR analysis. All qRT-PCR reactions were performed in triplicate. Primer sequences are listed in Table S3.

Tumor xenograft assay in nude mice

Female BALB/c nude mice (Mus musculus), 4 weeks old and weighing 18–22 g, were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. Animal experiments were performed in the SPF-grade Laboratory Animal Center of China Medical University. The mice were housed under controlled conditions, including a 12-h light/dark cycle, temperature of 20–24 °C, and relative humidity of 40–60%, with unrestricted access to food and water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of China Medical University (Approval No. CMUKT2025160).

Mice were randomly divided into five groups, with five mice per group: ov-PTHR1, sh-PTHR1, ov-NC, sh-NC, and sh-PTHR1 + ov-AXIN2. Stable PTHR1-overexpressing or PTHR1-knockdown Saos2 cells were used for subcutaneous implantation. The sh-PTHR1 + ov-AXIN2 group was established to determine whether AXIN2 overexpression could counteract the effects of PTHR1 knockdown in vivo.

For tumor implantation, each mouse was subcutaneously injected in the right axillary region with 1 × 10⁶ cells suspended in 100 µL serum-free DMEM and mixed with 30 µL Matrigel (Corning, Cat# 356234). Tumor size was monitored with a caliper every three days. The longest tumor diameter was defined as L, and the perpendicular shorter diameter was defined as W. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = 0.5 × L × W2. The experimental endpoint was defined as any of the following: tumor volume ≥ 1500 mm3, maximum tumor diameter ≥ 15 mm, obvious necrosis, ulceration or hemorrhage, body weight loss > 20%, marked reduction in appetite or activity, or severe distress such as labored breathing or abnormal posture. On day 28, or earlier if endpoint criteria were reached, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and euthanized by cervical dislocation. Xenograft tumors were excised, weighed, and collected for further analysis.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded xenograft tumor tissues were cut into 5 μm sections. After standard deparaffinization and rehydration, endogenous peroxidase activity was inhibited by treating the sections with 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) at ambient temperature for 10 min. Antigen retrieval was carried out in 0.01 M sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) under high-temperature conditions. After cooling to ambient temperature, sections were rinsed three times with PBS and blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-human Ki-67 primary antibody (rabbit monoclonal, Abcam, Cat# ab16667, 1:200). After washing with PBS, HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (ZSGB-Bio, Cat# ZB-2301, 1:500) was applied for 30 min at 37 °C. DAB detection kit (ZSGB-Bio, Cat# ZLI-9018) was used for color development, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Finally, sections were dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared with xylene, and mounted using neutral resin. For quantification, five non-overlapping high-power fields were randomly selected from each section under a light microscope at ×400 magnification, avoiding areas of necrosis or hemorrhage. At least 200 tumor cell nuclei were evaluated in each field, and brown nuclear staining was defined as Ki-67 positivity. The Ki-67 index was calculated as the percentage of Ki-67-positive cells among total counted cells. H-score was determined using the following formula: H-score = 1 × percentage of weakly positive cells + 2 × percentage of moderately positive cells + 3 × percentage of strongly positive cells, with values ranging from 0 to 300. All sections were assessed independently by two blinded pathologists, and disagreements were resolved by a third investigator.

TUNEL staining

Frozen tumor tissues were sectioned at 5 μm thickness, air-dried at ambient temperature for 30 min, and rinsed three times with PBS. To improve membrane permeability, sections were treated with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, Cat# T8200) for 5 min and then washed again with PBS. TUNEL staining was performed using the One Step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (Beyotime, Cat# C1086). The staining solution containing FITC-labeled dUTP and TdT enzyme was added to each section, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. After washing, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI solution (Beyotime, Cat# C1002) for 5 min at ambient temperature in the dark. Sections were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium (Beyotime, Cat# P0126) and imaged using a Nikon Eclipse C1 fluorescence microscope. Cells showing green fluorescence co-localized with DAPI-stained nuclei were considered TUNEL-positive. Five randomly chosen non-overlapping high-power fields were analyzed from each section at ×400 magnification. The apoptosis index was calculated as the percentage of TUNEL-positive cells among total DAPI-positive cells. Negative controls without TdT enzyme and positive controls pretreated with DNase I were included.

Immunofluorescence staining

Frozen xenograft tumor sections, 5 μm thick, were air-dried at ambient temperature for 30 min, washed three times with PBS, and blocked with 1% BSA for 1 h. Primary antibodies were diluted in PBS containing 1% BSA and applied as follows: Snail (1:200, Abcam, Cat# ab216347), Vimentin (1:200, Abcam, Cat# ab92547), and N-cadherin (1:200, Abcam, Cat# ab76011). Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After PBS washing, Goat anti-Rabbit IgG H&L Alexa Fluor® 488 secondary antibody (1:400, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A-11008) was added, followed by incubation for 1 h at ambient temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI solution (1 µg/mL, Beyotime, Cat# C1005) for 5 min. The sections were then mounted with anti-fade mounting medium (Beyotime, Cat# P0126), and fluorescence images were obtained using a Nikon Eclipse C1 fluorescence microscope under identical exposure settings. For each section, at least five non-overlapping high-power fields at ×400 magnification were randomly selected, excluding necrotic areas and regions with strong autofluorescence. Snail-, Vimentin-, and N-cadherin-positive cells were quantified based on fluorescence signals co-localized with DAPI-stained nuclei. Each group included at least three sections, and five fields were analyzed per section. Quantification was performed independently by two blinded investigators, with discrepancies resolved by a third evaluator.

Immunohistochemical staining for cleaved-caspase-3

Cleaved-caspase-3 expression was detected by immunohistochemistry to evaluate apoptosis in xenograft tumors. Paraffin-embedded tumor sections with a thickness of 5 μm were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and treated with 3% H2O2 (ZSGB-Bio, Cat# ZLI-9012) for 10 min to suppress endogenous peroxidase activity. Antigen retrieval was performed in 0.01 M sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0), followed by cooling and PBS washing. Non-specific binding was blocked with 2% BSA (Solarbio, Cat# A8020). Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit monoclonal anti-cleaved-caspase-3 primary antibody (Abcam, Cat# ab13847; specific for Asp175; 1:200). After washing, HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (ZSGB-Bio, Cat# ZB-2301; 1:500) was applied at 37 °C for 30 min. Chromogenic staining was developed with DAB solution (ZSGB-Bio, Cat# ZLI-9018), and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. The sections were subsequently dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral resin (ZSGB-Bio, Cat# ZLI-9557). Five non-overlapping high-power fields per section were randomly selected under a light microscope at ×400 magnification. Brown-stained positive cells were counted, and the positivity rate was calculated. Image analysis was performed independently by two blinded investigators.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Reporter activity was determined using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega, Cat# E1910). At 24 h after transfection in 6-well plates, the culture medium was removed, and cells were lysed with 200 µL of 1× Passive Lysis Buffer (PLB; Promega). Cell lysates were collected after gentle shaking at ambient temperature for 15 min. For detection, 20 µL of each lysate was added to a white 96-well plate. Firefly luciferase signals were recorded after adding 100 µL Luciferase Assay Reagent II, followed by Renilla luciferase measurement after the addition of 100 µL Stop & Glo® Reagent. Luminescence intensity was measured using a multimode microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1, Agilent) with the accompanying Gen5™ software (version 3.11 or later). Detection parameters were set as follows: integration time, 1 s; gain, automatic; emission wavelengths, default filters for Firefly luciferase at 560 nm and Renilla luciferase at 480 nm; and reading time, 0.5 s per well. Data were exported and analyzed using Microsoft Excel version 16.0 (Microsoft Corp.). Relative luciferase activity was expressed as the Firefly/Renilla ratio. Each group contained three technical replicates, and the assay was independently repeated three times.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and figures were prepared with GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, USA). Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Differences between two groups were evaluated using two-tailed independent-sample t-tests. For comparisons involving more than two groups, data distribution and variance equality were first examined using the Shapiro-Wilk test and a homogeneity of variance test. When data followed a normal distribution and showed equal variance, one-way analysis of variance was applied, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. If the variance homogeneity assumption was not satisfied, Dunnett’s T3 test was used instead. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Unless otherwise stated, all experiments were independently repeated at least three times, with three technical replicates included in each experiment.

Results

PTHR1 Is highly expressed in OS and associated with poor prognosis

To screen for candidate genes involved in OS progression, we first conducted differential gene expression analysis based on three independent GEO datasets: GSE12865, GSE126209, and GSE225588 (Fig. 1A). Each dataset was analyzed individually using GEO2R, including GSE12865 (Fig. 1B), GSE126209 (Fig. 1C), and GSE225588 (Fig. 1D). Venn diagram analysis was then conducted to identify overlapping DEGs among the three datasets (Fig. 1E-F). Seven common DEGs were identified, including four upregulated genes, PTHR1, MSR1, HLA-DMB, and ROR2, and three downregulated genes, LRRC2, CDH13, and POPDC3. Among the upregulated genes, PTHR1 showed the highest fold change (Fig. 1G), suggesting its potential relevance in OS progression.

To evaluate the prognostic relevance of PTHR1, survival analysis was performed in sarcoma cohorts from the Kaplan-Meier Plotter and TCGA databases. Patients with higher PTHR1 expression showed significantly shorter overall survival (Fig. 1H–I), suggesting a potential association between PTHR1 upregulation and poor clinical outcome. Consistently, RT-qPCR and WB analyses revealed higher PTHR1 levels in OS tissues than in paired cancellous bone tissues (Fig. 1J–K). Similarly, PTHR1 expression was elevated in OS cell lines compared with control osteoblastic cells (Fig. 1L-M; p < 0.05).

Collectively, the integrated bioinformatics and experimental results demonstrated that PTHR1 is significantly upregulated in OS and may contribute to OS pathogenesis and progression.

PTHR1 promotes proliferation, migration, invasion, and EMT in OS cells

To evaluate the role of PTHR1 in the malignant progression of OS, we established Saos2 cell lines with stable PTHR1 knockdown or overexpression and confirmed transfection efficiency by Western blot analysis (Fig. 2B). Cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration, and invasion were subsequently assessed using CCK-8 assays (Fig. 2C), flow cytometry (Fig. 2D), and Transwell assays (Fig. 2E). PTHR1 depletion markedly reduced Saos2 cell growth, migratory ability, and invasiveness, while increasing apoptosis (p < 0.05). Conversely, ectopic PTHR1 expression strengthened these malignant phenotypes and decreased apoptotic cell death (p < 0.05). WB analysis further showed that PTHR1 knockdown elevated the pro-apoptotic protein Bax but reduced the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and the mesenchymal markers Snail, Vimentin, and N-cadherin (Fig. 2F–G). The opposite expression pattern was observed after PTHR1 overexpression. These data suggest that PTHR1 facilitates OS cell growth, motility, invasiveness, and EMT while protecting cells from apoptosis (Fig. 2A).

To determine whether PTHR1 acts in a dose-dependent manner, Saos2 cells were transfected with graded amounts of PTHR1 expression plasmid (0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 µg). RT-qPCR and WB analyses confirmed gradient increases in PTHR1 expression (Fig. S1A). Functional assays showed that increasing PTHR1 expression significantly enhanced Saos2 cell proliferation (Figure S1B), migration, and invasion (Figure S1C–D; ANOVA, p < 0.05). These results further support a dose-dependent oncogenic role of PTHR1 in promoting malignant phenotypes of OS cells.

PTHR1 Enhances xenograft tumor growth and regulates EMT in OS

We further examined the in vivo effects of PTHR1 knockdown and overexpression using Saos2 cell-derived xenograft models (Fig. 3A). PTHR1 knockdown significantly reduced tumor volume and weight, decreased Ki-67 staining, and increased TUNEL-positive cells. In contrast, PTHR1 overexpression markedly increased tumor volume and weight, enhanced Ki-67 positivity, and reduced TUNEL-positive cell numbers (Fig. 3B-G). Cleaved-caspase-3 immunohistochemistry was performed to further evaluate apoptosis in xenograft tissues. In line with the TUNEL findings, PTHR1 knockdown increased the proportion of cleaved-caspase-3-positive cells, whereas PTHR1 overexpression reduced cleaved-caspase-3 staining (Fig. 3H). In addition, PTHR1 knockdown reduced the expression of the EMT-related proteins Snail, Vimentin, and N-cadherin, whereas PTHR1 overexpression showed the opposite effect (Fig. 3I-J).

Together, these in vivo findings support that PTHR1 promotes OS tumor growth by suppressing apoptosis and enhancing EMT, further indicating its potential as a therapeutic target in OS.

PTHR1 promotes OS progression by upregulating AXIN2

We next investigated whether AXIN2 mediates the pro-tumorigenic effects of PTHR1 in OS cells (Fig. 4A). PTHR1 knockdown reduced AXIN2 expression, whereas PTHR1 overexpression increased AXIN2 expression in Saos2 cells (Fig. 4B). To further determine the functional involvement of AXIN2, AXIN2 was overexpressed in PTHR1-silenced Saos2 cells, and transfection efficiency was confirmed by WB analysis (Fig. 4C). Functional assays showed that AXIN2 restoration partly reversed the suppressive effects of PTHR1 knockdown on OS cell growth, migration, and invasion, while decreasing apoptosis (Fig. 4D-F). Consistently, AXIN2 overexpression lowered Bax expression while increasing Bcl-2 and the EMT-associated proteins Snail, Vimentin, and N-cadherin (Fig. 4G–H). In the xenograft model, reintroduction of AXIN2 also counteracted the tumor-suppressive effect caused by PTHR1 knockdown (Fig. 5).

To further confirm the requirement of AXIN2 in PTHR1-mediated malignant phenotypes, AXIN2 was silenced in PTHR1-overexpressing cells. shAXIN2 effectively reduced AXIN2 expression (Figure S2A) and partially reversed PTHR1-induced migration and invasion (Figure S2B–C). WB analysis further showed that AXIN2 knockdown reduced the expression of EMT-related markers, including Snail, Vimentin, and N-cadherin (Figure S2D).

Collectively, these results suggest that PTHR1 promotes OS cell growth, migration, invasion, EMT, and tumor growth, while inhibiting apoptosis, at least partly by upregulating AXIN2.

PTHR1 upregulates PVT1 expression via the cAMP/PKA/CREB Axis

After confirming that PTHR1 promotes malignant phenotypes in OS, we next explored the upstream transcriptional mechanism underlying activation of the PVT1-associated ceRNA network. Previous studies have reported that miR-590-3p can target the 3′-UTR of AXIN2 and that several miR-590-3p-related lncRNAs, including Pvt1, are downregulated after PTHR1 knockout [13]. Prediction using miRWalk2.0 further indicated potential binding sites between hsa-miR-590-3p and both AXIN2 and PVT1. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that the PVT1/miR-590-3p/AXIN2 ceRNA axis may participate in PTHR1-mediated OS progression (Fig. 6A). We first examined the effects of PTHR1 knockdown and overexpression on PVT1 and miR-590-3p expression in Saos2 cells. PTHR1 knockdown decreased PVT1 expression and increased miR-590-3p levels, whereas PTHR1 overexpression produced the opposite effects (Fig. 6B-C). Given that PTHR1 can activate cAMP-dependent PKA signaling and that CREB1 has been reported to bind the PVT1 promoter and enhance its transcriptional activity [24, 25], we further investigated whether PTHR1 regulates PVT1 through the cAMP/PKA/CREB pathway. WB analysis showed that PTHR1 knockdown reduced p-PKA and p-CREB levels, whereas PTHR1 overexpression increased their expression (Fig. 6D). Treatment with the PKA agonist db-cAMP partially reversed the reduction of p-CREB and PVT1 expression caused by PTHR1 knockdown. Conversely, H89, a cAMP-dependent PKA inhibitor, attenuated the increases in p-CREB and PVT1 expression induced by PTHR1 overexpression (Fig. 6E-F). Consistently, db-cAMP partially restored PVT1 promoter activity under PTHR1 knockdown conditions, while H89 suppressed the enhanced promoter activity induced by PTHR1 overexpression (Fig. 6G). Further analysis showed that simultaneous knockdown of CREB1 and PTHR1 abolished the db-cAMP-induced increases in PVT1 expression and promoter activity (Fig. 6H-I). ChIP-qPCR further confirmed the direct binding of CREB1 to the PVT1 promoter (Fig. 6J). Collectively, these results demonstrate that PTHR1 upregulates PVT1 expression by activating the cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling axis.

PTHR1 regulates OS progression through the PVT1-miR-590-3p-AXIN2 ceRNA axis

We next validated whether PVT1, miR-590-3p, and AXIN2 constitute a functional ceRNA network in OS. Bioinformatics prediction identified a potential interaction site between PVT1 and miR-590-3p (Fig. 7B), and this interaction was verified using a dual-luciferase reporter assay (Fig. 7C). In Saos2 cells, PVT1 knockdown increased miR-590-3p expression, whereas PVT1 overexpression decreased miR-590-3p levels (Fig. 7D–E), indicating a negative regulatory relationship between PVT1 and miR-590-3p. A putative miR-590-3p binding site was also identified in the 3′-UTR of AXIN2 mRNA (Fig. 7F). Dual-luciferase assays showed that miR-590-3p mimics suppressed AXIN2-WT reporter activity, while miR-590-3p inhibitors enhanced it. However, neither miR-590-3p mimics nor inhibitors significantly changed the activity of the AXIN2-MUT reporter, supporting direct binding of miR-590-3p to the predicted AXIN2 3′-UTR site (Fig. 7G). Consistently, miR-590-3p mimics downregulated AXIN2 expression, whereas miR-590-3p inhibitors upregulated AXIN2 expression (Fig. 7H-J). To further validate this regulatory cascade, rescue experiments were conducted in PTHR1-silenced Saos2 cells. PVT1 overexpression increased AXIN2 expression, whereas this effect was eliminated by co-transfection with miR-590-3p mimics (Fig. 7K-N).

Discussion

This study systematically characterized the expression pattern and oncogenic role of PTHR1 in OS. PTHR1 has traditionally been investigated in the context of skeletal development and bone metabolism [26, 27], whereas its relevance to cancer, particularly OS, remains insufficiently defined [28]. Although limited evidence has suggested that PTHR1 participates in tumor cell motility and pathway activation in breast and bladder cancers [29, 30], its functional mechanism has not been fully clarified. In this study, analysis of public datasets together with experimental validation revealed increased PTHR1 expression in OS. Functionally, elevated PTHR1 was linked to enhanced proliferation, migration, invasion, EMT activation, and xenograft tumor growth, supporting its role as a pro-tumorigenic regulator in OS.

Mechanistically, our results showed that PTHR1 activates the cAMP/PKA signaling pathway, leading to CREB1 phosphorylation and subsequent transcriptional upregulation of lncRNA PVT1. Although the PKA/CREB1 axis has been implicated in various cancers, its connection with PTHR1-mediated lncRNA regulation in OS has not been previously clarified. Our findings establish a membrane-to-nucleus regulatory pathway in which PTHR1 signaling promotes CREB1-dependent PVT1 transcription, providing new insight into the transcriptional regulation of OS progression.

PVT1 is a well-established oncogenic lncRNA in multiple malignancies, particularly through its function as a ceRNA [31, 32]. Mechanistically, PVT1 enhanced OS malignancy by acting as a miR-590-3p decoy, thereby restoring AXIN2 expression. This finding expands the known functional network of PVT1 in OS. Unlike previous reports in which PVT1 mainly exerted its effects through miR-152 or miR-200c, our data identify a novel PVT1/miR-590-3p/AXIN2 regulatory pathway, suggesting that PVT1 may participate in OS progression through tissue- and context-specific ceRNA mechanisms.

AXIN2 is a key regulator of the Wnt signaling pathway and displays context-dependent functions in different tumor types [33]. It has been reported to act as a negative regulator of Wnt signaling with tumor-suppressive properties in some settings [34, 35], whereas oncogenic effects have been observed in lung and liver cancers [36, 37]. In the present study, AXIN2 was positively regulated by the PVT1/miR-590-3p axis and promoted EMT-related molecular changes in OS cells. Thus, AXIN2 may act as a tumor-promoting factor in OS, highlighting the context-dependent nature of its biological effects.

A major finding of this study is the construction of a five-component signaling axis, PTHR1/CREB1/PVT1/miR-590-3p/AXIN2, which links a membrane receptor, transcription factor, lncRNA, miRNA, and downstream effector into an integrated regulatory cascade. Compared with studies focusing on single non-coding RNAs or isolated signaling molecules, this axis provides a more comprehensive explanation for EMT activation and invasive progression in OS. Functional experiments further showed that this pathway influences OS cell growth, apoptosis, motility, invasiveness, EMT, and xenograft development, linking molecular events to aggressive tumor behavior.

From a clinical perspective, PTHR1 is a membrane receptor with potential drug accessibility, making it a candidate target for antibody- or inhibitor-based therapeutic strategies. In addition, PVT1 and miR-590-3p may serve as detectable biomarkers for diagnosis, risk stratification, or therapeutic response monitoring in OS. The identification of the PTHR1/CREB1/PVT1/miR-590-3p/AXIN2 axis therefore provides potential molecular targets for future precision therapy, liquid biopsy development, and nucleic acid-based treatment strategies.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, we mainly focused on the canonical G protein/cAMP/PKA/CREB1 pathway. As a class B G protein-coupled receptor, PTHR1 may also activate noncanonical signaling pathways [38]. Previous studies have shown that activated PTHR1 can interact with β-arrestin and sustain cAMP signaling from endosomes after receptor internalization [39]. β-arrestin can also mediate G protein-independent signaling and activate kinase cascades such as JNK, which may regulate gene transcription through nuclear downstream effectors [40]. In addition, β-arrestins are involved in GPCR-associated signaling networks and have been reported to regulate tumor cell growth, invasiveness, and metastatic behavior [41, 42]. PTHR1 may also trigger MAPK signaling through noncanonical mechanisms [11]. Therefore, although our results support the involvement of the PTHR1/PKA/CREB1 pathway in regulating PVT1 transcription, other signaling routes, such as β-arrestin-, JNK/AP-1-, or MAPK/ERK-dependent mechanisms, may also contribute to this process. In addition, this study lacks validation in large independent clinical cohorts. The potential links between the PTHR1/PVT1 axis and immune regulation, metastatic niche formation, or therapeutic resistance remain unclear. Future studies using larger patient cohorts, transcriptomic profiling, single-cell sequencing, and clinically relevant therapeutic models are needed to refine this regulatory network and evaluate its translational potential.

Overall, this study reveals that PTHR1 functions as a tumor-promoting regulator in OS and facilitates EMT and malignant progression through the CREB1/PVT1/miR-590-3p/AXIN2 axis. These results improve our understanding of OS progression and provide potential molecular candidates for future therapeutic development.

Conclusion

This study identifies PTHR1 as an important regulator of OS progression and provides evidence that PTHR1 activates the cAMP-PKA-CREB1 signaling pathway to transcriptionally upregulate PVT1. PVT1 functions as a ceRNA by sponging miR-590-3p, thereby relieving miR-590-3p-mediated repression of AXIN2 and contributing to EMT-related malignant phenotypes, including enhanced proliferation, migration, and invasion of OS cells. In vitro and in vivo experiments further support the functional relevance of the PTHR1/PKA/CREB1/PVT1/miR-590-3p/AXIN2 regulatory axis. Importantly, this axis should be interpreted as one contributory regulatory mechanism rather than the sole or dominant driver of OS progression. These findings expand the current understanding of OS molecular pathogenesis and suggest that components of this axis, including PTHR1, CREB1, PVT1, and AXIN2, may serve as potential targets for future therapeutic investigation.
