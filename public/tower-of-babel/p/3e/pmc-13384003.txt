What We (Don't) Know About Lactation in Heritable Aortic Diseases
Authors: Katherine Kilkenny, Rachel Axman, Mary J Roman, Richard B Devereux, Leonard Girardi, Jonathan W Weinsaft, Nupoor Narula
Published: 2026

The peripartum period is well-recognized to impart an elevated risk of aortic dissection, particularly in individuals with heritable thoracic aortic disease (HTAD). Advances in our understanding of aortic disease and risk stratification, particularly the role of aortic size and awareness of diagnosis in the prevention of type A dissection,1 have enabled effective surveillance strategies and timely elective surgical intervention. However, in certain cases, type A dissections can occur in the absence of traditionally recognized surgical thresholds. Similarly, type B dissections remain far less predictable, often occurring in the absence of significant aortic dilation.1 As a result, attention is increasingly being placed on identifying non–size-dependent triggers and associations of aortic vulnerability. This is particularly important in the third trimester and early postpartum period when a disproportionate number of dissections occur.1

Mechanistic insights from murine models have raised concern that lactation, via oxytocin signaling, may increase the risk for aortic dissection in vulnerable substrates.2,3 Habashi et al2 examined oxytocin-mediated activation of extracellular signal–regulated kinase (ERK) signaling in pregnancy-associated aortic dissection using a Marfan syndrome (MFS) mouse model. This homozygous, hypomorphic model (Fbn1mgR/mgR), which results in a ≥70% reduction of fibrillin-1 expression, demonstrates a distinct pattern of aortic involvement, with predominant dilation of the ascending aorta rather than the aortic root typically seen in humans with MFS who carry heterozygous pathogenic FBN1 variants. Pregnancy in this model was associated with 91.1% postpartum mortality driven by aortic dissection at a median of 11 days after delivery. Suppression of oxytocin signaling, either through immediate postpartum pup removal or administration of an oxytocin receptor antagonist, significantly reduced aortic events and improved survival. However, a 5-week treatment with hydralazine from the third trimester onwards also resulted in 95% freedom from aortic dissection in the lactating mice. A mouse model of Vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome created by Bowen et al3 similarly identified oxytocin-dependent ERK activation as a key driver of postpartum aortic rupture, with pathway inhibition conferring a substantial survival benefit.3 Again, treatment of the Col3a1G209S/+ VEDS mice with hydralazine from the 3rd week of pregnancy through four weeks of lactation resulted in improved survival.

Although the aforementioned elegant, molecular studies suggest an association between lactation and aortic dissection in HTAD, tempered risk with medical therapy is evident in the models, and human evidence of a lactation-dissection link remains sparse. In fact, the available data in humans have not demonstrated a signal of increased risk. The most contemporary data set from ROPAC III included 170 patients with HTAD, of whom 138 had lactation data.4 Postpartum major adverse cardiovascular events occurred in 2 of 92 breastfeeding patients compared with 8 of 46 nonbreastfeeding patients, including 1 aortic dissection (unspecified dissection type and HTAD diagnosis) in the nonbreastfeeding cohort. No aortic dissections were observed among breastfeeding patients and the mean diameters at the sinuses of Valsalva and ascending aorta were lower in breastfeeding vs nonbreastfeeding groups (35.1 vs 36.3 mm and 30.3 vs 30.6 mm, respectively). In a study by Matsuzaka et al5 of 19 pregnant patients with bicuspid aortic valve, 9 women breastfed without aortic dissection. Prior work by our group involving 74 women with MFS and 112 live births—including 58 observed pregnancies resulting in 52 live births—did not reveal an association between lactation and dissection in observed pregnancies, although breastfeeding data were not systematically captured.1 Age at pregnancy-associated aortic dissection was between 33 and 35 years. In a study by Youssef et al6 of 47 women with Loeys-Dietz syndrome (LDS) who had 40 live births, 11 reported breastfeeding without aortic dissection. There was 1 case of spontaneous coronary artery dissection 5 months postpartum in a 38-year-old woman with a pathogenic SMAD3 variant, although there was concomitant reported fibromuscular dysplasia and the diagnosis of LDS was made following dissection. Her aortic dimensions remained at nonsurgical thresholds during this pregnancy. A retrospective cohort study using a nationwide Japanese administrative claims database evaluated 226 deliveries among women with MFS, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, or LDS and identified 12 aortic dissections (5.3%) that occurred within 1 year of pregnancy, including 4 during pregnancy and 6 during a postpartum hospitalization.7 The diagnoses of these 12 women were not specified, nor was aortic size or dissection type. However, proximal aortic surgery was performed in 3 of 4 women who suffered an aortic dissection during pregnancy and 1 of 6 women who suffered an aortic dissection during the postpartum hospitalization, implying that at least 4 had a type A dissection. Although lactation data were not recorded, 15 (6.6%) patients received cabergoline, a dopamine agonist that inhibits lactation, at delivery. The proportion of patients who suffered postpartum aortic dissection did not significantly differ between the women who received cabergoline compared to those who did not (6.7% [1/15] vs 2.4% [5/211]). Although women who received cabergoline were classified as the “elective avoidance of breastfeeding” group, the indication for cabergoline was not reported and may reflect factors beyond lactation suppression. In this study, women who experienced aortic dissection were older than those who did not (median age 36 vs 31 years). Although the relationship between age and pregnancy-associated aortic dissection has not been consistently evaluated across studies, it is plausible that cumulative hemodynamic stress and age-related changes in vascular integrity contribute to aortic risk.

The lack of concordance between murine and human data highlights a key translational knowledge gap and raises the question of whether fundamental differences in oxytocin receptor distribution, signaling, and downstream effects underlie these divergent effects or whether additional factors may be key contributors to the occurrence of pregnancy-associated vascular dissection. Oxytocin levels rise throughout pregnancy, peak at delivery, and are either sustained at delivery-level concentrations in the presence of lactation or decline to pre-pregnancy levels by 8 weeks postpartum in the absence of lactation.2 Lactation is maintained through regular milk expression and nipple stimulation, which trigger prolactin release from the anterior pituitary to support milk synthesis and oxytocin release from the posterior pituitary to facilitate milk ejection. Both rodents and humans express oxytocin and its receptor within cardiovascular tissue, including the aorta. Experimental data from animal studies demonstrate estrogen-mediated upregulation of oxytocin receptor expression and local oxytocin concentration.2 Oxytocin and vasopressin are structurally similar, which allows cross-reactivity at each other’s receptors. In humans, oxytocin is generally associated with nitric oxide–mediated vasodilation, whereas rodents demonstrate more variable, sometimes biphasic, vascular responses, including transient vasoconstriction that has been attributed in part to vasopressin receptor activation.8 This may further contribute to why hydralazine, with its vasodilatory effect, was beneficial in the aforementioned mouse models. To the best of our knowledge, systematic comparisons of oxytocin and vasopressin receptor expression across human and rodent vascular tissue using standardized methodology are lacking.

Another consideration is the role of the ERK pathway, which has been implicated as a key mediator of aortic pathology in these murine models. Prior work in MFS identified ERK activation as a component of dysregulated transforming growth factor–β signaling, and subsequent studies suggest that ERK may serve as a convergence point for multiple upstream inputs within the aortic wall.2,3 Oxytocin signaling has been proposed as 1 such modifier, with experimental data demonstrating increased ERK activation in the peripartum period that can be attenuated with oxytocin receptor antagonism or suppression of lactation.2,3 However, ERK activation is influenced by numerous overlapping pathways, including angiotensin II, catecholamines, sex hormones, and endothelin signaling, many of which are also dynamically altered in the peripartum period.2,3 The relative contribution of any single stimulus remains uncertain, and the extent to which this signaling observed in animal models translates to human HTAD is unclear.

The extracellular matrix (ECM) serves not only as a structural scaffold but also as a dynamic signaling platform, providing mechanical and biochemical cues through a mechanosensitive feedback system that governs aortic wall integrity.9 In response to increased hemodynamic stress, this system can alter the phenotype of vascular smooth muscle cells to coordinate adaptive matrix remodeling.9 Fibrillin-1, a key ECM glycoprotein, assembles into microfibrils that support elastin deposition, regulate signaling pathways, including transforming growth factor–β, and affect smooth muscle cell mechanosensing of the ECM. The higher dissection rates observed in the severe phenotype MFS mouse models may reflect a more significant baseline deficit in ECM integrity and loss of signaling and sensing control, rendering the aortic wall susceptible to incremental oxytocin-driven ERK activation and precipitating a threshold-crossing shift toward maladaptive remodeling.

Beyond receptor-level differences, fundamental distinctions in lactation physiology represent another potential limitation in extrapolating findings from animal models to human disease. Compared to humans, rodents exhibit larger litter sizes, near-continuous nursing behavior, longer lactation periods relative to lifespan, and higher circulating oxytocin levels (approximately 1 order of magnitude greater when using the same assay platform).10 These differences likely result in greater cumulative and sustained oxytocin exposure in rodent models, potentially amplifying vascular effects beyond that seen in human physiology.

A significant limitation to translating the murine findings to humans is the reliance on murine models with severe disease phenotypes that may not reflect the clinical spectrum of human disease in whom pregnancy may be optimistically “sanctioned” after shared decision-making. In addition, lactation exposure is often incompletely or indirectly characterized in human studies, which frequently encompass heterogeneous patient populations with varying baseline aortic risk and may lessen the ability to detect a true association between lactation and aortic dissection, if present. We anticipate, based on experience and available data, that the lactation-associated risk is unlikely to approach the magnitude observed in experimental rodent models unless it is also demonstrated in animal models with a milder phenotype. Although the mouse models have raised important mechanistic insights into oxytocin-mediated pathways and the potential role of hydralazine, the current available evidence is insufficient to discourage lactation in patients with HTAD (Figure 1). Bridging this translational gap will require well-designed, prospective studies with rigorous characterization of pre-pregnancy aortic risk profiles, medical therapy, lactation exposure, and aortic outcomes. Given the morbidity and mortality of aortic dissection—paired with limited experimental data implicating lactation as a potential risk modifier—elucidating the mechanistic and clinical uncertainties surrounding peripartum aortic risk is essential to advancing evidence-based care.
