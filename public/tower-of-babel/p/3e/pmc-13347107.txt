Dietary Supplementation of Rumen‐Inert Fatty Acids and Rumen‐Protected Choline Modulates the Uterine Environment of Pregnant Beef Cows
Authors: Megan Bahr, Allyson McCarty, Zaeem Sarwar, Damon Smith, Matt Poore, Daniel Poole, Nadia Ashrafi, Romana Mimi, Stewart Graham, Ky Pohler, Jose Santos, Christian Maltecca, Felipe A C C Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEarly embryonic mortality is a major concern to the beef industry, as nearly 32.3% of multiparous cows will not maintain pregnancy past the first sixteen days of gestation. During this period, the uterine environment provides nutrients to support embryonic growth and development. Therefore, our objective was to (1) modulate the luminal metabolome at the onset of conceptus elongation in pregnant beef cows and (2) identify changes in the endometrial epithelial transcriptome during the peri‐elongation period. Cows received either TARG: 100 g of a rumen‐inert mono‐ and polyunsaturated fatty acid source plus 60 g of a rumen‐protected choline source (n = 50) or CON: 114 g of a saturated fatty acid source (n = 50) daily from 30 days before (d −30) to 30 days after breeding (d 30). Uterine cytobrush was collected from pregnant cows on day 14 for metabolomics (CON = 17, TARG = 15) and on Day 16 for transcriptomics analysis (CON = 6, TARG = 7). On Day 14, the concentration of 23 metabolites (p < 0.1) differed; the concentration of fatty acids, triacylglycerols, hexosylceramides, cholesterol esters, phospholipids, biogenic amines, carboxylic acids, methyl donors, and amino acids was greater in TARG. On Day 16, 675 genes were differentially expressed (FDR ≤ 0.1). Among these genes, 506 genes were upregulated in TARG, involving important genes in pathways such as interferon signaling, fatty acid transport, synthesis, metabolism, immune response, and cell‐to‐cell adhesion. In conclusion, the dietary supplementation provided herein modulated the uterine environment during the period of conceptus elongation.Keywords: cattle, conceptus, endometrium, maternal supplementation, pregnancy

Introduction

Pregnancy loss, specifically early embryonic mortality, has a large impact on the economic efficiency of the beef cattle industry (Diskin and Morris 2008). Embryonic mortality is greatest during the initial 30 days of gestation (Campanile et al. 2021; Reese et al. 2020), where approximately 48% of viable embryos fail to develop. During this time, critical milestones have to be achieved both by the embryo and the uterine environment, leading to maternal recognition of pregnancy (Betteridge and Fléchon 1988). Conceptus elongation plays a crucial role in these milestones (Ribeiro et al. 2016; Simintiras et al. 2019), as the concentration of interferon tau (INFT), a required messenger for maternal recognition of pregnancy, secreted by trophectoderm tissue, is directly dependent on the length of the elongating conceptus (Mathew et al. 2022). During this period of conceptus elongation, the uterine environment is the main supporter of embryonic development (Forde and Lonergan 2012; Martins et al. 2018). More specifically, the endometrial layers, constituted by luminal epithelial cells, glandular epithelial cells, and stromal cells, dictate the metabolite profile and its concentration that will be present in the uterine luminal fluid (Bazer et al. 2012; Forde and Lonergan 2012). Preparations of the endometrium to support uterine receptivity to the conceptus are modulated by INFT, estradiol, and progesterone (Forde and Lonergan 2012). These hormones modulate gene expression in the endometrium, influencing uterine function (Musavi et al. 2018; Silva et al. 2023a). Simultaneously, the growth and development of the embryo depend on the necessary nutrients provided by the uterine environment (Johnson et al. 2023). Therefore, an adequate supply of nutrients in the uterine environment plays a major role in embryonic growth and development, influencing early pregnancy establishment in ruminants.

Dietary supplementations have been successful in modulating the metabolite profile in the uterine environment (Burns et al. 2003; Cooke et al. 2014). It was previously reported that dietary supplementation of corn gluten meal led to a greater concentration of linolenic and arachidonic acid in the uterine tissues in cows (Burns et al. 2003). Similarly, dietary supplementation of soybean oil led to a greater concentration of eicosadienoic acid and linoleic acid in endometrial tissues (Cooke et al. 2014). Interestingly, the modulation of the metabolite profile in these experiments reflected in a greater expression of interferon‐tau and prostaglandin E synthase in the conceptus and interferon‐stimulated gene 15 (ISG15), myxovirus resistance 2 (MX2), and 20, 50‐oligoadenylate synthetase (OAS) in white blood cells despite no changes in the gene expression in the endometrium (Brandão et al. 2018; Cipriano et al. 2016). During the peri‐elongation period, there is an increased concentration of specific phospholipids, sphingolipids, ceramides, and oxylipins composed of mono‐ and polyunsaturated fatty acids and choline in the uterine luminal fluid (Ribeiro et al. 2016; Silva et al. 2023b; Simintiras et al. 2019). Simultaneously, there is an increased fatty acid uptake by embryonic tissues (Ribeiro et al. 2016). The elongating conceptus utilizes these fatty acids as an energy source, but these lipids are also an important constituent of trophectoderm and endoderm tissues (Ribeiro 2018). Therefore, a dietary supplementation strategy that provides the required lipids in the uterine luminal fluid could support conceptus elongation and mitigate early pregnancy loss in cattle.

Although successful modulation of the lipid profile in the uterine environment through dietary lipid supplementation has been previously reported (Burns et al. 2003; Cooke et al. 2014), the beneficial outcomes in early embryonic development, embryo‐maternal communication, and pregnancy establishment are contradictory (Brandão et al. 2018; Cipriano et al. 2016; Lopes et al. 2009; Stapples et al. 1998). Here, we tested the utilization of a dietary supplementation strategy containing rumen‐inert mono‐ and polyunsaturated fatty acids and rumen‐protected choline to support the biosynthesis of phospholipids, sphingolipids, ceramides, and oxylipins in the uterine luminal fluid. Choline is an important component of phosphatidylcholines, which are a type of phospholipid found to be abundant in cell membranes (Schalch Schalch Junior et al. 2022). Therefore, we hypothesized that pregnant cows receiving the dietary supplementation composed of mono‐ and polyunsaturated fatty acids and choline will have a greater concentration of lipids composed of these building blocks in the uterine luminal fluid 14 days after timed artificial insemination compared to cows receiving mostly saturated fatty acids. Secondly, we hypothesized that pregnant cows that received the targeted supplementation composed of mono‐ and polyunsaturated fatty acids and choline would have altered gene expression in uterine endometrial epithelial cells reflecting greater uterine receptivity and embryo‐maternal communication 16 days after timed artificial insemination. Our objectives were to compare the uterine luminal metabolome 14 days after timed artificial insemination and characterize the gene expression in uterine endometrial epithelial cells 16 days after artificial insemination of pregnant cows that received a dietary supplementation composed mostly of rumen‐inert mono‐ and polyunsaturated fatty acids plus rumen‐protected choline compared to cows receiving mostly saturated fatty acids.

Materials and Methods

Animals

One hundred Bos taurus, Angus, multiparous, lactating cows (595.78 ± 62 kg body weight, 5 ± 0.52 body condition score, and 77.06 ± 9.27 days postpartum at breeding; mean ± SD) were housed at the Butner Beef Cattle Field Laboratory located in Bahama, North Carolina. Prior to the start of the experiment (Figure 1A), all cows were managed as a single group grazing tall fescue (Festuca arundinacea) pastures with ad libitum access to a commercial mineral blend. Upon the start of the experiment, cows and their calves were moved to pens, fed a total mixed ration (TMR) of corn silage and soybean meal, and had ad libitum access to water. Cows were not exposed to bulls during this experiment. One cow was replaced prior to the start of treatments, and two cows were removed from the experiment after the start of the treatments due to poor adaptation (Figure 1B). All procedures involving animals were approved by the North Carolina State University Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol number 23‐115).

Experimental Design and Dietary Treatments

The timeline of the experimental design was established relative to timed‐artificial insemination (d 0). On d −45, cows were weighed and assigned a body condition score (BCS), on a zero to nine scale (Whitman et al. 1975), to be stratified and randomly assigned to a pen. Cows were allocated in pens housing 6 to 12 cows that contained individual feeding gates (Calan Broadbent Feeding Systems). On d −30, each cow within a pen was randomly assigned to receive 1) 114 g of mostly saturated blended fatty acids (Energy Booster 100; Milk Specialties, Eden Prairie, MN; CON), added was 27 g of limestone (Hubercarb Q40‐200 Calcium Carbonate, Huber Engineered Materials, Quincy, IL), and 30 g of soybean meal to balance for calcium and crude protein provided by the supplements in the TARG treatment or 2) 100 g of mostly unsaturated blended fatty acids (Essentiom; Church and Dwight Co. Inc., Princeton, NJ) plus 60 g of rumen‐protected choline (ReaShure; Balchem, Montvale, NJ; TARG; Table 1). Pens were divided into 3 groups to facilitate data collection, starting on different calendar days but following the same experimental design (Figure 1A). Supplements were top‐dressed daily on total mixed ration (TMR) from d −30 to d 30. Diets were isocaloric, isonitrogenous, and isolipidic. Each cow received a limit‐fed diet to ensure full consumption of the TMR and the supplementation. Diet was calculated to maintain a moderate BCS (5 or 6) of a 635 kg lactating cow with 13.6 kg peak milk production (National Academies of Sciences and Medicine 2016). Therefore, cows received 4.84% of their body weight in a base TMR composed of 94.7% corn silage and 5.6% concentrate. The concentrate was composed of 94.7% soybean meal, 2.3% trace mineral salt, 3% calcium carbonate, and 3 kg of vitamin A premix per ton.

On d −10, cows were synchronized using a 7‐day co‐synch + CIDR breeding protocol (Larson et al. 2006). Therefore, we placed a controlled internal drug‐release device (CIDR; EAZI‐Breed CIDR, Zoetis, Kalamazoo, MI, 1.38 g) and gave a gonadotropin‐releasing hormone injection (GnRH; Factrel, Zoetis, Kalamazoo, MI, 100 μg). In addition, cyclicity was assessed by ovarian ultrasonography and the presence of a corpus luteum (CL) using the B‐mode of M6 Vet Mindray Ultrasound (M6 Vet Ultrasound system, Mindray, Nanshan, Shenzhen, China). On d −3, CIDRs were removed, estrus detection patches (Estrotect, Rockway Inc., Spring Valley, WI) were applied, and a prostaglandin F2α injection was given (PGF2α; Lutalyse HighCon injection Zoetis, Kalamazoo, MI, 25 mg). On d 0, the size and location of the dominant follicle were determined by ovarian ultrasonography, a GnRH injection was given to all females, and females were bred by timed artificial insemination by the same technician. Semen from two bulls were balanced among treatments. Estrus detection patches were scored before breeding as described in Silva et al. (Silva et al. 2023a; 0: not rubbed off, 1: less than 50% rubbed off, 2: 50% to 70% rubbed off, 3: more than 70% rubbed off). On d 14, ovarian ultrasonography was conducted to measure the area of the CL using the ultrasound's caliper, and the Doppler mode was used to assess luteal blood perfusion. Blood perfusion was quantified as described in Pugliesi et al. (Pugliesi et al. 2014). On d 16, pregnancy diagnosis was conducted by conceptus recovery through uterine flushing (n = 43) as previously described in Ribeiro et al. (Ribeiro et al. 2016). On d 30, pregnancy diagnosis was conducted in the remaining cows by rectal ultrasonography (n = 55).

Blood Sample Collection and Analyses

Blood samples were collected on days 14 and 16 via caudal venipuncture for progesterone concentration, using K2EDTA blood tubes (BD Vacutainer, Fisher Scientific Co. L.L.C., Pittsburgh, PA). The samples were centrifuged at 1500 g for 10 min at 4°C. Plasma samples were aliquoted in duplicate and stored at −20°C until assayed. Progesterone was analyzed using the liquid‐liquid phase, double‐antibody RIA using assay reagents as described by Pohler et al. 2016, 2020). The intraassay coefficient of variation was 2.68% and interassay coefficient of variation was 3.41%.

Cytobrush Collection and Processing for Transcriptomics and Metabolomics Analyses

Uterine probing using an endocervical brush (Endocervical Sampling Brush, Viamed, Miami Lakes, FL) was performed on d 14 for metabolomics analysis and d 16 for transcriptomics analysis as described in Cardoso et al. (Cardoso et al. 2017) and recently reported by Silva et al. (Silva et al. 2021; Silva et al. 2023a; Silva et al. 2023b). On d 14, samples were collected from all cows where the technician was able to pass the cervix. Therefore, samples were collected from 42 cows in the TARG treatment and 49 cows in the CON. On d 16, a subset of cows from d 14 were randomly selected to receive a cytobrush collection. There were 23 cows in the TARG treatment and 20 cows in CON. However, only samples from cows diagnosed as pregnant either at d 16 or d 30 were analyzed from d 14 (CON = 17 and TARG = 15) and d 16 (CON = 8 cows and TARG = 7 cows). All cows collected on d 16 were removed from the experiment after collection. Briefly, the cows were given caudal epidural anesthesia (2% Lidocaine, Vet One, Boise, ID, 60 mg), then an 8” cytobrush was attached to a conventional artificial insemination (AI) gun, covered by a disposable AI sheath and sanitary sheath. The assembled AI gun was then inserted transcervically, the tip of the AI gun just into the uterine body where the cytobrush was exposed from the sheath, and gently rotated twice to collect the sample. The cytobrush was then retracted back into the AI sheath to prevent contamination from other parts of the reproductive tract. The AI gun in its entirety was then removed. This process was only done once on a given collection day. Then the cytobrush was added to a microcentrifuge tube filled with 1 mL of PBS, put on ice, and processed within an hour of collection.

To separate any cells or debris from the brush, the tubes were vortexed for 1 min at moderate speed. The brush was then removed, and the liquid content was centrifuged at 392 g for 7 min to separate cells and debris. The supernatant was aspirated through a 40μm micropipette tip cell strainer (Flowmi Cell Strainers, SP Bel‐Art, Wayne, NJ) and then through a sterile 0.22μm Polyvinyl Difluoride (PVDF; EZFlow Syringe Filter, Foxx Life Sciences, Salem, NH) syringe filter to remove any cells or additional debris. The filtrate was then snap‐frozen and stored in a −80°C freezer for subsequent metabolomics analysis. After the supernatant was removed, and the brush was returned to the original microcentrifuge tube with the cell pellet. The microcentrifuge tube containing the brush and cell pellet was then snap‐frozen and stored in a −80°C freezer for subsequent transcriptomics analysis. Cells obtained by this methodology were expected to be predominantly uterine endometrial epithelial cells (Rocha et al. 2022).

Targeted Small Molecule and Lipid Analysis in Luminal Fluid Using MxP Quant 500 XL

Luminal fluid samples, calibration standards, and quality controls (QCs) were processed following the manufacturer's protocol (biocrates Life Sciences, AG, Innsbruck, Austria). Standards and QCs were reconstituted in 100 μL H2O and mixed for 15 min at 1200 rpm. On a 96‐well plate, 10 μL of calibration standards, 20 μL of luminal fluid, QCs, and phosphate buffer were added, followed by drying under nitrogen (60 min). Derivatization was performed using phenylisothiocyanate (PITC) at room temperature for 60 min, then dried again under nitrogen (60 min). Metabolites were extracted with 5 mM ammonium acetate in methanol using orbital shaking (30 min), followed by centrifugation (2 min at 500 g). Extracts were diluted 1:1 with H2O for LC‐MS/MS analysis. For FIA and FIA XL analyses, 20 μL and 50 μL of extract were mixed with 480 μL and 450 μL of FIA solvent, respectively. All assay plates were sealed, mixed for 10 min at 600 rpm, and transferred to the autosampler for targeted metabolomics analysis. Sample extracts were analyzed using a Waters I‐Class ultra performance liquid chromatography coupled with a Sciex 7500 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer platform. Chromatographic separation was performed on the MxP® Quant 500 C18 column (biocrates, Austria) using mobile phases A (H2O + 0.2% formic acid) and B (MeCN + 0.2% formic acid). Both positive and negative mode gradients were 5.8 min, with differing %B composition between 2.0 and 4.5 min. Injection volumes were 5 μL (positive) and 15 μL (negative). For FIA and FIA XL analyses, an isocratic method was applied at 0.03 mL/min using kit‐provided solvent, with a 20 μL injection volume in both ionization modes. Data processing was conducted using biocrates WebIDQ software, with QC samples provided by the manufacturer at low, medium, and high concentrations.

RNA Extraction, Library Generation, and RNA Sequencing

To the microcentrifuge tubes containing the cell pellet and the brush 500 μL of the Buffer RLT was added and the tubes were vortexed at 12,500 rpm for 2 min to disrupt the cells. The lysate was pipetted directly into the QIAshredder spin column that was placed in a 2 mL collection tube and centrifuged at full speed for 2 min. To the homogenized lysate, 500 μL of 70% ethanol was added and mixed well by pipetting, not centrifugation. Approximately 500–700 μL (including any precipitate that may have formed) was transferred to a RNeasy spin column placed in a 2 mL collection tube (RNeasy mini kit, Qiagen, Dusseldorf, Germany). The collection tube was centrifuged for 15 s at 8000×g, discarded the flow through, and repeated the process with the remaining sample. To the RNeasy spin column, 350 μL Buffer RW1 was added and centrifuged at 8000 × g for 15 s, which washed the spin column membrane. After discarding the flow‐through, 10 μL DNase 1 stock solution was added to 70 μl Buffer RDD, and the tube was gently inverted to mix the solution. The tube was centrifuged briefly to collect liquid from the sides of the tube. Added directly to the RNeasy spin column membrane was 80 μL of the DNase1 incubation mix and it was placed on the bench top for 15 min. After 15 min, 350 μL Buffer RW1 was added to the RNeasy spin column, then centrifuged for 15 s at 8000 × g. After discarding the flow‐through, 500 μL Buffer RPE was added to the RNeasy spin column and centrifuged for 15 s at 8000 × g, then discarded the flow‐through. Added to the RNeasy spin column was 500 μL Buffer RPE and centrifuged for 2 min at 8000 × g. The RNeasy spin column was placed in a new 2 mL collection tube, and centrifuged for 1 min at full speed, then discarded the old collection tube. The RNeasy spin column was placed in a new 1.5 mL collection tube, and 30 μL RNase‐free water was added directly to the spin column membrane. To elute the RNA, the sample was centrifuged for 1 min at 8000 × g. This step was repeated using the eluate to recover a greater concentration of RNA from the column. Samples were stored at −80 for further analysis.

RNA samples from pregnant cows in the CON (n = 8) and TARG (n = 7) treatments at d 16 were sequenced by Novogene Corporation (https://en.novogene.com). The quality control of the RNA samples was performed before library generation. The sequencing library was constructed using the RNA‐NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswitch, MA, USA). The sequencing was performed using Novaseq. 6000 (Illumina, Sacramento, CA, USA). The final number of samples analyzed by RNAseq for d 16 were 13 samples for CON (n = 6) and TARG (n = 7) treatments, as two samples failed during library preparation due to low RIN. The quality control is reported in Table S1.

RNAseq Mapping, Quality Control, and Bioinformatics Analysis

The quality of the RNA‐seq reads was evaluated using the fastp software. Clean data reads were obtained by removing reads containing poly‐N, reads containing adapter, and low‐quality reads from raw data. During this step, Q20, Q30, and GC content were calculated. All of the downstream analyses were based on clean, high‐quality data. The ARS‐UCD1.2 bovine reference genome and gene model annotation files were directly downloaded from the genome website. The reference genome was built using Hisat2 (v2.0.5) and paired‐end clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using Hisat2 (v2.0.5) (Mortazavi et al. 2008). In a reference‐based approach, the mapped reads of each sample in the bovine genome were assembled using Stringtie (v1.3.3b) (Pertea et al. 2015). To calculate the reads numbers mapped to each gene the software FeatureCounts (v1.5.0‐p3) was used (Liao et al. 2014). Raw counts were filtered to remove low‐expression genes (≥ 10 counts in ≥ 3 samples). The number of uniquely mapped reads that were effectively used for the gene expression analysis averaged 16.1 million per sample, with a minimum of 12.8 million reads and a maximum of 24.0 million reads (median 16.1 million reads, 1st quartile 14.3 million reads, and 3rd quartile 16.5 million reads).

Detection of differentially expressed genes between TARG and CON treatments at d 16 was performed using a generalized linear model implemented in the R package DESeq. 2. Normalization was conducted using DESeq. 2's median‐of‐ratios method, followed by dispersion estimation using empirical Bayes shrinkage, and a negative binomial generalized linear model was fitted for each gene. The Wald test was used for the treatment effect significance, and the Log2 fold change shrinkage using apeglm (adaptive prior). DEGs were determined using false discovery rate‐adjustment (Benjamini–Hochberg) for multiple comparisons (FDR ≤ 0.1). Analysis of DEGs and fold changes was performed comparing TARG versus CON treatments. Sensitivity model outputs, including group and/or group × treatment, are in Table S2.

Ingenuity Pathway Analysis

The set of DEGs used in each analysis was determined based on a q‐value (FDR) of ≤ 0.1. Emphasis was given to the significant canonical pathways and bio‐functions, that were predicted to be increased or decreased based on a Z‐score ≥ 2.0 and ≤ −2.0, respectively. Furthermore, IPA was used to connect the dataset molecules with predicted upstream regulators and downstream functions (i.e., regulator effects analysis) using the IPA algorithms (Krämer et al. 2013). The predictions were deemed to be activated or inhibited based on the Z‐score criteria described above.

Univariate Analyses

Hormonal, ovarian, and production data were analyzed by ANOVA with the MIXED procedure of SAS (SAS ver. 9.4, SAS/STAT, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) and using cow as the experimental unit. The model included the fixed effects of treatment, group, and their interaction. The normality of the residuals and homogeneity of variances were examined using the graphical distribution of the residuals. Responses that violated the assumptions of normality were subjected to power transformation according to the Box‐Cox procedure in SAS. The LSM and SEM were back‐transformed for the presentation of the data. Significance was stated as p ≤ 0.05.

The concentration of metabolites was analyzed in RStudio Version 2023.06.1 + 524 (RStudio, PBC, Boston, MA). Prior to all the analyses conducted on the concentration of metabolites, data were log‐transformed to achieve normal distribution in the majority of the metabolites. Data filtering included metabolites measured in at least 75% of the samples or found in at least 60% in one sample group. The missing values in the remainder of the 394 metabolites were not substituted, as the methods used for statistical analyses handled missing values. The normality of the residuals and homogeneity of variances were examined using the graphical distribution of the residuals and Shapiro‐Wilk test. Normally distributed data was analyzed using a parametric test, such as limma, and non‐parametric data was analyzed by the Wilcox‐Mann‐Whitney test (Wilcox test). Multiple comparisons were performed using the Benjamini‐Hochberg procedure. However, to explore the data significance was stated as p ≤ 0.05 and tendency 0.05 < p < 0.1.

Power Analyses for Metabolomics and Transcriptomics

A post‐hoc power analysis was conducted to quantify the minimum detectable effect size (MDES) of our experimental design relative to our results. The metabolomics dataset was analyzed using a simulation‐based (parametric bootstrap) approach; all parameters were reproduced, such as the experimental design, sample sizes, and the observed variance structure of the log2‐transformed concentration of metabolites. The median (within‐group) standard deviation across metabolites was 0.77 log2 units. Based on CON = 17 and TARG = 15 samples, the study detected treatment differences of approximately 1.4 log2 units (2.6‐fold change) with 80% power when applying the Benjamini‐Hochberg multiple correction across 394 metabolites at q ≤ 0.05. Given these parameters, many true effects smaller than this threshold would not be detectable at adequate power, which explains the lack of metabolites with significant q‐values despite several normally significant (unadjusted) p‐values. The power‐effect size curve is reported in Figure S1. The transcriptomics dataset was analyzed as previously described by Zhao et al. (Zhao et al. 2018). The web interface utilized was (https://cqs-vumc.shinyapps.io/rnaseqsamplesizeweb/) and the parameters entered were: Sample size: 7; FDR: 0.10; Ratio of Normalization: 1; Total number of genes for testing: 16000; Expected number of differentially expressed genes: 675; Minimum fold change: 2.2; Average read counts: 20; Dispersion: 0.10. If an RNA‐Seq experiment is performed with at least 7 experimental subjects in each of the two groups, assuming that the total number of genes for testing is 16,000, the total number of differentially expressed genes is 675, that is, 4.2% of the genes, the desired minimum fold change is 2.2, and the significant threshold (FDR) is 10%, then the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis (statistical power) is 0.86. Hence, the estimated power is 0.86.

Results

Physiological, Ovarian, and Hormonal Parameters

There was no treatment × group interaction nor main effect of group in any of the variables analyzed (p ≥ 0.32). There was no significant difference in the cyclicity status between cows subjected to the TARG or CON treatments on day −10 (p = 0.44). On d 0, the diameter of the dominant follicle did not differ between cows in TARG and CON (p = 0.62; Table 4). On Day 14, the corpus luteum area as well as its blood perfusion did not differ between treatments (p ≥ 0.59, Table 4).

Discussion

The uterine environment supports embryonic growth and development during the early stages of gestation. Here, we proposed a supplementation strategy composed of rumen‐inert mono‐ and polyunsaturated fatty acids and rumen‐protected choline, aiming to modulate the uterine environment of pregnant multiparous cows. We probed the uterine environment at key timepoints for pregnancy establishment, such as the onset of conceptus elongation (d 14) and maternal recognition of pregnancy (d 16). Supporting our hypothesis, we demonstrated the modulation of the concentrations of 23 metabolites in the uterine luminal fluid during the peri‐elongation period in females receiving a dietary supplementation composed of mono‐ and polyunsaturated fatty acids and choline, despite no changes in the concentration of progesterone at day 14 post‐artificial insemination. In addition, we also demonstrated the modulation of 585 differentially expressed genes at day 16 in important pathways such as hormone regulation, interferon signaling, maternal immune response, and lipid synthesis in uterine endometrial epithelial cells of females receiving a dietary supplementation composed of mono‐ and polyunsaturated fatty acids and choline. Altogether, the dietary supplementation provided modulated the uterine environment during early pregnancy establishment (Figure 6).

Relative gene expression of genes between pregnant cows receiving TARG (n = 7) and CON (n = 6) treatments. Results are reported as mean ± SEM, all panels presented FDR ≤ 0.1. Genes are grouped based on relative function, Panel A: interferon‐stimulated gene, Panel B: hormonal receptors and regulators, Panel C: fatty acid transport, Panel D: fatty acid synthesis, Panel E: fatty acid metabolism, Panel F: extracellular matrix remodeling, Panel G: cell‐to‐cell adhesion, Panel H: immune response. Data were analyzed by a general linear model, the model included the fixed effects of treatment.

The concentration of unsaturated fatty acids and triglycerides was modulated by the TARG dietary supplementation strategy. Fatty acids and triglycerides are similar as they can both be used as energy sources for cells within the body (Zeng et al. 2023). However, fatty acids can also be components of more complex phospholipids and glycolipids, which play a vital role in biological membranes (Zeng et al. 2023). Linoleic acid (FA 18:2) had an increased concentration in the cows fed the TARG treatment. Linoleic acid is an essential fatty acid; therefore, it must be supplied in the diet as the double bond on carbon 9 from the terminal methyl group cannot be inserted through biological processes (Stapples et al. 1998). Linoleic acid can be desaturated and elongated to form C20:3, which serves as an immediate precursor for series 2 prostaglandins, including prostaglandin E2 (Stapples et al. 1998). PGE2 is a critical regulator of pregnancy establishment in cattle by promoting endometrial activity of IFNT (Cipriano et al. 2016). Docosahexaenoic acid (FA 22:6n‐3, DHA) was also found to have an increased concentration in the uterine luminal fluid of the pregnant cows that were fed the TARG supplementation. A temporal modulation in the concentration of DHA in the uterine luminal fluid has been previously reported, more specifically, the greatest concentration of DHA is on day 14 of the estrous cycle compared to days four and seven (Silva et al. 2023b). Docosahexaenoic acid has beneficial effects on the support of normal development and growth of tissues during fetal development (Roque‐Jimenez et al. 2020). Supplementing dairy cows with an algae product containing 10% DHA from days 27 to 147 postpartum showed increased expression of IFN‐stimulated genes in peripheral blood lymphocytes (Sinedino et al. 2017), which supports the rationale that DHA could be beneficial to early embryonic development. In summary, the increased concentration of linoleic acid and DHA may be beneficial in early embryonic growth, but further investigation regarding the importance of triglycerides in the uterine luminal fluid to embryonic growth outside of providing energy to cells and tissues is warranted.

The dietary supplementation strategy increased the concentration of two methyl donors as well as seven phospholipids in the TARG treatment on day 14. Betaine and choline were shown to have greater concentrations in the cows in the TARG treatment. Both of these metabolites can be used as methyl donors; however, betaine is not as well understood in ruminants (Coleman et al. 2021). It was reported that the unique pathway of methyl donors may have a role in embryonic development as well as placental function (Coleman et al. 2021). Although choline is a methyl donor, choline also serves as a building block for phosphatidylcholines (Schalch Schalch Junior et al. 2022). We found that one phosphatidylcholine (PC), PC32:3, had a greater concentration in the uterine luminal fluid in the cows supplemented with the TARG treatment. Phosphatidylcholines are a part of the class of phospholipids, which are the most abundant lipids in eukaryotic cells (Schalch Schalch Junior et al. 2022). Most phospholipids in the membranes are phosphatidylcholines, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylserine, and phosphatidylinositol (Murota 2020). Along with PC 32:3, phosphatidylinositol (PI) 17:1_18:2, and phosphatidylglycerol (PG) 18:2_22:4 were also greater in the uterine luminal fluid in the cows receiving the TARG treatment. In addition, phosphatidylserine (PS) 36:3, PI 18:1_22:0, PG 18:2_20:0, and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) 32:2 tended to show greater concentrations in the cows supplemented with the TARG treatment. However, there are not many studies conducted investigating these specific metabolites and their role in the uterine luminal fluid during the peri‐elongation period in cattle. When investigating the temporal modulation and P4 modulation of the uterine metabolome (Simintiras et al. 2019), it was reported that PC, PS, and PE were significantly affected by day but not by the circulating concentration of progesterone, whereas PI was not significantly affected by day or P4 (Simintiras et al. 2019). In sheep, when comparing the metabolite concentration of the uterine luminal fluid of cyclic and pregnant sheep at day 14 of gestation (O'Neil and Spencer 2021). It was reported that there were greater concentrations of cholesterol esters, phosphatidylglycerols, and phosphatidylserines in the uterine luminal fluid at day 14 of pregnant ewes, whereas phosphatidylinositol was found to have greater concentrations in the conceptus during this period (O'Neil and Spencer 2021). In our study, the cholesterol ester (CE), CE 18:2, tended to have a greater concentration in cows fed the TARG treatment. Overall, phospholipids play a vital role in early embryonic development, especially during the peri‐elongation period (Ribeiro et al. 2016; Simintiras et al. 2019), and the TARG supplementation increased the concentration of these lipids in the uterine luminal fluid.

The TARG supplementation strategy upregulated one interferon‐signaling gene and two sex steroid genes, and downregulated the expression of one sex steroid gene. The modulation of interferon‐stimulating genes and sex steroid receptors in endometrial epithelial cells is required for pregnancy establishment (Forde and Lonergan 2012). In our data, CTSL was upregulated in pregnant cows being fed the TARG supplementation. Cathepsin L (CTSL) gene expression is regulated by IFNT and progesterone and is the most abundant cathepsin (CTS) gene expressed in the endometrium during early pregnancy (Jain et al. 2021). Endometrial expression of CTSL is involved in the survivability of the embryo as it plays a role in uterine receptivity and implantation (Jain et al. 2021; Spencer et al. 2007). It was reported that the expression of CTSL mRNA increased as IFNT concentration increased and the conceptus elongated at day 16 of gestation in goats, leading to the conclusion that CTSL is an interferon‐stimulated gene, as they are directly correlated (Jain et al. 2021). In our data, cyclooxygenase‐2 (COX‐2) was downregulated in the TARG treatment, which contradicts a previous report reporting there was no significant difference in the expression of COX‐2 between pregnant cows fed a blend of unsaturated fatty acids and those that were fed a blend of saturated fatty acids (Brandão et al. 2018; Cooke et al. 2014). During pregnancy, it was reported that prostaglandin production is correlated with COX‐2 expression in the bovine endometrium (Parent et al. 2003), and endometrial expression of COX‐2 is necessary for uterine receptivity. It has been reported that COX‐2 is an important enzyme for the production of PGE2 and regulating the PGE2/PGF2alpha ratio, which contributes to the establishment of pregnancy (Kumar et al. 2013). There was no significant difference in the expression of PGE2 and PGF2alpha in our dataset between pregnant cows in the TARG and CON treatment. Lopes et al. reported that the inhibition of COX‐2 by linoleic acid precursors may be beneficial to pregnancy establishment as it may reduce PGF2alpha synthesis, which would improve pregnancy rates by delaying luteolysis (Lopes et al. 2009). Due to contradicting information, the expression of COX‐2 on Day 16 of gestation in bovine endometrial epithelial cells should be further investigated to determine the expression levels of beneficial versus detrimental effects on pregnancy. In our data set, two sex steroid receptors were found to be significantly upregulated in the TARG treatment, progesterone receptor (PGR) and estrogen receptor 2 (ESR2). It has been reported that progesterone regulates endometrial function independently of the expression of its main receptor, PGR (Pereira et al. 2022). It was reported that the expression of PGR was high on Day 5, decreased on Day 7, and decreased again on Day 13, but Day 16 was not significantly different from Day 13 of gestation (Okumu et al. 2010). Spencer et al. reported that continuous exposure to P4 resulted in the downregulation of PGR by Days 11–12 in cyclic sheep (Spencer et al. 2007). The similar decrease of PGR in both cyclic and pregnant ruminants is supported by Okumu et al. as it was reported no significant difference in PGR between the presence or absence of a conceptus (Okumu et al. 2010). This is likely due to the preparation for luteolysis; however, in pregnant animals, IFNT blocks luteolysis, which allows for the continuation of embryonic development (Mathew et al. 2022; Spencer et al. 2007). The gene ESR2 was upregulated in the cows supplemented with the TARG treatment. Estradiol (E2) mediates function through the alpha form ESR1 and beta form ESR2 (Okumu et al. 2010). Okumu et al. reported that the expression of ESR2 in the endometrium increased from Day 5 to Day 7 of gestation and expression is maintained on day 16 of gestation (Okumu et al. 2010). Lastly, the expressions of PGR and ESR2 are important to pregnancy establishment (Okumu et al. 2010). Collectively, the upregulation of genes in the TARG treatment, such as CTSL, ESR2, PGR, and COX‐2, seems to support the establishment of pregnancy in cattle.

Five genes involved in fatty acid metabolism, synthesis, and transport were upregulated in the TARG group. More specifically, the genes FADS1, ELOVL, HRH1, CHKA, and SLC1A4 were upregulated in TARG. The utilization of mono‐ and polyunsaturated fatty acids during gestation is important for conceptus elongation as the membrane of the conceptus is composed predominantly of phospholipids (Ribeiro et al. 2016). Fatty acid desaturase 1 (FADS1) is involved in lipid metabolism and lipid synthesis; FADS1 was recently found to be expressed in bovine endometrial tissue (Killeen et al. 2016). Expression of FADS1 in the bovine endometrium is involved in the synthesis of long‐chain fatty acids as a rate‐limiting enzyme (Killeen et al. 2016). These long‐chain fatty acids include eicosapentaenoic acid, docosaxhexanoic acid, and arachidonic acid (Killeen et al. 2016). During early pregnancy, supplementation of monounsaturated fatty acids increases eicosapentaenoic and docosaxhexanoic acid, leading to an increase in lipid mRNA transport genes (Roque‐Jimenez et al. 2020). It was reported that the increase of docosahexaenoic acid improves reproductive performance in dairy cows (Sinedino et al. 2017). Arachidonic acid has been reported to be a precursor to different types of phospholipids, specifically phosphatidylcholine, which is a major component of intracellular membranes (Yang et al. 2012). This information suggests the upregulation of FADS1 has the potential to be beneficial to pregnancy as it contributes to the synthesis of arachidonic, eicosapentaenoic, and docosahexanoic acid, and these fatty acids are beneficial to reproductive performance (Sinedino et al. 2017). In addition, choline kinase (CHKA), an enzyme responsible for catalyzing the phosphorylation of choline to phosphocholine (Abdollahi‐Arpanahi et al. 2019) was upregulated in cows in the TARG treatment. This is important because phosphocholine is used for the production of phosphatidylcholine. It was reported that the expression of the CHKA gene was increased 2.8‐fold in the conceptus during the onset of elongation (Ribeiro et al. 2016). This is likely to indicate that the upregulation of CHKA in the endometrium helps synthesize phosphatidylcholine, which is necessary to support conceptus elongation. Altogether, the upregulation of CHKA and FADS1 have been reported to contribute to fatty acid content that is utilized by the conceptus in early development, leading to improved reproductive performance.

We investigated several pathways regarding immune response; four chemokines were significantly different, with two genes upregulated and two downregulated in the TARG treatment. In addition, two toll‐like receptor genes were also downregulated in the TARG treatment. Regulation of immune response during gestation is crucial as it allows the cow to tolerate the semi‐allograft conceptus and prevent rejection (Rocha et al. 2021). Chemokines are responsive to sites where they are needed to regulate or strengthen an inflammatory response by acting on neutrophils and monocytes (Killeen et al. 2016). Although chemokines are important for immune response throughout the body, the different chemokines have shown various functions regarding pregnancy in cattle (Killeen et al. 2016; Sakumoto et al. 2017). In our data, CCL2 and CXCL8 were downregulated in TARG. Chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) was reported to have a greater expression in the endometrium at day 15 during early pregnancy compared to non‐pregnant cows (Sakumoto et al. 2017). Also known as interleukin 8, CXCL8 is produced by different cells, for instance, leukocytic cells and nonleukocytic cells (Wagener 2015). A report described a greater expression of CXCL8 in cows suffering from reproductive diseases (Wagener 2015). Contrary, CCL28 and CXCL17 were upregulated in the endometrium of cows receiving TARG treatment. Chemokine ligand 28 (CCL28) was reported to be upregulated in nonreceptive heifers in comparison with receptive heifers (Killeen et al. 2016). In addition, it was reported that CXCL17 is upregulated due to greater concentrations of IFNT (Sheikh et al. 2019). Sheikh et al. also reported that CXCL17 mRNA expression in pregnant cows was significantly greater from day 4 to day 35, with the greatest expression on Day 21 of gestation, which is approximately the time implantation occurs (Johnson et al. 2023; Sheikh et al. 2019). Lastly, our data also had the expression of two toll‐like receptors, both TLR6 and TLR7 were found to be downregulated in cows supplemented with the TARG treatment. Toll‐like receptors have been associated with diseases in the reproductive tract (Davies et al. 2008). Yoo et al. 2019 reported that the expression of TLR6 and TLR7 in the endometrium suggests TLR expression may play a critical role in immune response and uterine receptivity (Yoo et al. 2019). These findings suggest that the downregulation of TLR6 and TLR7 is potentially beneficial to the establishment of pregnancy, as increased expression can be associated with complications in pregnancy (Davies et al. 2008). Altogether, upregulation of CXCL17 and downregulation of CXCL8 appear to be beneficial for pregnancy establishment and regulation of immune factors. Whereas upregulation of CCL28 and downregulation of CCL2 have the potential to be detrimental to early pregnancy. As CCL28 is found to be upregulated in nonreceptive heifers (Killeen et al. 2016), and CCL2 is found to be upregulated in pregnant cows at Day 15 (Sakumoto et al. 2017). Based on this information, further investigation of CCL28 and CCL2 is warranted as to the overall effects on reproductive performance in early gestation.

In summary, the maternal supplementation containing rumen‐inert mono‐ and polyunsaturated fatty acids and rumen‐protected choline modulated the uterine environment of pregnant beef cows. It is important to note that this was a terminal study, and the fertility outcomes of cows receiving the TARG supplementation deserve further investigation. In addition, a limitation of this study is that the investigation of the uterine metabolome was exploratory, as metabolites were evaluated before multiple corrections. Nevertheless, supporting our hypothesis, there was an increased concentration of several metabolites in the uterine luminal fluid of pregnant cows, including linoleic acid, DHA, choline, and phosphatidylcholine, which are biologically linked to the composition of the TARG supplementation. Genes involved in pathways related to the establishment of pregnancy, such as those involving early embryonic development, pregnancy recognition, and fatty acid utilization, were upregulated in TARG. Lastly, in our study, we only investigated the gene expression on day 16 between CON and TARG dietary supplementation strategies. Therefore, it is unknown what the baseline gene expression is during the previous days of gestation. Altogether, our experiment presents novel data on the modulation of the uterine luminal fluid through dietary maternal supplementation at the onset of conceptus elongation and the uterine transcriptome at an important period for pregnancy establishment in cattle.
