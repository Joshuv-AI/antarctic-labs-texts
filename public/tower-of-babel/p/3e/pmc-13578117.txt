Prognostic value of modified magnetic resonance arterial and venous collateral scores in unilateral chronic middle cerebral artery occlusion
Authors: Xingru Xu, Taosheng Zuo, Baodong Gu, Qian Xu, Yun Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the value of modified regional leptomeningeal collateral (rLMC) scores based on high-resolution 3D time-of-flight magnetic resonance angiography with magnetization transfer contrast (MRA-MTC-rLMC) and susceptibility-weighted angiography (SWAN-rLMC) in assessing collateral circulation and predicting clinical outcomes in patients with unilateral chronic middle cerebral artery occlusion (CMCAO).MethodsIn this retrospective analysis of 39 CMCAO patients, we developed enhanced rLMC scores by incorporating the posterior cerebral artery territory and deep medullary veins into traditional scoring. The resulting MRA-MTC-rLMC (10 regions, 22 points) and SWAN-rLMC (11 regions, 24 points) scores were evaluated for predicting poor outcomes (TIA, cerebral infarction, or death) using ROC curve analysis.ResultsAmong 39 patients, 20 (51.3%) experienced poor outcomes. Both modified scores demonstrated excellent predictive performance, with AUCs of 0.938 for MRA-MTC-rLMC and 0.886 for SWAN-rLMC (both P < 0.001). The optimal cut-off of ≤ 11 for MRA-MTC-rLMC achieved 100% sensitivity and 89.5% specificity, while ≤ 12 for SWAN-rLMC showed 90% sensitivity and 89.5% specificity. Patients stratified by these cut-offs showed significant prognostic differences (P < 0.001).ConclusionThe modified MRA-MTC-rLMC and SWAN-rLMC scores may serve as potentially effective, yet preliminary, imaging tools for assessing collateral circulation in CMCAO. A favorable prognosis is indicated by high arterial compensation (elevated MRA-MTC-rLMC score) combined with preserved venous oxygenation (reduced SWAN-rLMC score), establishing their clinical utility for risk stratification.

Introduction

Unilateral chronic middle cerebral artery occlusion (CMCAO) is a significant cause of ischemic stroke, which can manifest as transient ischemic attack (TIA) or disabling acute ischemic stroke (AIS). Collateral circulation refers to the reverse blood flow perfusion to the ischemic region via potential anastomoses or neovascularization when a major supplying cerebral artery is severely stenotic or occluded, playing a crucial role in the disease course [1–3].With the rapid development and widespread application of high-resolution 3.0T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), the regional leptomeningeal collateral (rLMC) score has been increasingly used. This score can be applied to high-resolution three-dimensional time-of-flight magnetic resonance angiography (3D-TOF-MRA) enhanced with magnetization transfer contrast (MTC) (yielding the MRA-MTC-rLMC score) and to susceptibility-weighted angiography (SWAN) for assessing the asymmetrical veins sign (AVS) (yielding the SWAN-rLMC score). AVS includes the asymmetrical cortical veins sign (ACVS) and prominent deep medullary veins (DMV). While the SWAN-rLMC score has been frequently applied in acute ischemic stroke (AIS) [4–8], both the original MRA-MTC-rLMC and SWAN-rLMC scores primarily focus on anterior circulation collateral assessment and do not account for the contribution of the posterior cerebral artery (PCA) to collateral supply.In CMCAO, collateral compensation from the PCA is common. Therefore, we modified the MRA-MTC-rLMC and SWAN-rLMC scores by incorporating the PCA territory and DMV changes. This retrospective study analyzed clinical data and MRA-MTC/SWAN findings from 39 patients with unilateral CMCAO. It aimed to explore the value of these modified scores in assessing collateral blood flow and oxygen extraction fraction (OEF) status, with the ultimate goal of establishing an effective, non-invasive, and easily repeatable method for evaluating collateral circulation and predicting prognosis in CMCAO patients, thereby offering new insights for clinical management.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study enrolled 39 patients with CMCAO from Lianyungang Hospital Affiliated to Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine between January 2020 and December 2024. The cohort included 25 males and 14 females, aged 46–90 years (mean 67.18 ± 10.17). Clinical presentations included headache, dizziness, memory impairment, TIA, and slurred speech.Inclusion criteria: (1) Age > 40 y; (2) MRA-confirmed unilateral occlusion of the M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery (MCA); (3) Presence of vascular risk factors (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, alcohol consumption, smoking).Exclusion criteria: (1) Contraindications to MRI; (2) Coma or unstable vital signs; (3) Seizures at onset; (4) Acute cerebral infarction caused by MCA occlusion; (5) CMCAO resulting from dissection, infection, or immune-mediated vasculitis.All enrolled patients were followed up for 1 month to 5 years via specialized outpatient visits and/or telephone interviews. Poor prognosis was defined as the occurrence of TIA, cerebral infarction in the ipsilateral MCA territory, or death during follow-up. The time from onset to admission, discharge modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score, and admission blood pressure, lipid, and glucose levels were recorded. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local ethics committee, which waived the requirement for informed consent (Approval No: (KY)-02).

MRI protocol

MRI was performed using a GE 3.0T Discovery 750 scanner with a 32-channel head coil. All patients underwent conventional T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), T2-fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2-FLAIR), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) mapping, 3D-TOF-MRA (with MTC), and 3D SWAN sequences.

Scanning parameters: Conventional MRI: T1WI: TR/TE = 1750/21.8 ms; T2WI: TR/TE = 3598/107.3 ms; FLAIR: TR/TE = 8400/87 ms; DWI: TR/TE = 6000/73.5 ms.3D-TOF-MRA: TR/TE = 22/2.6 ms, flip angle = 15°, field of view (FOV) = 22 × 19.4 cm, matrix = 256 × 224.3D SWAN: TR/TE = 37.4/22.9 ms, matrix = 416 × 320, slice thickness = 2 mm, spacing = 0 mm, number of acquisitions = 1, number of excitations (NEX) = 0.70, bandwidth = 62.5 kHz, flip angle = 20°.

Image processing and analysis

The raw MRA and SWAN data were transferred to a GE AW 4.6 workstation for post-processing using dedicated software. Minimum intensity projection (MinIP) was applied to both 3D-TOF-MRA and SWAN source images.

Modified Scoring Criteria: MRA-MTC-rLMC Score: The original 9-region score was modified by adding the PCA territory, resulting in a 10-region system (total 22 points). The regions were: the 6 ASPECTS regions (M1-M6), the anterior cerebral artery (ACA) territory, the basal ganglia region, the PCA territory, and the sylvian fissure region.scoring for M1-6, ACA, Basal Ganglia, PCA: 0 = absent; 1 = less than contralateral side; 2 = equal to or greater than contralateral side.coring for Sylvian Fissure: 0 = absent; 2 = less than contralateral side; 4 = equal to or greater than contralateral side.SWAN-rLMC Score: The original 9-region score was modified by adding the PCA territory for cortical veins (CVS) and the DMV adjacent to the lateral ventricular body, resulting in an 11-region system (total 24 points). The regions were the same as the MRA-MTC-rLMC score plus the DMV territory.Scoring for M1-6, ACA, Basal Ganglia, PCA, DMV: 0 = absent; 1 = less than or equal to contralateral side; 2 = greater than contralateral side.Scoring for Sylvian Fissure: 0 = absent; 2 = less than or equal to contralateral side; 4 = greater than contralateral side.Lateralized ACA/PCA was defined as the affected-side ACA and/or PCA appearing dilated, more numerous, and/or elongated on MRA compared to the healthy side, supplying the ischemic region. A positive AVS was defined on SWAN as thickened and/or increased hypointense linear signals from CVS and/or DMV in the ipsilateral MCA territory compared to the contralateral side.

Two deputy chief physicians specializing in neuroimaging, each with over 15 years of experience and blinded to the clinical outcomes, independently evaluated the images. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 and MedCalc 15.0. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean (± standard deviation) and compared using independent samples t-test; non-normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as numbers (percentages) and compared using the Chi-square (χ²) test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the predictive ability of the different rLMC scores for poor outcomes, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. The optimal cut-off values were determined, and sensitivity and specificity were reported. The Hanley & McNeil method was used to compare AUCs. To better handle the variability of follow-up duration, we further performed time-to-event analyses using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and Cox proportional hazards models, with the modified score cut-offs as grouping variables, to more accurately assess the predictive ability of the scores for the timing of adverse outcomes. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and P < 0.01 was considered highly statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

The clinical presentations of the 39 CMCAO patients included headache/dizziness (n = 22), limb weakness (n = 9), TIA (n = 2), slurred speech (n = 2), and other symptoms like hand numbness (n = 3). Common vascular risk factors were hypertension (89.74%), hyperlipidemia (53.85%), diabetes (28.21%), alcohol consumption (23.08%), and smoking (20.51%). No significant differences in baseline clinical characteristics were found between the good and poor prognosis groups, including age, gender, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, alcohol consumption, and smoking (all P > 0.05, Table 1), indicating balanced baseline data. Specifically, gender distribution also showed no significant difference (P = 0.906, see Table 1).

Discussion

The widespread application of high-resolution 3.0 Tesla (T) 3D Time-of-Flight MRA (3D-TOF-MRA) and 3D Susceptibility-Weighted Angiography (SWAN) has significantly improved spatial resolution and signal-to-noise ratio while reducing acquisition time, enabling clearer visualization of both large and small vessels. The application of Magnetization Transfer Contrast (MTC) technology to 3D TOF MRA, which primarily suppresses signals from protein-rich tissues to indirectly reflect tissue protein content and changes, transfers the magnetization vector from bound water to free water. This mechanism enhances object contrast and allows for superior depiction of tortuous and dilated small collateral vessels [9] (e.g., leptomeningeal anastomoses [LMA], lenticulostriate arteries [LSA]).

The original regional Leptomeningeal Collateral (rLMC) score primarily evaluated collateral blood supply within the anterior circulation territory, omitting assessment of posterior circulation collateral contributions to ischemic regions, which are less frequently involved in acute MCA occlusion but crucial in Chronic Middle Cerebral Artery Occlusion (CMCAO). In clinical practice, MRA post-processed or source images of CMCAO patients often reveal collateral supply to the ischemic hemisphere via lateralized anterior cerebral artery (ACA) and/or posterior cerebral artery (PCA), as well as increased caliber, number, and length of LSA in the basal ganglia compared to the unaffected side (in this cohort, dominant lateralized PCA accounted for 76.9%). The posterior circulation frequently participates in forming collaterals between the PCA and MCA territories following CMCAO. We therefore modified the MRA-MTC-rLMC score by incorporating the PCA territory, expanding it from the original 9 regions to a 10-region system with a total score of 22 points. Based on the interaction plot analysis, an MRA-MTC-rLMC score ≤ 11 defined the poor collateral group, and a score > 11 defined the good collateral group. This modified MRA-MTC-rLMC score is better adapted for rLMC assessment in CMCAO. The prognosis significantly differed between the poor and good collateral groups defined by the modified MRA-MTC-rLMC score, with a highly significant statistical difference favoring the good collateral group (χ² = 31.722, P < 0.001).

SWAN utilizes a 3D Gradient Echo (GRE) sequence with full flow compensation and radiofrequency pulse spoiling techniques, generating contrast based on magnetic susceptibility differences between normal tissues or between tissue and pathology. It is currently the sole imaging modality capable of delineating intracranial veins and accurately reflecting changes in venous deoxyhemoglobin (DHB) content; conventional imaging techniques typically cannot visualize normal cortical vein signs (CVS) or deep medullary veins (DMV) with such clarity. The hypointense signal within cortical veins on SWAN results from an imbalance between oxygen supply and demand in hypoperfused tissue, leading ultimately to a relative increase in DHB and a relative decrease in oxyhemoglobin. Reduced blood flow and chronic ischemia in the CMCAO territory cause relative slowing of flow within the capillary bed, gradually increasing the Oxygen Extraction Fraction (OEF). Consequently, the concentration of DHB—both intravascularly and extravascularly—within the capillary network and draining venules rises. These susceptibility changes manifest on SWAN as prominent hypointense signals from dilated PCA CVS and DMV. In our cohort, PCA CVS was observed in 71.79% and DMV prominence in 56.41% of cases. Consequently, we modified the SWAN-rLMC score for CMCAO by adding regions for PCA CVS and DMV assessment, expanding it from the original 9 regions to an 11-region system with a total score of 24 points. Based on the interaction plot, a SWAN-rLMC score ≤ 12 defined the good collateral group, and a score > 12 defined the poor collateral group. This modified SWAN-rLMC score more accurately reflects rLMC status in CMCAO. The prognoses of the poor and good collateral groups defined by the modified SWAN-rLMC score were significantly different (χ² = 24.633, P < 0.001).

Cerebral hemodynamic and metabolic alterations following CMCAO are dynamic processes, primarily involving two compensatory mechanisms: vascular autoregulation and increased OEF. After CMCAO, blood circulation relies heavily on the microcirculation. In chronic cerebral ischemia, blood flows from arteries to veins within the microcirculation, where increased fluid shear stress in the arterioles [10, 11] triggers vasodilation, reduces vascular resistance, and leads to dilatation of small arteries [12] and wall thickening [13], thereby regulating cerebral blood flow. Subsequently, the compliance of small veins increases following arteriolar dilation, also resulting in dilatation [14], reflecting the opening of collateral pathways and increased blood flow velocity [15]. In CMCAO, the severity of vascular stenosis correlates with the extent of microvascular proliferation, with neovascular networks reported in up to 71% of asymptomatic CMCAO patients [16]. In our cohort, increased and tortuous LSA in the basal ganglia region were observed in 74.36% of cases. High-resolution vessel wall imaging reveals small flow-void collateral vessels surrounding the CMCAO, which correlate with infarct volume. 7T-MRI has confirmed that reduced LSA density is closely associated with lacunar or subcortical cerebral infarction [17]. Long-term cerebral hypoperfusion contributes to brain atrophy and cognitive dysfunction [18–21]. Once collateral compensation in CMCAO becomes insufficient, exceeding the capacity of the vasodilatory autoregulatory mechanism, cerebral blood flow falls significantly. The consequent increase in OEF leads to elevated DHB levels [22], which in turn manifests on SWAN as more numerous and/or prominent venous signals in the cortex and/or medulla of the affected hemisphere, i.e., Asymmetrical Veins Sign (AVS) and/or DMV dilation. AVS and DMV are more frequently observed in patients with large vessel occlusion or severe stenosis, reflect cerebral hypoperfusion, indicate tissue and cerebrovascular oxygen saturation, and serve as neuroimaging markers for assessing hemodynamic status. In our group, AVS was present in approximately 56.41%, and PCA CVS in approximately 71.79% of cases. Increased OEF resulting from persistent hypoperfusion is associated with an elevated risk of TIA or AIS within the affected vascular territory [23–25]. Among the 39 CMCAO patients in our follow-up cohort, 11 experienced cerebral infarction, 8 had TIA, and 1 died.

For CMCAO patients, a higher MRA-MTC-rLMC score indicates better collateral circulation and is associated with a more favorable prognosis, whereas a higher SWAN-rLMC score predicts a worse outcome. Both the MRA-MTC-rLMC and SWAN-rLMC scores demonstrated highly significant statistical value in predicting poor prognosis (P < 0.001), underscoring their substantial clinical utility for prognostic assessment in CMCAO. During follow-up of the 39 CMCAO patients, mRS scores were 0 in 34 cases, 1 in 3 cases, 4 in 1 case, and 6 in 1 case.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center, retrospective study with a relatively small sample size (n = 39), which may limit statistical power and introduce selection bias. Future multi-center, large-sample prospective studies are needed to validate the findings. Second, the follow-up period ranged from 1 month to 5 years, potentially introducing outcome variability; to mitigate this, we performed time-to-event analyses (Kaplan-Meier and Cox regression) as reported in the Results section. Future prospective studies should adopt standardized follow-up intervals (e.g., every 6 months) to reduce bias from temporal variation. Third, this represents a preliminary application of the modified MRA-MTC-rLMC and SWAN-rLMC scores for assessing collateral status in CMCAO, and it lacks direct comparison with digital subtraction angiography (DSA), the reference standard for collateral evaluation. To address this, we plan a prospective study in which an independent cohort of CMCAO patients will undergo DSA within one week after MRA/SWAN. Using DSA-based collateral grading (e.g., ASITN/SIR scale) as the gold standard, we will validate the sensitivity, specificity, and consistency of the modified scores. Fourth, future research could also benefit from combining CTA, CT perfusion (CTP), DSA, high-resolution MR, and MR DSA to further elucidate the relationship between these non-invasive scores and comprehensive collateral hemodynamics in CMCAO patients.

In summary, the modified MRA-MTC-rLMC and SWAN-rLMC scores may serve as potentially effective, yet preliminary, imaging tools for evaluating collateral circulation and are better suited for rLMC assessment in CMCAO. In CMCAO patients, a higher MRA-MTC-rLMC score coupled with a lower SWAN-rLMC score indicates robust collateral circulation in the ischemic territory. Conversely, a lower MRA-MTC-rLMC score with a higher SWAN-rLMC score signifies inadequate collateral circulation. This pattern reflects severely compromised perfusion pressure on the affected side, exceeding the compensatory capacity of vasodilatory autoregulation, and markedly reduced cerebral blood flow. Without active clinical intervention, this precarious hemodynamic state predisposes patients to ischemic stroke, cerebral hemorrhage, and/or accelerated cognitive decline.
