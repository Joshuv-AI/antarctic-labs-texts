Inclisiran Response in a Patient With Familial Hypercholesterolemia After Suboptimal Response to Evolocumab
Authors: Yi-An Pan, Hafiz Saifuddin Golwala, Vasant Shenoy, Karam Kostner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe discovery of proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) has transformed dyslipidemia management. Monoclonal antibodies (PCSK9 inhibitors [PCSK9i]) and small interfering RNA therapy (inclisiran) offer substantial low-density lipoprotein cholesterol reduction in patients intolerant of statins or those requiring intensification beyond conventional lipid-lowering therapy, including individuals with familial hypercholesterolemia.Case SummaryWe report a 34-year-old woman with familial hypercholesterolemia due to a heterozygous LDLR mutation (Afrikaner-2 variant) who had inadequate response to evolocumab but subsequently responded well to inclisiran.DiscussionTo our knowledge, this is the second reported case of this phenomenon. Data from ORION-3 and real-world German cohorts show that inclisiran achieves a more modest low-density lipoprotein cholesterol reduction compared to PCSK9i, likely because it selectively suppresses hepatic PCSK9 synthesis, whereas PCSK9i also neutralize circulating PCSK9 from extrahepatic tissues.Take-Home MessageThis case underscores the importance of clinical persistence and highlights the need for further research into pharmacogenomic determinants of variable interindividual responses to therapy.Key words: dyslipidemias, genetic disorders, primary prevention

Summary of the Case

FH = familial hypercholesterolemia; LDL-C = low density lipoprotein-C; PCSK9i = proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitor.

History of Presentation

A 34-year-old woman attended the endocrine clinic for review of long-standing dyslipidemia, diagnosed during childhood. She had a South African (Afrikaner) ancestry with strong maternal family history of premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD). Her mother had undergone coronary artery bypass grafting at the age of 26, and all maternal relatives had undergone coronary artery bypass grafting between the ages of 20 to 40 years. Shortly after referral, the patient underwent genetic testing for familial hypercholesterolemia (FH), which confirmed a heterozygous missense mutation in the LDLR gene (p.Val429Met) (FH Afrikaner-2). She had been taking atorvastatin 40 mg since childhood, but therapy was interrupted for pregnancy in 2019. Subsequent attempts at conceiving were unsuccessful despite multiple efforts, leading to irregular clinic follow-up during the first few years of diagnosis. In 2024, her mother died of sudden cardiac death, likely due to ischemic heart disease, at the age of 64. Following this, the patient abandoned the notion of subsequent pregnancy and committed to cardiovascular risk profile management more consistently.

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history was notable for Hashimoto's thyroiditis on levothyroxine therapy, polycystic ovarian syndrome, and obesity (weight: 128 kg; body mass index: 40.2 kg/m2).

Differential Diagnosis

The patient had confirmed FH due to a heterozygous LDLR mutation (Afrikaner-2 variant).

Investigations

At the time of initial referral, her lipid profile demonstrated persistent hypercholesterolemia while on established treatment with atorvastatin 20 mg daily, initiated by her family doctor, with low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels consistently ranging between 6 to 8 mmol/L (232-309 mg/dL; reference interval (RI): <2.6 mmol/L [<100 mg/dL]) and total cholesterol between 7 to 9 mmol/L (270-348 mg/dL; RI: <5.5 mmol/L [212 mg/dL]). Despite intensifying atorvastatin therapy to 80 mg daily, her LDL-C and total cholesterol had never fallen below 4 mmol/L (154.6 mg/dL; RI: <2.6 mmol/L [<100 mg/dL]) and 6 mmol/L (232 mg/dL; RI: <5.5 mmol/L [212 mg/dL]), respectively. She initially demonstrated a significant response to 10 mg of ezetimibe with a 45% reduction in LDL-C from baseline, but this effect was not sustained. Both LDL-C and total cholesterol remained elevated and continued to rise despite therapy, even after optimization of her hypothyroidism (Figures 1 and 2).

Management

In addition to atorvastatin and ezetimibe, the patient started evolocumab 140 mg every 2 weeks in late 2024. She also started semaglutide 0.25 mg per week for the management of obesity. Despite 6 months of evolocumab therapy, her LDL-C remained persistently above 4.0 mmol/L (154.6 mg/dL; RI: <2.6 mmol/L [<100 mg/dL]). Compliance and injection techniques were reviewed in clinic, with no gaps identified. She had no dermatological disorders and did not experience any injection-site skin reactions that could interfere with drug absorption. As alirocumab and monthly high-dose (420 mg) evolocumab were no longer available in Australia, switching to an alternative PCSK9 inhibitor (PCSK9i) was not an option. Thus, inclisiran was trialed given the ongoing need for intensive lipid-lowering therapy with her FH diagnosis and family history of premature ASCVD.

Outcome and Follow-Up

After 4 months of inclisiran therapy, the patient achieved a dramatic reduction in LDL-C to 1.9 mmol/L (73.4 mg/dL; RI: < 2.6 mmol/L [<100 mg/dL]), a response not seen on any other therapy previously trialed (Figures 1 and 2). She has experienced progressive weight loss with semaglutide and continues inclisiran every 6 months.

Discussion

FH remains a common but underdiagnosed cause of dyslipidemia. Polygenic FH is the most common cause, with a reported prevalence of around 1:250 to 500 individuals, and the disorder is attributable to variations of multiple genes, all of which affect cholesterol regulation; their additive effects results in dyslipidemia. Currently, more than 50 genetic loci have been identified that can influence LDL-C regulation and contribute to traits of hypercholesterolemia.1 Classical FH predominantly follows an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern and involves the LDLR, APOB, and PCSK9 genes, and these account for approximately 93%, 5%, and 2% of individuals with FH; many variants of mutation exist for each gene.1 Our patient harbored the Afrikaner-2 variant of LDLR mutation, which involves a defect in the epidermal growth factor (EGF) homology domain and is associated with poor recycling of the LDLR. Ex vivo studies using hamster ovary cells demonstrate a reduction in LDLR on the plasma membranes and reduced receptor functional capacity (2% of normal activity) in cells carrying such a mutation.2 Three founder variants of LDLR (FH Afrikaner-1, -2, and -3) account for most of the FH among South African Afrikaners. Previous small-scale studies comparing individuals with these heterozygous variants have shown that Afrikaner-2 is associated with a more severe clinical phenotype, with carriers exhibiting higher total cholesterol and LDL-C levels, as well as a greater likelihood of tendon xanthomas and ASCVD.3 The real-world clinical response to PCSK9i shows more interindividual variability compared to trial data, often attributed to patient-related factors, with medication noncompliance and adherence being the most common. Other contributors include therapeutic inertia and the absence of concurrent statin therapy (usually due to intolerance), improper administration techniques, and variable absorption kinetics due to injection-site reactions or preexisting dermatological disorders. Though development of anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) to PCSK9i can also limit drug efficacy, this is highly unlikely for alirocumab and evolocumab, which are both fully humanized monoclonal antibodies. Both the ODYSSEY and FOURIER trials showed a very low incidence of ADA, occurring in only 0.5% and 0.3% of patients for alirocumab and evolocumab, respectively, over several years.4,5

As PCSK9i acts by inhibiting LDLR degradation, the presence of functional LDLR and APOB is essential for evolocumab to lower LDL-C. Patients with homozygous FH may have a suboptimal response to PCSK9i therapy if they either severely lack functional LDLR or have impaired LDL-LDLR binding due to APOB mutations.6,7 Studies involving individuals with heterozygous FH generally demonstrate treatment response comparable to non-FH patients.7 In our patient, we would expect a reasonable response to evolocumab, as she was heterozygous for the Afrikaner-2 LDLR mutation. In her case, adherence to evolocumab and oral lipid-lowering therapy to therapy was confirmed, pharmacy records confirmed regular dispensing of medications, and she had strong motivation given her mother's sudden cardiac death. In clinic she was able to demonstrate correct injection technique, and there were no dermatological issues at injection sites that would affect medication absorption. The development of ADA is unlikely with only 6 months of drug exposures. The degree of intraindividual LDL-C variability throughout our patient's treatment is atypical for PCSK9i therapy and the majority of patients on oral lipid-lowering therapy. The apparent attenuation in lipid-lowering effect, particularly following initial response to ezetimibe and evolocumab, may have been influenced by factors such as changes in dietary habits, her progressive weight gain (Figure 1), or altered metabolic status, including the rise in thyroid-stimulating hormone observed during follow-up (Figure 2). While intraindividual lipid variability is not currently incorporated into routine clinical practice, it remains an area of growing interest, as greater lipid variability has been associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes.8

Typically, inclisiran achieves a more modest LDL-C reduction compared to PCSK9i, likely because it selectively targets hepatic PCSK9 synthesis compared to PCSK9i, which can also block circulating PCSK9 in other tissues such vascular smooth muscles and macrophage that all play a role in LDL-C disposal. Supporting this, the ORION-3 trial demonstrated that switching from PCSK9i to inclisiran resulted in a 16% increase in baseline LDL-C, while real-world data from German lipid clinics have demonstrated that inclisiran use after PCSK9i resulted in an attenuated LDL-C lowering effect, with 17.6% less LDL-C change at 3 months and 3.3% less at 9 months compared with PCSK9i-naïve patients.9,10

The response to inclisiran in our patient is atypical compared with both trial data and real-world observations, indicating the presence of an alternative mechanism enhancing inclisiran efficacy that is not yet understood. When comparing the modes of action of inclisiran and PCSK9i, potential mechanisms to explain this phenomenon include enhanced activity of asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGPR) leading to increased hepatocyte uptake of inclisiran, or post-translational modifications of PCSK9 that could alter the binding affinity of monoclonal antibodies. Further, inclisiran is a small interfering RNA that acts intracellularly by silencing hepatic PCSK9 mRNA, as opposed to monoclonal antibodies to PCSK9i, which are largely limited to the extracellular domain. In certain subtypes of genetic FH with defects in LDLR recycling, it could be postulated that the intracellular action of inclisiran is important and responsible for better drug efficacy. To our knowledge, this is the second reported patient to demonstrate a significant LDL-C response to inclisiran following a suboptimal response to PCSK9i therapy. In the previously reported patient, LDL-C decreased from 257 mg/dL to 76 mg/dL, representing a 70% reduction from baseline after switching to inclisiran.11 In our patient, LDL-C decreased from 251 mg/dL to 73.47 mg/dL, corresponding to an approximately 70% reduction from baseline.

Based on very limited evidence and the withdrawal of monthly high-dose evolocumab as a therapeutic option in some countries, inclisiran may hold better promise as a therapeutic option in those with FH carrying a genetic defect in LDLR expression and recycling.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is the second reported case of a significant inclisiran response in a patient with suboptimal response to PCSK9i therapy. This case highlights the importance of therapeutic persistence and supports trialing inclisiran as an option for patients who fail to respond to PCSK9i, particularly in those with an underlying genetic defect in LDLR function and recycling.

Take-Home Messages

•Patients with FH who respond poorly to PCSK9i may still achieve substantial LDL-C reduction with inclisiran, highlighting the importance of clinical persistence with therapy.
