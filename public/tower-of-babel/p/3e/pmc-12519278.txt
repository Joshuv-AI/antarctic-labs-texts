Longitudinal study of functional brain networks for processing infant-directed and adult-directed speech during the first year
Authors: Gábor P. Háden, Brigitta Tóth, István Winkler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryInfant-directed speech (IDS) plays a central role in early communication, initially expressing emotion and later supporting language development. This study examined how the brain’s functional network organization responds to infant-directed speech (IDS) versus adult-directed speech (ADS) during early infancy. Electroencephalography (EEG) data were collected from 49 healthy infants at 0, 4, and 9 months as they listened to the same fairy tale spoken in both IDS and ADS. Brain connectivity was assessed using phase lag synchronization across six frequency bands, and network topology was characterized using minimum spanning tree analysis. The delta network showed greater hierarchical and cost-efficient organization in response to IDS, particularly over frontal regions, with these differences becoming more pronounced with age. Over time, network integration increased and shifted toward the left hemisphere. These findings highlight how functional brain networks dynamically adapt to socially relevant speech input, reflecting the evolving communicative function of IDS during the first year of life.

Introduction

Research has shown that the first year of life, particularly around the ages of four and nine months, is crucial for infants’ language acquisition and processing.1 During this time, infants are sensitive to the statistical and abstract properties of speech, which helps them to map critical aspects of their native language.2 By nine months, they are already sensitive to the regular features of their native language’s sound patterns. This early exposure to language and the development of perceptual and vocalization capacities play a key role in the emergence of language in infants.3

The course of language acquisition varies considerably across infants, and it is influenced by how caregivers speak to their offspring.4,5 In response to communicative stimulation, neural networks distributed temporally and anatomically in the infant’s brain develop/adapt to comprehend language.6,7,8 Infant-directed speech (IDS) is a culturally near-universal feature of adult-to-infant communication from birth.9 Infant-directed speech, characterized by exaggerated prosodic features, plays a crucial role in early language development. Mothers modify their speech based on the child’s age, with slower speech flow and higher frequency intonation changes at six months.10 This “hyperspeech” maximizes predictability for the infant, aiding in early word recognition.11 Infants show a preference for this type of speech from birth, suggesting a natural inclination toward it (e.g., Pegg et al.12; Cooper et al.13; Fernald14; Cooper and Aslin15), which also changes throughout early development, a result that appears to be stable between multiple cultures and laboratories.16 However, little is known about how and when this preference is represented in infants’ cortical networks. The study aims to investigate developmental changes in the functional connectivity and network properties of cortical networks involved in speech processing, particularly between IDS and adult-directed speech (ADS). To this end, EEG was recorded from the same infants listening to the same fairytale in infant-directed and adult-directed speech registers at birth, four months, and nine months of age.

Caregivers modify certain linguistic and prosodic aspects of their speech when they address infants.14,15 The primary linguistic features of IDS include fewer words per utterance, increased repetitions, careful articulation, and reduced complexity.9,15 Its prosodic characteristics—exaggerated intonation, higher pitch, slower speech rate, longer pauses, and emphatic stress17—have been shown to influence early speech tracking in infants by facilitating cortical alignment with the speech envelope, specifically at low frequencies.18 This neural tracking of speech rhythms and prosodic contours, particularly in the delta and theta frequency bands, is critical for processing slow, rhythmic aspects of speech, such as syllable boundaries.18 Attaheri et al.19 extended this understanding by demonstrating that infants’ neural oscillations align with the rhythmic structure of sung IDS, especially within the delta and theta bands, which assist in segmenting speech into meaningful units, a foundational step in language acquisition. These oscillations thus form an early basis for recognizing speech rhythm and prosodic elements.

Studies suggest that IDS may facilitate infants' emerging ability to process the speech envelope. Ortiz Barajas et al.20 showed that newborns are sensitive to the amplitude and phase of speech envelopes, suggesting that foundational mechanisms for envelope processing are present from birth. However, their study did not use tracking metrics. This supports the idea that foundational mechanisms for speech envelope processing are present at birth and shaped by experience in early infancy. As early as two months, infants show neural sensitivity to rhythmic features and syllabic-level contrasts in speech.21,22 While infants exhibit early sensitivity to the rhythmic aspects of auditory input, including amplitude-modulated noise,23 evidence for neural tracking of continuous speech at delta and theta frequencies becomes more pronounced around 4 to 7 months.18,19 These early oscillatory mechanisms are believed to lay the groundwork for later phonetic and linguistic development. Therefore, IDS conveys emotional cues24,25 and appears to scaffold language learning by enhancing cortical tracking and the early differentiation of speech sounds.26

Our previous study has shown that the brain has picked up acoustic features of IDS at birth.27 There are at least three distinct but interrelated roles that IDS can play in development. These roles are not mutually exclusive and are hypothesized to become the primary focus of development at different stages of life. The first role, also the earliest in development, is conveying emotional states.24,25 Results showing larger frontal activations for IDS compared to ADS28 in newborns and young children (4–13 months old)29 are compatible with the idea of emotional control. The second role of IDS is facilitating language learning by aiding speech segmentation and phoneme discrimination.26 There is neural evidence for IDS/ADS discrimination on this level: infant-directed vowels elicit larger responses over the right hemisphere.30 Furthermore, discernible cues related to the grammatical structure of a language are also present in IDS,31,32 although it is unclear whether and when the brain utilizes these cues. Finally, the third role that IDS may play in development is to denote the infant as the addressee of communication, thus capturing their attention.33,34,35 The “ostensive signal hypothesis” suggests that the intonation pattern of IDS serves as an ostensive signal, thus stimulating joint attention and helping affect sharing.36,37 Ostensive communication-related brain activity appears over central electrodes in 5-month-old infants.38

Evidence suggests that IDS benefits language acquisition.25 Due to its effect on reading memory and mathematical skills, early language development predicts the child’s future performance in educational systems. Therefore, caregivers must speak to their offspring.39 It is known that conversation-like interactions (such as the peekaboo game) affect the infant’s brain activity and likely their brain development.40 Higher engagement in responsive parental communication during the first year of life is associated with better infant language skills.41 Conversely, one of the leading indicators of the failure to respond to the child’s needs is the “shallow” IDS pattern of depressed mothers.42 These findings underscore the developmental significance of infant-directed speech in newborns, 4-month-olds and 9-month-olds, as they represent critical stages in the infant’s early language acquisition and processing.1

Developing speech comprehension and vocabulary learning requires segmenting continuous speech into meaningful units,6 a process supported by neural oscillations that synchronize with the speech envelope in specific frequency bands. At seven months, infants can reliably segment at least a few words. Oscillatory activity in the delta and theta bands is foundational to speech segmentation, facilitating the parsing of continuous speech into syllabic and prosodic units, and subsequently supporting the recognition of phonetic and phrase-level boundaries necessary for comprehension.43 Indeed, neural oscillations entrain (synchronize) to the syllabic rate of speech sustainably, and neural entrainment to ongoing speech depends on the rate of preceding speech. Significantly, brain-to-speech synchrony at specific rates directly affects how words are understood.44 Early evidence suggests that infants’ brains, even at birth, respond to rhythmic cues in speech.20 Di Liberto et al.,21 in combination with Attaheri et al.,19 demonstrated that delta- and theta-band cortical tracking of the speech envelope is present from as early as two months of age. However, their results suggest that the more robust encoding of fine-grained speech features in these frequency bands emerges around seven months, marking a developmental milestone in speech perception. These findings suggest that while neural entrainment to speech rhythm occurs early, the phonetic-level processing that contributes to speech comprehension undergoes further developmental refinement. This aligns with evidence that infants can track slow auditory rhythms, including those in speech, from around four months of age.19,22 The significance of this ability is further underscored by the finding that IDS, characterized by slower amplitude modulations, enhances infants’ cortical tracking of speech.18 These findings collectively highlight the crucial role of brain oscillations in infants’ speech processing. Keshavarzi et al.45 further highlighted that as infants grow, their cortical encoding of speech becomes more refined, suggesting that neural oscillations facilitate increasingly sophisticated speech processing with age.

During speech perception, widely specialized, distributed cortical networks are dynamically engaged in parallel to accumulate information over multiple timescales.46,47,48,49,50,51 Long-range oscillatory synchronization (also referred to as functional connectivity – FC) is a potential candidate for assessing the operation of such neural mechanisms, as it measures coupling between cortical regions over time.52,53 For example, Obleser and colleagues54 found that the success of the comprehension of spectrally degraded speech is correlated with FC strength between cortical areas outside the auditory cortex, assumed to be involved in higher-order speech processes. Studies such as Keshavarzi et al.45 demonstrate how advanced EEG analyses and machine learning can decode speech responses in infants, with delta and theta oscillations offering particularly informative features.

The spontaneous dynamic interaction between distributed networks of functionally specialized brain areas undergoes significant changes across the early lifespan (from birth to four months), with substantial consequences for cognitive development through learning and plasticity.53 The organization of these functionally connected brain areas can be examined through graph representations, providing an abstract description of network elements and their interactions.55,56,57 In healthy adults, EEG/MEG functional networks exhibit (1) a small-world organization,58 (2) hierarchical modular topology,59,60 and (3) densely connected hub regions.61

This efficacy of communication between brain regions during rest is assumed to be crucial for healthy cognitive function,62,63 and it is relevant for the development of cortical circuits, as demonstrated by changes in neural synchrony during development.64,65 Generally, functional connections develop throughout the human lifespan.64,65,66 The development of synchronized oscillations between frontal and parietal regions, as well as between the hemispheres, is crucial for cerebral maturation.67,68 This network synchrony develops progressively over infancy, with studies showing that even at seven months, infants exhibit reliable multisensory brain responses to naturalistic stimuli.69

Few studies have assessed the early development of brain networks70 using EEG in healthy newborns.71,72,73 These studies have shown network reorganization resulting from maturation or changes in external stimulation after birth. Subnetworks develop later during infancy and gradually become differentiated during childhood, likely linked to cognitive abilities.60,74 Infants exhibit a strengthening of FC in frontocentral areas in the beta and alpha bands, while the structure of these networks undergoes a lesser degree of change.75 Abnormal FC topology has been associated with neurocognitive disorders, including autism spectrum disorders.76 Thus, the slower maturation of FC during the early postnatal period may predict subsequent language acquisition delays. EEG studies employing advanced analytical approaches, such as machine-learning models by Keshavarzi et al.,45 have demonstrated the potential to decode speech information from infants’ EEG responses, providing insights into how brain network efficiency and connectivity evolve with age.

While much developmental FC literature is based on resting-state data (e.g., Fransson et al.66; Uhlhaas et al.64), the present study investigates FC during continuous auditory stimulation. Task-evoked FC reflects dynamic, stimulus-driven coordination between brain regions, offering a complementary perspective to intrinsic networks. The stimulation-based approach directly assesses how cortical networks respond to specific inputs—in this case, speech in infant- and adult-directed registers—and how these networks evolve. Notably, stimulus-evoked FC is particularly suited for studying developmental changes in auditory and language-related networks, as it can reveal how infants’ brains functionally organize in response to communicative cues during a period of heightened plasticity and learning.

The present study examines the topological organization of large-scale brain networks using graph-theoretical measures. These metrics offer insights into how the brain’s network structure changes in response to IDS and ADS, providing a more detailed mechanistic understanding of how different types of speech are encoded in the infant brain. The minimum spanning tree (MST; Figure 1) approach provides a global, structure-preserving view of functional brain networks, enabling the comparison of overall network organization without arbitrary connectivity thresholds. This longitudinal study follows infants from birth to nine months, recording EEG responses to both IDS and ADS. Using graph-theoretical measures of FC, we aim to determine whether IDS enhances connectivity within neural networks associated with speech processing and how these networks evolve with age. We hypothesize that infants will exhibit an age-related increase in functional network complexity in response to IDS, with improvements in connectivity observed between 4 and 9 months of age. We assume that essential aspects of the developmental trajectory of speech processing during the first year can be extracted from EEG brain networks: (1) the development of speech-processing brain networks at different timescales and (2) changes in sensitivity to IDS relative to ADS.

Development of the differential processing of infant-directed speech during the first year of life

Brain network topology

The integration capacity of the delta brain network (measured by DIAM) differentiated between the processing of speech registers in a manner significantly changing over the first year of infancy (AGE × SPEECH REGISTER interaction in the DIAM parameter (F(2,74) = 4.110, p = 0.02, ε = 0.995, ηp2 = 0.1; 0m ID & AD > 4m ID, 9m ID & AD (p < 0.006, all)), see Figure 4A). Specifically, DIAM within the delta band network was higher when listening to ID than AD speech. The difference was significant at four months of age but not at the other two measurement points, resulting in significant interaction. Network hub topography

The scalp topography of network node “hubness” (measured by BC) differentiated the processing of speech registers and it was significantly changing over the first year of infancy, varying also across electrodes (AGE × SPEECH REGISTER × ELECTRODE interaction in BC for the beta band; see Figure 5; F(28,1036) = 1.722, p = 0.045, ε = 0.519, ηp2 = 0.044). Discussion

The present study investigated how speech encoding differs in newborns and four- and nine-month-old infants listening to IDS vs. ADS. While prior work has shown that delta and theta oscillations are essential for tracking the temporal dynamics of speech, the present study reveals that IDS shapes the global and local organization of the brain’s functional networks in these frequency bands, making speech processing more efficient and centralized in the infant brain. In summary, (1) MST shows that during IDS, the infant’s brain forms more efficient communication networks, suggesting that it can track the slow rhythms of IDS more effectively. (2) LF highlights the brain’s tendency to employ specialized, modular networks for processing key auditory features of IDS. (3) TH shows that IDS promotes central hubs, possibly in frontal regions, which coordinate the processing of speech rhythm and prosody. (4) DIAM reflects the brain’s ability to integrate information across the network more efficiently during IDS, which may facilitate early language learning. These metrics complement traditional measures of EEG oscillations by providing a network-level view of speech processing, offering insights into how the brain’s large-scale organization supports speech encoding. In conclusion, our findings indicate that the topology of brain networks undergoes significant changes in response to IDS, particularly in the delta, theta, and beta bands. These changes suggest that IDS engages auditory and linguistic circuits more strongly, helping to organize the brain’s network architecture to support early speech processing and language learning.

By considering these graph-theoretical measures in the context of infant brain development, we can infer that the organization and dynamics of neural networks are in a continuous maturation process. The present study demonstrated a qualitative change in the networks between 0 and 4 months of age in the delta and theta bands. Both in the delta and theta bands, a decrease in Diameter and an increase in Leaf Fraction can be observed from newborn to 4 months of age (Figure 2). LF, which measures the proportion of nodes with only one connection (leaves), reflects the degree of segregation in the network. A higher LF indicates more isolated nodes, suggesting greater modularity within the network. In the delta band, the present findings of higher LF suggest that the infant’s brain develops the ability to organize its processing into more modular sub-networks when listening to speech. This modularity may reflect the brain’s developing ability to isolate specific auditory features (e.g., syllable stress, pitch changes) characteristic of speech and essential for early language learning. DIAM measures the longest distance between any two nodes in the network, indicating the level of network integration. A lower DIAM suggests higher integration, meaning that information can flow more efficiently across the entire network. The lower DIAM with age suggests that the brain’s network is more integrated when at 4 months of age compared to newborn age. This increased integration in the delta band suggests that the infant’s brain can better coordinate information across distant brain regions when processing speech after 4 months of birth. Given that delta oscillations are critical for processing the slow temporal structure of speech (prosody19,20), the lower DIAM reflects the brain’s ability to integrate rhythmic and prosodic information efficiently, which is essential for segmenting speech and identifying linguistic units such as syllables. Research highlights the importance of delta (1–4 Hz) and theta (4–8 Hz) rhythms in speech-envelope tracking in infants aged 2–8 months, which is critical for parsing syllables and prosody.19,20 Delta oscillations align with slower prosodic features, such as syllable boundaries, while theta oscillations have been associated with tracking faster modulations in the speech envelope.19 Low-frequency theta and delta bands synchronize with syllabic and phrasal speech dynamics (∼5 Hz and ∼2 Hz, respectively), while beta-range activity has been proposed to support the processing of fine acoustic detail in speech in adults,43,77 though its role in infancy remains less clear. These frequency bands are crucial for processing infant-directed (IDS) and adult-directed speech (ADS). This aligns with the present results, where a more organized delta network during speech processing suggests that infants are well-adapted to their rhythmic cues early in development.

In contrast, no significant changes can be observed between 4 and 9 months (Figure 2). This can be interpreted as a shift occurring in speech-processing networks before 4 months. Essential developmental changes occur between these ages (e.g., a stronger preference for social sounds,78 the ability to participate in peekaboo-type games,79 and the ability to produce vowel-like sounds and babble80). Recent findings emphasize the role of speech-envelope tracking in language development, showing both innate predispositions and experience-driven adjustments.20,81,82 The absence of significant alpha-band results aligns with previous studies on infant language.70 The present study found that the delta band showed significant differences in brain-network topology when infants were exposed to IDS versus ADS. These findings align with earlier research by Kalashnikova et al.18 and Attaheri et al.,19 which indicate that IDS enhances cortical tracking in the delta band, particularly with its exaggerated prosodic features.

Specifically, the data revealed higher LF and TH values in the delta band when infants listened to IDS than AD, implying that the infant’s brain organizes its processing into more hierarchical and modular sub-networks when listening to IDS. This means that the infant’s brain organizes itself into sub-networks during IDS, isolating specific auditory features such as syllable stress and pitch changes and handling different aspects of prosody and rhythm, which are crucial to distinguishing speech structures and facilitating language acquisition in infants. These findings align with those of Attaheri et al.,19 who emphasized the delta band’s role in processing slow temporal modulations, thereby aiding speech segmentation. The present study found that delta band networks were more hierarchical (TH) during IDS than ADS. This suggests that IDS promotes a more centralized network, where key brain regions (or “hubs”) coordinate the flow of information across the network. This implies that certain brain areas (e.g., frontal cortex) dominate organizing and integrating speech information. In speech encoding, a higher TH during IDS could indicate that the infant’s brain relies on central hubs to process the exaggerated rhythmic patterns of IDS. These hubs may be critical for coordinating the temporal segmentation of speech, allowing infants to track syllable boundaries and speech rhythms more effectively. The hierarchical structure suggests that the infant’s brain employs specialized hubs for processing the prosodic richness of IDS, which are key to early speech learning. This hierarchical network is thought to support the segmentation of rhythmic patterns, as infants rely on exaggerated prosodic cues for parsing speech.83

The speech register effect on the DIAM of the network in the delta band is modified by age, with only 4-month-old infants showing a significant impact (Figure 4A). The present results of delta band networks showed a lower DIAM during IDS, indicating that the brain’s network is more integrated when processing IDS than ADS. This increased integration in the delta band during IDS suggests that the infant’s brain can better coordinate information across distant brain regions when processing IDS. Given that delta oscillations are critical for processing the slow temporal structure of speech, the lower DIAM in IDS reflects the brain’s ability to integrate rhythmic and prosodic information efficiently, which is essential for segmenting speech and identifying linguistic units, such as syllables. In other words, the delta band network was more cost-efficient during IDS, with fewer connections needed to maintain robust communication. This may suggest that IDS enhances network efficiency, emphasizing prosodic cues that assist in speech segmentation—a finding echoed by Keshavarzi et al.,45 who noted improvements in neural processing efficiency as infants mature. This is crucial for processing slow temporal structures, such as prosody, enabling the brain to integrate speech information across distant regions and facilitating early language acquisition.35

A significant age-related effect was observed in the delta band, particularly in 4-month-old infants. At this age, the network diameter was larger for ADS than for IDS, indicating a shift in processing efficiency. The age-specific effect in the current findings may suggest that prosodic cues in IDS are particularly well-tuned to the perceptual abilities of 4-month-olds, possibly due to caregiver adaptations in speech style as infants develop.35 The findings align with research by Thiessen et al.,26 which indicates that prosody supports speech segmentation during this developmental phase. The results underscore the delta band’s sensitivity to prosodic features, reinforcing its importance in early language acquisition.84

The present study found age-related changes in network dynamics in the theta band, particularly in the processing of IDS and ADS. Betweenness centrality (BC) decreased with age across most electrodes in the theta band, except at Pz, suggesting a shift toward a more distributed speech-processing network (Figure 3A). This transition likely reflects brain maturation as infants develop specialized neural circuits, consistent with findings by Ortiz Barajas et al.,20 who observed maturation in speech-envelope tracking during infancy, indicating improved the processing of complex speech in the first year of life. Topographical differences in the theta band between IDS and ADS were more pronounced in older infants, particularly in midline-right areas, suggesting a role in prosody processing (Figure 6). Interestingly, BC values were higher for ADS than IDS in the theta band, potentially due to the greater effort required to process ADS’s less prominent prosodic features.85

In newborns, higher BC may indicate a more centralized communication network, reflecting the brain’s reliance on key regions for early sensory and motor processing, as other areas are under-developed.73 As infants age, the decrease in BC suggests a move toward more regionally specialized networks with less dependence on central hubs. This reduction in “hubness” aligns with neural maturation, where the brain becomes more modular and specialized, supporting complex cognitive tasks such as language and social interaction. By 4 to 9 months, the brain is expected to show increased modularity, with regions becoming more functionally specialized.

Theta-band effects distinguishing IDS and ADS were significant in older infants, with the most substantial effects at 4 months (Figure 6). In line with previous research,23,86 the right hemisphere showed increased BC for the theta band, which is associated with processing suprasegmental speech features87,88 and is observed in this age group.85 The midline-right localization points to prosodic processing, yet the data show higher BC for ADS despite IDS’s more pronounced prosodic cues. This may be due to the increased effort needed to process ADS’s subtler prosody.

As infants develop from 0 to 9 months, the network shifts toward the left hemisphere, with frontal cortex nodes becoming more central, indicating growing network integration. This leftward shift aligns with increasing specialization in speech processing as infants' brain networks become more efficient and language processing moves toward left-hemisphere dominance.89 In the beta band, IDS showed higher BC over left frontal electrodes, whereas ADS exhibited higher BC over right frontal electrodes, a pattern consistent across all ages (Figure 5). This suggests the differential processing of rhythmic and possibly articulatory features, aligning with findings by Ní Choisdealbha et al.,22 who linked early rhythm tracking to later language outcomes. The left hemisphere’s stronger response to IDS may reflect early lateralization processes that support later language development.84 This lateralization reflects a trend toward left-hemisphere dominance for language, consistent with other studies.90,91 This lateralization mirrors the adult language-processing pattern, where left-hemispheric areas dominate syntactic and semantic processing, while prosody is processed more in right-hemispheric areas.86 The observed asymmetry in the beta band may reflect differences in how the acoustic features of IDS and ADS are processed, aligning with studies that highlight vowel hyper-articulation in IDS.92 Although hemispheric asymmetry develops later in childhood,93 early signs are visible in infants as young as 0–9 months.

Age interacted with the differences between IDS and ADS in the beta band over midline frontal and parietal electrodes. A notable pattern was the lower BC for IDS at 9 months, suggesting developmental shifts in network centrality. At 4 months, a BC dip was observed at the Pz electrode, particularly for ADS. This aligns with findings by Yin et al.,94 who identified a “V”-shaped connectivity pattern in language and motor areas, highlighting the dynamic nature of neural maturation. The changes underscore the importance of longitudinal studies, as they can capture age-specific patterns that are often missed in cross-sectional research.

The present study found that beta-band networks are more integrated and hierarchical during IDS than ADS. IDS displayed higher LF and TH values across all age groups, suggesting more efficient processing. This finding is consistent with research indicating that the infant brain operates with a “small-world” network organization, which balances local clustering and global integration for efficient communication.95 As infants develop, their networks transition from centralized to distributed hubs, facilitating the development of complex cognitive functions.96 These findings offer a network -level perspective on infant speech processing, complementing previous studies that have focused on phase-locked neural responses in the delta and theta bands.23 The study demonstrates that IDS contributes to the formation of hierarchical and cost-efficient neural networks, thereby scaffolding early language development. This supports the idea that IDS provides infants with crucial linguistic cues, aiding language acquisition.26,97 Our study highlights that as infants develop, their speech-processing networks become more integrated, exhibiting a clear shift toward left-hemisphere specialization. This development, however, is not linear. Instead, the brain undergoes multiple stages of reorganization, with different aspects of speech processing evolving in parallel across distinct EEG bands. These processes are reflected in the evolving role of IDS during the first year of life, likely influenced by changes in the properties of IDS itself. Sensitivity to IDS appears to fluctuate within specific neural networks, illustrating the complex dynamics of early brain maturation. The present findings suggest that IDS-specific differences in the delta, theta, and beta bands are not just early indicators of sensitivity to prosodic cues but also instrumental in shaping the structural organization of the brain’s functional networks. The increased hierarchical organization, cost efficiency, and frontal cortex centrality observed during IDS processing underscore its critical role in establishing early neural pathways that are fundamental for later language development. By 9 months, the shift toward more integrated and left-hemisphere-dominant networks suggests a transition from general attentional and emotional responses to IDS to more specialized linguistic processing.

These results highlight the dynamic nature of the developing brain, demonstrating how IDS actively influences the formation of neural networks. As infants mature, their brains become more adept at processing speech, with greater specialization in left-hemisphere areas and more integrated networks. This finding aligns with existing literature on early auditory experiences and brain lateralization, reinforcing that IDS is pivotal in supporting language acquisition.86,93 Efficient processing of IDS through hierarchical and specialized networks is a critical factor in early language development. IDS’s exaggerated prosodic features likely make it easier for infants to process linguistic and emotional information simultaneously, enhancing their ability to allocate cognitive resources efficiently.95 This efficient processing is essential in infancy when the brain is rapidly developing and must manage resources across various cognitive tasks. These findings suggest that IDS captures attention and arousal, supporting the development of efficient, hierarchical neural structures crucial for language learning.84,89

Limitations of the study

A significant limitation of the current study is that neonates were asleep, whereas 4- and 9-month-olds were awake. This confound may explain the more considerable changes between 0 and 4 months than between 4 and 9 months (A reasonable control would be to record 4- and 9-month-old infants asleep. This, however, is not easy to do. One possible source of the difference between newborns and infants is the sensitivity of PLI to spectral content that varies not only between ages but also between sleep/wake states.) On the other hand, neonatal sleep is not identical to sleep later in life.98 Some aspects of sleep -stage stimulus processing are altered to a lesser degree, although the effect on functional network connectivity remains unclear in infants.99 Therefore, it is likely that at least some of the brain-network differences found between newborns and the other two age groups reflect developmental changes. (Unfortunately, the recording time in newborns and the size of the group followed did not allow us to gather sufficient data to compare different neonatal sleep stages and the data collected at later ages.).

Another limitation is the lack of non-speech control. The validity of interpreting some of the topographical changes as specific to the development of speech processing would have benefited from a comparison with changes occurring in general sound processing during the same period. Again, the limited recording time prevented the collection of control data, especially in the two later age groups. However, changes toward a more distributed network may be a general tendency during early development toward the “ideal” small-world networks (a type of network where most nodes are not directly connected but can be reached from one another through a small number of intermediate links). Speech processing would benefit from this tendency, and areas undertaking more advanced speech processing are likely to emerge. On the other hand, the finding of increasing left lateralization aligns well with the literature, suggesting it is likely specific to speech processing.

The low number of electrodes recorded (a limitation imposed by the recording equipment used for newborn data collection) precludes detailed topography analysis. Given the low temporal resolution and the ambiguity of the sources, our study was limited to lateralization and inference to gross brain areas along the frontal-central-parietal direction, which are sufficiently reliable in the data. Furthermore, the topological measures reflect important large-scale structural properties of the newborn brain networks.73

Additionally, significant methodological differences exist when comparing data from newborns to older infants, which introduces challenges in interpretation. Differences in EEG signal characteristics, neural maturation, and sensitivity to speech stimuli across developmental stages could contribute to the observed effects. Future studies with additional methodological controls, such as sleep-state matching across age groups and expanded data collection, would help clarify the observed developmental changes. Moreover, our conclusions should be interpreted cautiously due to these methodological constraints and the need for additional data to fully support the findings. One limitation of our approach is the potential variability in EEG signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) across development due to anatomical changes such as fontanelle closure and increased skull thickness, which may influence connectivity estimates. While using high sample sizes and large numbers of epochs helps mitigate this issue, future studies combining EEG with anatomical imaging could better account for individual variability in head structure.

Furthermore, we acknowledge that transitioning from a gel-based EEG system in newborns to a saline-based system in older infants may introduce some variability due to potential differences in SNR. As a connectivity measure, PLI is sensitive to added noise, which could lead to an underestimation of connectivity in the saline-based system. While an ideal control would involve recording the same subjects with both systems to compare connectivity estimates directly, practical constraints prevent such an approach. Additionally, recalculating the results using alternative measures, such as the weighted phase lag index (wPLI), could provide further insights; however, differences in the number of electrodes and the recording conditions still pose challenges for direct comparisons. Future studies should systematically assess the influence of different EEG systems on connectivity estimates to ensure methodological consistency in developmental research.

The caretakers’ infant-directed speech changes together with the development of their child;100 however, see also Kalashnikova and Burnham.39 Thus, presenting the same ID stimulus to different age groups (which allows comparability in the acoustic/phonetic sense) or other age-appropriate ID speech segments (which may better match the infants’ current experience with ID speech) is complex. We chose the first alternative, but acknowledge that some effects could have been more substantial with the second alternative. A future study could contrast these two strategies within the same infants.

Experimental model and study participant details

EEG was recorded from 49 healthy, full-term infants (21 male) three times: at the ages of 1-4 days (0 months), 120-151 days (4 months), and 274-317 days (9 months) postpartum. All infants were firstborns, none of them twins. The mean gestation age was 39.95 weeks (SD=0.98), and the birth weight was 3411 g (SD=354.96). Data recorded from 11 infants were excluded based on the criterion of retaining 40.96 seconds of EEG signal after artifact rejection (at least 10 epochs of 4096 ms duration) in all experimental conditions and at all ages. Thus, 38 infants (14 male) were included in the final sample. Informed consent was obtained from one (mother) or both parents when the family was recruited for the longitudinal study. The study was conducted in full accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and all applicable national laws. The relevant ethics committees approved it: the Medical Research Council-Committee of Scientific and Research Ethics (ETT-TUKEB study approval number 17187-3) in Hungary and the United Ethical Review Committee for Research in Psychology (EPKEB study approval number 77_2015) in Hungary.

Stimuli and procedure

Procedure at birth

Infants were presented with recordings of a fairy tale delivered in Hungarian in IDS and ADS registers by a native Hungarian-speaking mother. When recording, the mother directed her words to her 4.5-month-old infant or the experimenter, respectively. Recordings were conducted in an anechoic room using a single sound channel and were later converted to a dual mono format. The duration of the two recordings differed from each other (ADS = 104 s; IDS = 144 s). Recordings were presented binaurally using the E -Prime stimulus-presentation software (Psychology Software Tools, Inc., Pittsburgh, PA, USA) with ER-1 headphones (Etymotic Research Inc., Elk Grove Village, IL, USA) connected via sound tubes to self-adhesive ear cups (Sanibel Supply, Middelfart, Denmark) placed over the infants’ ears. Stimuli were presented in two blocks. Within each block, the recording of both IDS and ADS registers was presented in random order and was once repeated in the same order in the first block. The order was reversed for the second block. The within-block inter-stimulus interval between recordings was 10 s. During the recording, both registers were presented four times, totaling 18 minutes. The experiment took place at the Department of Obstetrics, Gynecology, and Perinatal Intensive Care Unit, Military Hospital, Budapest, with only the infant and the experimenter present in the room. This experiment was part of a session combining multiple experiments, which were always presented in the same order: (1) fairy tale in IDS and ADS (Block I of the current study), (2) Hungarian words delivered in IDS and ADS (5 min., same speaker as in the current study; data published in Háden et al.27), (3) entrainment to speech -like artificial sounds (15 min.), (4) an oddball sequence with tones of two different durations (15 min.); (5) fairy tale in IDS and ADS (Block II of the current study), and (6) resting state (no stimulation; 5 min). The session was part of a longitudinal study to assess the roles of IDS and ADS in speech acquisition. Data from the other experiments and their associations will be published separately once the complete data set has been collected.

Procedure at four and nine months of age

The stimuli were identical to those used in the newborn-age recordings. However, the limited time in these age groups necessitated only one presentation of each stimulus/register (ca. 4.5 minutes overall duration). The order of the stimuli was randomized. Stimuli were presented in a sound-attenuated room using Matlab (MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA) and Psychtoolbox.101 The sound signal from the computer was amplified by a Yamaha A-S301 amplifier (Hamamatsu, Japan) and presented through a pair of Boston Acoustics A25 speakers (Woburn, MA, USA) at a comfortable volume level. The speakers were positioned ca. 1.75 meters in front of the participant, 70 cm apart. This experiment was part of a session combining multiple experiments. As it was always the first experiment, the rest are not listed. The experiment took place in the sound -attenuated and Faraday-shielded infant EEG laboratory of the Institute of Cognitive Neuroscience, located within the Research Centre for Natural Sciences. The infant sat comfortably in their mother's lap, while the experimenter used toys to keep the infant facing toward the loudspeakers and their attention away from the electrode net. The mother listened to music through closed-canal audiometric headphones to isolate herself from the experimental stimulation. If the infant became fussy, the playback stopped, and the experimenter attempted to pacify him/her. If pacifying was successful, the playback restarted from the beginning; otherwise, the experiment was discontinued.

Method details

EEG recording

EEG recording at birth

EEG was recorded using Ag/AgCl electrodes attached to the scalp at the following locations: Fp1, Fp2, Fz, F3, F4, F7, F8, T3, T4, Cz, C3, C4, Pz, P3, and P4 (location naming follows the international 10-20 system). The reference electrode was placed on the nose tip, and the ground electrode on the forehead. EEG was digitized with 24-bit resolution at a sampling rate of 1 kHz by a direct-coupled amplifier (V-Amp, Brain Products GmbH, Germany). The signals were online low-pass filtered at 110 Hz. The impedance values were monitored throughout the recording session and were kept under 10 kΩ whenever possible; however, no recordings were rejected based on impedance measurements. Although high impedance can introduce additional noise into the recordings, it may not be systematic in the entire group, and its effects are mitigated by filtering out the very low frequencies, which are more likely to be contaminated by drifts. Infants’ sleep state was determined based on behavioural criteria.102 Infants were predominantly asleep during the recordings, in quiet sleep 82 % of the time and in active sleep 8.2 % of the time.

EEG recording at four and nine months of age

EEG was recorded using a 60-channel HydroCel GSN net (64-channel v1.0 layout, with channels 61–64 connected to ground in the small paediatric caps used here) with a GES 400 DC amplifier, which passed the digitised signal to the NETSTATION v5.4.1.1 software (both from Electrical Geodesics, Eugene, OR, USA). Signals were recorded online at a sampling rate of 1,000 Hz with the Cz electrode as the reference. Electrode impedance during recording was maintained below 50 kΩ. Electrodes corresponding to the Fp1, Fp2, Fz, F3, F4, F7, F8, T3, T4, Cz, C3, C4, Pz, P3, and P4 locations were selected for further analysis to maintain compatibility with the recordings in newborns. Stimuli were presented with Matlab and Psychtoolbox.101

EEG data analysis

EEG was analyzed using the EEGLAB103 open-source Matlab (MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA) toolbox. The signals were first band-pass filtered (using a zero-phase band-pass, Hamming-windowed sinc FIR filter with a filter order of 33,000) to the 0.5–45 Hz frequency range, then re-referenced to the average of all electrodes. Continuous EEG data were segmented into 4096-ms-long epochs with 50 % overlap between successive epochs. This epoch length is sufficient to assess oscillatory activity in the delta-to-beta frequency bands, as it covers at least two cycles of the highest frequency.71,104 Segments contaminated by physiological artifacts (e.g., eye movements, muscle activity) or external artifacts (e.g., environmental noise) were rejected by visual inspection. A minimum of 10 epochs was considered sufficient105,106 for further functional-connectivity analysis. The final dataset consisted of an average of 49.81 epochs (SD = 17.13) per participant. The final epoch included into the analysis at newborn age was on average for ID 67.6 (SD = 27.8) and AD 49.9 (SD = 22.0); at 4 months the final epoch number was 51.7 (SD = 20.5) for ID and 36.1 (SD = 14.2) for AD; at 9 months the final epoch number for ID was 54.0 (SD = 10.8) and 39.5 (SD = 17.2) for AD. Statistical tests confirmed no significant differences in epoch numbers across comparisons. Epochs were then filtered (using zero-phase band-pass, Hamming-windowed FFT; filter order 1650) into five frequency bands (delta: 0.5–4 Hz; theta: 4–8 Hz; alpha: 8–12 Hz; beta: 12–30 Hz; gamma: 30–45 Hz). Studying connectivity across multiple frequency bands enables comparisons with existing literature and adult studies (see the recommendations in Tsolisou et al.107). This approach facilitates cross-species and cross-age comparisons, aiding in the identification of developmental changes and similarities/differences in neural-network organization. The strength of functional connectivity (FC) was calculated as the pairwise phase -synchronization strength between EEG electrodes, separately for the five frequency bands and each epoch. The phase-lag index (PLI), developed by Stam et al.,108 was computed using BrainWave software version 0.9.151.5 (http://home.kpn.nl/stam7883/brainwave.html). PLI is an undirected measure of connectivity that quantifies the consistency with which one signal is phase-leading or lagging relative to another.

Graph-theoretical measures

Network analysis was performed on the graph-theoretical representation of the functional connectivity matrix using the so-called minimum spanning tree (MST) approach (Figure 1A; for reviews, see58,109). The MST approach has been successfully employed to describe the FC-network properties of healthy newborn infants (e.g., hierarchical structure, degree distribution).73 In the current study, the edge-weighted undirected graph consists of nodes representing the EEG electrodes (N = 16) and edges (N = (16 × 16)/2 – 16) representing the connectivity values between each pair of nodes, with the edge weights being the inverse PLI. MST is a subset of the edges (N = 16) of a graph that connects all the nodes without cycles (creating only one possible path between each pair of nodes) and with the minimum possible total edge weight (using the edges with the strongest FCs). MST networks were constructed using Kruskal’s algorithm.110 An MST was separately built for each epoch and frequency band. In this study, we sought to understand the implications of these network properties for infant brain development. Graph measures, often used in adult studies, have been informative in understanding efficient information processing and integration in the mature brain. However, their interpretation in infants requires more nuanced consideration. While the infant brain differs significantly from the adult brain in terms of structure and function, these graph-theoretical metrics can still offer insights into the organization and maturation of neural networks during infancy.

Graph measures described by Stam and colleagues58,105 are illustrated in Figures 1B and 1C. The MST-network characteristic values were normalized by dividing them by the number of EEG channels used. The global MST-network characteristics were averaged across epochs, separately for each infant and frequency band. Diameter (DIAM), a measure of the network’s integration, refers to the largest distance between any two nodes in the MST, where distance corresponds to the number of edges needed to traverse between nodes (the shortest path). A lower diameter suggests that information can flow more efficiently across the entire network, implying greater integration. In the infant brain, a lower DIAM may indicate that communication across distant brain regions is more efficient, which could reflect a more cohesive neural architecture supporting early sensory and motor processing.

Leaf Fraction (LF) and Tree Hierarchy (TH) capture the network’s degree of segregation. LF is the number of nodes with only one connected edge divided by the total number of nodes in the MST. A higher LF, denoting a larger proportion of leaf nodes, implies a more segregated or modular network—reflecting isolated or less-integrated regions within the brain. Higher LF might suggest that the brain’s networks are still organized into more specialized areas in early development. Tree Hierarchy (TH), which ranges from 0 (line-like) to 1 (star-like/centralized), indicates the degree of hierarchy in the network. A higher TH value indicates a more organized, hierarchical network that supports complex information flow (for a mathematical description, see105,109).

Betweenness Centrality (BC), also known as “hubness,” assesses the influence of individual nodes (brain regions) within a network. It is the normalized fraction of all shortest paths connecting two nodes that pass through the particular node.56 BC is calculated separately for each node and can be plotted over the scalp to identify hubs.73 Nodes with high BC serve as central points of information flow, potentially playing a critical role in coordinating communication across the brain. While hubness has been linked to cognitive control and efficient information processing in adults, its role in infants is less clear—yet no less significant.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with the STATISTICA software package. Greenhouse-Geisser correction was employed to correct for sphericity violations111 where appropriate, and the correction factor (ε) is reported where applicable, as well as the ηp2 effect-size value. A Tukey correction was applied to the post-hoc pairwise comparisons. Both topological (LF, DIAM, and TH) and topographical (BC) measures were used to characterize the development of processing ID and AD speech during the first year of life. Note that extremely significant p = 0.0001 to 0.001 is shown with ∗∗∗, very significant p = 0.001 to 0.01 with ∗∗, and significant p = 0.01 to 0.05 with ∗.

Topology of the brain networks

For testing whether the brain networks were affected by development (AGE, levels of 0 vs. 4 vs. 9 months) and by the SPEECH REGISTER (with levels of ID vs. AD), repeated-measures ANOVAs were used to compare the characteristics of the infant FC networks, separately for each frequency band and network measure (LF, DIAM, and TH). Due to the potential confound caused by the different recording setups and sleep/awake states between newborns and the other two measurement times, we performed a secondary analysis testing network reorganization from four to nine months of age by limiting the above-described ANOVAs to 4 vs. 9 months of age.

Topography of the brain network hubs

For testing whether the hub nodes are affected by development during the first year, repeated-measure ANOVAs were conducted for BC, separately for each frequency band by using AGE (0 vs. 4 vs. 9 months), SPEECH REGISTER (ID vs AD) and ELECTRODE (the 15 electrodes common to all three age groups). Similarly to the topological variables, the same analysis was repeated with only the data from 4- vs. 9-month-old infants included.
