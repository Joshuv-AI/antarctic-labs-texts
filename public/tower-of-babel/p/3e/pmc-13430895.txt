Compliance with WHO-recommended eight+ antenatal care contacts among adolescent and young mothers in Kenya: policy implications from the 2022 Kenya demographic health survey
Authors: Joy Flora Dushime, Muhamed Mulongo, Prince Tsekpetse, Solomon Wani, Faith Oguttu, Sam Orech, Joseph KB Matovu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIn 2016, the World Health Organization (WHO) revised antenatal care (ANC) guidelines, recommending a minimum of eight ANC contacts among pregnant women to reduce maternal and neonatal mortality. However, little is known about the compliance with this recommendation among adolescent and young mothers in Kenya. This study assessed the compliance with the WHO recommended eight + ANC contacts and associated factors among adolescent and young mothers in Kenya.MethodsThis was a secondary analysis of the 2022 Kenya Demographic Health Survey (KDHS). Adolescent and young mothers aged 15–24 years who had given birth in the five years preceding the survey were included. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and logistic regression were used to determine the prevalence and the factors associated with compliance with the WHO recommended 8 + ANC contacts. Sampling weights were applied to account for survey design.Results3,458 adolescent and young mothers were included in the study. Of these, 735 (21.3%) were aged 15–19 years and 2,723 (78.7%) were aged 20–24 years. Most of them were married 1,853 (53.6%), had secondary level of education 1,732 (50.1%), and resided in rural areas 2,320 (67.1%). The majority 2,443 (70.7%) initiated ANC contacts in the second/third trimester, while 1,015 (29.3%) began within the first trimester. Overall, only 131(3.78%) of the study population complied with the recommended 8 + ANC contacts. Mothers who initiated their ANC contacts in the 2nd or 3rd trimester had 99% less odds of being compliant with the recommended 8 + ANC contacts (AOR: 0.01; 95% CI: 0-0.03). Those who delivered in health facilities had 3.33 times the odds of complying with the 8 + ANC contacts compared to those who delivered at home or elsewhere (AOR: 3.33; 95% CI: 1.30–8.55).ConclusionCompliance with the WHO-recommended 8 + ANC contacts among adolescent and young mothers in Kenya was very low. Early ANC initiation and facility-based delivery were the key factors associated with the compliance. Tailored interventions that encourage timely ANC and promote health facility-based delivery are needed to enhance compliance among this group.

Introduction

Pregnancy at young age coupled with low utilization of antenatal care (ANC) services pose a major public health problem due to its association with adverse outcomes for both the mother and the child [1]. Even though maternal healthcare interventions have improved considerably worldwide, maternal mortality ratio (MMR) remains high, especially among young mothers [2]. In 2023, more than 700 mothers died daily from preventable pregnancy and childbirth-related causes, with low- and-middle-income countries (LMICs) accounting for over 90% of these deaths [3].

Young mothers are particularly vulnerable to these deaths due to increased risks like anemia, eclampsia, obstructed labour, and puerperal sepsis [4]. These conditions also contribute to a higher likelihood of low birth weight, preterm births, and other serious neonatal conditions [5, 6]. In addition, maternal health issues such as unsafe abortion, hemorrhage, and other pregnancy-related complications are among the leading causes of death among young mothers [7].

In Kenya, maternal mortality remains a major problem. MMR increased from 342 per 100,000 live births in 2017 to 530 per 100,000 in 2020 [8, 9]. This trend indicates a delay in its efforts to achieve Sustainable Development Goal 3 (SDG3) of reducing MMR to below 70 per 100,000 live births, as well as the national target set by Kenya’s Ministry of Health of 140 per 100,000 live births by 2030 [10]. The high maternal mortality among adolescent and young mothers is a major factor contributing to the persistent burden of MMR in Kenya. According to the Health Information System-2021 in Kenya, data showed that maternal deaths among young women increased sharply from 31 in 2020 to 104 cases in 2021 [11]. While maternal mortality among young mothers is increasing, the WHO recommends eight or more ANC visits for all pregnant women as one of the interventions to improve outcomes of birth [12]. ANC is recognized as a crucial intervention for reducing maternal mortality through timely engagement with skilled providers, and early detection and management of pregnancy-related risks [13, 14]. However, despite its proven benefits, ANC coverage remains low globally, particularly among mothers aged 15–24 years [15, 16]. Studies conducted across various regions of Kenya have also shown a low utilization of ANC among young mothers, with the recent data indicating a proportion of only 39.37% [17].

Although existing studies have focused on compliance with WHO-recommended eight or more ANC contacts among all pregnant women, there is limited evidence specifically addressing this compliance among adolescent and young mothers. For instance, a study in Uganda, a pooled analysis conducted across 16 SSA countries, and another across 8 SSA countries assessed the compliance with 8 + ANC contacts, but all have focused on the broader age group of women aged 15–49 years [18–20]. While adolescent and young mothers (15–24 years) are included within the broader reproductive age group, they represent different life stages with distinct health needs, lived experiences, and social realities. Many are still in school, unmarried or newly married, and face specific challenges regarding sexual and reproductive health (SRH) service utilization such as stigma, lack of knowledge, lack of autonomy in healthcare decisions, among others [21]. Therefore, interventions targeted to this age group must be tailored to their context, which often differs from the broader age group. Targeting adolescent and young mothers is essential to provide more accurate understanding of their specific challenges and reproductive health needs, identify age-specific factors, and inform youth-sensitive policies and interventions that studies with broader age may miss.

In Kenya, most existing studies have mainly focused only on the previous standard of at least four ANC visits [22–24]. There remains limited evidence in understanding the compliance with the new guideline of eight + ANC contacts and its associated factors among adolescent and young mothers, a population at heightened risk of maternal mortality. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence and factors associated with compliance with the WHO recommendation of eight + ANC contacts among adolescent and young mothers in Kenya. The findings provide the implications for the implementation of the 8 + ANC policy.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a secondary analysis of data from the 2022 KDHS. The 2022 KDHS was conducted among women aged 15–49 years who were regular members of the selected households or who had spent the previous night there. Of the 32,156 women interviewed, the total number of women aged 15–49 years who had given birth during the previous five years were 19,530. Among these, 5,336 were aged 15–24 including 948 aged 15–19 years, and 4,388 aged 20–24 years. Of these, 1,870 had missing data on the number of their ANC contacts, and 8 did not know about the number of the ANC visits during their pregnancy and were, therefore, excluded from this analysis. Thus, a total of 3,458 adolescent and young mothers, who had complete data on their ANC contacts for their recent pregnancy, were included in this analysis, as shown in Fig. 1 below.

Study analysis sample

Data source

Data were obtained from 2022 KDHS, with prior permission from the MEASURE (Monitoring and Evaluation to Access and Use Results) DHS program. The MEASURE DHS program conducts standardized, nationally representative household surveys in LMICs, designed to facilitate comparisons across countries. These surveys are usually carried out in five-year intervals. Data collection for the 2022 KDHS was carried out between 17 February and 31 July 2022, and the interviews were conducted in Swahili or English language. The survey was conducted by the Kenya National Bureau of Statistics (KNBS), together with the Ministry of Health and other stakeholders. Technical support including training of data collectors and pretesting the study tools was provided by Inner City Fund International through the USAID-funded DHS program. The study used data on socio-demographic characteristics and obstetric characteristics collected through the child recode questionnaire.

The DHS data are publicly available and widely used by researchers worldwide for health research across different settings. Additionally, the authors’ experience in maternal and adolescent health research in sub-Saharan Africa supports appropriate interpretation of the findings within the Kenyan context.

Survey sampling

Participants for the 2022 KDHS were selected through a two-stage stratified sampling from households within clusters across all Kenyan regions. The 2019 Kenya Population and Housing Census data served as the sampling frame for selecting enumeration areas or clusters. In the first stage, 1962 clusters were selected from the Kenya Household Master Sample Frame (K_HMSF) using the Equal Probability Selection Method (EPSEM), including 1,026 clusters in rural areas and 666 in urban areas. However, 1,691 clusters were implemented because one cluster was not visited due to insecurity. In the second stage, 25 households were selected per cluster. In cases where a cluster had fewer than 25 households, all were included. This led to a total sample size of 42,022 households. More information about the survey methods and dataset is available at https://dhsprogram.com/data/.

Study variables

Outcome variable

The dependent variable in this study was the compliance with the WHO-recommended 8 + ANC contacts among adolescent and young mothers aged 15–24 years. This was determined based on the number of ANC contacts a woman attended during her last pregnancy. Those with eight or more contacts were categorized as being compliant with WHO recommendation, and those with less than 8 ANC contacts were categorized as non-compliant.

Independent variables

The independent variables were identified based on available literature [18–20, 25, 26] and a total of eighteen variables were included in analysis, as presented in Tables 1 and 2. These were considered as categorical variables and were coded as: sociodemographic characteristics including Age (15–19 and 20–24); Residence (Urban and Rural); Education (no education, Primary, Secondary and Higher); Religion (Christians, Muslims and Atheists/other); Marital status (not married, married, cohabiting and previously married); Wealth index (poorest, poorer, middle, richer and richest), distance to health facility (big problem and not a big problem); Currently working (no and yes); Husband/partner’s education (no education, primary, secondary, higher and non-applicable: non-applicable represents women who did not have a partner at the time of the survey); Sex of household head (male and female); Household Size (< 5 and 5+); Frequency of mass media exposure (TV, radio, and newspaper); Age at first birth (10–19 and 20–24); Place of delivery (health facility and home/others) and obstetric variables including Parity (< 3, 3–4 and 5+); Birth order (birth order 1, birth order 2 and birth order 3+); Wanted last pregnancy (wanted then, wanted later and wanted no more); Timing of first antenatal care contact (first trimester, 2nd/ 3rd trimester).

Statistical analysis

Before conducting the analysis, data were cleaned and dummy variables were generated. The data analysis was conducted using Stata version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Sampling weights were applied in both descriptive and regression analyses to correct for unequal selection probabilities and to ensure that representativeness, while accounting for the complex survey design, including clustering and stratification. Before proceeding with the analysis, data on adolescent and young mothers was assessed for completeness, and records with missing data on ANC visits were excluded. Data analysis was conducted at univariate, bivariate and multivariable levels. At univariate level, we summarized descriptive statistics using weighted frequencies and percentages. The distribution of ANC 8 + contacts across explanatory variables was done using chi-square test. At bivariate level, logistic regression was performed to assess the association between each independent variable and the outcome variable (compliance with the WHO-recommended 8 + ANC contacts). Independent variables with p < 0.20 were included in multivariable logistic regression model. Variables known from literature to be associated with ANC uptake (e.g., age, education, wealth index in ANC studies) were also included regardless their statistical significance in the bivariate analysis. Bivariate analysis results were presented as crude odds ratio (COR) with 95% confidence interval (CI), and p-values (p). Multivariable logistic regression results were presented as adjusted odds ratios (AOR), with their corresponding CI and p-values. Variables with p-value < 0.05 at 95% CI were considered as statistically significant. Multicollinearity was also assessed with a variance inflation factor (VIF), and no significant multicollinearity was found (minimum = 1.09, maximum = 3.26, mean = 1.61).

Ethical approval

This study used secondary data that is publicly available to registered users. The DHS survey instruments and procedures were reviewed and approved by ICF International Institutional Review Board, in compliance with US Department of Health and Human Services regulations for the protection of human subjects (45 CFR 46). The permission to use the data was obtained from MEASURE DHS through an online request process available at https://www.dhsprogram.com/.

Compliance was higher among women aged 20 to 24 years (n = 116; 4.3%), than women aged 15 to 19 years (n = 15; 2.0%) (p = 0.018). Women who wanted the pregnancy then had a higher compliance (n = 81; 5.1%) than those who wanted later or none (p = 0.022). Discussion

This study aimed to assess the compliance with the WHO-recommended 8 + ANC contacts and the associated factors among adolescent and young mothers in Kenya. The study findings highlighted the need for targeted and context-specific interventions to improve compliance with the WHO-recommended 8 or more ANC contacts among this population.

Our findings showed that about 1 in 25 of adolescent and young mothers aged 15–24 years complied with WHO recommendation of 8 + ANC contacts. This proportion is lower than what has been reported in similar DHS studies from countries such as Bangladesh (6%), Cameroon (8.9%), Myanmar (18%), and Nigeria (20%) where compliance was measured among women aged 15–49 years [25–28]. Notably, some of these studies reported higher compliance specifically among adolescent and young mothers aged 15–24 years. For instance, in Cameroon, 27.9% of women in this age group met the 8 + ANC contact recommendation. In Nigeria, a sub-national analysis showed that 9.5% of adolescent mothers (15–19 years) and 14.4% of young women (20–24 years) complied with WHO recommendation of 8 + ANC contacts, both higher than the finding observed in our study.

The differences observed between the present study and the aforementioned studies may be attributed to a combination of methodological and contextual factors. Our study specifically focused on adolescent and young mothers (15–24 years), whereas the other studies included all mothers aged 15–49 years. Most of these studies also included larger sample sizes (21,785 women in Nigeria, 17,863 in Bangladesh, 5,694 in Cameroon) compared with our sample size of 3,458. Larger and more inclusive datasets that include older women are more likely to reflect differences across regions, including areas with higher ANC service uptake, and subgroups with stronger health-seeking behavior, increased autonomy, childbirth experience, exposed to media, and awareness of maternal healthcare services, resulting in higher compliance proportions [29–31]. In contrast, our study included adolescent and young mothers who may face age specific factors such as limited knowledge of maternal health services, stigma related to early pregnancy, financial dependency, and lack of decision-making power that may have contributed to the lower compliance observed in the study [32].

Kenya’s late adoption of the 2016 WHO recommendation in 2022, together with ongoing health system challenges, such as shortages of healthcare staff, low retention of skilled personnel, and inequitable distribution of providers, particularly in hard-to-reach rural areas where most adolescent and young women reside may have also contributed to the low compliance with 8 + ANC contacts [33]. These workforce limitations compromise the continuity and accessibility of ANC services, especially for vulnerable groups such as adolescent and young mothers. In contrast, the other studies [25–28] were conducted in countries that adopted the WHO model earlier or have more robust support systems and outreach programs in place, which could explain their relatively higher compliance rates.

This study also found an association between timing of first ANC contact and the compliance with 8 + ANC contacts. Despite the WHO recommendation to initiate ANC contacts within the first trimester, the majority of adolescent and young mothers in Kenya initiated their first ANC contacts in the second or third trimester. Only a third of them started their first ANC visits in the first trimester of pregnancy, and of these, 12.7% completed 8 or more ANC contacts. This finding is lower than the rates reported in a study conducted in Uganda (47.2%) among adolescent mothers and a pooled analysis of DHS of 16 SSA countries (66%) among women aged 15–49 years, who started their first ANC in the first trimester [18, 34]. Although our study did not explore why some mothers initiated ANC visits late, previous studies showed that factors such as adolescent mother’s age, partner’s age, parity, decision maker on seeking health care, education, receiving care from skilled providers and knowledge of obstetric pregnancy danger signs were statistically significantly associated with timing to first ANC contact [34, 35].

Our findings also showed that adolescent and young mothers who delayed their first ANC contact until the second or third trimester had 99% less likely to comply with 8 + ANC contacts compared to those who began in the first trimester. This big drop in compliance shows the importance of timely ANC initiation. This finding was in line with a study in Nigeria [36] that showed that the later a pregnant woman initiates ANC visits, the less likely she is to complete 8 or more recommended visits. Similarly, a study in Benin showed that women who initiated their ANC visits after the first trimester had 97% less likely to complete 8 or more ANC contacts compared with those who initiated in the first trimester [37].

This association can be explained by both the time available during pregnancy and care seeking-behavior. Starting ANC early gives mothers enough time to attend all the recommended visits, while delaying the first ANC visit reduces the likelihood of reaching the recommended ANC contacts or even makes it impossible to complete all eight contacts before delivery [19]. Early ANC visits also enable timely detection and management of potential pregnancy complications and help build trust between mothers and healthcare providers [38]. This relationship, in turn, may encourage continued attendance of their ANC visits and greater appreciation of its importance. Therefore, the observed association between late ANC initiation and non-compliance may not only reflect health-seeking behavior, but also the limited time available to complete the visits. However, due to the use of secondary data, it was not possible to distinguish between these factors. These findings show the need for targeted interventions to promote timely ANC initiation among adolescent and young mothers. Community awareness programs should be strengthened through school programs, youth groups, community health workers, and health care providers to provide health information and promote youth-friendly health services that reduce stigma related to early pregnancy and encourage care seeking, including trainings to provide respectful and non-judgmental care. Addressing health system challenges, such as limited healthcare workforce and access barriers in remote areas, is also important to support increased early ANC visits among this population.

Our study also found that place of delivery was statistically associated with compliance with 8 + ANC contacts. Adolescent and young mothers who delivered in health facilities had 3.33 times the odds of complying with 8 + ANC contacts compared to those who delivered at home and elsewhere. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as place of delivery occurs after ANC period, and therefore cannot be considered as a direct causal predictor of ANC visits. The observed association may instead reflect a broader pattern of engagement with formal maternal health services. Women who planned to deliver in a health facility may have been more likely to attend ANC consistently for birth preparedness, screening, counselling, and continuity of care. These findings align with a study in Central Uganda and a similar DHS analysis in Myanmar that also reported a significant association between place of delivery and compliance with WHO recommended ANC contact [25, 39]. The study in Central Uganda showed that women who delivered in health facilities were 2.1 times more likely to have completed 4 or more ANC visits compared to those who delivered elsewhere [39]. Similarly, Myanmar found that women who delivered in health facilities were 2.35 times more likely to have completed the 8 or more ANC visits than those who delivered elsewhere [25]. While both the Myanmar study and the present study found a positive association between health facility delivery and compliance with 8 or more ANC contacts, the stronger association observed in Kenya maybe explained by the focus of our study on adolescent and young mothers, the population that often experience barriers to accessing care compared to older mothers [40]. In fact, when young mothers manage to utilize maternal health services, they may be more motivated and informed about pregnancy-related care, as they receive support and guidance from health facility, which encourage them to attend ANC contacts.

This study contributes to the existing literature by focusing specifically on adolescent and young mothers in Kenya, a group that is often underrepresented and faces unique challenges regarding sexual and reproductive health (SRH) service utilization; highlighting the need for targeted interventions. While factors associated with ANC utilization are documented, our findings provide evidence on how the factors operate within this population. Specifically, the study highlights the importance of early ANC initiation and sustained engagement with maternal health services in improving compliance with the WHO-recommended 8 + ANC contacts among adolescent and young mothers.

Policy implications

The low compliance with the WHO-recommended 8 + ANC contacts among adolescent and young mothers observed in this study indicates that the policy of 8 + ANC contacts has not yet been fully realized in this population. The observed association between the timing of ANC initiation and the compliance of 8 or more ANC contacts highlights the need for targeted interventions that promote timely ANC booking. These may include community awareness through school programs, youth groups, and community health workers. Additionally, health care providers should be trained to provide health information and promote youth-friendly health services that encourage care seeking.

We also found that mothers who delivered in a health facility were more likely to complete 8 or more ANC contacts. This implies that strengthening support within health facilities, including follow-ups, counselling, respectful and non-judgmental care are needed to improve the attendance and increase the likelihood to comply with the 8 or more ANC contacts.

Strengths and limitations

This study used a large sample size, nationally representative data to determine the prevalence of compliance with WHO recommendation of a minimum of 8 ANC contacts among adolescent and young mothers and its associated factors. However, its cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causality between the independent and outcome variables. Additionally, recall bias may be present, as participants were asked to remember events that occurred in five years prior to the survey. To reduce this, our analysis focused on the last pregnancy, which is less prone to memory lapses compared to distant ones. Furthermore, due to the use of secondary data, the study could not account for the timing and spacing of all ANC visits during pregnancy. Although the timing of first ANC contact was included, it was not possible to determine whether the subsequent visits followed the recommended schedule, which may have influenced the observed association. In addition, variables such as place of delivery are measured after the ANC visits, and should therefore be interpreted with caution, as temporal relationship with ANC visits cannot be established. Future studies using prospective data collection would be better suited to address these limitations.

Conclusions

Even though ANC is very important in reducing maternal deaths, this study found that compliance with WHO’s recommended eight or more ANC contacts among young mothers in Kenya is very low. Starting ANC contacts in the first trimester of pregnancy and delivery at health facilities played a major role in reaching the 8 or more ANC contacts. These finding provide evidence on implementation of the policy of eight ANC visits among this high-risk population.

To address this, policymakers and healthcare providers should prioritize strategies that promote early initiation of ANC, ideally within the first trimester. Enhanced efforts to improve delivery in health facilities may also contribute to better ANC adherence outcomes.
