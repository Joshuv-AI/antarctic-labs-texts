Generation of an induced pluripotent stem cell line from a patient with Varicose veins
Authors: Chikage Noishiki, Amit Manhas, Shaunak S. Adkar, Dipti Tripathi, David Wu, Safa Sadat, Lu Liu, Karim Sallam, Nicholas J. Leeper, Eri Fukaya, Nazish Sayed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chronic venous disease is among the most common vascular diseases globally. Varicose veins (VV), characterized by permanent dilation, elongation, and tortuosity of superficial veins, is a manifestation of chronic venous disease. Here, we generated an induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) line from PBMCs obtained from a patient with VV. The iPSC line exhibited typical morphology, maintained undifferentiated hPSC state markers, demonstrated a normal karyotype, and successfully differentiated into all three germ layers. This iPSC line provides a valuable platform to model VV pathogenesis and investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying venous dysfunction.

Resource utility

The iPSC line in this study was derived from a patient with severe varicose veins requiring surgical intervention for venous reflux. This line offers a valuable resource to model varicose veins in vitro, enabling mechanistic studies and drug discovery efforts. (See Table 1).

Resource details

Chronic venous disease and VV arise as a result of prolonged venous hypertension from both structural and functional causes (Fukaya and Kolluri, 2024). This is due to a combination of genetic predisposition, environmental factors, and age-related loss of structural integrity. It’s pathogenesis involves complex cellular and molecular mechanisms, including endothelial dysfunction, smooth muscle cell phenotypic switching, extracellular matrix degradation, and chronic inflammation. The management strategies range from conservative measures, such as compression therapy and lifestyle modification, to interventional approaches including sclerotherapy, endovenous ablation, and surgical vein removal. There are currently no available therapies that target disease prevention or mitigation; however, elucidating its pathophysiology on a cellular level can contribute to its development.

Recent genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified numerous genetic loci associated with susceptibility to VV, highlighting the important role of heritable factors in disease pathogenesis. Large-scale analyses, including studies of UK Biobank data, have uncovered over 30 independent loci linked to VV risk (Fukaya, 2018). Among these, Early B-cell Factor 1 (EBF1) has emerged as a significant susceptibility locus. EBF1, a transcription factor involved in the regulation of gene expression within the immune system, also plays critical roles in endothelial function and inflammatory responses, key processes in the pathophysiology of varicose veins. Pathogenic variants in EBF1 may disrupt vascular homeostasis by impairing endothelial integrity, altering smooth muscle cell function, and promoting inflammation. These alterations can contribute to venous wall remodeling, increased vessel distensibility, and valve dysfunction, ultimately predisposing individuals to chronic venous insufficiency and VV formation. Given its potential influence on angiogenesis and vascular stability, variations in EBF1 or its downstream targets represent an important area for future research in understanding and treating venous disease.

Leveraging patient-derived iPSCs as an in vitro disease model enables the detailed examination of critical cellular interactions and underlying mechanisms, potentially accelerating the development of new therapeutic interventions (Sayed, 2020). We generated an iPSC line from a 53-year-old female patient clinically diagnosed with VV. Notably, this patient harbored a point mutation corresponding to a single-nucleotide variation in the EBF1 gene (rs11135046). PBMCs were reprogrammed into iPSCs using Sendai virus vectors carrying the reprogramming factors c-Myc, Klf4, Oct3/4, and SOX2. By passage 13, the iPSC line exhibited typical morphology (Fig. 1A), and immunostaining confirmed the expression of key markers of the undifferentiated hPSC state, including TRA-1–60, SSEA3, and SSEA4 (Fig. 1B). Trilineage differentiation assays demonstrated the capacity of the iPSC line to differentiate into endoderm, ectoderm, and mesoderm lineages (Fig. 1C). RT-qPCR further verified the expression of NANOG and SOX2 (Fig. 1D), along with the absence of residual Sendai virus sequences (Fig. 1E). The iPSC line tested negative for mycoplasma contamination (Fig. 1F). Importantly, Sanger sequencing confirmed the presence of the EBF1 variant (rs11135046) (Fig. 1G), and KaryoStat analysis revealed a normal karyotype (Fig. 1H). Short tandem repeat (STR) analysis validated the genetic identity of the iPSC line, matching it to the donor’s PBMCs (archival data submitted with the journal). This patient-specific iPSC model provides a powerful platform for elucidating the molecular mechanisms driving VV pathogenesis and may facilitate the discovery of targeted therapies and personalized treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Reprogramming

PBMCs were isolated from patient blood using Percoll density gradient medium (GE Healthcare, #17089109). The PBMCs were subsequently purified with DPBS and cultured in 24-well plates as previously described (Manhas, 2024). Cells were maintained in StemPro®-34 SFM medium supplemented with 100 ng/mL SCF (Peprotech, #300–07), 100 ng/mL FLT3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #PHC9414), 20 ng/mL IL-3 (Peprotech, #200–3), 20 ng/mL IL-6 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #PHC0063), and 20 ng/mL EPO (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #PHC9631). Reprogramming was performed following the manufacturer’s protocol for the CytoTune™-iPSC 2.0 Sendai Reprogramming Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A16517). A total of 2 × 105 PBMCs were seeded into a single well of a 24-well plate and subsequently replated onto Matrigel-coated plates after transduction. Cells were maintained in StemPro®-34 medium until day seven post-transduction, after which they were expanded in StemMACS™ iPS-Brew medium (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-104-368). After 10–15 days in StemMACS™ iPS-Brew medium, individual colonies were manually picked and expanded as described previously (Tripathi, 2024).

Cell culture

iPSCs were maintained in StemMACS™ iPS-Brew XF medium at 37°C with 5% CO2. Upon reaching approximately 85% confluency, cells were dissociated using TrypLE™ Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #12605010) and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 minutes. A total of 1.2 × 105 iPSCs were seeded into each well of a 6-well plate in medium supplemented with 10 μM Y-27632 ROCK inhibitor (Selleck Chemicals, #Y27632) as described previously (Wu et al., 2025). Culture medium was replaced every two days, and cells typically reached confluency within 3–4 days.

Trilineage differentiation

At passage 20, the StemDiff™ Definitive Endoderm Differentiation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, #05110) and the Human Pluripotent Stem Cell Functional Identification Kit (R&D Systems, #SC027B) were used to differentiate the iPSC line into the three germ layers: mesoderm, ectoderm, and endoderm.

Immunofluorescence

At passage 19, iPSC-derived germ layers and undifferentiated iPSCs used for undifferentiated hPSC state marker analysis were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10–15 minutes. Cells were then permeabilized with 50 μg/mL digitonin for 10 minutes and blocked at room temperature using bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich, #A7030) and 5% serum (Sigma-Aldrich, #D9663; Thermo Fisher Scientific, #31873) for 30 minutes. Following overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies (Table 2), cells were washed and incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies (Table 2). Nuclei were counterstained with NucBlue (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #R37606) prior to imaging.

RT-PCR

At passage 14, 2 × 106 iPSCs were collected using TRIzol® reagent. Total RNA was isolated with the Direct-zol™ RNA Microprep Kit (Zymo Research, #R2062). cDNA synthesis was performed using the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, #1708891) under the following thermal cycling conditions: 25°C for 5 minutes, 46°C for 20 minutes, and 95°C for 1 minute. Gene expression of NANOG, SOX2, and Sendai virus (SEV) was quantified by RT-qPCR using primers listed in Table 2 and the TaqMan™ Gene Expression Assay (Applied Biosystems™, #4444556).

Karyotyping

At passage 14, 2 × 106 iPSCs were collected using TrypLE™. After centrifugation, the supernatant was removed, and the resulting cell pellet was frozen at −80°C. Chromosomal analysis was subsequently performed using the KaryoStat™ assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Short Tandem Repeat Analysis

At passage 15, Genomic DNA was isolated and purified from PBMCs and iPSCs using the QIAamp® DNA Micro Kit (Qiagen, #56304). DNA amplification was performed using the CLA IdentiFiler™ Direct PCR Amplification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A44660) under the following thermal cycling conditions: 95°C for 11 minutes; 28 cycles of 94°C for 20 seconds and 59°C for 3 minutes; followed by 60°C for 10 minutes and a final hold at 4°C for 2 minutes. Amplified products were analyzed by the Stanford Protein and Nucleic Acid (PAN) Facility using capillary electrophoresis on an ABI 3130xl Genetic Analyzer.

Mycoplasma detection

At passage 20, Mycoplasma contamination in iPSCs was assessed using the MycoAlert™ PLUS Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza, #LT07–705).
