Tools for dementia disclosure: a systematic review of ­family caregivers’ perspectives and experiences
Authors: Howard Haochu Li, Wilson Wing Chung Tang, Heman Lok Hei Leung, Zesen Gao, Kitman Chan, Vivian Weiqun Lou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesThis review focuses on tools used in dementia diagnostic disclosure, particularly from family caregivers’ perspectives. With dementia affecting millions globally, caregivers face challenges needing clear information and assistance. The review aims to (a) identify tools used, (b) assess caregivers’ preferences and challenges, and (c) highlight gaps to enhance communication strategies in dementia care.Research Design and MethodsThis systematic review, adhering to the PRISMA guidelines and registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024557806), synthesized evidence from 9 studies on tools for dementia disclosure, with a focus on family caregiver perspectives. A comprehensive search of 8 databases up to September 16, 2025, combined large language model-assisted screening and human review to identify qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods studies. Thematic synthesis analyzed caregiver preferences, tool effectiveness, and practice gaps.ResultsAcross 5 countries (United Kingdom, Australia, United States, Denmark, the Netherlands), written materials were universally valued (e.g., 80%–95% satisfaction), with visual aids and brain images less common but effective when used. Caregivers preferred direct, comprehensive information (e.g., 98% sought details about behavior and psychological symptoms of dementia) delivered face-to-face, with written backups, although retention challenges (e.g., 61% recall) and inconsistent tool provision persisted. Gaps included limited visual aid adoption and poor postdiagnostic support, highlighting unmet needs.Discussion and ImplicationsThis review highlights the importance of tools in supporting caregivers during dementia disclosure and calls for the development of a standardized Dementia Disclosure Evaluation Tool. Despite limitations (e.g., small sample sizes, urban bias), the findings lay the groundwork for improving caregiver support and guiding future research in diverse contexts.

Background and objectives

Dementia constitutes a pressing global health crisis, currently affecting approximately 57.4 million people worldwide, a figure projected to climb to 152.8 million by 2050 due to aging populations (Nichols et al., 2022). This growing burden encompasses specific conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), vascular dementia, and mild cognitive impairment (MCI), which impose significant physical, emotional, and economic tolls on those diagnosed and their families (Alzheimer’s Association, 2024). It is noted that AD severely diminishes patients’ quality of life, making them increasingly dependent on external care and affecting their loved ones as well (Shimizu et al., 2008). Studies highlight the psychological toll, including anxiety, depression, isolation, and hypervigilance for patients, and anxiety and depressive symptoms for caregivers (Dunham et al., 2020; Yates et al., 2021). These studies underscore the multifaceted challenges intensified by diagnostic disclosure, which is a critical juncture that shapes treatment, care planning, and emotional adjustment (Bamford et al., 2004). Dementia disclosure is the process of formally communicating a dementia diagnosis to the patient and, when appropriate, their family or caregivers, which involves a structured, empathetic conversation led by a health care professional to explain the diagnosis, its implications, and next steps, while addressing emotional, practical, and ethical considerations (Lecouturier et al., 2008; Werner et al., 2013). The goal is to ensure that the patient and their support system understand the condition and are empowered to plan for future care. Scholars stress the importance of this process for patient autonomy and highlight the ongoing debate over informing patients (Bamford et al., 2004; Pinner & Bouman, 2002) and frame it as a transformative moment for both patients and caregivers, who, as primary support providers, embark on a complex caregiving journey requiring clear, actionable information ­(Derksen et al., 2005). These needs are often unmet, as evidenced by caregivers’ dissatisfaction with follow-up care and underutilization of support services (Dunham et al., 2020; Kohl et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2024; Yates et al., 2021).

To support the diagnostic disclosure process and address the multifaceted challenges faced by caregivers, an array of tools (i.e., practical resources)—including written materials (e.g., leaflets, booklets, feedback sheets), visual aids (e.g., graphical representations), and brain images such as positron emission tomography (PET) scans—are increasingly employed to enhance comprehension, reduce uncertainty, and provide tangible resources (Kessels, 2003). A study from Taiwan reports that most individuals accessed AD information from diverse sources such as books, newspapers, and AD-specific booklets or fliers, underscoring the pivotal role of written materials (Lin et al., 2005). This perspective is reinforced by Bamford et al. (2004), who assert that written information outperforms verbal communication in facilitating patient understanding. Lecouturier et al. (2008) further highlight the importance of providing written materials that explain practical and emotional support available from health care, social services, and volunteer organizations, which are essential for postdisclosure planning. The use of visual aids and brain images is also on the rise. For example, Armstrong et al. (2019) note the diagnostic potential of amyloid PET scans to aid comprehension for patients and caregivers. Another study reports that the Alzheimer’s Biomarkers in Daily Practice (ABIDE) project provides a valuable digital aid (a spreadsheet calculator) for early AD biomarker detection in the form of prognostic models for MCI patients (van Maurik et al., 2017). These tools address the well-documented limitations of verbal information retention. A study reports an immediate forgetting rate of 40%–80% (Kessels, 2003), a finding that is echoed by another study’s observation of poor recall among patients and caregivers (Lecouturier et al., 2008). Studies suggest that structured delivery and adequate resources improve recall and satisfaction while reducing uncertainty (van Maurik et al., 2017; Yates et al., 2021). This approach addresses caregivers’ critical needs for diagnostic clarity, practical guidance, and emotional support, particularly amid their frustrations with inadequate follow-up and a lack of empathy (Bamford et al., 2004; ­Dunham et al., 2020; Sideman et al., 2023; Woods et al., 2019).

Despite advancements in dementia disclosure, significant research gaps remain, particularly in understanding family caregivers’ experiences with disclosure tools. First, studies often prioritize clinician practices and broad patient experiences, sidelining caregivers’ specific interactions with tools like written materials or visual aids (Jagoda et al., 2024; Werner et al., 2013). Similarly, research tends to focus on general attitudes toward disclosure rather than detailed caregiver perspectives, as seen in Shimizu et al. (2008) and Bamford et al. (2004), who note the inconsistent and limited evidence that overlooks nuanced views of both people with dementia and their caregivers. Second, tool application varies widely across different settings. For instance, Armstrong et al. (2019) highlight the importance of structured communication and amyloid PET scans in research contexts, while Perry et al. (2024) note the limited adoption of the ABIDE tool in memory clinics. These disparities reveal uneven tool availability and use, underscoring the need for standardization. Third, inconsistencies extend to care provision and follow-up. Although many caregivers receive adequate initial information, only half find follow-up care well-organized (Yates et al., 2021), and few feel informed about dementia’s chronic nature at diagnosis (Bamford et al., 2004). Finally, existing reviews, such as Bamford et al. (2004) and Jagoda et al. (2024), offer broad or patient-centric insights, while Perry et al. (2024) focus on predictive diagnostics without isolating caregivers’ tool-related needs, leaving their perspectives fragmented. This oversight is critical, as caregivers’ ability to process diagnostic information directly influences care quality and their well-being, with unmet needs linked to distress, depression, and posttraumatic stress symptoms (Dunham et al., 2020; Park et al., 2019; Yates et al., 2021).

This systematic review addresses these deficiencies by examining the use of tools in dementia disclosure, focusing on family caregiver perspectives and experiences. Its objectives are threefold: (a) to identify the types of tools employed in disclosure processes; (b) to assess caregivers’ preferences, satisfaction, and challenges with these tools; and (c) to highlight gaps in current practice to inform future communication strategies in dementia care. Encompassing empirical studies on dementia, including AD and MCI, this review prioritizes caregiver-centric insights from diverse international contexts, drawing on both qualitative and quantitative data to enhance support during this pivotal moment.

Research design and methods

This systematic review was conducted to synthesize evidence on tools used in dementia disclosure, with a specific focus on family caregivers’ perspectives. The methodology adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Moher et al., 2010) and drew on meta-synthesis principles from the Cochrane Qualitative and Implementation Methods Group (Noyes et al., 2018). The review protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024557806) to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was executed by two librarians (one specializing in social science and the other in medicine) to identify relevant studies across all languages, with particular attention to English and Chinese sources, reflecting the global scope of dementia research. Eight electronic databases were searched from May 10 to June 18, 2024 and September 10–16, 2025 (only focusing on 2024–2025), respectively: CINAHL, CNKI (China National Knowledge Infrastructure), Cochrane Library, Embase, ProQuest, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms) and free-text terms related to dementia (e.g., ­“dementia,” “Alzheimer’s disease,” “mild cognitive impairment”), ­disclosure (e.g., “disclosure,” “diagnosis communication”), and family caregivers (e.g., “caregivers,” “family members”). The full search strategy is available in Appendix 1 (see online supplementary material). Additional studies were identified through hand-searching reference lists of included articles.

Selection criteria

Studies were selected based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure relevance to the review’s objectives. Inclusion criteria were: (a) empirical studies reporting primary data on dementia disclosure processes, including AD and related conditions (e.g., MCI); (b) involvement of family caregivers as participants or focus of analysis; (c) description of tools (e.g., written materials, graphics, brain images) used in disclosure; (d) human subjects only; and (e) qualitative, quantitative, or mixed-methods designs. Exclusion criteria included: (a) reviews, scoping reviews, systematic reviews, or meta-analyses; (b) studies unrelated to dementia or disclosure or tool; (c) editorials, commentaries, or opinion pieces; (d) studies lacking primary or empirical data; and (e) nonhuman studies. Studies without abstracts were flagged as “maybe” for full-text review.

In line with recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), we used large language models (LLMs) for first-line article screening to prioritize relevant studies, enable parallel processing, and reduce workload by 40%–83% while maintaining high sensitivity, accuracy, and transparent reporting (Issaiy et al., 2024). A multistage screening process involving both LLM-assisted and human review was implemented. At the first stage, an initial pool of 729 unique records had their titles and abstracts screened. This stage utilized a custom-developed AI screening tool built using Python with a Gradio interface. The tool leveraged the Azure OpenAI Service API, specifically employing the GPT-4o model (Azure deployment name: “gpt-4o,” base model version: “gpt-4o-2024-08-06”).

To enhance reliability while allowing for broader initial capture, the screening prompt (incorporating the inclusion/exclusion criteria, detailed in Appendix 2—see online supplementary material) was applied to each title/abstract three separate times using a temperature parameter of 0.7. Temperature in LLM decoding scales next-token probabilities and higher values increase diversity but reduce stability. Based on evidence that GPT-4o performs stably within a controlled range (≤1.5) and that higher temperatures mainly increase format noncompliance without improving accuracy (Renze & Guven, 2024; Windisch et al., 2024), we selected a moderate temperature and enforced structured outputs to balance variability with reliable extraction. Each record was screened three times. A classification was accepted as final when at least two of the three runs reached the same result. This majority rule ensured high interrun reliability, with records ultimately labelled as “Yes” (include), “No” (exclude), or “Maybe.” Studies without abstracts were initially flagged as potentially relevant (“Maybe”) for further assessment. This process resulted in the exclusion of 384 records with consistent “No” classifications across the runs. To validate LLM accuracy, 21% (155/729) of titles and abstracts were manually reviewed by two authors (H.L. and W.T.), assessing decision rationales against the criteria. Observed agreement supported the exclusion of 384 articles based on majority-voted “No” decisions at this stage. We also positioned the LLM strictly as an assistive screener, not a replacement for dual human review, consistent with real-world evaluations reporting approximately 0.91 accuracy on over 24,000 records and high prevalence- and bias-adjusted agreement with human consensus (Guo et al., 2024). This approach reduces workload while preserving human oversight, especially for borderline (“Maybe”) cases.

At the second stage, full texts were sought for the remaining 235 articles (after removing 99 unretrievable and 11 duplicates). Full-text PDF documents were processed using the PDFPlumber library in Python to extract text content. The extracted text of each article was then screened using the same custom AI tool, but this time applying the screening prompt only once per article and using a lower temperature parameter of 0.1. This lower temperature was chosen to ensure more deterministic, consistent, and precise evaluation based strictly on the full content. Two authors (H.L. and L.H.L.) conducted manual checks on articles excluded by the LLM and reached a ­consensus to correct two false negatives. Subsequent manual full-text review identified seven studies explicitly reporting caregiver perspectives on disclosure tools. During our second literature search in September 2025, we retrieved 166 records. After removing 82 duplicates, 84 unique records remained for screening. After manual title/abstract and full text screening by two authors (H.L. and L.H.L.), we included two additional studies (see Figure 1).

Data extraction

Given the heterogeneity in study designs (qualitative, mixed-methods, quantitative) and outcomes, a narrative ­synthesis was deemed appropriate over meta-analysis (Popay et al., 2006). Two authors (H.L. and L.H.L.) independently extracted data from the nine included studies using a standardized form adapted from Bamford et al. (2004). Extracted data included: (1) reference (author, year); (2) country; (3) methods; (4) participants (e.g., number, type, response rates); and (5) summary of key findings related to tools and caregiver experiences. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached, ensuring reliability without necessitating a third reviewer. To minimize selection bias, studies were not excluded based on quality ratings, though quality assessments informed interpretation (see later). Outcomes sought included: (1) types of tools used in dementia disclosure (defined as written materials, visual aids, brain images, or novel tools like preparatory guides); (2) caregiver preferences and satisfaction (expressed desires for tool features or reported usability); (3) challenges and gaps (barriers to tool use or unmet needs); and (4) caregiver experiences (emotional and practical impacts, e.g., distress, retention). All results compatible with these outcomes were extracted from each study, including all reported measures (e.g., qualitative themes, survey percentages), time points (e.g., immediate or follow-up assessments), and analyses (e.g., thematic or frequency data). Where multiple results existed, priority was given to caregiver-specific data directly related to disclosure tools, as determined by consensus between two authors (H.L. and L.H.L.) to align with the review’s objectives.

Quality assessment

Study quality was assessed by H.L. and L.H.L. using the 2024 Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklists matched to the design: the CASP qualitative studies checklist (CASP, 2024b) and the CASP cross-sectional studies checklist (CASP, 2024a). Each CASP item was scored as follows: Yes = 1, Can’t tell = 0, and No = 0, and summed up to a total score based on 10 (Qualitative) or 11 (Cross-sectional) items. Before full scoring, two reviewers (H.L. and L.H.L.) calibrated on two exemplar papers to harmonize interpretations of items, (e.g., reflexivity and analytic rigor in qualitative studies; sampling/representativeness and measurement validity in cross-sectional studies). Reviewers then rated all studies independently at the item level and resolved the discrepancies by discussion—no third adjudicator was required in the process. The results were recorded in Table 1 (see online supplementary material). We have prespecified interpretive bands to guide the synthesis weighting: High (qualitative ≥ 9/10; cross-sectional ≥ 9/11), Moderate (qualitative 6–8/10; cross-sectional 7–8/11), and Low (qualitative < 6/10; cross-sectional < 7/11) (Natuhwera and Ellis, 2025; Viksveen et al., 2022). Considering the nature of CASP checklist, the scoring was not intended to exclude the studies, but rather to tabulate and highlight potential biases, which aided in the interpretation of the results and guided narrative weighting. This aligns with Cochrane recommendations for qualitative syntheses (Noyes et al., 2018).

Data analysis

A thematic synthesis approach was employed to analyze findings, following Thomas & Harden (2008). Data from each study were systematically coded to identify initial concepts (e.g., tool types, caregiver preferences), organized into descriptive themes (e.g., information needs, retention challenges), and synthesized into analytical themes (e.g., tool effectiveness, support gaps). This process focused on family caregivers’ perspectives, integrating qualitative insights (e.g., Gruters et al., 2021, “early diagnostic paradox”) with descriptive data (e.g., Nielsen et al., 2020, tool prevalence). Themes were cross-validated by H.L. and L.H.L. through iterative discussions, ensuring coherence. Gaps in tool use and caregiver experiences were identified, informing recommendations for practice and research. During thematic synthesis, differences in outcomes (e.g., caregiver preferences, tool effectiveness) were narratively assessed to identify potential influences, such as study design or geographic context. This rigorous, transparent analysis provided a cohesive narrative of caregivers’ needs and tool utility in dementia disclosure.

Results

Overview of included studies

This systematic review encompasses nine studies examining tools and information provision in the dementia disclosure process, with a particular emphasis on family caregiver perspectives (see online supplementary material, Table 1). The studies span five countries: the United Kingdom (n = 3; Abley et al., 2013; Bennett et al., 2019; Wald et al., 2003), Australia (n = 1; Mastwyk et al., 2014), the United States (n = 2; Armstrong et al., 2024; Lingler et al., 2016), Denmark (n = 1; Nielsen et al., 2020), and the Netherlands (n = 2; Gruters et al., 2021; de Rijke et al., 2025), published between 2003 and 2025. Methodologically, four studies adopted qualitative approaches using interviews and focus groups (Abley et al., 2013; Bennett et al., 2019; Gruters et al., 2021; Lingler et al., 2016), one employed a mixed-methods design with qualitative interviews and quantitative measures (Mastwyk et al., 2014), two used quantitative designs with semistructured interviews (Wald et al., 2003) and an online survey (Nielsen et al., 2020), one adopted modified Delphi methods (Armstrong et al., 2024), and one used observational methods (de Rijke et al., 2025).

Study quality

All qualitative and mixed-methods papers were of moderate-high quality according to CASP Qualitative Assessment, scored 8 or 9 out of 10s, with item 6 (researcher–participant relationship) and item 8 (analytic rigor) uncertain typically reflecting insufficiently explicit reporting rather than substantive methodological weaknesses; these did not change overall quality bands, given strong performance on core items like aims, design, and data collection. For quantitative cross-sectional studies, Nielsen et al. (2020) scored 11/11 and Wald et al. (2003) scored 10/11, leaving item 4 (measurement or classification bias) uncertain.

Participants included people with dementia or MCI and their caregivers, with sample sizes ranging from 10 dyads (Lingler et al., 2016) to 100 caregivers (Wald et al., 2003). Response rates, where reported in two studies, were robust with 96.2% in Wald et al. (2003) and 67.5% in Nielsen et al. (2020). Studies engaged diverse groups including caregivers from community mental health teams and memory clinics (Wald et al., 2003), patient–carer dyads from memory clinics (Abley et al., 2013; Gruters et al., 2021; Mastwyk et al., 2014), MCI dyads from a research center (Lingler et al., 2016), health care providers and recipients from memory assessment services (Bennett et al., 2019), specialist physicians (Armstrong et al., 2024; Nielsen et al., 2020), and care partners of people with subjective cognitive decline (de Rijke et al., 2025). This review synthesized these perspectives, focusing on tools like written materials, visual aids, and brain images, and their effectiveness in supporting caregivers during disclosure.

Tools used in dementia disclosure

The studies identified a range of tools employed in dementia disclosure, predominantly written materials, with selective use of visual aids, brain images, and a preparatory guide, reflecting varied approaches to supporting caregivers in the process of dementia disclosure. First, written materials were the most consistently utilized tool across all nine studies. Both ­Armstrong et al. (2024) and de Rijke et al. (2025) involved providing written materials to patients and caregivers after disclosure, with the shared aim of supporting understanding and aiding retention of important information. Wald et al. (2003) found 84% of 100 caregivers preferred written backups to verbal information from health professionals, reinforcing key topics like behavior and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD) and treatments. Abley et al. (2013) reported use of clinic letters, Alzheimer’s Society booklets, carers’ leaflets, and newsletters, though provision was inconsistent, with some caregivers recalling verbal scan explanations. Mastwyk et al. (2014) detailed a feedback sheet—adapted from Cognitive, Dementia and Memory Services—containing diagnosis, medications, side effects, and recommendations, provided to 32 patient–carer dyads, with 75% of patients and 80% of carers valuing it as a reference. Lingler et al. (2016) developed a one-page summary of PET amyloid imaging results and follow-up options, rated highly satisfactory by 10 MCI dyads (95% found it “easy to follow”). Nielsen et al. (2020) noted 81% of 54 physicians (44/54) routinely handed out written materials on dementia, with 76% (41/54) providing treatment-specific handouts. ­Bennett et al. (2019) included an information sheet in their service recipient’s tool, appreciated by 13 focus group participants for preparatory clarity. Gruters et al. (2021) mentioned leaflets and reports in some Dutch memory clinics, though not universally applied, with 14 patients and 13 family members suggesting written aids to improve retention. These materials consistently aimed to enhance comprehension and retention.

Second, explicit visual aids were less common, featured in only two studies. Lingler et al. (2016) used bar graphs (later pictographs) in disclosure scripts, with caregivers in 10 dyads finding them clear (mean satisfaction score 1.7–2.2 on a scale with 1 = strongly agree and 5 = strongly disagree), enhancing understanding of amyloid status. Gruters et al. (2021) employed traffic light visuals in focus groups to reflect neuropsychological assessment (NPA) experiences and recommended unspecified visual aids for disclosure to aid information retention, though implementation details were absent. Other studies did not report visual aids, which suggests limited adoption beyond specialized contexts.

Third, brain images were sparingly used as a disclosure tool. Lingler et al. (2016) uniquely recommended reviewing participants’ own PET scans during MCI disclosures, with caregivers in focus groups valuing this for reducing abstraction (e.g., “makes it less abstract”). Nielsen et al. (2020) reported 98% of physicians (53/54) shared brain scan results verbally or visually for dementia, though not specified as a caregiver tool. Abley et al. (2013) noted some caregivers recalled scan explanations (e.g., “the brain is shrinking”), but this was not systematic. The remaining studies did not mention brain images, which indicates their rarity in routine disclosure.

Fourth, studies introduced other tools. Bennett et al. (2019) introduced a service recipient guide, including an information sheet, notes, and prompt sheets (later simplified), designed to address caregiver needs like decision-making prompts and support options. No other study reported similar preparatory tools, making this a novel contribution. The feedback sheet used by Mastwyk et al. (2014), while written, also served a structured role akin to a tool, distinct from generic handouts.

Caregiver preferences and experiences with disclosure tools

Caregiver preferences and experiences with these tools highlighted needs for comprehensive information, optimal timing, effective delivery, and improved retention, shaped by their emotional and practical roles. First, caregivers sought detailed, prioritized information. Armstrong et al. (2024) found that caregivers preferred written summaries to support retention and practical impacts, such as hope and planning, with consensus highlighting their role in reducing emotional burden and establishing relationships. Wald et al. (2003) reported 98% of 100 carers wanted BPSD details, 97% treatment options, and 94% financial guidance, favoring the “rule of threes” (dementia, medications, BPSD) to manage overload. In de Rijke et al. (2025), care partners expressed needs closely aligned with patients, emphasizing the importance of understanding implications, with written summary pages facilitating this. Abley et al. (2013) found most carers prioritized being informed during assessments and receiving outcomes (e.g., test results), and valued practical advice like financial planning postdiagnosis. Mastwyk et al. (2014) noted 66% of 32 carers favored directness, with 80% desiring feedback sheets for diagnosis clarity. Gruters et al. (2021) found that family caregivers seek ongoing support, such as coping strategies for managing stress and behavioral changes, and highly value neuropsychologist feedback, like explanations of test results or advice, though such feedback is not consistently provided. Bennett et al. (2019) highlighted carer themes like “navigation of multiple journeys,” with recipients valuing tools addressing fear and decision making. Lingler et al. (2016) found care partners empowered by amyloid status knowledge, despite limitations. Nielsen et al. (2020) indirectly supported this via physician reports (95% provided information about psychosocial support), aligning with carer needs for support details.

Second, it is about timing and delivery. Preferences for timing varied, with a lean toward early, staged disclosure. Wald et al. (2003) indicated 75%–91% of carers wanted key information at diagnosis, though 11% preferred staggered delivery for later needs (e.g., carer constraints). Mastwyk et al. (2014) found 75% of patients and 80% of carers favored written summaries post-feedback, with 66% of carers valuing immediate directness. Echoing these findings, de Rijke et al. (2025) reported that summary pages were particularly valued for postdisclosure review, helping care partners revisit and understand information at their own pace. Abley et al. (2013) and Gruters et al. (2021) noted some carers preferred relevance-driven timing over immediacy, with face-to-face delivery supplemented by written aids consistently reported across included studies (Gruters et al., 2021; Nielsen et al., 2020; Wald et al., 2003). Bennett et al. (2019) emphasized preappointment guides to reduce uncertainty, a need echoed in Gruters et al.’s (2021) postdiagnosis relief findings.

Third, low retention and comprehension pose ongoing challenges for caregivers. Mastwyk et al. (2014) found that only 61% of 32 caregivers recalled diagnoses, compared to 20% of patients, though feedback sheets improved retention. Armstrong et al. (2024) highlighted that written summaries aid retention, while de Rijke et al. (2025) noted that although retention was not directly measured, written tools addressed ongoing information needs and provided emotional relief or certainty postdisclosure. Similarly, Gruters et al. (2021) reported that family members often forgot details and required written or visual aids weeks later. Abley et al. (2013) observed that caregivers struggled without written reinforcement, such as follow-up calls about test results. In contrast, Lingler et al. (2016) achieved 80% comprehension using graphics and take-home materials, which empowered patient–caregiver dyads. Likewise, Bennett et al. (2019) developed a guide with preparatory notes that focus groups rated highly for supporting retention.

Gaps and challenges in tool utilization

Despite tool use, caregivers identified gaps and challenges in consistency, visual aid adoption, and postdiagnostic support. Firstly, the inconsistent provision of tools and information represents a significant gap. Studies have reported variability in the availability of these tools. For instance, Armstrong et al. (2024) highlighted systemic challenges—such as insufficient time during visits, limited access to specialty care, and lack of clinician training—that hinder consistent tool utilization, including written summaries. de Rijke et al. (2025) further noted that the modality of disclosure (e.g., telephone consults) affected the expression of caregiver needs, with fewer needs identified compared to in-person sessions, underscoring an additional barrier to consistent support. For example, Abley et al. (2013) highlighted inconsistent communication, with delays of over 12 months, which frustrated carers. Gruters et al. (2021) found that family members often faced unclear follow-up, and the distribution of informational leaflets was not universal. According to Mastwyk et al. (2014), feedback sheets were effective, yet their use was not standardized across the clinics studied. Nielsen et al. (2020) showed that 81% of the 54 physicians surveyed used written materials, but the consistency of MCI information varied, with only 72% consistently using the term “MCI.”

Secondly, the limited use of visuals and images represents another significant gap. While Armstrong et al. (2024) did not focus specifically on visuals, their findings regarding the need for culturally sensitive materials suggest that the absence of tailored visual supports may particularly disadvantage underrepresented groups. Aside from the pictographs and brain images utilized by Lingler et al. (2016), visual aids were generally underutilized. de Rijke et al. (2025) also implied that summary tools—potentially including visual formats—require better integration, especially for cases where diagnostic certainty is low. Although Gruters et al. (2021) recommended the use of visuals, they did not provide specific guidelines. Nielsen et al. (2020) shared brain scan results verbally in 98% of cases, which was not intended as a tool for carers. Moreover, Abley et al. (2013) mentioned scans only sporadically, underscoring the lack of systematic visual support.

Thirdly, postdiagnostic support is another significant gap, which is highlighted by the evident lack of ongoing, caregiver-focused tools. Armstrong et al. (2024) identified a notable unmet need for culturally sensitive resources and highlighted how systemic barriers—such as inadequate follow-up infrastructure—may exacerbate gaps in ongoing support, especially for underrepresented caregivers. de Rijke et al. (2025) observed that tools like written summaries, while helpful for postdisclosure review, were not always integrated effectively, particularly for cases with diagnostic ambiguity (e.g., amyloid-negative results), leaving some caregivers with ongoing uncertainty and unmet informational needs. For example, Gruters et al. (2021) reported that most clinics failed to provide a designated contact for family members after diagnosis, leaving caregivers without consistent guidance. Similarly, Abley et al. (2013) noted that while group support options, such as memory classes, were available, their effectiveness was limited without reliable follow-up. Nielsen et al. (2020) indicated that follow-up was offered to 64% of dementia cases, compared to 81% of MCI cases, and was often led by nurses, but lacked tools tailored to caregivers’ needs. Although Lingler et al. (2016) recommended postdisclosure calls to address this gap, such practices remain rare, underscoring the need for standardized, caregiver-centric support systems.

Quality assessment and limitations

CASP scores affirmed the methodological quality of the included studies. Qualitative studies (Abley et al., 2013; Armstrong et al., 2024; Bennett et al., 2019; de Rijke et al., 2025; Gruters et al., 2021; Lingler et al., 2016) each scored 8 or 9 out of 10, demonstrating strong methodological fit and robust data analysis. However, six qualitative studies received a “can’t tell” rating for the relationship between researcher and participants that indicates many researchers have not been reflective enough about their own role and potential influence on their studies (Abley et al., 2013; Armstrong et al., 2024; Bennett et al., 2019; de Rijke et al., 2025; Gruters et al., 2021; Lingler et al., 2016). Four qualitative studies received a “can’t tell” rating for rigorous data analysis, which indicated that not all qualitative studies strictly follow the checklist in reporting data analysis (Abley et al., 2013; Bennett et al., 2019; Gruters et al., 2021; Lingler et al., 2016). Common limitations included small sample sizes (e.g., n = 14 in Armstrong et al., 2024; 10 dyads in Lingler et al., 2016), and potential selection bias, as seen with voluntary participant recruitment in de Rijke et al. (2025). Similarly, the mixed-methods study (Mastwyk et al., 2014) also scored 8/10, rating “can’t tell” in both rigorous data analysis and research and participant relationship, while the two quantitative studies achieved 10/11 (Wald et al., 2003) and a score of 11/11 (Nielsen et al., 2020), respectively, demonstrating clearer reporting. However, limitations included retrospective recall bias (Wald et al., 2003), and predominantly sampled urban and/or high-income populations, with limited representation from rural or low-resource settings. The heterogeneity of study designs and outcomes precluded meta-analysis (Abley et al., 2013; Armstrong et al., 2024; Bennett et al., 2019; de Rijke et al., 2025; Gruters et al., 2021; Lingler et al., 2016; Mastwyk et al., 2014; Nielsen et al., 2020), while the Western-centric focus of all included studies limits generalizability to more diverse or global contexts. Despite these limitations, high response rates (96.2% in Wald et al., 2003, and 67.5% in Nielsen et al., 2020) and caregiver-centric data enhance the reliability of these findings.

Discussion and implications

This systematic review of nine studies offers a robust exploration of dementia disclosure practices and the tools employed to support family caregivers across diverse settings in the United Kingdom, Australia, the United States, Denmark, and the Netherlands. Synthesizing qualitative, mixed-methods, and quantitative data through thematic analysis (Thomas & Harden, 2008), the findings reveal caregivers’ strong preference for clear, direct, and comprehensive information, the pivotal role of tools in enhancing comprehension and retention, and persistent gaps in consistency, visual aid adoption, and postdiagnostic support. These insights extend existing literature, providing actionable implications for practice and research to improve caregiver experiences during this transformative process.

Caregiver preferences and disclosure practices

Caregivers consistently favor direct and honest communication, a preference supported by broader research and evident across various studies (Byszewski et al., 2007; Yates et al., 2021). Mastwyk et al. (2014) found that most caregivers preferred a direct approach, a finding mirrored by Nielsen et al. (2020), who reported that 91% of Danish physicians use the term “dementia.” This marks a shift from historical reticence toward more transparent, autonomy-driven disclosure (O’Brien et al., 2021). However, Gruters et al. (2021) highlighted an “early diagnostic paradox,” where caregivers experience distress from NPAs yet find relief in diagnostic clarity. This complexity suggests that emotional preparation is as critical as clarity, which is consistent with another study focusing on navigating multiple emotional journeys (Bennett et al., 2019). This duality underscores the need for empathetic, staged support for caregivers beyond the initial disclosure (Dunham et al., 2020; Zhang, et al., 2025). Additionally, preferences may be influenced by demographic diversity, such as the predominance of female caregivers (e.g., 66% in Wald et al., 2003; 77% in Abley et al., 2013) and older age ranges (carers mean 64–72 years across studies), potentially skewing insights toward experiences of spouses or adult children in predominantly White populations, as ethnic diversity was rarely reported (e.g., 90% White in Lingler et al., 2016).

Caregivers have broad and prioritized information needs, consistent with prior research (Lecouturier et al., 2008; Wald et al., 2003). Wald et al. (2003) identified a “rule of threes”—dementia explanation, medications, and details on BPSD—with 98% of caregivers particularly desiring information on BPSD. Abley et al. (2013) emphasized the importance of providing caregivers with updates on assessments and outcomes, which highlighted the demand for structured, actionable information to help them cope and plan. Timing preferences for receiving information varied. Mastwyk et al. (2014) noted that 75%–80% of caregivers sought immediate written summaries, while Wald et al. (2003) found that 11% preferred staged delivery, supporting a call for flexibility (Werner et al., 2013). There is a universal preference for face-to-face delivery of information supplemented by written backups (Gruters et al., 2021; Wald et al., 2003), which addresses diverse literacy and emotional needs and aligns with Kessels’s (2003) insights on information retention.

Role and effectiveness of tools

Written materials have emerged as a cornerstone tool, universally adopted and effective for conveying information. High satisfaction rates—95% (Lingler et al., 2016) and 80% (Mastwyk et al., 2014)—underscore their critical role in addressing retention challenges (Gruters et al., 2021; Mastwyk et al., 2014). Feedback (Mastwyk et al., 2014) and summaries (Lingler et al., 2016) offer structured content, which is in line with the findings that written aids enhance health literacy (Pratt & Searles, 2017). However, the provision of these materials is inconsistent, as noted by Abley et al. (2013) and Gruters et al. (2021), reflecting variability in practice. This inconsistency echoes a critique of gaps between guidelines and practice, highlighting the need for standardized dissemination of written materials (Bamford et al., 2004).

Visual aids and brain images, though less common, have shown promise in enhancing comprehension and recall. The use of pictographs and PET scans resulted in high comprehension among participants (Lingler et al., 2016), which aligns with evidence that visuals improve recall (Houts et al., 2006). Caregivers valued brain images as tangible evidence (Lingler et al., 2016; Nielsen et al., 2020), yet their limited use elsewhere suggests barriers in training or resources (Wald et al., 2003). Similarly, a preparatory guide uniquely addressed predisclosure uncertainty and was appreciated by participants, which offers an innovative model not yet widely replicated and highlights the potential for further tool development (Blok et al., 2025).

Addressing retention and comprehension challenges

Retention challenges persisted despite the use of various tools, with Gruters et al. (2021) and Abley et al. (2013) noting that many caregivers struggled to remember information without written aids. This is consistent with a study reporting that 40%–80% of information is forgotten (Kessels, 2003). Lingler et al. (2016) reported an 80% success rate in retention with the use of visuals and family presence, which contrasts with Mastwyk et al. (2014) reporting 61% success rate. This suggests that multimodal tools and caregiver involvement are crucial (Lecouturier et al., 2008). Additionally, emotional distress may exacerbate retention issues (Gruters et al., 2021), which supports the need for staged delivery of information (Nielsen et al., 2020) and follow-up mechanisms (Lingler et al., 2016).

Implications for practice and research

The findings of this systematic review provide a robust foundation for developing a Dementia Disclosure Evaluation Tool (DDET) as a key implication for practice and research. This tool aims to enhance family caregivers’ experiences during dementia disclosure. Caregivers’ strong preferences for comprehensive, direct, and emotionally sensitive information—coupled with the proven effectiveness of tools like written materials, visual aids, and preparatory guides (Lingler et al., 2016; Mastwyk et al., 2014)—underscore the need for culturally sensitive, adaptable standardized tools to assess interactions with health care professionals and people with dementia. Persistent retention challenges (Mastwyk et al., 2014) and gaps in consistent tool provision, visual aid use, and postdiagnostic support (Abley et al., 2013; Gruters et al., 2021) highlight the urgency of evaluating and improving disclosure practices. In practice, a DDET could assess health care professional delivery (e.g., empathy, information on available resources, timing adaptability), caregiver–people with dementia communication (e.g., emotional support, care practices), and self-care (e.g., access to ongoing support), thereby bridging the gaps between guidelines and implementation. For research, it offers a framework to investigate tool efficacy across diverse settings, refine multimodal strategies, and address caregiver well-being, ultimately advancing dementia care by translating these insights into actionable, caregiver-centered improvements.

Clinicians should promote equitable access to written materials, such as feedback sheets, while recognizing that literacy levels, language barriers, or cognitive impairments may limit accessibility. They should also incorporate alternative formats, like audio or visual aids, to address uncertainty, particularly in MCI diagnoses (Nielsen et al., 2020). Delivery should be tailored using frameworks such as the “rule of threes” (Wald et al., 2003) and a preparatory guide (Bennett et al., 2019), adapting to individual preferences as suggested by Yates et al. (2021), and accounting for the heterogeneity of dementia types to avoid oversimplification. Enhancing postdiagnostic support with tools related to designated contacts (Gruters et al., 2021) or support groups (Abley et al., 2013) is critical to meeting ongoing needs (Sideman et al., 2023), ensuring flexibility to accommodate diverse cultural and socioeconomic contexts and mitigate risks of excluding minority groups. This effort should be strengthened by training clinicians in the integration of tools and empathy, drawing on models for breaking bad news ­(Nielsen et al., 2020) and the use of PET amyloid imaging (Lingler et al., 2016). Concurrently, research must evaluate the broader efficacy of visual aids beyond MCI contexts (Houts et al., 2006; Lingler et al., 2016), explore caregiver experiences with predementia disclosure tools (Derksen et al., 2005; ­Nielsen et al., 2020), assess the longitudinal impact of postdiagnostic tools (Gruters et al., 2021; Lindeza et al., 2024), and extend investigations to rural and non-English-speaking populations (Perry et al., 2024) as well as underrepresented minority groups to address health care inequalities and ensure inclusive tool development to ensure comprehensive and equitable support for caregivers navigating this pivotal process.

Limitations

This study is subject to limitations that may influence the generalizability and interpretation of its findings. The small sample sizes in some included studies, such as Lingler et al. (2016) with only 10 dyads, potentially limit the breadth of caregiver experiences captured, particularly for diverse dementia subtypes or settings beyond urban memory clinics. Retrospective designs, notably in Wald et al. (2003) with 100 caregivers, introduce recall bias, as participants’ reflections on disclosure may be distorted over time, a concern echoed in prior reviews (Perry et al., 2024). The evidence base is currently dominated by studies from urban, English-speaking settings (e.g., three UK-based studies), leaving rural and non-English-speaking populations underrepresented. This sampling bias may obscure cultural and resource-related differences in tool use and caregiver needs, where intersecting dimensions of gender, socioeconomic status, and culture compound caregiver stress and constrain access to support, underscoring the need for contextually tailored disclosure tools (Liu et al., 2022). Furthermore, ambiguities in recruitment rigor and analysis transparency in qualitative studies (e.g., CASP items 6 and 8 marked “Can’t tell”) suggest potential biases (e.g., selection and recall bias), which necessitates cautious interpretation of the synthesized caregiver perspectives. Participant diversity was limited, since samples focused on older adults, constraining insights for younger caregivers and early-onset dementia. Moreover, carers were predominantly female, patient sex ratios varied, and ethnic diversity was rarely reported, indicating underrepresentation of minority groups and cultural variations.

The review process itself also has constraints. The search scope was limited to eight databases, focusing on English and Chinese sources with searches, potentially missing studies in other languages, gray literature, or regional databases and introducing publication bias toward Western journals. We mitigated this via reference list hand-searching and PROSPERO registration.

Large language model-assisted screening introduces stochasticity from temperature parameters (T = 0.7 for titles/abstracts capturing borderline variability, T = 0.1 for full-texts maximizing determinism), affecting replicability. We addressed this with majority-of-three checks, manual validation on 21% of records, and human consensus on full-texts, correcting two false negatives (Guo et al., 2024; Issaiy et al., 2024).

Methodological decisions may introduce biases. For instance, not excluding low-quality studies could amplify weaker evidence, but all our included studies scored moderate-high on CASP. Narrative synthesis precluded meta-analysis and may add subjectivity, but this practice is guided by Thomas & Harden (2008) with iterative reviewer validation. While an exclusive focus on caregivers may overlook clinician and patient perspectives, our study was intentionally designed to serve caregivers specifically. These were aligned with PRISMA (Moher et al., 2010) and Cochrane principles (Noyes et al., 2018), with independent extraction to enhance objectivity. Also, high response rates (96.2% in Wald et al., 2003, and 67.5% in Nielsen et al., 2020) and caregiver-centric data enhance the reliability of these findings.

Conclusion

This systematic review of nine studies across five countries provides a comprehensive examination of tools used in dementia disclosure, with a specific focus on family caregiver perspectives and experiences. The findings affirm that caregivers value clear, direct, and comprehensive information, with written materials emerging as a cornerstone tool—evidenced by high satisfaction rates—while visual aids, brain images, and preparatory guides show promise but remain underutilized. Caregivers’ preferences for tailored timing, face-to-face delivery supplemented by written backups, and practical guidance highlight their critical role in navigating the emotional and practical complexities of dementia disclosure. However, persistent challenges, including low retention, inconsistent tool provision, and inadequate postdiagnostic support, reveal significant gaps in current practice. These insights underscore the urgency of developing a DDET to standardize and enhance disclosure processes, assessing health care professional interactions, caregiver-people with dementia communication, and self-care outcomes. By addressing these gaps, the DDET offers a pathway to bridge the divide between guidelines and practice, which ensures caregivers receive consistent, effective support. While limitations such as small sample sizes, recall bias, and a focus on urban English-speaking contexts temper generalizability, this review lays a robust foundation for future research to refine tool efficacy, explore diverse populations, and strengthen postdiagnostic care, ultimately improving the quality of life for caregivers and those they support in the dementia journey.
