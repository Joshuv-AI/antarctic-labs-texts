Bidirectional relationship between depression and long COVID symptoms: findings from the Sulcovid-19 longitudinal survey
Authors: Juliana Quadros Santos Rocha, Lara dos Santos Camilo, Suele Manjourany Silva Duro, Mirelle de Oliveira Saes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Long COVID is a condition that may manifest after SARS-CoV-2 infection, associated with complications such as depression and persistent symptoms. This study evaluated the relationship between prior depression and the occurrence of Long COVID, as well as investigated whether symptoms of this condition increase the risk of depression following infection in adults from southern Brazil. Using data from the longitudinal Sulcovid-19 study, the analysis included variables such as sex, age, skin color, marital status, income, smoking, body mass index, comorbidities, and hospitalization. The prevalence of Long COVID was 60 percentage points higher among individuals with prior depression, and this condition increased the probability of depression after infection by 65%. Associations were evaluated using Poisson regression in Stata 17.0. The results highlight the importance of investigating the implications of Long COVID and developing effective therapeutic approaches.

Introduction

Long COVID, also referred to as post-COVID-19 syndrome, is a multisystem condition characterized by the persistence of symptoms for at least three months after acute COVID-19 infection, lasting at least two months without an alternative diagnosis [7, 13]. Recent global analyses indicate that the prevalence of Long COVID ranges from 9 to 63%, being up to six times higher than other similar post-viral conditions. High-risk groups for developing Long COVID include women, individuals with anxiety, hypertension, heart disease, diabetes mellitus, musculoskeletal or respiratory problems, pre-existing comorbidities, and those who experienced hospitalization [13, 20]. People with Long COVID may experience a variety of symptoms persisting after acute infection, including fatigue, headaches, cognitive problems, and mental health disorders, with anxiety and depression being the most prevalent mental health-related symptoms [23].

Depression is recognized as one of the most severe mental health issues worldwide. Over the last 30 years, the number of new cases has increased by nearly 50%, currently affecting over 264 million people of all ages [14]. Studies suggest that pre-existing depression increases the likelihood of developing long COVID [30]; however, the precursor symptoms have not yet been clearly identified and emerging evidence indicates that changes in social cognition during the pandemic may also play a role in shaping behavioral and mental health outcomes [2, 3].

It is known that individuals with long COVID may develop conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, anxiety, and depression, along with persistent symptoms following acute COVID-19 infection [6]. Nonetheless, there is still a lack of studies investigating these associations. Therefore, this study aims to analyze the association between pre-existing depression and the occurrence of long COVID, as well as the possibility that long COVID symptoms increase the likelihood of developing depression after SARS-CoV-2 infection in an adult population from a municipality in southern Brazil.

Materials and methods

This was a cross-sectional study that used baseline data from the Sulcovid-19 study, a longitudinal study that monitored health indicators among individuals previously infected with SARS-CoV-2 in the municipality of Rio Grande, Rio Grande do Sul, Brazil. This coastal municipality has an estimated population of approximately 191,900 inhabitants [11].

The sample consisted of individuals aged 18 years or older who were diagnosed with COVID-19 between December 2020 and March 2021 through reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) testing, presented symptoms of the disease, and resided in the municipality where the study was conducted. The study was approved by the Health Research Ethics Committee (CEPAS) of the Federal University of Rio Grande (FURG), under the Certificate of Ethical Submission (CAAE) No. 39081120.0.0000.5324.

For participant identification, contact was established with the Municipal Department of Epidemiological Health Surveillance of Rio Grande, which provided a list of 4,014 individuals with positive SARS-CoV-2 RT-PCR results. This list included personal information such as name, address, telephone number, and presence of symptoms. Subsequently, individuals with available telephone contact or residential address were selected, totaling 3,822 eligible individuals for study participation.

Data collection was conducted through telephone interviews performed by trained interviewers who underwent a rigorous selection process and received detailed training. When necessary, home visits were also offered as an alternative to telephone data collection. Baseline data collection was carried out between June and October 2021, with participation from 2,919 individuals (631 losses and 272 refusals). Interviews were conducted, on average, 6.4 months after RT-PCR-confirmed infection (mean = 192.9 days; SD ± 42.7). Consent was obtained orally before the telephone interview and in person before the face-to-face interviews. Additional information regarding the study design and recruitment process can be found elsewhere [20].

The presence of depression was assessed through self-reported medical diagnosis, a strategy commonly used in population-based surveys in Brazil and internationally, such as the National Health Survey (PNS), the VIGITEL system, and the World Health Organization STEPS protocol [12, 16, 28]. The following questions were asked: “At any time in your life, has a doctor or psychologist ever told you that you had depression?” and “After your COVID-19 infection until now, has a doctor or psychologist told you that you have depression?” For this study, participants who answered “yes” to the first question and “no” to the second were classified as having depression diagnosed prior to infection. Participants who answered “yes” to both questions were classified as having depression diagnosed after SARS-CoV-2 infection.

The presence of long COVID was assessed through self-report, in which participants reported the occurrence of symptoms during SARS-CoV-2 infection and the persistence of these symptoms at the time of the interview, conducted on average 6.4 months after infection. Symptom presence was defined as an affirmative response for both conditions.

For this study, symptoms were analyzed in three ways: individually, globally (long COVID), and by body systems. In the individual analysis, 19 symptoms frequently associated with long COVID were investigated, including headache, shortness of breath, dry cough, productive cough, pain or discomfort when breathing, loss of taste, loss of smell, sensory alterations (such as numbness, tingling, pins-and-needles sensation, pressure sensation, and temperature perception changes), fatigue, sore throat, runny nose, nasal congestion, diarrhea, nausea, joint pain, muscle pain, memory loss, difficulty concentrating, and skin alterations [15].

In the global analysis, the variable “long COVID” was created, defined as the presence of at least one symptom reported by participants.

Additionally, symptoms were categorized according to affected body systems: digestive symptoms (nausea and/or vomiting), musculoskeletal symptoms (muscle pain, joint pain, and/or fatigue), neurological symptoms (headache, memory loss, and/or difficulty concentrating), respiratory symptoms (shortness of breath, dry cough, productive cough, pain or discomfort when breathing, runny nose, and/or nasal congestion), and sensory symptoms (loss of taste, loss of smell, and/or sensory alterations).

Exposure variables included: sex (female; male), age group (18–59; ≥ 60 years), skin color (White; non-White), marital status (married or living with a partner; single, separated, or widowed), income (0–1000; 1001–2000; 2001–4000; ≥ 4001), smoking status (no; yes or former smoker), body mass index (BMI) (underweight or normal weight; overweight or obese) [22], morbidities (0; 1–2; ≥ 3), and hospitalization (ward admission or intensive care unit [ICU]) (no; yes).

Initially, the prevalence of each individual outcome was presented according to exposure variables and their respective 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Poisson regression with robust variance adjustment was used to estimate crude and adjusted prevalence ratios for the association between depression and long COVID symptoms. All associations with non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals between categories were considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed using Stata statistical software, version 17.0.

Results

Overall, the sample was predominantly composed of adults aged 18–59 years (83.3%), women (58.6%), individuals with White skin color (77.9%), married or living with a partner (60.6%), and with complete secondary education (42.2%). Among participants, 19.4% (95% CI: 18.0–20.9) reported a diagnosis of depression prior to SARS-CoV-2 infection, while 7.9% (95% CI: 6.9–8.9) reported receiving a diagnosis during or after the acute phase of COVID-19. Regarding long COVID, 48.3% (95% CI: 46.5–50.1) reported persistence of at least one residual symptom after infection.

Discussion

This study aimed to analyze the association between pre-existing depression and the occurrence of long COVID, as well as to investigate whether the presence of persistent symptoms after SARS-CoV-2 infection increases the likelihood of subsequent depression. The findings demonstrated that individuals with depression prior to infection presented a higher prevalence of persistent symptoms, particularly neurological symptoms, while the presence of long COVID symptoms was associated with a higher probability of receiving a diagnosis of depression after infection.

The data indicate that individuals with a prior diagnosis of depression show a significantly higher prevalence of persistent COVID-19 symptoms, especially neurological symptoms such as memory loss, compared with those without a history of depression. Similarly, Dryden et al. [8] demonstrated that individuals with a history of depression had approximately twice the likelihood (IRR 2.37; 95% CI: 1.02–5.48) of developing long COVID.

The relationship between neurological symptoms and prior depression may be explained by underlying biological mechanisms. Depression is associated with dysregulation of the immune system, leading to increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [29]. This inflammatory state involves activation of peripheral immune cells and microglia in the central nervous system, promoting neuroinflammation that affects neurotransmission, synaptic plasticity, and mood-related neural circuits. In addition, factors such as chronic stress and activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis may promote sustained cytokine production, reinforcing the role of inflammation in the pathophysiology of depression [29]. Considering that SARS-CoV-2 infection is also associated with increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly IL-6 and TNF-α, it is biologically plausible that individuals with prior depression may present greater susceptibility to or intensification of neurological symptoms following infection [27]. Additionally, emerging evidence highlights the potential role of compounds such as N-acetylcysteine and acetyl-L-carnitine in modulating neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction—key pathways implicated in post-COVID neuropsychiatric manifestations [2, 3]. These agents may contribute to the restoration of neuronal homeostasis and improvement of symptoms such as fatigue, cognitive impairment, and depression.

Evidence suggests that the exacerbated inflammatory response during COVID-19 may contribute to the development of persistent depressive symptoms. Recent evidence suggests that impairments in the glymphatic system—responsible for clearing metabolic waste from the brain—may contribute to the persistence of cognitive and affective symptoms following SARS-CoV-2 infection [4]. Alterations in this system have been associated with neuroinflammation, blood–brain barrier dysfunction, and the accumulation of neurotoxic substances, mechanisms that may explain manifestations such as memory loss, attention deficits, and depression. This perspective supports a broader pathophysiological model in which neuropsychiatric manifestations of long COVID are not explained solely by psychological factors, but also by persistent alterations in brain homeostasis and neural clearance mechanisms [4].

For example, Pepe et al. [18] demonstrated that post-COVID-19 major depressive episodes present distinct clinical characteristics, including prominent physical and cognitive symptoms, as well as elevated inflammatory markers, suggesting a link between systemic inflammation and depression in this context. In addition, Guillen-Burgos et al. [9] observed that high levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines during SARS-CoV-2 infection were associated with the development of major depressive disorder (MDD) and severe outcomes, such as treatment-resistant depression and suicidal ideation, in COVID-19 survivors. These findings support the hypothesis that inflammation plays a central role in the pathophysiology of depression following SARS-CoV-2 infection.

A higher prevalence of long COVID symptoms, such as headache, memory loss, and attention difficulties, was observed among individuals with prior depression. A study conducted by Wang et al. [26] reported that the most common long COVID symptoms among individuals with prior depression were fatigue (56.0%, n = 786), olfactory or gustatory dysfunction (44.6%, n = 626), shortness of breath (25.5%, n = 358), confusion/disorientation/brain fog (24.5%, n = 343), and memory problems (21.8%, n = 306), showing some similarities, but also differences, compared with our findings. Although the literature suggests that prior depression increases the likelihood of developing long COVID [8], information regarding specific symptom profiles remains limited and lacks consensus. These results reinforce the importance of further investigation to address existing gaps in the literature.

According to the present study, there was a higher prevalence of depression diagnosis after infection among individuals presenting prolonged symptoms such as sensory alterations, memory loss, attention impairment, fatigue, joint and muscle pain, skin alterations, and musculoskeletal disorders. This may be explained by a combination of biological and psychological factors. Musculoskeletal disorders, joint and muscle pain, and fatigue may lead to reduced productivity, increased absenteeism, disability, changes in work and leisure routines, and limitations in daily activities [17, 25]. These conditions generate additional economic costs and disrupt daily functioning, negatively affecting quality of life and increasing the risk of depression [24]. For example, the study conducted among Chinese college students in the post-epidemic period identified a high prevalence of mental health symptoms during COVID-19 lockdown, with 27.0% of participants reporting anxiety, 34.1% depression, and 25.6% comorbid anxiety and depression. These findings highlight the substantial impact of pandemic-related stressors—such as social isolation, academic disruption, and uncertainty—on mental health outcomes, even in the absence of active infection. In line with this perspective, our findings in a Brazilian cohort also demonstrated a significant burden of depressive symptoms and cognitive impairment several months after SARS-CoV-2 infection. Taken together, these results suggest that the mental health consequences observed in the post-pandemic period are likely driven by a complex interplay between biological effects of the infection and broader psychosocial stressors. In this context, structural and social vulnerabilities, particularly in low- and middle-income settings, may further exacerbate these outcomes, reinforcing the need for integrated approaches to post-COVID care and mental health surveillance [10].

Symptoms such as sensory alterations, memory loss, and difficulty concentrating may be related to neuroinflammation, creating a biological environment that favors the development of depression [19]. The skin may reflect internal diseases and systemic dysfunctions [1], and this may also apply to long COVID. As the skin is a visible part of the body, dermatological changes may affect self-esteem and body image, potentially contributing to neuropsychiatric conditions [5].

Additionally, the literature indicates that psychosocial factors, such as stigma, chronic stress, and functional limitations, contribute to the persistence of depressive symptoms in individuals with long COVID. These factors negatively impact quality of life, increase psychological distress, and may interact with biological mechanisms, reinforcing the importance of a biopsychosocial approach [21].

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, only individuals who survived COVID-19 were analyzed, which may introduce survival bias, as individuals with more severe outcomes may not have been captured. In addition, although 19 of the most frequently reported long COVID symptoms were evaluated, more than 200 manifestations have already been described in the literature. Therefore, it is possible that overall prevalence and symptom distribution were underestimated. It is also important to note that during the early phase of the pandemic (2020), knowledge regarding long COVID was still limited, which may have restricted the comprehensiveness of symptom assessment.

Also, depression diagnosis was based on self-reported prior medical diagnosis, assessed at two time points (before and after SARS-CoV-2 infection), which may be subject to information bias or underreporting, particularly in populations with limited access to mental health services, potentially influencing prevalence estimates. Although this approach allowed for temporal classification, it did not enable differentiation between incident cases and the persistence or recurrence of pre-existing depression. Thus, reports of depression after infection may reflect ongoing or relapsing conditions rather than new-onset cases. Additionally, it was not possible to determine whether depression was active at the time of infection, which further limits interpretation.

Finally, an important limitation of this study relates to the operational definition of long COVID, defined as the presence of at least one residual symptom at the time of the interview (mean of 6.4 months post-infection). This relatively broad definition may reduce the specificity of the outcome and lead to a potential overestimation of prevalence, as it includes mild or nonspecific symptoms that are not necessarily directly attributable to SARS-CoV-2 infection. However, it is important to note that, at the time the study was conducted, there was no consensus definition of long COVID in the scientific literature, which justified the adoption of a more inclusive criterion.

Despite these limitations, this study has important strengths. Notably, the use of a population-based sample with COVID-19 diagnosis confirmed by RT-PCR reduces the possibility of exposure misclassification. In addition, the high response rate (> 75%) reduces the risk of selection bias and increases the internal validity of the findings. Another relevant aspect is the differentiated assessment of depression before and after infection, allowing exploration of temporality and potential bidirectional relationships between depression and long COVID. Furthermore, adjustment for potential confounders strengthens the robustness of the observed associations.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that individuals with pre-existing depression significantly increases the risk of specific neurological symptoms in long-term COVID-19, while individuals presenting persistent symptoms after SARS-CoV-2 infection are more likely to receive a diagnosis of depression following COVID-19. These results suggest a potential bidirectional relationship between these conditions. Furthermore, the findings support the hypothesis of shared biological mechanisms, possibly mediated by systemic inflammatory and neuroinflammatory processes.

From a clinical and public health perspective, these results highlight the need for integrated strategies for monitoring and longitudinal care among individuals with a history of depression or persistent post-COVID symptoms. In addition, they reinforce the importance of early screening for neuropsychiatric symptoms in patients with long COVID. Future studies are needed to clarify causal mechanisms, evaluate long-term functional impacts, and guide the development of targeted therapeutic strategies for these conditions.
