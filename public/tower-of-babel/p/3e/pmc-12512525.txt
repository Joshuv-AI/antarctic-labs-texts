Abrupt formation and spontaneous resolution of a mobile right atrial thrombus following intraoperative cardiopulmonary resuscitation during da Vinci robotic-assisted surgery: a case report
Authors: Yunfeng Jiang, Wenqing Ruan, Yangyang Xia, Chenlong Xie, Wei Yuan, Jiangang Song, Jian Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRight atrial thrombus is rare yet potentially life-threatening, carrying a substantial risk of pulmonary embolism and death. Management generally includes anticoagulation, thrombolysis, surgical thrombectomy, or catheter-based removal, whereas prognosis is poor when the thrombus remains untreated. We describe the intraoperative formation and spontaneous dissolution of a large, mobile right atrial thrombus that ultimately led to a favorable clinical outcome. During da Vinci robotic-assisted surgery, the patient experienced sudden cardiac arrest and was resuscitated with intraoperative cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Trans-esophageal echocardiography revealed a massive, free-floating thrombus in the right atrium, which subsequently resolved without the need for pharmacological or mechanical intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12245-025-01006-y.

Introduction

Right atrial thrombus (RAT) is an uncommon yet potentially life-threatening cardiovascular entity, accounting for approximately 5–10% of all intracardiac thrombi. A thrombus may arise through thromboembolic migration from deep veins after cardiac-device implantation (e.g., pacemaker leads), in the presence of structural heart abnormalities (e.g., right-atrial enlargement, tricuspid-valve disease), or during hypercoagulable states (e.g., malignancy, inherited thrombophilia). Intraoperative RAT is rare and may present alongside pulmonary embolism or be discovered incidentally on echocardiography [1]. Rapid recognition is critical because pulmonary embolism carries a high risk of hemodynamic collapse and death [2, 3]. Trans-esophageal echocardiography (TEE) is pivotal for diagnosing and managing RAT, as it delineates thrombus morphology and mobility, which are essential for determining the need for mechanical or surgical intervention [4]. Despite its clinical significance, evidence-based guidelines for managing mobile RAT are lacking; reported strategies include anticoagulation, surgical thrombectomy, and catheter-based extraction [5].

We present a rare case of abrupt formation and spontaneous dissolution of a large, mobile RAT detected with intraoperative TEE immediately after cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) during da Vinci robotic-assisted surgery. This case highlights the importance of intraoperative imaging and vigilance for thromboembolic complications in the perioperative setting, followed by a concise review of relevant literature.

Case report

A 57-year-old male (height: 174 cm; weight: 65 kg) with a history of recurrent rectal cancer and type 2 diabetes mellitus (well-controlled with metformin) was scheduled for da Vinci robotic-assisted bladder flap ureteral reconstruction to treat a ureteral fistula. The patient underwent initial surgery for rectal cancer four years ago and required a subsequent operation one year ago due to disease progression, during which a peripherally inserted central catheter was placed via the right upper extremity. Preoperative evaluation revealed elevated D-dimer levels (0.72 µg/mL), likely attributed to malignancy, with a normal coagulation profile. Chest computed tomography revealed multiple pulmonary nodules, suggesting metastatic disease.

Intraoperative monitoring consisted of electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, bispectral index (BIS), and invasive arterial pressure measurement via a right radial arterial line. Central-venous access was obtained through ultrasound-guided cannulation of the right internal jugular vein. Endotracheal intubation was performed under general anesthesia. Maintenance anesthesia consisted of sevoflurane (2–3%), propofol, and remifentanil, with a BIS maintained between 40 and 60.

Approximately 1 h after robotic instrumentation, the patient experienced abrupt hemodynamic collapse. End-tidal CO₂ fell from 59.7 mmHg to 19.0 mmHg, arterial pressure dropped to 80/40 mmHg, and SpO₂ declined to 80%. Despite sequential epinephrine doses (20 µg followed by 1 mg, cumulative 5 mg), cardiac arrest ensued. CPR was initiated immediately; methylprednisolone 80 mg was administered, and targeted temperature management was begun. Sinus rhythm at 100 bpm was restored after two cycles of CPR.

Bedside transthoracic echocardiography revealed a large, mobile thrombus in the right ventricle (Fig. 1A). During cardiac cycles, TEE demonstrated a mobile thrombus migrating across the tricuspid valve, with a circumference of 9 cm, an area of 4.57 cm² (Fig. 1B), and measuring 2.67 cm in length by 2.46 cm in width (Fig. 1 C). Biventricular systolic function remained preserved, and no valvular abnormality was detected. A repeat TEE 30 min after the arrest showed a complete, spontaneous disappearance of the thrombus (Fig. 2 A), with no evidence of embolism in the pulmonary artery or its branches (Fig. 2B). Because continuous real-time TEE monitoring was unavailable, the precise timing, mechanism, and destination of thrombus dissolution could not be determined. Pulmonary computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed no evidence of pulmonary embolism. Maximum intensity projection (MIP) images (Fig. 3A-C) confirmed patency throughout the pulmonary arterial tree, and volume-rendered technqiue (VRT) reconstructions (Fig. 3D-F) showed normal vascular anatomy without obstruction. Cerebral CTA revealed moderate stenosis of the right vertebral artery and the A3 segment of the left anterior cerebral artery. Laboratory testing revealed markedly increased levels of D-dimer (99.27 µg/mL), fibrin-degradation products (325 µg/mL), and factor VIII hyperactivity (407.7%).

A multidisciplinary team, including cardiology, cardiothoracic surgery, and radiology, reviewed the case and recommended supportive care with surgical completion of the planned procedure. The patient remained intubated and was transferred to the intensive care unit. Neurological function gradually improved, with BIS increasing from 85 to full consciousness by postoperative day 1.

Postoperative transthoracic echocardiography and lower extremity venous Doppler ultrasound confirmed the absence of residual intracardiac or deep venous thrombi. The patient was successfully extubated on postoperative day 1, discharged in stable condition on day 10, and reported no complications at the one-week follow-up.

Discussion

Although uncommon, RATs are clinically significant because they can precipitate sudden hemodynamic collapse. Morphologically, they are classified into two categories. Type A thrombi (mobile) originate from peripheral venous thrombosis and extend into the right-sided chambers, particularly the right atrium. They are highly mobile and may prolapse across the tricuspid valve. Free-floating Type A RAT accompanies massive pulmonary embolism in approximately 4–18% of cases. Type B thrombi (non-mobile) adhere to the right atrial or ventricular wall and are frequently associated with intracardiac foreign bodies (e.g., pacemaker leads, intravascular catheters) or structural anomalies. These thrombi usually display a polypoid morphology with a broad base, pose a lower risk of pulmonary embolization, and therefore confer a more favorable prognosis [6, 7].

The formation of RAT reflects Virchow’s triad: hypercoagulability, venous stasis, and endothelial dysfunction. The imbalance between physiological hemostasis and pathological coagulation involves complex interactions among platelets, coagulation factors, and cellular microparticles [2]. In our case, malignancy-associated hypercoagulability may be a key contributor. According to the European Society of Cardiology, the risk factors of venous thrombosis can be stratified into strong (e.g., hip or femoral fractures, joint replacement, major trauma, spinal cord injury), moderate (e.g., central venous catheters, chemotherapy, malignancy), and weak categories (e.g., prolonged immobility, advanced age, obesity). Our patient exhibited three moderate risk factors (i.e., central venous catheter, chemotherapy, and malignancy) and one weak factor, laparoscopic surgery. His preoperative coagulation tests and electrocardiography were within normal limits, suggesting an overall intermediate-to-low thrombotic risk [8].

Echocardiography remains fundamental for diagnosing RAT. Transthoracic echocardiography assesses atrial dimensions, morphology, and flow dynamics, whereas TEE offers superior resolution for identifying small or atypically positioned thrombi. In this case, bedside subxiphoid transthoracic imaging after CPR first disclosed a large thrombus in the right ventricle, subsequently confirmed by TEE.

Mobile RAT confers substantial hemodynamic risk. Reported complications include fatal pulmonary embolism, acute right-heart failure owing to tricuspid- or pulmonary-valve obstruction, and paradoxical embolism through a right-to-left shunt (e.g., patent foramen ovale). Approximately 4% of patients with acute pulmonary embolism exhibit RAT on echocardiography [4, 9].

Early recognition, however, is challenging. Classic manifestations, such as sudden dyspnoea, chest pain, or signs of right-heart failure, may be masked by general anesthesia and mechanical ventilation. Moreover, transthoracic echocardiography yields a false-negative rate of up to 60% for RAT detection, highlighting the diagnostic superiority of intraoperative TEE [10–13]. In our patient, TEE was indispensable for visualizing the thrombus and guiding management decisions. Transesophageal echocardiogram contributes to high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation [14].

Timely diagnosis and intervention of RAT are critical, given the reported mortality rates of 28–45% [12, 15]. However, consensus management guidelines remain lacking. Barrios et al. reported similar outcomes between anticoagulation and reperfusion therapies [16], whereas a meta-analysis of 207 patients revealed significantly higher mortality with anticoagulation alone compared to surgical or thrombolytic interventions (36.4% vs. 18.0%) [17]. In our patient, both anticoagulation and thrombolysis were unsafe due to active robotic surgery and high bleeding risk. Although mechanical removal was considered, spontaneous thrombus resolution rendered the need for invasive intervention unnecessary.

This case illustrates a rare instance of spontaneous RAT dissolution without clinical sequelae. Similarly, a previous case report described a large, mobile RAT that induced acute hemodynamic instability but resolved spontaneously during preparation for embolectomy, with only a minute residual thrombus subsequently retrieved [18]. Another report has also observed spontaneous resolution of a large RAT, captured by continuous intraoperative TEE monitoring, without hemodynamic compromise during abdominal aortic aneurysm surgery [19]. Collectively, these cases highlight the indispensable role of intraoperative TEE in revealing the dynamic nature of right heart thrombi, which may form and resolve spontaneously, thereby guiding urgent and individualized management. The distinctive feature of our case is the spontaneous resolution of a Type A intracardiac thrombus, which carries a significantly higher risk of morbidity, yet resolved without any therapeutic intervention.

The spontaneous dissolution of right atrial thrombi is likely a multifactorial process involving the synergistic effects of endogenous fibrinolytic pathways, mechanobiological endothelial responses, and inflammatory cell activity [20–22]. Acute events such as cardiac arrest or major surgery can trigger systemic stress and dramatic hemodynamic changes, leading to strong endothelial activation and massive release of tissue plasminogen activator [23, 24]. The resulting fibrinolytic activation, enhanced by plasminogen, further facilitated by mechanical fragmentation, and supported by infiltrating monocytes/macrophages that phagocytose the clot, may completely resolve small thrombi without symptoms. In contrast, the breakdown of larger thrombi can generate sufficient fibrin degradation products, causing a transient elevation in D-dimer levels.

The inherent thrombolytic capacity of the lungs may also contribute to clot clearance, potentially accounting for the absence of pulmonary embolism on postoperative CTA despite prior visualization of a large thrombus, a phenomenon termed “clinically silent pulmonary embolism” [25]. The patient may have experienced a paradoxical or micro-embolic embolism. Although postoperative pulmonary angiography could have confirmed the diagnosis, it was not performed due to the patient’s hemodynamic stability and absence of symptoms. Thus, the diagnosis remains uncertain. An intracardiac shunt could not be excluded, as a bubble study was not feasible during resuscitation.

Several limitations merit acknowledgment. Diagnosis relied exclusively on imaging, and no specimen was available for pathological evaluation because the thrombus dissolved spontaneously. Although echocardiography secured the diagnosis, the lack of histological analysis precludes definitive insight into the composition and underlying etiology of the thrombus.

In conclusion, although spontaneous thrombus resolution is rare, vigilant monitoring should precede any aggressive intervention. Trans-esophageal echocardiography must be prioritized for high-risk oncology patients undergoing prolonged robotic procedures. Further research is required to identify predictors of spontaneous thrombus dissolution and to develop risk-stratified management algorithms.
