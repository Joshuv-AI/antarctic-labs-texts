Biomimetic nanovesicle vaccines derived from dendritic cells sensitized with whole tumor antigens for melanoma immunotherapy
Authors: Jiaye Lu, Fengze Miao, Yingchao Zhao, Jun Liu, Yu Meng, Han Yan, Ruyi Wang, Quangang Zhu, Zongguang Tai, Zhongjian Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer vaccine research has made significant progress in antigen discovery, delivery systems, and combination therapies. However, optimizing antigen selection and delivery remains a current challenge. To address this, we utilized oxidatively treated tumor cells as a source of antigens, effectively enhancing their immunogenicity. Inspired by the antigen delivery capabilities of dendritic cells (DCs) via extracellular vesicles, we developed a novel biomimetic nanovesicle vaccine, diA-MADCVac. This vaccine inherits the targeting ability of DCs, enabling precise antigen delivery to lymph nodes. Moreover, as a carrier, it efficiently delivers both adjuvants and intact tumor antigens, thereby playing a synergistic role in activating the immune system.ResultBy targeting lymph nodes, diA-MADCVac effectively induces DCs maturation, activating tumor-specific T cell responses and generating trained immune memory. Furthermore, in a melanoma mouse model, diA-MADCVac significantly altered the tumor microenvironment, triggering a strong anti-tumor immune response and inhibiting tumor growth. This effect was characterized by increased secretion of cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α in the tumor, along with a higher number of infiltrating T cells, which enhanced the immune system's tumor-killing capacity.ConclusionThis study introduces a novel vaccination strategy utilizing the DC-derived nanovesicle vaccine, diA-MADCVac. Systematic studies and experimental validation demonstrate that diA-MADCVac exhibits excellent biocompatibility, ensuring its safety for in vivo use. This nanovesicle serves as an effective carrier, efficiently delivering both adjuvants and antigens to lymph nodes, thereby enhancing DC-mediated anti-tumor immune responses. Additionally, diA-MADCVac promotes trained immunity and enhances the tumor-killing capacity of immune cells. With its unique advantages, diA-MADCVac offers an efficient, safe, and promising new approach for tumor treatment, providing renewed hope for cancer patients.Keywords: Dendritic cell nanovesicle, Cancer vaccine, Whole tumor antigens, STING signaling pathway, Melanoma immunotherapy

Introduction

The global incidence of melanoma, an extremely aggressive and lethal cancer, is on the rise [1,2]. In recent years, the advent of immunotherapy has brought revolutionary changes to cancer treatment, especially for melanoma [3,4]. Among various immunotherapy strategies, the development of tumor vaccines has attracted significant attention. These vaccines activate antigen-presenting cells (APCs) to stimulate specific anti-tumor cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), thereby inducing immune memory and long-term anti-cancer immunity [5,6]. The current research focuses on identifying tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) that can elicit efficient immune responses and developing novel vaccine carriers to improve efficacy and safety [7,8].

Dendritic cells (DCs), as powerful antigen-presenting entities among APCs, are adept at processing self- and non-self-antigens and presenting them to immature T lymphocytes, thereby triggering specific immune responses and modulating immune tolerance [9,10]. DC-based vaccines have been approved to induce effective antigen-specific immune responses to eliminate tumor cells [11]. However, TAA pre-handling and loading, lymph node (LN) homing, and quality control present several challenges for DC vaccines. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop the next generation of DC-based vaccines to enhance their efficacy.

A primary challenge in developing DC-based vaccines is identifying TAAs that DCs can efficiently uptake and that can trigger cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs). Notably, the high heterogeneity of tumor cells makes it extremely challenging to construct target antigens expressed in all subpopulations [12,13]. Clinical studies have shown that most patients do not respond to tumor lysate-activated DCs [14,15], despite the potential for complete tumor lysates to inhibit the growth of escaping tumor cells [16]. This is attributed to insufficient immunogenic danger signals and inadequate tumor antigen delivery during DC immunization. Furthermore, DCs must migrate to lymphoid tissues to induce TAA-specific CTLs. However, only a small fraction of DCs can infiltrate the LNs following intradermal or subcutaneous injection. Cell-engineered nanovesicles (NVs), as highly biocompatible therapeutic carriers, can effectively mimic the physiological functions of their parent cells. They can carry medicines and antigens to tissues without requiring complicated alterations, and their synthesis and purifying procedures are straightforward [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. Incorporating vaccination adjuvants is a well-established strategy for enhancing immune responses to specific antigens [21,22]. STING agonists have demonstrated significant effectiveness as potent adjuvants for eliciting type I interferon (IFN-I) responses and inflammatory cytokines, with diaminobenzimidazole (diABZI) being particularly potent in this regard [[23], [24], [25]].

To address the aforementioned challenges of DC vaccines, this study designed a biomimetic DC nanovesicle vaccine (diA-MADCVac). The diA-MADCVac vaccine utilizes oxidized melanoma cell-engineered vesicles (oMNV) as antigens, co-loaded with the STING agonist diABZI. The carrier can effectively carry a variety of tumor antigens and a large number of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), and its nanoscale size facilitates the uptake of antigens by DCs. Following subcutaneous administration, the subcutaneous tissue harbors a high density of APCs, especially DCs. These APCs efficiently internalize vaccine antigens and migrate to draining LNs, where they initiate T-cell activation [26]. Furthermore, nanoscale vaccines can readily access lymphatic capillaries via gaps in the extracellular matrix, facilitating efficient accumulation within draining LNs [27]. This route also offers delayed drug metabolism, sustained antigen release, and prolonged exposure to APCs, which collectively contribute to reduced dosing frequency and diminished systemic toxicity [28]. The vaccine releases diABZI and cytokines within the body, triggering the primary immune response and activating the STING pathway to enhance the immune response, ultimately producing a durable anti-tumor immune response. diA-MADCVac demonstrates the ability to stimulate a robust adaptive immune response while simultaneously promoting trained immunity, making it a potential breakthrough in melanoma immunotherapy.

Materials and methods

Materials

The hydrophilic polycarbonate membranes were obtained from Merck Millipore Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The ATP kit, the BCA protein kit, the western lightning enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate, the caulobacter brilliant blue staining solution, DiI, DiO Lyso Tracker Green, and DAPI were bought from Beyotime Biotechnology (China). The lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) kit was bought from Yeasen (China). The diABZI STING agonist (Compound 3) and the STING inhibitor C-178 were bought from Selleck (S8796, USA). The PAGE gel preparation kit was bought from Epizyme (China). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies and APC anti-mouse CD80 were purchased from Proteintech (USA). Recombinant IL-2, LPS, IL-4 and GM-CSF cytokines were bought from PeproTech Inc. (Rocky Hill, USA). The mouse lymphocyte isolation solution and the ELISpot assay were purchased from Dakewe Biotechnology Co., Ltd (China). Mouse IL-6, IL-1β, IFN-γ, IFN-β, CXCL10 and TNF-α enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits were purchased from WellBio (China). All antibodies used in the flow cytometry experiments in vitro and BV421 anti-mouse CD3e were purchased from BD (USA). FITC anti-mouse CD4, PerCP-Cyanine5.5 anti-mouse CD8a and PerCP-Cyanine5.5 anti-mouse CD11c were purchased from Invitrogen™ (USA). Anti-FoxP3, anti-HMGB1 and anti-HSP90 antibodies were purchased from CST (USA). Other antibodies used in the immunofluorescence experiments, the anti-CRT antibody and the WB antibodies were purchased from Abcam (UK).

Cells and animals

The B16 and DC2.4 cell lines were grown in RPMI 1640 medium with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. Five-week-old C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Sbefu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and were maintained in the animal laboratory of Shanghai Skin Disease Hospital. All animal experiments were carried out completely according to the Guidelines for the Use of Laboratory Animals, which were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Skin Disease Hospital (Shanghai, China) with the ID number: 2024–55.

Preparation of oxidative melanoma nanovesicle (oMNV)

The cultured B16 cells were digested by trypsin, centrifuged, and washed twice with PBS. To obtain oxidative melanoma cells, B16 cells were incubated with 10 mL of PBS containing 350 μM HClO for 30 min. Following incubation, the cells were resuspended in RPMI 1640 media and incubated for 6 h.

Apoptosis was monitored and captured using a light microscope. The supernatant culture medium was taken at specific times (0 h, 1 h, 2 h and 4 h) of re-incubation. The matching test kits were utilized to quantify the contents of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) present in the supernatant.

The oxidative B16 cells were resuspended in PBS and homogenized using a high-pressure homogenizer (NATONE, NT-H3, China). After the pretreatment, the homogenized cell suspension was subjected to three sequential extrusions. These extrusions were performed through polycarbonate membranes with decreasing pore diameters, starting from 3.0 μm, followed by 0.45 μm, and finally 0.22 μm. A high-precision, high-pressure, gas-tight sterile filter extruder was used for this process (PhD Technology LLC, NanoAble-150, USA). Then, the oxidative melanoma nanovesicles (oMNV) containing whole tumor antigens were purified by ultrafiltration.

Preparation of diA-MADCVac

The DC2.4 cells were collected and plated at a concentration of 2.0 × 106/mL in 10 cm cell culture dishes, and were incubated with 10 mL of brand-new RPMI 1640 complete medium that included oMNV (50 μg protein/mL) for 4 h. Following uptake of oMNVs by DC2.4 cells, the cells were collected. diABZI was dissolved in DMSO and subsequently diluted with PBS. The oMNV-loaded DC2.4 cells were then resuspended in PBS containing diABZI. During the process of cell extrusion, the cell membrane undergoes recombination. Due to its lipophilicity, diABZI spontaneously intercalates into the lipid bilayer membrane of the newly formed vesicles, thereby achieving drug encapsulation. Moreover, free (unencapsulated) adjuvant is removed by high-speed centrifugation (40,000×g, 1 h).

The engineered biomimetic DC nanovaccines were designated as diA-MADCVac. Nanovaccines lacking oMNV and diABZI were termed DCNV, while those lacking only diABZI were referred to as MADCVac. And B16NV refers to untreated B16 cells subjected to high-pressure extrusion to produce nanovesicles. For short-term storage (less than a week), all nanovesicles were kept at 4 °C, whereas long-term storage required freezing at −80 °C with the addition of 2 % alginate as a cryoprotectant.

Characterization of oMNV and diA-MADCVac

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was utilized to observe the morphology of oMNV and diA-MADCVac. The nanovesicle suspension was incubated overnight after fixation. Before sampling, the suspension was mixed well in double-distilled and dripped onto the carbon film-covered copper mesh. After drying, the suspension was then stained with 1 % phosphotungstic acid dye, and was dried up naturally for 10–15 min. After that, the copper mesh was placed under the TEM to be observed (with an accelerating voltage of 75 kV), and the appropriate magnification was selected to be observed and photographed. The nanovaccines’ PDI and particle size were measured via the DLS method.

To quantify ATP levels in B16NV and oMNV lysates, we use a commercial ATP assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Proteins from B16NV and oMNV were collected and centrifuged, and the supernatant was mixed with loading buffer and boiled to ensure complete denaturation. The protein was then subjected to Western blot (WB) analysis.

During the preparation of diA-MADCVac, the oxidative B16 cells were labeled with DiO before extrusion. Concurrently, DC2.4 cells were labeled with DiI before antigen uptake. Subsequent steps were performed in accordance with the diA-MADCVac preparation protocol detailed in Section 2.4. The structural characterization of diA-MADCVac was captured by a Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (CLSM) (Nikon TI2-E + A1 R, Japan) and processed with associated software (NIS-Elements Viewer).

To assess long-term storage stability, particle size and PDI were monitored at specific intervals (days 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 19, and 26) under storage conditions of 4 °C and −80 °C. Additionally, the resistive pulse sensing (RPS) method was also used to evaluate the nanovaccines' zeta potential, and the BCA protein kit was used to quantify the nanovaccines’ protein content.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was used to measure the encapsulation efficiency (EE%) of diABZI in the nanovaccines. 90 % acetonitrile was used as the mobile phase in the assay, with a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The oMNV, DCNV, and diA-MADCVac were next subjected to nano-flow cytometry analysis in order to evaluate the expression of surface markers. APC-conjugated anti-mouse MHC II, PE-conjugated anti-mouse CCR7, Super Bright 436-conjugated anti-mouse PD-L1 and FITC-conjugated anti-mouse Galectin-9 antibodies were used to label the nanovesicles. A flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, CytoFLEX nano, USA) was used for gathering the data, and FlowJo software was used to analyze the findings.

Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE)

Membrane proteins on oMNV and diA-MADCVac were analyzed by SDS-PAGE. Proteins from the cells and nanovaccines were collected and centrifuged, and the supernatant was mixed with loading buffer and boiled to ensure complete denaturation. PAGE gels were prepared using the PAGE gel preparation kit, and electrophoresis was performed after adding samples to the sample wells. Following electrophoresis, the protein bands were clearly visible after the gel was stained with Coomassie brilliant blue and subsequently washed and decolorized. Images of the gels were then taken for documentation.

Western blot (WB)

Protein samples of B16NV and oMNV were extracted. WB analysis was then performed following the same procedure as outlined earlier in SDS-PAGE. After running the gel, the separated proteins were transferred to the membranes. The membrane was initially incubated overnight with the CRT primary antibody (1:1000), HMGB1 primary antibody (1:1000) and HSP90 primary antibody (1:1000), followed by washing. It was then incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:50,000) for 1 h. For detection, membranes were shortly incubated in Western Lightning ECL substrate and imaged with the imaging system.

Protein samples of DC2.4, DCNV and diA-MADCVac were extracted. After running the gel, the separated proteins were transferred to the membranes. The membrane was initially incubated overnight with the CCR7 primary antibody (1:500), followed by washing. It was then incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:20,000) for 1 h. For detection, membranes were shortly incubated in Western Lightning ECL substrate and imaged with the imaging system.

DC uptake of nanovaccines

The DC uptake of nanovaccines by DC2.4 was captured by a CLSM. For DC uptake, DC2.4 were incubated with PBS, oMNV, DCNV, and diA-MADCVac in confocal dishes for 4 h, respectively. Nanovaccines were labeled with DiI, lysosomes of DC2.4 cells were stained using Lyso Tracker Green, and nuclei were stained with DAPI. Fluorescence co-localization of vaccine and DC2.4 was captured by CLSM (Nikon ECLIPSE Ji, Japan) and processed with associated software (NIS-Elements Viewer).

Bone marrow-derived DC (BMDC) maturation

The tibias and fibulas of female C57BL/6J mice were used to separate BMDCs, which were then planted in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 106 cells per well. To encourage their differentiation and proliferation, 10 ng/mL interleukin-4 (IL-4) and 20 ng/mL granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) were added to the cell culture. Every three days, a new medium was installed. Under a light microscope, 80–90 % of the cells were seen to have differentiated into BMDCs on days 6–8. A new medium was added in place of the supernatant. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (1 μg/mL), DCNV (25 μg protein/mL), oMNV (25 μg protein/mL), MADCVac (25 μg protein/mL), and diA-MADCVac (25 μg protein/mL) were all added to BMDCs and cultured for 24 h.

The BMDCs were next subjected to flow cytometry analysis in order to evaluate the expression of surface markers. APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD11c, PE-conjugated anti-mouse CD80, and PE-Cyanine7-conjugated anti-mouse CD86 antibodies were used to label the cells. A flow cytometer (Beckman, Navios, USA) was used for gathering the data, and FlowJo software was used to analyze the findings.

The tumor killing capacity of splenic lymphocytes

The spleens of female C57BL/6J mice were used to isolate mouse splenic cells, which were then cultivated in RPMI-1640 complete media using a commercially available mouse lymphocyte isolation solution (Biotechwell).

To evaluate the tumor cell-killing ability of mouse splenic lymphocytes following drug administration, healthy 5-week-old female mice were immunized via subcutaneous injections of the vaccine on days 0, 3, and 5 (25 μg protein per injection). On day 7, splenic lymphocytes were extracted as previously described. The expression of surface markers on mouse splenic lymphocytes was evaluated with flow cytometry using APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD3, PE-conjugated anti-mouse CD8, and PerCP-eFluor® 710-conjugated anti-mouse CD4. Flow cytometric analysis of cells and data processing were conducted as previously described. The splenic lymphocytes extracted from nanovaccine-treated mice in each group were added to B16 cells (cell number ratio 40:1). After 6 h of co-incubation, B16 cell killing was assessed by quantifying LDH levels in the supernatant using an LDH assay kit.

Examination of immunization training capacity

BMDCs were planted in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 106 cells/well. By days 6–8, 80–90 % of the cells had differentiated into BMDCs, as observed under a light microscope. The cell medium was replaced with fresh medium. BMDCs were incubated with PBS, LPS (1 μg/mL), DCNV (25 μg protein/mL), oMNV (25 μg protein/mL), MADCVac (25 μg protein/mL), and diA-MADCVac (25 μg protein/mL) for 24 h. After 24 h of co-culture, the medium was rechanged, and the cells were cultured for an additional 4 days. The cells were then re-stimulated with 1 μg/mL LPS for 24 h. Following this stimulation period, mature DCs were analyzed using flow cytometry as previously described, and the secretion levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) by mature BMDCs were assessed using ELISA.

As outlined in the previous section, an in vitro immune training model was established to evaluate the role of the STING signaling pathway in this process. The experimental groups were designed as follows: PBS + C-178, MADCVac (25 μg protein/mL) + C-178, diA-MADCVac (25 μg protein/mL) + C-178, PBS, MADCVac (25 μg protein/mL), and diA-MADCVac (25 μg protein/mL). The cells were then re-stimulated with 1 μg/mL LPS for 24 h. Following this stimulation period, the BMDCs were next subjected to flow cytometry analysis to evaluate the expression of surface markers. PerCP Efluor 710-conjugated anti-mouse CD40, PE-conjugated anti-mouse CD80, APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD86 and Super Bright 600-conjugated anti-mouse MHC II antibodies were used to label the cells. DCs were analyzed using flow cytometry as previously described, and the secretion levels of IFN-β, CXCL10, IL-1β, and TNF-α by mature BMDCs were assessed using ELISA. Proteins from DCs were collected and centrifuged, and the supernatant was mixed with loading buffer and boiled to ensure complete denaturation. The protein was then subjected to WB analysis.

To examine the in vivo intrinsic immune training capacity of the vaccine, healthy 5-week-old C57BL/6 female mice were selected. PBS (100 μL/each), oMNV (25 μg protein/each), DCNV (25 μg protein/each), MADCVac (25 μg protein/each), and diA-MADCVac (25 μg protein/each) were administered via subcutaneous injection on the right dorsal side of the mice. Three weeks later, LPS (8 μg/each) was injected intraperitoneally, and peripheral blood serum was taken from the mice 4 h later. Cytokine concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in peripheral blood serum were quantified using ELISA.

In order to assess how vaccine training affects innate immunity and how it affects the adaptive immunological response, healthy female C57BL/6 mice aged five weeks were chosen. On the right side of their backs, mice obtained subcutaneous injections of PBS (100 μL/each), oMNV (25 μg protein/each), DCNV (25 μg protein/each), MADCVac (25 μg protein/each), and diA-MADCVac (25 μg protein/each). Three weeks after the initial subcutaneous vaccine injection, the mice received a secondary stimulation with nanovaccines. After three days, splenic cells were separated, and an ELISpot assay was used to evaluate the antigen-specific response of IFN-γ-expressing lymphocytes.

In vivo imaging of nanovaccines

To investigate the in vivo biodistribution and migration of the nanovaccines to the draining LNs, the nanovesicles were labeled with DiI in advance. C57BL/6J mice were subcutaneously injected with DCNV (50 μg protein/each), oMNV (50 μg protein/each), MADCVac (50 μg protein/each), or diA-MADCVac (50 μg protein/each). Whole animals and major organs (liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, heart, and inguinal LNs) were obtained for ex vivo fluorescence imaging 48 h after injection. Fluorescence imaging was evaluated with In-vivo Imager (VIEWORKS ART100, Korean). After 48h, inguinal LNs were harvested, fixed in 10 % formaldehyde solution, and prepared as frozen sections.

Animal models and immunization

The C57BL/6J mice, all female and 5 weeks old, weighing 14–15 g, were subcutaneously inoculated with 8 × 104 B16 cells in the right dorsal region. Tumor dimensions were periodically measured and documented using a Vernier caliper.

In the therapeutic investigations, mice were first given injections of B16 cells to create tumors. Five days later, the C57BL/6J mice were divided into five groups at random (n = 6/group): PBS (100 μL/each), DCNV (25 μg protein/each), oMNV (25 μg protein/each), MADCVac (25 μg protein/each) and diA-MADCVac (25 μg protein/each). Immunizations with nanovaccine formulations were administered 4 times at 5-day intervals. This dosing regimen. was selected based on a preliminary screening. Mice were turned into unconsciousness with isoflurane on day 24, and they were then killed by cervical dislocation. Mice's kidneys, LNs, and tumor tissues were removed and preserved in 4 % paraformaldehyde or PBS for further analytical research.

The C57BL/6J mice were divided into five groups (n = 6/group) at random for the preventative investigations, mice were administered with PBS (100 μL/each), DCNV (25 μg protein/each), oMNV (25 μg protein/each), MADCVac (25 μg protein/each) and diA-MADCVac (25 μg protein/each) 4 times, each 5 days apart. The mice's right dorsal area received a subcutaneous injection of 8 × 104 B16 cells on day 5 after the end of immunization, and this day is recorded as day 0. Mice were given isoflurane anesthesia on day 30, and entire blood samples were taken. The mice were then cervically dislocated to end their lives. For additional examination, tumor tissues were removed and preserved in 4 % paraformaldehyde.

The C57BL/6J mice were divided into five groups at random (n = 3 per group) for safety assessment, mice were administered with control (PBS), DCNV (25 μg protein/each), oMNV (25 μg protein/each), MADCVac (25 μg protein/each) and diA-MADCVac (25 μg protein/each) 4 times, each 5 days apart. On day 60, mice were sedated with isoflurane, and whole blood samples were collected before being killed by cervical dislocation. Major organs were subsequently removed and kept in 4 % paraformaldehyde for further investigation.

Flow cytometry (FCM)

The removed mouse LNs, spleen and tumor tissues from the animal immunotherapy group were prepared into cell suspensions and the erythrocytes in them were lysed. To analyze CD4 and CD8 T-cell subsets in spleens and tumors, FITC anti-mouse CD4, PerCP-Cyanine5.5 anti-mouse CD8a and BV421 anti-mouse CD3e were used to stain the cell suspension of spleens and tumors. DC analysis was performed using PerCP-Cyanine5.5 anti-mouse CD11c, APC anti-mouse CD80 and FITC anti-mouse CD86. Flow cytometric analysis of cells and data processing were conducted as previously described.

Histological evaluation

Briefly, paraffin tumor sections were dewaxed, hydrated, and treated to inactivate endogenous peroxidase enzymes. Using the in situ cell death detection kit for terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT)-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining, apoptotic cells in the tumor tissue were labeled, while cell nuclei were labeled with DAPI and blocked. Histological evaluation was conducted on representative sections randomly selected from each mouse. The primary antibody used in the staining was Ki67, while the secondary antibody was EnVision reagent (HRP/Rabbit and Mouse). 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) was used to develop the color, and hematoxylin was used to stain the cell nuclei. After that, the portions were sealed and dehydrated. A light microscope (Nikon, Japan) was used to view the slices, and pictures were taken for examination.

The extracted hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). Paraffin sections were deparaffinized with water, then baked at 60 °C for 1–2 h. The sections underwent standard dewaxing and hydration procedures. After hematoxylin staining for 10 min, excess color was removed by rinsing in running water. Differentiation was done using 0.7 % hydrochloric acid in ethanol for a few seconds, followed by rinsing under running water for about 15 min until the sections returned to blue. The sections were stained with alcoholic eosin for 30 s after being submerged in 95 % ethanol for 30 s. This process was repeated twice with 95 % ethanol for 30 s each time. The sections were then immersed in anhydrous ethanol for 30 s, repeated twice. Next, the sections were placed in xylene and carbolic acid for 30 s, followed by two immersions in xylene for 30 s each. Finally, the sections were mounted with neutral gum and covered with coverslips. After that, a light microscope (Nikon, Japan) was used to examine the slides.

Immunofluorescence

The mouse tumors were embedded in paraffin sections were prepared. The paraffin sections were then dewaxed and hydrated. The sections were antigenically repaired, microwave-boiled and cooled to room temperature. The sections were blocked with 3 % BSA for 30 min at room temperature following PBS washing. After that, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies for a whole night at 4 °C, including CD8 (1:100), CD3 (1:200), CD4 (1:400), CD163 (1:500), GR-1 (1:200), CD11b (1:500), CD68 (1:50), and FOXP3 (1:800). HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000) was added and allowed to sit at room temperature following PBS washing. Lastly, a digital slide scanner (3DHISTECH Ltd, Hungary) was used to examine the sections and take pictures.

Results

Preparation and characterization of biomimetic DC nanovesicle vaccine (diA-MADCVac)

B16 cells treated with HClO in vitro exhibited cytoplasmic lysis or apoptosis under the microscope (Supplemental Fig. 1A). This process was accompanied by a significant release of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Concentrations of these molecules in the cell culture supernatant were measured, revealing marked increases in ATP and LDH over time, with the highest levels observed 4 h after HClO treatment (Fig. 1B and 1C). The oxidized cells were then collected and used to prepare oMNV through high-pressure extrusion (Fig. 1A). With an average size of 176.3 ± 4.2 nm, the oMNV exhibited a nearly spherical shape and bilayer structure (Supplemental Fig. 1B and F). By measuring the levels of HSP90, CRT, HMGB1, and ATP in unoxidized B16NV and oxidized oMNV, compared with nanovesicles from untreated tumor cells, those from oxidatively treated tumor cells exhibited significantly higher levels of CRT, HMGB1, HSP90, and ATP. It can be seen that oMNV successfully carries DAMPs (Supplemental Fig. 1C and D).

Characterization of biomimetic nanovesicle vaccines. (A) Preparation procedure of diA-MADCVac; Content of ATP (B) and LDH (C) in the medium at different times after oxidization-treated cells (n = 3); (D) SDS-PAGE images of nanovesicles and their parent cells; (E) The particle size distribution and the representative TEM image of diA-MADCVac (scale bar: 100 nm); (F) Average zeta potential of the nanovesicles used in the study; (G) WB assay for protein expression in DCNV, diA-MADCVac and their parental DC2.4 cells; FCM analysis on the expression of the markers CCR7 (H), MHC II (I), PD-L1 (J) and Galectin-9 (K) on oMNV, DCNV, and diA-MADCVac. The data were analyzed using an ordinary one-way ANOVA comparison test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences with P < 0.05 (∗), P < 0.01 (∗∗), P < 0.001 (∗∗∗), P < 0.0001 (∗∗∗∗) were regarded as statistically significant. And ns means no significance.

The CLSM image reveals that diA-MADCVac is formed by homogenizing DC2.4 with oMNV (Supplemental Fig. 1E). The engineered biomimetic DC nanovaccines were designated as diA-MADCVac. Nanovaccines lacking oMNV and diABZI were termed DCNV, while those lacking only diABZI were referred to as MADCVac. The maturation rate of DC2.4 cells after antigen stimulation was measured by flow cytometry, identifying 50 μg protein/mL as the optimal antigen concentration (Supplemental Fig. 1G and 1H). DC2.4 cells loaded with oMNV were combined with an adjuvant (diABZI) to create a biomimetic DC nanovesicle vaccine (diA-MADCVac), which was further processed through high-pressure extrusion and purification (Fig. 1A). SDS-PAGE analysis confirmed that the vaccine's surface membrane proteins closely resembled those of the parental cells (Fig. 1D).

The diA-MADCVac showed an average particle size of 183.5 ± 2.6 nm, with a uniform distribution. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed a characteristic vacuolar membrane morphology for the diA-MADCVac (Fig. 1E). Its PDI value is about 0.06, showing good dispersion. Storage conditions (4 °C and −80 °C) did not affect particle size stability and dispersion over 26 days (Supplemental Fig. 1I and J).

The zeta potential of all vesicles ranged from −8.79 to −5.63 mV (Fig. 1F). The adjuvant content in the supernatant of diA-MADCVac after centrifugation was first detected by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). After calculation, 75 % of diABZI was successfully loaded into diA-MADCVac (Supplemental Fig. 2A and Supplemental Fig. 2B). Furthermore, western blotting (WB) confirmed the retention of CCR7 in the vaccine, highlighting its potential for DC migration to LNs (Fig. 1G). Surface protein expression levels of CCR7, MHC II, PD-L1, and Galectin-9 on oMNV, DCNV, and diA-MADCVac were quantitatively analyzed using nano-flow cytometry. The results revealed distinct surface protein profiles among the three nanovesicles, consistent with their differing cellular origins and immunological functions. Notably, both DCNV and diA-MADCVac exhibited significantly higher CCR7 expression compared to oMNV, suggesting enhanced LN homing capacity, a trait inherent to their DC origin. All three nanovesicles demonstrated detectable MHC II expression, with the highest level observed in DCNV, indicative of a robust antigen presentation potential. diA-MADCVac also retained elevated MHC II expression, and its co-stimulatory ligand composition may further synergize to promote T cell activation. In contrast, oMNVs showed markedly higher surface expression of the immunosuppressive molecules PD-L1 and Galectin-9 than both DCNV and diA-MADCVac, aligning with their tumor-derived immune-evasive properties. The substantially reduced expression of these checkpoint molecules in diA-MADCVac indicates that while it effectively delivers tumor antigens, it does not inherit significant immunosuppressive effects (Fig. 1H–K, Supplemental Fig. 3 B-E).

Immune activation in vitro of diA-MADCVac

To optimize vaccine manufacturing conditions, parameters such as incubation time and nanovesicle/adjuvant concentrations were refined. According to the bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) maturation assay, the optimized incubation time of oMNV and DC2.4 was 4 h, and the most effective nanovesicle/adjuvant concentrations were 25 μg protein/mL of diA-MADCVac and 550 ng/mL of diABZI, respectively (Supplemental Fig. 2A–2C). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that diA-MADCVac significantly enhanced BMDC maturation, evidenced by the highest proportion of CD80+ CD86+ CD11C+ cells(Fig. 2A and C). Although oMNVs carry tumor antigens and are theoretically capable of activating DCs, the results demonstrate that they poorly induce DC maturation. This phenomenon may be attributed to the presence of immunosuppressive molecules, such as PD-L1 and galectin-9, on the surface of oMNVs (Fig. 1J and K, Supplemental Fig. 3D and E). These molecules, derived from the parent tumor cells, are known to inhibit immune activation and may significantly compromise the immunostimulatory function of oMNVs. CLSM demonstrated efficient uptake of nanovaccines by DCs, with predominant localization on the cell surface and within lysosomes (Fig. 2B).

Besides DC maturation, diA-MADCVac also produced the most dramatic effect on lymphocyte differentiation, increasing the proportion of CD8+cytotoxic T cells (Fig. 2D and E). Furthermore, lymphocytes from diA-MADCVac-treated mice exhibited the strongest tumor-killing activity, as indicated by LDH release (Supplemental Fig. 7A and 7B).

Training immunity by diA-MADCVac

Both in vitro (Fig. 3A) and in vivo (Fig. 3D) models were created to assess whether diA-MADCVac offers trained immunity. In the in vitro model, BMDCs were cultivated with the nanovaccine for 4 days before being exposed to heterologous stimulation. In comparison to the other nanovaccine groups, the diA-MADCVac group produced the highest quantities of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the cell supernatant (Supplemental Fig. 9) and promoted the highest BMDC maturation upon secondary stimulation (Fig. 3B and C). The results imply that diA-MADCVac improves the training immunity. In the in vivo model, serum cytokine levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β showed similarly elevated patterns in the diA-MADCVac group (Fig. 3E). To further assess whether the provided training immunity from nanovaccines affects the adaptive immune response, an animal model was established. Treatment with diA-MADCVac significantly enhanced the IFN-γ response of spleen lymphocytes against tumor cells (Fig. 3F).

Additionally, we investigated the role of the STING pathway in training immunity. WB analysis revealed that vaccine treatment significantly enhanced phosphorylation levels of TBK1 and IRF3 (p-TBK1, p-IRF3) compared to the control group treated with STING inhibitor (Fig. 4A). These findings were strongly corroborated by ELISA. Secretion of IFN-β, the key chemokine CXCL10, and proinflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α) was significantly elevated in supernatants from the diA-MADCVac group. Critically, this diA-MADCVac-induced cytokine production was markedly suppressed by C-178 (Fig. 4B–E), demonstrating that STING pathway activation is necessary for initiating these downstream immune responses.

Furthermore, flow cytometric analysis revealed that diA-MADCVac potently promotes DC maturation, as evidenced by the upregulated expression of co-stimulatory molecules (CD80, CD86), MHC-II, and CD40. This effect was also reversed by C-178, which returned the DC phenotype to an immature state (Fig. 4F–I and Supplemental Fig. 10). This result directly links STING activation by diA-MADCVac to the functional maturation of antigen-presenting cells, providing a mechanistic basis for the subsequent robust T-cell activation observed in our study.

Potent delivery of diA-MADCVac to the lymph node

The targeting ability and accumulation of nanovaccines in LNs were investigated. Observation of LN sections revealed varying degrees of accumulation among the different groups, with the diA-MADCVac group showing the strongest fluorescence (Fig. 5A, C and 5D). Real-time imaging demonstrated that, while most nanovaccines in all groups remained at the injection site 48 h post-injection, MADCVac and diA-MADCVac exhibited migration to non-injection-site LNs. Notably, the diA-MADCVac group displayed the most significant accumulation in distant LNs (Fig. 5B).

Therapeutic immunization with diA-MADCVac on melanoma in vivo

To further assess the anti-tumor efficacy of the nanovaccine, a mouse melanoma model was established on the dorsal side, and the nanovaccine was injected subcutaneously (Fig. 6A). Subcutaneous tissue contains a high density of DCs that efficiently capture vaccine antigens and migrate to draining LNs, thereby initiating T cell activation. Body weight changes (Supplemental Fig. 11A) and tumor growth (Supplemental Fig. 11B) were dynamically monitored. All nanovaccine groups demonstrated varying degrees of tumor growth inhibition compared to the PBS group (Supplemental Fig. 9B, Fig. 6B and C). The results indicate that DCNVs alone exhibit a modest capacity to inhibit tumor growth, even in the absence of exogenously loaded antigens or adjuvants. This may be attributed to the spontaneous generation of trace amounts of immunostimulatory molecules during DCNV preparation, potentially leading to weak activation of innate immune cells, such as natural killer cells or macrophages. Additionally, DCs cultured in vitro may non-specifically internalize components from the culture medium, such as residual fetal bovine serum proteins or cellular debris, which could result in the presentation of low-abundance, non-tumor-specific antigens and subsequently elicit a broad-spectrum T-cell response. These fundamental immunostimulatory effects may explain the moderate tumor growth inhibition observed in the DCNV group compared to the PBS control group, which exhibits no inherent immunomodulatory function. Among these, diA-MADCVac exhibited the most pronounced inhibitory effect on tumor growth and provided the longest survival duration in mice (Fig. 6D). To investigate whether nanovaccines activated anti-tumor immune responses, we calculated the proportion of DC maturation in the draining LNs. The findings indicated that diA-MADCVac significantly enhanced DC maturation compared to other groups (Fig. 6E, Supplemental Fig. 12B). We also examined the differentiation of splenic lymphocytes and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. All nanovaccine groups promoted the differentiation of T lymphocytes into CD3+CD8+ cells and enhanced their infiltration into tumors, with diA-MADCVac showing the strongest effect (Fig. 6F and G, Supplemental Fig. 10D and Supplemental Fig. 12F).

Tumor tissue samples were further analyzed using Ki67 and TUNEL staining to assess tumor growth and apoptosis. Compared with the PBS control group, all groups exhibited varying levels of tumor necrosis and typical apoptotic features. Consistent with these findings, the diA-MADCVac group demonstrated the highest percentage of apoptotic tumor cells and the lowest proportion of Ki67-positive cells, confirming its potent anti-tumor activity in the mouse melanoma model (Fig. 6H–K).

Preventive immunization with diA-MADCVac on melanoma in vivo

A mouse melanoma model was created on the dorsal side of mice five days after prophylactic administration of nanovaccines (Fig. 7A). Tumor growth and survival were subsequently monitored. The results demonstrated that all nanovaccine groups significantly inhibited tumor growth compared to the control group (Fig. 7B and C). Among the groups, diA-MADCVac exhibited the most potent inhibition against tumor growth and the longest observed survival period (Fig. 7D). These results suggest that diA-MADCVac has a strong preventive effect against melanoma. Additionally, ELISA analysis of peripheral blood serum revealed that diA-MADCVac-treated mice had the highest levels of anti-tumor cytokines, suggesting its capacity to stimulate a strong systemic anti-tumor immune response (Fig. 7E–G). Immunofluorescence staining of tumor tissues further confirmed this observation. Mice in the diA-MADCVac group displayed a significant increase in different phenotypes of T cells within tumor tissues, while immunosuppressive cells such as Treg cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and tumor-associated macrophages (TAM, M2 phenotype) were significantly reduced (Fig. 7H).

Safety assessment

The safety of the nanovaccines was assessed using hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining and blood biochemical assays. The HE histological study indicated no significant alterations in the main organs of mice given nanovaccines compared to the ones untreated (Supplemental Fig. 13A). Additionally, serum levels of key biomarkers, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (Supplemental Fig. 13B), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (Supplemental Fig. 13C), creatine kinase (CK) (Supplemental Fig. 13D), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) (Supplemental Fig. 13E) were also measured to evaluate liver and heart function. No discernible variations in these markers were observed among the groups, suggesting that the nanovaccines did not cause significant toxicity.

Discussion and conclusion

Dendritic cell (DC) vaccines have emerged as a cornerstone in cancer immunotherapy. In this study, we developed a novel DC vaccine, diA-MADCVac, to tackle critical limitations of traditional DC-based tumor vaccines, such as inefficient tumor-associated antigen (TAA) processing, suboptimal lymph node (LN) targeting, and challenges in quality control.

Tumor cell oxidation using hypochlorous acid (HClO) induced primary necrosis, activating the immune system by releasing oxidative antigens. While these antigens promoted DC maturation and antigen presentation, tumor lysates alone exhibited poor immunogenicity and posed safety risks, leading to inadequate DC activation [29]. To address this, we designed oMNV by oxidizing tumor cells with HClO and encapsulating it within biomimetic DC nanovesicles using advanced nanobiotechnology. This design closely mimics natural cell functions, offering superior biocompatibility and stability. The incorporation of antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns into these vesicles ensured efficient antigen delivery. In vitro experiments demonstrated that oMNV significantly enhanced DC antigen uptake and processing, while the nanovesicle preparation method provided a versatile and scalable approach for cancer vaccine development.

Overcoming poor tissue residency and insufficient LN targeting remains a pressing challenge in tumor vaccine design. Conventional strategies, such as self-assembled aptamers and surface modifications, improve targeting but often require complex chemical procedures, increasing production costs and limiting broad application [30,31]. These methods also suffer from specificity constraints, as they target only certain DC subpopulations. In contrast, our approach utilizes DC-derived vesicles that naturally retain CCR7, a critical LN-homing receptor. CCR7 signaling enables DC migration toward draining LNs, enhancing vaccine efficacy [32]. The nanoscale size of diA-MADCVac further facilitates efficient antigen uptake and processing, promoting robust immune activation. Fluorescence imaging confirmed the precise targeting of DCs and LN tissues by diA-MADCVac, underscoring its superior delivery capabilities.

Adjuvants play a pivotal role in vaccine performance. STING agonists are promising immunomodulators that stimulate pro-inflammatory immune responses. However, the immune effects of STING agonists in tumor therapy remain controversial. Concerns regarding off-target activation in T cells and related toxicity issues persist, limiting their safety and efficacy in clinical applications [33,34]. In this study, the STING agonist diABZI was incorporated into the DC mimetic nanovesicle vaccine diA-MADCVac. Both ex vivo and in vivo experiments revealed that diABZI significantly enhanced DC phagocytosis, maturation, and cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) activation. This synergistic effect between diABZI and vaccine highlights the positive effects of diABZI in diA-MADCVac immunization and the potential of DC-derived nanovesicles as efficient carriers for immunostimulatory molecules.

Trained immunity, a phenomenon involving epigenetic modifications, metabolic reprogramming, and heightened cytokine secretion, has profound implications for cancer immunotherapy [35,36]. diA-MADCVac demonstrated the ability to induce trained immunity, enabling immune cells to respond more effectively to tumor antigens. Our findings validate the central role of the STING pathway in diA-MADCVac-trained immunity. Specifically, diA-MADCVac activates the STING-TBK1-IRF3 axis, driving the production of type I interferons, chemokines, and proinflammatory cytokines, and potently enhancing dendritic cell maturation and antigen presentation. This cascade of events fully defines the molecular basis for the induced trained immunity. By activating both innate and adaptive immunity, diA-MADCVac offers a promising dual-mode therapeutic strategy. However, the trained immunity observed here was less robust compared to previous reports [37], and the precise mechanisms, including epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming, were not fully elucidated [38,39]. Future studies should focus on exploring these underlying mechanisms to optimize the effects of trained immunity and expand its clinical applications.

While the application of oxidized tumor antigens and STING agonists (e.g., diABZI) has been documented [29,40]. But this study is designed to profoundly activate anti-tumor immunity by harnessing oxidative damage to boost tumor antigen immunogenicity, combined with the potent STING agonist diABZI to robustly stimulate the innate immune system. Notably, we innovatively employ dendritic cell-derived nanovesicles as a co-delivery platform. These nanovesicles not only inherit the LNs-targeting capacity of their parent cells but also, by virtue of their biomimetic membrane structure, efficiently encapsulate hydrophobic diABZI. Furthermore, they facilitate direct cytosolic delivery of both antigens and adjuvants through membrane fusion, thereby bypassing lysosomal degradation and enabling synchronized activation of immune signaling pathways. This strategy is anticipated to significantly enhance the activation efficiency of CD8+ T cells. Through a multi-level synergistic mechanism integrating enhanced immune recognition (antigen), potent immune activation (adjuvant), and intelligent targeted delivery (carrier), our approach provides a novel solution to overcome the limitations of existing vaccines, including suboptimal antigen presentation and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments.

This study demonstrates unique potential through its multi-faceted innovative design, distinguishing it from existing tumor vaccine strategies in clinical development. For example, unlike synthetic peptide vaccines such as ISA101, which rely on predefined single antigens [41](NCT02128126, NCT02426892), this study utilizes oxidized tumor pan-antigens to overcome antigenic limitations and elicit a broader immune response. Compared to dendritic cell vaccines like DCVax-L that load tumor lysates [42](NCT00045968), the DC-derived nanovesicle carrier used in this study not only inherits the targeting and immunogenicity of the parent cell but also enables simultaneous co-delivery of both antigens and adjuvants. Compared to strategies such as FAST, which uses broad-spectrum whole-cell antigens to suppress metastasis [43](NCT06756295), this study further integrates STING agonists (diABZI) to actively reverse the immunosuppressive microenvironment and achieve intracellular precise delivery via DC-derived nanovesicle. Additionally, unlike ATL-DC vaccines that combine TLR agonists like polyICLC [44](NCT01204684), this study targets the intracellular STING pathway (rather than TLR) and employs a biomimetic delivery system to more efficiently induce type I interferon responses and enhance cellular immunity, thereby avoiding potential tolerance issues associated with TLR activation. It should be noted that this research is currently at the preclinical stage, and its actual efficacy require systematic validation across multiple tumor models. The safety assessment in the current manuscript is relatively limited. This study is primarily focused on the preclinical validation of diA-MADCVac, with the core objectives of establishing design feasibility, preliminary immunogenicity, and antitumor efficacy of the biomimetic nanovaccine. Compared to clinically established vaccines with mature manufacturing processes and standardized large-scale production, this approach faces challenges such as the systematic investigations of long-term toxicity, standardized preparation of oxidized antigens and quality control for scalable manufacturing. Future work will focus on elucidating its mechanisms of action, leveraging insights from successful clinical strategies, and actively exploring combination therapies with immune checkpoint inhibitors and other modalities to facilitate clinical translation and expand options for cancer immunotherapy.

In conclusion, we successfully developed diA-MADCVac, an innovative biomimetic DC nanovaccine that integrates antigen-sensitized DCs, LN-targeting properties, and STING pathway activation. diA-MADCVac demonstrated exceptional biocompatibility, precise LN targeting, and potent anti-tumor efficacy. It effectively activated adaptive immunity, suppressed tumor growth and metastasis, and induced trained immunity, offering a groundbreaking approach for melanoma immunotherapy. These findings position diA-MADCVac as a versatile and powerful tool for melanoma prevention and treatment, paving the way for the next generation of cancer vaccines.
