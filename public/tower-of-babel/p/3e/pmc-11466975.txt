Characterization of stem cell landscape and assessing the stemness degree to aid clinical therapeutics in hematologic malignancies
Authors: Yuan-dong Feng, Jin Du, Hong-li Chen, Ying Shen, Ya-chun Jia, Peng-yu Zhang, Aili He, Yun Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHematological malignancies are a group of cancers that affect the blood, bone marrow, and lymphatic system. Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are believed to be responsible for the initiation, progression, and relapse of hematological malignancies. However, identifying and targeting CSCs presents many challenges. We aimed to develop a stemness index (HSCsi) to identify and guide the therapy targeting CSCs in hematological malignancies. We developed a novel one-class logistic regression (OCLR) algorithm to identify transcriptomic feature sets related to stemness in hematologic malignancies. We used the HSCsi to measure the stemness degree of leukemia stem cells (LSCs) and correlate it with clinical outcomes.We analyze the correlation of HSCsi with genes and pathways involved in drug resistance and immune microenvironment of acute myeloid leukemia (AML). HSCsi revealed stemness-related biological mechanisms in hematologic malignancies and effectively identify LSCs. The index also predicted survival and relapse rates of various hematologic malignancies. We also identified potential drugs and interventions targeting cancer stem cells (CSCs) of hematologic malignancies by the index. Moreover, we found a correlation between stemness and bone marrow immune microenvironment in AML. Our study provides a novel method and tool to assess the stemness degree of hematologic malignancies and its implications for clinical outcomes and therapeutic strategies. Our HSC stemness index can facilitate the precise stratification of hematologic malignancies, suggest possible targeted and immunotherapy options, and guide the selection of patients.Keywords: hematologic malignancy, Machine learning, Leukemia stem cell, Drug sensitivity, Bone marrow microenvironmentSubject terms: Cancer, Genetics

Introduction

Stemness is the capability of normal cells to differentiate into the various cell types that comprise the human body. Tathiane M Malta et al. developed mRNAsi and mDNAsi based on ES/iPSC transcriptional and epigenetical datasets, respectively1. These methods effectively quantified the cancer stemness in clinical tumors and provided useful guidance for predicting tumor recurrence and treatment. Studies have validated its significance in various tumors through the stemness index2,3.

Hematologic malignancies are malignant clonal proliferative diseases caused by genetic mutations and epigenetic changes in hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) or progenitor cells (HPCs) during their differentiation and development, with highly heterogeneous clinical manifestations and prognoses. Myeloid, lymphoid, stem cells, differentiated cells, or committed cells are the general lineages from which hematologic malignancies are classified4,5. With precise stratified diagnosis and treatment, the prognosis for patients with hematological malignancies has improved significantly. However, relapse remains an essential factor that results in a poor prognosis and a shorter overall survival rate for patients with hematological malignancies. One primary cause of relapse is the persistent presence of malignant cells, and CSCs, which have stemness and tumor initiation ability, are the root source of the persistence of malignant cells. CSCs exhibit stemness under various circumstances, including resistance to most conventional chemotherapy and the ability to remain dormant for long periods6.

HSCs are the best-understood mammalian somatic stem cells because of their hierarchical organization. Because of the advances in HSC research, LSCs were identified as the first CSCs, paving the way for the CSC hypothesis7. Both normal HSCs and malignant LSCs are the most well-known stem cell pair in humans and mice, and their research continues to pave the way for this field of study, with outcomes often applicable to other tissues and tumors. Eppert and colleagues developed an LSC/HSC-specific signature based on the gene expression of all functionally validated populations, demonstrating its influence on clinical outcomes in human leukemia8. However, the stemness index for hematological malignancies has been inadequately studied, and the mRNAsi has not demonstrated a strong correlation with clinical outcomes in hematological malignancies9. This correlation might be because mRNAsi does not capture the stemness features of HSCs, which are specific to the hematopoietic system. A more reliable and generalizable stemness index for hematological malignancies can be established based on HSCs.

There are many challenges in the field of hematological CSC research. On the one hand, CSCs are heterogeneous and dynamic; they can change their characteristics depending on the subtype of tumors, the microenvironment they reside in, and the interactions with other cells10. Therefore, it is not easy to identify CSCs. A common method is to use the specific surface markers of CSCs to distinguish them from normal and other tumor cells. For instance, CD34 + CD38- is a common marker for LSCs; functional assays have proven that LSCs can also be found in subpopulations other than the CD34 + /CD38- cells11. The CSC markers for other hematological malignancies are less well understood. The absence of accurate and convenient methods to identify CSCs impedes the progress of CSC-targeted therapy in hematological malignancies. On the other hand, targeting CSCs presents many obstacles, such as the resemblance between CSCs and normal stem cells that may induce adverse effects on healthy cells and the intricacy of the bone marrow niche that may shield CSCs from immune recognition and drug penetration12. In summary, as a pioneer in the field of CSC research, in-depth research on stemness in hematological malignancies helps us understand the pathogenesis of hematopoietic system diseases, promotes the discovery of new therapeutic targets and drugs, and drives research on CSCs in other tumor types.

In this study, we established features to measure the stemness of HSCs by using publicly accessible molecular profiles of normal hematopoietic cell states that varied in their stemness levels. A novel machine-learning algorithm based on OCLR was used to derive the HSC stemness index (HSCsi)13. We then explored associations between the HSCsi and hematological malignancies, novel oncogenic pathways, and somatic alterations. Notably, the feature revealed an outstanding power to distinguish LSCs from other leukemia cells and was highly correlated with the prognosis of hematologic malignancies. We analyzed the interaction networks among stemness index, genes, pathways, and drug sensitivity and identified anti-cancer drugs and potential compounds specific to selected molecular pathways that may eradicate CSC populations in hematological malignancies. Finally, using single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) and weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA), we obtained insight into the immune system-stemness interface in AML. Material and methods

Data collection and processing

Bioinformatics Array Research Tool (BART), an R web application, was used to log2-transform and RMA-normalize the raw RNA expression data from GEO datasets for further analysis14. We found 2,176 samples across normal peripheral blood mononuclear cells and 11 hematological malignancies, including leukemia (866), lymphoma (543), multiple myeloma (550), and myeloproliferative neoplasm (90). We also downloaded 173 hematopoietic and lymphoid cell lines’ RNA sequence data and Mutation information from the CCLE (https://sites.broadinstitute.org/ccle). Furthermore, we gathered RNA expression data of normal hematopoietic cells from 3 GEO datasets, including 26 hematopoietic stem cells, 52 multipotent hematopoietic progenitors, 33 unipotent hematopoietic progenitors, and other mature cells. Table S2 lists the samples in this study. 28 datasets from GEO were enrolled into this study: GSE109650, GSE109651, GSE115154, GSE12417, GSE136971, GSE17920, GSE19067, GSE19069, GSE24006, GSE24080, GSE24759, GSE25571, GSE26049, GSE30377, GSE31305, GSE34171, GSE35008, GSE35010, GSE37642, GSE38955, GSE49032, GSE51082, GSE58445, GSE63270, GSE68172, GSE76008, GSE9006, GSE98791.

OCLR-derived stemness index for HSCs

To calculate HSCsi based on mRNA expression, we constructed a predictive model using OCLR on the HSC samples from the GSE24759 dataset. GSE24759 samples were used in the training matrix to measure 13,059 mRNA expressions. We performed data normalization and applied OCLR to the samples labeled as HSC. We used leave-one-out cross-validation to test our approach by excluding each HSC sample in turn. The cross-validation experiment showed that almost every excluded HSC sample had a higher score than all non-HSC samples, resulting in an overall AUC of 0.9913. We computed a Spearman correlation between the weight vector of the model and the expression profile of the new sample, normalized between 0 and 1. 21 healthy bone marrow cells from GSE49618 run as controls for 100 AML samples from TCGA with both methylation and expression data to identify epigenetically regulated genes (Table S1). We used MethylMix algorithm to identify epigenetically regulated genes that are both transcriptionally predictive and differential together with the parameters of their methylation states. These methylation-related genes were used to construct the METH-HSCsi. mRNAsi were also calculated similarly based on the model’s weight vector from a previous study1. Simultaneously, another algorithm, Support Vector Machine-Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE), with penalty parameter tuning conducted by tenfold cross-validation, was used to validate the HSCsi signature15.

The differentially expressed genes (DEGs)-based HSCsi

Nine types of hematological malignancies from eight GEO datasets were divided by the median of HSCsi independently. According to adjusted P < 0.05, DEGs were identified. All DEGs were analyzed for the GSEA analysis, and the pathways that adjusted P < 0.05 were identified as oncogenic pathways (Fig. 3C).

Evaluation of associations between HSCsi and gene mutations in CCLE

Gene mutations and mRNA expression data of 172 hematopoietic and lymphoid tissue cell lines were downloaded from the CCLE. We considered any mutated genes in greater than 10 out of 172 cell lines. There are four types of mutations present in the 172 hematopoietic and lymphoid tissue cell lines: SNP (single nucleotide polymorphism), INS (insertion), DNP (duplication), and DEL (deletion). SNPs were considered to represent the wild-type sequence, while the other three types were classified as mutations. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to determine a significant difference in HSCsi between each gene-mutated and wild cases (Table S5, Fig. 3D).

Meta-analysis of LSC

We searched the GEO datasets and screened the LSC studies. Finally, five independent studies were selected for meta-analysis, all with available mRNA expression data, and the samples were sorted using flow cytometry. A meta-analysis was performed using RevMan 5.3 (RevMan 2014). We analyzed HSCsi as a continuous variable and calculated pooled estimates of the mean differences in HSCsi between the studies through a random-effects model. Meta-analyses were conducted using forest plots, and effect sizes are expressed as weighted mean differences with 95% confidence intervals. An I2 statistic was used to quantify study heterogeneity between trials for all analyses. A two-sided P > 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Evaluation of the associations between stemness index and clinical outcomes in hematological malignancies

We regarded the HSCsi as a single continuous covariate. The survival difference between high HSCsi and low HSCsi patients was compared using Kaplan–Meier (K-M) survival plots. Multivariate survival analysis was performed using Cox’s proportional hazard model. In AML, data from the same GPL platform in GSE12417 and GSE37642 datasets were background-corrected, log2-transformed, and batch-corrected through COMBAT for further analysis.

Identification of potential compounds targeting the hematological stemness index

To find potential compounds that could target pathways related to HSCsi, we used the CMap (Connectivity map), a resource that connects diseases, genes, and therapeutics based on cellular responses to perturbation16. The DEGs between samples in nine types of hematological malignancies with high HSCsi and low HSCsi were calculated using the ‘lmFit’ function of the R package ‘limma’ with default parameters17. The DEG top 300 genes (150 upregulated and 150 downregulated) were selected to query the Touchstone in the CMap database. As recommended by the database, compounds with an absolute value of the CMap connectivity score (tau) ≥ 90 were recorded as hypothetical therapeutic agents for hematological malignancies. Data and tools for CMap can be found at https://clue.io.

Clinically actionable genes and drug response analysis

From the GDSC (Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer), we downloaded the drug sensitivity AUC, half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50), and gene expression profiles of hematopoietic and lymphoid cell lines to assess drug response in cancer cell lines. We calculated Spearman’s rank correlations between gene expression, the AUC, and IC50 from the GDSC and used FDR < 0.05 for statistical significance.

We downloaded the potential therapeutic drugs for hematological malignancies. The FDA Drug Approvals and Databases website provided us with their prescription information (https://www.fda.gov/Drugs/InformationOnDrugs/). From Drugbank, Our team compiled a list of clinically actionable genes that were identified as targets for FDA-approved therapeutic drugs or biomarkers. For further analysis, we analyzed clinically actionable genes and their corresponding therapeutic targets.

We predicted the chemotherapeutic response for each sample from GSE12417 and GSE37642 on the GPL570 platform based on GDSC. To perform the prediction process, we applied the R package “pRRophetic”, which allows us to estimate the IC50 of the sample by using ridge regression, a technique that reduces the variance of the model and prevents overfitting. Moreover, we evaluated the prediction accuracy by using tenfold cross-validation, a method that splits the data into 10 subsets and uses one subset as the test set and the others as the training set. We repeated this process 10 times and averaged the results. We based our cross-validation on the GDSC training set, which contains drug sensitivity data for various cell lines. We selected the default parameters for the “blood” tissue type, which were optimized by the developers of the package18.

The Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm and subclass mapping

Responder conditions from TIDE and the Subclass Mapping algorithm from GenePattern were used to evaluate the immune checkpoint inhibitors treatment response between the HSCsi-high and HSCsi-low subgroups. Two direction median centered and log2-transformed RNA expression data were utilized for the aforementioned analyses19,20.

Assessment of tumor immune microenvironment

The proportion of immune cells in the AML bone marrow microenvironment was estimated using marker genes expressions-based intratumoral immune cells and ssGSEA, and the results are expressed as enrichment scores21. Estimation of Stromal and Immune cells in Malignant Tumors using Expression data (ESTIMATE) was used to calculate the stromal score, immune score, and ESTIMATE score of the sample to quantify the overall level of bone marrow immune components22.

Statistical analysis

The results are expressed as mean ± SEM. We conducted a statistical analysis using R version 4.2.1. We used the Shapiro–Wilk test to test the data for normality. We used a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test or a one-way ANOVA to assess normally distributed data when there were more than two experimental groups. We used the Mann–Whitney U-test for non-parametric assessment. We established a threshold level of significance α at P < 0.05.

Packages in R

The R packages used in this study are as follows: gelnet (v 1.2.1), ggplot2 (v 3.4.1), cowplot, venndiagram (v 1.7.3), GuangchuangYu/gglayer (v 0.0.4), ggsci (v 2.9), fgsea (v 1.24.0), dplyr (v 1.0.10), clusterProfiler (v 4.6.1), enrichplot (v 1.18.3), limma (v3.54.2), ggpubr (v 0.6.0), forestplot (v 3.1.1), pROC (v 1.18.0), survival (v 3.5.3), survminer (v 0.4.9), sva (v 3.20.0), crosslink (v 0.1.0), pheatmap (v 1.0.12), ComplexHeatmap (v 1.10.2), ggstatsplot (v 0.11.0), circlize (v 0.4.11), pRRophetic (v 0.5.0), GSVA (1.46.0), GSEABase (1.60.0), MethylMix (v 2.0) and WGCNA (v 1.72.1).

Results

mRNA expression-based hematopoietic stemness index

We analyzed hematological malignancies datasets from GEO for which transcriptomic profiles were available. First, a cluster analysis of mRNA expression profiles from GSE24759 was performed, and one outlier HSC sample was removed before training (Figure S1A). We then derived hematopoietic stemness indices using an OCLR algorithm trained on 38 distinct hematopoietic cell states. Using tumor-related epigenetically regulated genes, methylation regulatory HSCsi were generated (Figure S1B and Table S1). HSCsi and METH-HSCsi were calculated using OCLR-based RNA signatures applied to GEO datasets. Hematopoietic stemness indexes range from zero (low) to one (high) (Table S2), and the HSCsi signature was validated against SVM-RFE (Figure S1C). We validated the HSCsi and METH-HSCsi by applying them to datasets composed of Hematopoietic cells at different differentiation stages (GSE63270, GSE98791, and GSE24759). A high level of HSCsi was observed in nearly all hematopoietic stem cells and correlated inversely with differentiation stages (Fig. 2A top). As shown in the table, there are significant statistical differences among the three groups of cell types in terms of differentiation (P < 0.05, Fig. 2A bottom). To evaluate the efficacy of HSCsi to stratify HSC, we applied receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to compare the classifiers based on HSCsi, METH-HSCsi, and mRNAsi. The AUC of HSCsi was significantly higher than that of mRNAsi in the GSE98791 and GSE24759 datasets (Figure S1D, n = 87, P = 7.85E-6). We chose HSCsi because it was developed from HSCs, resembling their molecular characteristics. After batch correction, the HSCsi of hematological malignancy samples (GSE24080, GSE26049, GSE34171, GSE51082, GSE58445, and GSE9006) and hematopoietic cell lines (CCLE) were calculated. Hematologic malignancies display various degrees of stemness as revealed using HSCsi and METH-HSCsi (Fig. 2B and 2C). AML, acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and chronic myelocytic leukemia (CML) that are originated from HSCs or their precursor cells, show increased HSCsi compared with PBMC significantly. By contrast, CLL that progresses through the accumulation of mature lymphocytes had lower HSCsi (P < 0.001). we employed the weights of each gene in HSCsi model that listed in Table S1 as the criteria for ranking in the GSEA. Nine gene sets related to HSCs, LSCs, and embryonic stem cells (ESCs) in previous studies (Table S3), were compared with the HSCsi signature8,23–29. Across these sets, 1,075 genes were unique, with no more than 35 genes overlapping. There was a significant enrichment of these gene sets in HSCsi in all cases. We used 50 hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB) and performed functional annotation analysis on the HSCsi signature. In our study, MYC targets showed a significant positive signature enrichment, consistent with the mRNAsi signature in “Cancer Hallmark”1. A significant increase in NF-κB and interferon-gamma response signaling pathway activity was negatively correlated with stemness. (Fig. 3A and Table S3). We focused on the correlation of HSCsi with 30 core stem cell factors in at least 2 out of the 6 aforementioned HSC or LSC gene sets (Table S3). SEPHS1, ERG, and MLLT3 were significantly positively correlated with HSCsi (Fig. 3B and Table S3). The DEGs based on HSCsi in hematological malignancies was further applied for GSEA analysis of oncogenic signatures from MSigDB. We confirmed the engagement of KRAS, P53, ALK, ERBB2, TBK1, and SHH in stemness for hematological malignancies (Fig. 3C and Table S4). Finally, to further elucidate the association between HSCsi and gene mutations, we analyzed gene expression and mutation data in hematopoietic and lymphoid cell lines from the CCLE. ITGA7, SPHK2, and DNMT1 mutations that play an essential role in stem cells30–32 were positively correlated with the HSCsi (Fig. 3D, Table S5). All associated mutant genes were analyzed for Gene Ontology (GO). The top 15 GO categories and enriched 19 genes are shown in Figure S1E, with adenyl nucleotide binding being the most significantly enriched.

Stemness index can stratify CSCs in hematological malignancies

It is common for HSCs to express CD34 on their cell surface and lack CD38. ‘HSC-like’ cells are generally accepted as one type of LSCs. A meta-analysis of 5 LSC datasets, including 462 AML samples, revealed significant differences in HSCsi between CD34 + and CD34- cell populations sorted using flow cytometry (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, the Paired Student Test revealed significant differences in the HSCsi between xenograft validated LSC + and LSC- from 16 samples (GSE30377). The similar result was consistent in another dataset, including functionally validated 138 LSC + and 89 LSC– cell fractions were sorted from 83 primary AML patient samples (GSE76008). Although these studies have reported that the majority immunophenotype of LSC + cell fractions was CD34 + CD38-, a minority of CD34- cell fractions was proven as LSC + phenotype. There was a statistical difference in the HSCsi between LSC + and LSC- phenotype in CD34- subgroup (Fig. 4B, Table S2). A meta-analysis also showed significant differences in mRNAsi between CD34 + and CD34- cell populations sorted using flow cytometry, but there was no significance between xenograft validated LSC + and LSC- populations (Figures S2A and S2B). To evaluate the efficacy of HSCsi in stratifying LSCs, we applied ROC curves to compare the classifiers based on HSCsi and CD34 + CD38- phenotype. The AUC of HSCsi was significantly higher than CD34 + CD38- in the GSE76008 dataset (n = 227, P < 0.001). However, as evident from Veen plots, the two classifiers had an advantage in identifying the functionally validated LSC cells (Fig. 4C). Both classifiers were entered into multivariate logistic regression models to train and predict LSC. The combined classifier performed well consistently in the training (GSE76008) and test (GSE30377) groups. In the test group, considering 0.809 as the threshold value, 44 out of 54 samples were correctly identified, indicating the advantages and efficiencies of the combined classifier (Fig. 4D, Table S6). The HSCsi and mRNAsi revealed differences within the different types of HSCs. Short-term HSCs (ST-HSCs) exhibited higher stemness indices than long-term HSCs (LT-HSCs) in healthy donors (P < 0.001, Figure S2D). However, in AML, both the stemness indices revealed no difference between LT-HSCs and ST-HSCs. Notably, both the stemness indices in ST-HSCs from AML were lower than those from healthy donors, but there was no difference in LT-HSCs (not shown). In multiple myeloma (MM), CD138- populations exhibited stem cell properties and greater clonogenic potential in vitro and in vivo than CD138 + plasma cells, demonstrating higher stemness indices than CD138 + populations from human myeloma cell lines RPMI-8226 and NCI-H929 (P < 0.001, Figure S2C). Similar to the difference observed within HSC, populations in MM with low expression of CD24 and short-term stemness revealed higher stemness indices than those with high expression of CD24 and long-term stemness (P < 0.001, Figure S2E).

Correlation of HSCsi with clinical characteristics in hematological malignancies

We examined AML, MM, Peripheral T Cell Lymphoma (PTCL), and Diffuse Large B-Cell Lymphoma (DLBCL) to study the correlation of HSCsi with clinical characteristics. In AML, we found a strong association between the HSCsi with the French-American-British (FAB) classification, which is correlated with the stage of myeloid differentiation of the AML. Moreover, the survival analysis from two datasets indicated that HSCsi was reversely correlated with the overall survival of patients (Fig. 5A). Multivariate Cox survival analysis showed HSCsi and METH-HSCsi were prognostic factors independent of age and FAB (Table S2). In MM, the patients with elevated levels of HSCsi had significantly shorter overall and event-free survival times (Fig. 5B). The further multivariate Cox survival analysis of GSE24080 showed HSCsi was a prognostic factor independent of age and β-2 microglobulin, while METH-HSCsi was not significant (Table S2). In PTCL, the Adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATLL) subtype exhibited a higher level of HSCsi than Angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma (ATIL), extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma (ENKT), and ALCL (Anaplastic Large cell lymphoma) subtype. The high-level HSCsi was also associated with poor prognosis in PTCL (Fig. 5C). Multivariate Cox survival analysis showed HSCsi and age were independent prognostic factors in PTCL, while METH-HSCsi was not significant (Table S2). In DLBCL, the univariate and multivariate analysis of survival demonstrated that the prognostic power of HSCsi was independent of IPI clinical stage score and COO histological subtype (Fig. 5D).

HSCsi-associated molecular features on drug response across hematological malignancies

Assessment of the potential drug response to HSCsi-associated features, we focused on 2,549 differently expressed genes based on HSCsi in at least 7 out of 9 types of hematological malignancies. We used Spearman’s rank correlation to measure the relationship between the expression levels of these genes and the AUC values of 117 anti-cancer drugs from the GDSC database across 108 cell lines of hematopoietic and lymphoid origin. We focused on 171 HSCsi-associated genes significantly correlated with the sensitivity of at least 4 anti-cancer drugs (|rs|> 0.35, FDR < 0.05; Fig. 6A, Table S7). These anti-cancer drugs mainly targeted multiple biological processes, including DNA replication, Chromatin signature, phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase/mechanistic target of rapamycin (PI3K/mTOR) signaling, and receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) signaling pathways. For instance, the TPO2B gene was upregulated in high-score HSCsi samples in seven cancers; its mRNA expression is linked to drug sensitivity to 17 anti-cancer drugs (for instance, Ara-G, rs =—0.47, FDR < 1.35 × 10–03). Dysregulation of the PI3K/mTOR signaling pathway is an established feature in multiple cancer types, and PI3K/mTOR signaling is one of the major signaling pathways in the maintenance of cancer stemness33. We found a strong correlation between 16 drugs that target the genes in the PI3K/mTOR signaling pathway and the genes associated with HSCsi (Figure S3A). To delve into the potential drugs, we calculated Spearman’s rank correlations between HSCsi, AUCs, and IC50 values for the mentioned 117 drugs from the CCLE. The sensitivity of 30 anti-cancer drugs significantly correlated with HSCsi in AUCs and IC50 values (P < 0.05; Figure S3B). Eleven of these drugs had more genes (> 17) associated with them (Fig. 6B, Table S7). Our results highlighted the potential of combining other cancer therapies with anti-dedifferentiation drugs through extensive interactions across hematological malignancies cell lines between molecular features associated with HSCsi and drug response.

Therapeutic potential and clinical relevance of HSCsi-related genes

We analyzed the mRNA expression changes of 122 genes that are clinically relevant and targeted by 87 FDA-approved drugs for blood cancers in samples with high or low HSCsi. These drugs include immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy (Fig. 7). For instance, TOP2B was overexpressed in HSCsi-high samples in six tumor types (AML, fold change = 1.47, FDR = 2.77 × 10–8). The expression of all 122 clinically actionable genes was biased in at least 1 cancer type. Combining CSC-targeted therapy with other treatments may help patients with hematological malignancies who have high HSCsi. Notably, several immunotherapeutic targets were also affected by HSCsi. High expression of CD47 was observed in HSCsi-high samples in AML, indicating that CD47 inhibitors, such as magrolimab and lemzoparlimab, might have enhanced efficacy in hematologic malignancies with high HSCsi. To further explore the effects of HSCsi on the response to different drugs in patient samples, we performed an analysis using the GDSC database, which contains drug sensitivity data for various cancer cell lines. A thorough examination of the HSCsi and its association with drug response enables the detection of genes with clinical significance. We detected variations in drug responses for 9 FDA-approved tumor-targeted drugs in the GDSC database among hematological tumor cell lines. HSCsi was positively correlated (drug-resistant) with temsirolimus response in CCLE hematological tumor cell lines (|rs|= 0.25, P = 0.008), confirming the reported resistance to mTORC1 inhibition in hematological malignancies34. HSCsi was negatively correlated (drug-sensitive) with the venetoclax response (|rs|= 0.25, P = 0.008), matching the reported sensitivity to BCL-2 inhibition in LSCs35. These findings confirmed our analysis and offered clinically meaningful insights.

CMap analysis identifies potential stemness-targeting compounds

In order to search for compounds that might target pathways linked to stemness, we relied on CMap, a data-driven, systematic approach to discovering genetic, chemical, and biological condition16. At least three hematological malignancies were enriched for compounds associated with stemness. (Fig. 8A, Table S8). It was found that four compounds were significantly enriched in more than six hematological malignancies and that they inhibited tumorigenicity related to hematological stemness: Doxorubicin36, phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate37, the JAK3 inhibitor38, and Camptothecin39.

The CMap Mode-of-action (MOA) analysis of the 61 compounds (other than Valproic-acid, RHO kinase inhibitor III, and rilmenidine) revealed 65 mechanisms of action shared by the aforementioned compounds (Fig. 8B, Table S8). Nine compounds (bufalin, cinobufagin, digitoxigenin, digitoxin, digoxin, ouabain, proscillaridin, sarmentogenin, and strophanthidin) shared the MOA of ATPase inhibitor. Amsacrine, camptothecin, celastrol, doxorubicin, idarubicin, SN-38, and topotecan shared MoA as Topoisomerase inhibitors. Auranofin, celastrol, Manumycin-a, and IKK-2-inhibitor-V shared MoA as NF-κB inhibitors. Our analysis was consistent with previous studies. ATPase inhibitors, such as strophanthidin, digoxin, and ouabain, are effective against primary patient CD34 + AML cells40. NF-κB pathway inhibitors, such as auranofin and celastrol, induced apoptosis and proliferative inhibition of CD138 + cells in multiple myeloma41,42. Because of the CMap Target analysis, the aforementioned compounds share 90 distinct drug-target genes (Figure S4, Table S8). Eleven genes or gene families are targets of at least three different compounds, namely ATP1A1 (9 drugs), CDK2 (5 drugs), IKBKB (4), KDR (5), ATP1A2/A3/A4/B1/B2/B3/B4 (3), CDK4 (3), FXYD2 (3), GSK3B (3), HDAC1 (3), TOP1 (3), TOP2A (3). In this study, selective therapies were found to target hematological CSCs for certain types of cancer based on the similarities in the mechanisms of action among different compounds.

Prediction of the sensitivity of drug therapy for HSCsi subtypes in AML

To further understand the correlation between HSCsi and drug sensitivity in AML, we assessed the response of HSCsi-high and HSCsi-low subtypes to 10 common chemo/target drugs. Therefore, we used ridge regression to train the predictive model on the GDSC cell lines dataset and assessed the predictive accuracy by tenfold cross-validation. We estimated the IC50 for each sample in 2 AML datasets (GSE12417 and GSE37642, GPL570 platform) based on the predictive model of these 10 drugs. We observed a significant difference in the estimated IC50 between HSCsi-high and HSCsi-low for these 10 drugs. The HSCsi-high group was more sensitive to Ara-C, Bcl-2 inhibitor, Flt3 inhibitor, and HDAC inhibitor (Fig. 9A, Table S9).

Differential putative chemotherapeutic and immunotherapeutic response. (A) The box plots of the estimated IC50 for chemo and target drugs are shown as HSCsi-high and HSCsi-low. (B) TIDE algorithm demonstrated that HSCsi-low may be more likely to respond to immunotherapy than HSCsi-high (P < 0.001). (C) Submap analysis manifested that HSCsi-low could be more sensitive to the programmed cell death protein 1 inhibitor (Bonferroni-corrected P = .005).

Although immune checkpoint inhibitors are not yet approved as routine treatments for AML, many clinical trials use them alone or in combination with chemotherapy, revealing increased response rates43. We used the TIDE algorithm as a prediction tool and showed that immunotherapy was more likely to be effective for HSCsi-low (49%, 53/109) than for HSCsi-high (19%, 21/110, P < 0.001)19. Besides TIDE prediction, we compared the expression profiles of HSCsi-low and HSCsi-high subtypes through subclass mapping. Another published dataset with 47 immunotherapy-responding melanoma patients was used as a baseline20. The anti-PD-1 therapy revealed a greater response (Bonferroni corrected P = 0.005) with HSCsi-low compared to HSCsi-high (Fig. 9B and Fig. 9C, Table S9).

Association of HSCsi and immune cell populations in hematological malignancies

Given the critical role of LSCs and the immunologic microenvironment, we explored HSCsi with 148 genes from 5 immune pathways, including chemokine, receptor, MHC, immunoinhibitory, and immunostimulator. We distinguished 101 immune-associated genes significantly correlated with HSCsi at least 3 hematological malignancies (|rs|> 0.30, P < 0.05; Fig. 10A). Most of those genes (95/101) were significantly negatively correlated with HSCsi, including immunoinhibitor and immunostimulator, which indicated that immune cell populations might play a dominant role in correlation with HSCsi.

The lower panel shows the annotations of HSCsi, tumor purity, ESTIMATE score, immune score and stromal score. The clustering method used Euclidean distance and Ward linkage for hierarchical analysis. The analysis identified three different clusters of immune infiltration, which were designated as high immunity, median immunity and low immunity. To further elucidate the association between HSCsi and immune cell populations, we calculated the relative abundance of immune cells by ssGSEA based on expression profiling data from 2 AML datasets (GSE12417 and GSE37642; GPL570 platform). A total of 219 AML patients were included in the present study, for whom transcriptome profiling data and clinical characteristics were available. Based on this analysis, we found a significant correlation between the immune cells and the HSCsi. This correlation is consistent with publications reporting lymphocyte subpopulations of bone marrow microenvironment in AML. For further characterization, we performed unsupervised clustering to divide the cohort into three subgroups based on infiltration levels: high immunity (H, n = 75), median immunity (M, n = 89), and low immunity (L, n = 55). Furthermore, ESTIMATE was applied to evaluate the clustering efficiency, and patients with high immunity exhibited the highest ESTIMATE score, immune score, and lowest HSCsi. For taking into account the concurrent components of immune cell types that have activating or suppressive effects, We examined the correlation between higher immune cell counts and elevated levels of cytotoxicity. HLA expression and Cytolytic Activity (CYT) scores were highest in the patients with high immunity status, indicating the efficient cytotoxic function (Figs. 10B and S5A). We used WGCNA, a method for building gene co-expression networks and finding meaningful modules related to specific phenotypes, to further explore the genes associated with HSCsi. The HSCsi, tumor purity, stromal score, immune score, and ESTIMATE score were used to define the phenotypes. The DEGs between the HSCsi-high and HSCsi-low groups in AML datasets were identified with cut-off criteria of |logFC|> 1.5 and FDR < 0.05, and these DEGs were used to build a scale-free system. According to the parameters (soft-thresholding power β = 8, R2 = 0.94, minimum module size = 30, and the threshold for merging of modules = 0.25), a total of 6 modules (Merged dynamic) were obtained (Figs. 10C and S5B, Table S10). The module blue, with 439 DEGs, contained the greatest number of genes, followed by the module turquoise, which featured 316, respectively. There was a significant correlation between the blue module and the immune score (r = 0.83) as well as between the blue module and HSCsi (r = -0.79). However, the turquoise module significantly correlated with HSCsi (r = 0.88). We applied the cut-off criteria (|MM|> 0.8, GS > 0.1, Weight > 0.1) to identify 55 genes in the blue module and 44 genes in the turquoise module as hub genes, which had high connectivity in the clinically significant module. We then generated the interaction network for each module using the core genes (Figure S5C). The top 10 hub genes, with the highest connectivity in blue and turquoise modules, were analyzed for the interaction with stemness and immunity. The genes in the blue module were immune-related, positively correlated with ESTIMATE score and higher expression in the high immunity subgroup, but negatively strong correlation with HSCsi and lower expression in mild and low immunity subgroups (|r|> 0.5, P < 0.001). Those genes in the turquoise module were stemness-related and only showed a positively strong correlation with HSCsi. The mRNAsi of 143 AML patients from TCGA datasets showed the same correlation with the top 10 hub genes expression in the blue and turquoise modules as HSCsi did (Figs. 10D and S5D).

Conclusion

The present study was based on an integrated analysis of stemness in 2,200 primary human hematological tumors of 11 different types. We utilized a machine learning algorithm on transcriptomic data from HSCs to create a molecular measure of stemness. Subsequently, we used this metric to evaluate the transcriptomic characteristics of tumors based on their level of oncogenic stemness. Notably, this feature demonstrated exceptional ability in distinguishing LSCs from other tumor cells and was strongly associated with clinical outcomes of hematologic malignancies. The analysis provides an insight into potential compounds, clinical anti-cancer drugs, novel pathways, and actionable targets for possible CSC-targeted therapy of hematologic malignancies. Ultimately, we showed the complex interactions between leukemia and the bone marrow microenvironment through ssGSEA and WGCNA.

The application of stemness index in the clinic is extensive, contributing not only to fundamental research but also playing a significant role in the diagnosis of cancer, prognostic assessment, therapeutic guidance, and drug development. The stemness index is utilized to evaluate the similarity between tumor cells and stem cells, reflecting the self-renewal capacity and differentiation potential of tumor cells. This assessment aids in the identification of CSCs, which play a pivotal role in the initiation, progression, metastasis, and drug resistance of tumors. By calculating the stemness index, researchers can predict the prognosis of cancer patients. For instance, in prostate cancer (PRAD), a high stemness index is correlated with a poor prognosis44. Similarly, in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the stemness index is also used to predict patient prognosis and response to immunotherapy45. The application of the stemness index extends beyond basic research to clinical treatment. For example, in glioblastoma (GBM), a new classification based on the stemness index helps to screen subpopulations that are more sensitive to immunotherapy responses, thereby guiding clinical treatment46. The stemness index is closely related to the interaction with the tumor microenvironment (TME). Studies have shown that the stemness characteristics of CSCs can affect the formation and remodeling of the TME, thereby influencing tumor growth and metastasis. For example, in prostate cancer, the relationship between the stemness index and tumor immune infiltration has been systematically assessed, revealing the complex link between stemness characteristics and immune cell infiltration44. By analyzing the stemness index, researchers can identify factors associated with the invasiveness of malignant tumors and explore the application of these factors in drug development. For instance, in hepatocellular carcinoma, the stemness index is used to screen for long non-coding RNAs associated with stemness and to construct a prognostic risk model based on these molecules47. Compared to traditional CSC research methods such as flow cytometry, the transcriptome-based stemness index provides a more convenient analytical tool for the study of tumor stemness. With the continuous development of single-cell technology and the optimization and improvement of machine learning and other computational methods, the quantification method of stemness will be further enhanced in the future.

Hematologic malignancies are typically polyclonal and heterogeneous diseases, exhibiting significant variations between different hematologic tumor types and even between different subtypes of the same tumor. However, the vast majority of hematologic malignancies originate from HSCs or HPCs. There are firm linkages between CSCs and HSCs in hematologic malignancies. By applying OCLR to healthy HSCs, we developed a stemness index for HSC, which could help us better understand the development of the healthy hematopoietic system and the occurrence and progression of hematologic malignancies. Tathiane et al. first used OCLR to publish molecular profiles of normal cell types with different degrees of stemness and develop mRNAsi1. HSCsi better represents the characteristics of HSCs, as demonstrated using ROC curves in the identification of HSCs and LSCs. There are several stemness scores, based on genes differentially expressed between LSCs and non-LSCs, that can be used to predict clinical outcomes in AML, with the LSC-17 score being the most well-represented48. In MM, there was an association between those gene sets and clonogenic growth, Bortezomib resistance, poor clinical outcomes48. Previous studies have been restricted to a small range of tumor types and limited selection of genes.

HSCs are the first type of adult stem cells extensively studied and successfully applied to treat and cure patients. Based on a profound knowledge of the normal hematopoiesis process, LSCs are the earliest recognized cancer stem cells, which also serve as the foundation for the CSC theory model. The deep research in this field facilitates using the CSC model in other blood cancers and solid tumors. The HSCsi in the present study exhibited a consistent discrimination of cell differentiation degree that matched clinical features across and within different tumor types. HSCsi can also effectively distinguish LSCs. Researchers worldwide have conducted extensive research on identifying and targeting LSCs, but conclusive data haven’t been provided49–52. In the present study, the HSCsi cloud measures HSC function, revealing a high correlation with the stem cell characteristics of normal HSCs and an excellent ability to identify LSCs enriched in isolated acute leukemia cell populations. HSCsi significantly outperforms the classification using CD34 + CD38- and mRNAsi alone, providing another perspective from transcriptomic data for identifying LSCs beyond cell surface molecular markers. Moreover, HSCsi can predict the long-term survival rate of patients with AML, MM, PTCL, and DLBCL and has high clinical application value. However, both HSCsi and mRNAsi tend to characterize the stemness features of ST-HSCs in an activated state rather than LT-HSCs in a resting state. This difference may be because neither HSCsi nor mRNAsi used a more refined classification of stem cells when performing OCLR modeling, resulting in the heterogeneity within the stem cell population that led to this outcome. In AML, the stemness of LT-HSCs and ST-HSCs was closer to that of normal donors’ LT-HSCs, suggesting that AML’s LSCs tend to be in a resting state, and this result is consistent with that of previous research53. With the development of technical means such as single-cell sequencing to better understand HSC subtypes, establishing gene signatures based on LT-HSCs may reveal more profoundly the function and clinical significance of hematopoietic system tumor stem cells.

We found that HSCsi was associated with the following characteristics: The perturbation of specific mRNA transcription networks, the deregulation of signaling pathways, and the mutation of genes. Cancer stemness is associated with the core expression of SEPHS1, ERG, MLLT3, and other genes involved in normal and malignant hematopoietic stem-cell homeostasis and self-renewal54–56. Several signaling pathways crucial for stem cell homeostasis and differentiation are involved in HSCsi, such as the classical MYC, TNF-α, and IFN-γ. MYC can promote adult hematopoietic stem cell and megakaryocyte development57, whereas TNF-α and IFN-γ induce HSC differentiation and decrease the HSC pool size58,59. Our analysis revealed that both HSCsi-associated gene mutations and molecular signatures were implicated in ATP energy and amino acid metabolism. Notably, further analysis of these molecular features indicated that the PI3K/mTOR signaling pathway was the most prominent one. The relationship between PI3K/mTOR and stemness is still a contentious issue, with several studies demonstrating that PI3K/mTOR is essential for stemness. Under physiological conditions, the PI3K/mTOR pathway modulates HSC self-renewal by influencing cell metabolism, oxidative stress, and telomerase activity. In tumors, PI3K/mTOR can enhance tumor stem cell self-renewal and suppress differentiation via pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin, Notch, and autophagy60–63. However, according to the current clinical trial results, PI3K/mTOR single-target inhibitors only exert limited effects in a few specific tumors and are hard to maintain. Moreover, the currently clinically approved mTOR inhibitors often compromise treatment by activating the PI3K pathway because negative feedback mechanisms result in resistance64–66. This result is also consistent with our drug response analysis using data from GDSC. Our findings indicate that the PI3K/mTOR pathway plays a vital role in sustaining tumor stemness and is a key anti-tumor drug target, but to overcome resistance, multi-target inhibition or combination with other pathway inhibitors, such as BCL-2, should be considered67. Additionally, because of its crucial role in normal hematopoietic stem cells, one should also be wary of the toxic side effects caused by combined medication.

Remarkably, each of the 122 clinically actionable genes was biased in one or more of the nine hematological malignancies. These genes are targeted by FDA-approved cancer drugs, including immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy drugs68. The clinically actionable genes exhibit different trends among these three types of therapies. In immunotherapy, almost all clinically actionable genes were biased towards HSCsi-low samples, indicating that those patients could benefit from immunotherapy, as proven through the TIDE algorithm and Subclass Mapping in AML. In AML, immune checkpoint inhibitors show no benefit in combination with cytotoxic chemotherapy and hypomethylating agents69,70. HSCsi could help identify patients who respond better to PD-1 and other immunotherapies, which has significant implications for patient selection. In chemotherapy, many genes are biased towards HSCsi-high samples, among which many therapeutic drugs have been widely used clinically and have shown good efficacy in hematological malignancies, such as Anthracyclines, Anti-microtube, and Hypomethylation. Anthracyclines are a class of Topoisomerase inhibitors that are widely used in the treatment of hematological malignancies. CMap analysis also confirmed that Doxorubicin could effectively inhibit tumor stem cells. By contrast, the HSCsi-high group exhibited higher estimated IC50 values for Doxorubicin in AML. We speculate that this may be due to the CMap analysis tends to characterize the common transcriptomic response under the gradient concentrations (six different concentration gradients were set for Doxorubicin in nine cell lines, ranging from 40 nM to 10 uM, covering from very low concentration to IC50, and then to LD100). The latest research report also confirmed this point: standard-dose chemotherapy can effectively eliminate most leukemia cells, but it induces the expansion of a minority of leukemia stem cells. By contrast, low-dose doxorubicin can overcome anti-cancer therapy resistance in leukemia stem cells by inhibiting the synergistic activation of PTEN-PI3K-Akt and Wnt-β-catenin pathways36. In target therapy, besides the genes targeted by PI3K/mTOR inhibitors, those targeted by HDAC and PARP inhibitors also showed consistent overexpression in the HSCsi-high group. The two inhibitors have also been reported to effectively eliminate cancer stem cells in hematological malignancies36,71. In summary, our study could help us to distinguish the sensitivity of patients to various treatment regimens, make rational choices, and discover new CSC-targeted drugs and new regimens.

Increasing evidence suggests that CSCs and immune cells interact in hematological malignancies72. However, less is known about the factors that influence the nature of this crosstalk. We explored the relationship between stemness index and bone marrow immune microenvironment in AML. By applying WGCNA, we identified genes that were directly positively correlated with the stemness index and genes that were indirectly negatively correlated with the stemness index by affecting immunity. These genes could serve as targets of LSC therapy and immunotherapy. In the high stemness index group, both the number and activity of immune cells were significantly suppressed; a similar conclusion is confirmed in solid tumors73. LSCs can inhibit anti-tumor immune effects through multiple pathways74,75, suggesting that LSC-targeted therapy should be applied in the early stage of treatments and prioritized over anti-tumor immunotherapy.

The advantage of our developed HSCsi is that it is trained from the molecular profiles of HSCs, which are the most extensively studied and widely used in clinical applications in stem cell research. It reflects the tumor’s pathology and clinical outcomes and has clinical potential. The stem cell index of hematologic malignancies can provide a more refined stratification, thus a basis for predicting prognosis and selecting treatments. Based on this index, we also developed an objective analysis tool for LSCs and explored the genes, pathways, and drugs that may target hematopoietic CSCs. Moreover, it revealed the clinically available CSC-targeted therapy and guided the selection of regimens for hematologic malignancies.
