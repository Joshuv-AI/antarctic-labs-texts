Prenatal statin exposure and adverse birth outcomes in Asian women: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shuya Ye, Fan Wu, Dongli Sun, Xiao Zhang, Li Wang, Meng Zhang, Caihong Zheng, Yue Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe safety of statin use during pregnancy remains controversial. We aimed to evaluate the association between prenatal statin exposure and pregnancy outcomes in Asian women, including low birth weight, preterm birth, and congenital anomalies.MethodsWe conducted a PRISMA-compliant systematic review and meta-analysis of studies in Asian populations evaluating prenatal statin exposure. PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and trial registries were searched through January 31, 2026. Eligible randomized and observational studies included Asian populations and reported low birth weight, preterm birth, or congenital anomalies. Adjusted risk ratios were pooled using random-effects inverse-variance models, and risk of bias was assessed with standard tools.ResultsThree nationwide observational cohort studies from Taiwan and South Korea were included in the quantitative synthesis, and one Indonesian randomized trial of pravastatin for preeclampsia prevention was reviewed qualitatively. Prenatal statin exposure was not associated with congenital anomalies (RR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.89–1.08; I2 = 0%), but was associated with low birth weight (RR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.07–1.49; I2 = 49%). The association with preterm birth was not statistically significant (RR, 1.24; 95% CI, 0.95–1.62; I2 = 87%). Sensitivity analyses restricted to exposed versus unexposed comparisons yielded similar conclusions.ConclusionsThe available Asian evidence, largely derived from nationwide East Asian cohorts, suggests no clear association between prenatal statin exposure or continuation and congenital anomalies. A modest association with low birth weight was observed, whereas evidence for preterm birth remained inconclusive. The low-birth-weight finding should be interpreted cautiously because of residual confounding, comparator heterogeneity, prematurity-related low birth weight, and pharmacological heterogeneity across statins.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO CRD420251235975Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07379-0.

Introduction

Placenta-mediated disorders constitute a major biological pathway linking maternal cardiometabolic dysfunction to adverse perinatal outcomes, including fetal growth restriction and preterm birth [1]. Dysregulated lipid metabolism, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammatory activation during pregnancy can impair placental vascular development and nutrient transport, thereby constraining fetal growth and timing of parturition [2, 3]. Statins, through their pleiotropic effects on lipid handling, angiogenic balance, and inflammatory signaling, intersect with several of these placental pathways [4, 5]. As a result, statin exposure during pregnancy has attracted growing attention not only as an unintended pharmacologic exposure but also as a potential modifier of placental pathophysiology in high-risk pregnancies [6, 7].

Notably, the biological context in which placental adaptation occurs differs across populations. Asian women exhibit distinct cardiometabolic profiles during pregnancy [8], including differences in baseline lipid trajectories, insulin sensitivity, and pregnancy-associated vascular risk. These factors may influence placental lipid transport, angiogenic signaling, and inflammatory responses, thereby modifying susceptibility to placenta-mediated complications [9]. In parallel, ethnic variability in statin pharmacokinetics and transporter-mediated disposition has been documented [10], with pregnancy-related changes in hepatic uptake and intestinal efflux pathways [11] potentially amplifying systemic exposure in Asian populations. Together, these maternal and placental characteristics provide a strong biological rationale for population-specific evaluation of statin exposure and placenta-mediated birth outcomes in Asian women.

Previous large cohort studies of statin exposure during pregnancy have focused primarily on congenital anomalies as a principal safety endpoint and have not identified a consistent teratogenic signal [12, 13]. However, congenital anomalies reflect early embryonic development and may be less sensitive to later gestational disturbances in placental function. In contrast, outcomes such as low birth weight and preterm birth more directly capture downstream consequences of placental maladaptation, including impaired nutrient delivery, vascular insufficiency, and dysregulated inflammatory signaling. From a placental biology perspective, these outcomes may therefore provide a more informative window into the functional impact of maternal metabolic status and pharmacologic exposure during pregnancy.

Recent nationwide cohort studies from East Asia, including studies from Taiwan and South Korea, have reported data on prenatal statin exposure and birth outcomes [14–16]. These studies provide valuable evidence but differ in exposure timing, comparator, and analytical approach. These findings have not yet been systematically synthesized within a placenta-centered conceptual framework, nor interpreted in relation to randomized evidence [17] evaluating statins as targeted interventions for placenta-mediated disorders. Such integration would facilitate mechanistic interpretation and streamline clinical translation, especially for Asian populations whose maternal cardiometabolic risks and statin sensitivity may differ from Western cohorts.

Accordingly, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of the available Asian evidence to evaluate the association between prenatal statin exposure or continuation and adverse birth outcomes, including congenital anomalies, low birth weight, and preterm birth. We also summarized randomized pravastatin evidence qualitatively because of differences in study design, clinical indication, exposure intent and statin type. This review aimed to provide a cautious synthesis of the current evidence, clarify the extent to which findings are derived from predominantly East Asian nationwide cohorts, and identify key limitations related to confounding, comparator heterogeneity, and pharmacological heterogeneity across statins.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The study protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, CRD420251235975) on 25 November 2025.

Eligibility criteria

We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), non-randomized controlled clinical trials, prospective and retrospective comparative cohort studies, and case-control studies that evaluated statin exposure during pregnancy or periconceptional statin continuation. Eligible studies were required to involve women of Asian ethnicity or to be conducted in Asian populations. We excluded duplicate reports, case reports, case series, cross-sectional studies, pharmacokinetic studies in healthy adults, animal studies, reviews, expert opinions, editorials, letters to the editor and comments.

Information sources

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in five electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and ClinicalTrials. All databases were searched from inception to 31 January 2026. Reference lists of eligible articles and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify additional studies.

Search strategy

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms, including MeSH or Emtree terms where applicable, and free-text terms related to statins, pregnancy, prenatal exposure, congenital anomalies, low birth weight, preterm birth, and Asian populations. Key search terms included “statins”, “HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors”, “dyslipidemia”, “pregnancy”, “prenatal exposure”, “congenital anomalies”, “birth defects”, “low birth weight”, and “preterm birth”. Asian populations were operationalized using both geographical and ethnicity-based terms. Geographical terms included Asian countries or regions, whereas ethnicity-based terms included Asian, Asian ethnicity, East Asian, South Asian, Southeast Asian, and Asian American where applicable. During full-text screening, studies conducted outside Asia were considered eligible only when Asian-specific subgroup estimates or extractable data were reported separately.

No language restrictions were applied. The full database-specific search strategies, including Boolean operators, controlled vocabulary terms, free-text terms, search dates, filters and restrictions, are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Study selection

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility, followed by full-text review of potentially relevant studies. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. The study selection process was summarized using a PRISMA flow diagram.

Data extraction

Data were independently extracted by two reviewers using a standardized data extraction form. Extracted information included study characteristics (author, year of publication, country, study design), population size, statin exposure, comparator, timing of exposure, reported outcomes, and adjustment variables.

Kay et al. reported adjusted odds ratios after propensity score matching, whereas the other observational studies reported adjusted risk ratios. To harmonize effect measures, the adjusted odds ratios from Kay et al. were converted to approximate risk ratios using the following formula: RR = OR/[(1-P0) + P0 × OR], where P0 represents the event risk in the propensity score-matched unexposed group. The same transformation was applied to the lower and upper confidence limits [18].

Quality assessment, risk of bias, and certainty of evidence

Two reviewers independently assessed the methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies. Observational studies were evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), which assesses cohort selection, comparability, and outcome ascertainment. Scores of 7–9, 4–6, and < 4 were considered to indicate high, moderate, and low quality, respectively. Randomized controlled trials were assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool across the domains of randomization, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result. Detailed assessments are presented in Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Figure S1.

Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Risk of bias assessments were used to inform the interpretation of findings but were not applied as exclusion criteria. The certainty of evidence for each pooled outcome was assessed using the GRADE approach. Certainty was evaluated according to risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The GRADE assessment is presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Data synthesis

Effect estimates were summarized as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). When adjusted estimates were reported, these were preferentially used. Meta-analyses were performed using the inverse variance method. Adjusted risk ratios were pooled using the generic inverse-variance method on the logarithmic scale. Because clinical and methodological heterogeneity was anticipated across study populations, exposure definitions, and comparator groups, random-effects models were used for all pooled analyses. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic and the χ² test. Because fewer than 10 studies were included in each meta-analysis, publication bias was not formally assessed.

To address clinical heterogeneity in comparator definitions, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding the continuation versus discontinuation cohort and restricted the analysis to studies comparing prenatal statin exposure with no exposure or non-exposure. Additional subgroup meta-analyses by exposure timing, statin lipophilicity, statin intensity, dose, or maternal clinical factors were not performed because harmonized study-level estimates were not consistently available across the included studies. These prespecified but non-poolable questions were summarized narratively in Supplementary Table S4.

All analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan 5.3) software.

Results

Study selection

The initial database search identified 2762 records. After removal of duplicates, 1239 records were screened based on titles and abstracts. Full texts of 403 potentially relevant articles were assessed for eligibility. Ultimately, three nationwide observational cohort studies met the inclusion criteria for quantitative synthesis, and one randomized controlled trial was included for qualitative synthesis. The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Study characteristics

Three nationwide observational cohort studies were included in the quantitative synthesis. These studies were conducted in Taiwan and South Korea and were all based on population-level health insurance or administrative claims databases. The Taiwanese study [14] used multivariable Poisson regression models to adjust for maternal comorbidities and demographic factors, whereas one of the Korean studies [15] additionally applied propensity score matching to reduce confounding by indication. A third nationwide Korean cohort study [16] focused on women who had used statins before pregnancy and compared those who discontinued statins before the last menstrual period with those who continued treatment into pregnancy. This study used propensity score overlap weighting with additional inverse probability of selection weighting for selected fetal outcomes to address baseline differences between exposure groups.

In addition, one randomized controlled trial (INOVASIA) [17] conducted in Indonesia enrolled women at high risk of preeclampsia and evaluated pravastatin as an adjunctive preventive therapy in addition to standard treatment. Owing to major differences in study design, population, exposure indication, and clinical intent, the trial was included for qualitative synthesis only and was not pooled with the observational studies. Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

All three observational studies were rated as high quality using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, although residual confounding by indication and maternal disease severity could not be excluded (Supplementary Table S1). For the perinatal outcomes relevant to this review, INOVASIA trial was judged to have some concerns overall using RoB 2, mainly because allocation concealment, adherence, protocol deviations, and the prespecified analysis plan were not fully reported; missing outcome data and outcome measurement were judged to be at low risk of bias (Supplementary Figure S1).

According to the GRADE assessment, the certainty of evidence was low for congenital anomalies and low birth weight, and very low for preterm birth. The certainty for preterm birth was downgraded mainly because of substantial heterogeneity and imprecision. The full GRADE assessment is presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Synthesis of observational evidence

Low birth weight

Three observational cohort studies reported adjusted associations between prenatal statin exposure or continuation and low birth weight (< 2500 g). In the random-effects analysis, prenatal statin exposure or continuation was associated with a higher risk of low birth weight (RR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.07–1.49; I²=49%; Fig. 2). Because low birth weight may reflect prematurity, impaired fetal growth, or both, this finding was not interpreted as equivalent to small-for-gestational-age status or fetal growth restriction.

Preterm birth

Three observational cohort studies contributed data on preterm birth (< 37 weeks). In pooled analysis, the association between prenatal statin exposure or continuation and preterm birth did not reach statistical significance (RR, 1.24; 95% CI, 0.95–1.62; Fig. 3), and substantial heterogeneity remained (I2 = 87%). These findings indicate that evidence for preterm birth remained inconclusive.

Congenital anomalies

Three observational cohort studies reported adjusted estimates for congenital anomalies following prenatal statin exposure or continuation during pregnancy. Pooled analysis using an inverse-variance model showed no association between statin exposure and congenital anomalies (RR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.89–1.08; Fig. 4). No between-study heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 0%), indicating highly consistent findings across studies.

Evidence from the randomized controlled trial

One randomized controlled trial (INOVASIA), conducted among women at high risk of preeclampsia, evaluated pravastatin use during pregnancy as an adjunctive preventive therapy. The trial reported a lower rate of preterm delivery and higher birth weight in the pravastatin group, along with improved neonatal outcomes, although the reduction in preeclampsia itself did not reach statistical significance. Because the trial evaluated therapeutic hydrophilic pravastatin use in high-risk pregnancies rather than incidental or background prenatal statin exposure, it was not pooled with the observational studies and was summarized narratively.

Sensitivity analysis according to comparator definition

Because one included observational cohort used a continuation versus discontinuation design rather than a conventional exposed versus unexposed comparison, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding Cho et al. This analysis included only the two studies that compared prenatal statin exposure with no statin exposure or non-exposure among women with dyslipidemia. After exclusion of Cho et al., the pooled estimate for congenital anomalies remained non-significant (RR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.88–1.20; I²=0%; Supplementary Figure S2A). The association between statin exposure and low birth weight also remained statistically significant (RR, 1.40; 95% CI, 1.17–1.67; I²=0%; Supplementary Figure S2B). For preterm birth, the pooled estimate remained non-significant and heterogeneous (RR, 1.42; 95% CI, 0.77–2.65; I²=93%; Supplementary Figure S2C). These findings indicate that the main conclusions were not driven solely by the continuation versus discontinuation cohort, although comparator heterogeneity remains an important limitation.

Prespecified subgroup questions not amenable to meta-analysis

Several prespecified subgroup questions could not be addressed quantitatively because harmonized subgroup-specific estimates were not consistently available across the included studies. These included subgroup analyses by timing of exposure, statin lipophilicity, statin intensity, dose or duration, and maternal clinical factors such as familial hypercholesterolemia, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and hypertension. The available evidence and reasons for not performing these subgroup meta-analyses are summarized in Supplementary Table S4.

Discussion

Main findings

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of the available Asian evidence, largely derived from nationwide East Asian cohort studies, prenatal statin exposure or continuation was not clearly associated with congenital anomalies. In contrast, a modest association with low birth weight was observed, while the evidence for preterm birth remained inconclusive.

The sensitivity analysis excluding the continuation versus discontinuation cohort showed similar conclusions for congenital anomalies and low birth weight. The congenital anomaly estimate remained close to null, and the low birth weight association persisted. In contrast, the association with preterm birth remained statistically non-significant and heterogeneous. These findings suggest that the main conclusions were not driven solely by the inclusion of the continuation versus discontinuation cohort, although comparator heterogeneity remains important when interpreting the pooled estimates.

Overall, the current evidence does not suggest a clear teratogenic signal for statins in the included Asian cohorts. The more consistent signal was observed for low birth weight, but this finding should be interpreted cautiously because low birth weight is a non-specific birth outcome. It may reflect prematurity, impaired fetal growth, maternal disease burden, treatment decisions, or a combination of these factors.

Clinical implications

From a pediatric and perinatal perspective, the absence of a clear congenital anomaly signal is clinically relevant. Statin exposure during early pregnancy is often inadvertent, and concern about congenital anomalies is a common reason for patient anxiety and clinical uncertainty. The present synthesis provides some reassurance that available observational data do not show a clear increase in congenital anomalies. However, the certainty of evidence remains low, and these findings should not be interpreted as definitive proof of fetal safety for all statins, doses, and exposure windows.

A modest association with low birth weight was observed, but this finding requires cautious interpretation. A previous multicentre prospective study conducted predominantly in Western populations did not show a consistent association with low birth weight [19]. Low birth weight is not synonymous with fetal growth restriction or small-for-gestational-age status, as it may result from shortened gestation, impaired fetal growth, or both. Several included cohorts contained preterm infants, and gestational age-specific birthweight data were not consistently available. Only one included cohort reported small-for-gestational-age status, which was not significantly associated with statin exposure after adjustment [14]. Therefore, the pooled low-birth-weight signal should be interpreted as an association with an important birth outcome, but not as direct evidence that statins impair fetal growth. Residual confounding remains a major concern. Women prescribed or continuing statins during pregnancy are more likely to have dyslipidemia, diabetes, hypertension, obesity, vascular dysfunction, or other cardiometabolic risk factors. These conditions themselves may increase the risk of placental dysfunction, medically indicated preterm delivery, and low birth weight. Although the included studies used multivariable adjustment, propensity score matching, or overlap weighting, unmeasured factors such as maternal BMI, baseline lipid profile, disease severity, lifestyle, prescribing patterns, medication adherence, and treatment continuation behavior could not be fully addressed. Thus, statin exposure in observational cohorts may partly reflect underlying maternal cardiometabolic risk rather than an independent drug effect.

Ethnic differences in statin pharmacokinetics may also be relevant, as physiologically based pharmacokinetic modeling and previous pharmacological studies have suggested population-related differences in statin disposition [11, 20, 21]. However, this explanation should remain hypothesis-generating. The present meta-analysis cannot separate potential pharmacokinetic differences from residual confounding by maternal metabolic phenotype, lipid profile, comorbidities, statin type, dose, exposure timing, or treatment continuation. Therefore, heightened statin sensitivity in Asian populations should not be interpreted as a direct explanation for the observed low-birth-weight association.

The INOVASIA trial provides useful contextual evidence but should not be directly equated with real-world observational exposure. In that trial, hydrophilic pravastatin was used as a targeted intervention in women at high risk of preeclampsia, a condition characterized by angiogenic imbalance, endothelial dysfunction, and placental hypoperfusion. The trial reported more favorable perinatal outcomes, including lower preterm delivery and higher birth weight. This apparent contrast with the observational low-birth-weight signal may reflect differences in study design, clinical indication, background risk, exposure timing, and statin pharmacology. Observational cohorts included heterogeneous statin types, including lipophilic agents such as atorvastatin, simvastatin, lovastatin, fluvastatin, and pitavastatin, whereas pravastatin has relatively limited fetoplacental transfer. In one included cohort, lipophilic statins were associated with low birth weight, while hydrophilic statins were not [14]. These findings support caution in treating statins as a homogeneous drug class during pregnancy and limit causal interpretation of the low-birth-weight signal.

Clinically, the current findings are most useful for risk communication. Inadvertent statin exposure during early pregnancy should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of major fetal malformation risk. At the same time, pregnancies with statin exposure or continuation should be assessed in the broader context of maternal cardiometabolic disease, obstetric risk, fetal growth, and gestational age. Decisions regarding statin continuation or discontinuation during pregnancy should remain individualized and should balance maternal cardiovascular indications with fetal safety considerations.

Research implications

Future studies should better distinguish incidental early pregnancy exposure from clinically indicated statin continuation in women with high cardiovascular risk. More detailed reporting of statin type, lipophilicity, dose-response relationships, and timing of exposure is needed to clarify whether risks differ across individual agents. In addition, future cohorts should report gestational age-adjusted birthweight, small-for-gestational-age status, and fetal growth restriction, rather than low birth weight alone. Evidence from more diverse Asian populations is also needed, as current quantitative data are mainly derived from Taiwan and South Korea.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including a focus on Asian populations, the use of large nationwide cohorts, and preferential pooling of adjusted estimates. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, only three observational studies were available for quantitative synthesis, which limited statistical power, precluded formal assessment of publication bias, and restricted subgroup analyses. The certainty of evidence was low for congenital anomalies and low birth weight, and very low for preterm birth. Second, although this study focused on Asian populations, the quantitative synthesis was based on nationwide cohorts from Taiwan and South Korea; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting the currently available Asian evidence, predominantly East Asian data, rather than all Asian populations. Third, residual confounding by indication and maternal disease severity could not be fully excluded, despite adjustment, propensity score matching, or weighting in the original studies. Fourth, the included cohorts differed in comparator definition, including exposure versus non-exposure and continuation versus discontinuation among preconception statin users. Although sensitivity analysis showed generally consistent findings for congenital anomalies and low birth weight, this heterogeneity limits causal interpretation. Fifth, low birth weight could not be consistently separated from prematurity-related low birth weight or true fetal growth restriction, because gestational age-adjusted birthweight and small-for-gestational-age data were not uniformly reported. Sixth, pharmacological heterogeneity across statins could not be adequately addressed. The included observational studies grouped statins with different lipophilicity, placental permeability, transporter-mediated disposition, dose intensity, and exposure duration, whereas the randomized trial evaluated hydrophilic pravastatin as a targeted intervention for preeclampsia prevention. Harmonized statin-specific, lipophilicity-stratified, or dose-response estimates were not consistently available across studies. Therefore, pooling heterogeneous statin exposures as a single category may obscure clinically important differences between lipophilic and hydrophilic agents and limit causal interpretation of the low-birth-weight signal. Finally, exposure ascertainment was largely based on prescription or claims data, which may not fully reflect actual medication use or adherence.

Taken together, these limitations indicate that the observed association with low birth weight should be interpreted cautiously. The current evidence is useful for clinical counseling and hypothesis generation, but it does not establish a causal class effect of statins on fetal growth or adverse birth outcomes.

Conclusions

The available Asian evidence, largely derived from nationwide East Asian cohort studies, suggests no clear association between prenatal statin exposure or continuation and congenital anomalies. Prenatal statin exposure or continuation was modestly associated with low birth weight, whereas evidence for preterm birth remained inconclusive because of substantial between-study heterogeneity.

The association with low birth weight should be interpreted cautiously. Low birth weight could not be consistently distinguished from prematurity-related low birth weight, small-for-gestational-age status, or fetal growth restriction. In addition, residual confounding by maternal cardiometabolic disease, differences in comparator definitions, and pharmacological heterogeneity across statin types limit causal interpretation. Future studies should distinguish incidental early pregnancy exposure from clinically indicated statin continuation, report statin-specific and lipophilicity-stratified outcomes, and evaluate exposure timing, dose-response relationships, and gestational age-adjusted fetal growth outcomes in more diverse Asian populations.
