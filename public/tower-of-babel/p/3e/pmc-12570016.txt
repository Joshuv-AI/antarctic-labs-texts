Case report: post-delivery thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura complicated by malignant catatonia
Authors: Ameer Ahmed Alajali, Ahmed Abdulhussain Shahatta, Sajjad Ghanim Al-Badri, Ali Saad Al-Shammari, Khadija Naeem, Abbas Hamza Abbas, Hussein Rizgar, Nabeel Al-Fatlawi, Hussein Mohsin Hasan, Aditya duhan, Zaryab Bacha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is a rare, life-threatening thrombotic microangiopathy characterized by thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), renal dysfunction, fever, and neurological symptoms. Postpartum TTP, although uncommon, arises due to hormonal, immunological, and coagulation changes with pregnancy. We present a 21-year-old postpartum female developed TTP shortly after delivery, presenting with bluish skin patches, jaundice, leg swelling, and oliguria. The diagnosis of neurological TTP was presumed based on clinical presentation, as ADAMTS13 testing was unavailable. Treatment included plasma exchange, corticosteroids, and hemodialysis. Despite therapy, she developed severe neurological symptoms, including seizures, mutism, and autonomic instability, ultimately diagnosed as malignant catatonia, a rare neuropsychiatric complication of TTP. administration of intravenous benzodiazepines and rituximab led to significant clinical improvement and recovery. This case underscores complexity of postpartum TTP with rare complications like malignant catatonia, and highlights critical importance of early recognition, management, and maintaining a high index suspicion for atypical postpartum presentations.

Introduction

Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is a rare and life-threatening thrombotic microangiopathy resulting from the formation of microvascular platelet-rich thrombi, leading to organ ischemia and systemic complications. It is clinically characterized by a classic pentad: thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), renal dysfunction, fever, and neurological manifestations. While all five features may not always present simultaneously, their occurrence strongly indicates TTP and necessitates urgent medical intervention. This condition poses a significant challenge due to its rapid progression and the potential for severe outcomes, including multi-organ failure and death, if not promptly recognized and treated [1, 2].

Despite its rarity, TTP requires heightened clinical suspicion, particularly in vulnerable populations such as postpartum patients, where physiological changes during pregnancy and delivery may trigger its onset. Postpartum TTP, although infrequent, is a recognized complication arising from the complex interplay of hormonal surges, immunological modulation, and hypercoagulable states inherent to pregnancy and the postpartum period. These factors can disrupt the delicate balance of coagulation and fibrinolysis, potentially precipitating the severe endothelial dysfunction characteristic of TTP. The postpartum period presents a unique challenge as symptoms of TTP may overlap with other obstetric conditions, necessitating careful differentiation. Early identification and initiation of life-saving therapies, such as plasma exchange, are essential to mitigating morbidity and mortality associated with this rare but serious disorder [3, 4].

This case report describes a challenging presentation of TTP in a young postpartum patient, which was further complicated by the development of malignant catatonia—a rare neuropsychiatric manifestation. The case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for TTP in postpartum patients presenting with unexplained thrombocytopenia and MAHA and highlights the critical role of timely diagnosis and a multidisciplinary approach in managing complex and refractory cases. By detailing the clinical course, diagnostic challenges, and therapeutic interventions, this report aims to contribute to the understanding of atypical TTP presentations and their management, particularly in postpartum settings.

Case presentation

A 21-year-old female with no significant past medical or surgical history presented on zero day postpartum after her third uneventful pregnancy. Four days prior to delivery, she noticed multiple bluish skin patches on her limbs and leg swelling. By delivery day, her sclera had turned yellow. Post-delivery, she developed reduced urine output with headache and repeated vomiting and was admitted for evaluation. Over the following days, her condition deteriorated, prompting aggressive diagnostic and therapeutic interventions. On admission, the patient was mildly overweight, conscious, and fully oriented, with no respiratory distress. Examination revealed stable vitals, jaundice involving the skin, sclera, and trunk, pale conjunctiva, multiple purpuric lesions on upper and lower limbs, and bilateral pitting edema up to the knees. Systemic examination showed normal findings except for decreased urine output (50 mL/12 h). Initial investigations revealed a hemoglobin level of 9.7 g/dL, a platelet count of 70 × 109/L, elevated blood urea (60 mg/dL), and serum creatinine (2.3 mg/dL). Liver function tests showed slightly elevated liver enzymes, and total serum bilirubin was 16.8 mg/dL (mainly indirect). Peripheral blood film demonstrated numerous schistocytes, suggesting microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA). Reticulocyte count was 16%, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was 1934 U/L, Coombs test was negative, coagulation profile was normal, and virology screening was negative. By day two, the patient became anuric with worsening renal parameters and severe anemia (Hb: 6.1 g/dL, platelets: 36 × 109/L). A several differential diagnoses were considered, including HELLP syndrome, hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS), pre-eclampsia, metabolic disorders, and vasculitis but based on normal blood pressure, absence of proteinuria, and the patient had no history of exposure to medications known to cause thrombotic microangiopathy, which helped exclude other potential causes of thrombocytopenia and microangiopathic hemolysis. So clinical diagnosis of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) was made, and treatment with hemodialysis, plasma exchange (PEX), and methylprednisolone was initiated. The patient continued on hemodialysis and PEX with improvement in renal and hematological parameters. Follow-up blood film showed a gradual reduction in schistocytes, but platelet counts remained below 100 × 109/L, and LDH decreased to 700 U/L. After 15 days of admission, she completed seven dialysis sessions, resulting in normalized renal function and good urine output with subsided edema. She also completed 12 sessions of PEX, with the percentage of schistocytes reduced to 3%, reticulocytes at 5.8%, platelets at 31 × 109/L, and hemoglobin at 7.9 g/dL. On day 17, the patient developed sudden generalized seizures, high-grade fever, disorientation, and behavioral changes. Neuroimaging, including CT, MRI, MRV, and MRA, and cerebrospinal fluid analysis were normal (Fig. 1). Blood film and lab tests indicated rebound hemolysis with Hb at 3.5 g/dL, platelets at 36 × 109/L, and elevated LDH. Rituximab was initiated alongside PEX for presumed neurological TTP. Despite these interventions, the patient’s condition worsened, with stupor, mutism, abnormal eye movements, areflexia, and autonomic instability, including dyspnea and tachycardia. Over the next week, although hematological parameters normalized, neurological symptoms progressively deteriorated. By day 25, reassessment revealed hypertonia, posturing, and waxy flexibility, consistent with malignant catatonia. A trial of intravenous benzodiazepines led to marked transient improvement, confirming the diagnosis. Lorazepam therapy resulted in significant improvement in neuropsychiatric and autonomic symptoms. Discussion

Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is an uncommon yet serious condition that demands prompt diagnosis and treatment to reduce the risk of life-threatening complications [5]. This case highlights a particularly challenging presentation, complicated by catatonia, an infrequent but serious neuropsychiatric manifestation [1]. Catatonia in TTP presents clinically with a variety of neurological symptoms, including altered mental status, disorientation, seizures, and motor abnormalities. These symptoms are the outcome of microthrombi-induced ischemic brain damage. Catatonia is defined by motor immobility, mutism, negativism, posture, and waxy flexibility. In the setting of TTP, these symptoms might be part of a broader range of neurological manifestations, such as headaches, focal impairments, and encephalopathy [6–8]. The diagnosis of TTP in this patient was guided by the classic pentad: microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, renal dysfunction, fever, and neurological symptoms [9]. While not all features were present at initial presentation, the combination of schistocytes on the peripheral smear, refractory thrombocytopenia, and elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) strongly suggested TTP. This underscores the importance of a high index of suspicion, particularly in postpartum patients presenting with unexplained thrombocytopenia and MAHA [10]. Plasma exchange (PEX) combined with corticosteroids remains the foundation of TTP management. In this patient, the prompt initiation of PEX played a critical role in preventing further organ damage and stabilizing hematological parameters. Despite initial improvement, persistent thrombocytopenia and fluctuating hematological markers indicated refractory disease, prompting the introduction of rituximab [11]. The subsequent recovery of renal function and improvement in hematological indices emphasize the effectiveness of this multimodal treatment strategy. The neurological complications of TTP are diverse and can range from mild confusion to seizures and coma. In this patient, the sudden onset of seizures, disorientation, and autonomic instability raised concerns for neurological involvement of TTP [12]. Despite aggressive management, the neurological symptoms progressed, leading to suspicion of malignant catatonia—a rare neuropsychiatric disorder characterized by stupor, mutism, and autonomic dysregulation. Catatonia is a clinical diagnosis that requires a thorough exclusion of structural, metabolic, and infectious causes of encephalopathy. Normal neuroimaging and cerebrospinal fluid analysis in this case supported the diagnosis. The transient improvement with intravenous benzodiazepines provided further confirmation [13]. The rapid response highlights the potential for benzodiazepines to act as both diagnostic and therapeutic agents in catatonia, particularly when combined with multidisciplinary care [14]. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high level of clinical vigilance for atypical presentations and rare complications in postpartum patients, particularly those with unexplained thrombocytopenia and neurological symptoms. The favorable outcome achieved in this case underscores the critical role of a tailored, multidisciplinary approach to diagnosis and management. One limitation of this report is the absence of ADAMTS13 activity testing, which would have definitively confirmed the diagnosis of TTP. However, the clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and positive therapeutic response strongly support the diagnosis, emphasizing the importance of timely clinical decision-making in such scenarios.

Conclusion

This case underscores the complexity of postpartum thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and the need for timely diagnosis and multidisciplinary management. The development of malignant catatonia, a rare neuropsychiatric complication, highlights the diverse neurological manifestations of TTP. The favorable outcome following plasma exchange, corticosteroids, rituximab, and benzodiazepines emphasizes the importance of early intervention. This report reinforces the need for clinical vigilance in postpartum patients with unexplained thrombocytopenia and neurological symptoms and the value of adaptive therapeutic strategies in managing severe TTP cases.
