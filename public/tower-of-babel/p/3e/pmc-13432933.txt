Effects of Exercise Mode and Intensity on Retinal Microvascular Structure and Function in Heart Failure Patients with Preserved Ejection Fraction: A Pilot Exercise Intervention Trial
Authors: Lukas Streese, Egbert Thomas Ritter, Raphael Schoch, Julia Maria Kröpfl, Christoph Hauser, Benedikt Gasser, Thomas Dieterle, Arno Schmidt-Trucksäss, Henner Hanssen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe aimed to investigate the effect of different exercise modes on retinal microvascular endothelial function in patients with HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF).MethodsTwenty-eight patients were randomly allocated to either a moderate (MCT) or high-intensity interval training (HIIT) group for 12 weeks. Retinal microvascular structure and function were investigated by analyzing central retinal arteriolar (CRAE) and venular (CRVE) diameter equivalents, the arteriolar-to-venular diameter ratio (AVR), and the dynamic arteriolar (aFID) and venular (vFID) flicker light-induced dilation response.ResultsTwenty patients (mean age 70 ± 9 years) were included in the final analysis. After HIIT, AVR increased (0.80 ± 0.03 vs. 0.83 ± 0.04, p = 0.03) due to arteriolar widening. CRAE increased (157 ± 18 µm vs. 164 ± 18 µm, p = 0.05), but CRVE did not change (196 ± 27 µm vs. 197 ± 24 µm, p = 0.56). After MCT, AVR also increased (0.80 ± 0.08 vs. 0.83 ± 0.06, p = 0.02) without significant changes in CRAE (160 ± 19 µm vs. 165 ± 18 µm, p = 0.07) or CRVE (201 ± 14 µm vs. 199 ± 14 µm, p = 0.40). aFID improved after MCT (2.1 ± 1.6% vs. 3.1 ± 1.8%, p = 0.03) but not after the HIIT intervention (2.1 ± 1.7% vs. 2.4 ± 2.1%, p = 0.60). vFID did not statistically significant change neither in the HIIT (4.4 ± 2.2% vs. 3.4 ± 1.9%, p = 0.06) nor in the MCT (4.3 ± 2.2% vs. 5.5 ± 4.1%, p = 0.26) group.ConclusionMCT and HIIT are both adequate therapy options to improve microvascular health in patients with HFpEF.

Introduction

Chronic heart failure (HF) is a complex clinical syndrome characterized by shortness of breath, fatigue, fluid retention, and severe exercise intolerance. Vascular dysfunction represents a sensitive marker for severity of HF [1] and outcome prognosis [2] in patients with chronic HF. More than 10% of adults older than 70 years suffer from HF [3]. Patients with HF and a left ventricular ejection fraction of ≥50% are classified as patients with HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) [4]. HFpEF is the most common form of HF in older adults [5] and is closely associated with increasing age, female sex, obesity, arterial hypertension, ischemic heart disease, diabetes mellitus, and atrial fibrillation [6]. The treatment of patients with HFpEF remains challenging due to its high heterogeneity [7]. Nevertheless, exercise rehabilitation is a class I level A recommendation to improve exercise capacity of patients with HF [4]. In addition, exercise training is recommended to nearly all patients suffering from cardiovascular (CV) diseases to improve the general CV risk profile, vascular health, and therefore patient outcome [8]. Several short-term intervention studies have investigated the effects of exercise on vascular function, cardiorespiratory fitness, and the general CV risk profile. In their meta-analysis, Ramos et al. [9] analyzed the impact of moderate continuous training (MCT) or high-intensity interval training (HIIT) interventions on vascular function in six randomized controlled trials involving 182 participants. HIIT was defined as four intervals of 4 min at 85–95% of maximal heart rate (HRmax) with 3 min of active recovery (60–70% HRmax) in between. Both exercise modalities improved vascular function. However, HIIT was more effective and additionally showed a tendency for larger improvements of peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak), CV risk factors, oxidative stress, inflammation, and insulin sensitivity [9]. A further meta-analysis from Weston et al. [10] compared the efficacy of HIIT compared to MCT in ten studies with 273 patients with coronary artery disease, HF, hypertension, metabolic syndrome, and obesity. Patients undergoing HIIT showed almost double the increase in VO2peak compared to MCT. It is important to note that the risk of exercise-related CV events seems to be equally low for HIIT and MCT interventions in patients with coronary heart disease [11]. Nevertheless, there is still no clear recommendation regarding the most effective exercise intensity for improving the vascular health of patients with HFpEF. We have recently shown that 12 weeks of exercise therapy was effective to improve microvascular function in a case report of one male chronic HF patient [12]. Microvascular function was quantified using the static (SVA) and dynamic retinal vessel analysis (DVA), both approved diagnostic approaches to quantify systemic microvascular health [13]. DVA allows for the assessment of retinal microvascular endothelial function as a window to the systemic microcirculation [13]. In patients with HFpEF, endothelial dysfunction is a key pathophysiological trait that can be targeted by exercise treatment [14]. A recent published systematic review showed that several retinal vessel biomarkers are able to improve risk stratification and disease prognosis of patients with HF [15]. Especially, central retinal arteriolar (CRAE) and venular diameter equivalents (CRVE) as well as arteriolar (aFID) and venular (vFID) flicker light-induced dilatation are suitable vascular biomarkers to noninvasively quantify microvascular function [13, 15]. This pilot study is the first to investigate the effects of different exercise intensities on retinal microvascular function in patients with HFpEF.

Methods

Study Design

This single-center pilot exercise intervention trial – part of the HIT-HF study (Swiss National Science Foundation Grant No. 185217) – took place at the Department of Sport, Exercise and Health (DSBG) of the University of Basel between October 2020 and November 2022. A detailed study protocol has been published previously [16]. Briefly, male and female patients with HF were recruited by local cardiologists and screened for inclusion and exclusion criteria. Patients who fulfilled the inclusion criteria and signed a written informed consent were further investigated at the DSBG. Patients were randomized to either a HIIT or a MCT group after baseline assessments. Patients were re-invited to repeat baseline assessments after 12 weeks of intervention. Assessments, randomization, intervention, and statistical procedures are described below. The Local Ethics Committee for the Region of North-Western and Central Switzerland approved this study (EKNZ, Project-ID: 2019-00188). This study was planned and conducted considering the principles stated in the Helsinki Declaration [17].

In- and Exclusion Criteria

Male and female individuals ≥18 years who had the following structural or functional changes in echocardiography: LAVI >34 mL·m−2, LVMI >115 g·m−2 (men), >95 g·m−2 (women), or E/E′ ratio >13 and mean E′ septal and lateral wall <9 cm·s−1; high levels of N-terminal prohormone of brain natriuretic peptide >125 pg·mL−1; signs and symptoms of chronic HF: dyspnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, reduced exercise capacity, extended recovery after exercising, fatigue, or peripheral edema (lower leg, ankle); ejection fraction >50%; New York Heart Association classification of I, II, or III; at least 4 weeks on stable medical treatment or without signs and symptoms of cardiac decompensation; ventilatory threshold >40% of predicted VO2peak and VO2peak >10 mL·min−1·kg−1 at the screening visit were included. Exclusion criteria were planned cardiac interventions in the following 6 months, unstable angina pectoris, uncontrolled brady- or tachyarrhythmia, uncontrolled hypertonic blood pressure, severe uncorrected valvular heart disease, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, clinically significant concomitant disease states (e.g., advanced renal failure, hepatic dysfunction, insulin-dependent diabetes), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease grades III–IV according to the global initiative for chronic obstructive lung disease classification, on-going cancer treatment, significant musculoskeletal disease limiting exercise capacity, active infection, immunosuppressive medical therapy, blood transfusion within the previous 30 days, local eye diseases such as macular degeneration or glaucoma, pregnancy or lactation, noncompliance, drug or alcohol abuse, inability to follow the procedures of the study, e.g., due to insufficient language skills, psychological disorders (dementia, etc.), participation in another intervention study, enrollment of the investigators, their family members, and other persons involved in the study procedures, and life expectancy <6 months.

Randomization and Blinding

The randomization procedure has been published previously [16]. Briefly, the Castor software (Castor, Amsterdam, Netherlands) was used to perform a 1:1 random allocation of patients to either the HIIT or MCT group, stratified by sex. All vascular assessments were performed by experienced researchers who were blinded for group allocation and patient data. It was not possible to blind patients or study personal who performed the exercise intervention for group allocation. However, all vascular assessments were performed by blinded investigators.

Retinal Microvascular Assessments

Patients were asked to avoid intense exercises 24 h, alcohol intake and tobacco use 12 h, and nutrition intake 2 h prior the vascular examinations. Vascular assessments were performed at the same daytime at baseline and follow-up. One pupil of each participant was dilated with tropicamide 0.5% SDU Faure (Thea Pharma, Schaffhausen, Switzerland) at both time points to perform retinal vessel assessments using the retinal vessel analyzer system (Imedos Health GmbH, Jena, Germany) and a fundus camera (FF450; Carl Zeiss Ltd., Jena, Germany). An extensive description of the SVA and DVA protocol and the analytical procedures can be found elsewhere [13, 18]. Briefly, three images with an angle of 50° were taken of each participant at each time point to perform the SVA. Based on standard operating procedures [18], the CRAE and CRVE were calculated using a specified analytical software (VesselMap 2®; Imedos Health GmbH, Jena, Germany). Based on the CRAE and CRVE, the arteriolar-to-venular diameter ratio (AVR) was calculated. DVA was performed using the standard protocol provided by Imedos Health with three identical cycles with 50 s baseline, 20 s of high frequent flicker light (12.5 Herz), and 80 s recovery phase [18]. One arteriolar and one venular vessel segment were marked in the superior temporal quadrant at least one disc diameter away from the optic disc. Diameters of these segments were continuously measured over time. The RVA software (v.5.51; Imedos Health GmbH, Jena, Germany) measured aFID and vFID based on the principles of neurovascular coupling [19] as a noninvasive way to quantify microvascular function.

Cardiopulmonary Exercise Testing

VO2peak and peak heart rate (HRpeak) were analyzed based on an incremental symptom-limited exercise test on an electronically operated bicycle ergometer (Ergoselect 200, Ergoline, Bitz, Germany). One of three different ramp protocols (protocol 1: warm-up unloaded, then increase of 7 W·min−1; protocol 2: warm-up at 10 W, then increase of 10 W·min−1, protocol 3: warm-up at 20 W, then increase of 15 W·min−1) were used based on patient’s fitness level [20]. After a warm-up phase of 3 min., patients were motivated to pedal a constant rate of 60 rpm until individual exhaustion or symptoms of ischemia or serious cardiac arrhythmias appeared. Exhaustion was defined as a respiratory exchange ratio (VCO2/VO2) of >1.05 [21]. VO2peak as well as HRpeak were defined as the highest values during the exercise test, measured with a breath-by-breath analyzing test system (MetaMax 3B, Cortex Biophysik GmbH, Leipzig, Germany) and a 12-channel electrocardiography (Custo Med GmbH, Ottobrunn, Germany). VO2peak was averaged over 30 s.

Exercise Interventions

An extensive description of the HIIT and MCT has been published previously [16]. Briefly, both groups trained for 12 weeks, 3× per week, at the DSBG, supervised by sports scientists. Exercise intensity was controlled in every training session by heart rate, breathing rate, and a 1-channel ECG. HIIT and MCT had the same energy expenditure (isocaloric design). The MCT training group had a warm-up phase of 5 min, followed by resistant training with four exercises and a continuous endurance training phase on a bike ergometer (60–70% of HRpeak) in weeks 1–8. The training duration on the bike ergometer continuously increased from 17 min to 26 min in weeks 1–8. In weeks 9–12, patients started directly with the MCT phase without warm-up and resistant training for 44 min. The HIIT group also started with warm-up phases (60–70% of HRpeak) and resistant training in weeks 1–8, followed by an additional warm-up, an HIIT phase (85–95% of HRpeak), and a cool-down phase. The duration of the high-intensity intervals continuously increased during the 12 weeks of training from 2 × 1 min, 2 × 2 min., 2 × 3 min., 3 × 3 min., to finally 4 × 4 min, to avoid overtraining [16].

Statistical Analysis and Sample Size Calculation

Statistics were performed and graphs were designed using R version 3.6.1 or later (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All statistical tests were performed with a significance level of ≤0.05. Sample characteristics were descriptively described by using mean and standard deviation (SD). Intervention effects of the HIIT and MCT group on vascular parameters and patient characteristics were calculated separately using paired sample t tests after testing for normal distribution. Differences in vascular adaptations between the HIIT and MCT group were calculated using linear regression models. CRAE, CRVE, AVR, aFID, and vFID at follow-up were the dependent variables and the corresponding values at baseline, age, sex, change in blood pressure, change in body mass index, and change in VO2peak were independent variables. The main outcome in this study was the individual exercise intervention effect of the HIIT and MCT group on CRAE. Based on a pilot study, in which we investigated the potential exercise-induced effect on the retinal microcirculation in chronic HF, we expected a CRAE improvement of 10 µm (143 µm–153 µm) with an SD of 16 µm [12]. To reach a target power of 80% with a one-sided significance level of 0.05, we needed a sample size of n = 8 participants in each exercise group. We chose a one-sided significance level because we primarily showed a widening of CRAE after exercise interventions in previous studies [22, 23]. We planned to include 12 participants in each group to consider potential drop outs.

Results

Eight patients of the HIIT and 12 patients in the MCT group were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Medications were slightly different between the HIIT and MCT group (Table 1). The HIIT and the MCT group did not differ in age, height, New York Heart Association Classification (NYHA), B-type natriuretic peptide, left ventricular ejection fraction, left atrial volume indexed to body surface area, and left ventricular mass indexed to body surface area at baseline (Table 1). Both training modalities did not lead to statistically significant improvements of the body composition or changes of systolic blood pressure. The diastolic blood pressure improved in the MCT but not in the HIIT group (Table 1).

Retinal vessel diameters changed in both training groups. In the HIIT group, AVR improved significantly (0.80 ± 0.03 vs. 0.83 ± 0.04, p = 0.03) due to widening of the arterioles. CRAE increased significantly (157 ± 18 µm vs. 164 ± 18 µm, p = 0.05), but CRVE (196 ± 27 µm vs. 197 ± 24 µm, p = 0.56) did not change. In the MCT group, AVR also increased (0.80 ± 0.08 vs. 0.83 ± 0.06, p = 0.02) without significant changes in CRAE (160 ± 19 µm vs. 165 ± 18 µm, p = 0.07) or CRVE (201 ± 14 µm vs. 199 ± 14 µm, p = 0.40) (Fig. 2). The linear regression analysis showed no statistically significant group effects for CRAE, CRVE, and AVR (Table 2). aFID improved in the MCT group (2.1 ± 1.6% vs. 3.1 ± 1.8%, p = 0.03) but not in the HIIT group (2.1 ± 1.7% vs. 2.4 ± 2.1%, p = 0.60). vFID did not change significantly neither in the HIIT (4.4 ± 2.2% vs. 3.4 ± 1.9%, p = 0.06) nor in the MCT (4.3 ± 2.2% vs. 5.5 ± 4.1%, p = 0.26) group (Fig. 3). Discussion

Retinal vessel structure and function of patients with HFpEF showed clinically relevant improvements [24, 25] after 12 weeks of HIIT and MCT training, without statistically significant differences between groups. While the diameter of small arteries (CRAE) improved after HIIT only, the improvement of dilation capacity was only seen after MCT.

Retinal vessel diameters and retinal vessel function are known as valid vascular bed to quantify systemic microvascular health and therefore to improve CV risk stratification and to monitor treatment effects [13, 26]. The Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study has previously shown that wider CRVE and narrower CRAE are both associated with incident HF [27]. Widening of CRVE and narrowing of CRAE, resulting in a lower AVR, were associated with a higher prevalence of left ventricular hypertrophy and larger left ventricular size in a 16-year follow-up study with more than 10,000 individuals [27]. Patients with coronary artery disease, with and without HF, showed blunted aFID compared to healthy controls in a cross-sectional study [28]. Interestingly, aFID was able to differentiate between patients with coronary artery disease and ischemic HF, while pulse wave velocity and flow-mediated dilatation, two gold standard methods to quantify endothelial function in the microcirculation, failed to differentiate these disease severity states [28]. The EXAMIN AGE study showed that a walking-based HIIT for 12 weeks led to wider CRAE, narrower CRVE, and a higher AVR in CV risk patients compared to a control group with standard physical activity recommendations [29]. In addition, this study reported aFID improvements after HIIT in CV risk patients [30]. Patients with arterial hypertension showed narrower CRVE, a higher AVR, and a higher aFID after 8 weeks of HIIT compared to a control group in the HyperVasc study [23, 31]. Our current study showed comparable effects on retinal vessel diameters in patients with HFpEF, which might be independent of the training condition. The HIIT and the MCT group both showed AVR improvements. The AVR improved in both groups by 0.03 which represents a clinically relevant effect that is comparable to the EXAMIN AGE and HyperVasc study [23, 29]. These improvements are also comparable to previous exercise intervention studies in patients with elevated CV risk [29] or patients with hypertension [23]. Patients with elevated CV risk showed AVR improvements of 0.03 after 12 weeks of HIIT training [29] and patients with hypertension showed AVR improvements of 0.03 even after 8 weeks of HIIT [23]. It is important to note that the current study did not compare potential training-induced vascular adaptations with a control group with physical activity recommendations only. Therefore, the reported potential training-induced effects have to be interpreted with caution. However, the similarity of vascular adaptations with the effects of previous exercise intervention trials supports the relevance of the exercise effects in each intervention group.

The mean aFID improvement was 1.0 percentage points in the MCT group and 0.3 in the HIIT group. The mean vFID change was −0.8 percentage points in both groups. For aFID [24] and vFID [32], a clinically relevant training effect would be ≥0.6 and ≥1.3 percentage points, respectively, especially in individuals with low baseline values. Previous training studies have shown that exercise-induced changes range from 0.3 to 0.8 percentage points for aFID and from −0.2 to −0.1 percentage points for vFID. Interestingly, only the MCT group, and not the HIIT group, showed statistically and clinically significant aFID improvements after the training period.

To understand this result, it is essential to discuss structural and functional training-induced adaptations together. The HIIT but not the MCT group showed wider CRAE after the training period. We have previously shown that widening of CRAE, also called pre-dilation, a positive training adaptation, might reduce the functional capacity measured by aFID [33, 34]. The MCT group showed no significant structural adaptation, as quantified by CRAE. Therefore, the functional microvascular training adaptations can be quantified by higher aFID values in the MCT group, but not in the HIIT group, where positive structural adaptations may mask functional improvements. This interpretation remains speculative but is based on the findings of our previous studies [33, 34]. Future studies must clarify the mechanisms by which the aFID depends on, or is independent of, the structural adaptations of small arteries, for example, by using a protocol that induces myogenic constriction [34].

Previous studies have reported inconsistent results regarding whether HIIT or MCT might have a greater vascular effect in patients with HF. Wisloff et al. [35] showed that HIIT led to higher improvements of left ventricular remodeling and endothelial function compared to MCT in patients with postinfarction HF. Higher blood flow and shear stress during HIIT are responsible for increase plasma glutathione peroxide availability, which may contribute to reactive oxidative species reduction and increased nitric oxide bioavailability [36]. We have recently shown that HIIT activates a cascade of reprogramming DNA methylation mark on p66Shc gene promoter, leading to lower p66Shc and oxidative stress levels, and retinal microvascular amelioration. It is not clear whether HIIT or MCT in general and the specific training designs in the current study trigger similar mechanisms. Donelli da Silveira et al. [37] reported that HIIT and MCT showed similar effects on diastolic function improvements after 3 months of training in patients with HFpEF. The current study supports the assumption that HIIT and MCT are both effective training concepts to improve microvascular health in patients with HFpEF. However, it is important to note that the above-described studies differ in terms of vascular parameters, patient cohorts, and training designs, which limits their comparability.

Whether exercise-induced vascular adaptations in the retinal microcirculation are responsible for a long-term and systemic CV risk reduction has to be investigated in future studies. A recently published systematic review showed that the retinal microcirculation is a valid vascular bed to quantify microvascular alterations and exercise-induced training adaptations in patients with HF [15]. However, no study exists that investigated whether exercise-induced retinal microvascular adaptations lead to a long-term CV risk or mortality reduction in healthy individuals or patients with elevated CV risk. In previous exercise studies, we have shown that exercise-induced adaptations can differ between the retinal microcirculation and macrovascular biomarkers [29–31, 38]. Therefore, long-term follow-up studies are necessary to investigate whether short-term exercise-induced vascular adaptations in the retinal microcirculation led to a long-term reduction in systemic CV risk.

The main limitation of this study is the missing control group that received no exercise program. Therefore, the changes from baseline to follow-up in both groups have to be interpreted with caution. To prove potential superiority of HIIT over MCT with respect to improving microvascular health, the sample size would need to be larger. However, especially the vascular changes are comparable with previous studies in CV risk patients where training effects were compared to a control group. In addition, we cannot prove that the structural improvement in arterial diameter after HIIT masked the improvement in functional dilation capacity with an unchanged aFID post-exercise. The interpretation remains speculative but is solidly based on previous findings on the relation of diameter and functional dilation capacity [13, 34]. A high exclusion rate was observed due to the high prevalence of local eye diseases, which may have introduced selection bias. Local eye diseases were an exclusion criterion because they hinder the interpretation of retinal microvascular function as a marker of systemic cardiovascular risk. Therefore, it was important to exclude all patients with local eye diseases in both eyes. However, this limits the usability of retinal vessel biomarkers in older or high-risk cohorts. The missing post-VO2peak values limit the interpretation of exercise capacity adaptations. Mechanistic verification of the effects of exercise on microvascular structure and function need to be addressed in future larger scale studies. The main strength of our pilot study is the meticulous characterization of the patients with chronic HF allowing us to perform the first exercise intervention trail with retinal microvascular health as main outcome in patients with HFpEF.

In conclusion, HIIT and MCT both appear to be an effective exercise therapy to improve microvascular health in patients with HFpEF. Whether HIIT may be more effective in increasing arteriolar baseline diameters with increased, albeit masked, dilation capacity as compared to MCT needs to be investigated in future studies. Treatment options in HFpEF are scarce and our findings of exercise-induced improvements of microvascular health warrant further research to establish a causal relationship with clinical outcome in patient with HFpEF.
