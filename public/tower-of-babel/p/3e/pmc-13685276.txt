Changes in the pathogenic characteristics of hospitalized children with community-acquired pneumonia in Changzhou, China, 2018–2023
Authors: Feifei Hu, Keyi Ji, Meng Gu, Fang Yao, Tao Zhang, Qiuwei Wang, Maryam Zaid, Yunzhi Ling, Xiaoting Wu, Genming Zhao, Jianming Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCommunity-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is a significant cause of hospitalization and mortality in children. Non-pharmacological interventions (NPIs) implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic were associated with changes in the transmission dynamics of respiratory pathogens. The present study aimed to analyze changes in the pathogenic characteristics of hospitalized children with CAP in a single-center pediatric cohort in Changzhou, China, before and after the outbreak of COVID-19.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed demographic information, hospitalization records, and pathogen-detection results of children admitted for CAP at Changzhou Children’s Hospital, Jiangsu Province, China, from 2018 to 2023. The analyzed pathogens included eight target bacteria and seven respiratory viruses.ResultsA total of 29,552 children hospitalized with CAP were included, including 15,124 in the pre-pandemic period, 9024 in the pandemic period, and 5404 in the post-pandemic period. The male-to-female ratio was 1.42:1, and the median age was 16 months (IQR: 5.0–37.0). Hospitalizations generally peaked in autumn and winter from 2018 to 2022, whereas an off-season peak occurred in May 2023. The overall bacterial positivity increased from 9.94% before the pandemic to 13.96% during the pandemic and 13.68% after the pandemic, while viral positivity increased from 16.52% to 21.43% and 21.06%, respectively. In contrast, coinfection positivity decreased from 6.89% before the pandemic to 5.13% during the pandemic and 3.02% after the pandemic. Across all three periods, Escherichia coli consistently ranked first among bacterial detections, while RSV remained the predominant virus among viral detections.ConclusionsNoticeable shifts in the detection spectrum and positivity rates of common bacterial and viral pathogens were observed among hospitalized children with CAP following the implementation and relaxation of COVID-19 prevention and control policies. Continuous long-term surveillance is needed to refine targeted prevention strategies and optimize clinical treatment protocols for pediatric CAP.Clinical trialNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-14024-9.

Introduction

Respiratory infections are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in humans, particularly among infants and young children, and have been recognized as a global health issue [1, 2]. Among these infections, community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is the single largest infectious cause of hospitalization and death in children worldwide [3]. In 2019, pneumonia led to 740,180 child deaths, accounting for 14% of all deaths in children under 5 years old [4]. In China, an estimated 21.1 million new cases of pneumonia in children under 5 years of age occur each year, making it a public health concern [1, 5]. A diverse range of microorganisms can cause CAP, including viruses and bacteria, which are the most predominant [6]. In the present study, pathogen analysis mainly focused on selected target bacteria and common respiratory viruses, whereas fungi and atypical pathogens were not systematically included. However, due to factors such as the low positive rate of blood cultures, the use of antibiotics before sampling, and the difficulty of distinguishing upper-airway colonization or infection from true lower respiratory tract infection when interpreting microorganisms detected from upper-airway-related samples, identifying the causative pathogens of CAP in children remains challenging [7–9].

Since December 2019, coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), has spread globally [10]. On January 30 of the following year, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared COVID-19 a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) [11]. Subsequently, many countries adopted non-pharmacological interventions (NPIs), including wearing masks, social distancing, school closures, lockdowns, and travel restrictions, to reduce social contact and limit the spread of SARS-CoV-2 [12]. China was among the countries that implemented national NPIs early in the epidemic. In addition to encouraging mask-wearing and hand-washing in public places, these measures effectively curbed the spread of COVID-19 [13]. Studies have shown that such NPIs can influence the spread of SARS-CoV-2 and alter the global epidemiology of respiratory infections [14–17]. For instance, research conducted in China demonstrated a notable decrease in influenza and other respiratory illnesses among children during the pandemic, attributed to NPIs [18]. Longitudinal data on pathogen detection among hospitalized children with CAP in China remain limited.

This study aimed to describe the pathogenic characteristics of CAP among hospitalized children in Changzhou, China, from 2018 to 2023, and to compare changes in pathogen detection across the pre-pandemic, pandemic, and post-pandemic periods.

Methods

Data collection

For this single-center, retrospective observational study, we reviewed records of children hospitalized with CAP at Changzhou Children’s Hospital from 2018 to 2023. Changzhou Children’s Hospital is the sole tertiary-level specialized children’s hospital in Changzhou. In routine clinical practice, CAP was diagnosed by treating physicians based on clinical manifestations, physical examination findings, laboratory results, and chest imaging findings when clinically indicated. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age between 28 days and 60 months; (2) admission date from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2023; (3) the primary discharge diagnosis ICD-10 codes J09-J18 or J20-J22, as used in previous pediatric CAP studies [19, 20]. Basic information of enrolled cases, including hospitalization ID, sex, date of birth, admission date, discharge date, discharge diagnosis, as well as bacterial and viral test results, was extracted from the hospital information system. Based on China’s COVID-19 management, this study was divided into three periods: before the pandemic (pre-pandemic), during the pandemic (pandemic), and after the pandemic (post-pandemic). The pre-pandemic period spanned from January 2018 to December 2019, and the pandemic period from January 2020 to December 2022. According to the policy adjustment announced by the National Health Commission in late December 2022, China began managing COVID-19 under Class B infectious disease measures on January 8, 2023 [21]; therefore, the post-pandemic period was defined as January to December 2023.

Pathogen detection

Respiratory specimens, including oropharyngeal swabs, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, sputum, and respiratory secretions, were collected from hospitalized children for bacteriological or virological testing when clinically indicated. Pathogen testing was not performed uniformly for all children with CAP but was conducted at the physicians’ discretion based on treatment needs, clinical manifestations, disease severity, and specimen availability. For bacteriological testing, respiratory specimens were cultured on chocolate medium and blood agar (Autobio, China) according to routine clinical microbiology procedures. After incubation, colonies with predominant growth were selected for pure culture and further identification. Quantitative thresholds were not consistently recorded in the retrospective database. Bacterial isolates were identified using the EXS2600 fully automated microbial mass spectrometry detection system (Zybio, China) by comparing the generated mass spectral profiles with the system database. The target bacterial species included Haemophilus influenzae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Enterobacter cloacae, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Acinetobacter baumannii, which have been associated with community-acquired pneumonia [22, 23]. For virological testing, the D3 Ultra DFA Respiratory Virus Screening and ID Kit (Diagnostic Hybrids, USA) was used strictly following the protocol to detect influenza A virus (Flu A), influenza B virus (Flu B), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), adenovirus (ADV), and parainfluenza virus types 1, 2, and 3 (PIV1-3). Molecular testing, such as PCR, was not routinely and systematically performed for all children hospitalized with CAP during the study period. SARS-CoV-2 was not included in the routine virological detection panel or pathogen-detection analysis because routine and systematic SARS-CoV-2 testing was not performed for all children hospitalized with CAP throughout the study period.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.4.2). Continuous variables were described as medians with interquartile ranges and compared using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Demographic percentages were based on the relevant number of included children, whereas pathogen positivity rates were based on the number tested. To compare multiple pathogen positivity rates, the false discovery rate (FDR) method using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons. Multivariable logistic regression was used to examine the association between period and overall bacterial or viral positivity, with adjustment for age group, sex, and season of admission. Overall bacterial or viral positivity was defined as any positive result among children who underwent the corresponding test. Results were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value or FDR-adjusted P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General characteristics

As shown in Table 1, from 2018 to 2023, a total of 29,552 hospitalized children with CAP were included in the analysis. Among them, 12,187 (41.2%) were female and 17,365 (58.8%) were male. The male-to-female ratio was 1.42:1. The median age of these patients was 16 months (IQR: 5.0–37.0). Specifically, 7517 children (25.4%) were in the < 6 months group, 4570 children (15.5%) were in the 6 to < 12 months group, 5788 children (19.6%) were in the 12 to < 24 months group, and 11,677 children (39.5%) were in the 24 to ≤ 60 months group. A total of 15,124, 9024, and 5404 hospitalized children with CAP were enrolled in the pre-pandemic, pandemic, and post-pandemic periods, respectively. Moreover, there was no statistical difference in the sex distribution of patients among the three periods. However, the post-pandemic group had the highest median age of 27 months and the largest proportion of children aged 24 to ≤ 60 months (54.6%).

Monthly distribution

As shown in Fig. 1, the monthly distribution of hospitalized children with CAP varied across the three study periods. During the pre-pandemic period, the monthly patterns in 2018 and 2019 were generally similar, with higher numbers observed in winter. After the onset of the pandemic, monthly hospitalizations declined markedly, especially after January 2020, and remained lower than pre-pandemic levels despite fluctuations in 2021 and 2022. In the post-pandemic period, hospitalizations increased again after February 2023 and peaked in May.

Among the 9609 children hospitalized with CAP that underwent bacteriological testing, 1144 (11.91%) tested positive. Among the detected target bacteria, the highest positive rate was for Escherichia coli (2.62%), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (2.12%), Haemophilus influenzae (1.70%), Staphylococcus aureus (1.52%), Streptococcus pneumoniae (1.24%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (0.54%), Enterobacter cloacae (0.31%), and Acinetobacter baumannii (0.21%). The overall bacterial positivity was higher during and after the pandemic than before, increasing from 9.94% before the pandemic to 13.96% during the pandemic and remaining at 13.68% after the pandemic. Similar patterns were observed for Staphylococcus aureus (0.75%, 2.37%, and 2.11%) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (1.60%, 2.83%, and 2.19%). In contrast, Haemophilus influenzae decreased during the pandemic and increased afterward (1.76%, 1.31%, and 2.50%).

Among the 14,849 hospitalized cases with CAP that underwent virological testing, 2810 (18.92%) tested positive. Among the detected viral pathogens, the highest positive rate was for RSV (12.70%), followed by PIV3 (4.55%), Flu A (2.29%), PIV1 (0.99%), ADV (0.88%), PIV2 (0.68%), and Flu B (0.49%). There were statistically significant differences in both the overall viral detection rate and the positive rates for individual virus types across the three time periods. In particular, RSV remained the most frequently detected virus across all three periods, whereas the relative rankings of the other viruses varied across periods.

Among the 8888 hospitalized cases with CAP that underwent both bacteriological and virological testing, 508 (5.72%) tested positive for coinfection. Specifically, 237 cases (2.67%) were positive for bacterial-viral coinfection, and 271 cases (3.05%) were positive for viral-viral coinfection. Bacterial-bacterial coinfection was not systematically analyzed because the bacteriological results were based on routine culture records. There were statistically significant differences in both overall coinfection detection and viral-viral coinfection rates across the three time periods (Table 2). As shown in Fig. 2, the most common coinfections among the aforementioned hospitalized cases were RSV-PIV3, Flu A-PIV3, RSV-ADV, and RSV-Flu A. After adjustment for age group, sex, and season of admission, both the pandemic and post-pandemic periods remained associated with higher overall bacterial and viral positivity compared with the pre-pandemic period. Detailed results are presented in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3. Sensitivity analysis restricted to children with pneumonia-specific ICD-10 codes J12-J18 showed trends consistent with the primary analysis, with higher overall bacterial and viral positivity during and after the pandemic than before the pandemic (Supplementary Table S4).

Proportion of pathogens

As shown in Supplementary Figures S1 and S2, the composition of positive pathogen detections varied across the three study periods. Statistical comparisons of pathogen-specific positivity rates across periods are presented in Table 2. Among bacterial detections, Escherichia coli consistently accounted for the largest proportion before, during, and after the pandemic (22.71%, 21.88%, and 20.57%, respectively). Staphylococcus aureus increased in proportion from 7.50% before to 16.97% during the pandemic and remained relatively high after the pandemic (15.43%). Among viral detections, RSV remained the predominant virus across the three periods, increasing from 48.48% before the pandemic to 62.53% during the pandemic and 67.50% after the pandemic.

Discussion

We conducted a six-year retrospective observational study at the only tertiary-level pediatric hospital in Changzhou. The aim was to comprehensively understand the data on children hospitalized with CAP during this period. By comparing monthly numbers of CAP hospitalizations and respiratory pathogen detection across three distinct periods, we aimed to capture changes in the pathogenic characteristics of CAP among hospitalized children in Changzhou over the years.

A total of 29,552 hospitalized children with CAP were included in our study, with a male-to-female ratio of 1.42:1, consistent with other studies [5, 19]. We also noticed that after the pandemic, the median age of hospitalized children was 27 months, representing an increase compared with the previous two periods. This finding was similar to that reported by Abushahin et al. [12]. During the pandemic, NPIs were widely implemented to reduce COVID-19 transmission. These measures may also have reduced children’s exposure to common respiratory pathogens. Reduced exposure has been proposed to limit the acquisition or maintenance of pathogen-specific immunity, potentially increasing susceptibility and contributing to the post-pandemic resurgence of respiratory infections [24–26]. After the pandemic, 2950 children (54.6%) were in the 24 to ≤ 60 months group, most of whom were born or grew up primarily during the epidemic and may have been more susceptible to CAP, potentially due to weakened immunity. However, this interpretation remains speculative and may also be influenced by changes in admission patterns and healthcare-seeking behavior.

Consistent with the findings of other researchers [1, 27], our study revealed that the peak in hospitalizations among children with CAP occurred during the autumn and winter seasons. After the implementation of NPIs, such as social distancing, mask-wearing recommendations, and school closures, a significant reduction in respiratory infections among children was observed [18, 28–30]. Similarly, the number of childhood hospitalizations appeared to be lower during the pandemic period in our descriptive analysis. In Fig. 1, our attention was drawn to an unseasonal peak in the number of hospitalized children in 2023. This peak may have reflected the increased incidence of respiratory infections following the relaxation of NPIs, with an off-season surge in RSV cases as a possible contributing factor that year [31, 32]. As further evidenced by Supplementary Figure S2, RSV was the most prevalent viral pathogen in 2023, accounting for 67.5% of viral detections.

The incidence of bacterial pneumonia declined as childhood immunization rates increased, with viruses emerging as the most prevalent pathogens in CAP [3, 27, 33]. Our study revealed that virus positivity rates exceeded those for bacteria. Meanwhile, positivity rates for both bacteria and viruses increased significantly during the pandemic compared to the period before, and then decreased slightly after. A study conducted in western China showed an increase in viral positivity after the COVID-19 outbreak, similar to our findings [34]. The increase in the positivity rate of bacteriological tests after the pandemic warrants careful consideration. Changes in pediatric healthcare-seeking behavior during the COVID-19 pandemic may have influenced the observed positivity rates [35].

We observed that the detection positivity rates for most bacterial and viral pathogens varied significantly across three periods. However, the detection positivity rate of Streptococcus pneumoniae, a primary pathogen causing CAP [6], remained relatively stable throughout these periods. Ai et al. [36] also mentioned that the positivity rate of Streptococcus pneumoniae among children remained relatively stable both before and during the pandemic. In contrast, the positivity rate for Haemophilus influenzae declined during the pandemic but increased afterward. This difference may be partly related to the biological and epidemiological characteristics of Haemophilus influenzae, although this explanation remains speculative and requires further investigation. Further surveillance of S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae is warranted in future studies. Similar to the findings of Tang et al. [22], the overall coinfection rate among hospitalized children with CAP showed significant variation across different periods. Viral-viral coinfection appeared to change more markedly than bacterial-viral coinfection, possibly related to mechanisms such as respiratory viral interactions [37]. In addition, the bacterial-viral coinfection identified in this study should be interpreted cautiously, because culture-based bacterial findings may include colonizing bacteria. The detection positivity rate of RSV rose during the pandemic, consistent with other research findings and may also have been associated with the relaxation of NPIs and potential changes in the environmental resistance of RSV strains [38–40]. In line with a study conducted in Wuhan, China [41], RSV was identified as the predominant pathogen in coinfection cases. This finding underscores the necessity of strengthening targeted surveillance and conducting further in-depth research focusing on RSV.

When examining the proportion of targeted bacterial detection, we found that Escherichia coli, Haemophilus influenzae, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Staphylococcus aureus consistently ranked among the top across three different periods, similar to findings from other studies [6, 42]. However, during the pandemic, the proportions of these bacteria shifted, notably with Staphylococcus aureus rising from 7.50% to 16.97%. The increase in the proportion of Staphylococcus aureus may have been related to differences in antibiotic use and bacterial–viral interactions [22, 43, 44]. We also noted that Escherichia coli consistently ranked first across all three periods, which may be related to factors such as specimen collection and transportation that could have influenced its isolation rate [6]. A recent pediatric study also noted that sputum culture results may include both true pathogens and colonizing bacteria, particularly because commensal bacteria are frequently present in the upper respiratory tract of young children; therefore, culture-based bacterial findings should be interpreted cautiously [45]. When looking at viral pathogen proportions, we found that RSV consistently ranked first across all three periods, while PIV3 and Flu A were also commonly detected viruses. RSV was the most common viral cause of CAP [3]. As shown in Supplementary Figure S2, RSV was the predominant viral pathogen in all three periods. The absolute numbers of RSV-positive cases were 811 before the pandemic, 751 during the pandemic, and 324 after the pandemic.

Meanwhile, the relative proportion of RSV among viral detections increased from 48.48% before the pandemic to 62.53% during the pandemic and 67.50% after the pandemic. Possibly due to its ability to spread rapidly through direct contact and its prolonged survival outside the host, RSV was observed by some scholars to have been relatively less affected during the pandemic than other respiratory viruses [46, 47]. We further noted fluctuations in the proportion of Flu A. Due to overlapping transmission routes with COVID-19 and the implementation of NPIs during the pandemic, the incidence rate of seasonal influenza decreased by 64% in the 2019–2020 epidemiological year in China [48]. If influenza activity remained low within a community for an extended period, population susceptibility to influenza could increase [49], potentially leading to increased influenza activity following the relaxation of NPIs.

Our study has some limitations. First, it was a single-center retrospective study, and the results may be different in other regions. Second, pathogen testing was not uniformly performed for all children hospitalized with CAP; it was determined by physicians based on clinical judgment and treatment needs. Therefore, selection bias and confounding may have been introduced. Accordingly, the reported positivity rates reflect only the children tested and should not be interpreted as pathogen incidence in the overall cohort. Although multivariable analyses were adjusted for age group, sex, and season of admission, information on disease severity and preadmission antibiotic exposure was incomplete. Therefore, residual confounding may remain. Bacterial detection results should also be interpreted cautiously because of potential colonization and the use of routine laboratory judgment to assess predominant growth. Third, CAP cases were identified based on the primary discharge diagnosis ICD-10 codes recorded in the hospital information system. Although ICD-10 codes J09–J18 and J20–J22 have been used in previous pediatric CAP studies, J09–J11 mainly refer to influenza and J20–J22 are not specific to pneumonia. Sensitivity analysis restricted to pneumonia-specific ICD-10 codes J12–J18 showed similar trends, but potential misclassification cannot be completely excluded. Fourth, DFA and culture-based methods were used for pathogen detection, while PCR or multiplex PCR was not routinely and systematically performed for all children hospitalized with CAP during the study period. Therefore, some pathogens with low culture yields or those that are difficult to detect using conventional methods may have been missed, potentially leading to an underestimation of pathogen positivity. Fifth, COVID-19-associated pneumonia was not analyzed separately because SARS-CoV-2 testing was unavailable during 2018–2019 and was performed only when clinically indicated during the pandemic, rendering the data incomplete and unsuitable for systematic analysis. In addition, although Mycoplasma pneumoniae is an important pathogen in pediatric CAP, it was not systematically tested in all hospitalized children and was therefore not included in the pathogen-profile analysis, which may have affected the completeness of pathogen evaluation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study showed that the detection patterns of selected target bacteria and respiratory viruses among hospitalized children with CAP in Changzhou, China, varied across the pre-pandemic, pandemic, and post-pandemic periods. Differences were observed in the monthly distribution of hospitalized children, as well as in positivity rates and pathogen detection proportions. These findings highlight the need for continuous prospective surveillance, which may provide further evidence for the prevention and control of CAP in children.
