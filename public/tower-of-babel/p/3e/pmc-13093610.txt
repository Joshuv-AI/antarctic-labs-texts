Acute Postpartum Respiratory Distress With Right Ventricular Dysfunction Complicating Into Postpartum Cardiomyopathy: A Case of Suspected Atypical Amniotic Fluid Embolism
Authors: Aniceth Muchunguzi, Esmail Sangey, Hilary Chipongo, Najma Awadh, Kaushik Ramaiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAmniotic fluid embolism (AFE) is a devastating and unpredictable obstetric emergency typically characterized by sudden cardiopulmonary collapse and coagulopathy during or shortly after delivery. It is triggered by an anaphylactoid‐histamine‐mediated systemic response, evidently with no available confirmatory tests in living patients. Introduction

Amniotic fluid embolism (AFE) is an unpredictable, life‐threatening condition of pregnancy. Although its pathogenesis is not definitively understood, it is postulated by the entry of amniotic fluid with fetal elements and tissue factors into maternal circulation, triggering an anaphylactoid‐histamine‐mediated systemic response, acute pulmonary vasoconstriction, and cardiopulmonary compromise [1, 2]. AFE typically manifests during labor, cesarean section, or within 30 min postpartum; however, delayed presentations occurring several hours later have been documented [1, 3]. It may also occur during abortion, after abdominal trauma, or following amnio‐infusion [4, 5, 6].

The incidence of AFE varies globally, estimated at 2–15 per 100,000 pregnancies, with reported mortality ranging between 20% and 40% in developed settings [1, 7, 8]. Risk factors include advanced maternal age, multiparity, abnormal placentation, polyhydramnios, instrumental or cesarean delivery, eclampsia, labor induction, and underlying respiratory disease (such as asthma), several of which were present in this patient [7, 9].

Diagnostic criteria for AFE remain clinical and exclusionary [10]. Systems such as the United Kingdom Obstetric Surveillance System (UKOSS) and Society for Maternal‐Fetal Medicine (SMFM) emphasize sudden hypoxia, hypotension or cardiac arrest, coagulopathy, and absence of alternative explanation within 30 min postpartum [3, 6, 7, 11]. Echocardiographic findings, including right ventricular dysfunction, acute pulmonary hypertension, septal displacement (“MConnell's/D‐sign”), and biventricular dysfunction, may support this diagnosis [12].

AFE management is largely supportive: rapid cardiopulmonary resuscitation, invasive hemodynamic and respiratory support, correction of coagulopathy, and, when indicated, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) [2, 13, 14]. Adjunctive therapies such as glucocorticoids, point‐of‐care coagulation monitoring, and pulmonary vasodilators (e.g., inhaled nitric oxide, milrinone) may offer benefits [2, 13, 15].

Importantly, AFE remains a diagnosis of exclusion, and atypical or incomplete presentations frequently overlap with other postpartum cardiopulmonary conditions, particularly peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM), stress‐induced cardiomyopathy, and acute pulmonary edema. This overlap necessitates cautious interpretation and multidisciplinary evaluation. We describe a postpartum case presenting with acute respiratory distress and right ventricular dysfunction, suggesting the possibility of atypical AFE versus unmasked PPCM.

Case Presentation

Prenatal and Delivery Course

We present a 31‐year‐old gravida 2 para 1, African woman with mild normocytic normochromic anemia of pregnancy (Hb 10.5 g/dL) and otherwise no chronic medical illness who was admitted at 37 + 1 weeks of gestation age in true labor. She had a regular antenatal follow‐up, had been taking ferrous sulfate, folic acid, and prenatal vitamins, and had an uncomplicated course of pregnancy. Her previous pregnancy ended in a cesarean delivery secondary to failed induction, complicated by postpartum eclampsia. On admission, vital signs were stable: blood pressure 130/85 mmHg, pulse 89 bpm, respiratory rate 18 breaths/min, temperature 37°C, and oxygen saturation 97% on room air. Her physical and obstetric examination findings as well as fetal monitoring were unremarkable.

She underwent emergency cesarean section due to a previous uterine scar. A healthy male baby weighing 2.7 kg was delivered within 1 h of admission with an Apgar score of 8 and 10 at the 1st and 5th minute, respectively. The intraoperative course was uneventful.

Postpartum Deterioration

Eighteen hours postpartum, the patient developed acute shortness of breath, orthopnea, and palpitations without chest pain or fever. She had not received a blood transfusion or new medications. Physical examination revealed marked respiratory distress with respiratory 34 breaths/min, oxygen saturation 70% on room air, pulse 138 bpm, blood pressure 180/96 mmHg, diaphoresis, use of accessory muscles, and bilateral infra‐mammary rhonchi.

Differential Diagnosis and Investigations

Electrocardiogram (ECG) showed sinus tachycardia, regular rhythm, without ischemic changes. A contrast‐enhanced CT pulmonary angiography excluded pulmonary embolism but demonstrated bilateral dense pulmonary opacities (Figure 1). Initial Echocardiography (ECHO) revealed dilated right atrium and right ventricle (RV basal diameter 4.5 cm), with reduced tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE 1.5 cm), consistent with right ventricular systolic dysfunction. Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction was mildly to moderately reduced with global hypokinesia (LVEF 44%). There was no evidence of valvular abnormalities, intracardiac thrombus or vegetations. Formal quantitative parameters such as RV fractional area change (FAC), pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP), tricuspid regurgitant gradient, and RV/LV ratio were not obtainable due to the emergent bedside nature of the examination, and this is acknowledged as a limitation. Focused ultrasound demonstrated a dilated Inferior vena cava (2.2 cm) and multiple B‐lines. Laboratory investigations (Table 1), were not consistent with disseminated intravascular coagulation or significant hemorrhage.

Treatment

Initial management in the High Dependence Unit included supplemental oxygen via nasal prongs, then a non‐rebreather mask. But due to persistent hypoxemia (SpO2 87% on 15 L/min O2) and tachypnea up to 60 breaths/min, consistent with severe acute hypoxemic respiratory failure (SpO2/FiO2 ratio 95), the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit. She was initiated on continuous positive airway pressure ventilation (pressure control 17 cm H2O, PEEP 10 cm H2O, FiO2 70%), later de‐escalated to high‐flow nasal oxygen at 25 L/min, FiO2 45%.

Pharmacologic therapy, among others, included milrinone infusion (since aerosolized formulations were unavailable), diuretics (furosemide), Phosphodiesterase‐5 inhibitor (tadalafil), beta‐1 blocker (bisoprolol) and analgesics. Invasive mechanical ventilation and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation were avoided.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient improved with resolution of respiratory distress and weaning off oxygen support. She was transferred out of the ICU after 2 days and discharged home 2 days later. At 10 weeks postpartum, repeat echocardiography demonstrated persistent left ventricular systolic dysfunction with LVEF 36%, fulfilling diagnostic criteria for peripartum cardiomyopathy. She remains on guideline‐directed heart failure therapy with ongoing cardiology follow‐up.

Discussion

Acute postpartum respiratory failure is a major cause of maternal ICU admission and remains associated with significant morbidity and mortality [16, 17]. AFE is among the most devastating obstetric emergencies, with a clinical presentation that is both sudden and unpredictable [11, 16, 18]. Traditionally, AFE is characterized by an abrupt onset of hypoxia, hypotension, and disseminated intravascular coagulation occurring during labor or shortly after delivery, in the absence of fever [19, 20]. However, recent clinical evidence acknowledges a broader spectrum of atypical presentations, including incomplete, sub‐acute, or delayed‐onset AFE, often lacking one or more of the classical tetrad features [3, 8, 21, 22]. Our patient presented with severe acute respiratory distress (ARDS) as per the Kigali modification of the Berlin criteria [23], bilateral pulmonary infiltrates on CT‐PA, and echocardiographic evidence of RV strain with reduced LVEF (44%). However, the patient did not exhibit hypotension, cardiac arrest, or coagulopathy, and symptom onset occurred 18 h postpartum, which is atypical even for most reported delayed AFE cases. AFE remains a diagnosis of exclusion, and in the present case, its attribution is uncertain, despite the exclusion of pulmonary embolism, aspiration pneumonitis, and sepsis. Consequently, AFE should be considered a possible but unproven explanation rather than a definitive diagnosis [24, 25].

Peripartum cardiomyopathy represents an important alternative diagnosis. PPCM can present acutely with pulmonary edema, elevated natriuretic peptides, reduced left ventricular ejection fraction, and right ventricular involvement is increasingly recognized. A recent meta‐analysis by Wijayanto et al. reported right ventricular dysfunction in approximately 48% of PPCM patients, underscoring the significant echocardiographic overlap between PPCM and AFE‐related cardiopulmonary compromise. In this context, PPCM may have been the primary pathology, with the acute presentation representing its initial manifestation [26].

Echocardiography proved instrumental in delineating cardiovascular involvement. It revealed RV dysfunction with elevated pulmonary pressures, vascular overload and congestion. PPCM remained a possibility given the low LVEF. These echo‐based signs may also appear in AFE within minutes to hours of the inciting event [27]. A number of case series and reviews, including a systematic analysis by Wiseman et al., have identified RV dysfunction and biventricular dysfunction in over 60% and 13% of confirmed AFE cases respectively [12, 28].

While pulmonary embolism was excluded radiologically, imaging revealed dense bilateral patchy opacities consistent with capillary leak, suggestive of either non‐cardiogenic pulmonary edema or cardiogenic pulmonary edema secondary to cardiac dysfunction [29, 30]. Biomarker trends further support diagnostic ambiguity. The markedly elevated BNP with gradual decline reflects acute myocardial stress but is not specific to AFE. Troponin elevation was minimal and transient. While AFE may cause abrupt biomarker elevation due to systemic inflammatory response, acute pulmonary hypertension and myocardial stunning, PPCM is characterized by persistently elevated BNP associated with sustained ventricular dysfunction. The persistence of reduced LVEF at 10 weeks postpartum favors PPCM over isolated transient myocardial injury [4, 27]. Histological confirmation of AFE is often unavailable in living patients; therefore, diagnosis is primarily clinical, supported by imaging and exclusion of mimics [10].

Echocardiography played a central role in diagnosis and directing supportive therapy. Although some quantitative RV parameters were unavailable, the findings were sufficient to prompt targeted cardiopulmonary support with a favorable response to noninvasive ventilation and pharmacotherapy [31]. Milrinone infusion was initiated as an inodilator to reduce pulmonary vascular resistance and improve RV output [15]. Although inhaled forms of milrinone or nitric oxide may be preferred, their unavailability was mitigated by intravenous administration [13, 15, 32]. Adjunctive therapies included tadalafil, a phosphodiesterase‐5 inhibitor with pulmonary vasodilatory effects, diuretics (furosemide) for fluid offloading, and bisoprolol to blunt excessive adrenergic activity and mitigate cardiac remodeling [33, 34]. Noninvasive ventilation not only reversed hypoxemia but also potentially minimized ventilator‐associated complications and the need for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation [14, 35].

There is a well‐documented overlap between AFE‐induced myocardial stunning and PPCM, and in some cases, AFE may unmask underlying myocardial vulnerability [36, 37]. Regardless of etiology, the management of PPCM remains supportive, involving beta‐blockers, diuretics, and afterload reducers. Long‐term prognosis in PPCM varies: while approximately 50%–60% of patients recover full ventricular function within 6 months, others may have persistent dysfunction requiring chronic therapy [27]. This patient continues to receive standard heart failure treatment, with serial echocardiography planned to monitor cardiac recovery.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the diagnostic complexity of postpartum cardiopulmonary collapse, presenting with features suggestive but not diagnostic of atypical amniotic fluid embolism, followed by persistent left ventricular dysfunction fulfilling criteria for peripartum cardiomyopathy. It highlights the importance of cautious diagnostic attribution, echocardiography‐guided supportive management, multidisciplinary critical care and vigilant follow‐up for cardiomyopathy in postpartum patients recovering from unexplained respiratory failure. Future studies and registries are warranted to better define and understand the interplay between delayed/atypical AFE and postpartum cardiomyopathy.
