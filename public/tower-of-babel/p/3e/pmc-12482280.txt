Patient perspectives on participation in the Danish Breast Cancer Group Proton Trial: A qualitative research study☆
Authors: Kristine W Høgsbjerg, Anne W Kristensen, Mette Møller, Else Maae, Maja V Maraldo, Louise W Matthiessen, Sami Al-Rawi, Mette H Nielsen, Cai Grau, Birgitte V Offersen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The key barrier for trial participation was the geographic distance to the proton facility.•The main motivation for trial participation was the reduced risk of late effects, the proton trial's primary endpoint.•Most patients found the decision difficult, but their final choice was typically guided by a single, dominant factor.Keywords: Trial participation, Proton therapy, Breast cancer, Barriers

Introduction

The Danish Centre for Particle Therapy (DCPT) is Denmark’s national proton facility, providing treatment for patients where proton therapy is standard of care or part of clinical trials. These clinical trials are conducted in collaboration with Danish Multidisciplinary Cancer Groups, including the Danish Breast Cancer Group (DBCG) [1].

Adjuvant radiotherapy (RT) is standard following breast-conserving surgery, mastectomy for large tumours (pT3-4, >50 mm or with chest wall/skin involvement) and node-positive disease. While RT reduces the risk of recurrence and improves overall survival, it is associated with late effects, such as radiation-induced heart disease and secondary lung cancer, both of which increase with radiation dose to the heart and lungs, respectively [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. Proton RT offers the potential to improve target coverage while reducing doses to organs at risk, thereby potentially reducing these late effects [6]. The randomised controlled trial (RCT) DBCG Proton Trial (NCT04291378), initiated in 2020, investigates the efficacy of proton therapy in breast cancer (BC) alongside the UK PARABLE (REC 21/WS/0171) and RADCOM (NCT02603341) trials [[7], [8], [9]].

Although clinical trials are essential for establishing cancer treatment guidelines, participation rates among cancer patients remain low, ranging from 3 to 10 % [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. Enrolment is influenced by a range of patient- and physician-related factors [15,16]. The Ottawa Decision Support Framework (ODSF) emphasises that knowledge, personal values, and available decisional support shape patient choices [17]. Strengthening these elements may not only increase participation and enhance the external validity of trial results but also improve clinical decision-making and reduce the risk of patient regret [17].

This study aimed to identify barriers and motivators for trial participation from the patient perspective, to optimise decisional support, communication of trial information, recruitment, and trial design.

Methods

This qualitative study was conducted using the interpretive description methodological framework to explore patients’ experiences and reflections on participating in an RCT. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews with eligible participants. Reporting adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines [18].

The DBCG proton trial

The DBCG Proton Trial, an RCT comparing standard photon with proton RT in selected patients with BC or ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), was conducted at eight Danish RT clinics (Fig. 1). The primary endpoint is the 10-year incidence of ischaemic heart disease, with an anticipated reduction in risk regardless of pre-existing cardiac risk factors. Secondary endpoints include, among others, recurrence, secondary cancer, and locoregional morbidities. Photon therapy is delivered locally, while patients allocated to the proton arm receive their treatment at the DCPT.

The locations of the eight radiotherapy (RT) clinics and the distance to DCPT, the proton facility.

Proton RT is not included in standard patient information, as only a minority meet the trial’s inclusion criteria. After the planning CT—a computed tomography (CT) scan performed to obtain detailed anatomical information for radiotherapy—eligible patients are contacted by phone and informed about the trial. Those expressing interest receive written material and are invited to an in-person consultation with a clinical oncologist and nurse, where study procedures, randomisation, and treatment logistics are explained. Trial communication may vary depending on the healthcare professionals, clinical setting, and patient preferences. Some patients decline participation immediately after the initial phone call, without receiving full written or in-person information (Fig. 2). Patients who consent are randomised 1:1 to receive proton or photon RT (participants). Those declining participation (non-participants) are treated with standard photon RT at their local centre. Patients randomised to proton RT may experience a delay due to scheduling at DCPT. Those assigned for photon RT or non-participants proceed with treatment as planned or with minimal scheduling delays.

Sampling of patients

Patients were eligible for the proton trial if aged ≥18, diagnosed with invasive breast cancer or DCIS, indicated for adjuvant RT, and had a planning CT demonstrating a mean heart dose ≥4 Gy and/or ipsilateral lung V17 (the percentage of ipsilateral lung receiving ≥17 Gy) ≥37 %, with adequate target coverage as defined by the DBCG. Therefore, eligibility could only be determined after planning CT. Key exclusion criteria included previous breast cancer or chest RT. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in the trial protocol [8].

Patients eligible for this qualitative study were required to meet the DBCG Proton Trial inclusion criteria, irrespective of their actual trial participation. The sole exclusion criterion was the inability to complete an interview in Danish or English. To capture geographic and experiential diversity, this study aimed to recruit one participant and one non-participant from each RT clinic, totalling 16 patients, which was expected to achieve data saturation [19]. During standard care, treating physicians informed patients of their trial eligibility and sought permission to be contacted for this qualitative study. The interviewer (KWH) subsequently contacted those who agreed to schedule a telephone interview. Declining reasons were not recorded.

Ethical considerations

Informed patient consent was obtained from all patients after they received both oral and written information about the study. Consent was documented in both formats. The study protocol was approved and registered by the Central Denmark Region (Case no. 1-16-02-354-24) and followed the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design and data collection

Semi-structured telephone interviews were conducted from April to December 2024 using an interview guide developed to investigate factors influencing trial participation, trial information and reflections regarding randomisation (Table 2). The guide was developed in collaboration with the co-authors AWK and BVO, pilot-tested in two patients, and subsequently refined. One interviewer, a specialist registrar in clinical oncology (KWH), carried out all interviews. To minimise bias KWH had no prior relationship with the participants, avoided accessing medical records, collected patient characteristics only at the end of each interview to reduce preconceptions, disclosed her clinical background, and adopted a neutral interviewing style. To reduce recall bias, interviews were conducted between trial information and completion of RT.

Patients were informed that interviews would last approximately 30 min, with flexibility for individual needs. Open-ended questions were used to elicit patients' experiences with minimal interviewer interpretation. An interpretive approach enabled the interviewer to reflect on patients’ responses concerning the DBCG Proton trial’s aims and design [20].

Data analysis

Data analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis method within the interpretive description framework [[21], [22], [23], [24]]. All interviews were transcribed verbatim and imported into NVivo 15® (phase 1: familiarisation). Initial inductive coding involved careful reading of transcripts to identify relevant units of meaning (phase 2). Codes were examined for patterns, similarities, and differences across the dataset, enabling identification and grouping into preliminary themes (phase 3). These themes were then reviewed iteratively to ensure accurate reflection of the data and alignment with study aims (phases 4–5). Illustrative participant quotations were selected to support each theme (phase 6). No repeat interviews were conducted.

To ensure rigour and consistency, two interviews were independently analysed by AWK and KBB, with coding discrepancies discussed until consensus was reached.

Results

Fifteen patients were included, seven participants and eight non-participants (Table 1, Fig. 2), with a median age of 59 years (range 35–78). Participant inclusion was below the planned target, as one RT clinic was unsuccessful in including a participant. Twelve patients received chemotherapy for 4–6 months before RT (Table 1). All interviews except one were conducted as one-on-one telephone interviews; in one case, a relative was present as support at the patient’s request, but did not answer on behalf of the patient. Median interview duration was 31 min (range 18–42).

Distance to treatment facility as a barrier

The distance to the DCPT entailed various challenges for the patients, such as temporary accommodation in another city, extended travel times, reduced time with family and friends, limited leisure opportunities, and work disruptions. Patients frequently reported fatigue, which further exacerbated the burden of travel and relocation. The interviews revealed no indication that age influenced patients’ perception of distance as a barrier to participation. Many expressed that they would have enrolled in the trial and preferred proton RT if the treatment were available at their local clinic, indicating that distance, rather than the experimental nature of the treatment, was the main barrier (Table 3, citation 1.1).

Participants generally viewed the benefits as exceeding the inconvenience of travel, whereas non-participants more often saw distance as a significant barrier.

Timing for trial information

Several patients, both participants and non-participants, perceived the limited timeframe for decision-making as a barrier. Many indicated that earlier notification of trial eligibility and provision of trial-related information would have allowed more time to understand the study and make an informed decision (Table 3, citation 2.1). About half of the patients felt ready to decide quickly, whereas the remainder felt rushed to different extents. Among those who felt rushed, standard photon RT was often regarded as a more straightforward and less complex option (Table 3, citation 2.2). Additionally, patients described how having a clear understanding of their treatment plan provided a sense of stability, which served as an important coping mechanism during diagnosis and therapy. Sudden changes to the RT plan introduced uncertainty and appeared to disrupt their coping capacity (Table 3, citation 2.3). These changes were primarily experienced as a barrier among non-participants.

The importance of deliberation and decisional support

Most patients, both participants and non-participants, found the decision about trial participation difficult. It often involved a complex deliberation process that weighed perceived risks and benefits, and was accompanied by fear of making an incorrect choice. A few described the decision as straightforward. Some relied on heuristic decision-making rather than fully informed, value-based reasoning, which in some cases led to regret (Table 3, citation 3.1). Although trial information was generally considered sufficient, uncertainty about what was best for the individual still challenged the decision-making process. Several patients emphasised the need for decisional support, not only from healthcare professionals and relatives, but also from fellow patients with similar experiences (Table 3, citation 3.2).

Clinical equipoise and perceptions of experimental treatment

Patients’ perceptions of the trial were shaped by its experimental nature. Some perceived proton therapy as a potentially superior treatment, associated with fewer side effects, more frequent follow-ups, or the impression of personalised or privileged care (Table 3, citations 4.3–4.4, 4.6). Others, however, expressed concern about receiving an unproven treatment and feared being treated as ‘guinea pigs’ (Table 3, citation 4.1). This apprehension was reinforced by the impression that the trial treatment was unfamiliar to healthcare professionals, contributing to uncertainty (Table 3, citation 4.2). A few patients who perceived proton therapy as superior questioned the ethics of randomisation and why it was offered only within a trial (Table 3, citation 4.5). Overall, while most participants perceived proton therapy favourably, non-participants reported more mixed perspectives, ranging from fear of inferior treatment to recognition of potential benefits but declining participation due to other concerns or perceived limited clinical advantage.

Conflicting altruistic and egoistic needs

Decisions about trial participation often reflected tension between altruism and egoistic needs. Both participants and non-participants reported that they wanted to contribute to research (Table 3, citation 5.1), but for some, this was outweighed by personal limitations (Table 3, citation 5.2). Many patients, having undergone surgery and RT, sometimes alongside systemic therapy, reported mental and physical fatigue. This, combined with concerns about extended treatment duration, frequently discouraged trial involvement (Table 3, citations 5.3–5.4). The trial’s primary endpoint of reducing cardiopulmonary late effects was a strong motivator for some participants, perceived as directly relevant to personal benefit. Conversely, others judged the two treatment options as largely equivalent, either in terms of tumour control, potential side effects, or both, and therefore considered participation to offer minimal personal advantage. This conflict between altruistic and egoistic needs was evident across both patient groups, with participation outcomes largely reflecting perceived personal advantage.

Discussion

This study investigated perspectives on participation or non-participation in an RCT. The key factors influencing decisions were broadly similar across both groups, although the relative importance varied. Identified themes included distance to the treatment facility, timing and support for decision-making, perceptions of experimental treatment, and conflicting needs.

Consistent with Danish and Dutch studies on cancer trial participation, distance and the need for decisional support frequently emerged as barriers [12,16,[25], [26], [27]]. Notably, in the DBCG Proton Trial, 59 % of 363 participants originated from the three RT clinics nearest to DCPT, although these centres treat only 37 % of Danish breast cancer patients (data not shown).

Mental and physical fatigue were common and likely reflecting RT's placement late in the treatment course, typically after surgery and systemic therapy. Unlike the emotional responses often described at diagnosis, this cohort’s decision-making appeared shaped by treatment fatigue [26]. Fear or anxiety, prominent in other studies, were not dominant here, likely because immediate diagnostic anxiety had subsided and practical or fatigue-related considerations prevailed [12]. Since RT serves as an adjuvant therapy, rather than the primary curative treatment, this positioning may also influence patients’ perspectives regarding expected effect and treatment fatigue.

Trial design also contributed to barriers, particularly the limited time for decision-making and potential delays in treatment start. While most patients preferred receiving trial information earlier to allow more time for reflection, such flexibility is constrained by clinical procedures and legal regulations. Trial eligibility is based on heart and lung doses estimated from the planning CT, which is performed shortly before RT to ensure anatomical consistency and respiratory gating compliance. Moreover, the Danish Health Act mandates strict timelines for initiating cancer treatment, restricting delays for decision-making; for initial RT, treatment must commence within 14 calendar days of patient consent and no later than 28 calendar days after the referral is received [28]. Notably, patients were generally unwilling to delay treatment start. Certain clinical characteristics (e.g., pectus excavatum, contralateral implants, loco-regional RT indication, left-sided tumours) are associated with a higher risk of exceeding heart or lung dose constraints. However, their predictive sensitivity is limited, meaning that only a subset of eligible patients can be identified before planning CT. The current workflow, therefore, constrains early identification. To address this, two strategies could be considered: first, training clinicians to recognise these high-risk features, enabling earlier identification of some patients; and second, initiating CT planning earlier in the treatment course, particularly for patients undergoing chemotherapy with more adaptable timelines.

In this study, many patient decisions appeared to rely on heuristics instead of systematic evaluation, consistent with literature showing that choices often rely on intuitive reasoning [29,30]. Several patients desired peer support, such as “role models” or group discussions, to complement professional and family decision-making support [31]. Although evidence on the effectiveness of peer support interventions is mixed, well-designed programmes have demonstrated benefits in improving patient understanding and engagement in clinical trials [32,33]. Given the low estimated eligibility rate (8 %), in-person peer support may be logistically challenging; however, alternative modalities, such as informational videos featuring patients or clinical staff from DCPT, could be considered [34]. Additionally, incorporating tailored decision aids that address patients’ values and knowledge gaps may further facilitate informed and value-congruent decision-making [[35], [36], [37]]. However, a Cochrane review of decision aids for clinical trial participation concluded that current evidence is insufficient to determine whether such tools are more effective than standard information [37]. Improved trial communication and support tools may reduce regret and improve decision quality [[38], [39], [40]]. While physicians were largely perceived as neutral, a few patients detected preferences towards enrolment, suggesting that unintentional cues may influence decisions.

Selecting endpoints relevant to both clinicians and patients is essential for trial engagement [41,42]. In this study, as in previous research, the main motivations for participation were the prospect of personal clinical benefit and the desire to contribute to research and improve outcomes for future patients [[42], [43], [44]]. Variation in how patients evaluated the primary endpoint appeared to reflect differences in perceived benefit and, in some cases, their understanding of trial information. This highlights the importance of providing balanced, neutral information that clearly explains the primary endpoint, standard therapy, experimental therapy, and trial participation. Achieving this can be challenging, not only due to clinical equipoise but also because clinicians’ attitudes towards the trial may be influenced by their speciality background, implicit biases towards certain subgroups, and pre-existing treatment preferences. [15,[45], [46], [47]]. Training in recognising and managing implicit bias could enhance clinical practice [48]. From an ODSF perspective, addressing such biases is part of strengthening decisional support, ensuring that information is presented in a balanced way and aligned with patients’ values and informational needs.

One strength of this study was the inclusion of both participants and non-participants from all Danish RT clinics, increasing representativeness. All interviews were conducted, transcribed, coded, and analysed by a single researcher, ensuring methodological consistency. External coding further supported reliability and helped limit interpretive bias. Nonetheless, the researcher’s clinical background may have influenced data collection and interpretation, and physician-related barriers—although acknowledged as important—were not examined in this study [49,50]. Selection bias is also possible, as only patients who agreed to interviews were included. Those declining may have held stronger reservations or faced greater physical or emotional burdens. To address this, the study aimed for broad inclusion across regions and participant groups. Importantly, this study provides insight into the barriers and motivators influencing patients’ decisions about trial participation and their considerations of potential treatment modifications, particularly among those nearing the end of a prolonged treatment course. By contrast, most previous studies have interviewed patients either retrospectively or at the start of treatment, when different factors may influence decisions.

Data saturation was ensured by continuously analysing interviews as they were conducted. Recurrent themes indicated that sufficient depth had been reached, and additional interviews were unlikely to yield new perspectives [51,52].

Conclusion

This study describes the complexity of patient decision-making regarding clinical trial participation. Key factors influencing participation in an RCT included distance to the treatment facility, mental and physical fatigue after a long treatment period, timing of trial information and enrolment, treatment plan adjustments, and the interplay between altruistic motives and egoistic needs.

Our findings are consistent with the ODSF, which posits that decision-making is shaped by personal values/needs, knowledge of the trial, and the availability of support and resources (decisional support) [17]. We found that patients’ values and understanding of the study’s purpose and potential benefits played a key role in their decision to participate, whereas patients’ deliberative decision-making was constrained by limitations in time and resources, and our findings suggest that the timing of trial information — a factor that has been sparsely investigated — may also influence decisions and contribute to declining participation.

To address these barriers, early identification of eligible patients based on clinical characteristics, improved training in balanced trial communication, including raising awareness of implicit bias, and the development of peer support initiatives, such as online video materials, may improve inclusion rates and strengthen decisional support.
