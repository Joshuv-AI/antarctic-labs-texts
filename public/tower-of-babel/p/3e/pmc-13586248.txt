Hypoimmunogenic iPSC-derived hepatic organoids featuring a functional vascular network
Authors: Annalina Caroli, Marco Varinelli, Laura Vigani, Arianna Vallerga, Paola Rizzo, Daniela Rottoli, Sara Conti, Ilaria Craparotta, Stefano Fumagalli, Giuseppe Remuzzi, Ariela Benigni, Susanna Tomasoni, Piera Trionfini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The current management of end-stage liver diseases relies on organ transplantation but is constrained by donor scarcity and immune rejection. Induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived cells represent a promising alternative, as they can be expanded indefinitely, genome-edited to reduce immunogenicity and employed to develop more mature three-dimensional cultures. Here, we combined tissue engineering with hypoimmunogenic iPSC technology to generate off-the-shelf hepatic organoids that integrate parenchymal and non-parenchymal cells: hepatocytes, cholangiocytes, hepatic stellate cells, Kupffer cells and both vascular and sinusoidal endothelial cells. Lineage identity was validated by gene and protein expression analysis, electron microscopy and single-cell RNA sequencing. The complex microenvironment obtained recapitulates native liver composition and promotes hepatic maturation, as evidenced by the secretion of liver-specific proteins, including albumin and apolipoproteins. These properties, together with the presence of a functional endothelial network, represent a step towards universal cell therapies for end-stage liver diseases and hepatic metabolic disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58977-y.

Introduction

The global burden of liver diseases continues to rise, posing a major public health challenge due to high morbidity, mortality and healthcare costs1. While liver transplantation remains the gold standard for treating end-stage liver disease (ESLD), its effectiveness is limited by donor organ shortage and the risk of immune rejection, resulting in prolonged waiting lists and significant clinical and financial strains2. Beyond ESLD, genetic liver disorders such as alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, Crigler-Najjar syndrome and haemophilia A are characterised by impaired hepatic protein production and require auxiliary therapy with recombinant or plasma-derived proteins. Although beneficial, these treatments are hampered by short-term efficacy, high costs and substantial treatment burden3. These challenges underscore the urgent need for novel, donor-independent therapeutic strategies that could serve as a bridge to transplantation or even obviate the need for donor organs, while simultaneously providing a sustained supply of fully functional plasma proteins.

Primary human hepatocytes (PHHs) have long been considered a promising alternative to whole-organ transplantation due to their ability to replicate liver-specific functions, making them valuable for cell-based therapies. PHHs have been used clinically to treat Crigler-Najjar syndrome and have been evaluated preclinically for other conditions, including urea cycle disorders and factor VIII deficiency4. However, despite robust regenerative potential in vivo, PHHs exhibit limited proliferative capacity and rapid functional decline in vitro5, as well as scarce availability, restricting their therapeutic application. To overcome these limitations, efforts have focused on differentiating hepatocyte-like cells (HLCs) from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). Nevertheless, these cells frequently remain immature and only partially functional, especially in conventional two-dimensional cultures that fail to recapitulate the native liver microenvironment6. In contrast, iPSC-derived hepatic organoids provide a more physiologically relevant platform by reproducing the three-dimensional architecture and multicellular interactions inherent to liver tissue. These organoids perform essential hepatic functions in vitro, including protein synthesis and drug metabolism7. Recent studies on liver organoids have demonstrated that the inclusion of non-parenchymal cell types, such as endothelial and mesenchymal stromal cells, further supports hepatic maturation8. Despite these advantages, clinical translation is currently hindered by insufficient vascularisation, which compromises organoid viability, limits complete maturation in vitro and impairs engraftment following transplantation.

The generation of patient-specific autologous iPSCs remains impractical for routine clinical use due to high costs and long production timelines, particularly when precise correction of disease-causing mutations is required. Consequently, allogeneic iPSC-based strategies are preferred, as they enable industrial-scale production of ready-to-use, high-quality cells at lower cost. Because allogeneic iPSC derivatives are susceptible to immune rejection due to donor-recipient human leukocyte antigen (HLA) mismatch, we generated universal hypoimmunogenic donor cells by targeting beta-2-microglobulin (B2M) and class II major histocompatibility complex transactivator (CIITA) to ablate HLA class I and II expression, employing recent genome-editing strategies to modulate immune recognition and promote immune evasion9. These genome-edited iPSCs retained pluripotency, evaded immune surveillance and successfully differentiated into functional adult cell types10.

Building on these findings, we engineered hypoimmunogenic hepatic organoids from the genome-edited iPSCs for universal compatibility without the need for immunosuppression. Our organoids mimic native liver architecture and function, offering new promise as a donor-independent and cost-effective alternative to current liver replacement therapies.

Results

iPSC-derived hepatic organoid generation

To more faithfully recapitulate the structural and functional complexity of the liver in vitro, hepatic organoids were generated by co-culturing iPSC-derived hepatic endoderm cells, a pivotal intermediate stage in three-dimensional hepatic tissue development, together with hepatic stellate-like cells and endothelial-like cells (hepatic multilineage organoids, HMos) (Fig. 1a). This multicellular approach leverages the supportive role of non-parenchymal cells, which are known to enhance hepatocyte maturation, tissue organisation and functional stability11. In particular, endothelial cells supply nutrients and oxygen and actively drive organogenesis12, while hepatic stellate cells enhance organoid growth by modulating apoptosis and extracellular matrix deposition13,14. The efficient derivation of a homogeneous hepatic endoderm (HE) population from iPSCs15 was confirmed by immunofluorescence, demonstrating uniform expression of hepatocyte nuclear factor 4 alpha (HNF4α) and widespread cytoplasmic alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) (Fig. S1c). The differentiation of functional hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), expressing platelet-derived growth factor receptor beta (PDGFRβ) and vimentin (VIM), and endothelial cells (ECs), expressing vascular endothelial cadherin (CDH5) and von Willebrand factor (VWF), had been validated previously10.

Considering that both ECs and HSCs can originate from endoderm16,17, we also generated and characterised organoids composed exclusively of iPSC-derived HE cells (HE organoids, HEos) (Fig. 1a) to assess whether HE alone suffices to form properly structured hepatic organoids. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that, although similar in size, HEos exhibited heterogeneous shapes and irregular surfaces, whereas multilineage organoids were uniformly spherical with smooth cohesive surfaces (Fig. 1b).

Specific markers of the three lineages were detected in the organoids at both the mRNA and protein levels at different time points (hepatic markers: Fig. 1c-d; EC and HSC markers: Fig. S2). Notably, hepatic markers such as AFP, albumin (ALB), serpin family a member 1 (SERPINA1; alpha-1 antitrypsin, A1AT) and asialoglycoprotein receptor 1 (ASGPR1) increased progressively, indicating that 3D environment promoted HE maturation into hepatocyte-like cells, especially in the presence of ECs and HSCs in the hepatic multilineage organoids (Fig. 1c-d). Furthermore, immunofluorescence revealed that ECs in HMos established an extensive vascular network within the organoid core, whereas in HEos there were only a few newly formed ECs, derived from the hepatic endoderm, that remained isolated and unbranched (Fig. S2a).

Optimisation of the organoid culture

Despite the initial successful establishment of endothelial cell networks, HMos exhibited a progressive decline in CDH5 expression (Fig. 2a-b) from day 5 to day 16 of culture, suggesting that additional pro-angiogenic support was required. Considering the well-established role of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in EC maintenance and vascular development, the culture conditions were optimised by supplementing the medium with exogenous VEGF14. This adjustment restored CDH5 expression, as confirmed by immunofluorescence and RT-qPCR (Fig. 2c-d). Importantly, the addition of VEGF promoted not only EC growth, but also the formation of intricate branched networks extending to the organoid surface and surrounding other hepatic structures. In contrast, without VEGF supplementation, ECs remained confined to the organoid core, underscoring the critical role of VEGF in endothelial cell organisation and vessel formation.

Notably, VEGF addition did not alter hepatocyte marker expression (Fig. S3), confirming its selective effect on ECs.

Single-cell RNA sequencing of hepatic multilineage organoids

To initially define the cellular diversity within our organoids, we profiled the single-cell transcriptomes of 52,819 cells following quality control analysis. Unsupervised clustering of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data revealed the complex cellular composition of HMos (Fig. 3a; Fig. S4), identifying major parenchymal and non-parenchymal hepatic lineages, including hepatocyte-like cells, cholangiocyte-like cells, endothelial-like cells and hepatic stellate-like cells, consistent with single-cell atlases of human liver and hPSC-derived hepatic organoids18–24.

The hepatocyte compartment consisted of a single cluster annotated as hepatocytes, characterised by high expression of canonical hepatocyte genes (ALB, SERPINA1, APOA1, APOB, APOC3, ASGR1, ARG1, GLUL, CYP2D6) (Fig. 3b-c). Interestingly, the hepatocytes displayed a typical intralobular zonation pattern, demonstrated by scoring each cell for periportal and pericentral transcriptional signatures from published liver zonation datasets25. Opposing gradients of these scores across the hepatocyte manifold suggested zonation along the porto-central axis rather than the presence of discrete hepatocyte subtypes (Fig. S5).

Within the cholangiocyte lineage, four cholangiocyte-like subclusters were identified (Fig. S4). All subclusters expressed typical biliary markers (KRT19, SOX9, HNF1B, CFTR) (Fig. 3b-c) but exhibited distinct pathway profiles, highlighting the heterogeneity of this population in terms of differential functional specification (Fig S6), as previously reported26 .

The hepatic progenitor cluster occupied an intermediate position between hepatocyte and cholangiocyte clusters and was subdivided into three subclusters (Fig. S4). Their localisation and differential expression of hepatic specification genes defined a dynamic developmental trajectory, starting from bipotential hepatic progenitors characterised by SOX2 and EPCAM co-expressed with KRT19 and CLDN3, to a subcluster co-expressing hepatocyte and biliary transcription factors (HNF4A, HNF1B) with AFP and KRT8/KRT18 resembling bipotent hepatoblasts27 (Fig. 3a-b; Fig. S7).

The endothelial cluster partitioned into an endothelial subcluster (Fig. S4) expressing representative endothelial markers (PECAM1, CDH5, KDR, VWF, ICAM2, CLDN5) and a liver sinusoidal endothelial cell (LSEC)-like subcluster exhibiting a sinusoidal signature with high OIT3, LYVE1, FCGR2B, STAB1 and FGF23, consistent with recent studies28,29 (Fig. 3a-b; Fig. S6).

The hepatic stellate cluster displayed a classical stellate-cell core programme (LUM, DCN, COL1A1, DES, VIM, PDGFRβ, GATA4) (Fig. 3a-b) and was divided into a quiescent HSC-like subcluster, expressing niche and trophic factors (ITGA8, PTN, IGFBP7, EDNRA), and an activated HSC subcluster, characterised by up-regulation of profibrotic mediators (ACTA2, IL1B, DIO3, TNFAIP6, TFPI2)20 (Fig. S4 and S6).

A small cluster, annotated as neural off-target, occupied an intermediate position between hepatic progenitor and HSC clusters in the UMAP (Fig. 3a). Off-target populations with similar transcriptional profiles have been described in several endoderm-derived organoids24,30.

In-depth characterisation of hepatic multilineage organoids

We further characterised the main cell clusters identified by scRNA-seq through protein expression analysis. In addition, given the final goal of providing a scalable and clinically compatible cell source for regenerative medicine, we performed a parallel analysis in hepatic multilineage organoids derived from two previously generated hypoimmunogenic iPSC lines (M HYPO and 19L HYPO), having confirmed that the gene editing process did not affect their differentiation capacity towards HE (Fig. S1c), ECs and HSCs10. Immunofluorescence on paraffin-embedded sections and RT-qPCR revealed strong expression of HE, HSC and EC markers, thereby corroborating the cellular identity of these populations within the organoids. Both wild type (WT)- (Fig. 4) and hypoimmunogenic-derived (Fig. S8 and S9) HMos shared a similar cellular composition profile, underscoring the robustness and reproducibility of our differentiation protocol.

Hepatic stellate-like cells, identified by the expression of desmin (DES), VIM (Fig. 4, stellate cells) and PDGFRβ (Fig. 4, overview), formed a peripheral shell around the organoids. Notably, we did not detect the expression of the activated HSC marker ACTA2 (data not shown), suggesting that the activated HSC signature observed by scRNA-seq may reflect an early transcriptional priming state rather than a fully established pro-fibrotic phenotype.

Endothelial-like cells expressing CDH5, platelet and endothelial cell adhesion molecule 1 (PECAM1) (Fig. 4, endothelial cells; Supplementary Video) and VWF (data not shown) were distributed throughout the organoids, establishing a complex vascular-like network. We assessed the functionality of the endothelial network in HMo in vitro by live perfusion of rhodamine-conjugated Ulex europaeus agglutinin I (UEA-I), which progressively spread into the organoid and accumulated within endothelial branches (Fig. 5a-b). Subsequent whole-mount immunofluorescence confirmed that UEA-I-rhodamine signals overlapped with CDH5+ vascular structures (Fig. 5c), indicating successful perfusion of the endothelial network.

The HE derivatives represented the main population in the organoids, spanning from the centre to the outer edges. HLCs expressed key hepatic markers, including A1AT, ALB and HNF4α (Fig. 4, hepatocytes). Additionally, cholangiocytes positive for cytokeratin 19 (KRT19), SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9) and zonula occludens-1 (ZO1) were observed, organised into tubular-like structures resembling biliary ducts. These cells also express ADP-ribosylation factor-like protein 13B (ARL13B) on the apical membrane, indicative of the presence of primary cilia, organelles found on functional cholangiocytes. Alcian Blue/PAS staining revealed glycogen accumulation in hepatocyte cytoplasm and mucopolysaccharides deposition within the luminal epithelium of the organoids, consistent with cholangiocyte mucin production (Fig. 4, cholangiocytes), matching the profile of one of the cholangiocyte subclusters identified by scRNA-seq (Fig. S6; Cholangiocytes-3: TFF1, TFF2, MUC5AC, MUC5B, CLDN18, VSIG1, UPK1B, MSMB). Epithelial cadherin (ECAD; Fig. 4, overview) was expressed by both hepatocyte-like and cholangiocyte-like cells.

Surprisingly, while staining endothelial cells for vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2; kinase insert domain receptor, KDR), we observed a pronounced expression in a subset of KRT19+ but PECAM1- cells (Fig. 4, VEGFR2+ hepatic precursor; Fig. S10), likely corresponding to previously described bipotent endoderm-derived hepatic progenitors16,31.

Finally, a small number of cluster of differentiation 68 (CD68) positive cells were detected, likely representing the liver-resident macrophages known as Kupffer cells (Fig. 4, Kupffer cells), although they were not identified in the scRNA-seq analysis.

At the protein level, organoids did not express pluripotency markers (OCT3/4, NANOG, TRA-1–60, TRA-1–81), but contained a small SOX2+ KRT19+ population resembling hepatic progenitors (data not shown), confirming the scRNA-seq data (Fig. S7).

Electron microscopy further confirmed the identity and ultrastructural features of the distinct lineages within the organoids. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed large, polyhedral cells with centrally located round nuclei (occasionally binucleated), peripheral chromatin and prominent nucleoli. Their cytoplasm was rich in organelles and exhibited dense microvilli on one side, indicative of polarised organisation, consistent with HLCs32. Duct-like structures composed of cuboidal cells with luminal microvilli suggested the presence of cholangiocyte-like cells, while hepatic stellate-like cells were identified based on round-to-oval nuclei and abundant cytoplasmic lipid droplets (Fig. 6a)33,34. SEM imaging showed HLCs with a spherical shape and dense surface microvilli. Additionally, cells with irregular, flattened morphology and surface ruffling were observed near intercellular junctions, likely representing the Kupffer-like cells (Fig. 6b). Both TEM (Fig. 7a-a’’) and SEM (Fig. 7b-b’’) analyses revealed the presence of ECs with fenestrations lining capillary-like structures. These cells exhibited thin cytoplasmic profiles and membrane discontinuities, consistent with endothelial fenestrae typical of liver sinusoidal endothelial cells35. LSEC identity was further supported by the expression of lymphatic vessel endothelial hyaluronan receptor 1 (LYVE1)36, which was distributed throughout the organoid (Fig. 7c), and by the secretion of factor VIII protein (Fig. 7d).

After scRNA-seq low-resolution data reclustering of the endothelial compartment, we identified five subclusters arranged along a smooth trajectory (Fig. 7e-f). The central subcluster (endo5) exhibited the lowest KDR and GPR182 expression, consistent with a common endothelial progenitor state (Fig. 7g), and gave rise to two diverging branches: one leading to GPR182-high LSEC-like and the other to KDR-high angiogenic vascular endothelium-like population. Pseudotime analysis revealed progressive activation of lineage-specific transcriptional programmes along each branch, while cell-fate entropy peaked centrally and declined towards both poles (Fig. 7h), indicating a plastic endothelial progenitor population that gradually differentiates into either LSEC-like or vascular endothelial cell-like fates.

Functionality of iPSC-derived hepatic multilineage organoids

A comprehensive set of assays was performed to assess the functional maturity of the HMos (Fig. 8). ELISA confirmed the secretion of albumin, ADAMTS13 and complement factor H, with some expected clone-dependent differences. Albumin levels were comparable among clones, confirming a similar degree of hepatocyte differentiation and maturity, although levels tend to decrease in HMo-M HYPO. Similarly, the expression of factor H, which is secreted by hepatocytes, was similar in HMo-WT and HMo-19L HYPO but significantly lower in HMo-M HYPO. In contrast, ADAMTS13, derived from HSCs, was more abundant in HMo-M HYPO. As WT and M HYPO are isogenic, these discrepancies likely reflect differences in the ratio of hepatocytes to HSCs, resulting from the labour-intensive multistep differentiation process.

Metabolic competence was demonstrated by urea detection, indicating active ammonia detoxification and functional integration of the urea cycle, both hallmarks of mature hepatocyte activity37 and consistent with ARG1 and GLUL expression in the scRNA-seq data (Fig. 3b). HMo-19L HYPO exhibited higher urea production than HMo-WT and HMo-M HYPO, possibly due to genetic background differences or intra-experimental variability. Furthermore, apolipoproteins (APO) multiplex analysis revealed substantial production of APOA1, APOB and APOC3, consistent with scRNA-seq results (Fig. 3b) and reflecting the activation of hepatocyte-specific lipid metabolic pathways. APOA1 and APOC3 were similarly expressed, whereas APOB displayed variable levels among clones.

The organoids also expressed CYP2D6, CYP4A11, CYP2J2 and CYP4F12, which are among the most abundant cytochrome P450 enzymes responsible for detoxification of exogenous compounds in the liver (Fig. S11)38.

Discussion

We report the generation of vascularised, multilineage hepatic organoids from fully competent hypoimmunogenic iPSCs, offering a universal and scalable platform for disease modelling and cell-based therapy. Our system uniquely integrates parenchymal and non-parenchymal components, including hepatocytes, cholangiocytes, hepatic stellate cells, Kupffer cells and both vascular and sinusoidal endothelial cells. This multicellular approach recapitulates the complex hepatic microenvironment, supporting cell survival, functional maturation and three-dimensional tissue organisation8,14,39,40.

Hepatic stellate cells organised peripherally into a DES⁺ PDGFRβ⁺ shell, whereas endothelial cells formed CDH5⁺ networks that reflect the vascular architecture. VEGF supplementation promoted EC survival and integration, driving endothelial expansion throughout the organoid and extending the CDH5⁺ network to the periphery. One striking feature of our organoids is the presence of fenestrated endothelial cells, a hallmark of liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, observed by electron microscopy and confirmed by transcriptomic analysis, LYVE1 protein expression and factor VIII secretion. Within this 3D multicellular environment, hepatic endoderm-derived cells acquire a mature phenotype, expressing hepatocyte markers such as albumin, A1AT and ASGPR111. We also observed tubular structures surrounded by SOX9⁺, KRT19⁺ and ZO1⁺ cholangiocyte-like cells displaying apicobasal polarity, consistent with early bile duct morphogenesis41,42 and the shared embryonic origin of hepatocytes and cholangiocytes. Immunostaining revealed ARL13B+ primary cilia on these cells, confirming their identity and active ciliogenesis, a key feature of ductal epithelial function and bile flow regulation43,44. A less mature population co-expressing hepatocyte and cholangiocyte markers likely represents hepatoblasts, which are bipotent progenitors derived from the endoderm and capable of differentiating into both lineages45. Moreover, a KRT19⁺ VEGFR2⁺ cell population emerged, likely resembling the endoderm-derived intermediate progenitors previously described16,31, underscoring the organoids’ intrinsic capacity to recapitulate developmental processes and highlighting lineage plasticity during liver morphogenesis. The VEGFR2+ progenitors are known to support hepatic maturation and may give rise to hepatoblasts, hepatocytes, cholangiocytes and sinusoidal endothelial cells, perhaps explaining the observed fenestrated phenotype. The co-expression of endodermal and endothelial markers raises the possibility that some LSEC-like cells derive from hepatic endodermal progenitors, as demonstrated by previous reports46,47. Nevertheless, scRNA-seq trajectory reconstruction suggested LSEC specification from endothelial progenitors under the influence of the hepatic microenvironment. Future lineage-tracing or live-imaging approaches may resolve the precise origin of these cells. The spontaneous emergence of CD68⁺ cells, likely representing Kupffer-like macrophages, further increases the physiological fidelity of our model48 and enhances its relevance for disease modelling, especially in the context of chronic inflammation, fibrosis and viral infection49.

Single-cell RNA sequencing provided a closer look into the identity of the cell types comprising the hepatic organoids, revealing spatial hepatocyte zonation and multiple transcriptionally distinct cholangiocyte subgroups not detected by other analytical approaches. The dataset also identified coexisting populations at multiple maturation stages, consistent with the progressive differentiation that occurs during liver development. The presence of trajectories linking stem-like, liver-committed progenitors to more terminally differentiated hepatocyte- and cholangiocyte-like states suggests that the organoids retain developmental plasticity and that the progenitors likely sustain organoid regenerative capacity, partially confirming the immunofluorescence analysis. Incomplete maturity is a limitation of iPSC-derived organoids that remains unsolved50,51. Nonetheless, the presence of hepatoblast-like progenitors may be beneficial for regenerative applications, as they could support post-transplant maturation and long-term tissue maintenance, ensuring functional replacement continuity in a single administration therapy.

In the scRNA-seq dataset, endothelial cells and hepatocytes appeared to be among the least represented populations. However, immunostaining and RT-qPCR analyses demonstrated that both lineages were abundant in the organoids. This discrepancy could reflect suboptimal dissociation efficiency, as strong cell-cell and cell-matrix adhesions can reduce the recovery of these populations during the complex sample processing required for single-cell profiling. Similarly, already scarce Kupffer-like macrophages were further depleted. As a result, the scRNA-seq dataset probably underestimates these lineages and overestimates others, a potential limitation to consider when interpreting the data. Future studies should employ optimised dissociation protocols or alternative approaches, such as single-nucleus RNA sequencing or spatial transcriptomics, to achieve a more accurate representation of organoid composition 52.

The cellular complexity of our organoids enhanced hepatic functional maturation, as evidenced by the synthesis of urea and secretion of liver-specific proteins, including albumin and apolipoproteins. Notably, APOA1, APOB and APOC3 expression is relevant for modelling lipid-related diseases such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease and hyperlipidaemia, offering new opportunities for drug screening and therapeutic discovery. Our organoids also secrete clinically relevant plasmatic proteins, including ADAMTS13, factor H and factor VIII. ADAMTS13 prevents microthrombus formation and its deficiency causes thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, a rare and life-threatening condition. Factor H, a central regulator of the complement system, is implicated in haemolytic uremic syndrome and other complement-mediated diseases, while factor VIII deficiency leads to impaired thrombin generation and spontaneous bleeding episodes. These conditions lack effective, side effect-free therapies and could benefit from a cell source that engrafts and secretes functional proteins, as small increases in plasma levels substantially improve clinical symptoms. Recent studies showed that liver organoids incorporating iPSC-derived liver sinusoidal endothelial cells produced factor VIII and corrected the bleeding phenotype in haemophilic mice for 2–8 weeks50,53. This further supports the therapeutic potential of our organoids in continuous plasma protein secretion for hepatic metabolic defects and highlights hypoimmunogenicity as an added advantage over previous models.

By coupling organoid technology with hypoimmunogenic engineering, our approach aims to enable off-the-shelf applications across multiple recipients without HLA matching or lifelong immunosuppression. To date, hypoimmunogenic derivatives have been tested in a single phase I clinical trial (VCTX210, completion planned in 2025) for the treatment of type 1 diabetes using iPSC-derived pancreatic islets54, supporting the translational potential of this strategy for hepatic applications. The first hypoimmunogenic iPSC-derived liver organoids were recently integrated into an extracorporeal bioartificial liver system that improved inflammation, liver function and regeneration in two rat models of severe liver failure55. However, that model is unsuitable for congenital metabolic defects because it requires repeated infusions and consists mainly of hepatocytes, whereas our organoids are vascularised, multilineage and more functionally complex. Additionally, the functionality of the endothelial network was demonstrated by live perfusion of a rhodamine-conjugated, endothelial-specific agglutinin, revealing well distributed microvessels within HMos that are crucial for facilitating the delivery of nutrients, oxygen and growth factors from culture medium into the core of organoids. This finding holds great translational potential because it may enable a successful engraftment through the connection of the HMo endothelial network to host vessels. While promising for metabolic liver defects, our protocol requires further procedural implementation to enable cost-effective, industrial-scale production of uniform organoids.

In conclusion, our hypoimmunogenic, vascularised hepatic organoids represent a significant advancement towards the correction of metabolic liver diseases and future applications in organ replacement for ESLD. By combining developmental complexity, functional protein production and immune-evasive properties, they offer a scalable, off-the-shelf platform for disease modelling and cell therapy.

Materials and methods

iPSC culture and differentiation

We employed iPSCs derived from Normal Human Dermal Fibroblasts-Neonatal (Lonza) (WT, IRFMNi001-B) and its subclone double-knockout for CIITA and B2M (M HYPO, IRFMNi001-B-210). We also used another iPSC double-knockout clone (19L HYPO, IRFMNi009-A-8; Synthego) derived from human endothelial progenitor cells10. Cells were maintained on Matrigel hESC-Qualified Matrix (Corning) in mTeSR Plus medium (STEMCELL Technologies) with 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), detached twice a week using StemPro Accutase (Gibco) and plated at a density of 20,000–60,000 cells/cm2. On the day of seeding, 10 µM Y-27632 (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the medium. Mycoplasma testing was performed routinely using N-GARDE Mycoplasma Detection PCR Kit (Euroclone). The iPSCs were used within ten passages following karyotyping. The iPSCs were differentiated into hepatic stellate-like, endothelial‑like and hepatic endoderm‑like cells according to established protocols15,39,56 (Fig. S1). Cells were cryopreserved in HyClone defined foetal bovine serum (Cytiva) plus 10% DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich) and 10 µM Y-27632.

Generation and culture of organoids

To generate organoids, fresh or thawed iPSC-derived HE, ECs and HSCs were dissociated into single-cell suspensions and then seeded together at a 3:6:1 ratio (5000 total cells/well) in 96-well round-bottom ultra-low attachment plates (Twin Helix) and centrifuged at 200 x g for 2 min. The organoids were cultured for 16 days, corresponding to the time required for hepatic endoderm differentiation into hepatocyte-like cells. The medium was changed every other day and consisted of half Hepatocyte Growth Medium (HGM; Lonza) and half Endothelial Cell Growth Medium-2 (EGM-2; Lonza) supplemented with 20 ng/mL hepatocyte growth factor (HGF; Proteintech), 10 ng/mL oncostatin M (Proteintech), 100 nM dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich), 1 µM A83-01 (Sigma-Aldrich) and 50 ng/mL VEGF-165 (PeproTech). On days 1 and 7, 0.283 mg/mL Cultrex UltiMatrix Reduced Growth Factor Basement Membrane Extract (R&D) was added.

Single-cell RNA sequencing processing

Organoids (n = 4) were dissociated using 800 U/ml collagenase type IV (Gibco) in 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Merck) for 30 min on an orbital shaker at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Single-cell suspensions were loaded onto the Chromium X platform (10x Genomics) to capture 10,000 cells using the Chromium GEM-X Single Cell 3′ v4 kit (10x Genomics). cDNA amplification and library construction followed the standardised protocol. Libraries were assessed for quality and quantity using the 4200 TapeStation System (Agilent Technologies) and Qubit (ThermoFisher), respectively. Then, samples were pooled and sequenced on a NextSeq 2000 platform (Illumina) according to 10x Genomics recommended read lengths. Raw 10x Genomics single-cell 3’ BCL files were demultiplexed with Illumina BCL Convert using the manufacturer’s sample sheet and default settings with lane merging. FASTQ files were processed with Cell Ranger (10x Genomics, v9.0.1), aligning reads to GRCh38 with default parameters, yielding filtered feature-barcode matrices per organoid. Downstream analyses were performed in R using Seurat. For each sample, the filtered feature-barcode matrix was imported into a Seurat object, retaining cells with sufficient gene complexity and removing cells with low gene counts, extreme library size or gene complexity, or high mitochondrial and ribosomal content, using sample-specific robust thresholds (median + 3 median absolute deviations) and absolute caps of 30% and 70% of reads, respectively. Putative doublets were removed with scDblFinder (assuming a 5% doublet rate), retaining only cells classified as singlets; cell barcodes were prefixed with sample IDs and the sample of origin was stored as metadata. Doublet-filtered samples were merged into a single Seurat object. Counts were log-normalised (scale factor 10,000), highly variable genes were selected with the “vst” method and data were scaled while regressing out mitochondrial RNA content and S/G2M cell-cycle scores. PCA was performed on highly variable genes and Harmony was applied to the PCA space (SeuratWrappers) using the sample of origin as batch covariate to obtain a batch-corrected low-dimensional embedding. UMAP, a shared nearest-neighbour graph and Louvain clustering were computed on the Harmony embedding. A range of resolutions was evaluated with Clustree, and a resolution of 0.4 was selected for downstream analyses. Cluster markers were identified with FindAllMarkers (Wilcoxon test, positive markers only) (Supplementary information scRNA-seq, Supplementary cluster markers) and clusters were annotated into major lineages (hepatocytes, cholangiocytes, hepatic progenitors, endothelial cells, hepatic stellate cells and neural off-target) and finer subclusters based on canonical markers, curated gene signatures and UMAP topology.

Endothelial subclustering and trajectory analysis

Endothelial cells were defined from the global lineage annotation as clusters assigned to sinusoidal/LSEC-like or perivascular/tip-angiogenic endothelium; obvious contaminants and peripheral cells were removed by manual UMAP gating. The curated endothelial subset was then reprocessed with the same normalisation and dimensionality-reduction workflow used for the full dataset and low-resolution Louvain clustering yielded a small number of endothelial subclusters for trajectory analysis. Endothelial trajectories were inferred with Palantir on the PCA embedding: diffusion maps were computed, pseudotime was initialised from the endothelial cell with the lowest combined KDR and GPR182 expression (least lineage-committed state) and branch probabilities towards putative terminal states were obtained. For each cell, a cell-fate entropy score (Shannon entropy of branch probabilities) quantified the degree of fate commitment, with higher entropy indicating more plastic and lower entropy more fate-restricted endothelial states.

Hepatocyte subset and zonation analysis

Hepatocytes were refined by manual gating on the integrated UMAP to retain cells within the core hepatocyte manifold and reprocessed with the same normalisation and covariate-regression workflow used for the full dataset. Periportal and pericentral gene signatures were derived from published liver zonation data25 (Supplementary information scRNA-seq, Supplementary zonation signatures) by selecting genes significantly enriched in periportal versus pericentral zones and vice versa; top-ranked genes were mapped to human orthologues to obtain periportal and pericentral gene sets. For each hepatocyte, Seurat module scores were computed for both signatures, and a zonation index (ZonationIndex) was defined as the z-scored difference between pericentral and periportal scores, with positive values indicating a more pericentral-like and negative values a more periportal-like profile. In parallel, a data-driven zonation axis was obtained by PCA on hepatocytes, using the first principal component (PC1) as the dominant transcriptional gradient. PC1 was oriented to correlate positively with the pericentral score, centred and stored as Zonation_PC1, providing an independent continuous zonation coordinate used alongside ZonationIndex.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from 2D-cultured cells (≥ 106) using TRIzol (Invitrogen) and from spheroids (≥ 20) using the PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen). cDNA was synthesised from 1.5 µg RNA using the SuperScript VILO Kit (Invitrogen). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) and gene-specific primers (Table S1) on a ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Gene expression levels were normalised to the housekeeping gene hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HPRT) and reported as fold change.

Immunofluorescence and histological staining

2D-cultured cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Thermo Fisher Scientifics) for 15 min at room temperature (RT), washed with Phosphate Buffered Saline (Gibco), permeabilised with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) and blocked in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies in 2% BSA, followed by a 1-hour incubation at RT with secondary antibodies. Whole-mount organoid immunofluorescence was performed after fixation in 4% PFA for 30 min at RT following a previously described protocol57,58 with minor changes in incubation times. Briefly, organoids were dehydrated in ethanol pH 9 and cleared with ethyl cinnamate (Sigma-Aldrich)59. Alternatively, immunofluorescence was performed on 3 μm paraffin sections of formalin-fixed organoids. After dewaxing, antigen retrieval was carried out with Diva Decloaker (Biocare Medical) in a Decloaking Chamber NxGen (Biocare Medical) at 110 °C for 15 min. Sections were blocked in 1% BSA for 30 min, incubated with primary antibodies for 2 h at RT and then with secondary antibodies for 1 h. Slides were mounted with Fluorescence Mounting Medium (Dako). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Antibodies are listed in Table S2. For histological examination, sections were stained with Alcian blue/Periodic Acid-Shiff (PAS). Imaging was performed on a TCS SP8 microscope (Leica Microsystems) or an Axio Observer Z1 fluorescence microscope (ZEISS).

Live perfusion of organoids

Live perfusion of organoids was performed on a confocal scan unit (Nikon A1). After a 20-minute incubation at 37 °C with Hoechst (Invitrogen) in organoid medium, live organoids were mounted on the microscope stage and maintained at 37 °C, 5% CO2 and 90% humidity in a feedback-controlled microscope incubator (Okolab H-301). Images were acquired at baseline and 2 min after the addition of 20 µg/mL of rhodamine-conjugated Ulex Europaeus Agglutinin I (Vector Laboratory). Time-lapse imaging was done with a 5-minute time frame for 20 min to follow progressive UEA-I-rhodamine staining. Digital image analysis was performed using ImageJ. Briefly, a mask including the organoid volume was generated, fluorescence variation (ΔF) relative to baseline (F0) was calculated and expressed as ΔF/F0.

Hepatic biomarker analysis in organoid-conditioned medium

Organoid-conditioned medium was collected 48 h after the last medium change (day 16).

Albumin, ADAMTS13, factor VIII and urea levels were measured using the Human Albumin ELISA Kit (Abcam), Human ADAMTS13 ELISA kit (Abcam), Human Factor VIII ELISA kit (Cabru) and QuantiChrom Urea Assay Kit (CliniSciences), respectively, on an Infinite M200 PRO microplate reader (Tecan). APOA1, APOC3 and APOB were quantified using the Luminex xMAP-based immunoassay Milliplex Human Apolipoprotein Magnetic Bead Panel (Millipore) on a Bio-Plex 200 System (Bio-Rad). Factor H was quantified using an in-house sandwich ELISA60.

Results were normalised to total protein content, extracted through mechanical homogenisation with an Ultra-Turrax homogeniser (IKA) in cold RIPA extraction buffer and quantified by bicinchoninic acid assay (Pierce).

Electron microscopy

For transmission electron microscopy , organoids were fixed for 1 h in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer pH 7.4 (Electron Microscopy Sciences) and washed repeatedly in the same buffer. After fixation in 1% OsO4, samples were dehydrated in graded alcohol and embedded in Epon resin. Ultrathin sections were stained with UranyLess and lead citrate (Electron Microscopy Sciences) and imaged using a Morgagni 268D microscope (Philips).

For scanning electron microscopy , organoids were processed as above, dried in hexamethyldisilazane (Sigma-Aldrich), mounted on stubs, gold-coated (Agar Scientific) and imaged using a GeminiSEM 360 (ZEISS) (acceleration voltage 0.8–2.0 kV, magnification up to 35 K X, working distance 5.0–9.0 mm). After surface observation, organoids were fractured for internal structure analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were obtained from at least three independent experiments and analysed using GraphPad Prism software with one-way ANOVA followed by the Bonferroni test for multiple comparisons or unpaired Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons, as appropriate. Results are reported as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.
