Fine-grained avian wildlife species classification: a comparative study of MambaVision, vision transformers, and CNN architectures
Authors: Rhythm Kulsrrestha, Chiranjit Pal, Rahul Jain, Raj Jaiswal, Arvinder Kaur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bird species classification is a critical task in ecological research and conservation, enabling accurate monitoring of biodiversity and environmental health. This study evaluates seven advanced vision transformer models and five contemporary convolutional neural networks (CNN)-based models that were analysed using BIRDS-525 dataset of around 90,000 images of 525 distinct avian classes. The models are assessed based on accuracy, F1-score, and inference time. Vision Transformers, particularly MambaVision and ViT enhanced through fine-tuning, demonstrated superior accuracy and efficiency, with MambaVision achieving the highest nominal test accuracy of 99.47%; however, statistical analysis indicates that its performance is not significantly different from other top-performing models such as DenseNet201 and Vision Transformer (ViT) after Bonferroni correction. Rigorous statistical validation using pairwise t-tests and Chi-squared (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi^2$$\end{document}) tests identified a statistically indistinguishable top-performing cluster of nine architectures after Bonferroni correction, with only AlexNet and ShuffleNetV2 forming a significantly inferior bottom tier. A comprehensive comparative analysis of various deep learning approaches is also presented, evaluating their performance on the benchmark BIRDS-525 dataset against several old as well as recent state-of-the-art methodologies. The top five models are further evaluated on a custom dataset- Indian Bird Species Dataset (IBSD) consisting of total 361 images across five species of birds created by capturing images of locally available bird species in a real-time environment. The dataset includes noisy and cluttered images, and the models’ performance in these conditions provides preliminary insights into generalization under realistic capture conditions, with ViT showing the strongest overall real-world performance. The findings provide valuable insights into optimizing both CNN-based and transformer-based models for bird species identification, contributing to biodiversity monitoring and conservation efforts.

Introduction

Birds, which have existed for over 150 million years, are not only crucial to ecosystems but also play a significant role in conservation, pest control, pollination, and seed dispersal [1]. With over 10,700 described species worldwide [2], their ecological importance extends to advancing biodiversity preservation efforts and environmental sustainability.

Deep learning has significantly advanced image classification, with CNN-based approaches demonstrating increasingly strong results on large-scale benchmarks [3, 4]. A comprehensive review of prior work on avian species classification is presented in Sect. 2. However, these models often face challenges in distinguishing subtle interspecies variations within complex datasets [5]. Many approaches focus heavily on accuracy without considering inference efficiency, model scalability, or generalization to real-world field images. Moreover, few works have systematically compared a diverse range of models under uniform training conditions.

The limitations in handling complex models, huge datasets or challenging tasks of birds species classification are effectively mitigated through transfer learning, an approach leveraging pre-existing model weights from large-scale datasets to achieve gains in classification accuracy coupled with efficiencies in training time [6]. This technique not only improves performance on challenging datasets but also enables efficient learning with limited data. Additionally, the integration of emerging architectures like Vision Transformers (ViTs) offers further advancements by capturing long-range dependencies and excelling in fine-grained classification tasks. These AI-driven methodologies, including CNN-based pretrained models and ViTs, have revolutionized biodiversity monitoring. The cutting edge models like ViTs leverage for identifying and representing global relationships within the image data, making them ideal for tasks like bird species identification, which require understanding complex visual patterns [7].

The current study evaluates several advanced ViT models, such as Pyramid Vision Transformers (PVT), Data-Efficient Transformers (DeiT), SWIN Vision Transformers (SVT), Hierarchical Vision Transformers (Hiera), and SegFormer, applied to large-scale bird species classification datasets [8]. These architectures are recognized for their favorable scaling properties and operational efficiency, particularly when processing high-dimensional visual data, which renders them highly appropriate for identifying bird species across heterogeneous environments [9].

In addition to ViTs, State Space Models (SSMs) like Mamba provide an innovative approach to handling long-range dependencies in data [10]. Mamba achieves linear time complexity through its unique state-space formulation, offering significant advantages for processing sequential and spatial data. Although initially designed for sequential tasks, Mamba has been adapted for vision tasks by incorporating bidirectional encoding to capture spatial context [11, 12]. Furthermore, hybrid architectures like MambaVision integrate CNN layers for efficient early-stage feature extraction, and combine SSM and self-attention mechanisms in deeper layers, enabling the model to manage both short and long-range dependencies in image data. MambaVision tackles the issue of intensive processing requirements inherent in traditional attention layers through its specific architectural design [13, 14]. This design makes MambaVision a strong candidate for tasks like bird species classification, where diverse visual cues across multiple spatial scales are relevant, though its comparative advantages over other modern architectures are empirically evaluated in this study.

The present research assesses the effectiveness and computational efficiency of multifarious CNN-based models employing transfer learning, transformer-based architectures, and SSMs. We evaluate these fine-tuned models on a custom dataset as a pilot real-world validation to assess generalization under challenging capture conditions. Through a systematic analysis of ViT and CNN models, this study aims to identify the points of efficacy and areas requiring improvement of each approach, establishing the groundwork for subsequent progress in automated bird species detection.

Problem statement and contributions

Bird species classification in wildlife environments is challenging due to factors like inconsistent lighting, varying image quality, visual similarities between species, and complex natural backgrounds. Manual identification is labor-intensive and inefficient for large-scale studies. Automated systems struggle with issues such as camouflage, noisy environments, and dynamic bird movements, further complicating accurate detection. Earlier CNN architectures, particularly those without skip connections or multi-scale feature aggregation, are limited in capturing global contextual features, which can impact performance on fine-grained classification tasks where both local texture and global structure are discriminative.

The introduction of ViTs and SSMs has addressed these challenges by effectively capturing long-range dependencies and recognizing intricate patterns. ViTs, through self-attention, excel in fine-grained classification, while SSMs, such as the Mamba model, handle long-range dependencies with linear-time computational complexity. We note that the data-efficiency claim for SSMs is an architectural property; in this study, all models were fine-tuned on the full 84,635-image training set using transfer learning from ImageNet-1K pre-trained weights. This study employs transfer learning with these advanced models, utilizing the BIRDS-525 dataset [15] to account for diverse environmental conditions. The focus is on recognizing bird species in complex visual settings, with extended testing on a custom dataset of selected species. The goal is focused on the development of an intelligent system for bird species recognition that supports conservation, ecological research, and biodiversity preservation efforts. Literature survey

Recently, considerable research focus has been directed towards applying deep learning approaches for the task of classifying avian species, showcasing impressive results in feature extraction and classification across diverse datasets [1]. Researchers have explored various approaches to enhance classification accuracy, tackle dataset imbalance, and improve performance on fine-grained classification tasks. For instance, using the Caltech-UCSD dataset [1], employed Deep CNNs to achieve 89% accuracy by converting images to grayscale and leveraging TensorFlow for feature extraction. Similarly [16], evaluated four transfer learning architectures—ResNet152V2, InceptionV3, DenseNet201, and MobileNetV2—on a dataset of 58,388 images representing 400 classes of birds. ResNet152V2 outperformed all the models under consideration, registering a peak accuracy value of 95.45%, while DenseNet201 demonstrated a balanced performance with 95.09% accuracy and lower loss.In fine-grained classification [17], specifically targeted bird populations found exclusively in Taiwan through the application of the Inception-ResNet-V2 deep learning model. A unique data-swapping technique enhanced the model’s accuracy from 97.11% to 98.39%. Likewise [18], utilized CNNs to extract shape and color features, achieving 93% accuracy. Another study developed a CNN-based model to classify 20 bird species from 9,490 images, reaching an impressive accuracy of 98.75% [19]. Large-scale bird classification efforts have also been notable. Similarly [20], applied CNNs to classify endangered bird species, achieving 98% accuracy through extensive hyperparameter tuning and data augmentation.

Transfer learning has proven highly effective for smaller datasets. [21] achieved 98%-100% accuracy on the Kaggle-180-birds dataset by employing pre-trained models. Advanced techniques like Single Image Super-Resolution (SISR) have further improved bird detection in low-resolution images, such as those captured by drones. SISR enhances image clarity by reconstructing high-resolution images from low-quality inputs, making it invaluable for aerial imaging tasks [22]. Object detection frameworks like Faster R-CNN and YOLO have also contributed to real-time bird detection. [23] employed the YOLOv5 object detection algorithm for robust bird localization, followed by deep transfer learning models (VGG19, InceptionV3, EfficientNetB3) for species classification on the BIRDS-525. The EfficientNetB3 model demonstrated excellent performance, achieving a test accuracy of 98% and an F1-score of 98% on the BIRDS-525. [24] utilized a CNN-based on the EfficientNetB0 architecture with transfer learning and data augmentation to classify images of BIRDS-525. The model achieved approximately 87% validation accuracy and 86.7% test accuracy, with a weighted average F1-score of 0.866. [25] proposed a Weighted Average Ensemble (WAE) technique combining EfficientNetB5 and VGG19, selected from a pool of five deep learning models, for bird species classification on the BIRDS-525. Their WAE model achieved a testing accuracy of 97.18% and an F1-score of 97.10%.

[26] analyzed the performance of ResNet50, MobileNetV3, EfficientNet-B0, and Wide-ResNet50V2 for multiclass bird species identification using the BIRDS-525. EfficientNet-B0 outperformed the others, achieving a testing accuracy of 99.09%, with a precision of 99.08%, recall of 100%, and F1-score of 99.54%. [27] developed a Class Attention based Deep Bird species detection network (CADepBnet), which integrates Adaptive Graphs of Target Relations (AGTR) as a Class Attention Layer (CAL) into Inception-ResNet-V2. On the BIRDS-525, the CADepBnet model reported an accuracy of 99.18%, with a precision of 99.09%, recall of 99.21%, and F-measure of 99.15%. [28] applied a CNN model termed the ”Eagle-Eye vision engine” for bird species classification, evaluating it on the BIRDS-525 with 84,635 samples. Their methodology involved pre-processing, data augmentation, and transfer learning, resulting in a validation accuracy of 88.00% and an overall accuracy of 89%. [29] developed a skip connection-oriented Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to enhance feature extraction for bird classification. Evaluating their model on a subset of the BIRDS-525 dataset, they achieved an accuracy of 92%, which was an improvement over a baseline CNN model. [30] developed a new efficient classifier for bird classification based on transfer learning, specifically using the EfficientNetB5 model with a two-phase training process on the BIRDS-525. Their optimized model obtained an accuracy of 98.86% and an F1-score of 99% on this dataset.

While existing studies have demonstrated high accuracy in bird species classification using both CNN-based and transformer-based models, several challenges remain unaddressed. Many approaches focus heavily on accuracy without considering inference efficiency, model scalability, or generalization to real-world field images. Moreover, few works have systematically compared a diverse range of models under uniform training conditions. This study addresses these gaps by providing a rigorous comparative analysis of twelve models, including recent architectures like MambaVision, under consistent hyperparameter settings. Additionally, this work emphasizes inference speed and evaluates generalization using a challenging custom dataset (IBSD), thereby contributing to the development of more robust and deployment-ready avian classification systems. The following sections outline the methodology and comprehensively explain our approach.

A key limitation across the reviewed literature is the lack of systematic statistical validation of performance differences. Many studies report accuracy improvements of fractions of a percent without establishing statistical significance, making it impossible to determine whether observed differences reflect genuine architectural advantages or sampling variability. Additionally, generalization to out-of-distribution real-world images remains rarely evaluated. The present study addresses both gaps by applying Bonferroni-corrected pairwise statistical tests and evaluating cross-dataset generalization using a custom locally-captured dataset.

BIRDS-525 public benchmark dataset

We use the BIRDS-525 dataset that has been sourced from [15]. It comprises a collection of a total of 89,885 images of 525 bird species. Each image is 224 × 224 pixels, in color and in jpg format. This data collection comprises a training set of 84,635 images, alongside distinct test and validation sets each containing 2,625 images. The dataset exhibits an imbalanced distribution of bird species images, with each class containing at least 130 samples. The number of images per species varies significantly, with higher counts like 205 for the Eastern Yellow Robin and 194 for the American Dipper, contrasting with lower counts of 132 for the Rock Dove and 133 for the American Goldfinch. Custom dataset

This work introduces a new dataset- Indian Bird Species Dataset (IBSD) comprising five bird species that will be used to validate the deployed models and assess their robustness in real-world environments. As illustrated in Fig. 2, IBSD includes five bird species: Crow (142 images), Myna (68 images), Ostrich (28 images), Peacock (18 images), and Rock Dove (105 images). These images are collected from local areas in New Delhi, India, such as neighbourhood parks, nearby wild regions, and the Delhi Zoological National Park, New Delhi, India. The RGB images of the birds are captured using a Realme Narzo 50i smartphone with an 8-megapixel camera and a 1600 × 720 resolution under varying environmental and lighting conditions to simulate diverse real-world scenarios. The images contain natural noise and background clutter, and this dataset is introduced as a pilot evaluation resource for assessing model robustness under realistic capture conditions. It is important to note that IBSD is relatively small (361 images total) and exhibits significant class imbalance: Crow (142 images) is the most represented class while Peacock (18 images) and Ostrich (28 images) are severely underrepresented. The dataset is also geographically limited to the New Delhi region of India. Consequently, IBSD results should be interpreted as exploratory insights into model generalization, not as definitive conclusions about real-world performance.Fig. 2Bird species in IBSD: (a) Crow; (b) Myna; (c) Ostrich; (d) Peacock; (e) Rock Dove

Data preprocessing

For the experiments conducted herein, every image is adjusted to dimensions of 224 × 224 pixels, normalized using ImageNet means and standard deviations, and randomly flipped horizontally for augmentation during training, and the batch size is kept at 32. We acknowledge that advanced augmentation strategies such as RandAugment, MixUp, CutMix, and colour jitter—which are standard in current computer vision literature for mitigating domain shift and improving robustness—were not employed in this study. This is a methodological constraint that should be considered when interpreting the observed differences in cross-dataset generalization: the performance gaps between architectures may partly reflect differential sensitivity to the absence of regularization rather than purely inherent architectural properties. Incorporating such techniques is identified as a direction for future work.

Models description

This section of the paper delineates the specific high-performance models used for the vision tasks central to this research, including transformers like ViT, DeiT, PVT, SVT, SegFormer, Hiera, and the state-space-based MambaVision. We explore the striking features of their architectures and how they are crucial for bird species classification tasks.

ViT: As detailed in [31], the ViT methodology involves initially dividing input images into fixed-size patches. These patches are subsequently handled as individual tokens within a sequence, enabling the model’s self-attention mechanisms to effectively model both local feature correlations and global contextual dependencies. It excels in data-scarce and noisy datasets, capturing intricate image details. Data-efficient Image Transformer (DeiT): DeiT uses a distillation token mechanism to improve data efficiency and accuracy in training [32]. The distillation token, alongside the class token, computes attention-based relationships with both the sequence of patch embeddings and the class token through self-attention layers. At test time, the class and distillation tokens are combined through late fusion for prediction, improving the model’s accuracy while maintaining training efficiency.

Pyramid Vision Transformer (PVT): Characterized by its hierarchical structure, PVT [33] processes visual information across multiple resolution levels. This multi-scale feature extraction capability significantly benefits performance on downstream tasks demanding dense predictions (e.g., object detection, segmentation). PVT employs a progressive shrinking strategy in its patch embedding layers to reduce spatial dimensions and construct a feature pyramid. Spatial-Reduction Attention (SRA) is used to lower computational and memory overhead. PVT offers multiple variants (Tiny, Small, Medium, and Large), balancing parameter efficiency and performance for diverse vision tasks.

Hierarchical Vision Transformer (Hiera) Hiera is a simplified hierarchical vision transformer that uses Masked Autoencoders for pretraining, designed for scalability and high performance in image and video tasks. It employs multi-scale representations across four stages, starting with high spatial resolution and progressing to deeper layers for complex features. [36]. Key innovations include Mask Unit Attention, which performs local attention within predefined mask units during Masked Autoencoder (MAE) pretraining, and multi-scale decoding for improved feature fusion. These design choices make Hiera significantly faster and more accurate than prior models.

It introduces novel hybrid architecture integrating Mamba and Transformer elements, which strategically employs (CNN) layers for efficient local feature extraction alongside self-attention mechanisms to effectively model long-range dependencies. Evaluation

The evaluation of all the considered models, on both BIRDS-525 and IBSD, involves a comprehensive analysis using several key performance metrics in order to verify both the robustness and the practical suitability of the developed models for avian species identification applications. The evaluation criteria encompassed standard classification indicators – namely accuracy, F1-score, along with inference time as a measure of computational speed. Accuracy measures the overall proportion of correct predictions. The F1-score harmonically averages precision and recall, providing a balanced metric often used for imbalanced datasets. Inference time measures the time required to process and classify test images. This includes the total time for inference and the average batch inference time. For real-world scenarios, such as real-time bird species detection in ecological conservation, minimizing inference time is essential to ensure that the model can operate efficiently and effectively under time-sensitive conditions. Model performance is evaluated using Top-1 test accuracy, macro-averaged F1-score, and inference time. We note that Top-5 accuracy, while informative for fine-grained tasks with morphologically similar species, was not reported in this study; this is identified as a minor limitation.

Results and discussions

The experiments involve fine-tuning and evaluating twelve models for bird species recognition using transfer learning on the BIRDS-525 (see 3.1.1). The top-five performing models are further validated on custom IBSD (see 3.1.2) to assess generalization. The selected models include five CNN-based architectures: AlexNet [37], ResNet50 [38], MobileNetV2 [39], ShuffleNetV2 [40], and DenseNet201 [41], and seven transformer-based architectures: ViT [31], DeiT [32], PVT [33], MambaVision [14], SVT [42], Hiera [36], and SegFormer [35].

Experimental setup

All models are fine-tuned for 20 epochs using transfer learning on the BIRDS-525 training set, which contains 84,635 images (see Sect. 3.1.1 and Sect. 3.2). Training is done on an NVIDIA RTX A4000 GPU with CUDA and mixed precision for better efficiency. We use the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 10^{-4}$$\end{document}. The learning rate is reduced every 7 epochs using the StepLR scheduler with a decay factor of 0.1. The main loss function is cross-entropy. Both training and validation losses are monitored to track progress. All twelve models are trained using the same hyperparameters to ensure a fair comparison. These fixed hyperparameters are listed in Table 2. Model performance is evaluated using test accuracy, F1-Score, and inference time. This setup enables a fair comparison between CNN-based and transformer-based architectures for bird species recognition. The experimental process is divided into two stages. In the first stage, an internal comparison is conducted to identify the best-performing model. In the second stage, the top five models are then tested on the custom IBSD dataset to assess their performance on real-world images. A detailed class-wise accuracy comparison is also presented. Implementation and reproducibility details

The specific model variants used in this study are as follows: ViT-B/16 (google/vit-base-patch16-224), SwinV2-Tiny (microsoft/swinv2-tiny-patch4-window8-256), PVT-v2-B0 (timm/pvt_v2_b0.in1k), DeiT-Tiny (facebook/deit-tiny-patch16-224), SegFormer-B0 (nvidia/mit-b0), Hiera-Tiny (facebook/hiera-tiny-224-in1k-hf), MambaVision-T, DenseNet201, ResNet50, MobileNetV2, AlexNet, and ShuffleNetV2 (default). All models were initialized with publicly available ImageNet-1K pre-trained weights via HuggingFace Transformers or the timm library. No layers were frozen during fine-tuning; all model parameters were optimized end-to-end uniformly across all twelve architectures. All results are from a single training run; multi-run variance analysis is acknowledged as a limitation and is recommended for future work (see Sect. 5). Training and validation losses were monitored across all 20 epochs for all twelve models; all models exhibited stable convergence within the training period with no indication of underfitting.

Training was performed on an NVIDIA RTX A4000 GPU (16 GB VRAM) with CUDA support and mixed precision (FP16). No learning rate warm-up was applied; the StepLR scheduler was used throughout training from epoch 1. We note that for transformer-based models, a warm-up phase is generally considered beneficial and its absence may have slightly limited convergence for some architectures; this is acknowledged as a methodological limitation. Inference time (TIT and AITB in Table 3) was measured on GPU (NVIDIA RTX A4000) with batch size 32 on the full BIRDS-525 test set (2,625 images) under identical hardware conditions for all models. No GPU warm-up period was used prior to timing. Note that SwinV2-Tiny is pre-trained at 256 × 256 resolution but fine-tuned at 224 × 224 in this study, consistent with the unified training protocol applied to all twelve models. Statistical tests

To rigorously compare the twelve models, pairwise statistical tests were performed on all 66 independent model combinations. Because the test samples were independent and shuffled across both models, paired-sample methods were inapplicable. Welch’s independent two-sample t-test was applied as the primary parametric test on the binary per-sample outcomes (correct/incorrect), which is justified by the large sample size (N = 2625) that satisfies the Central Limit Theorem for binary proportions. A Chi-squared (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi^2$$\end{document}) test of independence with Yates’ continuity correction was additionally applied as a non-parametric confirmatory test; results of both tests were in strong agreement across all 66 pairs. The null hypothesis (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_0$$\end{document}) for each pair posited equal classification accuracy, tested at α = 0.05. Given the 66 simultaneous comparisons, a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha/66 \approx 0.00076$$\end{document} was applied to control the family-wise Type I error rate. Results under both uncorrected and Bonferroni-corrected thresholds are reported. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) and 95% Wilson confidence intervals are additionally reported for all model pairs and model accuracies, respectively. We note that while the twelve models were evaluated on the same test set of 2,625 images, their predictions were generated independently from separately trained models with distinct architectures and weight initializations. The independent-sample framing is therefore appropriate for comparing overall accuracy proportions [43]. The Chi-squared test is applied here to a 2 × 2 contingency table of counts (correct vs. incorrect for each model pair), which is its standard use case for testing association between categorical variables. This serves as a non-parametric confirmation of the parametric t-test, and yielded consistent results across all 66 pairs.

To assess cross-dataset generalization, each model’s per-class accuracy on the IBSD was compared to its corresponding BIRDS-525 test-set class accuracy using Fisher’s exact test (two-sided) as the primary test. This analysis was extended to all five top-performing models across five species classes. A p-value below 0.05 was taken to indicate a statistically significant accuracy difference between the two datasets.

The BIRDS-525 per-class test set contains exactly five images per class (2,625 test images across 525 classes), representing the complete per-class test partition established by the dataset authors [15]. Accordingly, the reference proportion in the cross-dataset comparison (5/5 = 100%) reflects full per-class test-set accuracy on BIRDS-525, not a chosen sample. We acknowledge that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_1 = 5$$\end{document} with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_1 = 1.0$$\end{document} violates the standard Z-test normal approximation assumption (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n \cdot (1-p) \geq 5$$\end{document}; here 5 × 0 = 0). Therefore, Fisher’s exact test (two-sided) is used as the primary statistical test for cross-dataset comparison, as it requires no large-sample assumption and yields exact p-values for any sample size, including \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_1 = 5$$\end{document}. Cases where both BIRDS-525 and IBSD class accuracies equal 100% yield a pooled standard error of zero and are reported as non-testable.

Quantitative performance evaluation on BIRDS-525

Table 3 summarizes the evaluation results achieved by the various architectures when applied to the test set of BIRDS-525, showcasing their test accuracy, F1-score, total inference time (TIT), and average inference time per batch (AITB). MambaVision achieves the highest nominal accuracy (99.47%) and F1-score (0.9945), along with efficient inference (TIT: 1.1363s, AITB: 0.0137s), while Vision Transformer (ViT) follows closely with (99.32%) accuracy and rapid inference (TIT: 0.7941s, AITB: 0.0096s). Swin Vision Transformer (SVT) shows (99.24%) accuracy and strong F1-score (0.9922), though its TIT (2.8086s) and AITB (0.0338s) suggest optimization potential.

Hiera is one of the fast models (TIT: 0.9473s, AITB: 0.0114s), but has comparatively lower accuracy (98.55%) and F1-score (0.9851). DeiT performs well with (98.90%) accuracy and TIT of (1.3315s), while Pyramid Vision Transformer (PVT) offers (99.05%) accuracy but with longer inference times (TIT: 1.7534s, AITB: 0.0211s). DenseNet201 delivers (99.43%) accuracy but has significantly longer inference times (TIT: 6.9448s). ResNet50 balances accuracy (99.31%) and efficiency (TIT: 1.1584s, AITB: 0.0140s), benefiting from residual connections, while MobileNetV2 offers (98.32%) accuracy and moderate inference times (TIT: 1.5082s). AlexNet, despite its fast inference (TIT: 0.2994s, AITB: 0.0036s), lags behind with lower accuracy (93.83%), reflecting the architectural limitations of early CNN designs, particularly the absence of residual connections and limited depth, which constrain feature representation capacity on fine-grained classification tasks. ShuffleNetV2 exhibits the weakest performance (87.80% accuracy), highlighting the trade-off between speed and classification precision.

Notably, nine of the twelve models achieve accuracy above 98.32%, with differences among the top nine architectures being numerically minor (ranging from 98.55% to 99.47%), the statistical significance of which is examined in the next section.

Statistical evaluation of model performance on the BIRDS-525

Our statistical analysis confirms that the observed performance differences are not due to chance and establishes a rigorous model hierarchy. The results from the parametric t-test and the non-parametric Chi-squared (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi^2$$\end{document}) test were in strong agreement, yielding nearly identical p-values for all 66 pairwise comparisons.

The analysis identifies three distinct performance tiers. First, a ‘bottom-tier’ comprising ShuffleNetV2 (87.81%) and AlexNet (93.83%), which were statistically significantly worse than all other 10 models (all p < 0.001). Under uncorrected thresholds, a transitional group including MobileNetV2 (98.32%), Hiera (98.55%), SegFormer (98.86%), and DeiT (98.90%) appeared inferior to the top performers. However, after Bonferroni correction, only MobileNetV2 occupies a borderline position; Hiera, SegFormer, DeiT, and ResNet50 are confirmed members of the statistically indistinguishable top cluster.

After Bonferroni correction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha/66 \approx 0.000758$$\end{document}), 23 of 66 pairwise comparisons were statistically significant. Under the uncorrected threshold (α = 0.05), 36 of 66 pairs were significant. The corrected results reveal a substantially broader top-performing cluster than uncorrected analysis suggests.

Specifically, nine of twelve architectures—MambaVision, ViT, SVT, DeiT, SegFormer, PVT, Hiera, DenseNet201, and ResNet50—are mutually statistically indistinguishable after Bonferroni correction, with all 36 pairwise comparisons within this group failing to reject the null hypothesis. Crucially, all within-cluster Cohen’s d values are below 0.10, confirming that the observed differences are of negligible practical effect size. For reference, MambaVision vs ViT: p = 0.478, d = 0.020; MambaVision vs DenseNet201: p = 0.852, d = 0.005; MambaVision vs Hiera: p = 0.000822 (above the Bonferroni threshold of 0.000758), d = 0.092.

MobileNetV2 occupies a borderline position: it is Bonferroni-significantly inferior only to MambaVision (p = 0.000074) and DenseNet201 (p = 0.000146), but is not significantly different from the remaining seven top-cluster models under Bonferroni correction. AlexNet and ShuffleNetV2 form a clear bottom tier, being significantly inferior to all other models under both uncorrected and Bonferroni-corrected thresholds (all p < 0.001). To preserve the class-index mapping of the 525-class trained model, the IBSD evaluation directory was structured to replicate the full BIRDS-525 class hierarchy: one placeholder image was placed in each of the 520 non-target class folders to maintain the correct label-to-index correspondence, while the five IBSD species folders contained the complete respective image sets. Inference was performed across all 525 classes, and predictions were subsequently filtered to retain only the five target species. This approach ensures that the model’s output logits map to the same class indices established during training, preventing systematic misclassification arising from label remapping—a standard procedure for evaluating fixed-head classification models on out-of-distribution data.

The ViT model achieves the highest overall accuracy, averaging 74.07%, with remarkable results for species such as Ostrich (92.86%) and Peacock (88.89%). Following ViT, the finetuned MambaVision demonstrates an overall accuracy of 66.52%, excelling in Ostrich classification (100%) and performing well with Myna (80.88%). However, it struggles significantly with Crow, achieving only 14.08% accuracy. The SVT model achieves an average accuracy of 61.59%, performing strongly for Ostrich (92.86%) and Peacock (83.33%), though its performance declined notably for Crow (32.39%). The PVT model displayed a moderate average accuracy of 55.38%, achieving reasonable results for Ostrich (85.71%) but performing poorly with Rock Dove (20.00%). Finally, the CNN-based DenseNet201 had the lowest overall accuracy at 47.99%. While it performs adequately with Ostrich (85.71%), it struggles with species like Crow (9.15%) and Rock Dove (18.10%), emphasizing the relative weaknesses observed, particularly when benchmarked against models employing self-attention mechanisms (Transformers).

The evaluation of the models—ViT, Mamba Vision, SVT, PVT, and DenseNet201, on the IBSD dataset reveal variations in performance due to the models’ architectures and dataset characteristics. The fine-tuned Mamba Vision, although competitive, struggled with Crow classification. The Crows in the IBSD dataset, found locally in Delhi, had subtle differences from the entirely black Crow used in training. One possible hypothesis is that MambaVision’s hybrid architecture, combining convolutional and attention-based processing, may be sensitive to subtle visual variations—which could simultaneously explain its perfect performance on Ostrich (100%, where test images are visually similar to training data) and its severe degradation on Crow (14.1%, where locally-captured Delhi crows differ from the predominantly all-black benchmark Crow class). However, this remains a post-hoc interpretation; without feature-level analysis such as Grad-CAM activation maps or targeted ablation studies, causal attribution is not possible. Future work should employ such tools to validate this hypothesis.

The pronounced performance gap between IBSD and BIRDS-525 for Crow and Rock Dove across all models indicates that these species exhibit greater visual domain shift in locally-captured Indian conditions compared to the training distribution. The differential impact across models also demonstrates that architecture choice meaningfully affects real-world generalization, independent of benchmark accuracy.

The extreme per-class variation in MambaVision’s IBSD performance—perfect accuracy on Ostrich (100%, 28/28) versus severe failure on Crow (14.1%, 20/142)—warrants specific discussion. We propose two non-exclusive explanations: (1) Visual distinctiveness: Ostrich is a morphologically distinctive species with consistent visual features (long neck, large body, distinctive plumage) that likely remain recognizable across training and IBSD capture conditions. Crow, however, is a more uniformly-coloured species where subtle regional variation (e.g., grey-hooded jungle crows common in Delhi vs. the fully black American Crow predominant in BIRDS-525) can cause systematic misclassification. (2) Sample size and class representation: With 142 IBSD Crow images, the failure is statistically robust (p < 0.001); with only 28 Ostrich images, the perfect result may partly reflect limited within-class variation in the IBSD Ostrich sample. Both explanations remain hypotheses pending feature-level analysis.

ViT excelled across most species, particularly Ostrich and Peacock, due to its patch-based processing and self-attention mechanisms. These features allow ViT to capture both local and global dependencies, making it highly effective for distinguishing species with distinct visual patterns. SVT performed well with Ostrich and Peacock, benefiting from its hierarchical design and window partitioning, which effectively captures local features. However, its reliance on local partitions likely caused it to miss broader context in the Crow images, resulting in lower accuracy for that species. PVT showed moderate performance. While its hierarchical structure works well for high-resolution images, PVT struggled with species like Rock Dove, which have subtler visual cues. Its spatial reduction attention mechanisms, while efficient, may have limited its ability to capture finer details. DenseNet201 performed the weakest, particularly with species like Crow and Rock Dove. The model’s dense connectivity promotes feature reuse but, lacking explicit attention mechanisms, may be less effective at capturing non-local dependencies required for fine-grained species discrimination [41]. This is consistent with DenseNet201’s weakest IBSD generalization (48.0% average), though the precise architectural cause requires ablation analysis to confirm.

In contrast, the fine-tuned MambaVision achieves a remarkable accuracy of 99.47%, thereby achieving the highest reported nominal accuracy on BIRDS-525 under the experimental conditions of this study, though statistical analysis confirms this advantage over other top transformer-based architectures is not significant. Its superior performance can be attributed to the inherent strengths of the underlying State Space Model (SSM) architecture. Unlike traditional CNNs that rely on local receptive fields, MambaVision employs a hybrid architecture combining convolutional feature extraction with a selective state mechanism that operates in linear time. This architectural design may contribute to its strong benchmark performance, though we note that ViT, despite its quadratic attention complexity, achieves comparable benchmark accuracy and superior real-world generalization in this study, suggesting that architectural efficiency alone does not determine classification quality in practice.

Conclusions

This study presents a comprehensive comparative evaluation of twelve deep learning architectures for fine-grained bird species classification. On the BIRDS-525 benchmark (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N = 89{,}885$$\end{document}, 525 classes), rigorous Bonferroni-corrected statistical analysis across 66 pairwise comparisons reveals that nine architectures—MambaVision, ViT, SVT, DeiT, SegFormer, PVT, Hiera, DenseNet201, and ResNet50—are mutually statistically indistinguishable, with all within-cluster accuracy differences carrying negligible effect sizes (Cohen’s d < 0.10). AlexNet and ShuffleNetV2 form a clearly inferior bottom tier. MobileNetV2 occupies a borderline position, significantly inferior only to MambaVision and DenseNet201 under strict Bonferroni correction. A key finding is the divergence between benchmark and real-world performance: while MambaVision achieves the highest nominal benchmark accuracy (99.47%), ViT demonstrates substantially stronger generalization on the real-world IBSD dataset (74.1% vs. 66.5% for MambaVision). This divergence is explained by ViT being the only model where the Rock Dove accuracy drop between datasets is not statistically significant (Fisher p = 0.582), while MambaVision’s Rock Dove drop does not reach statistical significance under Fisher’s exact test (p = 0.067, borderline). Critically, a consistent cross-model finding is the significant Crow accuracy drop on IBSD (all five models, Fisher p ≤ 0.023), indicating genuine domain shift for this species between the benchmark training distribution and locally-captured Indian conditions. CNN-based DenseNet201 shows the weakest real-world generalization (48.0% IBSD average), reinforcing that attention-based architectures generalize more robustly under dataset shift.

The IBSD dataset, while small and geographically localized, serves as a pilot real-world evaluation resource. The results highlight a notable gap between benchmark and real-world performance: the best-performing model on IBSD (ViT) achieves only 74.1% average accuracy, indicating that purely visual, single-modal classification approaches evaluated here are not yet deployment-ready for real-world ecological applications without further optimization. We note that recent multi-modal approaches incorporating visual-acoustic fusion have demonstrated higher accuracies (e.g., 94% for Indian bird species [45]), suggesting that combining modalities may be necessary to bridge this deployment gap. The IBSD results should be interpreted as diagnostic evidence of domain shift rather than evidence of deployment readiness. Addressing this gap—through larger and more geographically diverse training data, domain adaptation techniques, and targeted augmentation—represents a critical direction for future work. Through an extensive comparative evaluation with several recent and reputable methodologies on the BIRDS-525 dataset, this research offers critical insights into the current landscape of automated bird species identification and establishes a robust baseline for future advancements.

Future work should prioritize: (1) expanding IBSD with more species, greater geographic diversity, and balanced class representation to improve statistical reliability; (2) investigating advanced augmentation strategies (RandAugment, MixUp, CutMix) to improve domain robustness; (3) conducting ablation studies on MambaVision’s architectural components to empirically validate claimed long-range dependency modeling; (4) applying recently developed Mamba-specific interpretability tools such as MambaLRP [46] and hidden attention extraction [47] to provide feature-level explanations for the observed species-specific performance patterns; and (5) extending cross-dataset generalization analysis to diverse geographic and environmental conditions.
