Nitric oxide-dependent stabilization of vimentin confers chemoresistance in ovarian cancer
Authors: Gianmarco Melone, Recep Bayraktar, Hong Zhao, Sean O Hynes, Fidelma Kaar, Adam Freegrove, Jenying Deng, Agne Baseviciene, Ivonne Uzair, Karina Ortega Martinez, Liliana Guzman-Rojas, Maria Florencia Chervo, Mailin Li, Wei Qian, Jianying Zhou, Aireana Phillips, Kristina Diana A Zambo, Fotis Nikolos, Keith S Chan, Christoforos Thomas, Lewis Francis, Jenny C Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSC) is often diagnosed at an advanced stage and is characterized by an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that facilitates disease progression, therapeutic resistance, and poor survival. We recently demonstrated that nitric oxide (NO) blockade enhances the response of metaplastic breast cancer to PI3K inhibition and taxane therapy by reducing S-nitrosylation-mediated activation of the JNK/c-Jun pathway, thereby promoting tumor differentiation, suppressing stemness, and improving survival in patient-derived xenograft models. Here, we demonstrate that inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) is constitutively expressed in HGSC and that pharmacologic inhibition of NO signaling with the pan-NOS inhibitor NG-monomethyl-L-arginine (L-NMMA) enhances cisplatin efficacy by reprogramming the tumor microenvironment. By targeting NO signaling with either L-NMMA or through CRISPR-Cas9-mediated iNOS knockout and iNOS small interfering RNA (siRNA), we impaired epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) by inhibiting NO-mediated stabilizing effects on vimentin in both in vitro and in vivo models. Mechanistically, L-NMMA-mediated inhibition of iNOS signaling reduced S-nitrosylation, accelerated vimentin ubiquitination, and promoted its proteasome-dependent degradation. These findings identify iNOS-mediated S-nitrosylation as a key regulator of vimentin stability and EMT and suggest that therapeutic inhibition of NO signaling may increase cisplatin sensitivity in HGSC and improve patient outcomes.Keywords: MT: Oligonucleotides: Therapies and Applications, chemoresistance, cisplatin, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, high-grade serous ovarian cancer, iNOS, L-NMMA, nitric oxide, S-nitrosylation, tumor microenvironment, vimentin

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is a highly heterogeneous disease often diagnosed at a late stage when metastatic spread has already occurred throughout the abdominal cavity. Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, ovarian cancer remains one of the deadliest gynecologic malignancies, with fewer than half of the patients surviving 5 years after the initial diagnosis.1 According to the American Cancer Society, an estimated 21,010 women in the United States will be diagnosed with ovarian cancer in 2026, and approximately 12,450 are expected to die from this disease.2 Most primary malignant ovarian tumors arise from the ovarian surface epithelium.3 Epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) represents nearly 90% of all ovarian malignancies and is classified into several histologic subtypes, including serous, endometrioid, clear cell, and mucinous.4 Among these subtypes, high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSC) is the predominant histologic subtype and is most frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage.4,5 Patients with HGSC often develop resistance to conventional chemotherapy with carboplatin and paclitaxel. Therefore, there is an urgent need to discover novel targets and develop effective therapies to improve survival outcomes in these patients.

One of the main factors behind chemoresistance is cellular plasticity mediated by epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a dynamic process that enhances migratory and invasive properties, driving tumor dissemination and metastasis.6,7 EMT not only facilitates metastasis but also confers stem-like properties that enhance tumor initiation, therapeutic resistance, and disease progression.8,9 Moreover, ovarian cancer is characterized by a markedly reduced immune response against tumor cells,10,11,12 often described as a “cold” tumor microenvironment (TME). This immunologically cold phenotype contributes to the limited efficacy of immunotherapies in ovarian cancer.13,14,15

Ovarian cancer exhibits chronic inflammation mediated by the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines.16,17 These pro-inflammatory cytokines play a key role in sustaining a persistent inflammatory state, frequently triggering a cellular response characterized by inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS/NOS2) expression.18,19 iNOS is responsible for the release of nitric oxide (NO), and its overexpression can promote the activation of cancer-related genes/pathways. In particular, upregulation of the iNOS signaling pathway can induce chemoresistance and increase cancer cell proliferation, aggressiveness20 and angiogenesis.21 NO exerts its function through S-nitrosylation, a post-translational modification consisting of the covalent attachment of a nitrogen monoxide group to the thiol side chain of a cysteine.22 Several studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between aberrant S-nitrosylation and increased cancer risk.23,24 Consequently, increased levels of NO can lead to dysregulated S-nitrosylation, thereby promoting cancer cell proliferation, survival, and metastatic dissemination.24,25,26 As aberrant NO production has been associated with metastatic progression, understanding the role of the NO signaling pathway in EMT may be essential for developing effective therapies for ovarian cancer. Notably, Nω-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester (L-NAME), a specific inhibitor of iNOS, has been shown to reduce murine mammary carcinoma cells’ growth in vivo,27,28 but has not been extensively studied in ovarian cancer.

Siegert et al. demonstrated that between colorectal adenocarcinoma cell lines HRT-18 and HT-29, HRT-18 cells were more aggressive and invasive compared to HT-29 cells, which lacked NOS2 expression.29 Notably, treatment of HT-29 cells with NO donors increased their aggressiveness, indicating that dysregulated NO production can promote EMT. Interestingly, inhibiting the NO signaling pathway with the NOS2-specific inhibitor 1400W markedly reduced cell invasiveness and reversed the pro-metastatic effects of NO.29 A similar mechanism has been described in breast cancer, where S-nitrosylation was shown to activate β-catenin signaling in estrogen receptor (ER)-negative and basal-like breast cancer cells. Treatment with the NO donor Diethylenetriamine NONOate (DETA/NO) decreased cell adhesion while increasing vimentin expression, further supporting the role of NO in promoting EMT-related phenotypes.30

Here, we identify a specific molecular mechanism through which NO signaling promotes EMT by stabilizing vimentin in HGSC. We further demonstrate that pharmacological inhibition of the NO signaling pathway significantly enhances the efficacy of cisplatin both in vitro and in vivo. Considering these findings, NO likely plays a pivotal role in facilitating the metastatic spread and colonization of ovarian cancer cells beyond the primary tumor site. Together, our results suggest that targeting NO signaling may represent a promising strategy to overcome chemoresistance by modulating EMT in ovarian cancer.

Results

iNOS is constitutively expressed in HGSC and chemoresistant ovarian cancer cell lines

In contrast to the traditional concept of iNOS as an inducible enzyme expressed only under inflammatory stimuli, accumulating evidence indicates that NOS2 is constitutively expressed across different cancer types.31,32,33 To investigate whether this phenomenon also applies to ovarian cancer, we first examined NOS2 gene expression levels in the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) database. NOS2 mRNA levels were consistently observed in the ovarian cancer cell line panel, with OVCAR8 and TOV112D (derived from a stage 3C, Grade 3 endometrioid carcinoma) showing higher NOS2 mRNA expression compared with other ovarian cancer cells (Figure 1A). A similar NOS2 expression pattern was detected in NCI-60 panel of cell lines, where aberrant NOS2 expression was observed in OVCAR8 and NCI-ADR-RES (an adriamycin-resistant ovarian cancer cell line) (Figure 1B).

Next, to evaluate iNOS expression directly, we measured both protein and mRNA levels across our ovarian cancer cell line panel by western blot and reverse-transcriptase quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). Consistent iNOS expression was detected in OVCAR8, A2780cis, and A2780 cells at low levels, whereas CAOV3, SKOV3, and OVCAR3 lacked detectable expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figures 1C, 1D, and S1A). To further confirm constitutive iNOS expression, OVCAR8, A2780cis, and A2780 (low iNOS-expressing) cells were treated with LPS and a cytokine mix (TNF-α 10 ng/mL, IL-1β 10 ng/mL, IFN-γ 25 ng/mL). No alteration in iNOS expression was observed in OVCAR8 or A2780cis, whereas induction was detected only in A2780 cells, which normally express iNOS at low levels (Figures 1E, 1F, S1B, and S1C). Together, these findings demonstrate that iNOS is constitutively expressed in OVCAR8, A2780cis, and A2780 ovarian cancer cell lines.

To assess the clinical significance and prognostic value of iNOS expression, Kaplan-Meier survival analyses were performed on the publicly available dataset using KMplot.com. Although this analysis considered only survival in relation to NOS2 mRNA expression, which showed variable results across different subtypes, we observed notable associations in endometrioid ovarian cancer. Despite the relatively small cohort size due to the lower frequency of endometrioid ovarian cancer, we observed meaningful and significant results. In patients with endometrioid ovarian cancer, high NOS2 mRNA expression was associated with shorter overall survival compared with low expression in the absence of cisplatin treatment (p = 0.056; low expression n = 26, high expression n = 25) (Figure S1D). Importantly, under cisplatin treatment, patients with high NOS2 expression had significantly lower overall survival (p = 0.0088; low expression n = 16, high expression n = 15) (Figure S1E).

L-NMMA restores sensitivity to chemotherapy and modulates the TME via vimentin

We hypothesized that inhibition of the iNOS signaling pathway with NG-monomethyl-L-arginine (L-NMMA) would overcome cisplatin resistance and enhance its therapeutic efficacy in ovarian cancer. For in vitro functional studies, we selected two ovarian cancer cell lines with high iNOS expression (OVCAR8 and A2780cis).

We first performed cell viability assays to determine IC50 values for cisplatin and L-NMMA, as well as their combination. OVCAR8 cells were treated for 48 h with increasing concentrations of cisplatin (4, 8, 10, 12, 16, 20 and 24 μM) or L-NMMA (2, 4, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 20 and 30 mM). The IC50 values were calculated as 7.025 μM for cisplatin and 15.25 mM for L-NMMA in OVCAR8 cells (Figures 2A and 2B). Notably, combination treatment starting from 16 to 30 mM of L-NMMA and 10 μM cisplatin resulted in a greater reduction in cell viability compared with cisplatin alone (Figure 2C). When combined with L-NMMA, residual proliferation rates were as follows: 54.8% with 2 mM, 49.7% with 4 mM, 44.3% with 8 mM, 47.8% with 10 mM, 45.9% with 12 mM, and 45.3% with 14 mM. At 16 mM, L-NMMA combined with 10 μM cisplatin, proliferation was reduced to 40% (p < 0.05). Treatment with 20 mM L-NMMA plus cisplatin further decreased proliferation to 34.7% (p < 0.05). Finally, the combination of 30 mM L-NMMA and 10 μM cisplatin led to the lowest residual proliferation rate of 12.9% (p < 0.05).

Based on our previous findings in breast cancer, where iNOS inhibition enhanced the efficacy of alpelisib and taxane therapy primarily through suppression of EMT,34 we investigated whether iNOS expression similarly influences vimentin, a key EMT regulator, in ovarian cancer. We analyzed VIM mRNA and protein levels in our ovarian cancer cell lines. Vimentin protein and mRNA levels showed a trend similar to that of iNOS across the ovarian cancer cell line panel (Figures 2D, 2E, and S2A). The highest vimentin mRNA and protein levels were observed in A2780, with high levels also detected in OVCAR8 and A2780cis cells, whereas CAOV3, SKOV3, and OVCAR3 lacked detectable protein expression (Figures 2D, 2E, and S2A). In accordance with our in vitro findings, we also found a positive correlation between NOS2 and VIM mRNA expression in TNMPlot (Figure S2B), (r = 0.11 and p = 0.03). In addition, to determine whether iNOS inhibition affects vimentin expression, OVCAR8 cells were treated with increasing concentrations of L-NMMA (4, 6, 8, and 10 mM). Treatment with L-NMMA significantly reduced vimentin protein levels at 6, 8, and 10 mM concentrations, indicating that blockade of iNOS signaling downregulates vimentin expression (Figure 2F), whereas no significant change was observed at the mRNA level (Figure S2C). Overall, our in vitro findings indicate that the iNOS pathway plays a critical role in the regulation of vimentin, a protein known for its key role in EMT.35

iNOS downregulation reduces cell growth, increases chemosensitivity, and impairs EMT via vimentin

We next asked whether iNOS downregulation inhibits the growth of ovarian cancer cells and affects vimentin expression. To address this, we generated two independent NOS2 knockout (KO) OVCAR8 clones (KO-1 and KO-2) (Figures 3A and S3A). We observed that iNOS deficiency resulted in reduced vimentin protein expression in OVCAR8 cells (Figures 3A and S3B). Loss of iNOS expression also increased chemosensitivity, as both KO clones displayed decreased cell viability compared with OVCAR8 parental cells (Figure 3B). At the lowest tested concentration (4 μM), KO-1 and KO-2 cells showed viabilities of 42.41% and 58%, respectively, compared with 72.35% in OVCAR8 parental cells. At 8 μM cisplatin, viability for KO-1 was 55.1% in the parental cells compared to 28.71% in KO-1 and 43.71% in KO-2. At 10 μM, viability decreased to 48.43% in the parental line and 23.7% in KO-1, and 33.1% in KO-2. At 12 μM, the viability values were 46.1% for parental cells and 20.53% in KO-1, and 33.1% in KO-2. With 16 μM, viability dropped to 32.9% in parental cells versus 13.1% in KO-1 and 17.01% in KO-2. At 20 μM, parental cells showed 27.75% viability, while KO-1 was at 9.17% and 9.78% in KO-2. Finally, at the highest concentration of 24 μM, proliferation was reduced to 17.86% in the parental cells and only 2.23% in KO-1 and 2.72% in KO-2.

To confirm these findings and further examine the biological role of iNOS in ovarian cancer, we performed functional studies using two independent NOS2-targeting small interfering RNAs (siRNAs). Transient knockdown of NOS2 in OVCAR8 and A2780cis cells achieved >90% reduction in NOS2 mRNA (48 h) and protein (72 h) compared with control siRNA (Figures 3C, 3D, and S3C). iNOS knockdown consistently reduced vimentin protein levels, although quantitative analysis of VIM mRNA did not significantly change (Figure S3D). These results, consistent with CRISPR-Cas9-mediated KO, demonstrate that iNOS depletion suppresses vimentin expression primarily at the protein level, supporting a role for iNOS in regulating vimentin through post-translational mechanisms.

We next assessed the impact of iNOS inhibition on ovarian cancer cell migration and clonogenic growth. Scratch assays performed using the IncuCyte Live-Cell Analysis System revealed that siRNA-mediated NOS2 knockdown significantly impaired the migration of OVCAR8 cells (Figure 3E). After 12 h, the wound area was 70.37% in the scramble siRNA, 91.83% in siRNA1, and 100% in siRNA2, showing that iNOS impairment reduces OVCAR8 cell migration. After 24 h, the wound area was 44.87% in the scramble siRNA, 69.8% in siRNA1, and 85.39% in siRNA2 (p < 0.05). The last time point of 36 h showed a more significant difference between the scramble siRNA and the two NOS2 siRNAs, as the wound area in the scramble siRNA group corresponded to 19.4% vs. 38.05% in siRNA 1 and 64.2% in siRNA2 (p < 0.05). In addition, colony formation assays showed reduced clonogenic potential in OVCAR8 KO-1 and KO-2 clones as well as in siRNA-transfected OVCAR8 and A2780cis cells (Figures 3F and 3G). The total area in OVCAR8 parental used as control was 1.87 × 105 (measured as pixels), whereas 9.06 × 104 in the KO-1 and 1.17 × 105 in the KO-2. In the A2780cis cell line treated with scramble siRNA, the total area was 9.7 × 104 vs. 1.34 × 104 in siRNA1 and 1.19 × 104 in siRNA2. Collectively, these results indicate that iNOS inhibition disrupts vimentin expression, consequently reducing proliferation and increasing chemosensitivity in ovarian cancer cells.

iNOS inhibition regulates EMT and wound healing-associated pathways in ovarian cancer

We performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to compare global transcriptional profiles between parental (OVCAR8) cells and KO-1 (OVCAR8 NOS2 KO) cells and subsequently used ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA) to identify potential pathways associated with iNOS signaling. Sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR, and differential expression was assessed with DESeq2. RNA-seq analysis of parental and KO-1 OVCAR8 cells revealed significant transcriptional reprogramming, with dysregulation of genes implicated in EMT, cell migration, and cancer progression. A heatmap of the top 20 upregulated and downregulated genes (Figure 4A) demonstrated increased expression of interleukin 6 (IL-6), fibronectin 1 (FN1), and matrix metalloproteinase-1 (MMP1) in OVCAR8 parental cells, all of which modulate VIM expression and EMT.36,37,38 Consistent with this, KO-1 cells exhibited reduced expression of these genes, indicating that iNOS inhibition suppresses EMT-associated gene programs. Among the top downregulated genes in the parental group, LSAMP39 and SVEP1,40 both known for tumor-suppressive functions, were decreased, whereas the opposite trend was seen in KO-1 cells. A volcano plot further highlighted the separation of significantly upregulated and downregulated transcripts (Figure 4B). Functional clustering of dysregulated genes revealed enrichment of pathways related to wound healing, extracellular matrix organization, and regulation of cell shape (Figure 4C). Collectively, these results suggest that iNOS signaling contributes to transcriptional reprogramming of cytoskeletal and migration-associated genes that promote EMT and cancer progression.

L-NMMA-mediated inhibition of iNOS signaling improves the therapeutic efficacy of cisplatin in a syngeneic in vivo model

To validate the positive effects of combining L-NMMA with cisplatin observed in vitro, we tested the treatment in a syngeneic in vivo model. A total of 5 × 106 ID8-Luc murine ovarian cancer cells were injected intraperitoneally (i.p.), and tumor burden was monitored weekly for 12 weeks using region of interest (ROI) imaging. In addition, body weight and abdominal diameter were recorded to assess ascites development. Treatment started at week 5 and consisted of three cycles of cisplatin, L-NMMA, or their combination. At the endpoint, peritoneal samples were collected for pathological evaluation to detect metastatic spread. Selected immune cell populations were also analyzed in ascites samples to determine whether the combination treatment elicited an immune response (Figure 5A). At endpoint, combination therapy resulted in the lowest mean abdominal diameter (6.22 cm) compared with cisplatin (6.64 cm), L-NMMA (7.11 cm), and control (7.08 cm, p < 0.05) (Figure 5B). Notably, treatment with L-NMMA in combination with cisplatin was well tolerated, and no significant toxicities, including weight loss or treatment-related mortality, were detected (Figure S4A). Furthermore, ROI measurements were similar between the groups (Figure S4B). Representative peritoneal sections analyzed with QuPath are shown in Figure 5C, while Figure 5D highlights lymphovascular invasion (left), a mitotic figure between cancer cells (center), and the same mitosis at higher magnification (right). As L-NMMA alone showed minimal cytotoxic activity in vitro, pathologic evaluation of the in vivo samples was specifically designed to assess enhancement of cisplatin efficacy. Histologic analysis revealed that the combination treatment produced the lowest tumor area in peritoneal sections, with a mean of 9 × 105 μm2 compared with 2.2 × 106 μm2 in the cisplatin group and 4.67 × 106 μm2 in the control group (Figure 5E, p < 0.05). Consistent with this, the combination treatment also showed the lowest tumor cell counts relative to cisplatin and control (Figure 5F, p < 0.05). The combination treatment eliminated mitotic activity, with no mitotic counts detected in this group, in contrast to cisplatin and control (Figure 5G, p < 0.05). In addition to pathological evaluation, immune cell populations were analyzed in ascites samples. Combination treatment increased natural killer (NK)/NKT cells compared with cisplatin (Figures 5H and S5A). We also observed an expansion of the CD4+CD62L-CD44+ subset of CD4+ T cells, consistent with an activated phenotype, following combination treatment relative to cisplatin (Figures 5I and S5A). Notably, the combination also reduced the myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) population compared with cisplatin (Figures 5J and S5A, p = 0.0553). These findings indicate that L-NMMA-mediated inhibition of iNOS signaling, enhances cisplatin efficacy, and remodels NK-NKT, CD4+CD62L-CD44+ and MDSC populations, supporting its potential as a promising combination strategy in ovarian cancer.

L-NMMA iNOS signaling inhibition reduces vimentin stability through S-nitrosylation and ubiquitination, and reduces tumor growth in xenograft models

NO can modify proteins through post-translational mechanisms such as S-nitrosylation of cysteine residues and ubiquitination, thereby influencing protein stability and degradation.22,41 Based on our in vitro and in vivo findings, we hypothesized that vimentin stability could be regulated post-translationally through these NO-mediated modifications.42 To determine whether L-NMMA-mediated inhibition of iNOS signaling reduces vimentin levels via these mechanisms, we performed a series of experiments combining pharmacologic inhibitors with protein stability assays.

As L-NMMA treatment reduced vimentin at the protein level without affecting mRNA, we reasoned that iNOS inhibition might destabilize vimentin through post-translational mechanisms. To confirm this, we first performed a protein stability assay on vimentin using the protein synthesis inhibitor, cycloheximide (CHX). OVCAR8 cells were treated with CHX in the absence or presence of L-NMMA for 2 and 4 h. In CHX-treated OVCAR8 cells, vimentin protein level did not significantly change over time, but decreased by 32% after 2 h and by 71% after 4 h when also treated with L-NMMA (Figures 6A and S5B). As vimentin has been reported to be ubiquitinated,43 we next examined whether L-NMMA promotes vimentin ubiquitination. OVCAR8 cells treated with or without L-NMMA were analyzed by immunoprecipitation (IP) for ubiquitination. As shown in Figures 6B and S5C, L-NMMA treatment enhanced vimentin ubiquitination by 47% when compared to control and by 21% when compared to MG132, a potent proteasome inhibitor. To further explore the mechanism of vimentin deregulation, we analyzed S-nitrosylation levels by IP in OVCAR8 cells. S-nitrosylation levels of vimentin decreased in L-NMMA-treated cells (Figures 6C and S5D). To further validate the involvement of proteasome-mediated degradation, OVCAR8 cells were treated with increasing concentrations (1, 5, and 10 μM) of MG132 in the presence of L-NMMA. Treatment with MG132 reversed the reduction of vimentin induced by L-NMMA (Figures 6D and S5E). Taken together, these data indicate that L-NMMA-mediated inhibition of iNOS signaling accelerates vimentin ubiquitination and promotes its proteasome-dependent degradation.

Finally, to evaluate the role of iNOS signaling on tumor growth and therapeutic efficacy in ovarian cancer in vivo, we performed two independent xenograft experiments. OVCAR8 parental and OVCAR8 KO-1 cells (5 × 106) were injected i.p. into non-obese diabetic (NOD) severe combined immunodeficiency (scid) gamma (NSG) mice. OVCAR8 xenografts are among the most aggressive ovarian cancer models, with rapid i.p. progression and the largest tumor burdens in i.p. models, and survival endpoints commonly occurring within several weeks.44 In the first experiment, tumor growth was monitored by ROI imaging for 24 days. ROI signals were comparable between the two groups at day 0, but diverged beginning on day 4, with significant differences observed by days 20 and 24. At the endpoint, ROI curves showed a clear separation, with a marked reduction in the KO group, including an evident drop in ROI signal on day 24 (Figure 6E). In the second experiment, mice were treated with cisplatin to assess survival. Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed a survival advantage in the NOS2-KO group, with approximately 33% of mice being alive at day 20, whereas no survival was observed in the parental group (Figure 6F).

Discussion

The central finding of this study is that iNOS is constitutively expressed across ovarian cancer cells, including HGSC, where it contributes to chemoresistance. We demonstrate that pharmacological and genetic inhibition of iNOS signaling significantly enhances the efficacy of cisplatin in vitro and in vivo, possibly through suppression of EMT. Mechanistically, our data reveal that blockade of NO signaling induces vimentin destabilization through increased ubiquitination, revealing a novel connection between iNOS signaling and proteasome-mediated regulation of EMT. These findings not only validate the strong therapeutic potential of L-NMMA as an inhibitor of NO signaling in ovarian cancer but also provide mechanistic insight into how blockade of NO-related pathways promotes vimentin destabilization through enhanced ubiquitination.

The NOS family comprises three isoforms including neuronal NOS (nNOS or NOS1), endothelial NOS (eNOS or NOS3), and iNOS or NOS2, whereas nNOS and eNOS are constitutively expressed in neuronal and vascular endothelial cells and generate NO at low levels, iNOS is transcriptionally regulated and produces higher and sustained amounts of NO in response to pro-inflammatory cytokines.45,46 Although iNOS was originally characterized as an inducible enzyme, recent studies indicate its constitutive expression in human cancers, suggesting that iNOS may also have a tumor-intrinsic role independent of inflammation.47,48 Our findings show that iNOS is consistently expressed at high levels in OVCAR8 cells, representing HGSC, and in the endometrioid cisplatin-resistant A2780cis ovarian cancer cell line, when compared with other subtypes. In addition to cancer cells, constitutive iNOS expression is also observed in cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), inflammatory cells, and endothelial cells, suggesting that NO signaling may be broadly active within the ovarian TME.49 As demonstrated in several studies, pathological outcomes driven by elevated NO levels include acquired chemoresistance,50 enhanced tumor proliferation,51 and immune dysfunction.52 However, its precise role in ovarian cancer remains to be fully elucidated. It should be noted that high doses of L-NMMA may be potentially toxic both in vitro and in vivo. Moreover, elevated concentrations of L-NMMA could affect cell proliferation by disrupting the balance of reactive oxygen species (ROS). In this context, NO has been reported to play important antioxidant functions during TNF-α induced oxidative stress.53 However, due to the dysregulated cellular metabolism and the increased NO expression observed in several cancer types, including ovarian cancer, the reduction of NO levels may confer more benefits than adverse effects at the cellular level. Furthermore, to obtain measurable effects, particularly in combination with chemotherapy, high doses of L-NMMA are required, as we previously reported.34 In addition, L-NMMA may also induce side effects in vivo when doses are beyond the optimal range. A clinical study in patients with metastatic renal cell carcinoma reported that L-NMMA administration was associated with reduced cardiac output and increased pulmonary vascular resistance. However, after identifying the optimal dose of L-NMMA, the high-cardiac output state was reversed, while the beneficial effects of the treatment were preserved without significant adverse effects.54 Our group recently reported in a phase 1/2 clinical trial that L-NMMA, when combined with taxanes, improves chemotherapy response in breast cancer.55 In this study, we extend these findings to ovarian cancer, revealing for the first time that L-NMMA enhances sensitivity to cisplatin in both in vitro and in vivo models. The response to chemotherapy is a key determinant of clinical outcome in most cancers, but it is particularly critical in ovarian cancer due to its high rate of recurrence. Therefore, targeting tumor proliferation and metastatic progression remains essential to improving patient survival. Considering this last aspect, NO-blockers may be administered following surgical debulking and chemotherapy to prevent recurrence and inhibit the EMT transition in residual cancer cells. While elevated NO levels have been implicated in human cancer progression,56 our study reveals that NO signaling drives the expression and induction of specific EMT markers in ovarian cancer. We found that vimentin expression is strictly correlated with iNOS expression in ovarian cancer, identifying NO signaling as a novel contributor to vimentin stability and EMT dynamics. A recent study also reported a strong correlation between EMT and the acquisition of chemoresistance.57 Therefore, based on our findings, HGSC and ovarian cancer cell lines with acquired chemoresistance may benefit from NO blockade in combination with standard chemotherapy regimens. Nonetheless, given the distinct genomic backgrounds of the analyzed models, OVCAR8 (HGSC) and A2780 wild-type and A2780cis (endometrioid ovarian cancer), differences in drug responsiveness, proliferation rates, activation of signaling pathways, and the development of chemoresistance are expected. Furthermore, Domcke et al. reports that although A2780 and SKOV3 (a clear cell ovarian cancer cell line) are among the cell lines most commonly used for publications, they are not the best models for HGSC.58 Despite this heterogeneity, all cell lines used in this study constitutively expressed iNOS and converged on a mesenchymal phenotype characterized by high vimentin expression. In this work, we further demonstrated that NO is able to stimulate and modulate EMT itself. This shared feature of iNOS overexpression across different ovarian cancer cell lines and subtypes emerges as a key finding, supporting the relevance of NO targeting as a common therapeutic strategy for genetically diverse ovarian cancer models. These findings indicate that pharmacological inhibition of NO signaling may serve as an effective approach to disrupt NO-mediated communication within the TME and enhance the therapeutic response. In addition, we demonstrate that combining L-NMMA with cisplatin increases chemosensitivity in in vitro and in vivo models of ovarian cancer. In line with these findings, our syngeneic in vivo model further demonstrated that impairment of NO release through L-NMMA, in combination with chemotherapy, induces a significant immunomodulation of the tumor immune microenvironment. Specifically, this treatment was associated with an increase in NK-NKT cell population and a concomitant reduction in MDSCs. These results are consistent with previously reported immune-stimulatory effects of L-NMMA described in the literature and support the notion that NO blockade may enhance antitumor immunity in addition to its direct effects on tumor cells.55 Consistent with our previous findings in metaplastic breast cancer, where this combination strategy modulated c-Jun activity,34 our data demonstrate that L-NMMA enhances cisplatin sensitivity in ovarian cancer by inducing vimentin destabilization via ubiquitination. As inhibition of NO signaling affected vimentin expression only at the protein level, we focused on post-translational regulatory mechanisms. Vimentin contains a single, highly conserved cysteine residue (Cys328) located within the coil-2B domain, which is critical for filament organization and cytoskeletal network dynamics.59,60 Cys328 functions as a redox-sensitive hotspot, with its thiol side chain acting as a direct target for endogenous and exogenous oxidative modifiers.59,60 However, the impact of modifications within this hotspot region on vimentin stability following NO signaling inhibition remains largely unknown in cancer. Accordingly, we examined whether vimentin is S-nitrosylated at Cys328 in OVCAR8 cells treated with or without L-NMMA. Interestingly, we found that S-nitrosylation can be specifically mediated by iNOS through highly conserved motifs, consistent with the findings reported by Jia et al.61 Mechanistically, we found that L-NMMA treatment reduces S-nitrosylation while promoting ubiquitination of immunoprecipitated vimentin samples. Given the potential of site-specific drugs such as withaferin A (WFA) in the highly conserved cysteine residue (Cys328), this site may play a potential role as a therapeutic target, as reported by Bargagna-Mohan et al.62 In addition, previous studies have demonstrated that modification of Cys328 is essential for electrophile-induced disruption of the vimentin network.59 When Cys328 is unavailable for further modification, vimentin filaments become resistant to disassembly. This observation parallels the protective effect exerted by S-nitrosylation, as proposed in this study. In addition to the proposed Cys328-dependent destabilization mechanism, our previous work provides evidence for an alternative transcriptional regulation of vimentin in metaplastic breast cancer cells.34 In that study, both NOS2 KO and L-NMMA treatment reduced vimentin expression, associated with suppression of EMT regulators (Zinc Finger E-Box Binding Homeobox 1 [ZEB1[, Hypoxia inducible factor 1 alpha [HIF-1α[, and phospho-Akt) and restoration of epithelial markers, whereas TGF-β activation restored vimentin and ZEB1 levels. These findings suggest that NO may control vimentin, at least in part, through EMT-associated transcriptional programs. Accordingly, decreased NO levels following L-NMMA treatment may attenuate EMT transcription factor activity, thereby indirectly contributing to vimentin downregulation.34 We aimed to develop a combinatorial therapy that could interfere with this vimentin hotspot, the tumor stroma and the metastatic process, as shown in our xenograft model.

In conclusion, we demonstrated the crucial functions of the NO signaling-mediated vimentin stabilization through post-translational modifications. In addition, our study provides NO-signaling-based therapeutic strategy to improve cisplatin sensitivity for ovarian cancer.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell culture conditions

Human HGSC cell lines SKOV3 and OVCAR3 were obtained from Dr. Francesca Taraballi. Human HGSC cell line CAOV3 was purchased from ATCC (Cat. # HTB75). Endometrioid ovarian cancer (EndOC) A2780 (Cat #93112519) and endometrioid ovarian cancer cisplatin-resistant A2780cis (Cat. #93112517) were purchased from ECACC. OVCAR8-RFP-Luc were generated after transfection using a lentiviral vector. Murine epithelial ovarian cancer cell line (MEOC) ID8 was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Cat. #9Q04TP) and ID8-Luc were purchased from Creative Biolabs (Cat. #IOC-ZP310). OVCAR8 and OVCAR8-RFP-Luc were cultured in RPMI1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C supplied with 5% CO2. SKOV3 was maintained in McCoy’s 5A medium with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37ºC and 5% CO2. OVCAR3 were cultured in RPMI1640 with 20% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 1 mg/mL of bovine insulin at 37°C with 5% CO2. A2780 was maintained using RPMI1640 supplied with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 2 mM glutamine at 37°C and 5% CO2. A2780cis were cultured under the same conditions as A2780 with the addition of 1 μM of cisplatin. All ovarian cancer cell lines were tested for mycoplasma contamination using a Mycoplasma PCR Detection kit (Cat. #G238).

siRNA transfection assay

OVCAR8 and A2780cis cells were transfected with siRNA targeted to NOS2 using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Cat. #11668027). All predesigned siRNAs were purchased from Dharmacon (non-targeting siRNA control pools, Cat. #D-001206-13-05, siRNA1 anti-NOS2, Cat. #D-009240-01-0005, siRNA2 anti-NOS2, and Cat. #D-009240-02-0005). Cells were transfected with siRNA at a final concentration of 50 nM according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following a 6 h incubation, the medium was replaced with complete growth medium, and cells were cultured for an additional 48 or 72 h.

RealTime-Glo MT cell viability assay

To assess the cytotoxic effects of cisplatin and L-NMMA, both as monotherapies and combination therapy, on cell viability, OVCAR8 cells were seeded in white opaque 96-well plates (Greiner Bio-One, Cat. #655074) at a density of 1,500 cells per well in 100 μL of medium. After 24 h, cells were treated with varying concentrations of cisplatin and/or L-NMMA for 72 h. Following incubation, cell viability was determined using the MT Cell Viability Substrate and NanoLuc luciferase (Promega, Cat. #G9711), and luminescence was measured with a luminometer (GloMax Navigator, Promega, Cat. #E5302). Each experiment was conducted in triplicate, and dose-response curves were generated using GraphPad Prism software (v.9.5.1).

Colony formation assay

Colony formation assays were performed using OVCAR8 and A2780cis cells. Cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 500 cells per well. After incubation for 48 h, cells were transfected with scramble siRNA and NOS2 siRNAs and cultured for 10–14 days. Colonies were then stained with crystal violet and quantified by using ImageJ software. All experiments were independently repeated in triplicate.

Wound healing assay/IncuCyte live-cell analysis system

OVCAR8 and A2780cis cells were plated into Sartorius ImageLock plates at densities of 3 × 104 and 6 × 104 cells per well, respectively. After 24 h, uniform scratches were generated in each well using the Sartorius WoundMaker tool, followed immediately by transfection with scrambled or NOS2 siRNAs. Real-time monitoring of wound closure was performed using the IncuCyte Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius, Essen Bioscience), with images captured every 6 h over 3 days. Wound closure was quantified using the automated analysis algorithms provided in the IncuCyte S3 2022B Rev2 software (Sartorius, Essen Bioscience).

Total RNA extraction RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using RNeasy mini kit from QIAGEN (Cat. #74104) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA was reverse transcribed with the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #4368813) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RT-qPCR was performed using the QuantStudio 12K Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #4471134). The following primers for TaqMan Gene Expression Assay were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific; human VIM (Cat. #Hs00958111_m1) and human NOS2 (Cat. #Hs01075529_m1). The ACTB (Cat. #Hs01060665_g1) was used as an endogenous control, and relative mRNA expression was calculated using 2- ΔΔCT method.

Immunoblotting

Whole-cell lysate was extracted using cell signaling lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-hydrochloride [pH 7.5], 150 mM sodium chloride, 1 mM disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetate, 1 mM EGTA, 1% triton, 2.5 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 1 mM beta-glycerophosphate, 1 mM sodium orthovanadate, and 1 μg/mL leupeptin, Cat. #9803). Harvested cells were lysed for 15 min on ice and then centrifuged at 17,000× g for 15 min at 4°C. Protein concentrations were determined using the Thermo Fisher Scientific BCA Protein Assay kit (Cat. #23225). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE gel and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore Sigma, Cat. #NX0095). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated with primary antibody at 4°C (rabbit anti-iNOS [1:1,000, Cell Signaling, Cat. #95423], rabbit anti-vimentin [1:1,000, Cell Signaling, Cat. #5741], rabbit anti-B-actin horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated [1:7,000, Cell Signaling, Cat. #5125]) and then incubated with a secondary anti-rabbit horseradish peroxidase-conjugated antibody (secondary anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated antibody [1:3,000, Cell Signaling, Cat #7074]). Chemiluminescence signals were detected using Clarity ECL Western Blotting Substrates from Bio-Rad (Cat #1705060S).

Generation of KO clones using the double nickase assay

Transfections were performed in 6-well plates, with 1.5 × 105 cells seeded per well. All reagents used in the procedure, including the double nickase plasmid (SC-400666-NIC), the transfection reagent (SC-395739), and the transfection medium (SC-108062), were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. Puromycin was sourced from Gibco. For the transfection, 3 μg of the double nickase plasmid was resuspended in transfection medium and incubated for 5 min. The transfection reagent was prepared using the same procedure. The plasmid solution was then combined with the transfection reagent and incubated for 30 min at room temperature to allow complex formation. The resulting transfection mixture was added dropwise to the cells in antibiotic-free medium. After 5 h, the transfection solution was removed and replaced with fresh antibiotic-free medium. Forty-eight hours after transfection, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 1 μg/mL puromycin to initiate selection. Surviving clones were selected for green fluorescent protein (GFP), expanded, and screened for successful NOS2 KO using western blot analysis.

RNA-seq

Human ovarian cancer cells with NOS2-KO and corresponding control cells were subjected to bulk RNA-seq. Total RNA was extracted, and libraries were prepared using a poly(A) selection kit followed by paired-end sequencing on an Illumina platform. Raw sequencing reads were quality-checked and trimmed prior to alignment to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR. Gene-level counts were obtained with featureCounts, and differential expression analysis (DEG) between NOS2-KO and control groups was performed using DESeq2. Genes with an adjusted p value <0.05 and |log2-fold change| >1 were considered significant. Results were visualized as a heatmap of the top differentially expressed genes and a volcano plot summarizing fold changes and statistical significance. Functional enrichment of DEGs was assessed through Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis using clusterProfiler, with results presented as dot plots.

CHX treatment

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and incubated under standard culture conditions for 24 h. Following incubation, L-NMMA was added to the culture medium at a final concentration of 10 mM. After an additional 24 h, cycloheximide (CHX; Abcam, #ab120093) was administered at a final concentration of 10 μM. Cells were harvested at 2, 4, and 6 h post-CHX treatment, and vimentin degradation was analyzed by western blotting.

MG132 treatment

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and incubated under standard culture conditions for 24 h. L-NMMA was then added to the medium at a final concentration of 10 mM. After an additional 24 h, MG132 (Cell Signaling, #2194) was administered at concentrations of 1, 5, and 10 μM. Cells were collected 6 h after MG132 treatment for further analysis.

Immunoprecipitation

Cells were seeded in 10 cm culture dishes at a density of 5 × 105 cells per dish and incubated under standard conditions for 24 h. L-NMMA was then added at a final concentration of 6 mM, followed by MG132 at 5 μM. After 6 h of treatment, cells were harvested, and IP was performed using an anti-vimentin antibody (1:1,000; Abcam, #ab137321) with the Abcam IP kit (#ab206996), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Detection of ubiquitination was obtained using an anti-ubiquitin antibody (1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology, #58395).

Animal models

All animal protocols were approved by the Houston Methodist Hospital Research Institute Animal Care and Review Office (IACUC number IS00007220). For the syngeneic in vivo model, 6-week-old C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Charles River. ID8-Luc cells (5 × 106) were injected into mice i.p. in 200 μL PBS. Body weight and general health status were recorded throughout the study.

To generate the xenograft model, all mouse experiments were performed with 6-week-old NOD NSG mice obtained from Jackson Laboratory. OVCAR8-RFP-Luc (5 × 106) was injected into mice via i.p. injection in 200 μL of PBS. Mice were monitored for tumor growth and overall health, with body weight measured regularly using a laboratory balance. Bioluminescence signals from labeled cells were analyzed using the PerkinElmer In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS) Spectrum computed tomography (CT) under automatic luminescence detection mode.

Ascites immune profiling

The following mouse antibodies were used in a flow cytometry for analyzing mouse CD62L (Tonbo, ref. # 55-0251-U025), CD44 (Tonbo, ref. # 60-0441-U025), CD49b (Tonbo, ref. # 20-0491-U025), NK-1.1 (BDBiosciences, ref. # 562921), CD45 (Tonbo, ref. # 85-0451-U025), CD4 (Cytek, ref. #R7-20270), LY6c (Tonbo, ref. # 65-5932-U025), CD11b (BD Biosciences, ref. # 564985), Ghost Dye Violet540 (Tonbo, ref. # 13-0879-T100), TruStain FcX (CD16/32) (BioLegend, ref. # 101320).

Image analysis

H&E slides from mouse tissues were scanned as whole slide images (Tag Image File Format [TIFF]-based format) using a 20× objective with a DP200 scanner (Roche Diagnostics). The images were then analyzed using QuPath open-source software∗ v.0.5.1. Tumor deposits were identified and annotated by a consultant/attending level clinical histopathologist (SH). Thereafter, the cell detection parameters were set to default. A binary classifier type, which was trained to identify tumor cells and lymphocytes, was applied within the annotated areas (tumor spots) on the image to prevent overcalling of other cells, including mesothelial or other tissue as tumor cells. The tumor areas, tumor cell count, total area, and total cell count were calculated based on default cell parameters in QuPath and the classifier as described previously.

Mitotic counts were assessed visually per standard clinical assessment using 10 high-power fields. Lymphovascular invasion was also assessed by visual assessment of the slide by a consultant/attending level clinical histopathologist (SH).

Statistical analysis

Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or ordinary one-way ANOVA, when possible, was performed using GraphPad Prism v.7.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) to evaluate the effects of two independent factors and their interaction on the dependent variable. Prior to analysis, data were checked for normality and homogeneity of variances. When significant effects were detected, multiple comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s test to identify specific group differences. Results were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.
