Prevalence of Epstein-Barr virus infection in North African cancer patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis protocol
Authors: Yosra Souiden, Imen Ayouni, Sarra Mestiri, Sihem Hmissa, Nabiha Missaoui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpstein-Barr virus (EBV) is a well-recognized oncogenic virus associated with the development of various malignancies. In North Africa, EBV-related cancers, such as nasopharyngeal carcinoma, gastric cancer, and lymphomas, present a significant health challenge due to their high mortality rates. Despite the common occurrence of these malignancies, the prevalence and impact of EBV infection in this context remain largely unexamined. Addressing this knowledge gap is crucial for understanding the virus’s role in cancer pathogenesis and for informing effective prevention and treatment strategies.MethodsElectronic databases will be searched for literature published up to 2025 using a Boolean search approach combining common terms and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) for EBV infection, cancer, and North Africa. The review will only include studies carried out in North Africa that assess the prevalence of EBV infection in patients with cancer. Using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools, the quality and potential risk of bias of all selected full-text articles will be evaluated.DiscussionThis protocol outlines a systematic review and meta-analysis aiming to evaluate the prevalence of EBV infection in cancer patients in North Africa to better understand its role in the region’s cancer burden. Insights from this study will support improved prevention, early detection, and more personalized treatment strategies, ultimately aiding the reduction of cancer mortality in the region.Systematic review registrationThis protocol has been published in the PROSPERO International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (http://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO), under the registration number CRD42024594373.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-026-03257-z.

Introduction

Cancer poses a significant global health burden, accounting for approximately 20 million new cases and nearly 10 million deaths worldwide in 2022, according to the latest GLOBOCAN data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) [1]. In Africa, cancer is becoming a serious health concern that requires urgent action to address rising incidence and mortality. Despite a relatively low incidence rate—1,185,216 new cancer cases in Africa, representing only 5.9% of all cancer cases worldwide—the continent faces a disproportionately high mortality rate. In 2022, 763,843 cancer-related deaths occurred in Africa, corresponding to 64.5% of cases, with more than 20% in North Africa [1, 2]. Furthermore, projections indicate that by 2045, Africa will experience a 70% increase in new cancer cases, driven by factors such as population growth, aging, and infections [3].

Understanding the factors contributing to this growing burden is crucial. DNA mutations that interfere with normal cellular growth, division, and death are the primary cause of cancer. These changes are influenced by a combination of intrinsic and extrinsic factors. Internal factors comprise gene mutations, inflammatory and immunological responses, DNA replication errors, and modifiable elements such as age. External factors include infectious agents, smoking, dietary habits, and radiation exposure [4]. Infectious microorganisms, particularly viruses, are responsible for almost 20 percent of human malignancies. These pathogens can evade immune surveillance and trigger persistent inflammation. They may also insert their genetic material into host cells, disrupt regulatory pathways, and promote oncogenic transformation [5]. Cancer-causing viruses are categorized into DNA viruses—such as EBV, hepatitis B virus (HBV), human papillomaviruses (HPV), Merkel cell polyomavirus (MCPyV) and Kaposi sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV)—and RNA viruses, including human hepatitis C virus (HCV) and T-lymphotropic virus 1 (HTLV-1) [6].

EBV, alternatively referred to as human herpesvirus 4 (HHV-4), belongs to the γ subfamily of herpesviruses [7]. Its genome is made up of over 85 genes and is roughly 170 kb long of linear double-stranded DNA [7]. This highly prevalent virus infects over 90% of the global population [8–10]. Following primary infection, EBV establishes lifelong latency in B lymphocytes and is primarily transmitted through saliva [11, 12]. While most infections are asymptomatic, EBV has been implicated in the development of several malignancies. It contributes to approximately 1.5% of all human cancers worldwide [13].

The oncogenic role of EBV was first recognized through its association with Burkitt lymphoma (BL) in the 1960 s [14]. Since then, EBV has been linked to a spectrum of lymphoproliferative and epithelial malignancies [15]. Beyond BL, EBV is strongly associated with several lymphoproliferative disorders, notably diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma, and Hodgkin lymphoma, as well as less common entities such as primary effusion lymphoma and plasmablastic lymphoma [16]. Among epithelial cancers, EBV has a well-established link to gastric and nasopharyngeal carcinomas [17], and emerging evidence suggests a role in breast cancer [18]. EBV has also been associated—albeit less consistently—to other epithelial malignancies, including bladder cancer, cervical cancer, thyroid cancer, renal carcinoma, salivary gland lymphoepithelial carcinoma, lung lymphoepithelioma-like carcinoma, and leiomyomas/leiomyosarcomas in immunocompromised individuals [19–31].

The association between EBV and cancer is particularly evident in regions such as North Africa and Southeast Asia. In these areas, EBV-related malignancies, including gastric cancer (GC) and nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), show higher incidence rates. NPC, in particular, is endemic in Algeria, Morocco, and Tunisia. However, despite this epidemiological relevance, the prevalence of EBV infection among cancer patients in North Africa remains poorly characterized, and its role in carcinogenesis is still understudied [32–34]. EBV-positive GC also exhibits distinct clinicopathological features that warrant further investigation to clarify the virus’s contribution to disease development [35]. Assessing EBV prevalence across different tumor types and patient demographics is therefore essential to better understand EBV epidemiology and its potential oncogenic impact in this region.

Given the high proportion of late-stage cancer diagnoses in North Africa, improving early detection remains a major public health priority. Addressing this challenge requires a deeper understanding of EBV’s distribution across different cancers and population subgroups. Integrating EBV screening into diagnostic pathways may support earlier identification of EBV-associated malignancies when targeted toward high-risk populations. Importantly, evaluating EBV prevalence alongside key patient variables—such as age, gender, cancer type, and comorbidities—could help identify subgroups at heightened risk. Such multidimensional assessment could guide timely interventions, reduce cancer-related mortality, and facilitate the development of more personalized therapeutic strategies, especially for cancers such as NPC and GC, where clinical behavior and treatment response vary by EBV status [36, 37]. Beyond clinical insights, determining EBV prevalence in relation to demographic and clinical variables would inform tailored prevention strategies and support optimized resource allocation—an essential consideration in settings with limited healthcare infrastructure and restricted access to advanced therapies.

This systematic review and meta-analysis therefore aim to determine the prevalence of EBV infection among cancer patients in North Africa. It will also evaluate associated demographic and clinical factors, including any reported comorbidities. By clarifying EBV’s role in the regional cancer burden, this work will support improved prevention strategies and enhance early detection practices, thereby enabling more personalized approaches to cancer management in North Africa.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Studies assessing the prevalence of EBV infection among cancer patients in North Africa, including Tunisia, Algeria, Morocco, Libya, Egypt, Mauritania, Sudan, and Western Sahara.

Exclusion criteria

Studies conducted outside of North Africa and/or those that focused on cancer patients without EBV infection.

Study design/characteristics

Observational studies (i.e., cross-sectional, cohort, case-control) reporting the prevalence of EBV infection among cancer patients will be eligible for inclusion. Otherwise, studies that provide data enabling the calculation of these outcomes will also be taken into account. This review will exclusively include studies conducted in North Africa.

Population

This review will encompass patients diagnosed with malignancies and concurrent EBV infection.

Exposure

This refers to the presence of EBV infection among patients with malignancies.

Comparators (controls)

Cancer patients or healthy controls without EBV infection.

Outcomes

Prevalence will be defined as the proportion of all participants diagnosed with EBV infection among cancer patients.

Search strategy methods for the identification of studies

A thorough and comprehensive search strategy will be developed across four electronic databases—Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed, and Google Scholar—to identify studies reporting the prevalence of EBV infection in patients with malignancies. Searches will be conducted from the beginning to March 31, 2025. Further pertinent publications will be identified through gray literature and manual searches of reference lists from included studies. To ensure completeness, authors will be contacted for unpublished data or clarifications. Relevant non-English articles will also be considered, with translations or author input obtained when necessary. The search strategy will be tailored to each database and will incorporate both Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and relevant keywords. The detailed PubMed search methodology is presented in Table 1. Data management and study selection

All retrieved articles will be imported into Zotero citation manager (version 6.0.36) and then exported to Rayyan (https://www.rayyan.ai) [38]. Duplicates will first be identified using Rayyan’s built-in deduplication tool during the title and abstract screening phase, followed by manual verification to confirm true duplicates. After duplicate removal, two investigators (Y.S. and I.A.) will independently and concurrently screen the titles and abstracts of the remaining studies to exclude definitively irrelevant publications. The full texts of potentially relevant articles will then be retrieved and assessed. Additional duplicates, including instances of the same study published in different journals, will be identified during the full-text assessment. In such cases, only one version will be retained for data extraction. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion with a third investigator (N.M.).

Data collection process

Relevant information will be extracted from the text, tables, and figures and recorded in a standardized data extraction sheet designed specifically for this review by two investigators, Y. S. and I.A. This sheet will include the following details: first author, publication year, study design, population studied, sample size, sample type, patient characteristics (age, sex, pathological type, stage, grade, etc.), EBV detection method, EBV detection parameter and the proportion/number of EBV-positive cases. Data extraction will be conducted independently by two authors Y.S. and I.A. and discrepancies will be resolved by discussions with a third author N.M.

Risk of bias in individual studies

Authors Y.S. and I.A. will independently evaluate the risk of bias in the included studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute’s critical appraisal checklists for observational studies, which are tailored for cross-sectional studies, case–control studies, and cohort studies as appropriate [39]. Any disagreements in the risk of bias assessment between the two authors will be settled by consensus and discussion, with the assistance of a third author (N.M.). The outcomes of the risk-of-bias assessments and relevant data will be recorded in a standardized sheet for subsequent statistical analysis and data synthesis.

Quality of evidence

To assess the certainty of the evidence, the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [40, 41] will be applied manually for each outcome. The assessment will take into account the following GRADE domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

Data synthesis

Data analysis and statistical computations will be performed using RevMan version 5.4.1, provided by the Cochrane Collaboration. Cochran’s Q χ2 statistics and Higgins’ I2 statistic method will be used to assess heterogeneity among the considered studies [42]. A random-effects model will be applied to pool prevalence estimates in the meta-analysis. These estimates, along with their 95% confidence intervals, will be visually summarized using forest plots. I2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% will be interpreted as indicating low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [43]. Subgroup analyses will be conducted based on cancer type, study population, and tumor stage or grade. Additional variables such as study design and risk-of-bias assessment may also be considered. Publication bias will be visually assessed using funnel plots. Key characteristics of the included studies—such as study aim, population, cancer type, risk of bias, and main outcomes—will be presented in summary tables. This systematic review will follow standard methodological procedures and will be reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [44]. The study selection process, including reasons for exclusion, will be detailed using a PRISMA flow diagram, while tables will present the characteristics and outcomes of the included studies. The results of the quality assessment and the level of evidence will be described narratively. These findings will be supplemented with tables or graphs, along with GRADE summary tables to outline the certainty of the evidence.

Discussion

EBV has emerged as an important field of study in cancer, especially in North Africa where cancer epidemiology differs markedly from other regions [1, 45]. By investigating the prevalence of EBV among cancer patients in this region, we aim to shed light on the virus’s contribution to cancer development and progression within a distinct genetic, environmental, and socio-economic context. The prevalence of EBV varies amongst North African nations, such as Tunisia, Algeria, Morocco, Libya and Egypt, due to differences in healthcare systems, socio-economic conditions, and environmental influences like co-infections. For instance, there is a strong correlation between EBV and NPC in Algeria, Tunisia, Morocco, and Libya. However, in Egypt, EBV is more commonly associated with Hodgkin lymphoma [45, 46]. Furthermore, it has been shown that exposure to malaria and genetic predispositions affect the patterns of EBV-related malignancies, particularly in cases of BL [45]. These variations highlight the need for targeted research to understand the impact of EBV on cancer in North Africa. Even EBV’s role in oncogenesis is well known worldwide, research focused on specific regions proves crucial.

The high prevalence of EBV related cancers in North Africa is a big concern for both clinical practice and public health. Early detection of EBV in high-risk patients can lead to better diagnostic and treatment strategies especially for NPC and lymphomas [47, 48]. Antiviral and immunotherapy for EBV positive tumors and other targeted therapies can be guided by routine EBV screening in cancer diagnostics. Initiatives like vaccine development and infection control protocols can lead to decrease in EBV related cancer rates which will have long term impact on the region’s public health.

Despite recent advancements, significant gaps remain in the literature on EBV-related malignancies in North Africa. Larger, multicenter studies are needed to better understand regional variations in EBV prevalence. Most research has focused on limited types of cancers, such as HL and NPC [16, 32]. A systematic review and meta-analysis could help address these gaps. These methods would offer a broader view of EBV’s role across a wider range of cancers. They would also combine data from multiple studies and account for differences in methodology and region. Additionally, this approach would enhance statistical power. It would yield more reliable prevalence estimates and allow comparisons across different cancer types. These findings could guide public health strategies. They may include routine EBV screening, vaccine development, and targeted therapies for EBV-positive cancers. This could ultimately improve cancer outcomes and clinical care across North Africa.
