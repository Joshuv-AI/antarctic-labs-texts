The carbon isotope ratio of alanine is a biomarker of added sugar and sugar-sweetened beverage intakes: a pooled analysis of 4 studies
Authors: Jessica J Johnson, Sambit Ghosh, Pamela A Shaw, Marian L Neuhouser, Johanna W Lampe, Lesley F Tinker, Ross L Prentice, Natasha Tasevska, Laurence S Freedman, Bert B Boyer, Scarlett E Hopkins, Sarah H Nash, Susanne B Votruba, Jonathan Krakoff, Diane M O’Brien
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The alanine carbon isotope ratio (CIR) biomarker was positively associated with added sugar (AS) and sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) intakes in multiple studies from the United States. Association strengths varied, and Ala CIR also correlated with protein source in certain studies.Objectives:This study examined Ala CIR associations with AS and SSB intakes and animal protein ratio (APR; animal protein/total protein), and adjustment for APR, by pooling data from 4 previous studies.Methods:We pooled diet and biomarker data from 4 studies (n = 346). These included a cross-sectional study of Yup’ik Alaska Native adults (n = 62), a 12-wk randomized controlled feeding study in males (n = 32), a 2-wk habitual intake controlled feeding study in postmenopausal females (n = 153), and a 15-d habitual intake controlled feeding study of adults (n = 99). We estimated correlations between amino acid CIRs and diet and performed multivariable regression of Ala CIR on standardized intake variables to determine simultaneous associations with AS (g/d) or SSBs (servings/d) and APR. We included study by intake interactions to allow for heterogeneity among studies. We then performed models where leucine (Leu) CIR was included to adjust for APR.Results:There were positive correlations between Ala CIR and AS intake (r = 0.54; 95% CI: 0.46, 0.61), log-SSB intake (r = 0.63; 95% CI: 0.56, 0.69), and APR (r = 0.32; 95% CI: 0.22, 0.41). Study-specific slopes for the relationship between Ala CIR and AS or SSB intake were similar in models with and without adjustment for APR. Across studies, slopes ranged from 0.34 (95% CI: 0.08, 0.61) to 1.75 (95% CI: 1.29, 2.20) for AS intake in models with APR and from 0.35 (95% CI: 0.01, 0.68) to 1.11 (95% CI: 0.81, 1.40) for SSB intake in models with APR. Replacing APR with Leu CIR resulted in similar slopes between Ala CIR and AS/SSB intake.Conclusions:The Ala CIR is a robust biomarker of AS/SSB intake. Potential associations with APR can be adjusted for using a simultaneously measured biomarker.The DBD study was registered at clinicaltrials.gov as NCT01237093 and the NPAAS, an ancillary study of the Women’s Health Initiative, at clinicaltrials.gov as NCT00000611.Keywords: biomarker of food intake, nutrient biomarker, δ13 C of amino acids, biomarker study, stable isotopes

Introduction

Added sugar (AS) and sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) intakes have been positively associated with risks of obesity and diet-related chronic disease [1–5]. These associations are based on intakes estimated using dietary self-report, which incur various types of measurement error [6–8]. Underreporting of energy intake is common, is positively associated with BMI (in kg/m2), and is also related to macronutrient intake, making it difficult to align dietary intake amounts or patterns with outcomes [8]. The use of an objective biomarker of AS/SSB intake may help identify and adjust for error in self-report measurements [9–12]. The natural abundance carbon isotope ratio (13C/12C; CIR) is a potential biomarker of AS/SSB intake, because corn and sugar cane, the major contributors to United States added sugars and SSBs [13], have distinctly higher CIRs relative to other foods [14,15].

Studies of CIRs as biomarkers of AS and/or SSB intake have had mixed results [16–19], likely due to the use of corn in the livestock feed industry, which results in animal products that also have elevated CIRs [20,21]. The CIR of the nonessential amino acid (AA) alanine, which is linked to sugar metabolism via the glucose-alanine cycle [22], has shown promise as a more sensitive and specific biomarker of AS intake than whole tissue CIRs. The Ala CIR had a high, positive association with SSB intake and no statistically significant association with meat intake in 2 United States studies, 1 cross-sectional [23] and 1 highly controlled, in which participants were randomly assigned to either high or low AS/SSBs [24]. However, the Ala CIR was only moderately associated with AS intake and was also associated with animal protein intake in 2 short-term controlled feeding studies of usual intake with narrower distributions of AS intake and very low SSB intake [25,26]. There are several other AA CIRs, such as the leucine CIR, which are more highly correlated with animal protein [24–26]. Moreover, in studies where the Ala CIR was associated with animal protein, the use of additional AA CIRs improved biomarker models of AS intake, possibly by adjusting for animal protein intake or biomarker measurement error [25,26]. Thus, the performance of an Ala CIR-based AS biomarker may vary across study populations with varying dietary patterns, and SSB intake may be a stronger influence on the Ala CIR than AS intake more generally. Studies that include a wide range in sugar intake are needed to better understand the potential of the Ala CIR as an AS biomarker. Pooling data from different studies with diverse dietary patterns is an approach to achieve sufficient variability in sugar intake.

The objective of this study was to determine the Ala CIR, measured in plasma or serum, association with intakes of AS, SSB, and animal protein in a larger sample size and across wide distributions of these intakes by pooling biomarker and dietary data from a cross-sectional and 3 feeding studies conducted in adult (aged 18–82 y) populations within the United States. We pooled individual-level data and harmonized both AA CIR and dietary variables to enable explicit comparisons across studies and to address the following questions: 1) whether the Ala CIR is more strongly associated with SSB intake than AS intake, 2) how animal protein intake influences these associations, and 3) whether a second biomarker, the Leu CIR, can be used to account for animal protein intake. We modeled the Ala CIR response to AS, SSB, and animal protein intakes and included interaction terms with a study variable to allow for heterogeneity among studies. We also modeled the Ala CIR using the Leu CIR to test whether it could control for the influence of the animal protein intake variable.

Methods

Study cohorts and participant data

The 4 studies included in this pooled analysis were conducted within the United States and had varying designs (Table 1), but they all had the primary objective of testing relationships between dietary biomarkers and dietary intakes. The Center for Alaska Native Health Research (CANHR) Neqem Nallunailkutaa (The Foods’ Marker) study of the University of Alaska Fairbanks (UAF), hereafter referred to as the CANHR study, was a cross-sectional study in which male and female participants (n = 68) were recruited from 2 Yup’ik Alaska Native communities in southwest Alaska between 2008 and 2009 [23, 27]. Usual dietary intakes were assessed using self-report methods: participants completed 4 nonconsecutive 24-h recalls over a 4-wk span. The recalls were interviewer-administered using the Nutrition Data System for Research (NDSR; Nutrition Coordinating Center, Minneapolis, MN; version 2008). Fasting blood was drawn ~2 wk after the final recall. Nonessential AA CIRs were measured in red blood cells (RBCs), and their positive associations with SSB and AS intakes were published [23]. The present study includes a previously unpublished reanalysis of AA CIRs from the CANHR study, using plasma samples and updated methods for AA CIR measurement, to harmonize with other studies. Further, we only included participants (n = 62) who were 18 y or older at the time of consent.

The Developing Biomarkers of Diet (DBD) study was a randomized controlled feeding study (clinicaltrials.gov identifier: NCT01237093) to test the simultaneous effects of SSB, meat, and fish intakes on natural abundance isotopic biomarkers [19,24]. The primary outcomes of the DBD study were changes in the total carbon and nitrogen stable isotope ratios, measured in plasma, RBCs, and hair, due to the dietary intervention [19]. Amino acid CIRs were added as outcomes after the clinical trial had begun. The DBD study was conducted at the Obesity and Diabetes Research Center of the National Institute of Diabetes, Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) in Phoenix, AZ between 2010 and 2020. The participant flow chart for this study is Supplemental Figure 1. Male participants (n = 32) were domiciled at the research facility for 12 wk after random assignment to 1 of 8 weight-maintaining diets, which differed in the presence/absence of SSBs (14% kcal/d), meat (19% kcal/d), and fish (6% kcal/d) in all possible combinations. Fasting blood was sampled every 2 wk. In this pooled study, we include previously published AA CIR measurements derived from plasma samples collected postintervention from all completed participants [24]. The menus for each of the 8 diets were used to derive dietary intake variables for the present study, as described further below.

The Nutrition and Physical Activity Assessment Study (NPAAS) feeding study was a controlled feeding study, conducted between 2011 and 2013, based on participants’ habitual intake: free-living participants were provided with all foods and most beverages according to individualized menus based on their usual diets [25,28]. The NPAAS feeding study was an ancillary study of the Women’s Health Initiative (WHI), and its participants were postmenopausal females (n = 153) recruited from the WHI study center in Seattle, WA. The participant flow chart for this study is Supplemental Figure 2. Prior to the feeding study, the usual diet of each participant was assessed using a 4-d food record and a standardized interview with the study dietitian. From this, 14 d of controlled diet data were prepared with adjustment for food record biases, especially regarding total energy intake. Mean dietary intake variables of the consumed foods were derived from the provided foods less the weight of any returned foods. Fasting blood was drawn at baseline and on the final day of the feeding period. The positive associations between postfeeding serum AA CIRs and dietary intakes, including AS and animal protein, were published [25]. This pooled study includes previously unpublished AA CIR data measured in postfeeding plasma samples.

The Arizona feeding study had a very similar design to the NPAAS feeding study in that controlled diets were based on participants’ usual diets [29]. The research team at Arizona State University recruited males and females (n = 100) across age and BMI groups in Phoenix, AZ, between 2016 and 2019. The participant flow chart for this study is Supplemental Figure 3. The usual diets of participants were assessed using 2 consecutive, 7-d weighed food records. During the 15-d controlled feeding period, participants were given ~1.25 times the amount of food reported in their food records and were allowed to consume as much as they wanted from the provided foods. Mean dietary intakes were derived from the provided foods less any returned foods, which were weighed. Fasting blood was drawn at 3 time points: at baseline, on the final day of the feeding period, and 5 wk postfeeding. The associations between final day postfeeding serum AA CIRs and dietary intakes, including AS and the animal protein ratio (APR), were published, with AA CIR data excluded for n = 1 participant [26]. These data are included in this pooled analysis.

We report limited demographic and anthropometric characteristics in this pooled study, and additional participant characteristics can be found in the previously published studies cited above. In this study, we include participant age (y), sex, BMI, and body weight (kg).

Ethics/institutional review board approval

The CANHR study and its inclusion in this pooled study were separately approved by the UAF institutional review board (IRB) and the Yukon-Kuskokwim Health Corporation Human Studies Committee and Executive Board. The DBD feeding study was approved by the NIDDK IRB, and the inclusion of data in this pooled study was approved by the UAF IRB. The WHI project and its ancillary studies were reviewed and approved by the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center (Fred Hutch) IRB, and the reanalysis of the NPAAS feeding study samples and the inclusion of those data in this study were determined to be not human subjects research by the UAF IRB. The AZ feeding study was approved by the Arizona State University IRB, and the inclusion of data in the pooled study was approved by the UAF IRB. All participants for each study signed written informed consent.

Stable isotopic biomarker measurements in serum/plasma

The AA CIR analysis methods for the DBD and AZ feeding studies are detailed in the studies by Johnson et al. [24,26], and the new AA CIR measurements for the CANHR study and the NPAAS feeding study were made using the same sample preparation methods (i.e., acid hydrolysis, N-acetyl methyl ester derivatization, and phosphate buffer purification) described in these studies. CIR measurements of derivatized AAs were made using gas chromatography combustion-linked isotope ratio mass spectrometry (GC-C-IRMS). Samples were measured using duplicate or triplicate injections onto a TRACE 1310 GC interfaced to a Delta V Plus IRMS via an IsoLink II System (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The CIR is measured and reported as a delta (δ) value relative to Vienna Peedee Belemnite (VPDB; 13C/12C = 0.0111802), according to the following: δ13C = (13C/12Csample – 13C/12CVPDB) /(13C/12CVPDB) and reported in parts per thousand, or per mil (‰), units [30].

The derivatization of AAs adds carbon atoms and can incur kinetic isotope effects; thus, the CIRs of derivatized AAs must be calibrated against the CIRs of derivatized standard AAs with known values [31]. Single-point calibration has been common practice for GC-C-IRMS measurements of AA CIRs and was used in the studies cited earlier [24,26]. The new AA CIR measurements for the CANHR study and the NPAAS feeding study were made using a batch-specific, 3-point calibration that was separately defined for each AA. We used commercial AA standards at natural abundance for the low δ13C standard and used them to dilute isotopically enriched (98%) AA standards to achieve target δ13C values for the medium and high δ13C standards. We list the commercial AA standards used in the 3-point calibration and for quality assurance in Supplemental Table 1.

We prepared and analyzed paired plasma and serum samples in a subset of participants (n = 28) from the AZ feeding study. We used final day postfeeding samples and the single-point AA CIR calibration for this supplementary analysis. These data are not included in the pooled analysis but were used to evaluate whether AA CIRs measured in plasma and serum are comparable.

The number of AAs for which CIR measurements are reported varied across studies. In this pooled analysis, we report the CIRs for 6 AAs that have known, reproducible correlations with diet and lower measurement error relative to other AAs. These were the nonessential AAs—alanine, a combined measure of glutamic acid and glutamine, and proline—and the essential AAs—leucine, phenylalanine, and valine. The between batch reproducibility of AA CIRs was determined using an AA quality assurance standard and a laboratory plasma sample included in each batch (Supplemental Table 2).

Dietary variable harmonization

Dietary intakes were calculated or recalculated, as necessary, in an identical manner across the 4 pooled studies. Three studies already had nutrient and food level data exported from NDSR: the CANHR study (version 2008), the NPAAS feeding study (version 2010), and the AZ feeding study (version 2016–2018). For the DBD study, each of the 8 weight-maintaining diets was made up of repeating 6-d menus. We entered the 2200-kcal menus for each diet into NDSR (version 2021) and multiplied nutrient and food intakes by an energy (kcal) scaling factor, which is the unit specific formula used to calculate weight-maintaining calories for males on the domiciled unit: kcal/d = 9.5 kcal/d × body weight (kg) + 1973/d kcal [32]. Scaled nutrient and food intakes were averaged over the 6-d diet menus to obtain final mean daily values.

We extracted the following intakes from NDSR nutrient files: energy (kcal/d), total sugar (g/d), AS (g/d), total protein (g/d), animal protein (g/d), and vegetable protein (g/d), then we summed nutrient intakes by day for each individual. We calculated AS density (% kcal/d) as daily kcal from AS divided by daily total energy (kcal). We calculated the APR as daily animal protein (g/d) divided by daily total protein (g/d). We determined daily intakes of SSBs (servings/d) and meat (g/d) using NDSR food group and servings count files. Our definition of an SSB serving included 8 oz of a caloric soft drink, sweetened fruit drink, sweetened prepared tea or coffee drink, sweetened nondairy milk, caloric energy drink, or caloric nondairy meal replacement beverage, and we assigned 1/2 SSB servings to 1 cup servings of sweetened dairy beverages [25,26]. Each serving of SSB was multiplied by its AS content at the food level before averaging at the day level to obtain AS from SSBs (g/d). Our definition of meat included all servings of red meat and poultry, including processed, and each 4 oz serving was converted to g before averaging by day. For the CANHR study, red meat and poultry servings were coded as either market (i.e., purchased at the store) or traditional (i.e., wild game) [23]. Only servings coded as market were included in the meat (g/d) variable in this pooled analysis. We list the NDSR food group codes used to define SSB and meat servings in Supplemental Table 3.

Statistical methods

We calculated summary statistics (median and 25th, 75th percentiles) in the pooled sample for participant characteristics, AA CIR measures, and mean daily values of key dietary intakes. We calculated Pearson correlations between AA CIRs and dietary intakes, after checking these relationships for linearity in the pooled sample. We log-transformed SSB intake [ln(servings/d) + 1] and meat intake [ln(g/d) + 1] before calculating correlations. Correlations in the pooled sample do not account for differences across studies, the most salient of which pertained to the CANHR study, which had a different design/dietary assessment method and a distinct background diet relative to the other studies. We performed a sensitivity analysis, calculating diet-biomarker correlations excluding the CANHR study, to account for potential confounding due to the higher overall AA CIR values in the CANHR study, which results from a diet with high marine food intakes [33]. We also determined the partial correlations between AS/SSB intake and the Ala CIR after regressing both variables on the APR. We conducted all statistical analyses in R version 4.3.1 (R Core Team, 2023).

We used multivariable linear regression models to determine the relationship between the Ala CIR and AS/SSB intake, without and with adjustment for animal protein (i.e., APR), in the pooled sample. We did not use random-effects models or meta-analysis methods because of the small number of included studies; instead, we included a dummy variable for study in our linear regression models. We included an interaction between the study variable and each of the dietary intake variables to allow for heterogeneity in these relationships across studies. We also tested an interaction between AS/SSB intake and APR to explore whether the relationship between the Ala CIR and AS or SSB intake varied at different levels of APR (i.e., whether there was effect modification). Although we had a priori determined it was necessary to include a variable for study, we did also regress Ala CIR on AS or SSB intakes without a variable for study to demonstrate the additional variation explained when accounting for study.

We performed separate regressions for each of 4 sugar variables: AS (g/d), AS density (% daily kcal), SSBs (servings/d), and AS from SSBs (g/d). We chose to include the APR, rather than animal protein (g/d) or meat (g/d) intake, because this variable had the highest correlation to the Ala CIR in the pooled sample. Predictor variables were mean centered and scaled to their respective SDs. We used robust sandwich variance estimators from the sandwich package [34] to account for potential heteroskedasticity in the residuals. We checked model residuals for linear associations with sex, age, BMI, and body weight to determine whether any of these participant characteristics should be included. We calculated study-specific slopes for the association between the Ala CIR and AS/SSB intakes in all models, and we plotted fitted regression results to visualize these associations. Slopes were calculated and fitted regression plots were made using the interactions package [35].

The Leu CIR was the AA CIR most highly associated with meat intake [24] and the APR [26] in 2 previous studies, and so we focused on this additional biomarker as a way to control for the APR. We explored whether the Leu CIR could account for the influence of APR on the Ala CIR by replacing APR with the Leu CIR in models of the Ala CIR. Again, we included interactions with study, so that we could calculate and visualize study-specific slopes. We then tested for correlation between Ala CIR model residuals and the APR.

We used 2 one-sided t tests [36] to determine whether AA CIRs measured in serum were equivalent to AA CIRs measured in plasma. We used the “t_TOST” function for paired samples from the TOSTER package [37] and set the upper and lower equivalence bounds to ±0.3‰, which is smaller than the mean analytical error for the 6 AA CIRs tested, as previously reported for the AZ feeding study [26]. Statistically significant P values (α = 0.025) were used to reject the dual null hypotheses that the mean difference between serum and plasma AA CIRs was lower than −0.3‰ or higher than 0.3‰ (i.e., analytical error).

Results

The participant characteristics for the pooled study population (n = 346) are summarized in Table 2. Due to the larger sample size from NPAAS, the pooled study population was in the majority female (68%) and over 55 y. Body weight and BMI were approximately normally distributed in the study population, and their median values were 71.8 kg and 26.4, respectively. The median AS intake was 56.5 g/d and was 10.5% of daily total energy. Median SSB intake was low at 0.2 servings/d, and nonconsumers (n = 45) made up 13% of the study population. Median APR was 0.66.

The Ala CIR had positive correlations to AS intake, SSB intake, AS density, and AS from SSBs in the pooled study sample (Table 3). The highest overall correlation was between the Ala CIR and AS from SSBs (r = 0.64; 95% CI: 0.58, 0.70). The Ala CIR also correlated with the APR, although this correlation was approximately half the size of the correlations with AS/SSB intakes. The Glx CIR also had positive correlations with the AS and SSB intakes; however, they were similar in size to its correlation with the APR. The Leu CIR had the highest correlation to the APR (r = 0.63; 95% CI: 0.56, 0.69). The only AA CIR with no correlation to the APR was the Phe CIR, which instead was positively associated with meat intake. In the sensitivity analysis excluding the CANHR study, the positive correlations between the Ala CIR and the AS/SSB intakes persisted, although they were lower (e.g., AS intake r = 0.44) (Supplemental Table 4). There were also higher correlations in the sensitivity analysis between meat intake and the AA CIRs, except for the Ala CIR. The APR did not have a strong effect on the relationship between the Ala CIR and AS/SSB intake in the pooled sample. The partial correlations of Ala CIR with AS and SSB intakes, after regressing both variables on APR, were r = 0.57 (95% CI: 0.50, 0.64) for AS and r = 0.65 (95% CI: 0.59, 0.71) for SSB intake. In linear models of the Ala CIR that accounted for differences in study, there were significant positive associations with AS and SSB intakes across all studies, except in the NPAAS feeding study before adjustment for APR (Figure 2, Tables 4 and 5). The interaction with study was significant, and the DBD study had a higher slope (1.63; 95% CI: 1.19, 2.08; and 1.09; 95% CI: 0.80, 1.38) for the relationship between AS and SSB intake, respectively, and the Ala CIR than the nonrandomized studies (model 1) (Tables 4 and 5). The slopes were very similar in models of the Ala CIR that adjusted for APR (model 2) (Tables 4 and 5). The interaction between study and the APR was also significant; however, the relationship between the APR and the Ala CIR was not significant for every study (model 2) (Supplemental Tables 5 and 6). In regressions of the Ala CIR on AS intake with adjustment for APR, the study-specific slopes for APR were significantly positive for the AZ (0.65; 95% CI: 0.41, 0.89) and NPAAS (0.37; 95% CI: 0.18, 0.57) feeding studies. The APR slopes for these 2 studies were also significantly positive in regressions on SSB intake (AZ slope = 0.68; 95% CI: 0.44, 0.91; NPAAS slope = 0.38; 95% CI: 0.19, 0.57). In models that included the interaction between AS/SSB intake and the APR, this interaction was not significant (model 4) (Supplemental Tables 5 and 6), and the relationship between the Ala CIR and AS/SSB intake remained consistent within studies across varying levels of the APR, with slight increases with higher APR and small intercept shifts (Supplemental Tables 7 and 8). Residuals of Ala CIR models that included only AS or SSB intake were correlated to the APR: r = 0.20 (95% CI: 0.10, 0.30) for AS intake and r = 0.22 (95% CI: 0.12, 0.32) for SSB intake. There were no associations between participant characteristics and the residuals of models including either AS or SSB intake (Supplemental Figure 4).

We did not find significant differences in any of the 6 AA CIRs, measured in paired plasma and serum samples (n = 28), that exceeded analytical error. The results of this equivalence test can be found in Supplemental Table 15.

Discussion

In this pooled analysis, we made explicit comparisons of the Ala CIR biomarker’s relationship to intake of AS, SSB, and the APR across 4 studies with diverse designs and underlying diets. We found positive associations between the Ala CIR and both AS and SSB intakes in the pooled sample and across individual studies. The proportion of animal-sourced protein (i.e., APR) in the diet affected the Ala CIR in 2 studies. However, we found that the Leu CIR, an additional biomarker that is co-measured with the Ala CIR, can be used to account for the effect of APR on the Ala CIR.

The data harmonization in this pooled analysis allowed direct comparison of studies that previously did not have comparable dietary and/or biomarker data. In the randomized controlled study (DBD), the dietary exposures were varied as presence/absence, and continuous AS and SSB intakes had not been determined. After placing these data on the same scale as the other studies, it was evident that the intake-biomarker relationship in the DBD study was higher than in the other studies. The DBD study was a domiciled feeding study and had the highest level of dietary control among the studies, which may have contributed to the higher associations. The wide range of intake, with half of the participants in this study below the 25th percentile and the other half above the 75th percentile of SSB intake in the pooled sample, was likely another contributing factor.

We did not find evidence that the Ala CIR was more sensitive to SSB than AS intake, as might be expected from the higher proportion of corn and sugar cane-derived sugars in SSBs in the United States relative to AS [38]. This comparison has only been made once before within the CANHR study, where the RBC Ala CIR had a slightly higher correlation with SSBs (r = 0.70) than with AS intake (r = 0.59) [23]. SSB intake was so low in 2 of the previous studies that the relationship was not assessed [25,26]. Here, the correlation between the Ala CIR and SSB intake was again slightly higher than the association with AS in the pooled sample. However, Ala CIR models including either SSB or AS intake had similar R2 values. SSBs remain the top source of AS for adults [39] and children and adolescents [40] in the United States. Thus, it may not be important from an epidemiological standpoint to differentiate the 2 intakes.

Another objective of this study was to characterize the influence of APR on the relationship between the Ala CIR and AS/SSB intakes. Although we found a significant correlation between the APR and the Ala CIR in the pooled sample, it was smaller than the correlation with either AS or SSB intake. Further, in linear models, this association was significant in only 2 studies, the NPAAS and AZ feeding studies. This variation may be due to differences in the types of animal protein sources, suggesting that the effect of the APR on the Ala CIR differs across populations. We also tested for effect modification by APR on the relationship between AS/SSB intake and the Ala CIR by including an interaction between APR and AS/SSB intakes in the linear models. This interaction term was not significant, however, the sample size and the relatively narrow distribution for APR (i.e., only n = 2 with APR < 0.25) may not have provided enough power for the detection of a significant interaction. Thus, the potential effect of APR should continue to be considered in future studies.

We tested whether the Leu CIR could be used to adjust for the APR in models of the Ala CIR and found that including the Leu CIR removed residual correlations with the APR. The Leu CIR controlled for effects of APR on Ala CIR in studies where they were present (i.e., the NPAAS and AZ feeding studies). The Leu CIR captures APR, because as an essential AA, bodily Leu and its CIR [41] come directly from dietary protein sources. The same sources of CIR variation (i.e., corn and corn-fed meat) in dietary Ala will drive CIR variation in Leu, and this allows the Leu CIR to control for them. The Leu CIR is obtained in the same analysis as the Ala CIR, making it a convenient biomarker of the APR in this context.

The relative validity of the Ala CIR, as with any other biomarker of food or nutrient intake, will depend on the application [42]. The evidence suggests that the Ala CIR will respond in the context of an intervention, where intake of AS or SSBs is changing against a relatively stable background diet. The caveats to this are the relatively long half-life (i.e., 2.2 wk) of the Ala CIR [24] and that it is only sensitive to sugars derived from sugar cane or corn and so may be less sensitive in geographic areas with high beet sugar intake. This second caveat of having varying sensitivity to certain components within a food or to certain foods within a food group is shared by metabolomic biomarkers and should not be viewed as an unacceptable limitation. For example, the SSB biomarker panel of Gibbons et al. [43] was made up of cola metabolites, and the most informative SSB biomarkers across separate cohorts of children, adolescents, and young adults were caffeine metabolites [44]. Future studies should test the best ways to combine multiple biomarkers that capture different aspects of the same intake, for example, modeling presence/absence of SSBs with metabolites and modeling quantity with the Ala CIR.

More work is needed to validate the Ala CIR as a biomarker that can be used in epidemiological contexts [42,45]. The current study reiterates the robustness of the Ala CIR biomarker across varying populations. However, the dose-response relationship has not been determined in a randomized intervention, as the DBD study only varied the presence, and not the amount, of SSB intake. Nor has a well-validated predictive intake-biomarker relationship been established in a free-living population, despite significant relationships in 2 of the studies included in this pooled analysis [25,26]. Both laboratory and statistical methods for reducing the analytical error of AA CIRs should be explored [46], as this will make quantitative biomarker-diet relationships more precise. A related target criterion is reproducibility across laboratories; all AA CIR measurements in this pooled study were made in the same laboratory.

The primary strength of this pooled analysis was the individual-level harmonization of dietary and biomarker data, which allowed us to make explicit comparisons across studies. The use of similar sample types and laboratory methods minimized differences in AA CIR measurements across studies due to analytical error. The main limitations were the low number of studies included and the low number of high AS and SSB consumers, as well as low APR consumers, which contributed to high uncertainty in effect estimates despite the larger sample size. The low number of studies prohibited random-effects modeling and meta-analysis, which can be used to determine the relative importance of between-study variation and dietary effects. Although our results show the Ala CIR is a robust biomarker in widely varying background diets, the included studies are not representative of all dietary patterns in the United States, and further validation may be warranted where the study location or population has a distinct diet. The generalizability of the biomarker to youth populations also needs to be tested.

In summary, this study synthesized the studies to date testing the Ala CIR as a biomarker of AS and SSB intakes in the United States. This quantitative comparison complements qualitative and semi-quantitative reviews that cite the Ala CIR as a biomarker of AS or SSB intake [13,42,45,47]. The Ala CIR can be used to capture changes in AS or SSB intake in United States diets of varying composition and thus is appropriate to test the efficacy of dietary or behavioral interventions. The influence of dietary protein source on the Ala CIR may be controlled for using the Leu CIR. Further studies with wider ranges of AS or SSB intake should be conducted, so that we can test the ability of the Ala CIR in combination with the Leu CIR and other biomarkers to capture intake without bias in an epidemiological context.
