Self‐control and the sustainable citizen
Authors: Nathalie André, Roy Baumeister
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSustainable development requires individuals to make mindful, long‐term choices that preserve the planet for future generations. Becoming a sustainable citizen involves awareness, responsibility, and a commitment to long‐term thinking—qualities that rely heavily on self‐control. Although self‐control is a limited resource, it can be trained and applied across various domains, contributing to healthier lifestyles and reduced environmental impact. Yet, this ideal faces strong resistance in societies that prioritize immediate gratification, consumerism, and comfort. Bridging the gap between sustainable intentions and actual behavior requires supportive social, educational, and institutional frameworks as proposed by the conceptual model of the two‐way dynamics of sustainable citizenship. By fostering environments that make self‐discipline easier and more meaningful, individual actions can eventually lead to collective change and the redefinition of societal norms. This suggests that effective sustainability policies should combine individual self‐control interventions with structural changes that reduce the cognitive burden of pro‐environmental choices.

CHOOSING SUSTAINABILITY: WHY SELF‐CONTROL MATTERS

Sustainable development means living, producing, and consuming today without destroying the planet's resources or ecological balance, so that today's (and tomorrow's!) children can also enjoy a good quality of life. We might therefore define a sustainable citizen as someone who reflects carefully on the consequences of their actions for the planet and for others, participates actively in society rather than remaining passive, and prioritizes long‐term thinking over immediate gratification. In this sense, the sustainable citizen acts in a responsible, conscious, and committed way in the face of environmental, social, and economic challenges.

However, such choices often require resisting immediate desires or indeed overriding humans' aversion to wasting effort (André et al., 2026; Brehm & Self, 1989). Self‐control thus emerges as a foundational skill for becoming a sustainable citizen. Self‐control can be defined as the ability to alter one's responses, especially in the direction of desirable goals and standards. Abundant evidence indicates that the individual's powers of self‐control are limited (Baumeister et al., 2000, 2024). Willpower (i.e. the energy needed to exert effortful self‐control) is periodically low, which enables impulsive and automatic processes to take over, sometimes with maladaptive results. Fortunately, however, the individual's powers of self‐control can be increased by exercising self‐control, and indeed, regularly exercising self‐control in one domain will improve self‐control in other domains (Baumeister et al., 2006; Friese et al., 2017; Muraven et al., 1999).

Many behaviors in everyday life require the ability to control impulses. Particularly in the health domain, behaviors such as jogging, flossing, or avoiding harmful habits depend on self‐control. Individuals who are able to regulate such healthy or risky habits tend to enjoy better physical and mental health. Self‐control hence contributes to personal well‐being. Self‐control is equally beneficial for the environment. People who manage their habits well can achieve a smaller ecological footprint. For example, they may choose to walk or cycle instead of using vehicles that burn fossil fuels, or carpool instead of driving alone. Even recycling requires a measure of self‐control (though its global benefits are debated). So does using less electricity, which may require accepting lower indoor temperatures in winter or less air conditioning in summer.

Likewise, buying locally or supporting small‐scale producers can reduce emissions linked to long‐distance shipping. Reducing overall waste, planning meals to avoid food waste, or avoiding unnecessary purchases are all behaviors that demand some form of habit control, which includes planning, anticipating, and resisting impulsive consumption (e.g. Li et al., 2021; Wyss et al., 2022).

Empirical evidence indicates that trait self‐control has a small effect on specific pro‐environmental behaviors (r = .001–.12) but shows a stronger association with environmental well‐being (r ≈ .20; Strömbäck et al., 2023). Although self‐control is not the primary driver of sustainable action, it plays a consistent and meaningful role by helping individuals regulate emotions, resist short‐term temptations, and maintain long‐term environmental goals. These regulatory capacities may operate at both individual and collective levels, supporting sustained engagement in pro‐environmental behaviors (Table 1).

THREE MAJOR CONTRADICTIONS BETWEEN MODERN SOCIETY AND SUSTAINABLE CITIZENSHIP

THE CHALLENGE OF SELF‐CONTROL IN A SOCIETY ORIENTED TOWARD EASE

Self‐control is a skill that develops gradually throughout childhood and adolescence, and it relies heavily on the educational, familial, and social environment. Yet this development faces a natural obstacle: All else being equal, human beings tend to choose the path of least resistance due to biological mechanisms that aim to avoid wasting effort. Reconciling this inclination with the need for deliberate and sustained personal investment is a major educational and societal challenge.

At the same time, if society expects responsible citizens, it must also address the contradictions it itself generates. It is unrealistic to expect individuals to resist excessive consumption when the environment—through advertising, marketing strategies, and economic discourse—constantly encourages it. Supporting self‐control means designing environments that make restraint not only possible but also desirable.

FROM THE INDIVIDUAL TO THE GLOBAL: A TWO‐WAY DYNAMIC MODEL

Self‐control is a cross‐cutting skill that can trigger positive spillover effects—benefits that reduce the effort needed to adopt other sustainable behaviors (Figure 1). When individuals practice self‐discipline in one area (e.g. food), they may find it easier to apply it in another (e.g. energy use). Studies show that pro‐environmental behaviors can produce positive or negative spillovers (e.g. Van der Werff & Steg, 2018). Whereas trait self‐control weakly predicts specific green actions, it relates more strongly to environmental well‐being (Strömbäck et al., 2023), and self‐control can be strengthened through practice (Audiffren et al., 2022). As shown in Table 1, self‐control interventions likewise increase sustainable behaviors, suggesting that training self‐control may initiate cross‐domain behavioral cascades that support more consistent environmental engagement. Implementations would typically consider self‐control training interventions (habit formation programs and planning and monitoring strategies) and contextual approaches such as environmental restructuring (nudges and default options) and group interventions that establish shared social norms for sustainable behaviors.

The conceptual model illustrates how individual sustainable actions can either be facilitated by existing social norms (collective to individual) or contribute to establishing new norms through barrier‐breaking efforts (individual to collective), creating a reinforcing cycle between personal agency and societal transformation.

Moving from the individual level to broader societal change, however, is often more difficult than the reverse. Yet this shift can occur when enough individuals make similar choices, even if initially uncoordinated. The accumulation of these actions can eventually reshape social norms and produce collective change—possibly reaching a tipping point that transforms global outcomes.

To overcome these contradictions, self‐control must be encouraged and supported by contextual factors. This support can take many forms: increasing individuals' moral commitment and self‐awareness, strengthening habits so that sustainable behaviors become automatic, or shaping institutional and social contexts through reminders, incentives, and shared norms. Ultimately, making effort acceptable by restructuring the environment could be one of the most effective ways to induce lasting behavioral change (André et al., 2024).

CONCLUDING REMARKS

Self‐control can contribute to the immediate and future health of individuals, as well as to the long‐term sustainability of our planet's resources. Although the capacity for self‐control is limited, it can be improved by exercise. Therefore, and fortunately, using self‐control for either personal or planetary benefit can improve one's powers to achieve the other benefit also. Future research should examine how self‐control capacity interacts with policy interventions and socioeconomic contexts to maximize environmental impact. Ultimately, sustainable citizenship emerges not from self‐control alone but from the synergy between individual agency and supportive environments—a reminder that collective transformation requires both personal discipline and institutional courage.
