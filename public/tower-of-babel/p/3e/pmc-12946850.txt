Prenatal and postnatal atrazine-induced endocrine and ovarian chemical biotransformation protein disruption in offspring
Authors: Omotoyosi Adeyanju, Maria Estefanía González-Alvarez, Collins Antwi-Boasiako, Mary J Laws, Amy T Desaulniers, Aileen F Keating
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtrazine (ATZ) is an endocrine-disrupting chemical, and ATZ exposure during perinatal development is linked to reproductive dysfunction and behavioral abnormalities in adulthood. Gestational ATZ exposure can adversely affect birth weight, fetal growth, and onset of puberty. To investigate ovarian effects of ATZ exposure on female offspring, timed pregnant nulliparous gilts were provided ad libitum access to water that contained vehicle control (CT; 0.002% (v/v) ethanol; n = 3) or an environmentally relevant ATZ dose (20 µg/L; n = 4); thus piglets were exposed from gestation day 28 through farrowing and lactation (∼99 days). Umbilical cord blood serum was assayed for hormone concentrations, ovarian follicles were classified and counted, and abundance of proteins involved in folliculogenesis, steroidogenesis, chemical biotransformation, DNA damage, and cell viability in piglet ovaries were quantified by Western blotting. Exposure to ATZ decreased (P < .05) body weight at postnatal day 10, ovarian abundance of cytochrome P450 (CYP) isoform 2E1 (CYP2E1), ATP-binding cassette subfamily B member 1 (ABCB1), CYP11A1, peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor α, CYP1A1, CYP1B1, pAKT, RAD51, and serum progesterone. There was a tendency (.05 < P < .10) for reduced birth weight, serum 17β-estradiol, atretic follicle number, and ovarian GSTP1 and for increased PARP1 abundance after ATZ exposure. Overall, this study suggests that ATZ exposure during gestation and lactation may impair ovarian function.Keywords: ovary, atrazine, endocrine

In utero development is critical for proper cellular differentiation and physiological integrity [1]. Disruptions during embryonic and neonatal development can permanently reprogram organ systems, with some alterations persisting into adulthood [2-4]. In the ovary, xenobiotic exposure during prenatal and early postnatal life can alter fertility and adversely affect germ cells, thus affecting future generations [2, 5]. Given the precision required for gametogenesis, interference by xenobiotics during these developmental windows poses a threat to reproductive health.

Atrazine (ATZ) is a preemergent and postemergent broadleaf triazine herbicide used on a wide variety of crops [6, 7]. In the United States, ATZ is the second most widely used herbicide [7] and is environmentally persistent, due to abiotic hydrolysis and sunlight resistance [8]. Environmental degradation of ATZ is mediated primarily by microbial activity, such as cytochrome P450 (CYP)-catalyzed dealkylation and dechlorination [9] or chemical and photochemical reactions [10]. The half-life of ATZ is approximately 49 days in nonsterile, aerobic topsoil and up to 115 days in sterile conditions [11]. Due to the low adsorption capacity in soil, ATZ is frequently detected in surface and groundwater systems, where it degrades at a slower rate [7, 12].

Animal exposure to ATZ occurs via dermal, oral, and inhalation routes [8, 13]. In vivo, the half-life of ATZ is approximately 39 hours and excretion is mainly via urine in mammals [8]. A broad spectrum of developmental toxicities are associated with ATZ exposure, including immunotoxicity in male [14, 15] and female mice [16], hepatic and renal oxidative stress [17], and reproductive toxicity [8]. Impaired mammary gland development and inadequate lactation [18], decreased serum testosterone [19], decreased inhibin, increased serum follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone in male rats [20], and an increased ratio of females in African frogs [21] are consequences of ATZ exposure. Progesterone (P4) is affected by ATZ exposure, with elevated serum P4 concentrations in pseudopregnant rats [22], increased P4 production in cultured rat granulosa cells and human adrenal cortical carcinoma cells [23], as well as alterations in P4 signaling in embryonic zebrafish [24].

In this study, the effects of gestational and early-life exposure to ATZ on ovarian function in piglets were investigated. While previous research has predominantly focused on rodent or adult exposures, data on the developmental consequences in large mammals, particularly livestock species, remain limited. Pigs share key physiological and developmental similarities with humans, making them a valuable translational model for toxicological studies [25, 26]. This study used a porcine model during prenatal and early postnatal exposure windows to identify ovarian mechanisms of ATZ toxicity. The primary independent end points of interest were piglet body weight, umbilical cord hormone measurements, ovarian weight, ovarian follicle composition, and abundance chemical biotransformation, phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase (PI3K) pathway, steroidogenesis, peroxisome proliferator–activated receptors (PPARs), and oxidative stress proteins.

Atrazine preparation

ATZ stock solutions (1 mg/mL) were prepared by dissolving powder in 100% ethanol. Aliquots were stored at −80 °C until use (1 week-4 months). An aliquot of ATZ stock solution was diluted into tap water (473 L) to yield the drinking water treatment containing ATZ (20 µg/L). The same volume of the vehicle (100% ethanol) was added to the control drinking water (final ethanol concentration = 0.002% (v/v)). Drinking water containing either vehicle control (CT) or ATZ was prepared as needed (∼every 9 days) and stored in 2 identical polyethylene tanks until use.

Animal exposure

Animal procedures were approved by the University of Nebraska–Lincoln (UNL) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Seven postpubertal Duroc gilts from 3 litters were obtained from the UNL Swine Teaching Herd and were housed in individual pens within the same room in the UNL Animal Science Building. Gilts were fed a standard swine ration for each stage of production and received ad libitum water access throughout the trial. Prior to treatment, gilts were synchronized with Matrix (7 mL; Merck Animal Health) for 15 days prior to an intramuscular injection with P.G. 600 (5 mL; Merck). Gilts were bred via artificial insemination to the same Yorkshire sire (DNA Genetics) eliminating an effect of sire, and pregnancy was confirmed via ultrasound. Thereafter, littermate gilts were randomly assigned to treatment, which consisted of ad libitum access to drinking water containing either vehicle CT (0.002% (v/v) ethanol; n = 3) or ATZ (20 µg/L; n = 4). This concentration of ATZ equates to an average dose of approximately 178 µg/day (average water disappearance = 8.9 L/day). Both treatments were delivered via a homemade polyvinyl chloride pipe waterer (19-L capacity). Dosing commenced 28 days post breeding, which is after implantation in swine (13-18 days after fertilization) [27]. Pipe waterers were cleaned biweekly with detergent and bleach (1%) to prevent biofilm.

Sows farrowed and nursed their piglets ad libitum. Umbilical cord blood was collected and stored at 4 °C until serum isolation. Serum was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until analysis. A total of 12 female piglets from CT-treated sows and 8 female piglets from ATZ-treated sows were produced. One ATZ-exposed sow litter contained only male piglets; thus, there were 3 ATZ-exposed litters with female offspring. Within 24 hours of birth, piglets were weighed, treated with an intramuscular iron injection (1 mL; Pharmacosmos), and received oral Scourguard (1 pump; Bimeda). At postnatal day (PND) 10, a total of 20 female piglets (CT-12; ATZ-8) were humanely euthanized via inhalation of carbon dioxide, exsanguination, and decapitation by a board-certified veterinary pathologist. Thus, the total CT and ATZ treatment duration for piglets was approximately 99 days (89 days of gestation plus 10 days postnatally). Ovaries were weighed, and one ovary was flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for later protein extraction while the other ovary was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (48 hours; shaking at 4 °C) for follicle counting.

Hormone assays

ELISA (DRG Instruments GmbH) kits were used to quantify pregnenolone, P4, testosterone, estrone, and estradiol (E2) from piglet umbilical cord blood serum samples were measured as per manufacturer protocols.

Protein isolation and Western blotting

Piglet ovaries were randomly selected (1 ovary per litter; n = 3 per treatment group), homogenized in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl and 1 mM EDTA [pH ∼8.5]), and centrifuged twice at 10 621 relative centrifugal force for 15 minutes to isolate supernatant. Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA assay. Absorbance values were detected at 560 nm by an Eon Microplate Spectrophotometer (Bio-Tek Instruments Inc). MINI-PROTEAN TGX gels were used to separate 10 µg of ovarian protein for 15 minutes at 60 V, followed by 1 hour at 100 V. Protein was transferred using semi-dry transfer (iBlot) or by wet transfer for 1 hour at 100 V on ice from the gel to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was incubated for 1 hour at room temperature with a solution of 5% nonfat dried milk with 1X PBST (semi-dry transfer) or 1X TBST (wet transfer) to reduce nonspecific binding. Membranes were incubated overnight in primary antibodies as detailed in Table 1 and washed thrice for 10 minutes each with 1X PBST or 1X TBST after incubation in primary antibody overnight. Secondary antibody solutions as described in Table 1 were prepared in 5% nonfat dried milk with 1X TBST or 1X PBST and membranes incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. After incubation, the membranes were washed repeating the aforementioned steps followed by a 7 minutes incubation in ECL Signal Fire reagent. In the darkroom, film exposure of the membranes was performed. Densitometric analysis of the appropriately sized band was performed using Image J software (NCBI). Each membrane was stained with Ponceau S and the entire stained lane on the membrane was quantified to account for any discrepancy in loading or transfer efficiency. Two appropriately sized bands were evident on Western blots for CYP1A1, ABCB1, ABCC1, CYP11A1, ESR2, and pBRCA1 and were both quantified. Negative control Western blots were performed omitting either the primary or secondary antibody.

Ovarian follicle enumeration

One randomly selected piglet paraformaldehyde-fixed ovary from each litter was embedded in paraffin wax and sectioned on a microtome at a 5-µm thickness (n = 3/treatment). Every sixth section was mounted on a slide and stained using hematoxylin and eosin Y. Every 12th section was used for follicle quantification; therefore, follicles were counted every 60 µm across the ovary. The tissue was imaged with an Olympus CX43 microscope with an Olympus EP50 camera and 40× objective. Healthy and atretic follicles were counted. Primordial, primary, and secondary follicles were counted only when the nucleus was visible to prevent duplicate measures. Follicles were classified as previously described [28]; follicles with intense pyknotic staining or those containing misshapen or shrunken oocytes were considered atretic. Each ovary was sectioned at 5 µm thickness and every 12th section counted to ensure a distance between sections of 60 µm. There were 5 sections per ovary, and to estimate total follicle per ovary, the raw data were multiplied by 5. Multiple oocytes/nuclei follicles (MOFs), also known as nested oocytes/nuclei, were identified as follicles containing more than one oocyte/nuclei surrounded by a single layer of granulosa cells and theca cells and were counted. Three categories to assess MOF were considered: 2 to 6 MOF had 2 to 6 oocytes/nuclei within 1 granulosa layer, 7 to 10 MOFs were characterized by the presence of 7 to 10 oocytes/nuclei within a single layer of granulosa cells, and the greater than 10 MOF category had more than 10 oocytes/nuclei within 1 granulosa cell nest.

Statistical analyses

For birth weight, PND10 body weight, ovary weight, and umbilical cord hormone measurements, the individual piglet was used as the experimental unit. For Western blotting and follicle counting, litter was considered the experimental unit and one ovary per litter was used. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.3.0 software, and CT vs ATZ effects were analyzed by unpaired t test with Welch’s correction. A P value less than or equal to .05 was defined as a statistically significant result between treatments. A P value greater than .05 and less than .10 was considered a tendency toward a biologically meaningful difference.

Discussion

A porcine model of in utero and neonatal ATZ exposure was used to test the developmental toxicity of ATZ on the offspring ovary due to exposure from gestation day 28 to PND10. The timing of the exposure from postcoital day 28 to postnatal day 10 was to ensure that implantation and early embryonic development were not affected by the exposure resulting in abortion. The fetal ovary is formed in the pig at approximately gestation day 30 with primordial follicles comprising about 80% of follicles by gestation day 95 with some primary follicles also being present [29]. Therefore, at the initiation of exposure, the fetal ovary was formed but folliculogenesis including germ cell nest breakdown [29] was included in the exposure paradigm. Extending the exposure to day 10 after birth allowed inclusion of lactational effects as well.

Environmental presence of ATZ in the US Midwestern states is documented at levels higher than investigated in this study [30], and ATZ is routinely used for urban weed control [7]. Studies assessing postnatal exposure to ATZ above environmentally relevant concentrations have reported either an increase [17] or a decrease in body weight in rats [31, 32] and reduced food intake [33]. Developmentally, delayed vaginal opening following exposure to ATZ [34] and its metabolite, diamino-s-chlorotriazine, have been reported [35]. In this study, gestational and early-life exposure to ATZ at an environmentally relevant concentration decreased piglet birth weight and PND10 body weight, similar to findings in the rat [22]. Decreased ovarian weight in rats [33] and quails [36], but increased ovarian weight in zebrafish post-ATZ exposure [24], are reported. No changes in the ovarian weight of ATZ-exposed piglets compared to the CT-treated piglets were observed in this study, which was also reported previously in rats [22], potentially attributable to the much lower dose of ATZ employed in this study compared to those reported elsewhere.

The AKT pathway is modulated upstream by PI3K in response to extracellular stimuli [37]. Phosphorylated PI3K phosphorylates and activates AKT (pAKT), subsequently activating proteins promoting ovarian follicle stimulation [38, 39] and viability [40]. Exposure to ATZ prolonged FSH-driven pAKT protein abundance in cultured rat granulosa cells [41]. In exposed piglets herein, pAKT was decreased, but there was no effect of ATZ on other PI3K-related proteins such as IRS1, total AKT, or the ratio of pAKT/AKT. Adverse ovarian outcomes, ranging from premature ovarian failure [40] to metabolic [37] and endocrine [42, 43] disruption, have been reported when pAKT is disrupted, and it has been demonstrated that PI3K signaling regulates ovarian biotransformation proteins [44-46]. Thus, the observation that pAKT is decreased by ATZ in the piglet ovary could be consequential in terms of ovarian follicle viability, steroidogenesis, and chemical biotransformation both acutely and in the long term.

Pregnenolone, a product of hydroxylation of cholesterol, is the first product of ovarian steroidogenesis and serves as the precursor for other steroid hormones [47, 48] but no treatment effect of ATZ on pregnenolone was observed in umbilical cord blood in this study. Progesterone mediates ovarian, uterine, and mammary gland development [49, 50] and is an intermediate in the production of androgens and estrogens [51]. Exposure to ATZ influences serum P4, with various studies reporting both increases and decreases depending on the animal model, dosage, and duration of exposure [23, 24, 41, 52-55]. In utero ATZ exposure in 4 rat strains revealed a strain-dependent decrease in serum P4 regardless of the duration of dosing [52]. In pigs dosed with ATZ for 19 days prior to estrus, increased serum P4 was observed 24 to 48 hours before the onset of the subsequent estrus [56]. In the present study, decreased serum P4 was observed in the umbilical cord blood of ATZ-exposed piglets, supporting an endocrine-disrupting effect of ATZ on ovarian steroidogenesis due to in utero exposure.

Estrogens are essential for regulation of the female reproductive system and for development of secondary sexual characteristics [57]. Increased estrone has been reported in male [58] and female [59, 60] rats exposed to ATZ but there was no ATZ effect on serum estrone concentration in piglet umbilical cord blood. Exposure to ATZ decreased E2 in cultured rat granulosa cells [61] and in pig serum [56]. Similar to P4, a rat strain effect is evident with regard to the ability of ATZ to reduce E2 [62]. In the present study, ATZ exposure tended to increase serum E2 concentration in piglet umbilical cord blood, supporting endocrine disruption in utero.

Chemical exposures can affect ovarian follicle number [63-69]. Although there were no differences in the number of primordial, primary, secondary, or MOFs due to ATZ exposure in this study, decreased primordial follicle number and increased MOF number have been reported in adult mice exposed to 100 mg/kg ATZ during in utero development [70 , 71]. In addition, atretic follicle number was increased at ATZ doses above 100 mg/kg in mice [34]. In this study, the number of atretic follicles was decreased in piglet ovaries by ATZ exposure, suggesting dysfunction in removal of damaged oocytes from the ovary which may be species or dose dependent.

Germ cell nest breakdown for proper primordial follicle formation occurs in utero in some species and postnatally in others. In the porcine ovary, MOFs are observed in the postnatal ovary until approximately PND20 [29]. The MOFs in this study were those that contained multiple oocytes or nuclei as recently described [72] and as noted earlier, MOFs were increased in number in ATZ-exposed mice [70]. There was no effect of ATZ observed on MOF number in the present study. Independent of treatment, it was noted that MOFs containing more than 10 oocytes/nuclei were lower in number when compared to the 7 to 10 MOFs, and MOFs containing 2 to 6 oocytes/nuclei were highest in number. These data suggest that in the pig ovary postnatally, MOFs reduce in size sequentially from those containing more than 10 oocytes/nuclei to MOFs containing 7 to 10 oocytes/nuclei and further to the 2 to 6 oocyte/nuclei category as the oocytes get encapsulated with granulosa cells to form proper primordial follicles. This agrees with the finding that in swine at PND 90, approximately 80% of follicles were primordial and approximately 5% were primary follicles [29]. In ovaries from 6- to 21-week-old Yucatan mini-pigs, germ cell nests were also observed postnatally, with approximately 3 oocytes per nest being observed [72]. Thus, while ATZ did not affect MOF number, these findings shed light on the normal physiological mechanisms in the porcine ovary related to follicle formation during neonatal development.

Biotransformation of ATZ involves both phase I and phase II enzymatically catalyzed processes, with excretion primarily occurring through urine and feces [73]. In rats, approximately 65% of an ATZ dose was excreted in urine [74] and approximately 18% in feces within 72 hours of administration [75]. Metabolites of ATZ include diaminochlorotriazine (DACT) [76, 77], atrazine mercapturate, desisopropylatrazine, and desethylatrazine [78]. The AHR transcriptionally activates Cyp1a1, Cyp1a2, and Cyp1b1, and in turn, these enzymes catalyze oxidative detoxification or activation of a ligand [79]. There is conflicting information on whether ATZ does [80 , 81] or does not [82] activate the AHR. No effect of ATZ exposure on the AHR was noted herein, potentially indicating that the AHR is not involved in the ovarian effects of ATZ, or that the dissimilarity in dose and duration of ATZ exposure, or a species difference in the response, exists. The CYP proteins initiate ATZ biotransformation [83]. Increased renal and cardiac Cyp1a1, Cyp1a2 [83, 84], Cyp1b1, Cyp2c18, Cyp2d6, Cyp3a4, Cyp3a7, and Cyp4b1 [85] were observed following ATZ exposure in the quail and human, respectively. In the liver, CYP1A2, CYP3A4, and CYP2C19 participated in human ATZ metabolism [86] and in rat ovaries, Cyp1b1 messenger RNA (mRNA) was increased in vivo and in vitro following ATZ exposure [61]. In exposed piglets, ovarian CYP1A1, CYP1B1, and CYP2E1 protein abundance were decreased by ATZ, suggesting their involvement in ovarian ATZ biotransformation. This reduction in CYP1B1 was in line with observations in adult zebrafish [24]. The GST enzymes catalyze glutathione conjugation to ATZ [73] and hepatic mRNA encoding 1 GST isoforms was increased in ATZ-exposed female rats [87]. Post-ATZ (50 mg/kg) exposure, ovarian GST was decreased in pseudopregnant rats [22]. In pig oocytes, ATZ exposure caused excessive accumulation of superoxide radicals and decreased glutathione level and mitochondrial membrane potential [88]. In this study, ATZ tended to decrease GSTP1 protein abundance in PND10 piglet ovaries, suggesting that GSTP1 is involved in ovarian ATZ biotransformation.

The phase III biotransformation ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter proteins mediate transmembrane efflux of a wide range of xenobiotic and endogenous substrates [89-94], including in the ovary [95]. A pesticide mixture including ATZ increased ABCB1 in melanoma skin cells following chronic exposure [96]. In this study, ovarian ABCB1 but not ABCC1 was decreased following exposure to ATZ in the piglet ovary, suggesting a specificity for ABCB1 over ABCC1 in ATZ biotransformation.

As previously mentioned, there was a tendency for increased E2 in umbilical cord serum due to ATZ exposure. Estrogen receptor (ESR) signaling regulates several molecular pathways underlying folliculogenesis, oocyte maturation, and ovulation [97, 98]. Although ATZ has been reported to not bind to ESR in vivo [99 , 100], an in vitro assay demonstrated ATZ inhibition of [3H]-E2 to the ESR [99]. It has also been reported that ATZ induces several estrogen target genes [101]. In this study, neither ESR1 nor ESR2 were affected by ATZ exposure, possibly due to the early developmental stage examined.

Following the observed decrease in serum P4, the level of the P4 receptor (PR) was investigated in the ovaries of PND10 piglets. In rats, Pgr mRNA was decreased following ATZ exposure [61]. While PR has been detected in ovaries of 38-day-old piglets [63], it was not detectable in PND10 piglet ovaries (data not shown), which may be due to the age of the piglets at euthanasia. The timing at which ovarian PR expression begins is variable between species and organs. For example, there was no evidence of ovulatory capacity in guinea pigs implanted with P4 implants until approximately PND15 [102], suggesting a lack of PR responsivity within the early neonatal ovary.

ATZ and its metabolites have been reported to increase or decrease steroidogenic protein abundance. Exposure to ATZ upregulated ovarian mRNA levels of Cyp11a1, 3β-Hsd, and 17β-Hsd [36], while Cyp11a1 and Cyp17 were decreased by ATZ and its metabolite, DACT, in mouse testes [103]. Increased Star mRNA was observed in ATZ-exposed rat granulosa cells in vitro [41], but not in vivo post ATZ exposure [61]. There was no effect on STAR protein abundance observed in the present study, suggesting that StAR may not be an ATZ target in the neonatal pig ovary. The CYP11A1 enzyme converts cholesterol into pregnenolone [104-106], and decreased abundance of CYP11A1 protein was observed due to ATZ exposure in this study, with a potential implication on altering the ovarian hormonal milieu. Biosynthesis of E2 from testosterone is catalyzed by CYP19A1 [107], and ATZ decreased Cyp19a1 mRNA in rat granulosa cells [61, 108], but increased Cyp19a1 in the quail ovary [36] and in Leydig cells [109-111]. In the piglet ovary, however, no effect of ATZ exposure was observed, suggesting that the tendency for increased E2 was not attributable to an effect on abundance of ovarian CYP19A1.

The PPAR proteins are transcription factors involved in steroidogenesis, angiogenesis, tissue remodeling, cell cycle progression, apoptosis, and lipid metabolism, processes that are critical for normal ovarian function [112]. PPARα regulates cellular uptake, activation, and β-oxidation of fatty acids [113, 114]. In the ovary, PPARα is localized to theca, stroma [112], and granulosa cells, and decreases steroidogenic proteins, thereby modulating E2 synthesis [115]. A decrease in the abundance of PPARα due to gestational and early-life ATZ exposure was noted herein, potentially contributing to the changes in E2 and P4 in this study. Following ATZ exposure in Xenopus laevis tadpoles, a decrease in Pparβ mRNA was observed [116] but no change in PPARβ or PPARγ protein abundance was noted herein, suggesting that PPARα, but not PPARβ or PPARγ, is involved in ATZ-mediated ovarian effects on neonatal gilts.

DNA damage repair is critical for maintaining germ cell genomic integrity [117]. In earthworms and fish, dose-dependent DNA damage due to ATZ exposure occurs [118, 119]. Decreased porcine oocyte maturation [120] and increased spindle defects [88] have also been reported following ATZ exposure. In fish testes, ATZ exposure decreased XPC, a DNA damage sensing and repair protein [121]. A diminished ovarian reserve and premature aging are associated with impaired DNA damage repair protein abundance [122], among which PARP1, BRCA1, and RAD51 have been identified as essential for oocyte survival and quality. PARP1 dysfunction contributes to the pathogenesis of immune-associated ovarian failure [123]. In mice, ATZ exposure induced ovarian inflammatory cytokine activation, pathways with direct crosstalk to PARP1 activity [34]. Similarly, in piglet ovaries, increased PARP1 abundance following gestational and early-life ATZ exposure was observed, suggesting the presence of DNA damage. RAD51 is widely expressed in the oocyte and granulosa cells of all follicle stages and is increased in germinal epithelium and granulosa-lutein cells with aging [124]. RAD51 coats single-stranded DNA at break sites, facilitating homology search and strand invasion, which is critical for accurate DNA repair [125]. Decreased RAD51 is associated with accelerated loss of the ovarian follicular reserve and accumulation of DNA damage in oocytes [126]. Clinically, RAD51-related ovarian deficiencies correlate with symptoms such as dysregulated hormone concentration, primary amenorrhea, decreased ovarian reserve, and infertility in women [127]. Although no study has directly linked ATZ exposure with RAD51 dysregulation in the ovary, gestational ATZ exposure disrupted prophase I of meiosis and affected normal follicle formation in female mice [70]. A decrease in the abundance of RAD51 in the ovary of piglets exposed to ATZ in utero and postnatally was observed, suggesting heightened ovarian vulnerability to genotoxicants. The DNA repair protein, BRCA1, functions in ovarian physiology beyond tumor suppression [128]: Brca1−/− mice have fewer oocytes, smaller litter size, and fewer primordial follicles at PND5 [128]. While compromised BRCA1 function leads to accumulation of DNA damage in oocytes [126], promoting reproductive senescence [126, 128], in this study, ATZ did not alter ovarian BRCA1 abundance, suggesting that either DNA damage involving BRCA1 repair was absent, or that, perhaps, the temporal pattern of DNA repair may been completed earlier in this exposure paradigm and prior to tissue collection. DNA damage can be preceded by the generation of reactive oxygen species [129, 130], and ATZ exposure reduced SOD1 in earthworms [118] and fish [119] but not in the piglet ovaries in the present study. Thus, oxidative stress may not occur due to ATZ exposure in the piglet ovary at the developmental stage and exposure paradigm investigated.

To summarize, in utero and postnatal ATZ exposure decreased birth and final body weight of female piglets, decreased umbilical cord blood P4, decreased atretic follicle number, increased abundance of PARP1 protein, and decreased ovarian protein abundance of CYP1A1, CYP1B1, CYP2E1, GSTP, ABCB1, pAKT, CYP11A1, PPARα, and RAD51. There was also a tendency for increased E2 in umbilical cord blood. There are several limitations of this study: The single time point of exposure precludes examining dynamic changes that occurred across gestation and lactation; effects on liver function are not investigated, which could contribute to the amount of ATZ and metabolites reaching the ovary as well as metabolism of E2 and P4; low number of sows; and the targeted approach to assess proteins of interest. While the latter maintained study focus, it does not discount that there are other end points that may counter the observed ovarian changes as well as being potentially additive to the effects noted. The findings of this study, however, do support that ATZ causes endocrine disruption of ovarian steroidogenesis, alterations to follicular atresia, and that ovarian DNA damage may be a consequence of ATZ exposure. The changes to E2 and P4 may directly influence observed protein abundance alterations induced by ATZ, and this is worthy of consideration. While this study examined the implications of ATZ exposure in piglet ovaries following gestational and neonatal exposure, the duration of time that these effects will persist post cessation of ATZ exposure remains unknown. Thus, knowledge gaps remain regarding species-specific ATZ effects, influence of dose and duration of exposure, and the hierarchy of tissue-level effects.
