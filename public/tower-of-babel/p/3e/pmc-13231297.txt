Sexual Orientation–Related Discrimination Among LGB+ Medical Students With Disabilities
Authors: Zoie C. Sheets, Mytien Nguyen, Jasmine K.M. Lopez, Amy Addams, Christopher J. Moreland, Dowin Boatright, Lisa M. Meeks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study examines sexual orientation-related discrimination among lesbian, gay, bisexual, and nonheterosexual (LGB+) medical students with disabilities.

Introduction

Discrimination in medical education is well documented and disproportionately affects students across sex, race or ethnicity, and sexual orientation.1,2,3 Discrimination experiences are associated with higher rates of burnout4 and attrition,5 threatening workforce sustainability and exacerbating inequities. The consequences are especially profound for learners with intersecting minoritized identities who face compounded risk.4,5

Medical students who identify as lesbian, gay, bisexual, asexual, pansexual, and queer (LGB+) and medical students with disabilities (MSWD) make up a considerable, yet still underrepresented presence in medicine.4,5 Although both groups report higher rates of burnout2,4 and discrimination1,6 than their peers who were not disabled or LGB+, limited data describe the experiences of learners who identify as both LGB+ and MSWD. This study examines experiences of discrimination based on sexual orientation at the intersection of LGB+ and disability identities.

Methods

Deidentified data was obtained from the Association of American Medical Colleges Graduation Questionnaire (GQ) respondents from 2020 to 2022. Students self-reported sexual orientation, disability status, sex, and race and ethnicity. LGB+ status was determined based on participants’ responses to the sexual orientation item on the GQ. Prior to 2022, available response options were bisexual, gay, or lesbian and heterosexual or straight. Beginning with the 2022 GQ, the response options were expanded to include asexual, pansexual, and queer. Across both versions of the questionnaire, respondents who selected any option other than heterosexual or straight were categorized as LGB+.

Discrimination based on sexual orientation included receiving lower evaluations or grades, being denied opportunities, and being subjected to offensive remarks or names. Experience of multiple discrimination types was defined as having reported 2 or more forms of such discrimination.

Adjusted logistic regression was used to estimate the odds of discrimination by disability status and sexual orientation, as well as their intersection, adjusting for sex, race, and ethnicity. Analyses were performed using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp) following the STROBE reporting guideline. The University of Michigan institutional review board exempted this study. Informed consent was not required due to the use of deidentified data.

Results

Among 50 139 GQ respondents, 45 313 (90.4%) had complete demographic and survey data; 4330 (9.6%) identified as LGB+, 3807 (8.4%) as MSWD, and 710 (1.6%) as LGB+ MSWD. Compared with their peers, LGB+ students and MSWD reported higher rates of being denied opportunities because of sexual orientation (LGB+: OR, 6.44; 95% CI, 5.14-8.07; MSWD: OR, 1.85; 95% CI, 1.38-2.48); being subjected to offensive remarks/names due to sexual orientation, (LGB+: OR, 23.70; 95% CI, 20.48-27.43; MSWD: OR, 1.66; 95% CI, 1.38-1.99); receiving lower evaluations or grades due to sexual orientation (LGB+: OR, 9.93; 95% CI, 7.82-12.62; MSWD: OR, 1.78; 95% CI, 1.30-2.44) (Table 1), and multiple discrimination types (LGB+: OR, 12.7; 95% CI, 9.93-16.25; MSWD: OR, 1.67; 95% CI, 1.21-2.31) (Table 2). At the intersections, LGB+ MSWD experienced the highest rates of discrimination related to sexual orientation compared to nondisabled heterosexual students: denied opportunities (OR, 11.15; 95% CI, 7.47-16.64), offensive remarks/names (OR, 37.55; 95% CI, 29.85-47.25), lower evaluations or grades (OR, 17.42; 95% CI, 11.69-25.95]) (Table 1), and experiencing multiple discrimination types (OR, 21.15; 95% CI, 14.17-31.59) (Table 2).

Discussion

In this national study of more than 45 000 graduating medical students, those who identified as LGB+ and disabled reported the highest rates of sexual-orientation–based discrimination. Compared with nondisabled heterosexual peers, LGB+ MSWD reported 11-fold higher odds of denied opportunities, 38-fold higher odds of offensive remarks, and 17-fold higher odds of receiving lower evaluations or grades because of their sexual orientation. These findings suggest that learners with intersecting minoritized identities experienced multiplicative risk of discrimination in medical training.

The compounded impact of ableism and heteronormativity may shape how LGB+ MSWD are perceived and evaluated regarding their performance, professionalism, and ability. Biased assessments can result in meaningful loss of opportunity,7 affecting access to opportunities, with downstream implications for competitiveness in the transition to residency, such as research and induction into honor societies.8

This study has limitations. All data were self-reported and may be subject to underreporting due to stigma and fear of discrimination.9,10 Additionally, the 2020 to 2022 GQ did not capture disability-related discrimination, which may overlap or exacerbate experiences attributed to sexual orientation among LGB+/MSWD. Nonetheless, the magnitude and consistency of findings indicate a persistent pattern of inequity with meaningful implications for learners’ experiences and educational outcomes.

Future studies should investigate disability-related discrimination among LGB+ MSWD, examine mechanisms that drive intersectional mistreatment and discrimination, and explore institutional practices that effectively mitigate opportunity loss. Ensuring safe training environments for LGB+ MSWD is essential to diversifying and strengthening the physician workforce.
