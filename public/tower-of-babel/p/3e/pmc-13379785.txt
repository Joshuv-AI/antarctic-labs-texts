Identifying Predictors of Knowledge, Attitude and Practice Intentions Regarding Advance Care Planning Among Medical and Nursing Undergraduates: A Cross‐Sectional Study Based on Latent Profile Analysis
Authors: Yicheng Su, Danlei Yang, Nannan Jiang, Jingwen Shi, Qiankun Liu, Li Ma, Pengyao Li, Hui Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study aimed to explore latent categories of nursing and medical students' knowledge, attitude and practice intentions regarding advance care planning (ACP), and to identify the factors influencing these categories. It sought to provide an evidence base for developing personalized, precision‐targeted advance care planning educational interventions.MethodsFrom October to December 2023, a stratified cluster random sampling method was employed to select a sample of 1006 undergraduate students in clinical medicine and nursing (Years 1–4) from a medical university in eastern China. A total of 935 valid questionnaires were recovered. Research instruments included a self‐developed demographic questionnaire, the advance care planning knowledge, attitude and practice intentions Questionnaire, the Death Attitude Profile‐Revised, and the Meaning in Life Questionnaire. Latent profile analysis determined the optimal number of categories; logistic regression identified factors influencing different advance care planning knowledge, attitude and practice intentions categories; a nomogram was constructed using R software, and predictive performance was evaluated using area under the curve (AUC), Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and 1000‐bootstrap calibration curves.ResultsLatent profile analysis identified two latent categories: a “collaborative” group (33.6% of the sample) and a “lagging” group (66.4% of the sample). The collaborative group scored higher than the lagging group across all dimensions of advance care planning knowledge, attitude and practice intentions questionnaire. Logistic regression analysis indicated: palliative care training (OR = 1.5, 1.028–2.190), resuscitation experience (OR = 0.64, 0.424–0.966), clinical medicine student (OR = 0.537, 0.376–0.766), urban student (OR = 0.626, 0.419–0.935), high death escape scores, low natural acceptance scores, and low meaning in life questionnaire scores were more likely to be classified as “lagging type”. Senior students (OR = 7.217, 4.089–12.737) and those with positive major attitude (OR = 3.572, 1.228–10.386) were more likely to be classified as “collaborative type”. The nomogram demonstrated good discriminatory ability (AUC = 0.864; 95% CI 0.841–0.887; Brier score = 0.14) and high calibration (χ2 = 13.845, p = 0.086; calibration slope = 0.97).ConclusionsThe established nomogram model identified the influencing factors of the knowledge, attitude and practice Intentions level of advance care planning among medical undergraduates, providing empirical evidence for the educational intervention of advance care planning for nursing and medical undergraduates. Universities needed to carry out refined advance care planning education in a targeted manner based on the different characteristics of students.Reporting MethodTo strengthen the validity of the research, it was planned, conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist.Patient or Public ContributionAll participants contributed to the conducting of this study by completing self‐reported questionnaires.Keywords: advance care planning; death attitude; knowledge, attitude and practice intentions; latent profile; meaning in life; nomogram; nursing and medical undergraduates

Introduction

Advance Care Planning is a process where patients articulate their end‐of‐life care preferences while mentally competent, sharing these wishes with healthcare professionals and/or family members. It promotes patient‐centred care, reduces decision‐making conflicts, and improves quality of life. Medical and nursing students form the core team for future ACP implementation. Their competency in ACP and the factors influencing it are crucial. Furthermore, there is still a lack of sufficient research exploring the knowledge, attitudes, and practice intentions of advance care planning (ACP) from a psychological perspective.

Background

ACP refers to a process in which patients, while still mentally competent, use their personal experiences and values to articulate their wishes for end‐of‐life medical care, and share these wishes with healthcare professionals and/or family members or friends (Sudore et al. 2017; Lum et al. 2015). ACP facilitates the expression of patients' values and/or care preferences, reduces decision‐making conflicts between patients and their families, alleviates patient suffering, improves quality of life, and preserves patient dignity (Lyon et al. 2014; Ke et al. 2020).

The Healthy China 2030 plan (Bei et al. 2018) emphasizes the importance of covering the full life cycle—“from the fetal stage to the end of life”—through comprehensive health services and healthcare guarantees, fully safeguarding people's health. ACP supports these national objectives by promoting full‐life‐cycle health management, comprehensive healthcare, and improved quality of life. Currently, ACP in mainland China remains in its nascent stage, primarily focused on conceptual promotion (Zhang et al. 2021). Influenced by traditional Chinese culture, death is often regarded as an unlucky topic and people generally avoid discussing it directly. Generally speaking, most Chinese patients and their families decline to initiate advance directive discussions during treatment unless there is no opportunity to receive any treatment (Xing et al. 2017). Besides, in the context of East Asian culture, end‐of‐life medical decisions are usually family‐centred and ignore the individual decisions of patients, which further hinders the development of ACP (Pun et al. 2023).

Current research (Li et al. 2025; Peimani et al. 2025) has mainly targeted patients, family members, and healthcare professionals. However, undergraduates majoring in clinical medicine and nursing, who will form the mainstay of the future healthcare workforce, are a pivotal group for advancing ACP. Their understanding, attitudes, and behavioural intentions play a crucial role in the broader adoption of ACP (Schrijvers et al. 2014). No comprehensive ACP training system for students currently exists in China. They lack adequate formal palliative care education in school (Cheng et al. 2021). Hence, ACP education for Chinese medical and nursing students urgently requires more research attention.

Studies have shown that factors such as students' resuscitation experience, palliative care training, academic year, professional attitude, and clinical practice may influence ACP knowledge, attitude and practice Intentions (KAP) proficiency levels (Shi et al. 2020). Emotional factors (such as attitudes toward death) are also crucial in undergraduate palliative care education (Thiemann et al. 2015), but the relationship between these factors and the ACP competencies of medical and nursing students has not been fully explored. Furthermore, the potential differences within subgroups and the corresponding educational strategies may vary substantially. Therefore, it is necessary to further categorize medical and nursing students to lay a foundation for subsequent individualized ACP education. Existing studies have classified survey respondents based only on ACP scores for knowledge, practice and attitude, and further analysis of possible individual differences is needed. Latent profile analysis (LPA) is a statistical method that classifies participants into different subgroups based on specified variables, aiming to identify latent characteristics of the respondents (Berlin et al. 2014). Given the differences in characteristics and educational intervention approaches for medical and nursing students at various ACP KAP levels, early implementation of targeted ACP education during undergraduate studies is of critical importance. Therefore, this study is the first to apply the latent profile analysis to establish subgroups; the aim is to explore the potential characteristics of ACP KAP levels in different categories of medical and nursing students and included important psychological predictors: meaning in life and attitudes toward death, filling a gap in prior research. Having further assessed the factors influencing ACP levels in medical and nursing students, to provide a basis for improving medical and nursing students' ACP KAP and developing individualized educational interventions.

Methods

Study Design

This cross‐sectional study was conducted from October to December 2023, involving undergraduate students in their first to fourth years from the School of Clinical Medicine and the School of Nursing at a medical university in eastern China.

Participants and Sampling

In this study, a stratified cluster random sampling method was used to recruit undergraduate students from a medical university in Eastern China. The sampling strata were defined by academic year (Years 1–4) and major (clinical medicine and nursing), resulting in 8 independent strata. Within each stratum, each intact class served as the primary sampling units for cluster sampling, and the sample size was allocated proportionally according to the number of students in each stratum. In the development of models for binary outcomes, the principle of 10 events per variable (Riley et al. 2020) (10 EPV) is typically applied to ensure the accuracy of the model sample size. This rule requires at least 10 outcome events for each predictor variable included in the model. Based on the preliminary univariate analysis, 10 predictor variables were initially identified for inclusion, necessitating a minimum of 100 events. This study enrolled a total of 935 participants, with 314 observed events, which satisfies the sample size requirement and supports the reliability of the research findings. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) first‐ through fourth‐year undergraduates majoring in clinical medicine and nursing, and (2) informed consent and voluntary participation in this study. Exclusion criteria: (1) Students who were absent or on leave during the investigation period; (2) Completed the questionnaire in less than 3 min or failed attention checks (e.g., trap questions); (3) Submitted duplicate responses (identified by IP address); Finally, a total of 1006 students participated in the survey, with 935 students returning the questionnaire, resulting in a response rate of 92.9%.

Data Collection

Data were collected through an online survey administered via the Tencent Questionnaire platform. Prior to data collection, all participants provided written informed consent. The consent form clearly stated the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, and the measures taken to ensure anonymity and data confidentiality. For each of the selected classes, the class leader organized an offline meeting during which students were invited to scan a QR code to access the questionnaire on their mobile devices under the class leader's supervision. The questionnaire uses uniform guiding language to expound the purpose of the research. To ensure data quality and minimize response bias, several measures were implemented: (1) trap questions were embedded to verify attention; (2) a balanced five‐point Likert scale was used; and (3) the question order was randomized. The Tencent platform was configured to prevent duplicate submissions based on IP address and to record completion time. Questionnaires completed in less than 3 min were excluded. Participants who completed the survey received an incentive of 5 Chinese yuan (CNY). In the system settings, duplicate submitted questionnaires can be detected based on the device IP, and the specific filling time of the questionnaire can also be retrieved and ensures that the submission can only be made after all the questionnaire contents have been completed. All submitted data were transmitted directly to a secure server and stored within the Tencent Questionnaire system. Only the research team had access to the responses, ensuring anonymity and protecting participants' privacy.

Measures

General Information Questionnaire

Drawing on a literature review, the researchers developed a self‐designed general information questionnaire. It covered variables such as major, gender, grade, religion, region, major attitude, resuscitation experience, and palliative care training.

Advance Care Planning Knowledge, Attitude and Practice Intentions Questionnaire

Advance Care Planning Knowledge, Attitude and Practice Intentions Questionnaire was developed by Ding et al. (2021), and is primarily intended to assess ACP‐related knowledge, attitude and practice Intentions among Chinese medical students. The questionnaire is composed of three sections: the first section (Knowledge Survey) includes 12 items, Correct answers are worth 1 point, errors or ignorance are worth 0 points, and the total score is 12 points. Five of the entries are reverse scoring questions. Our approach to such entries is to perform score conversions to ensure consistency with the direction of the other topics. The raw scoring is reversed in the score conversion, raw 1 → reversed 0. The second section (Attitude Survey) contains 13 items, rated on a five‐point Likert scale from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree,” for a total of 65 points. The third section (Practice Intentions Survey) consists of 8 items, also rated on a five‐point Likert scale from “very unwilling” to “very willing,” for a total of 40 points. A higher overall score on the questionnaire indicates a higher level of ACP KAP among students. Cronbach's alpha coefficient was 0.826 and in this study Cronbach's alpha coefficient was 0.856 with good reliability.

The Chinese Version of the Death Attitude Profile–Revised

We used the Chinese version of the Death Attitude Profile‐Revised (DAP‐R) (Tang et al. 2014). It comprises five dimensions—fear of death, death escape, natural acceptance, approach acceptance, and escape acceptance—across a total of 32 items. Each item is scored on a five‐point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” (1 point) to “strongly agree” (5 points). Score range from 32 to 160 points. With higher scores indicating greater identity with the respective subscale. The Cronbach's alpha coefficient for the Chinese version of the DAP‐R scale was 0.875, and the Cronbach's alpha coefficient in this study was 0.825.

Meaning in Life Questionnaire

We used the Chinese version of the Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ) (Wang et al. 2016). The questionnaire contains 10 items, each rated on a seven‐point Likert scale from “strongly disagree” (1 point) to “strongly agree” (7 points). Score range from 10 to 70 points. Higher scores indicate greater feelings of meaning in life. Item 9 is reverse‐scored. The Cronbach's alpha coefficient for the MLQ scale was 0.931, and the Cronbach's alpha coefficient in this study was 0.914, indicating good reliability and validity.

Data Analysis

Mplus 8.3 was used to conduct LPA on the ACP KAP item‐mean scores of nursing and medical undergraduates. The fit indices included the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and the sample‐size adjusted BIC (aBIC). Smaller values of these indices indicate better model fit. Entropy values ≥ 0.8 suggest a classification accuracy exceeding 90%, and the closer the value is to 1, the more accurate the classification. The Lo–Mendell–Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR) and the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT) were used to assess differences in model fit, where p < 0.05 indicates that the new model is superior to the previous one. By comparing all the fit indices across different models, the optimal model was selected. SPSS 28.0 was employed for descriptive statistics. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical or ordinal variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Group differences were assessed using the chi‐squared test or t‐test. There was no multicollinearity among the variables (VIF < 5). In the logistic regression analysis, the dependent variable was the categorical of ACP KAP for 935 undergraduate students. R 4.4.1 was used to establish and evaluate the predictive model of potential predictors, with odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) serving as effect estimates. The final model was presented as a nomogram, where each variable was assigned a specific score; the predicted probability of ACP KAP categories was obtained by summing the scores for each variable and drawing a vertical line through the total score. For internal validation, 1000 bootstrap resamplings were conducted. Model performance was further evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test and calibration curves.

Ethical Approval

Ethical approval was sought and obtained from the Independent Ethics Committee of the Bengbu Medical University (LH250201002xie); all participants submitted paper‐based informed consent forms, and non‐participation did not affect academic progress.

Results

Demographics

A total of 1006 questionnaires were distributed. After excluding duplicates (n = 24), incomplete responses (completion time < 3 min, n = 19), and invalid information (n = 28), 935 valid questionnaires were retained, yielding an effective response rate of 92.9%. There were statistically significant differences between the two groups (the Lagging Group vs. the Collaborative Group) in terms of gender, region, resuscitation experience, palliative care training, major (Clinical Medicine vs. Nursing), major attitude, and grade (p < 0.05). Regarding attitudes toward death and the MLQ, the Lagging Group scored significantly higher on the death escape subscale and significantly lower on the natural acceptance subscale and the total score of the MLQ compared to the Collaborative Group (p < 0.001). No significant statistical differences were observed between the groups for the remaining variables; see Table 1 for details.

Descriptive Statistics

Overall, the ACP KAP Questionnaire score was 77.6 ± 19.4, the MLQ score was 41.9 ± 11.3, and the scores for each dimension of attitudes toward death are shown in Table S1. The highest knowledge dimension accuracy rate was “The decisions made by the surrogate decision‐maker should align with the patient's own wishes” (K8), whereas the lowest was “A living will is a document voluntarily signed by an individual while they are of sound mind” (K10). The highest‐scoring item in the ACP attitude dimension was actively learning about the treatment preferences of patients with advanced conditions (A5), whereas the lowest‐scoring item was understanding that knowing the true medical condition helps improve the patient's quality of life (A9). The item with the highest score in the ACP practice intentions dimension was “answering family members' questions related to ACP” (P5), while the item with the lowest score was “discussing death” (P1). The dimension with the highest score on the DAP‐R was approach acceptance, while the lowest score was escape acceptance (Figures S2–S5).

Correlation Analysis of Knowledge, Attitude and Practice Intentions Regarding ACP With Attitudes Toward Death and Meaning in Life

The total ACP KAP score was negatively correlated with death escape (r = −0.27, p < 0.01), with the knowledge component contributing most significantly (r = −0.40). Knowledge was also significantly correlated with natural death acceptance (r = 0.13). The sense of meaning in life positively correlated with the ACP KAP total score (r = 0.16), knowledge (r = 0.23), natural acceptance of death (r = 0.35), and approach acceptance of death (r = 0.12) but negatively correlated with death escape (r = −0.25) (Table S2, Figure S1).

Latent Profile Analysis of Knowledge, Attitude and Practice Intentions Regarding ACP

This study examined five latent models (Categories 1–5, Table 2). Overall, the AIC, BIC, and aBIC tended to decrease as the number of categories increased. In the three‐, four‐, and five‐category models, the Entropy values are all lower than that of the two‐category model. To obtain reliable and accurate subgroup analysis results from LPA, a minimum sample size of 300 cases is required (Li et al. 2023), with each subgroup comprising at least 30 participants (Dalmaijer et al. 2022). However, all three models (the 3‐, 4‐ and 5‐category) contain subgroups with a sample size of less than 10%, suggesting insufficient representativeness. Conversely, when the model was set to two categories, both LMR and BLRT tests showed p < 0.05, with entropy reaching 0.985. Considering these factors, the two‐category model was confirmed as the optimal latent trait curve solution. Category 2 accounted for 33.6% of participants, with scores exceeding Category 1 across all dimensions, hence labelled “Collaborative group”, category 1 is labelled as “lagging Group” (See Figure 1 and Table 3).

Logistic Regression Analysis of ACP Knowledge, Attitude and Practice Intentions Categories Among Undergraduates

Using the lagging group as the reference group, a binary logistic regression model was constructed (Table 4). Compared with rural students, urban students are more likely to be associated with belonging to the lag group (95% CI = 0.419–0.935; p = 0.022). Students without resuscitation experience during internships (95% CI = 0.424–0.966; p = 0.034) and those in clinical specialties (95% CI = 0.376–0.766; p = 0.001) were also more likely to be associated with belonging to the lag group. Students who had received palliative care training were more likely to be associated with belonging to the collaborative group (95% CI = 1.028–2.190; p = 0.036). The odds of fourth‐year students belonging to the collaborative group were 7.22 times those of first‐year students (95% CI = 4.089–12.737; p < 0.001). Compared with students who reported disliking their major, those with a positive major attitude were associated with higher odds of collaborative group membership (OR = 3.572;95% CI = 1.228–10.386; p = 0.019).

Discussion

This study employed LPA and predictive modelling to explore potential factors influencing different categories of ACP KAP among medical and nursing students. The overall ACP KAP scores (77.6 ± 19.4) for clinical medicine and nursing students indicate inadequate preparedness in providing ACP. Therefore, further attention and measures are needed to enhance the ACP implementation capabilities of medical and nursing students in China.

Unlike previous studies focusing on average levels or variable relationships, this research is the first to employ LPA to capture the heterogeneity in ACP KAP levels among medical and nursing students. LPA has gained increasing application in fields such as education and psychology (Yang et al. 2024). This method identifies independent classes by explaining the relationship between external continuous variables through latent class variables and classifying them into different classes based on their characteristics (Vrieze 2012). This study identified two latent classes: Category 1 Collaboration Type (33.6%) and Category 2 Lagging Type (66.4%). Most students lack sufficient ACP competency. Students classified as “lagging” demonstrate weaker mastery of ACP KAP. This suggests they may not yet have developed a fundamental cognitive framework for ACP or lack the opportunities and confidence to engage in authentic clinical dialogues. Without targeted training before entering clinical settings, such students are prone to avoid ACP‐related tasks during internships or residency training, exhibiting “covert avoidance” behaviours (Gibbins et al. 2010). VR immersive experiences or puzzle games (Qian et al. 2025; Taubert et al. 2019) based on literary works can be used to alleviate death anxiety and stimulate interest in learning about advanced care planning. A U.S. study (Weber et al. 2023) developed a three‐year ACP curriculum employing diverse teaching methods including group discussions, role‐playing, case analysis, and video reviews. Upon completing the course, students demonstrated overall improvement in ACP discussion competency, with group discussions and direct practice opportunities with simulated patients identified as the most beneficial components. Collaborative learners possess a relatively well‐structured knowledge base, positive emotional attitudes, and adequate communication skills. Peer teaching methods can leverage their positive influence to drive learning outcomes.

However, there is currently a lack of consistent educational models and best practice standards (Boland et al. 2019). This underscores the need to further refine the curriculum design of the ACP. The vertically integrated curriculum (Radwany et al. 2011) design requires exposure to death education and ACP concepts from the early undergraduate years, incorporating case‐based teaching throughout clinical rotations to progressively enhance empathy and ACP readiness. The continuous refinement of the curriculum is essential for delivering high‐quality ACP, enabling students to provide professional and compassionate palliative care services.

The findings of this study indicate that major, grade, palliative care training, resuscitation experience, meaning in life, attitudes toward death, major attitude, and region significantly predicted the ACP KAP categories among medical and nursing students. Compared to the lagging group, senior students were more likely to belong to the Collaboration group, and the proportion of students classified into this group increased with grade. This finding aligns with the research by Eliza Lai‐Yi Wong et al. (Ng and Wong 2021), this may be attributed to the sustained professional practice and formal education in palliative care during senior years. The statistically significant relationship between ACP KAP further supports this.

Unlike the learning environment of junior‐year students, clinical practice provides senior students with extensive patient interactions. These experiences may help them develop more positive attitudes toward care and overcome fears associated with engaging with terminally ill patients (Stocklassa et al. 2024; Berndtsson et al. 2019), thereby increasing acceptance of ACP and promoting ACP‐related clinical behaviours. A Korean study (Kim et al. 2019) similarly demonstrated that practical experience during internships enhanced medical students' knowledge and skills in end‐of‐life care. Encouraging students to observe and participate in the diagnosis and prognosis of critically ill patients during clinical rotations may help improve their palliative care capabilities (Ibrahim et al. 2022). Similar to this study, medical and nursing students with resuscitation experience exhibited higher ACP KAP levels. Furthermore, palliative care coursework is crucial for knowledge and attitude toward ACP (Vellani et al. 2023). Our findings indicate that students who completed palliative care training were more likely to be classified in the collaboration group. Students trained through palliative care courses acquired fundamental skills and knowledge to initiate ACP conversations (Parikh et al. 2017), this result aligns with (Yang et al. 2021) prior research showing that students who received palliative care training often exhibit higher ACP KAP levels. Students who did not receive palliative care training were more uncertain about end‐of‐life care and decision‐making (Pieters et al. 2019).

It is noteworthy that compared to nursing majors, undergraduate medical students are more likely to be categorized as having lagging ACP knowledge reserves. The root cause may not stem solely from differences in course resources or faculty allocation, the “cure‐oriented” culture prevalent in medical education may also diminish students' emphasis on ACP. In the professional context (Mott et al. 2014) emphasizing “saving lives and treating the injured,” discussions about death and end‐of‐life decisions are often perceived as ‘failure’ or “giving up”. Nursing education places greater emphasis on patient‐centred care principles. Nurses frequently interact with patients and their families in clinical practice (Wang et al. 2020), particularly assuming crucial roles in communication and emotional support during end‐of‐life care (Wang and Yuan 2022). This experience may prompt nursing students to engage with and comprehend the significance and value of ACP earlier and more deeply. In terms of emotional aspects, major attitude, meaning in life, attitudes toward death are all significant predictors within the ACP KAP category. First, a positive major attitude (Guven Ozdemir et al. 2025) enhances medical and nursing students' patient‐centred capabilities, thereby increasing their sensitivity to perceiving the actual needs of terminally ill patients (Levy et al. 2021). This is crucial for promoting collaborative ACP discussions between clinicians and patients in clinical settings. During education, these competencies can be actively cultivated through simulated role‐playing and increased off‐campus internships to promote student‐patient interaction. Second, meaning in life can influence attitudes toward palliative care by alleviating death anxiety (Thiemann et al. 2015; Xu and Yu 2024). Medical and nursing students with negative attitudes toward death (such as death avoidance) exhibit lower overall ACP KAP scores, which may impact their competency in advance care planning. Consistent with previous international research (Ben Natan et al. 2024; Draper et al. 2024), medical school students' negative attitudes toward death may affect their care for terminally ill patients. Previous studies (Gan et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2023) indicate that negative attitudes toward death significantly diminish medical students' quality of care for terminally ill patients. In this study, students who avoid death were more likely to be classified as laggards, potentially undermining their ability to implement advance care planning. Conversely, students who accept death as natural were categorized as collaborators. Consistent with previous research (He et al. 2023), a positive attitude toward death, particularly one grounded in acceptance of its natural course, is crucial in the hospice care process. The fear of death profoundly influences humanity's pursuit of meaning in life. This pursuit is considered one of the distal defence mechanisms within fear management. When confronted with death‐related events, people often seek meaning through their own cultural worldview (Zheng et al. 2025). Therefore, undergraduates with higher levels of life meaning are more likely to be categorized into the collaborative group, and they may be more inclined to implement ACP in the future. Compared to rural students, urban students are more likely to be categorized into the lagging group. This may be due to differences in the growth environments of urban and rural students. Influenced by the traditional concept of “fallen leaves returning to their roots,” most elderly people prefer to pass away at home (Tang 2000). Students from rural areas often participate as family members in symptom management and end‐of‐life decision‐making, thereby gaining a better understanding of the practical value of ACP. In contrast, the convenience of urban health care resources makes it easier for urban residents to access hospital services, and older adults living in cities have the highest probability of dying in medical institutions (83%) (Zhou et al. 2025). Therefore, students from urban areas may be more familiar with a hospital‐centred, technology‐driven model of end‐of‐life care. Although, it has been demonstrated that (Lai et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2024) home‐based end‐of‐life care is more cost‐effective than hospital‐centred medical services, rural China still faces practical challenges such as insufficient primary end‐of‐life care resources and a lack of professional training. Therefore, the ACP education should adopt a differentiated strategy, enhance rural students' ability to communicate for ACP, enabling them to listen to elderly individuals' wishes as informal family caregivers to facilitate home‐based ACP. For urban students, teach skills in interpreting ICU informed consent and coordinating multidisciplinary family conferences to prevent overtreatment. In summary, ACP educational content should be tailored to students' diverse circumstances to promote the localized development of ACP in China.

Practical Implications

The nomogram prediction model developed in this study can serve as an assessment tool for university to accurately identify students' potential ACP KAP categories, providing quantitative evidence for personalized teaching. For different student categories, tiered interventions are recommended: For the “lagging” group, educators should embed death education and meaning in life counselling in early stages, using VR simulations and role‐playing to reduce death anxiety and solidify foundational cognition. For the “collaborative” group, instructors should proceed directly to advanced training, enhancing communication skills through standardized patient interactions and clinical case simulations. Curriculum design should be vertically integrated: introduce life‐and‐death education early in undergraduate studies, reinforce ACP case practice during internships, and leverage the peer influence of senior students and nursing majors to form a multidimensional teaching system fostering coordinated development of “cognition, emotion, and skills.” This model should be continuously applied for outcome validation, and further validation is needed in more regions.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths, and unlike other studies, it is the first to use the LPA approach to categorize medical students' ACP KAP, exploring the underlying characteristics. The nomogram linked to practical applications provides a powerful reference for teaching ACP in practice. Secondly, we added important psychological predictors to the research model, linking meaning in life and attitude toward death to ACP KAP, and explored the relationship between socio‐cultural and psychological factors and Chinese medical students' ACP KAP, bridging the gap of previous studies. There are also several limitations. First, the sample was only selected from undergraduate clinical medicine and nursing students at a medical university in Anhui Province, China. Although we performed internal validation using the bootstrap method, external validation in a multi‐centre setting could further enhance the generalizability of the model. Second, there may be potential residual confounding. Third, there may be misunderstandings or limitations in students' understanding, and there is some potential response bias in the self‐reported results, but the researcher minimized this bias in a variety of ways such as anonymous reporting, randomizing the order of questions, and balancing the options. Self‐selection bias might have been introduced due to the voluntary nature of participation. Although the 92.9% response rate helps mitigate this concern. Finally, cross‐sectional studies cannot establish causal relationships; therefore, future longitudinal studies are warranted to further validate these findings.

Conclusion

This study focused on the current status and influencing factors of ACP KAP among Chinese undergraduate nursing and clinical medicine students, and explored the relationship between meaning in life and attitude toward death and ACP competency. The importance of balancing skills and psychology during undergraduate education and clerkships was emphasized. Future ACP education should begin by identifying students' natural acceptance of death and their tendency to avoid death‐related thoughts, fostering a positive meaning in life, and developing personalized ACP curricula based on life and death education. Long‐term longitudinal follow‐up could further validate the validity of this study's model by exploring the interplay between sense of meaning in life and attitudes toward death, as well as the long‐term effects on ACP KAP.
