Effect of isofetamid on SDHI-resistant isolates of Botrytis cinerea
Authors: Yuzuka Abe, Yoshitake Desaki, Nanami Kuwahara, Yohei Fukumori, Shintaro Tsukuda, Kosuke Tsunematsu, Sayaka Komori, Munekazu Ogawa, Satoshi Araki, Shigeru Mitani, Kazumi Suzuki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Tests of sensitivity to isofetamid conducted with Botrytis cinerea isolated from Hokkaido (2011–2016) and other districts in Japan (2017–2018) revealed the absence of isolates with reduced sensitivity. Isolates of B. cinerea with mutation H272Y/R in sdhB, collected in Germany and Japan, resulted in high and moderate resistance to boscalid and many other succinate dehydrogenase inhibitors (SDHIs), respectively. However, these isolates were as sensitive as wild-type isolate to isofetamid. In contrast, isolate BC-50 [P225F] and BC-49 [H272L] caused moderate to high resistance to isofetamid. Considerable differences in mycelial growth, conidiation, conidial germination, and pathogenicity were not observed between these resistant isolates and sensitive isolates. A competitive assay between resistant isolate BC-50 [P225F] and BC-49 [H272L] and B05.10 [wild type], however, revealed the disappearance of resistant isolates after several generations of mixed incubation in in vitro and in vivo conditions.

Introduction

Succinate dehydrogenase inhibitors (SDHIs) are important broad-spectrum agricultural fungicides. Since the introduction of SDHIs, such as boscalid and penthiopyrad, which are effective against ascomycetes, several new compounds have been developed. These are widely used as foliage sprays and seed treatments for cereals, fruits, vegetables, turf, and ornamental plants.1–4) Therefore, this family of fungicides is an important component of disease control agents, along with demethylation inhibitors (DMIs) and quinone outside inhibitors (QoIs).4–8)

Isofetamid, N-[1,1-dimethyl-2-(4-isopropoxy-o-tolyl)-2-oxoethyl]-3-methylthiophene-2-carboxamide (code name: IKF-5411, trade names: KENJA®, ZENBY®, KRYOR®, HAREGI®, KABUTO®, ASTUN®) is a new fungicide developed by Ishihara Sangyo Kaisha, Ltd.9–12) We have previously reported that isofetamid is an SDHI (FRAC code 7). It specifically inhibits succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) activity in ascomycetes and does not affect the activity of SDH in oomycetes, plants, and mammals.11) The structure of isofetamid is unique in that it contains a phenyl-oxo-ethyl thiophene amide, which is the first halogen-free compound among the SDHIs marketed since 2000.13,14) We have also provided details regarding the fungicidal spectrum of isofetamid and its effect on the infection processes of Botrytis cinerea. Furthermore, we found that isofetamid did not exhibit cross-resistance to existing fungicides.11) We also reported the biological properties of isofetamid in greenhouse pot tests and the practical effects of isofetamid in field trials.12)

Although preliminary studies have reported that isofetamid has good biological activity against isolates resistant to other SDHI fungicides, its precise biological properties have not been investigated in detail.9,10) In this study, we reported (i) distribution of sensitivity to isofetamid in field populations of B. cinerea in Japan, (ii) effect of isofetamid on B. cinerea isolates resistant to other SDHI fungicides, and (iii) fitness parameters of B. cinerea isolates resistant to isofetamid and competition tests between isolates sensitive and resistant to isofetamid. Based on these results, we discussed the possibility of advantageous position of isofetamid among many SDHI fungicides.

Materials and methods

1. Distribution of sensitivity to isofetamid in field populations of B. cinerea in Japan

1.1. Baseline sensitivity to isofetamid in field populations of B. cinerea isolated in Hokkaido from 2011 to 2016

Baseline sensitivity to isofetamid among 1378 strains of B. cinerea isolated in Hokkaido, Japan from 2011 to 2016 was determined. The aerial conidia of B. cinerea in the field were trapped in a selective medium (modified version of Okada10,15)) using an air sampler. After incubation at room temperature for 5–7 days, single colonies were isolated on potato sucrose agar (PSA) medium. Isolated strains were incubated on PSA medium under continuous lighting at 20°C for 7 to 14 days, and the formed conidia were collected and used for sensitivity test. Sensitivity tests were conducted using a microtiter test referring to the testing methods for boscalid,16) and the EC50 values of isofetamid for the inhibition of mycelial growth were calculated. An isofetamid suspension concentrate (SC) of 400 g/L was used.

1.2. Sensitivity to isofetamid in field populations of B. cinerea isolated from several districts in Japan from 2017 to 2018

The sensitivity to isofetamid and boscalid among 290 strains of B. cinerea isolated from several districts in Japan from 2017 to 2018 was evaluated. Conidia were collected, and sensitivity tests were conducted using the method described in Section 1.1. Boscalid 50.0% dry flowable (Cantus®, BASF Japan, Ltd.) was also used.

2. Effect of isofetamid on B. cinerea isolates resistant to other SDHI fungicides

2.1. Activity of SDHI fungicides against resistant isolates of B. cinerea

Resistant isolates of B. cinerea (BC-42 and 43 [both H272R], BC-48 [H272Y], BC-51 [N230I], BC-50 [P225F], and BC-49 [H272L]) and sensitive isolate B05.10 [wild type] were used for a microtiter test, and the activity of SDHI fungicides against these isolates was evaluated. These resistant isolates (one of each) except BC-42 and BC-43 [both H272R] were isolated in Germany and kindly provided by Dr. M. Hahn of the University of Kaiserslautern. Two isolates BC-42 and BC-43 with H272R were isolated as boscalid-resistant isolates in Hokkaido. Genetic analysis of their sdhB, C and D genes revealed the presence of independent mutations in the sdhB gene; such as H272Y/R/L (substitution of histidine at position 272 with tyrosine, arginine or leucine), P225F (substitution of proline at position 225 with phenylalanine), and N230I (substitution of asparagine at position 230 with isoleucine), however, there was no change in amino acids of sdhC and D subunits (unpublished results). Sensitivity tests were conducted using a microtiter test10,16) and the EC50 values of isofetamid, fluopyram, boscalid, fluxapyroxad, and penthiopyrad for inhibiting mycelial growth were calculated. Fluopyram 500 g/L SC (Luna Privilege®, Bayer CropScience), penthiopyrad 20.0% SC (Aphet®, Mitsui Chemicals Crop & Life Solutions, Inc.) and fluxapyroxad 62.5 g/L EC (Imtrex®, BASF) were also used.

2.2. Control efficacy of SDHI fungicides against gray mold caused by resistant isolates of B. cinerea

The same isolates of B. cinerea described in Section 2.1. were used for pot tests against the gray mold of kidney beans, and the efficacy of the SDHI fungicides against gray mold caused by these isolates was evaluated. The adaxial surface of leaves of kidney beans seedlings (cv. Taisho-kintoki) were sprayed with test solutions of SDHI fungicides using a handgun sprayer (40 mL/0.2 m2; equivalent to 2,000 L/ha). Inoculation was conducted after the solutions dried. The conidia of each pre-cultured isolates were suspended in 50% potato sucrose (PS) liquid medium. Filter papers containing conidial suspensions of resistant and sensitive isolates of B. cinerea (1.0×106 conidia/mL, 100 µL) were placed on the detached leaves of the treated seedlings. The inoculated leaves were incubated at 20°C for 3 days. The fungicidal activity was evaluated by measuring the lesion diameter and was expressed in terms of disease suppression effects (0–100%) as described previously.11,12) The minimum effective concentration (MEC) indicated the concentration resulting in more than 95% suppression effects.

3. Comparison of fitness parameters between B. cinerea isolates resistant and sensitive to isofetamid

3.1. Comparison of mycelial growth on medium at different temperatures and pH values

Mycelial growth at different temperatures and pH values of the incubation medium was compared between resistant isolates BC-50 [P225F], BC-49 [H272L], BC-51 [N230I] and BC-48 [H272Y] and sensitive isolate B05.10 [wild type]. The mycelia of each pre-cultured isolate were punched using a cork borer. For different temperature conditions, each mycelial disc was transferred onto potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium and incubated at 7, 20, and 30°C for 7 days. For different pH conditions, each mycelial disc was transferred onto PDA medium adjusted to pH 3.8, 5.0, and 6.8 and incubated at 20°C for 7 days. After incubation, colony diameter was measured.

3.2. Comparison of conidiation, conidial germination, and pathogenicity

The degree of conidiation, conidial germination, and pathogenicity were compared between resistant isolates BC-50 [P225F], BC-49 [H272L], BC-51 [N230I] and BC-48 [H272Y] and sensitive isolate B05.10 [wild type]. For conidiation, the mycelia of each pre-cultured isolates were punched using a cork borer. Each mycelial disc was transferred onto PDA medium and incubated at 20°C for 14–21 days until the formation of conidia. After incubation, the mycelia with conidia were punched out using a cork borer. Two mycelial discs of 5 mm diameter were added to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes containing 1 mL of sterile water and vortexed for 10 sec to prepare conidial suspensions. The number of conidia was counted using a microscope and the number formed per unit area on the medium was calculated.

Conidial suspensions of each isolate (1.0×106 conidia/mL, 100 µL) were spread onto 1.5% water agar and incubated at 20°C for 20 hr to germinate. After incubation, the number of germinated conidia was counted under a microscope and the germination rate was determined.

Regarding pathogenicity, the conidia of each pre-cultured isolates were suspended in 50% PS liquid medium. Filter papers containing conidial suspensions (1.0×105 conidia/mL, 100 µL) were placed on the detached leaves of kidney beans seedlings. The inoculated leaves were incubated at 20°C for 3 days. After incubation, lesion diameters were measured.

4. Competition tests between B. cinerea isolates resistant and sensitive to isofetamid

Equal volumes of conidial suspensions of isolate BC-50 [P225F] and B05.10 [wild type] or isolate BC-49 [H272L] and B05.10 [wild type] were prepared for each generation, and their sensitivity to isofetamid was evaluated by counting the number of germinated conidia under a microscope. Conidial suspensions (2.0×104 conidia/mL) of isolate BC-50 [P225F] and B05.10 [wild type] or isolate BC-49 [H272L] and B05.10 [wild type] were mixed in a ratio of 1 : 1. Mixed conidial suspension (50 µL) was dropped in the center of the PSA medium and incubated at 20°C for 21 days. The formed conidia were suspended in yeast bacto acetate (YBA) liquid medium at twice the concentration and this was used as the first generation conidial suspension. Subsequently, to culture the next generation and beyond, 50 µL of the conidial suspensions were dropped onto PSA medium and incubated at 20°C. Isolate BC-50 [P225F] and B05.10 [wild type] were cultured six times and isolate BC-49 [H272L] and B05.10 [wild type] were cultured twice. Each conidial suspension (50 µL) was added to a 96-well plate to evaluate the sensitivity of each generation to isofetamid. In addition, 50 µL of isofetamid solution (2 µg/mL) or water was added and incubated at 20°C for 1 or 2 days. After incubation, the number of germinated conidia was counted under a microscope and the germination rate was calculated. Germinated conidia were regarded as resistant to isofetamid and counted accordingly. Experiments were repeated 2 times, showing the similar tendency. Overall germination rate in the absence of isofetamid was >90% in all generations, indicating that test conditions were stable.

In vivo competition tests were also conducted on eggplant fruits. Conidial suspensions (2.0×104 conidia/mL) of isolate BC-49 [H272L] and B05.10 [wild type] were mixed in a ratio of 1 : 1. Mixed conidial suspension (50 µL) was inoculated in the center of the sterilized cut surface of eggplant fruits (cv. Senryo 2) and incubated at 20°C for 7 days. Inoculation and incubation on eggplant fruits were repeated twice and the conidial germination was observed under a microscope as described above. In vivo competition test was conducted one time. Overall germination rate in the absence of isofetamid was >90% in all generations, indicating that test conditions were stable.

5. Statistical analyses

We performed one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Holm’s sequential Bonferroni post hoc test or Tukey’s post hoc honestly significant difference (HSD) for multiple-sample analyses using the multiple-sample comparison program (http://astatsa.com/OneWay_Anova_with_TukeyHSD/). The sample sizes and number of replicates for all the sets of assays and analyses are indicated in the legends of the corresponding figures.

Results

1. Distribution of sensitivity to isofetamid in field populations of B. cinerea in Japan

1.1. Baseline sensitivity to isofetamid in field populations of B. cinerea isolated in Hokkaido from 2011 to 2016

The EC50 values for mycelial growth inhibition of the 1378 isolates were obtained from the microtiter test (Fig. 1). The distribution of EC50 among 1378 isolates collected in Hokkaido from 2011 to 2016 ranged from <0.01 to 1.0 µg/mL, and the mean EC50 value was 0.11 µg/mL. Significant differences were not observed among distribution patterns of EC50 of each year, resulting in a single peak distribution of EC50 values ranging from 0.03 to 0.1 µg/mL. A unimodal distribution pattern of EC50 values was observed, and the distribution patterns of EC50 values did not change considerably over the six surveyed years (Fig. 1). These distribution patterns show baseline sensitivity to isofetamid because these isolates were not exposed to isofetamid until 2016.

Fig. 1. Baseline sensitivity to isofetamid among field populations of Botrytis cinerea isolated in Hokkaido from 2011 to 2016. EC50 values of mycelial growth inhibition of 1378 isolates were obtained from the microtiter test.

1.2. Sensitivity to isofetamid in field populations of B. cinerea isolated from several districts in Japan from 2017 to 2018

The EC50 values for mycelial growth inhibition of the 290 isolates were also obtained from the microtiter test. The distribution of sensitivity to isofetamid was also compared to that of boscalid (Fig. 2). The distribution pattern of EC50 of isofetamid was similar to that in Hokkaido. Significant differences were not observed among distribution patterns of EC50 of isofetamid. A unimodal distribution pattern of the EC50 values was observed (Fig. 2). These sensitivity tests revealed the absence of isolates with reduced sensitivity to isofetamid. In contrast, several isolates with reduced sensitivity to boscalid were detected during the survey in 2017–2018. The isolates with reduced sensitivity to boscalid showed EC50 values of >5 µg/mL (Fig. 2).

EC50 values of mycelial growth inhibition of 290 isolates were obtained from the microtiter method. Distribution of sensitivity to isofetamid was compared with that to boscalid.

2. Effect of isofetamid on B. cinerea isolates resistant to SDHI fungicides

2.1. Effect of isofetamid on mycelial growth of B. cinerea isolates resistant to SDHI fungicides

The EC50 values of mycelial growth inhibition were obtained from microtiter test. We tested mycelial growth among isolates with mutations H272R, H272Y, N230I, P225F, or H272L, which we were able to obtain, as well as a sensitive isolate. The EC50 values for each SDHI are listed in Table 1. The EC50 value of the isolate sensitive to isofetamid was 0.11 µg/mL and there was no significant difference between the sensitive isolate B05.10 [wild type] and isolate BC-51 [N230I]. However, isolates BC-42 and 43 [both H272R] and BC-48 [H272Y] showed hypersensitivity to isofetamid. In contrast, isolates BC-50 [P225F] and BC-49 [H272L] were less sensitive to isofetamid (Table 1).

3. Comparison of fitness parameters between B. cinerea isolates resistant and sensitive to isofetamid

3.1. Comparison of mycelial growth at different temperatures and pH values

Mycelial growth at different temperatures and pH values of the incubation medium was compared between resistant isolates BC-50 [P225F], BC-49 [H272L], BC-51 [N230I] and BC-48 [H272Y] and sensitive isolate B05.10 [wild type] (Supplemental Fig. S1a and b). Five isolates were incubated at three temperatures, 7, 20, and 30°C. Mycelial growth at 7 and 30°C was smaller than that at 20°C. Especially, growth at 30°C was inhibited considerably. All four isolates with sdhB mutation showed a significant decrease in colony diameter at 7°C. Isolate BC-48 [H272Y] exhibited a significant decrease at 20°C. However, the mycelial growth at 20°C (except BC-48 [H272Y]) and at 30°C was similar among the four isolates with sdhB mutations and the sensitive isolate (Supplemental Fig. S1a).

Mycelial growth at different pH values of the incubation medium was also compared between resistant isolates and sensitive isolate (Supplemental Fig. S1b). Five isolates were incubated on PSA medium at pH 3.8, 5.0, and 6.8. Isolate BC-51 [N230I] exhibited a significant increase in mycelial growth at pH 3.8. At pH 6.8, however, all four isolates showed a significant decrease in colony diameter. Mycelial growth of each isolate on media at pH 5.0 was similar among the four isolates with sdhB mutations and the sensitive isolate (Supplemental Fig. S1b).

3.2. Comparison of conidiation, conidial germination, and pathogenicity

The degree of conidiation, conidial germination, and pathogenicity were compared between resistant isolates BC-50 [P225F], BC-49 [H272L], BC-51 [N230I] and BC-48 [H272Y] and sensitive isolate B05.10 [wild type] (Supplemental Fig. S2a, b, and c). Five isolates were incubated on PDA medium, and the numbers of conidia was counted and compared. Isolates BC-50 [P225F], BC-49 [H272L] and BC-51 [N230I] exhibited a significant increase in sporulation (Supplemental Fig. S2a). Then, the percentage of conidial germination of the five isolates was compared, showing no considerable difference in the germination rates of the conidia (Supplemental Fig. S2b). Finally, the pathogenicity of the five isolates was compared using kidney bean leaves. Isolates BC-49 [H272L] and BC-48 [H272Y] showed a significant decrease in pathogenicity (Supplemental Fig. S2c).

The fitness parameters of the different isolates were affected differentially. Considerable differences in mycelial growth, conidiation, conidial germination, and pathogenicity were not observed between these resistant isolates and sensitive isolates (Supplemental Figs. S1 and S2). Therefore, these results do not support major fitnesss cost due to mutation in the sdhB subunit compared with sensitive isolate.

4. Competition tests between isolates resistant and sensitive to isofetamid of B. cinerea

In vitro competition tests were conducted by mixed incubation on PSA medium. Equal volumes of conidial suspensions of isolate BC-50 [P225F] and B05.10 [wild type] or isolate BC-49 [H272L] and B05.10 [wild type] were plated on PSA medium and the conidia obtained after incubation at 20°C were incubated in the presence of isofetamid (1 µg/mL) in a microtiter test. Germinated conidia were considered as resistant isolates. Figure 3a shows the results for isolate BC-50 [P225F] versus the sensitive isolate B05.10 [wild type]. The germination rate of conidia presumed to carry the P225F mutation gradually declined. After six generations, the germination rate of conidia presumed to carry the P225F mutation declined to nearly 0%, and the sensitive isolate became dominant. Figure 3b shows the results of isolate BC-49 [H272L] versus the sensitive isolate B05.10 [wild type]. The results were similar to those of the isolate BC-50 [P225F] versus the sensitive isolate. The germination rate of conidia presumed to carry the H272L mutation rapidly declined, and the sensitive isolate was dominant. Therefore, in vitro competition tests between isolates BC-49 [H272L] and BC-50 [H225F] versus sensitive isolate B05.10 [wild type] showed that these isolates resistant to isofetamid gradually disappeared after several generations in mixed conditions with sensitive isolate.

Considering both in vitro and in vivo results comprehensively, isolates which reduced sensitivity to isofetamid may have lower fitness and are therefore more likely to be eliminated in the field.

Discussion

Generally, the mechanisms of fungicide resistance are mainly caused by the mutation of target sites, detoxification of the fungicide molecules, and increased efflux of fungicide molecules.5,8) Many cases of resistance to SDHIs are thought to be caused by mutation in the target sites (i.e., sdh). Since the introduction of boscalid and penthiopyrad into the market, the development of isolates resistant to SDHIs has been reported in many plant pathogens.1,6–10) Now, the resistance risks of SDHIs are medium to high according to FRAC. Analysis of the coding regions of sdh in isolates resistant to SDHIs revealed the presence of mutations in the conserved amino acid residues of sdhB, sdhC, and sdhD, regardless of the type of fungi. The locations of the mutations and the types of substituted amino acids were diverse.1,6–10)

Sensitivity tests for isofetamid conducted with gray mold fungus isolated from Hokkaido (2011–2016) and from other districts in Japan (2017–2018) revealed the absence of isolates with reduced sensitivity to isofetamid. A unimodal distribution pattern of EC50 values was observed, and the distribution patterns of EC50 did not change considerably during the surveyed years (Figs. 1 and 2). These distribution patterns in Hokkaido shows baseline sensitivity to isofetamid because these isolates were not exposed to isofetamid until 2016. In contrast, several isolates with reduced sensitivity to boscalid were detected during the survey in 2017–2018. Our preliminary sequence analysis of sdh indicated that the H272Y/R substitution in sdhB existed in the investigated boscalid-resistant isolates (unpublished results).

The mutations involved in resistance in B. cinerea were mainly concentrated in sdhB. The frequency of detection of H272Y/R in sdhB was relatively high, whereas the other mutations such as H272/L, P225F and N230I were rarely detected.1,8,9,17–20) Sensitivity of various isolates to SDHIs was different, depending on the chemical structures of each chemical.1,8) Isolates with three mutations, i.e., H272Y, H272R, and H272L, were resistant to boscalid; however, isolates with H272Y and H272R were sensitive to fluopyram.1,8,9,17–20) In contrast, mutation of P225F caused moderate to high resistance to all SDHIs investigated.1,8–10) The results in this study, though based on a limited number of isolates, indicated that isofetamid possessed excellent efficacy against isolates with mutation H272Y and H272R, but not against isolate with mutation H272L (Table 1 and 2). This cross-resistance pattern is similar to that of fluopyram. These differences in cross-resistance among SDHIs were also observed in Corynespora leaf spot fungus of cucumber. The H278Y mutaion in sdhB, which corresponds to H272Y in B. cinerea, did not reduce the sensitivity to fluopyram.21,22) Isofetamid showed excellent efficacy against this type of mutant fungus.10) We hypothesized that this difference in sensitivity might be caused by the unique chemical structures of isofetamid and fluopyram.8,13,14) In contrast to other SDHIs, isofetamid and fluopyram do not contain N atoms in the ring structure of the carboxylic acid moiety. These fungicides are not affected by mutations in histidine (H272 in Botrytis or H278 in Corynespora), which are thought to interact with SDHIs containing a chemical structure such as a pyridine or pyrazole moiety. Furthermore, both fungicides are characterized by a spacer structure linking both ring structures, which may affect the cross-resistance to different SDHIs.1,13,14)

Isofetamid showed reduced sensitivity to isolates BC-49 [H272L] and BC-50 [H225F] in both in vitro tests and pot tests (Table 1). However, the frequency of detection of these resistant isolates in the fields was low.17–20) Considerable differences of mycelial growth, conidiation, conidial germination, and pathogenicity were not observed between these resistant isolates and sensitive isolate, however, competition tests between isolates BC-49 [H272L] and BC-50 [H225F] versus the sensitive isolate B05.10 [wild type] showed that these resistant isolates gradually disappeared after several generations (Supplemental Figs. S1 and S2, and Figs. 3 and 4). These results in this study indicated that the low detection frequency of these mutants in the fields may be related to the fitness costs of these isolates. Isolates with mutations H272L and H225F may have reduced SDH activity; therefore, they could not survive when mixed with other isolates under field conditions as discussed in previous studies.1,8,9,17,20) These findings indicate the advantageous position of isofetamid among SDHIs against the important plant pathogen, B. cinerea.

Another mechanism of fungicide resistance may be the increased transport of fungicides outside fungal cells by efflux pumps.23) Fungicide transport has been studied for multidrug resistance (MDR) mechanism, and two classes of efflux pumps have been identified to be involved in fungicide resistance: ATP–binding cassette (ABC) transporters and major facilitator superfamily (MFS) transporters in B. cinerea.23) Our preliminary experiments revealed that isofetamid also showed high efficacy against MDR isolates of B. cinerea with reduced sensitivity to fludioxonil and cyprodinil, which are fungicides indicating presence of MDR resistance.24) This finding also indicates the unique characteristics of isofetamid.
