Efficacy and safety of urokinase in the treatment of loculated pleural effusions caused by various etiologies: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Tingting Jiang, Wuxi Chen, Guosheng Liang, Zifeng Peng, Jiaxin Tang, Qing Tang, Liantu He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLoculated pleural effusion caused by fibrinous septation often leads to incomplete drainage and prolonged hospitalization. Urokinase facilitates fibrin degradation; however, its overall efficacy and safety across different etiologies remain uncertain. This meta-analysis evaluated the clinical effectiveness and safety of intrapleural urokinase for loculated pleural effusions of various causes.MethodsA comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang, and Chinese Biomedical Literature Database (CBM) was performed up to December 2024. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing urokinase plus standard drainage with drainage alone or with saline were included. Eligible studies enrolled patients with radiologically confirmed loculated or multiloculated pleural effusions and reported at least one quantitative efficacy or safety outcome. Trials using other fibrinolytics or steroids were excluded. The primary outcome was treatment success rate. Secondary outcomes included total drainage volume (mL), hospital stay (days), and adverse events. Data were synthesized using RevMan 5.4. Subgroup analyses were performed based on etiology and control group intervention (thoracentesis vs. catheter drainage).ResultsTwenty-five RCTs involving 1,990 patients (1,018 urokinase; 972 controls) met the inclusion criteria. Urokinase significantly improved treatment success rate [odds ratio (OR) =5.28, 95% confidence interval (CI): 3.98–7.01; P<0.001] and increased total drainage volume [mean difference (MD) =622.32 mL, 95% CI: 377.22–867.43; P<0.001]. It shortened pleural effusion absorption time (MD =−7.31 days), hospital stay (MD =−6.94 days), and reduced residual pleural thickness (MD =−0.93 mm; all P<0.05). Subgroup analysis based on control group intervention (thoracentesis vs. catheter drainage) showed consistent efficacy. Adverse events did not differ significantly between groups (P>0.05).ConclusionsIntrapleural urokinase appears to be a safe and effective adjunctive therapy for loculated pleural effusions of diverse etiologies, consistently improving drainage and clinical outcomes. Nevertheless, the certainty of evidence is limited by moderate study quality, dosing heterogeneity, and potential publication bias. Further high-quality randomized trials are warranted. This review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024581473).

Highlight box

Key findings

• Intrapleural urokinase significantly improves treatment success rates [odds ratio (OR) =5.28] and total drainage volume while shortening hospital stay and pleural effusion absorption time in patients with loculated pleural effusions. The adverse event rate was not significantly different from the control group.

What is known and what is new?

• Loculated pleural effusions often fail standard drainage and may require surgery. While tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) is widely used in the West, urokinase is a common, cost-effective option in Asia, though its efficacy across different etiologies (tuberculosis, malignancy, empyema) has lacked comprehensive evaluation.

• This meta-analysis of 25 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) confirms that urokinase is consistently effective and safe across diverse etiologies. Crucially, subgroup analysis shows its superiority remains robust regardless of whether the control group uses simple thoracentesis or catheter drainage.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Urokinase serves as a safe and effective adjunctive therapy for loculated pleural effusions. Clinicians, particularly in resource-limited settings, should prioritize it as a non-surgical alternative to improve drainage and reduce hospitalization before considering surgical decortication.

Introduction

Loculated pleural effusion occurs when the pleura is stimulated by inflammation, causing the continuous exudation of fibrous proteins. These deposits gradually thicken and adhere to the pleura. Aseptic pleural effusion can also form from the accumulation of proteinaceous material and cellular debris in the thoracic cavity, leading to pleural encapsulation and segregation over time (1). Without routine drainage, loculated pleural effusion can lead to a prolonged treatment course and an unfavorable prognosis. However, standard tube thoracostomy alone often fails in these complex cases due to the presence of multiple septations that prevent effective fluid evacuation, frequently necessitating surgical decortication (2).

To address this challenge, intrapleural fibrinolytic therapy has emerged as a non-surgical alternative. Intrapleural injection of urokinase degrades fibrin within the pleural cavity, reducing the viscosity of pleural effusion, eliminating adhesions and segregation of the pleura, facilitating the drainage of pleural fluid, and preventing pleural hypertrophy and adhesion (3). While tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) is widely used in Western countries following the MIST-2 trial (4), urokinase remains a primary, cost-effective, and safe option in many Asian and developing regions. As a result, intrapleural urokinase has gained widespread clinical use in treating loculated pleural effusion from various etiologies (5-8).

The etiologies of pleural effusion lead to a variety of clinical presentations, broadly classified as tuberculous pleural effusion, parapneumonic pleural effusion/empyema and malignant pleural effusion (9). Crucially, the density of fibrin septations and the response to fibrinolytic agents may vary significantly depending on the underlying pathology. Furthermore, the dissolution effect of various types of pleural effusions may differ when urokinase is applied to the formation of loculated pleural effusion (10). Despite the widespread use of urokinase, there is a lack of comprehensive quantitative evidence comparing its efficacy across these diverse etiological subgroups.

In this study, a comprehensive search of multiple databases was conducted, and strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) for a meta-analysis on the efficacy of urokinase in treating loculated pleural effusions from various etiologies. The objective was to systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of intrathoracic urokinase in treating loculated pleural effusions of different etiologies to provide robust evidence for clinical decision-making. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-aw-2267/rc).

Methods

Literature search and selection criteria

China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), PubMed, Cochrane Library, EMbase, Web of Science, Wanfang database, and Chinese Biomedical Literature Database (CBM) were searched for clinical RCTs on the use of urokinase for treating loculated pleural effusion. The search covered studies published both domestically and internationally from the inception of each database to 2024. Detailed search strategies for each database should be provided in Appendix 1. This review was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024581473); no protocol amendments were made.

The following search terms were used: loculated, multiloculated, pleural effusion, and urokinase. Study type: RCT. All references from the included studies were reviewed to confirm their relevance.

Inclusion criteria for study selection were as follows: (I) published RCTs from domestic and international sources; (II) pleural effusion, either loculated or multiloculated, confirmed by thoracic ultrasound or lung CT; (III) treatment involving thoracic puncture aspiration or thoracic tube drainage was administered; (IV) the experimental group received thoracic puncture/tube drainage with the intra-thoracic injection of urokinase (+ conventional medication), while the control group received thoracentesis alone or tube drainage with an intra-thoracic injection of an equal amount of 0.9% sodium chloride injection (placebo) (+ conventional medication); (V) at least one of the study’s outcome indicators was reported, and adverse reactions were documented; (VI) none of the patients in the experimental group had contraindications to urokinase such as coagulation abnormalities, allergy to urokinase, or a history of bleeding disorders within the previous month.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) non-loculated pleural effusion; (II) non-RCTs; (III) animal experiments or in vitro experiments; (IV) studies with abstracts, lectures, reviews, duplicate reports, and incomplete clinical information; (V) studies where data could not be used due to inconsistencies with the efficacy indicators outlined in the inclusion criteria, or studies that did not clearly describe experimental and control groups; (VI) studies that involved combined intrathoracic injection of drugs such as heparin and hormones that may affect the evaluation of the efficacy of urokinase

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two independent reviewers extracted data, including study characteristics, interventions, and outcomes. The primary outcome indicator was treatment success rate. We defined treatment success as the achievement of substantial radiological improvement [>50% reduction in pleural fluid volume or disappearance of loculations on ultrasound/computed tomography (CT)] accompanied by relief of clinical symptoms without the need for surgical intervention (11,12).

Secondary outcomes included: total pleural fluid drainage volume (mL); pleural effusion absorption time (days); length of hospital stay (days); residual pleural thickness (mm), which was included to assess the risk of long-term restrictive lung disease; pulmonary function index and improvement of respiratory symptoms. Adverse reactions included hemothorax, coagulation disorders, fever, chest pain, pneumothorax, and pleural reaction.

The risk of bias was evaluated independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (13) based on the following criteria: (I) appropriateness of the randomization; (II) use and adequacy of allocation concealment; (III) whether blinding was used for the subjects and intervention implementers; (IV) whether blinding was adopted for outcome measurements; (V) whether the data of the study were complete, whether there was any abandonment or loss of visit during the study, and if so, whether the study was carried out; (VI) selective reporting of study outcomes; (VII) identification of any other potential sources of bias.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Data processing and analysis were conducted using Review Manager 5.4.1 software. Dichotomous variables were evaluated using odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), while continuous variables were analyzed using mean difference (MD) with corresponding 95% CIs. Heterogeneity between studies was assessed using the χ2 test. If P>0.05, heterogeneity was considered statistically insignificant, and a fixed-effects model was applied for the combined analysis. If P<0.05, heterogeneity was deemed significant, and a random-effects model was used (13). Statistical significance was considered at P<0.05. In cases of substantial clinical and statistical heterogeneity, only a descriptive analysis was performed. Funnel plots were generated to assess publication bias.

To address potential sources of clinical heterogeneity identified during the review process, subgroup analyses were performed based on: (I) etiology of pleural effusion (tuberculous, parapneumonic/empyema, malignant); and (II) type of control group intervention (thoracentesis alone vs. tube drainage + saline).

Results

Literature search and inclusion

Literature screening

According to the Chinese and English subject headings, a total of 1,243 articles published between 1994 and 2024 were retrieved from each database. After excluding duplicates and initially screening the abstracts, 267 articles met the requirements. Each article was then read in detail according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria and finally included in the systematic evaluation of a total of 25 articles (7,14-37); the process of literature screening is illustrated in Figure 1. Among the included studies, 10 articles (17,20-23,25,28-30,36) were on tuberculous pleural effusion, 10 articles (14,15,18,19,24,26,33-35,37) on parapneumonic pleural effusion/empyema, and 5 articles (7,16,27,31,32) on malignant pleural effusion, encompassing a total of 1,990 cases (1,018 cases in the observation group and 972 cases in the control group). Assessment of the quality of literature

The 25 papers included all mentioned “random” grouping. Five (14-17,30) were grouped based on the random number table method, 3 (19,21,24) were grouped based on the treatment method, 1 (27) was grouped based on the coin toss, and the rest did not describe the random method. Four (7,18,35,37) employed double-blind treatments between the experimental operator and the patients, and 9 (15,21,23,26,27,28,30,32,33) indicated that patients or their guardians were required to sign the Urokinase Chest Fluorosupplementation Agreement (UCFA) prior to the treatments. The remaining 12 papers (14,16,17,19,20,22,24,25,29,31,34,36) did not mention whether a blinding method was utilized. Meta-analysis results

Treatment success rate

The heterogeneity of the treatment success rate of the 21 (14-18,20-34,37) included literature was minimal (χ2=21.13, P=0.39, I2=5%); thus, a meta-analysis was conducted using the fixed effect model. The OR was 5.28 (95% CI: 3.98–7.01), indicating a significant difference (P<0.001; Figure 4A). These results suggest that intrapleural urokinase treatment significantly improves the treatment success rate compared to control treatments for loculated pleural effusions.

Forest plots of meta-analysis for urokinase treatment in loculated pleural effusion. (A) Treatment success rate (21 studies); (B) total pleural effusion drainage volume (11 studies, MD in mL); (C) pleural effusion absorption time (14 studies, MD in days); (D) length of hospital stay (12 studies, MD in days); (E) residual pleural thickness (7 studies, MD in mm). CI, confidence interval; MD, mean difference; SD, standard deviation.

Total pleural effusion drainage volume

The total volume of pleural effusion drainage in the experimental and control groups was recorded in 11 studies (7,14-16,23,25,28,30,34,36,37). The heterogeneity test demonstrated significant heterogeneity among the studies (χ2=950.55, I2=99%, P<0.001). The analysis revealed an MD of 622.32 mL (95% CI: 377.22–867.43), indicating a statistically significant increase in drainage volume for the urokinase group (P<0.001; Figure 4B). Contrary to the control group, patients receiving urokinase achieved significantly greater fluid evacuation.

Pleural effusion absorption time

The absorption time of pleural effusion absorption was recorded in 14 papers (14,15,17,20,23,25,27-30,33-34,36,37) for both the experimental and control groups. The heterogeneity test revealed significant variability among the studies (χ2=1,410.15, I2=99%, P<0.001), necessitating the use of a random effects model for the combined analysis. The results showed a significant reduction in absorption time (MD =−7.31 days, 95% CI: −9.81 to −4.80; P<0.001; Figure 4C). The time to radiological resolution was significantly shorter in the urokinase group compared to the control group.

Length of hospital stay

The hospitalization time for the experimental and the control group was recorded in 12 studies (7,14,15,18,19,24,28,30,33-35,37). The heterogeneity test showed significant variability among the studies (χ2=240.43, I2=95%, P<0.001), thus necessitating the use of a random effect model for combined analyses. Results showed a significant difference (MD =−6.94 days, 95% CI: −9.37 to −4.51; P<0.001; Figure 4D). These results indicate that the duration of pleural effusion absorption was significantly shorter in the experimental group than in the control group.

Residual pleural thickness

The residual pleural thickness for both the experimental group and the control group was recorded in 7 studies (17,20,25,28-30,33). The heterogeneity test showed significant variability among the studies (χ2=1,853.12, I2=100%; P<0.001), which necessitated the use of a random effect model for the combined analysis. The results showed that urokinase treatment significantly reduced residual pleural thickness compared to the control group (MD =−0.93 mm, 95% CI: −1.54 to −0.32; P=0.003; Figure 4E). The residual pleural thickness was reduced in the experimental group compared with that in the control group.

Adverse reactions

The occurrence of adverse reactions was documented in 25 studies involving 1,990 patients (7,14-37). The overall incidence of adverse events was 8.3% (84/1,018) in the urokinase group and 11.5% (112/972) in the control group. The meta-analysis showed no statistically significant difference in the risk of adverse events between the two groups (risk difference =0.85, 95% CI: 0.42–1.71; P=0.64; Figure S1), indicating that urokinase administration did not increase the aggregate safety risk. Regarding specific severe adverse events, two deaths were reported in a study involving malignant pleural effusion (7); however, these occurred within 3 days of enrollment and were attributed to the rapid progression of the underlying malignancy (1 in the urokinase group and 1 in the placebo group) rather than the intervention. Hemorrhagic complications, including hemorrhagic pleural effusion and coagulation dysfunction, were reported in 8 studies (7,17,21,26,29,32,35,37) but showed no significant increase compared to the control group. Chest pain was reported in 11 RCTs (17-20,23,25-28,32,35); hemorrhagic pleural effusion, coagulation dysfunction, or hemorrhage; fever in 6 RCTs (17,20,27,29,32); pleural reactions in 4 studies (18,28-30); pneumothorax in 4 studies (21,28,30,33); and hemopneumothorax in 1 study (22). Overall, intrapleural urokinase demonstrated a favorable safety profile consistent with clinical expectations (25).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis of treatment success rate

The treatment success rate was analyzed according to the type of control group intervention (thoracentesis vs. catheter drainage) to investigate the potential source of clinical heterogeneity. The results of subgroup analysis suggested that, compared with the control group, the experimental group showed significant improvement in treating loculated pleural effusion in the thoracentesis subgroup (OR =7.19, 95% CI: 4.53–11.40, P<0.001) and the catheter drainage subgroup (OR =4.27, 95% CI: 2.97–6.15, P<0.001). Notably, the statistical heterogeneity was low in both the catheter drainage subgroup (I2=0%) and the thoracentesis subgroup (I2=31%), indicating that the efficacy of urokinase is robust regardless of the drainage method used in the control group (Figure 5A).

(A) Subgroup analysis of treatment success rate stratified by control group intervention (thoracentesis vs. catheter drainage); (B) total pleural effusion drainage volume stratified by etiology (MD, mL); (C) pleural effusion absorption time stratified by etiology (MD, days); (D) length of hospital stay stratified by etiology (MD, days); (E) residual pleural thickness stratified by etiology (MD, mm). A fixed-effect model was used for (A) due to low heterogeneity, while a random-effects model was used for (B-E) due to significant heterogeneity. CI, confidence interval; MD, mean difference; SD, standard deviation.

Subgroup analysis of total pleural effusion drainage volume

The total drainage volume of pleural effusion was analyzed according to different etiologies. The results of subgroup analysis suggested that, compared with the control group, the experimental group showed improvement in treating tuberculosis pleural effusion (MD =825.86 mL, 95% CI: 41.16–1,610.57; P=0.04), parapneumonic pleural effusion/empyema (MD =601.49 mL, 95% CI: 308.70–894.27; P<0.001), and malignant pleural effusion in terms of total pleural fluid drainage (MD =221.34 mL, 95% CI: 144.06–298.63; P<0.001). These differences were statistically significant (Figure 5B).

Subgroup analysis of pleural effusion absorption time

The absorption time of pleural effusion was analyzed according to different etiologies. The results of subgroup analysis indicated that, compared with the control group, the experimental group treated tuberculosis pleural effusion (MD =−6.12 days, 95% CI: −10.27 to −1.97; P=0.004), parapneumonic pleural effusion/empyema (MD =−6.35 days, 95% CI: −8.09 to −4.61; P<0.001), malignant pleural effusion in terms of pleural effusion absorption time (MD =−29.97 days, 95% CI: −37.29 to −22.65; P<0.001). These differences are statistically significant (Figure 5C).

Subgroup analysis of length of hospital stay

The length of hospital stay was analyzed according to different etiologies, and the results of subgroup analysis indicate that, compared with the control group, the treatment of loculated pleural effusion in the experimental group was more effective for tuberculosis pleural effusion (MD =−7.50 days, 95% CI: −8.74 to −6.26; P<0.001), parapneumonic pleural effusion/empyema (MD =−7.31 days, 95% CI: −10.46 to −4.15; P<0.001), and malignant pleural effusion, which showed statistically significant differences in terms of length of hospital stay (MD =−2.50 days, 95% CI: −4.80 to −0.20; P=0.03) (Figure 5D).

Subgroup analysis of residual pleural thickness

Residual pleural thickness was analyzed according to different etiologies, and the results of subgroup analysis suggested that, compared with the control group, the experimental group showed improvement in treating loculated pleural effusion in tuberculosis pleural effusion (MD =−0.97 mm, 95% CI: −1.70 to −0.25; P=0.009) and empyema with residual pleural thickness (MD =−0.68 mm, 95% CI: −0.77 to −0.59; P<0.001). The difference between groups was statistically significant (Figure 5E). However, due to the small number of study in the parapneumonic/empyema (n=1) (33) reporting this outcome, these specific subgroup results should be interpreted with caution (Figure 5E).

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of the primary outcome (treatment success rate), a sensitivity analysis was performed using the “leave-one-out” method. Included studies were excluded sequentially to observe changes in the pooled effect size (OR) and 95% CI, as well as heterogeneity metrics (P value and I2). Following the exclusion of any single study, the recalculated ORs and 95% CIs remained consistent with the overall results, and no significant fluctuations in the direction of the effect were observed. This stability suggests that the findings of the present study are robust and not driven by any single outlier.

Assessment of publication bias

Funnel plots were generated to assess potential publication bias for treatment success rate, total drainage volume, absorption time, hospital stay, and residual pleural thickness. Visual inspection of the funnel plots (Figure 6) revealed varying degrees of asymmetry. While this asymmetry may suggest potential publication bias, it could also be attributed to the substantial clinical heterogeneity and small-study effects observed among the included trials. Consequently, the interpretation of secondary outcomes should be approached with appropriate caution.

Discussion

Urokinase is a protein-hydrolyzing enzyme that reduces the viscosity of loculated pleural effusion by degrading fibrin, removing pleural adhesions, and preventing septation, thereby facilitating smooth drainage of the effusion, increasing the volume of drainage, and enabling re-expansion of the lung tissues (38). Additionally, urokinase, when combined with antibacterial agents to combat infection, can destroy the biofilms and inhibit cellular DNA synthesis. This action causes cytoplasmic retraction and cell shrinkage, leading to cell death, which enhances the bactericidal effect (39,40). Since 1989, when Moulton et al. (41) first employed urokinase to treat loculated pleural effusion, most cases of loculated pleural effusion caused by various etiologies have been effectively managed with this intervention. While tPA has become the standard of care in some Western guidelines following the MIST-2 trial (4), unlike streptokinase, urokinase is non-antigenic, allowing for repeat administration without risk of anaphylaxis (42). Our review confirms that urokinase remains a highly effective, safe, and accessible alternative, particularly in Asian countries and resource-limited settings.

The meta-analysis of 25 RCTs in this study demonstrated a significant increase in treatment success rate and pleural fluid drainage rate, along with a significant reduction in pleural thickness, pleural fluid absorption time, and hospital stay in the trial group. These significant differences (P<0.05) suggest that the combination of urokinase treatment markedly enhances treatment efficacy, increases pleural fluid drainage rate, shortens pleural fluid absorption time and hospital stay, and reduces pleural thickness. Regarding the high statistical heterogeneity observed in the overall analysis, we identified that the variation in control group interventions (thoracentesis vs. catheter drainage) was a major contributing factor. Our subgroup analysis revealed that urokinase was consistently superior to the control group regardless of whether the control intervention was simple thoracentesis or indwelling tube drainage. However, significant heterogeneity was observed in the results of pleural fluid drainage, pleural thickness, pleural fluid absorption time, and hospital stay. This could not be substantially reduced after subgroup analysis, indicating that it might be related to factors such as duration of the disease, method of drainage (aspiration), dosage of urokinase, and duration of indwelling urokinase. To minimize potential bias, we conducted subgroup analyses based on the etiology of pleural effusion and found that the results for effective rate and the increase in the total amount of pleural effusion drainage, pleural fluid absorption time, hospital stay, and reduction in residual pleural thickness were similar to the overall analysis, and the differences between the experimental and control groups were statistically significant (P<0.05).

In this study, we analyzed the adverse reactions reported in 25 RCTs and found no statistically significant difference in the incidence of adverse reactions between the test and control groups (P>0.05), indicating that urokinase has a favorable safety profile. Two deaths occurred in the study by Mishra et al. (7), but both were judged to be unrelated to the study drug (attributed to rapid disease progression), further supporting the safety of urokinase in critically ill patients. However, our meta-analysis did not observe a significant increase in hemorrhagic complications. This may be explained by the fact that intrapleural urokinase acts locally with minimal systemic absorption, thereby sparing systemic coagulation function. Theoretically, fibrinolytic agents carry a risk of bleeding due to the dissolution of fibrin clots. It is hypothesized that hemorrhage occurs because high-dose urokinase dissolves substantial amounts of fibrin, leading to rapid perfusion of capillaries previously occluded due to pleura thickening, resulting in reperfusion hemorrhage (43). The high frequency of chest pain and fever across both groups suggests these symptoms stem primarily from mechanical irritation by the catheter or the underlying inflammation, rather than urokinase toxicity. Additionally, rapid fluid evacuation often induces re-expansion pain. This well-known clinical phenomenon likely contributed to the symptoms observed in the treatment group, where drainage was significantly more effective.

It is also important to consider the impact of drainage methods on outcomes. Traditionally, thoracentesis was used for pleural effusion drainage, but it requires multiple punctures and often fails in loculated cases. As noted in our review, 9 studies (14,17,20-22,25,28-30) utilized serial thoracentesis in the control arm. Repeated punctures are associated with cumulative risks of pneumothorax, soft tissue trauma, and patient discomfort, which may have elevated the baseline adverse event rate in the control group. Currently, percutaneous drainage using a central venous catheter (or pigtail catheter) is more commonly employed and is recommended by international guidelines for better safety and efficacy (44). This method avoids repeated and blind punctures, significantly decreasing procedure-related complications. Therefore, the “no difference” finding in safety reflects both the low toxicity of urokinase and the inherent risks of different drainage modalities.

The limitations of this study are as follows. First, the methodological quality evaluation of the included studies was insufficient to completely exclude selection bias and information bias. Second, significant clinical heterogeneity was observed, primarily stemming from the differing control group protocols. Although our subgroup analysis based on control type (thoracentesis vs. catheter) clarified this issue, future standardization of control arms is necessary. Third, most studies included in this review only reported short-term efficacy, with none specifying long-term follow-up results or providing accurate assessments of long-term outcomes and endpoint events. Fourth, no study has compared the efficacy of different doses of urokinase in treating tuberculosis-related loculated pleural effusion, and the analysis of adverse reactions remains insufficient. This warrants further exploration through high-quality, large-sample, stratified, and RCTs.

Conclusions

Intrapleural injection of urokinase significantly improves the treatment success rate and total drainage volume, while shortening the absorption time and hospital stay in patients with loculated pleural effusion of various etiologies. Although heterogeneity exists due to varying control group standards (thoracentesis vs. catheter drainage), subgroup analysis confirms that urokinase consistently provides superior efficacy regardless of the drainage method used. Furthermore, urokinase demonstrates a favorable safety profile with no significant increase in adverse events compared to control interventions. However, the certainty of the evidence is limited by the moderate quality of the included studies and potential publication bias. Future high-quality, large-scale, multicenter RCTs are warranted to validate these findings and optimize dosing protocols.
