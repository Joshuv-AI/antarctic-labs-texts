A 53-year-old woman with rectosigmoid adenocarcinoma was seen in the radiation oncology clinic for her fifth and final fraction of neoadjuvant radiotherapy, where she reported chills, fatigue, shortness of breath, and swelling in her left calf. She had received a diverting loop colostomy 3 months prior, but no surgical resection or chemotherapy. She had a remote history of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) but was not receiving current anticoagulation treatment. Her medications included ondansetron, metoprolol, and tramadol–acetaminophen. She was febrile to 39.2°C, peripheral oxygen saturation (Spo2) was 92% on room air, blood pressure was 104/62 mm Hg, and heart rate was 98 beats/min.

We admitted the patient to the hospital, where septic workup and empiric antibiotics were started. Ultrasonography showed a large DVT in the left leg. Subcutaneous enoxaparin was initiated at a therapeutic dose (1 mg/kg twice daily).

The next day, computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) showed a large saddle pulmonary embolism extending to the subsegmental arteries of all lobes bilaterally, with concern for pulmonary infarction in the lower lobes (Figure 1A) and evidence of right heart strain. At this point, the patient’s vitals were stable.

Before her evening dose of enoxaparin, the patient’s blood pressure decreased, fluctuating between 83/51 mm Hg and 93/58 mm Hg. She was alert and in no apparent distress, with Spo2 of 95% on room air and other vital signs within normal limits. Her lactate level was 3.2 (normal 0–2) mmol/L, and her troponin I level was 18 (normal 0–12) ng/L. Her borderline hypotension and elevated cardiac biomarkers placed her in the intermediate–high-risk pulmonary embolism category. However, her hemoglobin level was 69 (normal 120–160) g/L, down from 90 g/L the day prior and 110 g/L the week before, and she reported passing black, tarry stool per rectum since admission. A blood transfusion was ordered, her evening anticoagulation dose was held, and her management was discussed in detail among medical oncology, critical care medicine, and interventional radiology.

The patient’s worsening hypotension despite 24 hours of fluid resuscitation and anticoagulation was concerning for impending decompensation if the clot burden was not rapidly reduced. However, her anemia and black, tarry stools were concerning for occult gastrointestinal bleeding, likely secondary to the recent radiotherapy or the colorectal cancer itself. Although catheter-directed thrombolysis was indicated for this patient, we considered that it might precipitate catastrophic gastrointestinal bleeding. Therefore, we deemed pulmonary mechanical thrombectomy most appropriate.

Pulmonary angiography performed by an interventional radiologist showed a large thrombus in the right pulmonary artery with ipsilateral poor pulmonary perfusion. They advanced a catheter into the right pulmonary artery and performed a thrombectomy using a mechanical thrombosuction device (Lightning, Penumbra) (Figure 2A). Completion angiography showed substantial improvement in right pulmonary blood flow, and the patient’s clinical status improved, with systolic blood pressure increasing to 112 mm Hg (Figure 2B). The interventional radiologist similarly performed a mechanical thrombectomy of the left lower lobe pulmonary artery. Intraprocedural blood loss totalled 1500 mL, which was largely replaced by concurrent transfusion.

(A) Pulmonary angiogram during the mechanical thrombectomy. The arrow indicates one of the filling defects on the initial pulmonary angiogram. (B) Completion angiogram showing complete clearance of previously demonstrated thrombus.

We placed an inferior vena cava (IVC) filter following the mechanical thrombectomy in lieu of full-dose anticoagulation, because of her suspected gastrointestinal bleed. She remained high risk for recurrent pulmonary embolism and was started on prophylactic-dose enoxaparin (40 mg/d) after the procedure.

The patient’s hemoglobin stabilized over the next few days, and she was discharged taking apixaban (5 mg/d) 7 days later. At 2-week follow up, her breathing and leg swelling were much improved. No residual pulmonary embolus, pulmonary artery dilatation, or right heart strain were found on CTPA performed 6 weeks after the procedure (Figure 1B). As the patient was tolerating anticoagulation, the IVC filter was removed after 4.5 months, and apixaban was continued.

Discussion

Acute pulmonary embolism is the third most common acute cardiovascular emergency,1 and the incidence is rising as the aging population accumulates chronic medical conditions that predispose to venous thromboembolism.2 Cancer causes a four-to sevenfold increased risk of developing venous thromboembolism, which represents the second leading cause of death in patients with cancer, after the cancer itself.2

Death from pulmonary embolism occurs not from hypoxia, but from pulmonary hypertension and increased right ventricular afterload pressures leading to right ventricular ischemia and cardiogenic shock.1 Whereas treatments to rapidly reduce clot burden can avert this hemodynamic collapse, they also risk severe blood loss. Selecting the optimal management strategy therefore depends on balancing the patient’s risk of hemodynamic collapse with the likelihood of precipitating a life-threatening hemorrhage.

Research has identified clinical features and biomarkers that can help predict a patient’s risk of death from pulmonary embolism. 3 Risk stratification into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk pulmonary embolism identifies patients with a high likelihood of decompensation and helps determine treatment.3

In low-risk pulmonary embolism, patients are vitally stable with few comorbidities, and their risk of clinical deterioration is low.1 Patients presenting with low-risk pulmonary embolism are treated with anticoagulation.

Conversely, high-risk pulmonary embolism is defined by acute hemodynamic instability: sustained hypotension (systolic blood pressure < 90 mm Hg, or a decrease in systolic blood pressure ≥ 40 mm Hg from baseline) for a minimum of 15 minutes or the need for vasopressor support.3 These patients are at imminent risk of death, with an in-hospital mortality of 30%.3 Patients presenting with high-risk pulmonary embolism are treated by reperfusion, usually systemic thrombolysis.

Intermediate-risk pulmonary embolism falls in the middle. These patients are hemodynamically stable, but with comorbidities or clinical parameters of pulmonary embolism severity that indicate an increased likelihood of decompensation. This category may be further subdivided into intermediate–low risk (either right ventricular strain on imaging, or elevated biomarkers, or neither) and intermediate–high risk (both right ventricular strain on imaging and elevated biomarkers).3

The 2 main categories of pulmonary embolism treatment are anticoagulation and reperfusion therapy. Anticoagulation prevents thrombus growth, which without further treatment will dissolve over weeks or months. For low-risk pulmonary embolism, this is usually all that is needed. For intermediate-risk pulmonary embolism, low-molecular-weight heparin is recommended as the initial therapy, given its faster onset of action than that of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs).3 Low-molecular-weight heparin and the DOAC apixaban are equally safe for patients with an increased bleeding risk, such as patients with gastrointestinal cancer.4 Current guidelines recommend the use of anticoagulation in low-risk, intermediate–low-risk, and most intermediate–high-risk patients.3

Reperfusion therapy aims to rapidly reduce clot burden, and it is indicated for patients in whom anticoagulation alone will not reverse hemodynamic compromise in a timely manner. These include high-risk and select intermediate–high-risk patients who are likely to deteriorate. Systemic thrombolysis is the first-line reperfusion therapy. By actively breaking down fibrin clots, it takes effect within 2 hours. However, there is an associated risk of hemorrhage. Contraindications to thrombolysis (Box 1) are not uncommon — 70% of patients with pulmonary embolism and unstable vital signs do not receive thrombolysis at all,7 likely because patients who are at a higher risk of dying from pulmonary embolism are also more likely to have comorbidities that confer a prohibitively high bleeding risk. Although systemic thrombolysis is the first-line treatment in high-risk pulmonary embolism, guidelines recommend against its use in intermediate–high-risk pulmonary embolism.3

Catheter-directed therapies have gained interest over the last decade. These procedures are performed most commonly by interventional radiologists and may be considered in select patients with high- and intermediate–high-risk pulmonary embolism (Box 2). These therapies include catheter-directed thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy. Catheter-directed thrombolysis involves infusion of low-dose thrombolytics directly into the pulmonary artery via an appropriately placed catheter and produces clinical benefit after 4 to 6 hours. It has been shown to reduce the risk of death compared with systemic thrombolysis, with a bleeding risk comparable to that of anticoagulation. 1 Catheter-directed thrombolysis may be considered on an individual basis for patients with intermediate–high-risk pulmonary embolism where a high likelihood of clinical deterioration exists.

For example, presence of elevated lactate, persistent tachycardia, borderline hypotension, hypoxemia, or rapidly escalating oxygen requirements, presentation with syncope or pre-syncope.

Catheter-directed thrombolysis should be used to treat high-risk pulmonary embolism only in cases where mechanical thrombectomy is not available or is for other reasons unsuitable.

Mechanical thrombectomy involves the physical removal of thrombus from the pulmonary artery using suction, with or without prior mechanical fragmentation, and does not use any thrombolytics. Mechanical thrombectomy has shown a consistent reduction in the risk of bleeding compared with systemic thrombolysis, 8 and initial clinical trials have shown its safety and efficacy in intermediate-risk patients.9 Its immediate and effective restoration of hemodynamic stability is especially advantageous,2 but it can be associated with substantial blood loss as blood is removed with thrombus. Additionally, mechanical thrombectomy involves the placement of a large-bore (16 to 17F) catheter inside the pulmonary artery, which requires the patient to lie comfortably with minimal motion for around 1 to 1.5 hours in the angiography suite. Mechanical thrombectomy may be considered in patients with high-risk or intermediate–high-risk pulmonary embolism in whom thrombolysis is contraindicated.3

The patient presented here deteriorated gradually and required ongoing risk stratification. As her systolic blood pressure did not drop below 90 mm Hg for 15 consecutive minutes, she was considered intermediate–high risk. However, her borderline vitals, concomitant DVT,10 ongoing hypotension (systolic blood pressure 90 mm Hg to 100 mm Hg), and elevated lactate3 were risk factors for further deterioration, which was the indication for reperfusion therapy.3

Ongoing risk stratification is crucial for optimal management of a patient with pulmonary embolism. Whereas most patients are adequately managed with anticoagulation alone, select intermediate–high-risk and high-risk patients can undergo reperfusion therapy with catheter-directed therapy. Mechanical thrombectomy may provide better earlier outcomes than catheter-directed thrombolysis, although we await data from ongoing clinical studies and long-term results.
