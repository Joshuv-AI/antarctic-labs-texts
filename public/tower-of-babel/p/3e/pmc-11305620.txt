Fecal Microbiota, Live-jslm for the Prevention of Recurrent Clostridioides difficile Infection
Authors: Paul Feuerstadt, Carl V. Crawford, Xing Tan, Victoria Pokhilko, Lindy Bancke, Samson Ng, Beth Guthmueller, Monique R. Bidell, Glenn Tillotson, Stuart Johnson, Andrew M. Skinner
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Goals:To assess fecal microbiota, live-jslm (REBYOTA, abbreviated as RBL, formerly RBX2660) efficacy and safety in participants grouped by recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection (rCDI) risk factors and treatment-related variables.Background:RBL is the first microbiota-based live biotherapeutic approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for the prevention of rCDI in adults after antibiotic treatment for rCDI.Study:Treatment success rates across subgroups for PUNCH CD3 (NCT03244644) were estimated using a Bayesian hierarchical model, borrowing data from PUNCH CD2 (NCT02299570). Treatment-emergent adverse events were summarized for the double-blind treatment period within 8 weeks.Results:Treatment differences between RBL and placebo at 8 weeks were similar to the total population for most subgroups. Treatment effect sizes were similar between CDI tests, higher for oral vancomycin courses >14 days versus ≤14 days and higher for antibiotic washout periods of 3 days versus ≤2 days. The largest reductions in the rate of rCDI with RBL versus placebo were observed for participants with a 3-day CDI antibiotic washout period and participants with ≥4 previous CDI episodes. Most RBL-treated participants experienced TEAEs that were mild or moderate in severity and related to preexisting conditions.Conclusion:This analysis provides further evidence of RBL efficacy and safety across subgroups, including those at high risk for rCDI.

The gut microbiome comprises a broad consortium of commensal microbes that provide numerous benefits, including colonization resistance against pathogens such as Clostridioides difficile. 1 C. difficile, an anaerobic, spore-forming, toxin-producing bacterium acquired from the environment through fecal-oral transmission,2 is the leading cause of health care–associated infections in the United States3 and has been declared an urgent public health threat by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.4 C. difficile infection (CDI) and recurrent CDI (rCDI) are associated with substantial morbidity, mortality, and health care resource utilization.4–6

Multiple risk factors for CDI have been identified and include many antibiotics; advanced age; hospitalization; immunosuppression; underlying comorbidities such as congestive heart failure (CHF), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM); and use of gastric acid–suppressing agents [proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and H2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs)].7,8 Antibiotics, including the CDI standard-of-care (SOC) agents vancomycin and fidaxomicin,7,9,10 destroy gut microbes through their elimination pathways through the intestinal tract, resulting in an even more disrupted microbiome than before treatment (ie, dysbiosis) and reduced colonization resistance.11

A therapeutic option to prevent rCDI is restoration of the gut microbiome after treatment of the acute infection with SOC antibiotic therapy.12,13,14 Fecal microbiota, live-jslm (REBYOTA, abbreviated here as RBL, formerly known as RBX2660) is the first single-dose, microbiota-based live biotherapeutic product (LBP) approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the prevention of recurrence of CDI in adults, following antibiotic treatment for rCDI.15 RBL is manufactured from human fecal matter sourced from qualified donors that is screened for a panel of transmissible pathogens in alignment with FDA requirements to help address patient safety. It is formulated as a 150-mL rectal suspension and does not require bowel preparation before administration.

RBL efficacy was evaluated in 2 multicenter, prospective, randomized, placebo-controlled trials: PUNCH CD2 (phase 2b)16 and PUNCH CD3 (phase 3).17 In PUNCH CD2, treatment success at 8 weeks was achieved by 65.8% of participants who received 1 dose of RBL plus 1 dose of placebo and 44.2% of participants who received 2 doses of placebo in the modified intent-to-treat (mITT) population.16 To analyze outcomes in the PUNCH CD3 mITT population, a Bayesian hierarchical model was used that dynamically borrowed information from the PUNCH CD2 trial. The model-estimated rates of treatment success were 70.6% for RBL and 57.5% for placebo, representing a 13.1-point treatment difference, with a posterior probability of superiority of 0.991.17 In both trials, RBL was well tolerated, with mostly mild to moderate adverse events (AEs). Given the similarities between these 2 trials that allowed for Bayesian model borrowing, we aimed to analyze clinically important risk factors that may have impacted the efficacy and safety outcomes of RBL in the study populations. These findings could help inform clinical decision-making around the use of RBL in real-world care settings.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

PUNCH CD2 (NCT02299570)16 and PUNCH CD3 (NCT03244644)17 were prospective, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 2 (PUNCH CD2) and 3 (PUNCH CD3) trials, assessing the efficacy and safety of RBL in participants with rCDI. Enrolled participants were ≥18 years old with documented rCDI, who had completed SOC antibiotic therapy before treatment with rectally administered RBL or placebo. Per the study definition, documentation of rCDI at study entry included either: (1) at least 2 recurrences after a primary episode and completion of at least 2 rounds of SOC oral antibiotic therapy (PUNCH CD2) and at least 1 recurrence after a primary episode with completion of at least 1 round of SOC oral antibiotic therapy (PUNCH CD3); or (2) at least 2 episodes of severe CDI resulting in hospitalization (PUNCH CD3 stipulated hospitalization within the past year). The antibiotic washout period was 24 to 48 hours in PUNCH CD2 and 24 to 72 hours in PUNCH CD3.

Outcomes

Treatment success was defined as the absence of CDI diarrhea within 8 weeks of study treatment. Treatment failures (ie, those with CDI recurrence) were confirmed with central laboratory testing for the presence of C. difficile toxin and adjudicated by the Data Safety Monitoring Board (PUNCH CD2) and Efficacy Adjudication Committee (PUNCH CD3). The central laboratory testing algorithm included the use of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) if the C. DIFF QUIK CHEK COMPLETE ® results were discordant (ie, GDH+/Toxin−).18 Treatment-emergent AEs (TEAEs) in the safety population were summarized for the double-blind treatment period within 8 weeks and censored at treatment failure. The safety population included all participants for whom treatment was attempted and/or completed. TEAEs were defined as any AE occurring on or after the day of first treatment and were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA), version 20.0. TEAEs are presented by MedDRA system organ classes (type) and preferred terms (exact description of medical condition). Serious AEs (SAEs) were defined as AEs or adverse reactions meeting any of the following outcomes: event resulted in death, the event was life-threatening, the event required hospitalization ≥24 hours or prolongation of existing hospitalization, persistent or significant incapacity or substantial disruption of the ability to conduct normal life functions, congenital anomaly/birth defect, or an important medical event. SAEs with an onset on or after the day of first treatment are referred to as serious TEAEs.

Safety Oversight

PUNCH CD2 and PUNCH CD3 included stopping rules requiring study pause or termination if a pathogenic intestinal infection was detected for which RBL or its administration was considered a probable cause or if any series of events of major significance such as death or other SAE (including systemic infection) similar in nature in different participants was causally connected to RBL.

Participant Consent

PUNCH CD2 and PUNCH CD3 were conducted in the United States and Canada according to the ethical principles of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki, Good Clinical Practice guidelines, principles of informed consent, and requirements of publicly registered clinical trials. All participants provided written informed consent, and all protocols received institutional review board/research ethics board approval before commencement.

Subgroup Analysis

The mITT population, defined as all randomly assigned participants who successfully completed treatment and did not discontinue the trial during the first 8 weeks for reasons unrelated to CDI, served as the primary analysis population for this subgroup analysis. Only the single-dose RBL group and placebo control group from PUNCH CD2 were included (not the 2-dose RBL group), because PUNCH CD3 participants received 1 dose of RBL or placebo. Treatment success rates were assessed in subgroups according to risk factors for CDI recurrence [age, number of prior CDI episodes, any PPI/H2RA use, and baseline comorbid conditions (CKD, CHF, and T2DM)] and treatment-related variables (qualifying CDI diagnostic test, duration of oral vancomycin therapy before blinded treatment, and duration of CDI antibiotic washout before blinded treatment). Participants with baseline comorbid conditions were identified based on medical history preferred terms (CHF: cardiac failure congestive; T2DM: diabetes mellitus and type 2 diabetes mellitus; and CKD: chronic kidney disease, end-stage renal disease, renal failure, renal failure chronic, and renal impairment). Because comorbid conditions can increase the risk of AEs and adverse CDI-related outcomes, TEAEs were summarized for participants with CKD, T2DM, and CHF, as well as the total population.8,19

Statistical Analysis

Using the Bayesian hierarchical model borrowing data from the PUNCH CD2 trial, treatment success rates were estimated for PUNCH CD3 trial participants with risk factors for CDI recurrence and treatment-related variables. Two-sided 95% credible intervals (CrIs) for the treatment effect sizes were calculated, where CrI overlaps with zero indicated nonsignificance. Safety and observed efficacy for the subgroups, as well as demographic and other summary statistics, were assessed using the combined PUNCH CD2 and PUNCH CD3 population. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS Life Science Analytics Framework 5.2.3 (SAS Institute Inc., Houston, TX, USA), R,20 version 4.2.1, RStan package (Stan Development Team. RStan: the R interface to Stan. R package version 2.26.13. https://mc-stan.org/), and Stan, version 2.26.1.

RESULTS

Demographics and Baseline Characteristics

The combined PUNCH CD2 and PUNCH CD3 mITT populations included a total of 216 RBL-treated participants and 128 placebo-treated participants (Table 1). The mean age was 60 years; 68% of participants were female, and 93% were White. Approximately 60% of participants had 2 or 3 CDI episodes before study entry (ie, experienced 1 or 2 CDI recurrences), inclusive of the qualifying event. Most participants (70%) were diagnosed via PCR, most (88%) received vancomycin alone for the qualifying CDI episode, and most (77%) had an antibiotic washout period ≤2 days before receiving placebo or RBL. PPI/H2RA use was reported by 28% of participants. A higher proportion of participants treated with RBL compared with placebo had CKD (12% vs 6%) and T2DM (13% vs 6%); a similar proportion of participants had CHF (7% and 6%).

Efficacy

Treatment success rates were numerically higher for RBL participants compared with placebo participants across all subgroups (Fig. 1). As previously reported from the PUNCH CD3 analysis, model-estimated rates of treatment success at 8 weeks were 70.6% with RBL compared with 57.5% with placebo in the total population.17 RBL significantly reduced the risk of rCDI in participants with at least 4 previous CDI episodes compared with placebo [20.8 (95% CrI, 3.3 to 38.0)]; the treatment effect was smaller in participants with 2 or 3 previous episodes of CDI [9.5% (95% CrI, −3.6 to 22.8)]. Treatment effect sizes were higher for participants who did not use PPI/H2RAs [14.9% (95% CrI, 2.7 to 27.2)] compared with those who did [8.8% (95% CrI, −12.6 to 30.1)]. Among participants with comorbidities, treatment effect sizes were similar for those with T2DM [10.9% (95% CrI, −25.7 to 47.6)] and CKD [13.3% (95% CrI, −28.5 to 56)] but smaller for those with CHF [1.7% (95% CrI, −39.3 to 40.1)]. RBL significantly reduced CDI recurrence compared with placebo in subgroups without comorbidities [no CHF: 14.2% (95% CrI, 3.2 to 25.2); no CKD: 13.9% (95% CrI, 2.8 to 24.9); no T2DM: 14.2% (95% CrI, 3.0 to 25.4)]. Treatment effects were similar for participants ≥65 years [12.2% (95% CrI, −4.1 to 28.7)] and <65 years [14.7% (95% CrI, 0.7 to 28.7)].

Safety

Within 8 weeks of double-blinded treatment, TEAEs were reported by 51.8% (115/223) of RBL-treated participants and 45.0% (59/131) of placebo-treated participants (Table 2). Most participants experienced TEAEs that were at most mild [RBL: 23.9% (53/222); placebo: 22.9% (30/131)] or moderate [RBL: 22.1% (49/222); placebo: 19.1% (25/131)] in severity. TEAEs were frequently related to preexisting conditions [RBL: 26.6% (59/222); placebo: 24.4% (32/131)].

The proportion of participants with CKD, T2DM, and CHF who experienced TEAEs ranged from 29% to 50% (Table 2). Similar to the total population, most participants experienced TEAEs that were at most mild or moderate in severity.

Serious TEAEs were reported by 4.1% (9/222) of RBL-treated participants and 1.5% (2/131) of placebo-treated participants (Table 3). Serious TEAEs spanned various system organ classes, with gastrointestinal disorders being the most reported events in RBL-treated participants [1.4% (3/222)]. No single event type (dictionary-derived term) was reported by more than 1 participant in either the RBL or placebo groups. One death due to cardiorespiratory arrest was reported in a participant on day 37 after receiving 1 dose of RBL; this event led to the only study discontinuation in this analysis. The participant had a history of severe cardiovascular disease, coronary artery bypass grafting (×4), atrial fibrillation, cerebrovascular accident, tachycardia, Parkinson’s disease, epilepsy, and dementia. All serious TEAEs, including the event leading to death, were deemed related to CDI recurrence and/or preexisting conditions; none were considered related to RBL or its administration. In participants grouped by comorbidities, serious TEAEs were reported by 2 participants (6.9%) with T2DM (anemia and asthenia) and 1 participant (7.1%) with CHF (chronic osteomyelitis) treated with RBL. All 3 events were deemed related to preexisting conditions and unrelated to RBL or its administration. There were no reported infections caused by pathogens traceable to RBL, which is consistent with the overall population.

DISCUSSION

RBL was approved by the FDA for the prevention of the recurrence of CDI in November 2022 and represents an available and accessible treatment option for patients at risk for rCDI. RBL administration after SOC antibiotic treatment for rCDI has shown consistent efficacy across phase 2 and phase 3 clinical trials.17 In this subgroup analysis, treatment differences between RBL and placebo for rCDI at 8 weeks were similar to the total population for most subgroups categorized by risk factors for CDI recurrence and treatment-related variables. However, some groups appeared to experience greater benefit from RBL relative to placebo, including those with multiple recurrences, those who received longer SOC antibiotic courses, and those with a longer washout period before RBL administration.

A number of patient factors that have been associated with a higher risk for rCDI and poor outcomes were individually evaluated in this study, including prior CDI episodes, advanced age, concomitant PPI/H2RA use, and comorbid CKD, CHF, and T2DM. Although SOC antibiotics are generally very effective at controlling acute CDI, infection recurrence (typically defined as occurring within 8 weeks per guidelines) occurs in up to 35% of patients after a primary episode, with the risk of recurrence substantially increasing after 1 episode of rCDI; multiply recurrent CDI is reported by up to 65% of patients with 1 or more prior recurrences.2,21,22 In this analysis, all patients had at least 1 episode of rCDI. Although the observed and model-estimated RBL treatment success rates were similar, the RBL treatment effect was greater in participants who experienced 3 or more episodes of rCDI (ie, ≥4 episodes) compared with 1 or 2 episodes of rCDI. These results further illustrate that SOC therapy alone becomes less effective at preventing recurrence in multiply recurrent patients and underscores the therapeutic potential of using an FDA-approved microbiome restoration product such as RBL.

Advanced age8,23,24 and the use of gastric acid–suppressing agents are also notable risk factors for CDI.25,26 Results from this analysis showed that RBL prevented rCDI at a similar rate in participants 65 years and older compared with those younger. However, RBL had a smaller treatment effect among those receiving PPI/H2RA therapy at baseline versus those who were not on gastric acid–suppressing medications. The reduced treatment effect was consistent with previously published results in older PUNCH CD3 participants with gastrointestinal disorders, most of whom had gastroesophageal reflux disease being treated with PPI/H2RAs.27 The same analysis found consistent rates of RBL treatment success across subgroups over 65 years old with cardiac disorders, CKD, and GI disorders.27 RBL treatment success rates were similar among participants with T2DM and those with CKD. In contrast, a much lower treatment effect was observed among participants with CHF than those without CHF. However, small sample sizes limit interpretation, and the impact of multiple comorbidities and other risk factors on outcomes was not analyzed.

CDI is a clinically complicated infection in that diagnostic testing, antibiotic management, and its recurrent nature all pose challenges that may also impact the outcomes of a microbiome-based therapeutic product. While symptoms such as diarrhea are characteristic of CDI, diagnosis is challenging in that no single laboratory test is considered optimal.7,9 PCR-based assays only detect the presence of 1 or more genes specific to toxigenic C. difficile strains but do not detect active toxin production. Therefore, PCR positivity does not distinguish colonization from active infection, and inclusion in randomized controlled trials based on a PCR-positive result alone can potentially impact treatment efficacy rates. Any available CDI diagnostic assay paired with clinical symptoms consistent with CDI diarrhea was allowed for inclusion in both PUNCH CD2 and PUNCH CD3. In both studies, most of the qualifying CDI episodes were diagnosed by a PCR-based assay, in agreement with current clinical practice trends.28 In this subgroup analysis, RBL reduced the risk of rCDI at a rate similar between participants with PCR-based and toxin EIA–based assays for inclusion of the qualifying CDI episode.

Current guidelines from the Infectious Diseases Society of America, Society for Healthcare Epidemiology of America, and American College of Gastroenterology recommend the use of fidaxomicin and vancomycin (preferably in a taper/pulse regimen) for the management of recurrent CDI.7,9 Although guidelines generally favor the use of fidaxomicin, vancomycin use remains common in clinical settings. Vancomycin was the most commonly used CDI antibiotic to treat the enrolling rCDI episode before blinded treatment in PUNCH CD2 and PUNCH CD3. A larger risk reduction was observed among participants who received longer courses of vancomycin compared with shorter courses, which may imply the use of taper/pulse strategies. Vancomycin taper/pulse regimens are thought to encourage the germination of spores into vegetative cells, making them vulnerable to therapy while also allowing microbiota to recover.29 Indeed, these regimens have previously been shown to minimize the risk of recurrence.30 Because of the limited number of participants who received fidaxomicin, the treatment effects of fidaxomicin duration were not evaluated.

Lingering presence of SOC antibiotic before administration of a microbiome-based product may kill live microbes, negatively impacting microbiome restoration and clinical efficacy. While there is no standardized recommendation on the duration of antibiotic washout before administration of a microbiome-based product, an analysis of randomized controlled trials of fecal microbiota transplantation for rCDI found a range of 24 hours to 72 hours across studies.31 The washout period was 24 to 48 hours in PUNCH CD2 and 24 to 72 hours in PUNCH CD3. Accordingly, most participants in this subgroup analysis had an antibiotic washout period of 2 or fewer days. Interestingly, RBL had a larger treatment effect in participants with a 3-day washout, which may suggest greater clearance of residual CDI antibiotic activity at this time point compared with <48 hours after treatment course completion. While a bowel preparation is sometimes administered before a microbiome-based product to enhance antibiotic washout, this strategy was not studied before RBL administration in an effort to simplify the process and patient experience; any effects on treatment success could not be assessed. Given bowel preparation is not required before administration of RBL, this may theoretically improve the patient experience by not reintroducing CDI-associated symptoms such as diarrhea.

To date, nearly 1000 participants have received RBL, with a low number of SAEs related to treatment.32 The most frequently reported TEAEs were gastrointestinal events that occurred in close proximity to treatment and resolved within a few days. Here, although TEAEs were reported by a higher proportion of participants with serious comorbid conditions compared with the total population, most reported TEAEs were mild or moderate in severity. Serious TEAEs were reported by a similar proportion of participants with comorbid conditions who received RBL as those who received placebo.

Limitations

While RBL efficacy was consistently higher versus placebo across a range of subgroups, this analysis was not powered for statistical significance, and the results cannot be generalized to all populations. The majority of participants were White, and most received vancomycin for the qualifying CDI episode. The results are also limited by the small number of participants with specific comorbidities, particularly those with CHF. Participants with fulminant CDI, life expectancy <12 months, inflammatory bowel disease, and immunocompromising conditions were excluded from PUNCH CD2 and PUNCH CD3. Additional studies in “real-world” populations are warranted to better characterize risk factors that may impact LBP use. Final results from an open-label phase 3 RBL trial (PUNCH CD3-OLS), which included participants with inflammatory bowel disease and immunocompromising conditions, as well as a separate subgroup analysis of participants on concomitant systemic antibiotics, will be reported in the future.

CONCLUSION

Treatment differences between RBL and placebo for rCDI were similar to the total population for most subgroups categorized by risk factors for CDI recurrence and treatment-related variables that are commonly encountered in clinical practice. Among participants who experienced AEs, most had events that were mild or moderate in severity. Together, this analysis provides further evidence RBL is efficacious and safe in a broad population of patients with rCDI, including those at high risk for future recurrence.
