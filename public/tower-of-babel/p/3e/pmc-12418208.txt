Validation of ReadSmart4U: An Automated Tool for Neuropsychological Assessment Reporting
Authors: Jeongbin Park, Chae Hyun Kim, Sungjin Park, Hae In Kim, Ji Won Han, Ki Woong Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeuropsychological assessments are critical to cognitive care, but are time-consuming and often of variable quality. Automated tools, such as ReadSmart4U, improve report quality and consistency while meeting the growing demand for cognitive assessments.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study analysed 150 neuropsychological assessments stratified by cognitive diagnosis (normal cognition, mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer’s disease) from the Clinical Data Warehouse of a university-affiliated referral hospital (2010–2020). Reports were generated for each assessment by ReadSmart4U and certified clinical psychologists (CCPs). Three blinded CCPs assessed report quality using the Integrated Scoring for Quality Assessment of Structure (ISQAS) for absolute quality and the Integrated Scoring for Quality Comparison of Superiority (ISQCS) for paired comparisons. Domains assessed included terminology accuracy, interpretation accuracy, usefulness and writing quality.ResultsReadSmart4U-generated reports outperformed CCP reports in all ISQAS domains, with mean overall quality scores of 87.3 ± 3.4 vs. 74.5 ± 6.7 (P < 0.001). Domain-specific scores were higher for terminology accuracy (31.4 ± 1.5 vs. 26.8 ± 2.1), interpretation accuracy (32.2 ± 1.7 vs. 27.3 ± 2.4), usefulness (10.8 ± 0.9 vs. 9.2 ± 1.2) and writing quality (14.5 ± 0.6 vs. 12.7 ± 1.1; all P < 0.001). In the ISQCS evaluations, ReadSmart4U reports were judged superior in 80.2% of cases for overall quality and 58.0–88.2% for domains (P < 0.001).ConclusionReadSmart4U significantly improves the quality and consistency of neuropsychological assessment reports compared to CCPs, reducing workload and supporting the integration of automated tools into clinical workflows to improve cognitive care.

INTRODUCTION

The rising prevalence of cognitive impairment and dementia, in conjunction with the global ageing of the population, has resulted in a significant increase in demand for neuropsychological assessments.12 These assessments are of critical importance for the diagnosis and management of cognitive disorders, as they evaluate key domains such as memory, attention, and executive functions.1345

Despite the clinical importance of neuropsychological reports, the preparation of such documents remains a time-consuming process.6 A previous survey reported that it took clinical psychologists approximately two hours to complete a neuropsychological assessment report. Furthermore, 73% of referral sources reported that the slow turnaround time for neuropsychological reports negatively affected their patient care.6

Attempts to optimize the reporting process have predominantly concentrated on improving the efficiency of manual report generation. However, these approaches are constrained by the limited availability of certified clinical psychologists (CCPs) and the inherent variability in report quality. Moreover, the training and onboarding of new psychologists to meet the growing demand for their services requires a considerable investment of time and resources.7 In order to address these challenges, automated tools for the interpretation and reporting of neuropsychological assessments are emerging as a promising solution.

ReadSmart4U is a web-based automated reporting tool developed for the purpose of generating interpretation statements for neuropsychological assessment results in under five minutes. Following the entry of raw scores from completed neuropsychological assessments and demographic information, the tool automatically generates structured interpretation statements. ReadSmart4U does not conduct additional clinical evaluations; rather, it streamlines the reporting process by analyzing existing assessment data.8 According to the prevailing tradition in the field, CCPs undertake the generation of neuropsychological reports through a multi-step process. This process is initiated by the administration and scoring of various tests. Next, the results are interpreted in the context of the patient's history. Then, a comprehensive narrative is drafted. Finally, the report is finalized after revisions. The duration of this process is approximately two hours, as indicated by a previous survey,6 and it can be further prolonged by complex cases or heavy caseloads.

The objective of this study is to assess the validity of ReadSmart4U by conducting a systematic comparison of the quality of its reports with those produced by CCPs. This evaluation encompasses reports generated for individuals across three diagnostic groups to assess whether ReadSmart4U performs consistently across this range of cognitive functioning: normal cognition (NC), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and Alzheimer’s disease (AD). In particular, this study seeks to ascertain whether the automated reporting provided by ReadSmart4U can match or exceed manual reporting by CCPs in terms of accuracy, usefulness, and overall quality. By addressing these questions, this study aims to elucidate the potential role of automated tools like ReadSmart4U in enhancing diagnostic efficiency and consistency in neuropsychological assessments, without compromising the clinical value and reliability of the reports.

METHODS

Study design

This study employed a retrospective cross-sectional design to evaluate the validity and quality of automated neuropsychological reports generated by ReadSmart4U in comparison to those written by CCPs. The study was conducted at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (SNUBH) using de-identified patient data from its Clinical Data Warehouse (CDW). SNUBH is a tertiary care academic medical center affiliated with Seoul National University College of Medicine, situated in Seongnam, South Korea. The CDW incorporates both structured and unstructured data from a multitude of hospital information systems, encompassing electronic health records, laboratory results, medical imaging reports, medication prescriptions, and clinical notes. All patient identifiers were anonymized using a de-identification process compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act and other relevant local data privacy regulations. Only de-identified data were used in subsequent analyses.

Data source and extraction

A total of 10,981 unique patient records were collected from the CDW between April 1, 2003 and March 31, 2022. The participants were stratified into three diagnostic groups (NC, MCI, and AD). Then 150 participants (50 individuals with NC, 50 individuals with MCI, and 50 individuals with AD) ultimately chosen by stratified random sampling to ensure proportional representation of the three diagnostic groups. Diagnostic classification was determined in accordance with the International Classification of Diseases, the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR), and the results of standardized cognitive tests. Specifically, participants with a CDR score of 0 and no significant cognitive impairment were categorized as NC; those with a CDR score of 0.5 and mild cognitive deficits were categorized as MCI; and those with a CDR score of ≥ 1 and notable cognitive impairments were categorized as AD. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) aged 50 years or older, 2) a confirmed diagnosis of NC, MCI, or AD, and 3) availability of complete neuropsychological assessment results and corresponding interpretation reports.

The neuropsychological assessments comprised raw scores from the Korean version of the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Packet (CERAD-K) Neuropsychological Assessment Battery (CERAD-K-N),910 the Digit Span Test (DST),11 the Frontal Assessment Battery,12 and the Executive Clock Drawing.13 All participants were Koreans and their demographic and clinical characteristics are shown in Table 1.

The sample size was determined through a priori power analysis. A statistical power of 80% (β = 0.20) was targeted in order to reduce the risk of Type II error, with the significance level (α) set at 0.05 and a medium effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.25) assumed. The analysis considered three independent diagnostic groups as a between-subjects factor and two reporting methods (manual and automated) as a within-subjects factor. This configuration is consistent with a two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) design. The power analysis indicated that a minimum of 42 cases per group would be required to achieve the desired power under these conditions. To ensure robust statistical inference and account for potential variability or data exclusions, a total sample size of 150 cases was selected, comprising 50 cases per group.

Further details regarding the registry can be found on the clinical trial registration webpage.14

Generation of ReadSmart4U reports

In order to facilitate the generation of automated reports, the raw scores derived from the 150 neuropsychological assessments (one per participant) were entered into ReadSmart4U, a web-based tool that generates interpretation statements based on the input data. The mean time required to input the data was less than two minutes for each report (0.79 ± 0.02 seconds/report), with a mean completion time of less than five minutes for each report, including review and minor revisions. The system processed the raw data and demographic information in order to produce structured reports (Fig. 1, Supplementary Figs. 1, 2, 3).

Assessment of the report quality

To ensure impartiality, the quality assessments were conducted by three independent, blinded raters who were not affiliated with the ReadSmart4U project or its developers. All raters were experienced clinical psychologists with an average of 12.0 ± 4.4 years of experience.

The quality of the reports was evaluated using two distinct approaches. Firstly, 150 reports generated by ReadSmart4U and 150 reports written by CCPs were randomly intermixed using a true random number generator in SPSS to create a pool of 300 reports. Then the quality of each report was evaluated individually using the Interpretation Statement Quality Assessment Scale (ISQAS, Supplementary Table 1). Secondly, the reports were paired for direct comparison. For each neuropsychological assessment, the ReadSmart4U-generated report and the CCP-written report were presented as a pair. These pairs were evaluated using the Interpretation Statement Quality Comparison Scale (ISQCS, Supplementary Table 2) to determine which report in each pair was superior. The primary variables of interest, as defined by clinical guidelines from the American Psychological Association and the American Academy of Clinical Neuropsychology, included overall quality, terminology accuracy, interpretation accuracy, usefulness, and writing quality. These metrics formed the basis for the evaluation criteria in both the ISQAS and ISQCS tools. The ISQAS and the ISQCS were both developed ex nihilo for the purposes of this study.

In both approaches, the raters were blinded to the source of the reports. In order to guarantee consistency and eliminate any potential bias, all reports were standardized in terms of both sentence format and organizational structure.

The ISQAS and ISQCS are 25-item bespoke tools developed for the specific purpose of evaluating report quality. They are identical in terms of the evaluation items they employ, but differ in their scoring methods. The ISQAS employs a 5-point Likert scale (0 = very dissatisfied, 1 = dissatisfied, 2 = neutral, 3 = satisfied and 4 = very satisfied) to evaluate each of its 25 items, facilitating a comprehensive quantitative assessment of individual reports. In contrast, the ISQCS is designed to facilitate direct comparisons between pairs of reports. For each item, raters were required to select one of three options: “A is superior,” “Equal,” or “B is superior,” based on their evaluation of the two reports in the pair. Both tools comprise 25 items: 9 items for terminology accuracy, 9 items for interpretation accuracy, 3 items for usefulness, and 4 items for writing quality. The ISQAS demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.966, indicating high reliability. The ISQCS, while not scored quantitatively like the ISQAS, enabled robust pairwise comparisons, offering a complementary perspective on relative report quality. Poor overall quality, poor terminology accuracy, poor interpretation accuracy, poor usefulness, and poor writing quality have been defined as ISQAS scores of ≤ 25, ≤ 9, ≤ 9, ≤ 3, and ≤ 4, respectively. These scores indicate an average item score of 1 (dissatisfied) or below (very dissatisfied). For the ISQCS evaluation, an interpretation statement was deemed superior if it had more items rated as superior across the 25 total items or within specific domains, including terminology accuracy, interpretation accuracy, usefulness, writing quality, and overall quality.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared between groups using one-way ANOVA, while categorical variables were compared using χ2 tests. Post-hoc corrections for multiple comparisons were performed using Fisher’s least significant difference procedure.

The internal consistency of the ISQAS was evaluated using the Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient.

To investigate the effects of author (CCPs versus ReadSmart4U), diagnosis (NC, MCI and AD) and their interaction on the ISQAS scores of the interpretation statements, a repeated measures ANOVA was performed using a crowd scoring strategy.15 In this analysis, the ISQAS scores of psychologists and ReadSmart4U were treated as a within-subjects repeated-measures factor, while author and diagnosis were treated as between-subjects factors. In instances where an interaction between author and diagnosis was observed, a paired t-test was employed to compare the crowd ISQAS scores between psychologists and ReadSmart4U. The crowd score for each item was obtained by averaging the item scores provided by the three raters. The frequency of poor ISQAS scores was compared between psychologists and ReadSmart4U using McNemar’s test.

A comparison of the respective superiority of the interpretation statements written by psychologists and those generated by ReadSmart4U was conducted by comparing the frequency of interpretation statements rated as superior within each pair.

For all analyses, a two-tailed P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The required sample size for a repeated measures ANOVA was estimated using G*Power (version 3.1; Kiel University) and all other statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences for Windows (version 20.0; International Business Machines Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethics statement

This clinical trial (Korean Clinical Trials Registry identifier: KCT0008914, https://cris.nih.go.kr/cris/search/detailSearch.do/25440) was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of SNUBH (IRB No. B-2205-758-002) on August 8, 2022. The design and conduct of this study were in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.16 Because this clinical trial was conducted retrospectively and all data were anonymized, participation consent forms from participants or their legal guardians were waived. To ensure data privacy and security, all data were encrypted and protected. The data were not taken outside of the institution, and analysis of the data were conducted on an office server. Access to the server and each data file generated during the research process was secured with a strong password. The study design, data collection and analysis were conducted by the research team independently, and the corresponding author did not influence these processes despite their financial interests in PlanB4U Co., Ltd.

RESULTS

The mean age of the participants was 73.4 ± 7.3 years, with 62.7% of them being female. The AD group exhibited greater age, a higher proportion of females, and lower levels of education compared to the NC and MCI groups (P < 0.001). A comprehensive summary of the demographic and clinical characteristics is presented in Table 1 and detailed neuropsychological performance is summarized in Supplementary Table 3.

As demonstrated in Fig. 2, reports generated by ReadSmart4U scored significantly higher than those written by CCPs across all ISQAS domains. Mean overall quality scores were 87.3 ± 3.4 for ReadSmart4U reports and 74.5 ± 6.7 for CCP reports (P < 0.001). Similarly, ReadSmart4U reports outperformed CCP reports in terminology accuracy (31.4 ± 1.5 vs. 26.8 ± 2.1, P < 0.001), interpretation accuracy (32.2 ± 1.7 vs. 27.3 ± 2.4, P < 0.001), usefulness (10.8 ± 0.9 vs. 9.2 ± 1.2, P < 0.001), and writing quality (14.5 ± 0.6 vs. 12.7 ± 1.1, P < 0.001). Among the interpretation statements written by CCPs, a small proportion had poor ISQAS scores: 2.7% for overall quality, 2.7% for terminology accuracy, 1.3% for interpretation accuracy, 6.7% for usefulness, and 2.0% for writing quality. In contrast, none of the interpretation statements generated by ReadSmart4U exhibited poor scores in any ISQAS domain (P < 0.001 for all comparisons). In addition, reports generated by ReadSmart4U were longer than those written by CCPs (Fig. 2). The results of the repeated measures ANOVA indicated that there were significant main effects of author (ReadSmart4U vs. CCPs) and diagnosis (NC, MCI, AD) on ISQAS scores (P < 0.001 for all domains, Table 2). A significant interaction between author and diagnosis was observed (P < 0.05), with the greatest differences in scores noted in the NC group. Post hoc paired t-tests confirmed that the reports generated by ReadSmart4U outperformed those generated by CCP across all diagnostic groups (P < 0.001, Fig. 3).

DISCUSSION

This study sought to evaluate the validity and quality of neuropsychological assessment reports generated by ReadSmart4U, a web-based automated interpretation tool, in comparison to those written by CCPs. The findings, based on both individual report evaluations (ISQAS) and paired comparisons (ISQCS), consistently demonstrated that reports generated by ReadSmart4U were superior or comparable to those produced by CCPs across multiple quality domains, including terminology accuracy, interpretation accuracy, usefulness, writing quality, and overall quality. This study was designed to address potential bias by employing independent raters and conducting all analyses in a blinded and standardized manner. The results remained consistent across all diagnostic groups, thereby supporting the validity of the findings irrespective of the authors' affiliations with the developing company.

The results indicate the potential of automated tools such as ReadSmart4U to address significant challenges in neuropsychological assessment reporting. A notable outcome was the markedly elevated ISQAS ratings for ReadSmart4U reports across all diagnostic categories. This indicates that the implementation of automated reporting not only minimizes the variability in quality but also enhances the consistency and reliability of interpretation statements. Moreover, the absence of poor ISQAS scores in ReadSmart4U reports, in comparison to a small proportion of poor scores in CCP-generated reports, serves to illustrate the tool’s capacity to maintain a consistently high standard of reporting.

The paired comparison (ISQCS) provided further evidence of the superiority of ReadSmart4U in specific domains, particularly in interpretation accuracy and terminology accuracy, which are of critical importance for clinical decision-making. The superior performance of ReadSmart4U in these domains may be attributable to the inherent consistency of its standardized algorithms. While human experts may exhibit variability in their choice of terminology or interpretive emphasis, ReadSmart4U employs validated templates and data-driven rules that ensure uniformity and reduce errors. These findings suggest that automated tools can alleviate some of the cognitive and time demands placed on CCPs, enabling them to focus on more nuanced aspects of patient care, such as integrating contextual information and providing personalized recommendations.

The findings of this study corroborate and extend prior research on the potential of automated tools to standardize and enhance clinical workflows in neuropsychology. Prior research has identified considerable inconsistency in the quality, length, and content of neuropsychological reports produced by CCPs, which is often attributed to factors such as individual expertise, time constraints, and institutional protocols.1718 ReadSmart4U addresses these limitations by providing a scalable solution that ensures consistency and reduces the likelihood of human error during the reporting process.

It is noteworthy that the superior performance of ReadSmart4U in domains such as terminology accuracy and interpretation accuracy serves to reinforce the notion that automated systems, when properly designed, are capable of handling highly technical and structured tasks in a more effective manner than manual methods. These findings are consistent with those of earlier studies on automated reporting systems in other healthcare domains, such as radiology and pathology, where automated tools have been demonstrated to enhance reporting accuracy while reducing turnaround times.1920 The implications for clinical neuropsychology are considerable. By reducing the time required to generate reports to less than five minutes, ReadSmart4U enables clinicians to manage higher case volumes without compromising report quality. This efficiency is of particular importance in the context of global ageing and the increasing prevalence of cognitive disorders, where the demand for neuropsychological assessments far exceeds the current capacity of trained professionals. Moreover, the tool's capacity to generate consistently high-quality reports may facilitate the reduction of disparities in access to neuropsychological services, particularly in under-resourced or rural settings where specialized expertise is scarce. By integrating automated systems such as ReadSmart4U, such settings can achieve a level of service quality comparable to that of well-resourced institutions, thereby increasing the accessibility of cognitive care.

It is our understanding that ReadSmart4U is the inaugural software to be capable of automatically generating comprehensive interpretation statements for neuropsychological assessment results. Although several computerized tools, including the Cambridge Neuropsychological Automated Test Battery,21 the Vienna Test System,22 Protocol for Online Neuropsychological Testing,15 the RCFT digital pen drawing23 and the CERAD-K app,24 facilitate cognitive assessments on mobile platforms, they are constrained in their ability to provide detailed interpretations alongside raw scoring results. This limitation frequently necessitates additional manual effort from clinicians, which may result in delays in decision-making. ReadSmart4U addresses this gap by offering fully automated and clinically interpretable reports, thereby establishing itself as a pioneering tool in the field of neuropsychology.

The performance of ReadSmart4U was found to be superior to that of CCPs, irrespective of the diagnostic category. However, a notable Author × Diagnosis interaction was identified through post hoc analyses. These analyses indicated that the discrepancy in report quality between ReadSmart4U and CCPs was most evident in the NC group. As demonstrated in Fig. 3, the report quality of ReadSmart4U remained consistently high across all diagnostic groups, while the report quality of CCPs was comparatively lower for participants classified as NC compared to those with MCI or AD. It is hypothesized that in clinical practice, CCPs may allocate less time and attention to reports for individuals with NC, given that these individuals typically do not require the same level of treatment planning or follow-up assessments as those with MCI or dementia. The observed discrepancy in report quality may be attributable to the tendency of CCPs toward simplified reporting in the NC group.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, while the ISQAS and ISQCS provided robust measures of report quality, it is important to acknowledge that these tools were specifically developed for this study and have not yet undergone external validation. It would be beneficial for future research to prioritize validating these tools across diverse clinical contexts in order to enhance their generalizability. Additionally, planned follow-up studies aim to replicate and validate these findings in independent, multi-institutional settings, which will further minimize potential biases. Secondly, the dataset was derived from a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. To establish the broader applicability of ReadSmart4U, studies involving diverse clinical settings and populations are necessary. Thirdly, the scope of this study was restricted to cognitive conditions such as AD and MCI. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the tool's utility, it would be beneficial to evaluate its performance in other cognitive disorders, such as vascular dementia or dementia with Lewy bodies. Fourthly, it is not possible to eliminate the possibility of subjective bias among the raters, which could result in an over- or underestimation of the quality of the reports. Despite the implementation of a crowd-scoring strategy, which entailed averaging the item scores of three raters to ensure a more balanced and reliable assessment, and the inclusion of experienced, CCPs as raters, the influence of rater subjectivity may not have been fully controlled due to the limited number of raters. It would be beneficial for future studies to consider including a larger and more diverse pool of raters from various medical institutions to enhance the robustness of the evaluations. Finally, given the absence of documentation by the CDW pertaining to the years of experience of the CCPs or the involvement of trainees, the potential impact of these factors on the quality of the report could not be assessed.

CONCLUSION

ReadSmart4U has been demonstrated to exhibit consistent superiority over manual reporting across a multitude of quality domains, while concomitantly markedly reducing the time and effort required for report generation. However, it is imperative to acknowledge that the primary intent of ReadSmart4U is not to supplant the clinical judgment of psychologists. This is due to its inability to account for a number of contextual factors, including the subject's demeanor, the level of cooperation, and the environment in which the neuropsychological test is administered.

In conclusion, ReadSmart4U is a valuable complementary tool that enhances the efficiency, consistency, and scalability of neuropsychological reporting. Its integration into clinical practice holds great potential to improve access to high-quality cognitive care while allowing psychologists to focus on more nuanced aspects of patient care. Further research should explore the broader applicability of this tool across diverse clinical settings and populations, as well as its potential integration with other technologies to further support clinical decision-making.
