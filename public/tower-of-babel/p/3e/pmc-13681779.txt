Neuroepigenetic programming by the maternal environment: part A—epigenome-wide DNA methylation profile in the offspring’s olfactory bulb following preconceptional weight loss in mice
Authors: Karine Badonnel, Aurélie Chaulot-Talmon, Valentin Costes, Anne Frambourg, Gabriel Costa-Monteiro-Moreira, Mélanie Jouin, Lorraine Bourgeois-Brunel, Anne Gabory, Hélène Kiefer, Hélène Jammes, Christine Baly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExperimental models and epidemiological data suggest that the maternal nutritional environment during gestation plays important roles in brain development with consequences on cognitive health. Epigenetic marks, such as methylation and histone modifications, are likely to be involved in this programming, but little addressed the impact of maternal metabolism at the preconceptional period. In mice, we recently demonstrated lasting effects of a maternal preconceptional weight loss on metabolic outcomes and behaviors related to food intake in the male offspring, exhibiting a reduction in olfactory sensitivity, along with a lack of fasting-induced olfactory-based motivation. To evaluate if this effect was driven by epigenetic modifications in the olfactory bulb, we profiled brain DNA methylation, a fundamental process in mediating the relation between early life environment and long-term behaviors in mice according to the maternal status.ResultsUsing oxidative reduced representation bisulfite sequencing to specifically target 5mC in mice exposed to preconceptional weight loss versus controls, we identified 11,055 differentially methylated cytosines and 150 differential methylated regions (DMRs) located in 111 genes in the olfactory bulb, a main region involved in odor processing and integration. Among the cytosines differentially methylated, half were hypermethylated. Methylation remodeling according to the maternal group preferentially affected noncoding intragenic regions, distal regulatory elements, and open sea CpGs, while promoter CpG islands remain relatively resistant to modification in DNA methylome. The functional annotation of DMRs pointed out genes related to histones kinases, energy signaling, synaptic function, and cellular differentiation, suggesting that targeted methylation changes may simultaneously influence epigenetic regulation, metabolism, and neurobiology.ConclusionThanks to a single-base analysis of differential methylated CpGs, we present novel evidence that the maternal dietary environment prior to conception exerts maternally-driven epigenetic effects, markedly altering 5-methylcytosine patterns in the adult offspring brain. This long-lasting remodeling of the offspring’s olfactory bulb methylome may underlie the described alterations in olfactory-driven behaviors of offspring born to WL mother.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13148-026-02167-0.Keywords: Brain, Epigenetics, Methylation, DOHaD, Weight loss, Olfaction, oxRRBS, Preconception diet

Introduction

Over the past decades, the DOHaD (Developmental Origins of Health and Diseases) concept, which states that environmental factors during prenatal and early postnatal development can significantly impact long-term health, has explored the broader factors of early life experiences and their targets. Increasing evidence shows that the maternal dietary environment has a major influence on the developmental programming of non-communicable diseases in the offspring. Inadequate maternal nutrition affects placental development and function, fetal growth, and long-term physiology and metabolism, thereby predisposing the progeny to metabolic, cardiovascular, autoimmune, or endocrine diseases, as well as altered cognitive function and mental health [1–3]. Epidemiological studies linking maternal exposure to obesogenic diets with increased risk of neurobehavioral disorders are strongly supported by preclinical models of maternal obesity. These models have demonstrated impairments in offspring cognitive functions related to learning and memory [4], reward response [5, 6], anxiety [7, 8], and olfactory-mediated behaviors [9, 10]. Overall, maternal dietary imbalance can induce profound, sex-specific, and long-lasting effects on neurodevelopment of offspring [8, 10, 11].

These findings strongly support the importance of adopting healthy dietary patterns before and during pregnancy. Beneficial effects of preconception interventions on pregnancy outcomes and offspring development are increasingly recognized, yet poorly studied compared to gestational interventions [12]. There is also limited research on how maternal undernutrition affects brain metabolism and associated functions in the offspring, particularly during the preconceptional period. A prenatal famine rat model affects key pathways related to energy metabolism and glutamate metabolism in the prefrontal cortex, whereas a moderate diet restriction during the entire pregnancy alters the endocannabinoids in the hypothalamus, the hippocampus and the olfactory bulb [13–15]. In a mouse model, we demonstrated that a maternal preconceptional weight loss (WL) applied on obese mice normalized metabolic phenotypes in male offspring, but unexpectedly, impairs olfactory sensitivity measured by electroolfactogram and alters fasting-induced motivation for food reward in males [16]. However, metabolomic analyses of olfactory bulb and hypothalamus failed to reveal any molecular correlates supporting these behavioral phenotypes [17]. While all these data highlight the maternal preconceptional nutritional status as a determinant of long-term cognitive function, molecular mechanisms associated with lasting effects remain to be solved.

Epigenetic regulation has emerged as a prime candidate mechanism mediating such long-term effects of maternal environment on offspring brain function [1, 18, 19]. Among epigenetic modifications, DNA methylation on 5-methylcytosine (5mC) is one of the most extensively studied. It is crucial for neuronal development, learning, memory, and synaptic plasticity [20]. DNA methylation acts as a relatively stable repressive mark, shaping neuronal gene expression programs and modulating brain physiology in response to environmental influences, including maternal diet [12, 21]. For instance, maternal imbalanced nutrition alters methylation and expression of dopamine and opioid-related genes in reward-associated brain regions [22], and maternal overnutrition induces hypermethylation of pro-opiomelanocortin regulatory elements in the hypothalamus [23]. Thus, persistent remodeling of the offspring brain methylome appears to be a hallmark of developmental programming by maternal metabolic status. However, most studies are based on conventional reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS), which does not distinguish between 5mC and 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), thereby limiting the accuracy of methylation profiling. Oxidative RRBS (oxRRBS), by selectively converting 5hmC and leaving 5mC unmodified, enables a precise quantification of true 5mC levels across the genome [24]. This methodological advance allows exploration of methylation landscapes at base-pair resolution, and could be easily combined with RRBS. Indeed, the combined use of RRBS and oxRRBS enables the inference of 5mC and 5hmC levels, but only at CpG sites with sufficient coverage in both libraries [25].

In the present study, we applied oxRRBS to investigate genome-wide DNA methylation changes in the olfactory bulb (OB) of male offspring exposed to maternal preconceptional weight loss. The role of DNA methylation modulation in the OB, a key brain structure for odor integration, highly sensitive to metabolic status [26, 27], is poorly understood. Besides, the OB is modulated by the maternal diet at various levels. It has been shown to be transcriptionally affected by a maternal high-fat diet (HFD) and to display lipidomic changes following a moderate maternal diet restriction [10, 15]. Cell dynamics alterations associated with aggravated spatial learning and memory capability are observed under maternal HFD in the neurogenic region of the OB [8]. Importantly, DNA methylation has been reported to be dynamically modulated in the OB under various physiological and pathological conditions [28, 29]. We hypothesize that maternal preconceptional nutrition induces long-lasting remodeling of the offspring’s bulbar methylome, potentially underlying the observed alterations in olfactory-driven behaviors.

Materials and methods

OB sampling and genomic DNA extraction

The animal model used in this study received ethical approval (visa 12/062) from the COMETHEA ethical committee (COMité d’ETHique pour l’Expérimentation Animale), registered with the Comité National de Réflexion Ethique sur l’Expérimentation Animale under number 45, in accordance with European Union legislation (Directive 2010/63/EU, 22 September 2010) and the National Charter on the Ethics of Animal Experimentation. This model has been described in detail elsewhere [16, 30]. Briefly, control dams (CTRL), fed a control diet throughout the experimental period, or weight-loss dams (WL), initially fed a high-fat diet for two months and switched to a control diet two months prior to conception, gave birth to offspring that were fed a control diet from weaning onwards. The metabolic and behavioral trajectories of male and female offspring were monitored up to the age of 6 months, at which point their olfactory bulbs (OB) were collected for molecular analyses. Genomic DNA from CTRL (n = 4) and WL (n = 3) OB samples from males, as they exhibited a modification in their olfactory sensitivity, were isolated using the Qiagen Micro Kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Each sample was eluted in 50 μl of buffer using the spin columns provided in the kit.

oxRRBS library production

The oxRRBS libraries were prepared according to the protocol described in [31], with the additional oxidation step. Briefly, 200 ng of genomic DNA from WL and CTRL OB samples were digested with MspI (Thermo Scientific), end-repaired, and ligated to 55 bp Illumina adapters designed for paired-end sequencing. Size selection was performed with SPRISelect (Beckman Coulter) to isolate fragments ranging from 150 to 400 bp (corresponding to genomic fragments of 40–290 bp plus adapters).

DNA underwent additional purification using Bio-Rad Micro Bio-Spin P-6 SSC columns, followed by denaturation with 1 M NaOH and oxidation using an oxidant solution (15 mM KRuO₄ in 0.05 M NaOH). The DNA was then re-purified using the same Bio-Rad columns as above.

Subsequently, samples were bisulfite-converted twice consecutively using the EpiTect Bisulfite Kit (Qiagen), in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol for DNA extracted from FFPE tissues. The bisulfite-converted DNA was then amplified with Pfu Turbo Cx Hotstart DNA Polymerase (Agilent) in 16 PCR cycles. Libraries were purified with Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter) and sequenced on NovaSeqXplus to produce 100 bp paired-end reads (Integragen SA, France).

Bioinformatic analyses

OxRRBS-Seq were analyzed using an integrated pipeline that combined custom in-house scripts written in Python, R, and Shell, along with external tools [32]. The sequences displayed the expected nucleotide composition based on MspI digestion and bisulfite conversion, as confirmed by FastQC quality control (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Quality checks and trimming were performed using TrimGalore v0.4.5 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trimgalore/), which removed adapter sequences, low-quality bases (Phred score below 20), and reads shorter than 20 nucleotides.

The bisulfite conversion rate, estimated from unmethylated cytosines added in vitro during the end-repair step, averaged 100% across samples (Fig. 1A), with a minimum conversion rate of 99.9%. High-quality reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome GRCm39 assembly using Bismark v0.24.0 HISAT2/2.2.1 [33]. Only CpG sites covered by at least 10 uniquely mapped reads (CpGs10) were retained for further analysis. For each CpG10, a methylation percentage was calculated per sample using the formula: (number of reads with "C" × 100) / (number of reads with "C" + number of reads with "T"), as determined by Bismark methylation calling. These methylation percentages were visualized using the Integrative Genome Viewer (IGV genome browser [34].

An analysis of oxRRBS-Seq data was conducted to identify cytosine positions uniquely methylated and to assess differential methylation between WL and CTRL samples. Mean methylation percentages were calculated separately for each group (n = 3 for WL, n = 4 for CTRL). Analyses were restricted to CpGs10 positions with valid values in at least 3 WL and 3 CTRL samples, a minimum read coverage of 10 present in at least three samples per maternal group (WL or CTRL). This subset of CpGs10, served as the background dataset. Differentially methylated cytosines (DMCs) were identified using methylKit v1.0.0 [35]. A CpG10 was classified as a DMC if the SLIM-adjusted q-value was below 0.01 and the average group difference reached at least 20%. Thanks to the high sequencing depth and coverage, the tightly controlled experimental conditions and the additional safeguards such as methyl diff > 20% and the use of congenic mice minimizing genetic diversity, we are confident of the robustness and reproducibility of our findings.

Differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were defined as regions containing at least three DMCs, with an inter-distance of 100 bp or less between each DMC. Missing values were present in both DMC and DMR datasets due to samples that did not meet coverage thresholds. Annotation of background CpGs10, DMCs, and DMRs was performed as described in [36], relative to gene features, CpG density, and repetitive elements, using an in-house pipeline. Reference files were downloaded from Ensembl (ftp://ftp.ensembl.org/pub; release 104). The following criteria were applied: transcription start site (TSS; − 100 to + 100 bp relative to the TSS), Promoter (− 2000 to − 100 bp relative to the TSS), transcription termination site (TTS; − 100 to + 100 bp relative to the TTS), shore (up to 2000 bp from a CGI) and shelf (up to 2000 bp from a shore). A site or region was considered part of a CGI (or respective shore or shelf) if it overlapped by at least 75%. Repetitive element overlap was annotated irrespective of the extent of overlap. Genomic coordinates of regulatory elements were obtained from the ENCODE Candidate Cis-Regulatory Elements (cCREs) for the mouse genome (GRCm39), accessible via the UCSC Table Browser. This dataset comprises elements identified through integration of DNase hypersensitivity, histone modification (H3K4me3, H3K27ac), and CTCF-binding data. Each element is classified into one of five categories based on its regulatory signature: enhD for distal enhancer-like signatures, enhP for proximal enhancer-like signatures, prom for promoter–like signatures, CTCF for CTCF-only sites, and K4m3 for DNase–H3K4me3–associated regions. Genes containing DMCs in intragenic regions and/or in upstream (up to − 10 kb from the TSS) or downstream (up to + 10 kb from the TTS) regions were subjected to enrichment analysis using DAVID with default settings [37]. This window was used to evaluate the differential of methylation affecting both gene body and proximal regulatory regions potentially influencing expression of genes, even if a functional association remains to be demonstrated. The 4,031 genes covered by OxRRBS (i.e., genes containing at least one background CpG10) were used as the reference. Functional clusters with a false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

DNA methylation profiling in the olfactory bulb of mice born to maternal weight loss and control group

Sequencing parameters

The metrics that describe the distribution and representation of the genomic CpGs sites analysed and compared between the WL and CTRL groups are summarized Fig. 1.

On average, oxRRBS sequencing generated 68 million read pairs per sample, with a mean Phred quality score of 39, ensuring that more than 94% of the bases sequenced were of high quality (Fig. 1A). The bisulfite conversion rate was also near 100%. Reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome with a unique mapping efficiency of 67.6%. On average, the uniquely mapped reads aligned to 1.9 million CpG sites, with a mean coverage of 149.1. Of these, 1.3 million CpGs with at least 10 reads (CpGs10) were retained for downstream analyses. The CpG were fairly distributed across all chromosomes, although the Y chromosome contains relatively few positions compared to the autosomes and to the X chromosome (Fig. 1B).

Identification of differentially methylated CpGs (DMCs) and regions (DMRs) linked to maternal weight loss

The genome-wide differential DNA methylation analysis between WL and CTRL groups was done on a dataset of 1,688,438 CpGs, each covered in at least three samples per group, using MethylKit. This stringent approach allowed us to identify 11,055 differentially methylated cytosines (DMCs), distributed across nearly all chromosomes (Fig. 1C) and located within or proximal to 4,031 genes. Among them, 5,738 DMCs (51.9%) were hypermethylated in the WL group, while 5,317 were hypomethylated. Both types of changes were evenly distributed across the genome without preferential chromosomal enrichment (Fig. 1D). As illustrated by the Manhattan plot (Fig. 1E), most DMCs were dispersed across the genome, although a few loci formed clear hotspots, including 14 DMCs at Rn18s on chromosome 17 or 12 DMCs at various anonymous Gm (gene model) positions (10) and at UTY (2) on chromosome Y. These findings are noteworthy, because Rn18S belongs to ribosomal DNA (rDNA), which has been described as an epigenetic sensor of environmental conditions such as nutrition, stress, or aging. In addition, UTY encodes a histone demethylase, directly involved in chromatin regulation and epigenetic remodeling, highlighting that methylation changes at this locus could intersect with core mechanisms of transcriptional control. Additional loci such as Spi1, Sec24, Nfix, D9wsa49, Cdc34b, Tmem267 contained fewer DMCs, yet stood out from the background due to their high statistical significance. These observations indicate that, while maternal WL induces widespread epigenetic remodeling across the offspring methylome, few loci undergo prominent changes.

The amplitude of methylation differences was substantial, ranging from –73.5% to + 69.5% (Fig. 1F, volcano plot), underscoring that both gain and loss of DNA methylation occurred with biologically meaningful magnitudes. Finally, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) based on DMCs revealed a clear segregation between WL and CTRL olfactory bulb samples along the first component (Dim1, 56.2% of explained variance; Fig. 1G), highlighting that maternal WL imprinted a robust and distinct epigenetic signature in the offspring OB.

CpGs and DMCs distribution indicate that noncoding intragenic regions, distal regulatory elements, and open sea CpG are preferentially targeted

Among the 11,055 DMCs identified between CTRL and WL samples, 1278 were located in regulatory regions (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1), such as promoters (n = 141, 11%), distal (enhD; n = 652, 51%) or proximal enhancers (enhP; n = 343, 27%), CTCF (n = 82, 6%) or H3K4me3 (n = 60, 5%). Notably, the majority of identified DMCs were located within gene bodies, whereas only ~ 18% were found exclusively in flanking regions (± 10 kb) without additional intragenic DMCs in the same gene.

When comparing the distribution of the 11,055 DMCs with the 1,688,438 total CpG provided by the oxRRBS library to analyze the relative distribution of DMCs to the overall CpG background, we observed several notable deviations in CpGs representation across genomic and regulatory contexts (Fig. 2A). At the gene-annotation level, intergenic regions contained a modest fraction of total CpGs (8.2%) but showed a higher proportion in DMCs (14%) (Fig. 2B). Similarly, introns accounted for 21.5% of CpGs but harbored 38% of DMCs, pointing to a strong methylation remodeling in noncoding intragenic regions. Exonic regions displayed nearly comparable distributions (19.5% vs. 18.9%), while untranslated regions showed subtle features: 5′UTRs were substantially lowered in DMCs (19.6% vs. 6.6%), and 3′UTRs were twofold increased (1.4% vs. 2.9%). In contrast, promoters were depleted in DMCs (4.8%) compared to their CpG content (20.3%). TTS and flanking regions (gene upstream/downstream regions) also exhibited an overrepresentation of DMCs compared with CpG coverage (3.81% to 4.8% for TTS and 5.56% to 9.93% for flanking regions). In addition, 19.4% of DMCs mapped to repetitive regions, matching the proportion of CpGs located in repetitive elements (Fig. 2C). However, specific repeat classes displayed striking modulations: long terminal repeats (LTRs) and SINE elements showed a more than twofold increase in DMCs relative to their CpG representation (3.5% vs. 5.7% and 2% vs. 5.6%, respectively), tandem repeat were almost five-fold decreased (4.83% vs 0.87%), while LINEs were not affected (3.6% vs. 3.8%). CpG density categories further highlighted selective targeting: the vast majority of DMCs were located in open sea regions (82.5%), considerably exceeding their CpG baseline (39.1%) (Fig. 2D). Conversely, CpG islands, which contained more than half of all CpGs (52.7%), contributed to only 6.2% of DMCs. CGI shores and shelves showed moderate enrichments. Finally, within regulatory annotations, DMCs were slightly overrepresented at CTCF binding sites and H3K4me3-marked regions, while enhancers were proportionally affected (46% vs. 51% for distal enhancers; 28% vs. 27% for proximal enhancers). Promoter-associated regulatory regions, however, displayed a lower fraction of DMCs (11%) than expected from CpG content (19%). Altogether, these findings indicate that methylation remodeling according to the maternal WL preferentially affects noncoding intragenic regions, distal regulatory elements, and open sea CpGs, while a relative stability of promoter methylation is observed under the studied conditions.

Functional enrichment analysis of DMCs revealed enriched pathways orchestrating histone modifications, neuronal connectivity, and metabolic regulation

We performed a functionally grouped network of enriched categories (KEGG, GO and Reactome) on genes exhibiting differentially methylated cytosines (DMC) in intragenic or nearby regulatory regions to identify biological pathways potentially affected by DNA methylation changes (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 2 for an exhaustive list).

Functional enrichment analysis of DMC located within or proximal to genes notably revealed a striking overrepresentation of terms related to protein kinase activity (GO:0004672), particularly histone-specific kinase activities (GO:0035403, GO:0140823, GO:0044024), including modifications of H2A, H2B, H3, and H4, indicating that DMCs preferentially occur in genomic regions potentially involved in post-translational histone modifications. Broader kinase activities—such as AMP-activated protein kinase, DNA-dependent protein kinase, ribosomal protein S6 kinase, and 3-phosphoinositide-dependent protein kinase—were also enriched, suggesting potential impacts on chromatin remodeling, but also transcriptional regulation, and cellular stress-response pathways. Together, these results point to a model in which differential DNA methylation may fine-tune kinase-mediated histone modifications, thereby influencing chromatin accessibility and downstream gene expression.

DMC were also mapping within or close to several gene loci enriched for terms related to neuronal signaling (mmu04912, mmu04723…) and synaptic function (GO:0050804, GO:0014069…), including signal transduction, synapse, glutamatergic and excitatory synapses, postsynaptic density, dendritic spines, and neuron projections. Notably, neurexins and neuroligins (R-MMU-6794361), which mediate pre- and postsynaptic adhesion, were closely linked to the central synapse node, highlighting a WL-supported modulation of synapse formation, maturation, and plasticity. Additional enriched terms involved RAC/CDC42/RHO GTPase cycles, small GTPase signaling, zinc ion binding, and transcriptional regulation, reflecting a possible impact on the coordinated control of synaptic signaling, cytoskeletal remodeling, and gene expression essential for learning, neuronal connectivity, and cell differentiation.

Genes harboring DMC also showed significant enrichment in metabolic and endocrine pathways, emphasizing potential epigenetic regulation of hormone signaling and energy homeostasis. Key pathways included insulin, glucocorticoid, gonadotropin-releasing hormone, prolactin, estrogen, oxytocin, and growth hormone signaling, as well as cAMP, cGMP-PKG, Rap1, and lipid metabolism pathways such as lipolysis in adipocytes. These findings suggest that DNA methylation changes related to maternal WL may fine-tune gene networks controlling metabolism, hormone secretion, and systemic physiological processes, potentially impacting glucose homeostasis, energy balance, and endocrine signaling.

Finally, several DMCs were located within or near genes involved in pathways enriched for cytoskeleton organization, cell–cell adhesion, and neuronal development. Key processes included actin cytoskeleton organisation (GO:0030036), lamellipodia (GO:0030027) and filopodia (GO:0030175) dynamics, adherens and tight junction formation, and neural crest cell migration (GO:0001755). Terms related to neuron projection development (GO:0031175) and axon guidance (GO:0007411) indicate that epigenetic modifications may influence nervous system morphogenesis through coordinated regulation of cytoskeletal structure, intercellular adhesion, and cell migration.

DMRs are located within or proximal to genes related to metabolism, neuronal development and epigenetic regulation

The 150 DMRs identified between CTRL and WL OB samples are distributed across the genome (Supplementary Table 3), with 111 found associated with a gene or in a genomic locus proximal to the gene (Fig. 4 for illustrating examples). Of these, 64% (n = 71) exhibited a gain of methylation in the WL group. Thirteen DMR were located in important regulatory regions, such as promoters (n = 1, hypermethylated), enhancer distal (enhD; n = 5, two hypomethylated and 3 hypermethylated) or proximal (enhP, n = 5, all hypermethylated), CTCF (n = 1, hypermethylated) or H3K4me3 (n = 1, hypomethylated) (Table 1).

The 111 DMRs were located within or proximal to genes involved in metabolic signaling, neuronal development, and epigenetic regulation. Some illustrating DMRs located in genes associated with the main network enrichment described in Fig. 3 are shown in Fig. 4.

Among DMR located within or close to genes involved in metabolism and insulin signaling, the upstream transcription factor 1 (Usf1) gene, which harbors an hypermethylated DMR within an EnhD (Table 1) encodes a crucial and multifunctional transcription factor that plays significant roles in transcriptional regulation of multiple genes involved in glucose and lipid metabolism [38]. Irs1 is also a key component of the insulin signaling pathway, while Bhmt is a methyltransferase implicated in insulin resistance and adipocyte differentiation. Ksr2 plays a central role in energy homeostasis by regulating glucose metabolism, fatty acid β-oxidation, and energy expenditure. Tcf25 and Galnt2 also contribute to the regulation of cellular energy and lipid metabolism, affecting insulin sensitivity and response to nutrient availability. The Mgll gene encodes a key enzyme in lipid metabolism, particularly in presynaptic axon terminals and glial cells, where endocannabinoids, known regulators of energy intake, are produced and degraded. Rora further complemented this network by regulating neuronal energy metabolism, synaptic plasticity, and neuronal survival through fatty acid oxidation and modulation of glycolytic and lipid metabolic enzymes. Finally, Fto, also expressed in the hypothalamus, modulates appetite, satiety, and lipid metabolism by removing m6A marks from mRNAs, whereas Rbm15B directs the methylation machinery to specific transcripts to deposit these marks, together illustrating a complementary system of dynamic epigenetic regulation.

Regarding epigenetic regulation, several DMRs were identified within or in the vicinity of genes with direct roles in histone regulation and chromatin remodeling (Fig. 4). Cbx4, a Polycomb group protein and component of Prc1, contributes to transcriptional repression through histone H2A ubiquitination and recognition of methylated histones. Arid1B, a subunit of the SWI/SNF complex, is essential for ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling, thereby modulating histone–DNA accessibility and transcriptional regulation. Rad21l, a meiosis-specific cohesin subunit, participates in chromatin organization and histone–nucleosome interactions critical for proper chromosome pairing. Arid3A, another member of the ARID family, is implicated in chromatin binding and transcriptional regulation through interactions with histone-rich genomic regions. Jade1, a component of histone acetyltransferase complexes, directly modifies histones via acetylation, facilitating transcriptional activation. Finally, Ncor2 acts as a nuclear corepressor by recruiting histone deacetylases (HDACs) to chromatin, establishing a transcriptionally repressive environment. Together, the genes exhibiting a DMR highlight multiple layers of histone regulation in the dataset, spanning histone modification, nucleosome remodeling, chromatin organization, and transcriptional repression or activation.

Additional DMRs were located in genes critical for neuronal development and synaptic plasticity. Pik3cd which contains an hypermethylated DMR with 6 DMC located in an EnhP contributes to axonal growth and dendritic branching [39, 40] (Table 1). In addition, Sema6B, DmrtA2, CamtA1, Pxn, and Mical3 are involved in neuronal differentiation, synapse formation, neuronal migration, and actin regulation, respectively, all of which are essential for synaptic plasticity. Necab2 modulates the function of GABAergic interneurons and participates in mitochondrial homeostasis and oxidative stress response. Cacna2d2 regulates calcium entry and neurotransmitter release, influencing synaptic transmission and neuronal excitability.

Several other DMRs were found in genes involved in cell cycle regulation, epigenetics, and mitochondrial metabolism, including Cdkn1c, a cell cycle inhibitor and regulator of embryonic growth containing 2 DMRs of 5 and 10 DMCs (Fig. 4), Mkrn3, a classic example of parent-of-origin epigenetic control, and Slc25a26/Mrm2, essential for mitochondrial methylation and energy production. Interestingly, DMRs were also identified in 6 zinc finger genes (Zfand3, Zfp444, Zfhx2, Zfhx3, Zfp469, and Zfp423), as well as in 2 genes coding associated regulatory proteins (Zfta and Zc3h12b) (Fig. 4). These transcriptional regulators have distinct but complementary roles: Zfhx3 (8 DMCs) is involved in neuronal differentiation and circadian rhythm regulation, Zfhx2 plays a role in neurodevelopment and may influence gene expression patterns in the central nervous system, Zfp423 (8 DMCs) contributes to adipocyte differentiation and progenitor cell fate, while Zfand3 improves glucose tolerance in liver and Zfp444 is implicated in broader transcriptional control and stress-response pathways. Zfp469 (6 DMCs) is linked to structural development and tissue integrity, and associated proteins like Zfta and Zc3h12b modulate transcriptional activity or RNA stability. The presence of DMRs in these loci suggests that epigenetic modulation may fine-tune zinc fingers–mediated gene networks, influencing downstream pathways in neuronal development, metabolic regulation, and cellular stress responses.

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to characterize epigenetic modifications in the offspring’s methylome associated with olfactory deficits in male progeny exposed to maternal preconceptional weight loss (WL). Our data demonstrate for the first time that the maternal dietary environment prior to conception exerts profound epigenetic modification of maternal origin, leading to significant alterations in 5-methylcytosine (5mC) patterns in the adult offspring brain. 11,055 differentially methylated cytosines (DMCs) contained within 150 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) are identified in the olfactory bulb (OB) by oxidative reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (oxRRBS). These DMRs overlap with more than one hundred genes predominantly involved in histones and protein phosphorylation, neuronal plasticity, and metabolism-related processes. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that maternal preconceptional WL deeply reshapes the methylation landscape in the offspring brain, generating selective epigenetic features that may underlie the previously observed reduction in olfactory sensitivity and impaired fasting-induced olfactory-driven motivation in male progeny [16] Since developmental effects of in utero environments on brain DNA methylation are largely sex-dependent [41, 42], it would be interesting to extend our investigation to the females. Additionally, examining earlier stages or other brain regions and/or peripheral tissues related to the metabolism could clarify both the specificity and the origin of the observed epigenetic effects. However, if we cannot formally distinguish between (i) stable epigenetic programming established early in development and maintained over time, and (ii) indirect effects driven by potential changes in cellular composition of the OB, we favor the hypothesis of an early-life epigenetic programming. Indeed, a previous study conducted in the same animal model on placenta and liver fetal tissues demonstrated that the WL preconceptional environment significantly altered the expression of genes involved in the epigenetic machinery and the metabolism [16]. Future studies combining longitudinal sampling and cell-type-resolved epigenomic approaches would help to resolve the supporting mechanisms.

Our data fits with the growing literature on the DOHaD concept considering neuroepigenetic dysregulation as a putative molecular mechanism supporting long-lasting behavioral and metabolic effects, but it adds a new dimension to previous research by incorporating broader and more integrated datasets. Indeed, the memory of early gestational nutritional events has been already associated with epigenetic modification at adulthood in the brain, but most studies have been focused on a targeted genomic region or on a unique regulatory pathway (as reviewed in [20]. For instance, gestational obesity is associated with hypermethylation of the hypothalamic insulin receptor promoter region [43], or of Kiss1 promoter in the offspring [44]. Moreover, the preconceptional period, which is increasingly recognized as a crucial window during which maternal nutritional status may influence the outcome of pregnancy and the health of the offspring, has been still poorly explored and also has been focused on few genes in a context of obesity (see [45]. Recently, Allan and coll. have shown that pre-conceptional maternal obesity can lead to lasting epigenetic changes in the offspring’s brain affecting an isoform regulation of the Homer1, a gene encoding a synaptic scaffold protein that regulates glutamatergic synapses and spine morphogenesis [46]. Therefore, to the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to show the major impact of a maternal preconceptional WL on the methylation status in the OB using high throughput methodologies. From the literature and from our genomic data, it is still unclear if these epigenetic modifications are directly related to metabolic alterations linked to the obese status of mice prior to the feeding restriction, to the feeding restriction itself or to indirect physiological and potentially psychosocial stress related to food transition affecting maternal hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, which in turn may affect the offsprings’ phenotype [47, 48]. To date, few studies have investigated these potential interactions and future studies might focus on this.

Such a fine analysis of DNA methylation at base resolution in the OB adds to the knowledge of the epigenetic methylation-related marks regulating this important region of the brain [28, 49, 50]. Despite its contribution, this study has inherent limitations that should be considered. First, the sample size used here could be considered as relatively small compared with other molecular studies. However, it is consistent with similar epigenetic studies [51, 52] and sufficient for robust detection of DNA methylation differences, given the high sequencing depth (~ 70 million read pairs/sample), coverage (> 10 ×), and the tightly controlled experimental conditions. We also applied a stringent effect size threshold (> 20%) and used congenic mice to minimize inter-individual variability. These measures support the reliability and reproducibility of our findings, even if we nevertheless acknowledge that the limited sample size could increase the risk of false positives at the single CpG level. Moreover, given the resolution of our assay, as oxRRBS quantitatively maps 5mC in a fraction of the genome enriched for CpGs islands, our study has evaluated methylcytosine (5mC) contribution in CpGs, but not in non-CpG positions in CHH and CHG contexts, which also participate to epigenetic regulation in the OB [28]. In addition, DNA hydroxymethylation, a significant epigenetic modification that interacts with and modulates methylation dynamics in response to environmental factors, was not considered here. Analyzing the respective contribution of each methylation mark on a subset of CpGs positions shared by both libraries clearly demonstrates that both epigenetic marks have complementary roles in the regulation of OB methylation according to the maternal environment [25]. Studying the balance between methylation and hydroxymethylation effects would require more comprehensive analyses within specific cell types that constitute the OB and across the whole genome. The data provide an interesting illustration of the change in the epigenome landscape worthy of further investigation.

Curiously, the methylation remodeling according to the maternal WL preferentially affects noncoding intragenic regions, distal regulatory elements, and open sea CpGs illustrating a targeted rather than a stochastic impact toward regulatory and non-coding regions. The relative depletion of DMCs in CpG islands and promoter regions suggests that core transcriptional programs remain protected from environmentally driven perturbations, in line with their essential role in maintaining cellular identity [53, 54]. Instead, DMCs were preferentially enriched in intergenic and intronic regions, as well as open sea CpGs, which are typically associated with distal regulatory elements. Thus, methylation changes may exert their programming effects by fine-tuning gene regulation through enhancer activity and three-dimensional chromatin organization rather than by directly altering promoter-driven transcription [55]; Roadmap Epigenomics Consortium, 2015). The enrichment of DMCs at enhancer regions and CTCF-binding sites reinforces this interpretation, highlighting genome topology and long-range interactions as sensitive targets of epigenetic remodeling [56]. In the brain, epigenetically-controlled enhancers enable neuronal differentiation, experience-dependent gene transcription, and neuroplasticity [57, 58]. By physically contacting promoters through the formation of chromatin loops, distal enhancers can also modulate neurogenesis [59]. This may be particularly relevant in the OB, where neurogenesis contributes to neuronal plasticity. Furthermore, the overrepresentation of DMCs in SINE and LTR elements, contrasted with the relative stability of LINEs and satellite repeats, suggests that the regulation of transposable elements could play a role in reshaping chromatin accessibility and local gene expression landscapes, as shown in other biological situations [60, 61]. Indeed, SINE and LINE elements are frequently methylated [53], and variations in their methylation level has been shown to be associated with changes in gene expression or brain diseases [62–64]. Together, these findings point toward a model in which preconceptional WL does not disrupt the stable core of promoter–CpG island regulation, but instead modulates more flexible and responsive genomic compartments. Such a strategy may allow the developing brain to adapt to environmental cues by altering enhancer activity, chromatin structure, and repeat element regulation, mechanisms consistent with the principles of neuroepigenetic plasticity [65].

Our findings suggest that differential DNA methylation in the WL may serve as a key regulator of multiple interconnected cellular processes for programming OB functioning through distinct pathways, even if a link between DNA methylation changes and gene regulation or phenotypes remains speculative. First, the enrichment of DMC in histone-specific kinase activities and the presence of DMRs in genes associated in chromatin folding suggests that epigenetic modifications, particularly phosphorylation of histones H2 and H3, may influence chromatin structure and long-term gene expression in the OB. Although less investigated than acetylation or methylation, histone phosphorylation on serine, threonine, or tyrosine residues, contributes to chromatin condensation, DNA damage response, and transcription regulation, and has been linked to several human diseases, including neurodevelopmental disorders and cognitive impairments [66]. In the brain, it participates in synaptic plasticity and learning, contributing to the regulation of immediate early genes in response to neuronal activity [67, 68]. For example, phosphorylation of histone H3 alters nucleosome dynamics, and correlates with transcriptional active loci [69, 70]. Thus, any alteration in DNA methylation of genes related to histone phosphorylation in the OB could modulate neuronal activity and olfactory signal transmission to the upper regions of the brain, thus potentially contributing to the olfactory deficits observed in WL offspring [16]. Moreover, consistent with previous findings in fetal placenta and liver of WL offspring, where the expression of other epigenetic modifiers, particularly those related to histone acetylation, was altered, our results raise the possibility that multiple early-activated epigenetic pathways contribute to broader, long-term adaptive responses [30].

Secondly, the enrichment of pre- and post-synaptic or neuronal plasticity-related terms, including neurexins, neuroligins, and Rho GTPase signaling with some DMRs located in genes or in proximal loci of genes encoding synaptic plasticity, implies that DMCs may contribute to the fine-tuning of neuronal connectivity. Neurexins and neuroligins are trans‑synaptic cell-adhesion molecules that crucially organize synapse formation and differentiation. Presynaptic neurexins bind to postsynaptic neuroligins, forming a "handshake" that bridges the two neurons and initiates synaptic assembly. These interactions play fundamental roles in both excitatory (glutamatergic) and inhibitory (GABAergic) synaptogenesis in the glomeruli, shaping synaptic specificity and network balance to orchestrate the precise connectivity underlying olfactory processing in the OB [71–73].

Third, the relative enrichment in metabolic and endocrine pathways with DMRs located in genes involved in glucose and lipid metabolism, probably highlights the link between energy homeostasis, hormone signaling, and systemic physiological regulation. Glucose, metabolic hormones and endocannabinoids-related lipids profoundly influence olfactory bulb (OB) function, positioning the OB as a neuroendocrine hub linking energy balance to smell perception [26, 27]. Our differential analysis fits with data showing that the maternal HFD modulates numerous genes associated with endocrinology or with metabolism and energy homeostasis [10] and that a moderate diet restriction modulates endocannabinoids in the offspring [15]. Therefore, the WL-induced modification in the methylation of genomic regions associated with metabolism-related hormones and pathways adds to the knowledge that the OB DNA is a target for indirect metabolic imbalances affecting the mother before conception with all other nutritional factors held constant. Also, the enrichment in cytoskeletal dynamics and cell adhesion processes suggests that DNA methylation changes may also influence structural remodeling during neuronal development.

More intriguingly, the identification of DMRs in several zinc fingers transcriptional factors (TFs) is particularly interesting, since TFs are important for proliferation, specification, migration, differentiation/maturation and survival of OB progenitors and/or neurons [74, 75]. As for other genes, any modification in the methylation status of TFs regulatory regions could interfere with TF expression, thus impacting cell identity. Globally, there is still limited data on how DNA methylation changes within genes encoding zinc-finger transcription factors could contribute to long-term programming. For example, ZFP57, a transcription factor that is involved in imprinting regulation and maintenance, shows a significant change in methylation associated with autism [76]. One of our candidates, ZFP423, undergoes promoter demethylation during adipogenic differentiation, a process influenced by maternal nutritional status and relevant to obesity and metabolic disease programming [77]. Also, in the brain, recent evidence from epigenome analyses and imaging studies have revealed intriguing mechanisms in neuronal cells connected with TF expression: a default chromatin structure at activity-dependent genes is formed independently of neuronal activity, while neuronal activity modulates spatiotemporal dynamics of TFs and their interactions with epigenetic factors (EFs) [78]. Accordingly, the DMRs at these loci highlight a potential role for epigenetic modifications in modulating zinc finger transcription factor networks, ultimately influencing pathways governing neuronal differentiation, metabolic homeostasis, and cell adaptation. Among the genes affected by a differential of methylation, some are also concerned by hydroxymethylation, another major epigenetic modification in the central nervous system [25].

Together, these observations support a model in which DNA methylation acts as a versatile modulator, integrating chromatin-level control, neuronal function, and metabolic regulation, with potential implications for both neurodevelopmental and metabolic phenotypes. Only a transcriptional or proteomic study could provide a stronger link between DMRs and behavioral data, although establishing a direct connection between DNA methylation level, transcriptional repression and protein expression remains challenging.

The modulation of the OB methylation landscape as observed here could support long lasting offspring’s trajectories. Indeed, identified DMRs display a unique convergence of epigenetic regulation, metabolism, and neurobiology, suggesting that targeted methylation changes may simultaneously influence cell energy, hormonal signalling and synaptic functions to shape neuronal activity.

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that the brain is sensitive to maternal weight loss through extensive epigenetic remodeling of CpGs methylation in numerous genes. While supported by an experimental model, our results suggest that translational studies should consider that the long-lasting effects of maternal preconceptional nutritional imbalance may be enacted by epigenetic regulatory mechanisms involving DNA methylation. Interventions that target epigenetic modulators, either through dietary, environmental, or pharmacological strategies, may offer new opportunities for preventing or attenuating neurodevelopmental risk associated with maternal nutrition.
