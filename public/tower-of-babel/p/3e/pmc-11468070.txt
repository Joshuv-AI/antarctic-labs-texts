Flexible Allyl‐Modified Gelatin Photoclick Resin Tailored for Volumetric Bioprinting of Matrices for Soft Tissue Engineering
Authors: Alessandro Cianciosi, Sabrina Stecher, Maxi Löffler, Petra Bauer‐Kreisel, Khoon S. Lim, Tim B. F. Woodfield, Jürgen Groll, Torsten Blunk, Tomasz Jungst
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractVolumetric bioprinting (VBP) is a light‐based 3D printing platform, which recently prompted a paradigm shift for additive manufacturing (AM) techniques considering its capability to enable the fabrication of complex cell‐laden geometries in tens of seconds with high spatiotemporal control and pattern accuracy. A flexible allyl‐modified gelatin (gelAGE)‐based photoclick resin is developed in this study to fabricate matrices with exceptionally soft polymer networks (0.2–1.0 kPa). The gelAGE‐based resin formulations are designed to exploit the fast thiol‐ene crosslinking in combination with a four‐arm thiolated polyethylene glycol (PEG4SH) in the presence of a photoinitiator. The flexibility of the gelAGE biomaterial platform allows one to tailor its concentration spanning from 2.75% to 6% and to vary the allyl to thiol ratio without hampering the photocrosslinking efficiency. The thiol‐ene crosslinking enables the production of viable cell‐material constructs with a high throughput in tens of seconds. The suitability of the gelAGE‐based resins is demonstrated by adipogenic differentiation of adipose‐derived stromal cells (ASC) after VBP and by the printing of more fragile adipocytes as a proof‐of‐concept. Taken together, this study introduces a soft photoclick resin which paves the way for volumetric printing applications toward soft tissue engineering.

Introduction

The fast‐growing development of additive manufacturing (AM) techniques has contributed to the rapid progress of biofabrication, which represents the computer‐assisted production of convoluted biological substitutes based on a controlled 3D patterning of building blocks. These span between single cells or cell aggregates, bioactive molecules, biomaterial substrates, and cell‐material hybrid constructs processed with bioprinting or bioassembly techniques, followed by tissue maturation.[ 1 ] Despite the great advances, AM technologies present limitations like slow fabrication rate and stratificational artifacts, associated with the 2D discretization of the computer assisted digital (CAD) model, thus limiting the resolution of the more convoluted construct features.[ 2 ] Vat photopolymerization (VP) represents the current state of the art of AM platforms due to the improved spatial resolution, pattern accuracy and fabrication rate. In 2017, the concept of holography inspired the invention of a novel volumetric AM technique which paved the way for a VP paradigm shift. This platform allows to fabricate complex structures from a photoresin precursor, in seconds, by a holographic patterning of light fields.[ 2 ] After this first conceptualization of volumetric printing a computed axial lithography (CAL) method, inspired by computer tomography (CT), was introduced. This study showed the possibility to fabricate a 3D object, with features around 0.3 mm, by rotating a synthetic photopolymer‐laden reservoir within a dynamically evolving light field in seconds. The light patterns irradiate the entire building volume from multiple angles. A series of back projections of the desired CAD model provides a cumulative light dosage which exceeds the gelation threshold of the photoresin. The solidification of the material is then ideally induced solely in the voxels which correspond to the designed 3D construct.[ 3 ] The great potential of CAL was first applied in biofabrication in a study which demonstrated the volumetric bioprinting (VBP) of centimeter‐scale living tissue constructs, in seconds, based on a methacrylated gelatin (gelMA) photoresin.[ 4 ] In biofabrication, gelMA‐based hydrogels have been extensively used for their suitability for photocrosslinking strategies for preserving their shape in physiological conditions.[ 5 ] The methacrylic moieties of gelMA are crosslinked via chain‐growth free‐radical polymerization which often leads to a inhomogeneous polymer network. Thiol‐ene click chemistry represents an alternative crosslinking approach which enables to better control the 3D hydrogel network architecture. Indeed, this polymerization strategy is based on an orthogonal reaction between a carbon‐carbon double bond, which is often grafted on the gelatin backbone, and a thiol group of a crosslinker molecule. Moreover, the thiol‐ene crosslinking approach is characterized by a higher reaction kinetic and oxygen insensitivity, and enables the use of low concentration of monomers and limits the hydrogel shrinking, thus the polymerization stress.[ 6 ] The further step in resin design for VBP was in fact toward the implementation of the thiol‐ene crosslinking strategy as indicated by a previous study which focused on the development of a gelatin‐norbornene (gelNOR)‐based photoresin.[ 7 ] Norbornene has not been the only carbon‐carbon double bound functional group grafted on a gelatin backbone, indeed gelatin has been also functionalized with allyl glycidyl ether (AGE). In 2017 different types of allyl‐modified gelatin (gelAGE) with different molecular properties, G1MM and G8LL, were introduced for the first time as flexible biomaterial platform for the biofabrication of chondrocyte‐laden constructs via extrusion‐based printing and digital light processing (DLP).[ 8 ] However, the gelAGE concentrations used for printing were relatively high (up to 30%) and furthermore the printed constructs were limited to simple lattice geometries. Moreover, another biofabrication application of gelAGE was demonstrated in a study about extrusion‐based printing of low‐density (3%) and low‐viscosity (4 mPa s) gelAGE‐based bioinks by exploiting the dual component photoinitator system Ru/SPS.[ 9 ] However, the low viscosity obtained with the employed hydrogel precursors platform cannot fulfill the requirements of VBP. The introduction of new processable VBP resins is still a major challenge in light‐based biofabrication to advance the technology. The capability to fabricate soft hydrogels with VBP is particularly challenging since the photoresin needs to comply with viscosity values (>10 Pa s) that enable to stabilize the forces at the interface between the precursor and the cured resin. Furthermore, a sufficiently high viscosity sustains the gravitational force experienced by the cured object during printing and limit the radical propagation.[ 10 ] Therefore, in this study, a gelAGE product, obtained by applying tailored reaction conditions, was exploited to meet the VBP processability criteria such as sufficiently high viscosity and optical transparency, while maintaining a remarkably low polymer concentration. Furthermore, compared to the previous gelAGE studies, the resin formulations were composed utilizing a different crosslinker, a four‐arm thiolated polyethylene glycol (PEG4SH), and lithium phenyl‐2,4,6‐trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) as photoinitiator. By systematically varying precursor concentrations, the mechanical properties of the printed constructs could be modulated.

Designing a suitable soft photoresin may distinctly enlarge the potential application of VBP toward engineering soft tissue, for instance by enabling the fabrication of adipose tissue analogs. There is a strongly growing need for engineered adipose tissue for regenerative applications and 3D tissue models,[ 11 ] however, to date there are still only few applications of AM for adipose engineering,[ 12 , 13 ] often limited to extrusion‐based bioprinting of high concentration biopolymers like gelatin (15%),[ 14 ] gelMA (10%),[ 15 ] and gelMA with κ‐carrageenan (15% total polymer content).[ 16 ] Moreover, extrusion‐based 3D printing presents several limitations associated with long printing times during which the cells are not exposed to the culture conditions, and high shear forces created in the nozzle which can be detrimental for the cell viability.[ 17 ]

The aim of this study was the development of a novel and straightforward gelAGE based photoclick resin for high‐throughput volumetric AM of exceptionally soft hydrogels in tens of seconds. In contrast to the previous gelAGE studies showing only simple lattice geometries with extrusion‐based and DLP printing,[ 8 , 9 ] complex self‐standing structures and perfusable systems were demonstrated to be printable.

Furthermore, this work reveals the capability to tune the polymer concentration, due to the high flexibility of the gelAGE platform, which enabled to achieve mechanical properties of the gelAGE‐based hydrogels ranging from around 0.2 to 1.0 kPa, thus representing a favorable microenvironment for adipose culture and facilitating the printing of both undifferentiated adipose‐derived stromal cells (ASC) and, for the first time in VBP, differentiated adipocytes.

Results and Discussion

VBP is a novel fabrication technique that shows great potential to produce convoluted biologically relevant structures in periods of a few seconds. Although VBP represents a great advancement for VP, several physicochemical features that need to be met (e.g., optical transparency and viscosity above 10 Pa s) are limiting the design and development of biomaterial‐based photoresins. In this study, we aimed to enlarge the availability of VBP processable bioresins introducing a gelAGE platform for the fabrication of hydrogels adapted for soft tissue engineering applications (Figure 1 ). A specific gelAGE biopolymer was synthesized and utilized to generate, for the first time, gelAGE‐based resin formulations for VBP. The gelAGE type was characterized by a lower average molecular weight (M w = 90.8 kDa, M n = 45.3 kDa, dispersity = 2.0) compared to type A gelatin (M w = 164.3 kDa, M n = 123.1 kDa, dispersity = 1.3) used as educt (Figure S1, Supporting Information), however, the molecular weight was distinctly higher than that of gelAGE used in previous extrusion‐based printing studies (15 and 50 kDa, respectively).[ 8 , 9 ] The allyl‐functionalized gelatin chains were denatured due to the combined effect of temperature (65 °C) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 2 m) during the reaction,[ 18 ] although the reduction of the average molecular weight did not interfere with the intrinsic thermally reversible nature of the polymer. The molecular properties of our gelAGE, in particular molecular weight and degree of modification (DoM), enabled to develop gelAGE‐based formulations for VBP with very low concentrations (in contrast to Bertlein et al.)[ 8 ] while maintaining a sufficiently high viscosity (in contrast to Soliman et al.).[ 9 ] The DoM was characterized by proton‐nuclear magnetic resonance (1H‐NMR, Figure S2, Supporting Information) and it was expressed as moles of allyl groups per gram of gelatin (0.82 mmol g−1). The DoM was almost fivefold higher than for the gelNOR (0.01 to 0.16 mmol g−1)[ 7 ] used in a previous VBP study, which could suggest the formation of stiffer hydrogel matrices.[ 19 ] Nevertheless, the photoresin formulations with 2.75% and 6% of gelAGE, respectively, in combination with PEG4SH and LAP as photoinitiator proved to be softer than the gelNOR analogs printed with comparable concentrations (2.5% and 5%; storage modulus: around 1.0 and 10 kPa).[ 7 ] In addition, the flexibility of the gelAGE platform enabled to tailor the stiffness of the fabricated hydrogels toward soft polymer networks favorable for the application with both ASC and differentiated adipocytes.

Screening and Optimization of gelAGE‐Based Photoresin Formulations

Initially, a library of gelAGE‐based resin formulations was systematically investigated (Table 1 ) to determine the concentration span of gelAGE and PEG4SH, in the presence of 0.1% of LAP as water‐soluble photoinitiator. The concentration of the crosslinker was determined in accordance with the degree of allyl‐functionalities present on the gelAGE substrate by providing, if not stated otherwise, an equimolar ratio between the carbon‐carbon double bond and the thiol moieties. The suitability of the different formulations was rationalized based on the precursor's physical properties, for example, transparency and viscosity, which represent key aspects for VBP. The former property is crucial to enable the light patterns to travel through the entire volume depth of the resin‐laden reservoir, in contrast to other resin‐based 3D printing techniques like DLP and stereolithography (SLA).[ 7 ] Furthermore, the thermally induced physical gelation of the modified gelatin increases the photoresin viscosity limiting the radical and molecular diffusion which negatively affect the resolution of the printed structure.[ 3 ] Moreover, the viscosity needs to be sufficiently high (>10 Pa s), to balance the gravitational force avoiding the 3D construct sedimentation during the printing process.[ 3 , 10 ] A thorough screening was conducted on 15 different gelAGE concentrations, from 2.5% to 6% (with an equimolar allyl to thiol ratio) using systematic increments of 0.25%. The analysis highlighted 2.75% of gelAGE as the lowest processable concentration, below which the thermal gelation was absent. Furthermore, 6% of gelAGE was identified as the highest concentration, however, the molar ratio between allyl and thiol moieties was adjusted to 1: 0.75 to avoid phase separation, which was observed when using an equimolar ratio. This physical phenomenon occurring at the interface between gelatin and PEG in aqueous solution has been previously demonstrated to be correlated with protein concentration and with exposure to low temperatures. At low temperature, the gelation, particularly, of gelatin‐rich phase aqueous solution is very fast, thus several microdomains are created and eventually merged into a macroscopic phase creating a single turbid solution.[ 21 ] Indeed, the opalescence observed only in the high concentration gelAGE‐based solution was induced after gelation at low temperatures (around 0 °C). Additionally, it was reported that the phase separation phenomenon in binary polymer aqueous solution of gelatin and PEG is also influenced by the concentration of the latter. As the concentration of PEG increases, there is a shift toward lower temperatures of the critical separation point, along with an increase in the melting enthalpy of gelatin. These observations suggest a strengthening of the hydrogen bonding between gelatin chains, ultimately leading to phase separation in aqueous gelatin/PEG systems.[ 22 ] These findings are in line with the observations made after a thorough screening of different gelAGE‐based resin concentrations. The full overview of screened compositions (Table S1, Supporting Information) was added to the supporting information as extension to Table 1. The rationale of the screening was to target a polymer concentration window of processable gelAGE‐based photoresins. Although the range was broad due to the high flexibility of the precursor system, subsequently the study focused on the boundary concentrations to better understand their effect on the physicochemical and mechanical properties of the two hydrogel formulations and further investigate their suitability for VBP of ASC and adipocytes.

Physicochemical Characterization of the gelAGE‐Based Photoresins

Hydrogels have been demonstrated to serve efficiently as platforms for tissue regeneration considering their ability to recapitulate many physicochemical properties of the extracellular matrix (ECM), thus possibly mimicking important attributes of soft tissues.[ 23 ] In this work, the physicochemical properties of the gelAGE‐based photoresin were thoroughly characterized. The efficiency of the photocrosslinking reaction was quantitatively evaluated using the sol fraction values (mass loss at day 1) for the photoresins with both low (2.75%) and high (6%) gelAGE concentration. The sol fraction describes the amount of non‐crosslinked polymer which leaches out of the hydrogel network after 24 h. The photoresin characterized by low gelAGE content (2.75%) was crosslinked with 2.75% of PEG4SH thus using an equimolar ratio of allyl to thiol moieties, contrary to the high gelAGE concentration (6%)‐based photoresin which was covalently crosslinked with 4% of PEG4SH, resulting in a thiol deficit (1:0.75). The low concentration gelAGE‐based resin had a significantly (p < 0.05) higher sol fraction (21 ± 9%) compared to the high concentration analog (9 ± 6%), thus indicating the formation of fewer radical species over time and further suggested an unaffected photocrosslinking efficacy for the high concentration formulation, although in presence of a thiol deficit. Nevertheless, the crosslinking efficacy of the low concentration resin aligned with the sol fraction values of other gelatin‐based hydrogels, as gelMA (15–30%)[ 24 ] and gelatin (11–24%)[ 25 ] in the presence of LAP (0.05%) and Ru/SPS, respectively. The water absorption of the gelAGE‐based hydrogel matrices was quantified with the swelling analysis over time (Figure 2A). The high concentration gelAGE‐based resin showed swelling values between 9% and 11%, which were significantly lower than the low concentration resin (around 13%, over 14 days). The gelAGE‐based photoresins stability against degradation over time was determined by the percentage of mass loss (Figure 2B). The high concentration resin showed a significant (p < 0.05) mass loss increment from day 1 to day 4 (from around 21% to 31%), before reaching a plateau until day 14. The low concentration resin did not show significant changes over time, but by trend values were invariably greater than for the high concentration analog. Taken together, both swelling and mass loss values of high and low concentration photoresin reached a plateau within 14 days, thus suggesting that both hydrogels reached an equilibrium. Moreover, the data describing the swelling and the mass loss of the two different hydrogel systems suggested the presence of a lower crosslinking density for the low concentration resin which correlated with its higher sol fraction and swelling ratio.[ 26 ] Furthermore, the difference in crosslinking density could be correlated to the divergence in mechanical properties between the low and high concentration gelAGE‐based resins.[ 27 ] Although both resins demonstrated a remarkably soft hydrogel matrix, the unconfined uniaxial compression test highlighted an exceptionally low Young's modulus (E) for the low polymer concentration resin (Figure 2C). The mechanical characterization was performed with low strain rate (γ˙ = 0.001 s−1), considering its influence on the behavior of biological tissue.[ 28 ] After crosslinking, the low concentration gelAGE‐based resin showed a value of E around 0.2 kPa which increased over 14 days until reaching a value of around 0.4 kPa. On the other end, the high concentration resin exhibited, after crosslinking, an E value around 1.0 kPa followed by a decrement until day 14 when the Young's modulus reached roughly 0.75 kPa. When evaluating the trends of the Young's modulus for both resins between day 1, 4, 7, and 14 in physiological conditions (phosphate buffer saline, pH = 7.4, at 37 °C), some fluctuations were observed. Particularly, for the high concentration resin, there was a significant (p < 0.0001) softening of the hydrogel from 0.91 to 0.74 kPa, between day 4 and 7, which could be related to the slightly increased swelling of the polymeric network, from 10.83% to 11.16%, in the same time span.[ 29 ] There was no significant change in the elastic modulus between day 7 and 14. In contrast, the Young's modulus of the low concentration resin did not exhibit significant differences between day 1 to 14. The observed fluctuation of the E value trends over the course of the entire 14 days, although statically significant, resulted in less than 0.20 kPa for the low concentration resin and around 0.25 kPa for the high concentration resin between day 0 and 14, respectively. These variations appear relatively small in comparison to a gold standard gelMA‐based hydrogel platform that showed E values fluctuating about 5 kDa, over a long‐time span.[ 30 ] The results of the mechanical characterization of the gelAGE‐based resins corresponded well with literature data regarding the uniaxial compression tests performed on adipose tissue samples with low shear rate, thus indicating Young's moduli around 1.0 kPa.[ 31 , 32 ] The E values of both gelAGE‐based resins, 0.2 to 1.0 kPa, can be also contextualized among other biopolymer‐based resins for VBP. For comparable volumetrically printed gelNOR concentrations (2.5% and 5%), E values between 1.0 and 10 kPa were showed.[ 7 ] VBP of gelMA (5%) alone and in combination with iodixanol (10%) showed E values between 2.0 and 5.0 kPa.[ 33 ] Furthermore, a recent study on VBP of silk‐based bioresins showed Young's moduli between roughly 0.2 to 1.0 kPa, depending on the material type (silk sericin or silk fibroin) and their concentrations (2.5%, 5%, and 10%).[ 34 ] When comparing to the silk‐based material, besides the differences in the chemical composition, gelatin‐based solutions can withstand a wide range of temperature and possess an inherent thermoreversible sol–gel transition.[ 35 ] This physicochemical feature is particularly useful for VBP to control the external environment simply and precisely to induce a physical gel with suitable viscosity. In contrast, in silk‐fibroin‐based solutions conformational changes occur at 37 °C, which lead to irreversible β‐sheet formation, thus yielding a permanent physical gel.[ 36 ] So far, the silk‐based resins have been shown in VBP mainly for C2C12 myoblast culture and osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow‐derived stromal cells.[ 34 ] In the present study, the gelAGE‐based formulations were demonstrated for VBP of ASC with subsequent adipogenic differentiation, and for the first time, for VBP of differentiated adipocytes. It was not within the scope of the current study to directly compare silk and gelAGE formulations. Nevertheless, future studies may include detailed comparisons of available inks in VBP with regard to physicochemical characteristics, and the culture of certain cell types and the generation of specific tissues, respectively.

Rheological Characterization of the gelAGE‐Based Photoresins

The viscoelastic properties of the gelAGE‐based resins were characterized with oscillatory measurements at room temperature conditions (21 °C). Indeed, the gelAGE‐based hydrogel precursor solutions were in a liquid state at 21 °C and easy to handle, in contrast to native‐ and other functionalized‐gelatin derivatives which are usually highly viscous.[ 37 ] First, the linear viscoelastic region (LVR) was evaluated and an oscillatory frequency of 10 rad s−1 and a shear strain of 5% were selected for the oscillatory measurements. The gelAGE‐based photoresins were always put in an ice bath for about 20 min to cool them down prior to volumetric printing, thus the thermoreversible properties of the gelAGE yielded a physical gel with an ideal viscosity. In this study, a new approach was introduced to better simulate the experimental temperature profile to which the photoresins were exposed following the above‐mentioned resin preparation procedure, by live monitoring the temperature in situ and input the obtained ramp for the rheological analysis. Notably, the resulting temperature ramp followed a logarithmic trend (from 21 to −2.3 °C, Figure S3, Supporting Information). This is an expected deviation from the standard linear temperature ramp used in rheology. Considering the fast heat exchange happening within 280 µL of precursor solution used in the rheological setup (gap between the parallel plates: 500 µm) we expect the outcome of the temperature sweep to be sufficiently accurate. Temperature sweeps (Figure 3A,B) demonstrated the presence of a cross‐over point indicating a sol to gel transition, thus the formation a stable physical polymer network for the gelAGE‐based resins. Furthermore, the temperature sweeps showed a substantial shift of the crossover point among the gelAGE‐based resins suggesting a dependency between the gelatin concentration in the precursor solutions and the time and temperature of gelation.[ 38 ] Particularly, the high concentration precursor solution showed a faster physical gelation at a higher temperature, in comparison to the low concentration resin. The stability of the physical gel was confirmed by the frequency sweeps (Figure 3C,D), which showed non‐frequency dependent constant trends of both storage and loss modulus. Rotational tests (Figure 3E) were performed on both gelAGE‐based resins directly after the formation of a stable physical polymer network, at −2.3 °C, with a shear rate of 5 s−1. The viscosity trends over time showed an increase of more than sixfold for the high concentration resin (94 Pa s) compared to the low concentration (15 Pa s), although in both cases the viscosity remained stable and above the 10 Pa s threshold to avoid the sinking of the printed structures during printing.[ 3 , 10 ] To simulate the volumetric printing process, a photorheological analysis was performed on the physical gels using a UV–vis irradiation for 35 s, which led to the formation of covalently crosslinked polymer networks in the presence of LAP. After only 10 s of exposure to UV–vis light, the time sweeps (Figure 3F) showed a steep increase of the storage modulus of roughly two orders of magnitude for the high concentration resin and almost three orders of magnitude for the low concentration gel.

Light Dose Optimization and Volumetric Printing Resolution

The bioink design paradigm for biofabrication and tissue engineering highlighted the complex correlation between a cell‐friendly microenvironment of the polymer matrix and the shape fidelity of the printed constructs, leading to the definition of an ideal biofabrication window where both aspects meet at high levels. This concept paved the way for the development of new biofabrication technologies and bioinks which enable the production of cell‐friendly or even cell‐instructive tissue models with a high shape fidelity.[ 39 ] For VP printing techniques, the shape fidelity and the resolution of the fabricated constructs are mainly influenced by the photo‐chemical properties of the resin which determine its reactivity.[ 40 ] The photocrosslinking efficiency of the thiol‐ene click chemistry has been often used in VP printing techniques as highlighted, for instance, by DLP printing of gelAGE,[ 8 ] and more recently by VBP of gelNOR which underlined an improved printing speed compared to the chain‐growth polymerization of gelMA.[ 7 ] In the current study, the kinetics of the photocrosslinking process was evaluated through photorheological measurements, even though it represented only an approximation of the VBP photocrosslinking conditions. In this regard, a systematic light dose screening test (Figure S4, Supporting Information) was performed on the two gelAGE‐based photoresins to better identify a narrow window of ideal light dosages for each formulation. The light dose test was carried out using a built‐in software function (Readily 3D SA, Lausanne, Switzerland) which enabled to project light on a static resin‐laden cuvette to obtain a designed arrangement of dots sorted by different light intensities and exposure times, thus enabling to identify the critical gelation threshold (CGT). This parameter is relevant for VBP since represents the minimum required light dose to obtain a crosslinked gel, below which the light is absorbed and above which overcuring of the desired structure occurs. Based on the dose test, it was possible to demonstrate that the gelAGE‐based resins showed fast printing times of around 20–30 s depending on the resin concentration. As expected, the introduction of cells to the resin formulation at densities of 1 × 105 and 1 × 106 cells mL−1, respectively, affected the printing times and increased them by 10 and 16 s according to the added cellular density (Table 2 ). These findings were in accordance with previous analysis where light scattering by the introduction of cell suspensions to the resin formulations affected the minimal light dosage to overcome the gelation threshold.[ 33 ] In this context, it should be noted that different cell types and larger cellular aggregates, such as organoids, may further affect the light dose required for gelation.[ 33 ]

Notably, for the gelAGE‐based resins without embedded cells it was possible to achieve features around 100 µm in correspondence to the S‐shaped filament (Figure 4A‐i) of 3D constructs, which have been introduced in a previous study.[ 33 ] Nevertheless, already at day 0 the S‐shaped strand with the smallest resolution presented a more altered pattern compared to the other two constructs with diameters of 200 and 500 µm. Moreover, the S‐shaped strands with 500 and 200 µm diameter demonstrated to maintain their S‐shaped filaments with high fidelity to the initial model even after 7 days of incubation in physiological conditions, in contrast to the construct with a 100 µm diameter strand which started to degrade in the middle part of the S‐shaped filament (Figure 4A‐ii). Taken together, these results suggested the possibility to successfully achieve stable structures with 200 µm resolved features, in line with the VBP of gelNOR‐based resins,[ 7 ] even though the first proof‐of‐concept S‐shaped construct with 100 µm strand highlighted an encouraging avenue to improve the construct resolution further. Moreover, it was possible to fabricate complex geometries with the gelAGE‐based photoresins. The focus was put toward details that are particularly difficult to produce with VBP as self‐supporting features such as those represented in the hollow cage (Figure 4B‐i,ii) and sharp edges as shown in the pyramid structure (Figure 4B‐iii). In addition, the possibility to fabricate perfusable structures as shown by the branched geometries filled with high molecular weight blue‐dextran was demonstrated (Figure 4B‐v,vi).

The specific structures were chosen to demonstrate the variety of differently shaped objects that can be obtained with VBP in tens of seconds utilizing the newly developed resins. With regard to adipose tissue engineering, perfusable branched geometries may facilitate sufficient vascularization, and the possibility of printing a variety of delicate and complex shapes may enable the generation of sophisticated composite constructs, for example, needed in breast reconstruction. Indeed, only recently, poor vascularization and relatively oversimplified construct design have been suggested as unresolved issues impeding the translational application of bioprinting technology in the area of breast tissue regeneration.[ 41 , 42 ] In the present study, the focus of the biological experiments was still on the proof‐of‐principle of volumetrically printing ASC and adipocytes in the newly developed resins. Nevertheless, in the long run, the demonstration of printing more complex objects may facilitate to overcome major challenges associated with soft tissue reconstruction and the generation of advanced tissue models.

Volumetric Bioprinting of Adipose‐Derived Stromal Cells (ASC) and Subsequent Differentiation

In vitro evaluation of the gelAGE‐based photoresin cytocompatibility, using both low (2.75%) and high (6%) gelAGE concentration, was performed on ASC‐laden 3D constructs (cylinders, diameter: 6 mm, height: 1 mm) using either 1 × 105 or 1 × 106 cells mL−1 cellular densities depending on the analysis. The maximum limit of cellular density was chosen based on a previous VBP study.[ 7 ] Light doses between 420 and 480 mJ cm−2 (for 2.75% gelAGE), or 320 and 380 mJ cm−2 (for 6% gelAGE), depending on the respective cell densities, were applied for volumetric bioprinting of 10 cylinders at the same time, resulting in printing times between 32.1 and 48.1 s (see also Table 2 for details). Furthermore, an average LED intensity of 9.98 mW cm−2 and a rotation speed of 74.8 and 77 ° s−1 (projection rate: 260 and 267 Hz, respectively) was used for the high and low cellular density, respectively. ASC within the volumetrically bioprinted 3D hydrogels were subsequentially differentiated toward the adipogenic lineage using a common hormonal cocktail (Figure 5A). Live/dead staining confirmed high survival of cells directly after printing and during consecutive culture with more than 90% viable cells in both hydrogel formulations (Figure 5C). ASC exhibited a predominantly round morphology (Figure 5C; Figure S5, Supporting Information), indicating a rather weak adhesion to the hydrogel matrix, which in turn has previously been suggested to be favorable for adipogenic differentiation.[ 43 ] After 14 days of adipogenic culture, well‐differentiated, lipid‐laden cells were visualized within the gels by Oil Red O staining, in contrast to undifferentiated cultures (Figure 5D). Large fat vacuoles inside the differentiated cells were found to be surrounded by perilipin 1, a key adipose‐specific lipid droplet protein (Figure 5D, right side). Gene expression analysis of adipogenic marker PPARγ, a master regulator of early adipogenesis, as well as fatty acid‐binding protein 4 (FABP4) as a late marker of adipogenic differentiation, confirmed sustained adipogenic differentiation of ASC in the printed constructs (Figure 5E). No significant differences in adipogenesis were observed comparing the two hydrogel formulations, which was not surprising since the Youngs´s modulus was very low and within the range of fat tissue in both conditions.[ 32 ] The results were also in line with reports in non‐printed culture systems showing that adipogenic differentiation of human mesenchymal stem cells was well achievable in soft matrices exhibiting comparable stiffness.[ 44 ] Taken together, the gelAGE‐based photoresins provided a favorable environment for adipogenic differentiation of human ASC and as such represent a suitable platform for further investigations of adipogenesis in printable constructs.

In adipose tissue engineering, 3D bioprinting represents a promising approach since it enables the computer aided design and fabrication of detailed constructs with accurate spatiotemporal control of ink material and cell deposition. This is particularly desirable in the development of complex structures for breast reconstruction including sufficient vascularization,[ 41 , 42 ] and of advanced disease models involving adipose tissue.[ 13 , 45 ] Extrusion‐based printing including multi‐nozzle bioprinters, in principle, facilitates the fabrication of hierarchical structures with various cell types positioned in specific patterns, however, the construct design is often limited and extended printing times are necessary.[ 41 ] In contrast, VBP allows to generate complex and filigree structures with high throughput in tens of seconds. Furthermore, as a nozzle‐free technology, VBP induces distinctly lower shear stress upon cells, as compared to extrusion‐based processes, and thus may be especially suitable for the printing of fragile cells such as differentiated adipocytes (see also next section). Only recently, the convergence of extrusion‐based and VBP was introduced, which may further increase the options to create complex structures with multi‐material and multi‐cellular features in a fast and accurate manner.[ 45 ] While our current study demonstrates the suitability of the newly developed resins for VBP and the functionality of cells after printing, future research must explore in more detail, also in comparison to traditional tissue engineering approaches, the advantages of VBP for adipose tissue engineering.

Volumetric Bioprinting of Differentiated Adipocytes

For the generation of more complex adipose constructs or models, also involving other cell types, it may be advantageous to be able to print adipocytes directly, thus avoiding a long differentiation phase of the printed constructs in the presence of a multi‐component adipogenic induction cocktail. Differentiated adipocytes, however, are fragile cells because of their lipid‐laden morphology and are therefore easily damaged by shear stress as occurring in classical extrusion‐based bioprinting.[ 46 ] As a consequence, large nozzle diameters and low pressure are needed for gentle printing conditions in this context.[ 47 ] This is, however, associated with low resolution and prolonged fabrication times, which in turn may also be harmful for the processed cells. VBP as a layer‐less and nozzle‐free biofabrication platform represents an attractive method for the manufacturing of constructs containing fragile cells, furthermore the fast fabrication speed could benefit the cell survival.

In a proof‐of‐concept study, adipocytes obtained by differentiating ASC for 14 days prior to printing were volumetrically bioprinted (Figure 6A), using both the low gelAGE concentration (2.75%) (Figure 6) and the high gelAGE concentration (6%) photoresins (Figure S6, Supporting Information). After the differentiation phase, these cells exhibited a lipid‐rich phenotype with abundant lipid vacuoles in the cytoplasm, and the expression of adipogenic marker genes for early (PPARγ) and late (FABP4) adipogenesis was distinctly up‐regulated (Figure S7, Supporting Information), thus demonstrating the status of clearly differentiated adipocytes. For VBP of cylinders (diameter: 6 mm, height: 1 mm) containing adipocytes, the same light doses and printing times, depending on gel formulation and cell density, were used as for ASC constructs (see Section 2.5. and Table 2 for details). The viability of the volumetrically bioprinted adipocytes was high with around 90% viable cells one day after printing (Figure 6B; Figure S6A, Supporting Information). Oil Red O staining revealed the presence of lipid‐rich cells inside the hydrogels (Figure 6C; Figure S6B, Supporting Information) indicating that the adipocytes were not impaired by the printing process. To further assess the functionality of the printed adipocytes, the secretion of adipokines was analyzed. Adiponectin, which is produced by differentiated adipocytes, was secreted by the printed cells predominantly early after printing, whereas leptin, another classical adipokine, exhibited a distinct increase during culture after printing (Figure 6D; Figure S6C, Supporting Information). This secretion profile was triggered by the different cultivation regimes of the cells before and after printing. ASC were treated prior to printing with the classical hormonal adipogenic cocktail leading to a differentiated phenotype associated with the secretion of adiponectin. The observed decrease in adiponectin secretion at later stages has also been reported previously in a different 3D culture system.[ 13 ] Maintenance culture of adipocytes in the printed constructs in medium with only insulin triggered the secretion of leptin, a marker of adipocyte maturation.[ 48 ] Taken together, these results highlighted the successful volumetrically bioprinting of differentiated adipocytes in gelAGE‐based resin without limiting cell viability or function. This, in turn, could pave the way for the fabrication of more complex constructs, involving fat cells, such as breast tumor models or pathophysiological models of obesity.

Conclusions

In this study a novel gelAGE‐based photoclick resin was developed for VBP of exceptionally soft hydrogel matrices (0.2–1.0 kPa) toward 3D culture of ASC and differentiated adipocytes. Currently, available resins for VBP are still scarce, and the newly introduced gelAGE‐based formulations contribute to fulfill the urgent need to enlarge the library of processable VBP resins. The orthogonal thiol‐ene click chemistry crosslinking strategy was exploited to fabricate 3D cell‐laden constructs and more complex cell‐free structures in tens of seconds and with a high‐throughput. A thorough systematic optimization screening was performed by varying formulations and allyl to thiol ratios and helped to identify a broad operational window of gelAGE‐based photoresins, which further underlined the flexibility of the biomaterial platform. The work focused on the comparison of two gelAGE‐based formulations selected at the borders of the operational window, characterized by a low (2.75%) and a high (6%) gelAGE concentration, to understand the differences of their physicochemical and mechanical properties and evaluate their suitability for VBP of ASC and adipocytes. The analysis of the physicochemical properties demonstrated the high photoreactive efficiency of the platform as indicated by the sol fraction and swelling outcomes, further the polymer network stiffness of the gelAGE‐based formulations varied from 0.2 kPa for the low concentration (2.75%) to around 1.0 kPa for the high concentration (6%). Rheological measurements were conducted to demonstrate the thermo‐induced gelation of gelAGE and to highlight its viscosity profile which needed to sustain the gravitational force and the motions at the interface between the cured resin and its precursor. Although the photorheological analysis was important to demonstrate the kinetics of the photocrosslinking process, a light dose test was performed to better identify the CGT for the two gelAGE‐based formulations which enabled to identify an ideal light dosage window which was demonstrated, expectedly, to be significantly influenced by the presence of cells.

The gelAGE‐based photoresins enabled adipogenic differentiation of ASC due to the provided soft microenvironment, furthermore both gelAGE‐based resins demonstrated a successful, proof‐of‐concept, fabrication with VBP of differentiated adipocytes with high viability and function. These promising biological outcomes pave the way for further investigation of adipogenesis and for the fabrication of more complex adipose tissue models, and may be extended to other soft tissue such as brain, lung, and endothelial tissue.

Experimental Section

Synthesis of Allyl‐Modified Gelatin (gelAGE)

The gelAGE synthesis was adapted and optimized based on previously published protocols.[ 8 ] Type A gelatin from porcine skin (Sigma‐Aldrich, Saint Louise, USA) was dissolved in deionized water (10%) for 2 h at 37 °C.NaOH (2 m, Sigma‐Aldrich) in a concentration of 0.4 mmol gram of gelatin−1, and AGE (Sigma‐Aldrich) in a concentration of 24 mmol gram of gelatin−1 were added to the gelatin at 65 °C for 2 h. The alkaline environment induced by the NaOH deprotonated the amino groups on the gelatin backbone. The induced nucleophilic site on the gelatin attacked the exposed carbon site on the AGE ring through a nucleophilic substitution. High temperature in combination with alkaline conditions was responsible for shortening the native gelatin chains yielding smaller molecular weights. The synthesized gelAGE product was dialyzed (Spectra/Por 6, Fischer Scientific, Hampton, USA; molecular weight cut‐off = 1 kDa) against deionized water and then lyophilized.

Molecular Characterization of Allyl‐Modified Gelatin

Aqueous gel permeation chromatography (GPC) analysis (0.1 m NaNO3, 0.02 wt% NaN3) was conducted using a Malvern system (Malvern, Herrenberg, Germany) and consisted of a Viskotek GPCmax (in‐line degasser, 2 piston‐pump and autosampler), column oven (35 °C), refractive index (Viskotek), and SEC‐MALS 20 (Viskotek) detector. Column set was 2× A6000M (length: 300 mm, width: 8 mm, porous polyhydroxymethacrylate polymer, particle size A6000M = 13 µm). The flow rate was 0.7 mL min−1. Calibration was performed using PEG standards (Malvern). The molecular weight of the PEG standards was comprised between 0.4 to 700 kDa. Samples were allowed to dissolve overnight (3 mg mL−1) and filtered through a syringe filter (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, USA; 0.45 µm regenerated cellulose) prior to analysis.1H‐NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Biospin 400 MHz spectrometer (Bruker, Billerica, USA) with D2O as solvent. As internal reference the signal at δ = 4.79 ppm was used. The phenylalanine peaks at δ = 7.45–7.25 ppm were used as reference signals for AGE modifications and calibrated to 5 protons. To determine the DoM, the proton integral of the allyl moiety was compared to the phenylalanine (Phe). On average, 1 g of gelatin contains 21 mg of Phe (2.1%). The composition of acid‐treated porcine skin gelatin was acquired from the gelatin handbook of the gelatin manufacturers institute of America (GMIA). gelAGE‐Based Photoresin Preparation

The freeze‐dried gelAGE together with a commercially available PEG4SH (JenKem Technologies, Plano, USA), in a powder state, were dissolved in PBS (onefold, Sigma‐Aldrich). Eventually, the photoinitiator LAP (Sigma‐Aldrich) was diluted into the resin precursor (0.1%) from a freshly prepared stock solution (1%) in PBS. The resin precursor solutions were allowed to thoroughly dissolve at 37 °C before the transfer in a custom‐made glass vial (Readily3D SA), then the glass vial was transferred into an ice bath for 20 min, to obtain a physical gel, prior to the volumetric printing process.

Dose Tests for Light Dosage Optimization

500–700 µL of gelAGE‐based resin formulation was prepared as previously reported and transferred into polystyrene transparent cuvettes (Brand, Wertheim, Germany) and cool down into an ice bath for 20 min. The cuvette was placed into a custom‐made holder (Readily 3D SA) and transferred to a commercial Tomolite v1.0 (Readily 3D SA). The dose test built‐in function was used to fabricate a pattern of pillars (diameter: 500 µm, pillars spacing: 500 µm) following customized range of light intensities and exposure times to identify the CGT. Three consecutive sets of dose tests with increasingly narrower ranges were used to better optimize the printability window based on the CGT evaluation.

Volumetric Printing of gelAGE‐Based 3D Constructs

Photoresins with low (2.75%) and high (6%) gelAGE concentration were prepared as indicated above. The 3D printed structures were fabricated with a commercial Tomolite v1.0 (Readily3D SA). The average light intensity across all prints was set to 9.98 mW cm−2. The CAD files were either inspired by previous literature (e.g., hollow cage, S‐shape filaments,[ 33 ] and branch structures)[ 7 ] or obtained with the software Thingiverse (MarkerBot Industries, New York, USA). After the volumetric printing step, the glass vial was transferred into a water‐bath (37 °C) for a minute to fully dissolve the uncrosslinked hydrogel precursor, then the printed structure was gently removed from the vial and washed thoroughly with warm PBS. The non‐crosslinked resin was recycled for further printing sessions, which enabled to fabricate many constructs (e.g., cylinders, diameter: 6 mm, height: 1 mm) for high‐throughput screenings (e.g., biological assays). The perfusion of the hollow channels of the branch structure was highlighted by using high‐molecular weight blue‐dextran (Sigma‐Aldrich; Dextran blue from Leuconostoc mesenteroides, M W = 2 × 106 Da).

Volumetric Bioprinting of Cell‐Laden gelAGE‐Based 3D Constructs

Both the freeze‐dried gelAGE and the PEG4SH powder were sterilized under UV light (λ max = 254 nm, UVGL‐25 compact UV lamp, Analytik Jena, Jena, Germany) for 30 min in sterile conditions. The LAP stock solution was filtered with a 0.45 µm sterile filter (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Nalgene syringe filter 0.2 µm polyethersulfone (PES) membrane) prior to dilution into the final cell‐laden resin precursor. Both 1 × 105 and 1 × 106 cells mL−1 were suspended in 7 mL of photoresin precursor solution into a glass vial, which was closed with an air‐tight cap. The vial was then placed into an ice bath before volumetric printing as prior indicated. The average light intensity across all prints was 9.98 mW cm−2. Cell‐laden 3D printed structures were then transferred into cell culture media.

Physicochemical Characterization of gelAGE‐Based Hydrogels

The swelling and mass loss analysis of both gelAGE‐based photoresins was adapted from previous studies.[ 25 , 49 ] In brief, cylindrical hydrogels (diameter: 6 mm, height: 1 mm, N = 4) were fabricated with low and high concentration gelAGE‐based resins. Samples were weighed after the crosslinking to obtain the initial mass (m initial, t 0) and then lyophilized to get the dry weight (m dry, t 0). The other samples (N = 4), grouped per time points (day 1, 4, 7, 14), were incubated at 37 °C in PBS with systematic buffer changes over time. Samples were blotted dry and weighed to obtain the swollen mass (m swollen, t) and lyophilized to get the dry weight (m dry, t).

The swelling ratio (q) of the hydrogels over 7 days was calculated based on Equation (4). (4) q=mswollen,tmdry,t

The percentage of sol fraction which corresponds to the amount of non‐crosslinked polymer that leaches out form the hydrogel network after 24 h, was calculated based on Equation (5). (5) %solfraction=mdry,initial−mdry,t=24hmdry,initial×100

Thereby, m dry,intial was calculated using the actual macromer fraction as reported in Equations (6) and (7). (6) actualmacromerfraction=mdry,t0minitial,t0 (7) mdry,initial=minitial×actualmacromerfraction

The mechanical properties of the hydrogels were measured in an unconfined, uniaxial compression test, using a dynamic mechanical analyzer (BOSE, ElectroForce 5500 system, Framingham, USA). Cylindrical samples (diameter: 5 mm, height: 3.5 mm, N = 4) were incubated in PBS at different time points (day 1, 4, 7), and subsequently subjected to a strain ramp until 25% compression of the sample height was reached (0.01 mm s−1), preload buffer time 20 s. The Young's modulus was calculated from the slope of the stress–strain curve.

Rheological Measurements

Rheological properties of gelAGE‐based resins formulations were characterized using an Anton Paar MCR 702 rheometer (Anton Paar, Graz, Austria) with a 25 mm parallel plate geometry at a 500 µm gap. A solvent trap was used to limit evaporation phenomenon during the experiments. All measurements were conducted by loading the freshly prepared hydrogel precursors on the lower plate at room temperature (21 °C). After being transferred into a glass vial (Readily 3D SA) that was cooled down using into an ice bath, the photoresins were exposed to a temperature range (from 21 to −2.3 °C) which was monitored live with a digital thermometer equipped with a type K thermocouple (RS PRO, Noida, India; polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE); diameter: 2 mm, length: 1 m) that was immersed into the hydrogel precursor solution. The obtained temperature profile was input to the rheometer for the oscillation analysis of the thermo‐induced sol to gel transition of the gelAGE‐based resin precursor to better resemble the experimental conditions of the physical gelation of the photoresins. The oscillatory measurements were performed with 5% strain and 10 rad s−1 angular frequency, these values were selected from the LVR. The rotational measurements for determining the viscosity profile were performed using a shear rate of 5 s−1. Photorheological measurements were performed by coupling the rheometer with a UV–vis light source (Dr. Hönle, bluepoint 4, Gräfelfing, Germany) equipped with a flexible light guide. The wavelength spectrum was narrowed using an optical filter between 390 and 500 nm. The glass of the lower enabled the UV–vis light to crosslink the precursors using an exposure time of 34 s at 7.5 cm distance (light probe to the lower plate). These crosslinking parameters were selected to better mimic the light dose of the volumetric printing process.

Adipose‐Derived Stromal Cells (ASC) Culture

Human ASC (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) were expanded in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium/Ham's F‐12 (DMEM/F‐12; Life Technologies, Carlsbad, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Life Technologies), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (100 U mL−1 penicillin, 0.1 mg mL−1 streptomycin; Life Technologies), and 3 ng mL−1 basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF; BioLegend, London, UK) (cell expansion media) in T175 cm2 flasks (Greiner Bio‐One, Frickenhausen, Germany) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Medium was changed every other day. Cells of passage 6 at 80–85% confluence were harvested using 0.25% trypsin‐EDTA solution (Life Technologies) or adipogenic differentiation was initiated. For the latter, cell expansion media was additionally supplemented with insulin (final concentration 1.7×10−6 m; PromoCell, Heidelberg, Germany), dexamethasone (1×10−6 m; Sigma‐Aldrich), 3‐isobutyl‐1‐methylxanthine (IBMX, 5 × 10−4 m; Serva‐Electrophoresis, Heidelberg, Germany), and indomethacin (2 × 10−4 m; Sigma‐Aldrich).

After VBP cell‐laden constructs were cultured in 48‐well plates. Depending on the experimental condition, DMEM/F‐12 supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin (100 U mL−1 penicillin, 0.1 mg mL−1 streptomycin) (cell culture media) or cell culture media supplemented with insulin (final concentration 1.7 × 10−6 m) (adipogenic maintenance media) was provided. Media was changed every other day.

Cell Viability Analysis and DNA Assay

Cell viability within hydrogel constructs was assessed using a live/dead cell staining kit (PromoKine, Heidelberg, Germany). Hydrogels were washed with PBS (Life Technologies) and incubated in 300 µL live/dead staining solution (4 × 10−6 m ethidium homodimer III, 1 × 10−6 m calcein acetoxymethyl ester) for 45 min at room temperature, on an orbital shaker. Constructs were imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX51/XC30, Hamburg, Germany) and post processed with Olympus cellSens Dimension imaging software and ImageJ.

Hydrogels were washed with PBS (Life Technologies), harvested in phosphate/saline buffer (5 × 10−2 m phosphate buffer, 2×10−3 m Na2EDTA × 2H2O, 2 m NaCl, pH = 7.4; all obtained from Carl Roth, Karlsruhe, Germany), homogenized via TissueLyser II (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) at 25 Hz for 5 min and sonicated (Sonopuls; Bandelin electronic, Berlin, Germany). Total DNA content was determined using the Quant‐iT PicoGreen DNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Waltham, USA) according to manufacturer's instructions and subsequent fluorometric measurement at an excitation wavelength of 480 nm and an emission wavelength of 520 nm (Tecan GENios pro; Tecan, Crailsheim, Germany).

Histological and Immunohistochemical Analysis

Cell‐laden hydrogels were harvested and fixed in 3.7% buffered formalin at 4 °C overnight, embedded in Tissue Tek O.C.T. (Sakura Finetek, Torrance, USA) and incubated in wet chambers overnight at room temperature. Samples were then shock frozen in liquid nitrogen and cryosectioned (7 µm per section; cryostat CM 3050S, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).To visualize accumulated lipids, cryosections were rehydrated with PBS, stained with Oil Red O solution (3 mg mL−1 Oil Red O in 60% isopropanol; Sigma‐Aldrich) for 6 min at room temperature, washed with PBS and finally mounted with mounting medium (Glycergel; Sigma‐Aldrich).

For immunohistochemical analysis, cryosections were washed three times with 0.2% triton X‐100 (Sigma‐Aldrich) in PBS. After three additional washing steps in PBS, cryosections were blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin in PBS for 1 h at room temperature and incubated in a humidified chamber overnight with the primary antibody anti‐perilipin1 (PA5‐7202, 1:400; Thermo Fisher Scientific) diluted in 1% BSA in PBS. After three washing steps with PBS, sections were incubated with the secondary antibody (goat anti‐rabbit Alexa488, ab150077, 1:400, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) diluted in 1% BSA in PBS. Sections were washed three times with PBS and mounted with DAPI mounting medium ImmunoSelect (Dako, Hamburg, Germany).

All sections were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX51/DP71, Hamburg, Germany) and post processed with Olympus cellSens Dimension imaging software and ImageJ.

Quantitative Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT‐PCR)

For gene expression analysis, cell‐laden hydrogels were homogenized in TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies) using a TissueLyser II (Qiagen) at 25 Hz for 5 min, followed by RNA isolation according to the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA synthesis from total RNA was performed using the High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, Germany). qRT–PCR was carried out using self‐designed, intron spanning primer pairs (Table 3 ). Mesa Green qPCR MasterMix Plus MeteorTaq polymerase (Eurogentec, Seraing, Belgium) was used for detection. Resulting gene expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene eukaryotic translation elongation factor 1 alpha (EF1α; self‐designed). The 2−ΔΔCT method was used to determine the x‐fold increase of expression levels of each gene.[ 50 ] Obtained values were further normalized to the respective day 0 sample values.

Detection of Secreted Adiponectin and Leptin of Cell‐Laden Hydrogels after VBP

Supernatants of adipocytes‐laden hydrogel cultures were collected at day 2 and 6, after VBP and stored at −20 °C until further use. Adiponectin and leptin concentrations were determined utilizing human adiponectin/Acrp30 DuoSet ELISA and human leptin/Acrp30 DuoSet ELISA (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA), respectively, according to the manufacturer's instructions. Statistical Analysis

Physicochemical and mechanical analysis of the gelAGE‐based hydrogels over time were performed in four replicates (N = 4). Live‐dead and PCR analysis of the ASC‐laden hydrogels were performed in triplicates (N = 3). The results were reported as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad software, Boston, USA). Assumption of normality was checked with a Shapiro‐Wilk test. The comparison between time points for both adipogenic markers (adiponectin and leptin) for low and high concentration resin, respectively, was conducted through a paired t‐test. Multiple comparison between the experimental groups were conducted through a two‐way ANOVA, with a Tukey post‐hoc test. Values of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
