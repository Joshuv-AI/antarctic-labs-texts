m⁶A-associated GAS6 expression is associated with pathogenic activation of fibroblast-like synoviocytes in rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Shu Li, Feng Li, MengYuan Xu, Nan Cheng, Lei Wan, XiaoJun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by persistent synovial inflammation and progressive joint destruction. Although N6-methyladenosine (m⁶A) modification is increasingly recognized as an important epitranscriptomic regulatory mechanism, its role in RA remains incompletely understood. This study integrated transcriptomic analysis, m⁶A methylome profiling, and functional experiments to investigate the potential role of m⁶A-associated growth arrest-specific 6 (GAS6) in RA. Synovial tissue samples from patients with RA and healthy controls (HC) were subjected to mRNA sequencing, and integrative analyses were performed using public m⁶A epitranscriptomic datasets. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and serum samples were collected from patients with RA and HC subjects. GAS6 and MER proto-oncogene, tyrosine kinase (MERTK) expression levels were measured by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and western blotting, and interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) levels were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Small interfering RNA (siRNA) was used to silence GAS6 and MERTK in RA fibroblast-like synoviocytes (RA-FLS). Cell viability, apoptosis, migration, and protein expression were evaluated using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), flow cytometry, wound-healing assays, immunofluorescence staining, and western blotting. Integrated multi-omics analysis identified 323 genes with both differential expression and altered m⁶A modification, with enrichment in efferocytosis-related pathways, T helper 17 cell differentiation, and cellular senescence. Among these candidates, GAS6 showed prominent m⁶A hypermethylation and significant upregulation, suggesting a potential epitranscriptomic association. GAS6 was also linked to the efferocytosis-related receptors MERTK and AXL in pathway and interaction analyses. In patients with RA, GAS6 and MERTK expression levels were significantly increased and were associated with inflammatory burden and disease activity. In vitro, silencing GAS6 or MERTK attenuated several pathogenic features of RA-FLS, including enhanced proliferation, migration, pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and resistance to apoptosis. Integrative omics analysis suggests that aberrant m⁶A modification exhibits a potential epitranscriptomic association with activation of the GAS6/MERTK signaling axis in RA. As a hypothesis-generating study, these findings provide new insights into the context-dependent role of the m⁶A-associated GAS6/MERTK axis in RA and suggest that this axis may represent a potential biomarker or therapeutic target warranting further mechanistic investigation. Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic autoimmune disease characterized by persistent synovial inflammation, progressive cartilage destruction, and bone erosion1. It affects a significant proportion of the population worldwide and imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden. Although the advent of disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs and biologic therapies has improved clinical outcomes, a considerable number of patients still fail to achieve sustained clinical remission, highlighting the complex and heterogeneous nature of RA2. Within the inflamed joint microenvironment, fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) play a central and aggressive pathogenic role3. Under chronic inflammatory conditions, FLS undergo profound phenotypic changes, acquiring tumor-like features such as excessive proliferation, enhanced migration, resistance to apoptosis, and robust production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and matrix-degrading enzymes4. These properties allow FLS to actively drive synovial hyperplasia and joint destruction. However, the upstream regulatory mechanisms that sustain this pathogenic stromal activation remain incompletely understood.

Efferocytosis, the process by which apoptotic cells are recognized and cleared by phagocytes, is essential for maintaining tissue homeostasis and orchestrating the resolution of inflammation5. Defective efferocytosis leads to the accumulation of dying cells, secondary necrosis, and the continuous amplification of autoimmune responses. The TAM family of receptor tyrosine kinases—comprising TYRO3, AXL, and MER proto-oncogene, tyrosine kinase (MERTK)—serves as a major regulatory system governing apoptotic cell clearance and immune tolerance6. Growth arrest-specific 6 (GAS6) is a vitamin K-dependent secreted glycoprotein that functions as a universal bridging ligand for TAM receptors, participating in diverse biological processes including cell survival, proliferation, immune regulation, and efferocytosis. Among the TAM receptors, MERTK has been extensively characterized for its protective, anti-inflammatory signaling in professional phagocytes such as macrophages7. Interestingly, recent studies indicate that the dysregulation of the GAS6/TAM axis correlates with altered synovial inflammation and disease severity in RA8. Elevated levels of soluble TAM receptors have been detected in RA serum and synovial fluid, suggesting complex shedding and signaling dynamics9. While MERTK generally promotes resolution in myeloid cells, the functional consequences of aberrant GAS6/MERTK signaling in the stromal compartment (i.e., FLS) during RA pathogenesis remain poorly defined.

In recent years, N6-methyladenosine (m⁶A)—the most abundant internal modification in eukaryotic messenger RNA—has emerged as a crucial layer of epitranscriptomic regulation10. The dynamic deposition, removal, and recognition of m⁶A marks are coordinated by methyltransferases (“writers,” e.g., METTL3, METTL14), demethylases (“erasers,” e.g., FTO, ALKBH5), and specific binding proteins (“readers,” e.g., YTH domain family)11. Through these regulators, m⁶A modification dictates RNA splicing, export, translation, and decay. Increasing evidence reveals that m⁶A epitranscriptomic dysregulation is deeply intertwined with autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, particularly RA. Altered m⁶A machinery has been linked to macrophage polarization, T-cell imbalance, and FLS aggressiveness12,13. Nevertheless, little is known about whether m⁶A-related epitranscriptomic mechanisms govern the expression of genes associated with efferocytosis and inflammatory resolution in the RA microenvironment.

In the present study, we employed an integrative candidate-screening strategy combining synovial transcriptomic profiling, public m⁶A-related epitranscriptomic datasets, and functional experiments in RA-derived FLS. By intersecting differentially expressed genes with differentially m⁶A-modified transcripts, we identified GAS6 as a prominently altered candidate associated with efferocytosis-related signaling. We further investigated the clinical relevance of GAS6 and MERTK in patients with RA and explored their functional impact in RA-FLS. Our findings suggest that the m⁶A-associated GAS6/MERTK axis is linked to synovial inflammation and pathogenic activation of RA-FLS, providing a candidate framework for understanding how epitranscriptomic alterations may contribute to stromal pathology in RA. This integrative design was intended as a hypothesis-generating strategy for candidate prioritization.

Materials and methods

N6-methyladenosine sequencing data analysis

Data processing of raw reads was quality checked by using FastQC (v.0.10.1; https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) and trimmed for low-quality bases and adaptors if necessary by using Trim Galore (v.0.6.10; https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/). Clean reads were mapped to the human genome version 38 (GRCh38) by using HISAT2 (v.2.0.4; http://daehwankimlab.github.io/hisat2/). The mapped reads of each sample were assembled by using StringTie (v.1.3.1; https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/stringtie/) in a reference-based approach. The longest isoform was retained if a gene had more than one isoform. Methylated sites on RNAs (peaks) were identified by MACS software (v.2.2.7; https://github.com/macs3-project/MACS). Differential m⁶A modified peaks between IP and input samples were identified using exomePeak2 (v.1.16.2; https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/exomePeak2.html). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package (v.4.12.0;https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/clusterProfiler.html), and enriched terms were interpreted with consideration of multiple-testing-adjusted significance values where applicable.

mRNA sequencing data analysis

Data processing of raw reads was quality checked by using FastQC (v.0.10.1; https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) and trimmed for low-quality bases and adaptors if necessary by using Trim Galore (v.0.6.10; https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/). After quality control, sequence data were processed with STAR (v.2.7.11a; https://github.com/alexdobin/STAR) to generate read alignments with hg38. Raw read counts for annotated genes were obtained with featureCounts (v.2.0.6; https://subread.sourceforge.net/) with default settings, normalized and analyzed using DESeq2 (v.1.44.0; https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/DESeq2.html). Genes with a DESeq2-derived P value < 0.05 and a fold change ≥ 2 (|log2 fold change| ≥ 1) were considered differentially expressed. The top 50 genes ranked by P value were selected for clustering analysis. To understand the potential roles of differentially expressed genes, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package (v.4.12.0; https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/clusterProfiler.html) within the R environment (v.4.4.0; https://www.r-project.org/), and enriched terms were interpreted with consideration of multiple-testing-adjusted significance values where applicable.

Clinical data

This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Anhui University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No: 2023AH-52; Approval Date: July 27, 2023). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. This study included 15 patients with RA from the outpatient and inpatient departments of the Rheumatology Department at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui University of Chinese Medicine and 15 healthy controls (HC) recruited from the hospital’s health examination center (Table 1). Participants were enrolled between August and December 2023, and written informed consent was obtained from all subjects. RA diagnosis was based on the 2010 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) criteria14, together with the revised 1987 ACR classification criteria and scoring system15. Inclusion criteria were as follows: fulfillment of the ACR/EULAR classification criteria, age 18–60 years, provision of written informed consent, and good compliance. Exclusion criteria included severe comorbidities affecting the heart, brain, lungs, liver, kidneys, or hematopoietic system; other metabolic disorders; pregnancy or lactation; and severe joint deformities. Peripheral blood was collected from 15 patients with RA and 15 HC subjects for isolation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and serum. Synovial tissue samples were collected from three patients with RA and three age- and sex-matched control subjects and stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction and transcriptomic sequencing.

Cell culture and transfection

Human FLS and RA-FLS cells were purchased from iCell (Shanghai, China) and used according to the supplier’s instructions. The culture medium was discarded, and the cells were washed twice with PBS (Hyclone, Shanghai, China). Cells were then digested with 1 mL trypsin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C for 2 min. Once the cells became rounded, digestion was terminated with fresh medium. The cells were collected into 15-mL centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. Cells were passaged at a ratio of 1:1 or 1:2 every 2 days, and experiments were performed using cells at passage 7. For transfection, RA-FLS were seeded at 5 × 10⁵ cells/well in 6-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. When the cells reached 70%–80% confluence, transfection was performed using siRNAs targeting GAS6, MERTK, or a negative control (si-NC), as described below.

CCK-8

Cells from each group were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured in 5% CO₂ at 37 °C for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. Cell morphology was observed under an inverted microscope. At each time point, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution (BIOSS, Beijing, China) was added to each well, and cells were incubated for 2 h. A blank control was included, and absorbance (A) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Cell scratch assay

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested by trypsinization, followed by centrifugation and resuspension in fresh culture medium. The cell suspension was adjusted to a concentration of 5 × 10^5 cells per well and seeded into 6-well plates, which were incubated overnight in a 37 °C, 5% CO_2 incubator. Once the cells reached confluence, a scratch was made using a sterile pipette tip, aligned with pre-marked lines on the plate. After scratching, the cells were washed three times with PBS to remove any detached cells, and fresh serum-free medium was added. Images were captured at 0 h (0 h) to record the initial wound area. The cells were then cultured for an additional 24 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO_2 incubator, and images of the wound were taken again at 24 h to assess cell migration. Migration rate was calculated using ImageJ (v.1.53k; https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/).

Immunofluorescence staining and imaging

Human FLS and RA-FLS from the different experimental groups were cultured on coverslips for immunofluorescence staining. After aspirating the culture medium, the cells were washed three times with PBS-T (PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20; ebiogo, Hefei, China). The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, followed by three washes with PBS-T. Permeabilization was achieved by incubating the cells with 0.5% Triton X-100 at 37 °C for 30 min. After removing Triton X-100, the cells were washed three times with PBS-T. To block non-specific binding, cells were incubated with goat serum blocking solution at 37 °C for 30 min. After the blocking step, the blocking solution was aspirated without additional washing. Primary antibodies (GAS6, rabbit, 1:200, SanYing, Wuhan, China; TGF-β1, rabbit, 1:100; AXL, rabbit, 1:100; DOCK1, rabbit, 1:100; MERTK, rabbit, 1:200; Rac1, rabbit, 1:100, all from Bioss, Beijing, China) were added at the recommended dilutions, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS-T and incubated with the secondary antibody, Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (CY3, 1:400, ebiogo, Hefei, China), at 37 °C for 30 min, protected from light. Following removal of the secondary antibody, the cells were washed three times with PBS-T and mounted using an anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium containing DAPI. Fluorescent images were acquired using a digital slide scanner.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA synthesis was performed using 1.0 µL of RNA as template. Quantitative PCR was carried out with an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of amplification (95 °C for 20 s, 60 °C for 1 min). The PCR reaction mix (10 µL total volume) included: 5 µL of 2× SYBR Green Master Mix (Novoprotein, Suzhou, China), 1 µL each of 10 µmol/L forward and reverse primers, 1 µL of cDNA, and 2 µL of RNase-free water (Generay Biotech, Shanghai, China). Gene expression was quantified using the 2^-ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences used in this study are shown in Table 2.

Flow cytometry

Cells were collected by centrifugation, digested with EDTA-free trypsin, and then washed with cold PBS. Approximately 1–10 × 10⁵ cells were resuspended in 500 µL of 1× binding buffer, which was prepared by diluting 5× binding buffer with distilled water. To each tube, 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC (LinkBio, Hangzhou, China) and 10 µL of PI were added. After gently mixing, the cells were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 5 min. Apoptosis was analyzed by flow cytometry, and the apoptosis rate was calculated using FlowJo (v.10.10.0; https://www.flowjo.com/). To ensure reliable quantification, single-stained controls (Annexin V-FITC only and PI only) were used to calculate and apply the compensation matrix to minimize spectral overlap. The complete gating strategy, including initial FSC/SSC-based cell selection and subsequent singlet discrimination (doublet exclusion), is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1. The apoptosis rate was calculated as the sum of early apoptotic and late apoptotic cells.

ELISA

Serum was isolated from peripheral blood samples collected from the study subjects, and RA-FLS culture supernatants were collected from the corresponding experimental groups. The levels of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and TGF-β1 were measured using commercial ELISA kits (GeneMed, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blotting

Cultured cells and clinical PBMC samples were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing PMSF, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatants containing total protein were collected for subsequent analysis. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, with gels prepared as follows: stacking gel (2 ml) and separating gel (10 ml) using acrylamide, Tris, SDS, APS, and TEMED. Equal amounts of protein (5–10 µl) were mixed with 5X SDS sample buffer, boiled at 95 °C for 15 min, and loaded onto the gel. Electrophoresis was performed at 80 V for 1 h. Proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Bedford, MA, USA) using a semi-dry system, and transfer times were adjusted for specific proteins: GAS6 (50 min), MERTK (120 min), ERK (40 min), TGF-β1 (50 min), FAK (70 min), CRK (40 min), DOCK1 (140 min), AXL (60 min), Rac1 (25 min), and GAPDH (30 min). After transfer, membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 2 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies: GAS6 (1:1000, rabbit, bioss), MERTK (1:2000, rabbit, Abcam), ERK (1:1000, rabbit, CST), TGF-β1 (1:1500, rabbit, bioss), FAK (1:1000, rabbit, bioss), CRK (1:2000, rabbit, bioss), DOCK1 (1:2000, rabbit, Abcam), AXL (1:2000, rabbit, bioss), Rac1 (1:2000, rabbit, bioss), and GAPDH (1:2000, mouse, Zsbio). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:20,000, Zsbio) for 1.2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using an ECL kit (Thermo Fisher), and band intensities were quantified with ImageJ (v.1.53k; https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/). β-actin was used as the internal reference gene for qRT-PCR, whereas GAPDH served as the loading control for Western blotting, based on the optimized experimental conditions and the validated performance of the corresponding primers and antibodies in our laboratory.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (v.23.0; https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics) and GraphPad Prism (v.9.0; https://www.graphpad.com/). Schematic diagrams (Fig. 9) and final layouts, were assembled and finalized using Adobe Illustrator (v.28.6; https://www.adobe.com/products/illustrator.html). Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for experimental assays and as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, for clinical data. Because the clinical sample size was limited (n = 15 per group), non-parametric statistical methods were used for clinical comparisons, including the Mann–Whitney U test for two-group comparisons. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to evaluate associations between GAS6/MERTK expression and clinical or inflammatory parameters. ROC curve analysis was used to assess discriminatory performance. For in vitro experiments involving multiple groups, comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by an appropriate post hoc multiple-comparison test when the data met assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance; otherwise, the corresponding non-parametric test was applied. Western blot densitometric analyses were performed on independent experiments as specified in the corresponding figure legends. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Differential gene expression analysis in RA synovial tissue

Principal component analysis (PCA) of the transcriptome data revealed clear separation between the RA and control groups, indicating distinct gene expression profiles (Fig. 1A). Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, with significance thresholds of P < 0.05 and |log2(fold change)| > 1. Genes with log2(fold change) > 1 were classified as upregulated, whereas those with log2(fold change) < −1 were considered downregulated. A total of 1,123 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, including 454 upregulated and 669 downregulated genes (Fig. 1B, C). GO enrichment analysis showed that these DEGs were mainly involved in extracellular structure organization, mitotic nuclear division, and extracellular matrix organization in the biological process category; extracellular matrix, collagen-containing extracellular matrix, and condensed chromosome centromeric region in the cellular component category; and glycosaminoglycan binding, extracellular matrix structural constituent, and heparin binding in the molecular function category (Fig. 1D-F). KEGG pathway analysis showed that the DEGs were mainly enriched in pathways related to the cell cycle, PI3K-Akt signaling, cellular senescence, ABC transporters, p53 signaling, and Hippo signaling (Fig. 1G).

Intersection analysis of m⁶A-modified genes and mRNAs in RA

To investigate the role of RNA m⁶A modification in RA, we first profiled the differential m⁶A methylation patterns in PBMCs from 5 RA patients and 5 healthy controls using the publicly available dataset GSE193913. Applying thresholds of |Fold Change| > 1.5 and P < 0.05, we identified 262 significantly hypermethylated and 397 significantly hypomethylated m⁶A peaks in RA patients compared to controls, as visualized in the volcano plot (Fig. 2A). Concurrently, mRNA expression analysis revealed 797 upregulated and 457 downregulated DEGs in RA PBMCs, presented via volcano plot and heatmap (Fig. 2B, C). Subsequently, an intersection analysis between the differentially m⁶A-modified genes (DMGs) and DEGs was performed, unveiling 323 overlapping genes (Fig. 2D, E). To elucidate the biological significance of these intersecting genes, we conducted GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. GO analysis indicated that these genes were predominantly enriched in BP such as platelet degranulation, hemostasis, and blood coagulation. Regarding CC, they were primarily localized to the secretory granule lumen, cytoplasmic vesicle lumen, and vesicle lumen. For MF, significant enrichment was observed in cyclosporin A binding, enzyme inhibitor activity, and protein serine/threonine kinase inhibitor activity (Fig. 2F). KEGG pathway analysis further demonstrated that these intersection genes were significantly involved in critical pathways including Efferocytosis, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, Cellular senescence, FoxO signaling pathway, TGF-beta signaling pathway, and Th17 cell differentiation (Fig. 2G). These findings suggest that the genes co-regulated by both m⁶A modification and transcriptional changes are critically implicated in immune response, cellular homeostasis, and RA pathogenesis.

GAS6, an m⁶A-associated candidate, is linked to efferocytosis-related receptors MERTK and AXL

Based on the intersection analysis of m⁶A-modified genes and mRNA-seq data, we further analyzed the GSE193913 dataset and compared its differentially expressed genes with those identified in our synovial mRNA-seq dataset. This comparison revealed five commonly upregulated and five commonly downregulated genes (Fig. 3A). Among these candidates, GAS6 showed the most prominent alteration in the m⁶A modification analysis (P = 2.17256E-08). Further validation using the GSE181614 dataset confirmed significant upregulation of GAS6 in RA samples (Fig. 3B). KEGG pathway annotation indicated that GAS6 is positioned within an efferocytosis-related signaling network (Fig. 3C). In addition, pathway mapping and protein-protein interaction analysis demonstrated strong associations between GAS6 and the TAM family receptors AXL, MERTK, and TYRO3 (Fig. 3D). Taken together, these findings identify GAS6 as an m⁶A-associated candidate linked to efferocytosis-related pathways in RA. However, direct functional assessment of efferocytosis was beyond the scope of the present study.

Differential expression and clinical correlations of GAS6 and MERTK in RA patients

To validate the clinical relevance of GAS6 and MERTK in RA, PBMC and serum samples from patients with RA and healthy controls were analyzed by qRT-PCR, ELISA, and Western blotting (Fig. 4A–C). Results revealed significantly elevated expression levels of GAS6, MERTK, IL-1β, IL-6, and TGF-β1 in patients with RA compared with healthy controls (P < 0.001). Conversely, IL-10 levels were significantly reduced (P < 0.001). Western blotting further confirmed the upregulation of GAS6 and MERTK proteins in PBMC samples from patients with RA (Fig. 4C), with GAS6 exhibiting a more pronounced increase than MERTK (P < 0.05). Spearman’s rank correlation analysis demonstrated that GAS6 expression was significantly associated with disease activity and inflammatory burden in RA. Specifically, GAS6 showed positive correlations with MERTK, ESR, CRP, RF, CCP, DAS28-ESR, IL-1β, IL-6, and TGF-β1, whereas it was negatively correlated with IL-10. Likewise, MERTK expression was positively correlated with ESR, CCP, DAS28-ESR, IL-1β, IL-6, and TGF-β1, while showing a negative correlation with IL-10 (Table 3). To further evaluate their clinical potential, ROC curve analysis was performed to assess the predictive value of GAS6 and MERTK for disease activity in RA. GAS6 demonstrated a relatively strong predictive performance (AUC = 0.889, P < 0.001, cut-off value = 3.972, sensitivity = 100.0%, specificity = 80.00%). By contrast, MERTK showed a lower and statistically non-significant predictive value (AUC = 0.667, P = 0.120, cut-off value = 3.932, sensitivity = 80.00%, specificity = 53.33%) (Fig. 4D). Therefore, the ROC result for MERTK should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size. Overall, these findings suggest that GAS6 and MERTK are associated with RA pathogenesis, whereas GAS6 may have greater potential as an indicator of inflammatory activity and disease severity in this cohort.

GAS6 promotes proliferation, inflammation, and gene expression in RA-FLS

To elucidate the functional role of GAS6 in RA-FLS, we conducted integrated analyses of cell proliferation, cytokine secretion, and gene and protein expression. CCK-8 assays revealed that RA-FLS exhibited significantly higher viability than normal FLS (P < 0.001), whereas siRNA-mediated knockdown of GAS6 markedly suppressed RA-FLS proliferation (P < 0.001)(Fig. 5A), indicating a pro-proliferative role of GAS6. ELISA analysis of culture supernatants showed that GAS6 depletion significantly reduced the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TGF-β1 (P < 0.001), while notably increasing the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5B). At the transcriptional level, qRT-PCR demonstrated that silencing GAS6 markedly downregulated the mRNA expression of MERTK, TGF-β1, ERK1, and ERK2 (P < 0.01, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5C). Western blot analysis further confirmed corresponding decreases at the protein level, with GAS6 knockdown significantly lowering the expression of GAS6, MERTK, TGF-β1, and ERK (P < 0.05, P < 0.01, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5D). Collectively, these findings indicate that GAS6 is associated with a more pathogenic RA-FLS phenotype, characterized by increased proliferation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production together with reduced IL-10 levels, in parallel with altered expression of MERTK-related signaling components including ERK and TGF-β1.

GAS6 modulates TGF-β1 expression and cell migration in RA-FLS

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to assess the expression of GAS6 and TGF-β1 in normal FLS, RA-FLS, RA-FLS+siRNA-GAS6-NC, and RA-FLS+siRNA-GAS6. RA-FLS exhibited markedly increased fluorescence intensity of both GAS6 and TGF-β1 compared with normal FLS (P < 0.001), indicating significant upregulation in diseased cells. Silencing GAS6 substantially reduced the fluorescence signals of both proteins (P < 0.001) (Fig. 6A). These results demonstrate that GAS6 positively regulates TGF-β1 expression in RA-FLS. To evaluate the functional consequences of this regulation, a scratch-wound assay was performed to assess cell migration. RA-FLS displayed a significantly higher migration rate compared with normal FLS (P < 0.001), consistent with an activated phenotype. Knockdown of GAS6 markedly suppressed migration (P < 0.001) (Fig. 6B). Together, these findings indicate that GAS6 is linked to enhanced TGF-β1 expression and migratory capacity in RA-FLS, supporting its association with synoviocyte activation in RA.

MERTK modulates RA-FLS proliferation and inflammatory signaling

We explored the function of MERTK in RA-FLS by evaluating cell viability, inflammatory cytokine production, and key signaling molecules. Cell proliferation measured by CCK-8 assay was markedly enhanced in RA-FLS compared with normal FLS (P < 0.001). Upon MERTK knockdown, RA-FLS viability was significantly reduced (P < 0.001) (Fig. 7A). ELISA analysis revealed that RA-FLS secreted higher levels of IL-1β and IL-6 (P < 0.001) but lower levels of IL-10 (P < 0.001) compared with normal FLS, whereas MERTK silencing reversed these changes toward control levels (P < 0.001). TGF-β1 expression was partially normalized following MERTK knockdown (P < 0.01) (Fig. 7B). qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that mRNA levels of MERTK, FAK, CRK, DOCK1, AXL, and Rac1 were significantly increased in RA-FLS relative to controls (P < 0.05, P < 0.01, P < 0.001) and markedly downregulated after MERTK silencing (P < 0.05, P < 0.01, P < 0.001) (Fig. 7C). Consistent with these findings, Western Blotting revealed elevated protein expression of MERTK and its downstream molecules in RA-FLS(P < 0.001), which was significantly reduced upon MERTK knockdown (P < 0.05, P < 0.01, P < 0.001) (Fig. 7D). Collectively, these results indicate that MERTK is associated with proliferative and inflammatory phenotypes of RA-FLS, and its inhibition attenuates these pathogenic processes together with reduced expression of FAK, CRK, DOCK1, AXL, and Rac1. However, these downstream changes should not be interpreted as exclusively MERTK-specific, as other upstream regulators may also contribute in RA-FLS.

MERTK knockdown reverses protein upregulation and promotes apoptosis in RA-FLS

To examine the impact of MERTK knockdown on the expression of AXL, DOCK1, MERTK, and Rac1 proteins and on apoptosis in RA-FLS, we conducted immunofluorescence and flow cytometry analyses. Immunofluorescence data indicated a significant increase in the relative mean fluorescence density of AXL, DOCK1, MERTK, and Rac1 in the RA-FLS group compared to FLS (P < 0.001). Conversely, MERTK knockdown led to a marked decrease in the expression of these proteins relative to the RA-FLS group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 8A). These outcomes demonstrate that MERTK knockdown reverses the upregulation of the aforementioned proteins in RA-FLS, supporting an association between MERTK expression and the elevated levels of these proteins in RA-FLS. In apoptosis studies, flow cytometry showed a significantly reduced apoptosis rate in RA-FLS compared to FLS (P < 0.001), whereas MERTK knockdown significantly elevated the apoptosis rate relative to RA-FLS (P < 0.01) (Fig. 8B). Collectively, these findings indicate that MERTK knockdown promotes apoptosis in RA-FLS, suggesting that MERTK is associated with apoptosis resistance in these cells.

Discussion

Our study identifies a potential epitranscriptomic association in RA, in which altered m⁶A-related patterns are linked to increased GAS6 expression and pathogenic features of FLS. Through an integrated analysis of m⁶A-modified transcripts and differentially expressed genes in RA, we identified 323 intersecting candidates enriched in efferocytosis-related signaling, cellular senescence, and PI3K-Akt pathways. Among these, GAS6 exhibited prominent m⁶A hypermethylation and significant upregulation, accompanied by strong predicted interactions with its cognate receptors MERTK and AXL. Clinical validation demonstrated that GAS6 and MERTK expression levels were significantly elevated in patients with RA and correlated strongly with inflammatory burden and disease activity. Furthermore, in vitro functional assays revealed that silencing GAS6 or MERTK in RA-FLS attenuated excessive proliferation, migration, pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and apoptosis resistance. Collectively, these findings support a context-dependent association of the GAS6/MERTK signaling axis with pathogenic stromal behavior in RA.

The aggressive nature of FLS is a major driver of pannus formation and joint destruction in RA. Therefore, deciphering the molecular networks that sustain FLS hyperactivation is critical for developing stroma-directed therapies4. Recently, the epitranscriptomic regulation of FLS has garnered significant attention. For instance, RA-FLS-derived exosomes can modify the joint microenvironment by transferring circular RNAs that alter m⁶A-dependent gene expression in neighboring chondrocytes17. Within FLS themselves, the m⁶A writer WTAP has been shown to upregulate NLRP3 to promote pyroptosis18, while m⁶A readers like IGF2BP3 and IGF2BP2 regulate the stability of target transcripts (e.g., RASGRF1 and GSTM5) to dictate FLS proliferation and invasion19,20. Additionally, the demethylase FTO facilitates apoptosis escape and inflammatory signaling via NF-κB pathways21. Our study expands this growing framework by proposing that genes participating in efferocytosis-related signaling, specifically GAS6, may also be subject to m⁶A-associated dysregulation, thereby linking epitranscriptomic alterations to stromal cell survival and activation.

Crucially, the biological implications of the GAS6/TAM receptor system must be interpreted in a highly cell-context-dependent manner. In professional phagocytes such as macrophages, MERTK is widely recognized as a fundamentally anti-inflammatory and pro-resolving receptor. During canonical efferocytosis, MERTK activation contributes to apoptotic cell clearance and is associated with anti-inflammatory mediator production, including IL-105,22. Indeed, studies in experimental arthritis models indicate that the loss of MERTK in the myeloid compartment can exacerbate joint inflammation23. Conversely, excessive receptor shedding mediated by ADAM17 can generate soluble MERTK, which acts as a decoy to neutralize ligands and impair resolution24.

Our findings in RA-FLS do not contradict the protective role of MERTK in macrophages. Instead, they highlight that in the stromal compartment, the consequences of GAS6/MERTK signaling may diverge from those in myeloid cells. In hyperplastic FLS, enhanced GAS6/MERTK signaling appears to be associated with pro-survival, migratory, and pro-inflammatory phenotypes rather than immune resolution. This dichotomy is reminiscent of tumor biology, where MERTK overexpression on cancer cells promotes chemoresistance, survival, and metastasis, contrasting with its homeostatic role in tissue-resident macrophages. Therefore, our results suggest that indiscriminately activating or inhibiting TAM receptors in RA could yield mixed outcomes depending on the target cell population, emphasizing the need for cell-specific therapeutic strategies.

The therapeutic landscape of RA is increasingly exploring avenues to restore immune homeostasis by mimicking efferocytosis. Recent innovations utilizing apoptotic-mimicking nanovesicles or engineered macrophage membranes carrying “eat-me” signals have shown promise in skewing macrophages toward a regulatory M2 phenotype and preventing RA progression in murine models25–27. While enhancing MERTK function in macrophages via these nano-therapies is beneficial, our data caution that simultaneously hyperactivating the GAS6/MERTK axis in FLS might paradoxically support stromal hyperplasia. Additionally, while we observed that MERTK knockdown in RA-FLS altered the expression of FAK, CRK, DOCK1, AXL, and Rac1, these downstream motility and survival components can be influenced by multiple overlapping upstream receptors. Thus, our data reflect the involvement of MERTK-associated signaling within a broader, complex network of RA-FLS activation, rather than an exclusively MERTK-specific linear pathway.

Several important limitations in the present study must be acknowledged. First, the primary synovial transcriptomic analysis was based on a small cohort (3 RA vs. 3 controls), limiting statistical power and the ability to capture disease heterogeneity. Second, our multi-omics integration utilized a public PBMC-derived m⁶A dataset cross-referenced with synovial mRNA data; because epitranscriptomic landscapes are highly tissue- and cell-specific, PBMC m⁶A profiles may not perfectly mirror the modifications occurring in synovial FLS. Third, although GAS6 emerged as a hypermethylated and upregulated candidate, we did not directly validate m⁶A enrichment on the GAS6 transcript via m⁶A-RIP-qPCR, nor did we map specific modification sites or manipulate the upstream writers/erasers (e.g., METTL3 or FTO) to prove direct causality. Therefore, the relationship is strictly presented as an inferred bioinformatic association rather than an elucidated regulatory mechanism. Fourth, while efferocytosis-related pathways guided our candidate selection, we did not perform functional apoptotic cell engulfment assays, nor did we evaluate professional phagocytes. Consequently, any functional implications regarding actual efferocytosis clearance remain speculative and are strictly restricted to pathway-level associations.

Looking forward, future investigations should aim to directly bridge these gaps. Establishing the precise causal mechanism will require m⁶A-RIP-qPCR, site-directed mutagenesis, and targeted knockdown of m⁶A regulators to ascertain how GAS6 mRNA stability or translation is governed in RA-FLS. Moreover, advanced single-cell spatial transcriptomics and FLS-macrophage co-culture systems are necessary to dissect how GAS6/MERTK signaling differentially impacts stromal survival versus macrophage-mediated efferocytosis28. Ultimately, clarifying these compartment-specific roles will determine whether selectively targeting the epitranscriptomic regulation of the GAS6/MERTK axis holds viable translational potential for managing rheumatoid arthritis.

Conclusion

In summary, as a hypothesis-generating study based on correlative evidence, our data suggest that aberrant m⁶A-related modification is potentially associated with activation of the GAS6/MERTK axis in RA and with pathogenic phenotypes in RA-FLS (Fig. 9). Through integrated multi-omics analysis and functional validation, GAS6 was identified as a candidate molecule associated with inflammation, migration, proliferation, and apoptosis resistance in synoviocytes. These findings provide new insights into the context-dependent role of the m⁶A-associated GAS6/MERTK axis in RA and suggest that this axis may represent a potential biomarker or therapeutic target that warrants further cell-specific and mechanistic investigation.
