Effectiveness of teaching basic life support resuscitation using virtual reality – a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Piotr Konrad Leszczyński, Klaudia Weronika Jędral, Maria Malm, Bożena Muraczyńska, Grzegorz Kobuszewski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKnowledge and skills in Basic Life Support (BLS) resuscitation procedures are essential for the correct recognition of circulatory arrest and the implementation of rapid management, resulting in increased survival rate of the victim. Numerous techniques for teaching cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) are being used, yet there is insufficient data on the effectiveness of using virtual reality (VR). The aim of the study was to evaluate the short- and three-month didactic effect in teaching BLS procedures and chest compressions using modern virtual reality (VR) technology.MethodsA total of 205 subjects were included in the study, assigned by randomized controlled design to a study group (A) implementing VR training and a control group (B) participating in traditional training. The participant group consisted of 73.91% women and 26.09% men, with a mean age of 21.80 ± 5.46 years. The process was divided into two stages, which assessed the level of knowledge and skills immediately after the specific type of training, as well as three months later. Practical skills were assessed in detail according to a checklist. The authors conducted a series of statistical analyses to compare the groups and indicate relationships. A significance level of α = 0.05 was assumed for all analyses.ResultsThe mean score of the overall quality of resuscitation immediately after the training in case of VR course participants was M = 45.61% (SD = 34.13), while that of the traditional course participants was M = 67.0% (SD = 32.56), with p < 0.001. The level of knowledge obtained was found to be comparable (p = 0.269). High satisfaction levels were identified by participants in both groups. The three-month evaluation (three months after the training) showed no significant intergroup differences (p = 0.218). The mean quality of resuscitation was M = 60.32% for the traditional group and 54.32% for the VR group. In the long term, it is noteworthy that there was a significant decrease in the quality of chest compressions after traditional training (by 6.68 p.p.), wherein this parameter increased among VR course participants (by 8.67 p.p.).ConclusionsTeaching methods involving virtual reality to train CPR gain three-month effectiveness that is comparable to traditional training, despite the lack of this conclusion confirmed in short-term evaluation. Modern VR technologies make it possible to achieve optimal first aid teaching results, minimizing the participation of instructors, and ensure perfect repetition of procedures, which is applicable to teaching large groups with a high level of satisfaction.Trial registrationISRCTN registry, ISRCTN71238249, 23/10/2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07827-y.

Background

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) performed by witnesses to an incident is an extremely important part of the chain of survival [1]. Prompt and correct performance of the Basic Life Support (BLS) regimen increases the victim’s chances of survival up to 50–70% [2]. Out-Of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest (OHCA) is reported in about 420,000 cases per year in the United States, with about 275,000 cases reported in Europe [3]. The etiology of sudden circulatory arrest can vary, but is most often due to cardiogenic causes, meaning: caused by disorders of the vascular and circulatory systems [4]. There are studies updating the guidelines for persons without prior medical training, so that the algorithms developed would be both easy to remember and effective, bringing tangible benefit to those affected. Knowledge and skill in providing assistance according to the BLS algorithm includes assessing consciousness and breathing, recognizing OHCA symptoms, calling for help, starting CPR early and using an Automated External Defibrillator (AED). The CPR cycle, comprising of alternating 30 chest compressions and 2 rescue breaths, should be continued until medical services arrive. Sometimes, it is acceptable to perform only chest compressions while omitting providing inhalations, an action that may be technically or psychologically difficult for a witness to the emergency.

Teaching members of the public the principles of first aid, especially how to perform CPR, constitutes a key element at every education stage. Objective assessment of the victim’s condition will influence the correct diagnosis, calling appropriate emergency services and having the dispatcher provide adequate services and resources, in the form of paramedics, air ambulance, water lifeguards, firefighters, etc [5–11]. The quality of chest compressions performed by a bystander and training to perform resuscitation when fatigued are extremely important [12]. Traditional first aid training is based on classes conducted directly by the instructor using specialized training equipment - phantoms, enabling the formation of practical skills. However, over the last years, the development of various types of information technologies has introduced novel possibilities and forms of teaching, including in the field of resuscitation. Various aspects of medicine use multimedia, interactive and virtual reality modules [13–15]. Medical simulation methods as well as solutions using artificial intelligence are being dynamically developed [16–19].

The latest teaching methods use VR technologies to make event simulations more realistic, sometimes deceptively similar to the real environment. Visual and audio experiences allow one to get into the situational task and experience emotions, which can result in better memory of the activities performed. The authors defined the purpose of the study as an attempt to evaluate the short- and three-month effectiveness of the BLS teaching method using virtual reality technology, compared to training conducted by the traditional method.

Methods

The study was conducted in 2024 at the Center for Medical Simulation at the University of Siedlce (Poland), following a favorable review by the Research Ethics Committee (Resolution 6/2023). The study group consisted of two hundred and sixteen students and lecturers. The authors chose this group because of the possibility of selecting individuals to participate in the three-month evaluation. Both knowledge tests, practical tasks and training satisfaction evaluation surveys were evaluated. Informed consent was obtained from all study participants. Participants received a certificate of participation after completing the training. No one received financial compensation for taking part in the study. Clinical trial registration number: ISRCTN71238249, 10/09/2024. The research protocol and CONSORT checklist are provided in the supplementary files.

The research tool consisted of proprietary pre-test and post-test questionnaires containing 10 questions each, and a course satisfaction evaluation questionnaire consisting of 6 criteria. The knowledge tests included questions on first aid, divided into 5 thematic subcategories (BLS algorithm, airway clearing and assessment of the victim’s breathing, calling for help, chest compressions, rescue breaths). A maximum of 2 points could be earned in each thematic category. To assess practical skills, checklists were constructed taking into account a 0–1 point classification for activities from the BLS algorithm and on a 0–3 point scale for CPR performed. The test subjects worked on a medical simulator with Ambu® CPR Software to record CPR activities in detail. When assessing practical skills, attention was paid to safety assessment, yelling for help, assessing the state of consciousness, clearing the airways, calling the emergency medical services, removing clothing from the victim, starting CPR with chest compressions, the correct position for performing chest compressions, performing chest compressions with the palm of the hand and unjustified cessation of chest compressions. The overall quality of CPR, compression rate, compression depth and chest relaxation were also evaluated.

The chest compressions training lasted for 2 min, during the skills evaluation right after the training and three months after the course, the time for performing compressions was 1 min. The author’s questionnaire for evaluating respondents from the training included 6 categories of questions from the areas of: satisfaction with the course (Ethical Leadership Scale – ELS), usefulness and utility of the training (Perceived Usefulness – PU), ease of training use (Perceived Ease Of Use – PEOU), technical aspect (Patient Satisfaction Questionnaire – PSQ), substantive quality of the course (Psychological Climate Questionnaire – PCQ), and teaching effectiveness (Personal Teaching Efficacy – PTE) according to a five-point Likert scale [20].

Stage 1

In the first stage, all respondents completed a pre-test that diagnosed and determined their knowledge of first aid just before the training. They then attended a theoretical lecture on basic resuscitation procedures in adults. The lecture was given by a paramedic who discussed the principles of BLS procedures for 20 min. The knowledge test consisted of 10 questions, each with four answer options and only one correct answer. Pedagogues agree that such tests are a sufficiently reliable tool for evaluating knowledge [13]. Thematic scope concerned the procedure according to the Basic Life Support (BLS) scheme recommended by the European Resuscitation Council. After the theoretical part, the subjects were randomly divided into two groups using a dice-throwing method with controlled randomization, as the subjects completed a questionnaire with inclusion criteria before the training, and some of the subjects confirmed absolute contraindications (epilepsy) in the context of receiving training with VR technology. The questionnaire with inclusion criteria also included sociodemographic questions such as age, sex, place of residence and education. The exclusion criteria were age less than 16, lack of consent to the study, partial or no completion of questionnaires and tests, and failure to participate in the indicated form of training full time. Additional pre-assessment criteria included participation in first aid training, with the date of the most recent training provided and completed no later than within the last three years, the use of VR technology, any episodes of nausea or vomiting during the ten days preceding the study, pregnancy or suspected pregnancy, the presence of an implanted pacemaker, stimulator, cardioverter or other cardiac assist device, a diagnosis of epilepsy, mental disorders, or a history of motion sickness.

Relative contraindications to participating in training using VR technology included nausea and vomiting, pregnancy, having any heart-action-supporting device, mental disorders, or motion sickness. And the only absolute contraindication was epilepsy, which excluded the subject from participating in the VR training (in which case the subject was assigned to the control group).

The subjects in Group A were the control group and received training with the traditional method provided by instructor who discussed the course topics, showed the BLS algorithm and conducted practical training on a medical simulator with digital CPR quality assessment (Resusci Ann type). Respondents in Group B took a course using VR technology with special goggles and wearable wireless trackers. At the end of stage one, participants filled out a questionnaire to assess their satisfaction with the training.

Stage 2

In the second stage, which took place 3 months after the completion of stage one, respondents from both groups completed a post-test to assess their long-term level of knowledge after the training, and took part in a simulation scene during which their practical skills were assessed according to a prepared checklist. In both stages, the authors chose to teach and evaluate only chest compressions, without performing rescue breaths.

VR technology

Software called “4HELP-VR RESCUE SYMULATOR” was used during the training in Group B. The stand for this study was a space in one of the rooms of the Medical Simulation Center, isolated from external conditions. The equipment included a computer unit with a monitor, a phantom (an adult torso, Resusci Ann type) modified to record the quality of chest compressions, and mobile items such as four HTC VIVE TRACKER sensors (tracking devices to enhance the immersion and experience of virtual reality immersion) and VR goggles (HTC VIVE Headset, screen size 5.6”; refresh rate 90 Hz; weight 440 g; accelerometer; gyroscope) with headphones. Two trackers were placed against the simulator’s torso, and another two were attached to the subjects’ wrists so that the app could track hand movements while allowing the participant to perform effective chest compressions seamlessly. This allowed us to accurately measure the quality of compressions and detect the position of the hands on the simulator’s chest (Fig. 1).

The task began with mounting two trackers and VR goggles with headsets on the test subject. Once started, the program guided the participant through the calibration process. The calibration process involved following the instructions of a virtual guide - a paramedic who stayed with the participant throughout the course. The calibration took place in a virtual room, where there was a first aid kit, gloves and a door, after opening which the participant was carried to the scene (Fig. 2).

Fig. 2The scene of the incident as seen from the perspective of a simulation participant

The scene of the incident as seen from the perspective of a simulation participant

The incident scene comprised a street where a woman was lying. The virtual instructor informed what to do in the correct order (e.g., look around to make sure it is safe, approach the victim, check for consciousness, tilt the head, and then check breathing). The actions performed on the victim were physically performed on a phantom, so that the test subject had the sensation of actually touching a woman lying on the street visible in the virtual world. The system calibrated the simulator’s placement in the real training room with images generated in the VR world. Once that was done, one had to grab the virtual phone and call for help.

After calling for help, it was necessary to start CPR consisting solely of chest compressions. They were also performed on a real medical simulator, which was transformed into a realistic-looking victim in the virtual world. Chest compressions were performed for a period of time set by the examiner (there was complete freedom in the duration of compressions recorded). The time set for performing compressions during training was 2 min.

While performing them, the participant in the VR world saw a manometer showing the rate of compressions and a potentiometer indicating the depth of compressions. The visual feedback also provided arrow information about the need to increase the depth of compressions and relax the chest. The respondent also heard guidance from the virtual instructor, who continuously adjusted the rate, depth, or chest relaxation during CPR (Fig. 3). The HERO 1 Sensor used (a proximity device adapted to the design of various medical simulators on the market that collects information about the quality of the simulator’s chest compressions being conducted) allowed for the ongoing digital transmission of data from the simulator to the system.

Fig. 3Pressure gauge and potentiometer seen from the subject’s perspective while performing chest compressions

Pressure gauge and potentiometer seen from the subject’s perspective while performing chest compressions

As the real-life instructor finished measuring the quality of CPR, the participant saw an ambulance approaching, and a board with detailed information on the quality of chest compressions was displayed after a while. Some of the data showed included overall resuscitation outcome [%], compression depth [% and mean in mm], compression rate [% and mean/min], the total number of chest compressions performed, quality of correctly performed chest relaxations [%], and quality of correctly achieved compression depth [%].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Statistica 13.3 software [TIBCO Software Inc. (2017). Statistica (data analysis software system), version 13. http://statistica.io.] and Microsoft Excel 365. A significance level of α = 0.05 was assumed for all analyses. To characterize the study group and the results of BLS training, counts and percentages were used, in addition to descriptive statistics such as mean, standard deviation, median, minimum and maximum values. The data was additionally presented in bar charts, column charts and box-and-whisker plots.

Initially, the Shapiro-Wilk W test was used to test the normality of the distribution of continuous variables, as well as the normality of their distribution within individual groups. The test rejected normality in most cases. However, for large groups (N > 100), it was decided to use parametric tests. Then, an analysis was performed using the Student’s t-test to compare the two groups separated by training type in terms of a continuous variable. In addition, the Student’s t-test for dependent samples was chosen to compare the pre-test and post-test results, as well as short- and three-month effectiveness of BLS. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare traditional and VR training in terms of an ordinal or continuous variable for smaller groups (N < 100). Pearson’s Chi2 test was chosen to test the relationship between two nominal variables, i.e. the effectiveness of BLS management algorithm in both groups; when the expected counts were less than 5, Fisher’s exact test was used as an alternative. Finally, the change in the short- and three-month assessment of the effectiveness of BLS management algorithm was assessed using the McNemar test.

Results

Characteristics of the study group

A total of 216 people between the ages of 17 and 67 were enrolled in the study, with the study group involving predominantly young persons, with a mean age of 21.80 ± 5.46 years. The vast majority of participants were women (n = 159; 73.61%). Secondary education was declared by 184 people (85.19%). More than half of the respondents (n = 120; 55.56%) indicated rural areas as their place of residence, while only 5.56% lived in a city with a population of more than 100,000. One in three survey participants had completed a first aid course in the last few years, but no less than a year before the study. The “lifelong learning” strategy developed by the European Resuscitation Council stipulates that training must be repeated every 6–12 months to maintain an optimal level of skills and knowledge [21], therefore a minimum interval of one year since the last training was considered sufficient. The majority of participants (n = 152; 70.37%) attended BLS training for the first time during the ongoing study. The majority of persons had no contraindications to the VR method (n = 194; 89.81%), 15.74% of participants had relative contraindications (pregnancy, mental disorders, motion sickness), and one had absolute contraindications (epilepsy). 51.39% of the respondents took part in training using the VR method, while 48.61% took part in training using the traditional method. The training method was assigned randomly (by dice throwing). The exceptions were those with absolute contraindications to the VR method, i.e. epilepsy, who were redirected to the group receiving training by the traditional method for safety reasons. The entire study (2 stages) was completed by 94.91% (n = 205) of participants, which was the final inclusion criterion. Some participants were not present at the final stage of the study after 3 months. The characteristics of the study group are shown in Table 1.

Three-month effectiveness of training

The mean score for the post-test was 81.51 ± 11.97%, significantly higher than the mean score for the pre-test − 57.92 ± 16.33% (t=−18.643, p < 0.001). The average pre-test results in the group assigned to traditional training were 58.38 ± 16.82% and were similar to the VR training participants (57.48 ± 15.92%). The post-test results were slightly better in the case of traditional training − 82.50 ± 11.33% compared to 80.64 ± 12.49%. There were no significant differences in knowledge between Group A and B before the training (t = 0.406, p = 0.686) and after the training (t = 1.109, p = 0.269), confirming the unified form of content delivery in the traditional and VR methods.

Statistical analysis showed no significant differences in the effectiveness of CPR training in terms of CPR quality between participants in traditional and VR training three months after stage 1. However, it can be noted that the subjects who completed traditional training had a slightly higher overall quality of CPR, better depth of compressions and relaxation (Table 6; Fig. 6).

There were no statistically significant differences in the three-month effectiveness of CPR training on the principles of CPR between traditional and VR trainees. However, it was observed that subjects who completed traditional training were slightly more likely to correctly assess safety and establish an airway, while VR trainees never interrupted chest compressions (Table 7).

Trends in skill level changes in three-month assessment

Attention is drawn to the short- and three-month trend of the assessed resuscitation parameters and management of the BLS algorithm, depending on the form of training (traditional vs. VR training). Participants in the Virtual Reality course demonstrated a statistically significant improvement, according to the Student’s t-test, in CPR quality three months after the first exam for all parameters: Overall quality of resuscitation (t = −2.141, p = 0.034), Compression rate (t = −2.571, p = 0.012), Compression depth (t = −2.450, p = 0.016), and Relaxation (t = −2.829, t = 0.006). On the other hand, the change in these parameters among participants in the traditional training turned out to be statistically insignificant (p > 0.05).

Furthermore, VR training demonstrated significant improvements in the effectiveness of the BLS management algorithm, according to the McNemar test, regarding Correct position for performing compressions (p = 0.003), Compressions performed with the palm of the hand (p = 0.012), Safety assessment (p = 0.011), Yelling for help (p = 0.025), Removing victim’s clothing (p = 0.004), and Calling for help (p = 0.002). Only the changes in the Assessment of consciousness (p = 0.055) and Clearing the airways (p = 0.627) were statistically insignificant. In the case of Unjustified cessation of compressions, too few participants experienced a change to perform the McNemar test. Meanwhile, the group trained by the traditional method showed downward trend after three months in Compressions performed with the palm of the hand (p = 0.006) and Removing victim’s clothing (p < 0.001). Effectiveness of traditional and VR training depending on demographic variables

Statistical analysis showed no significant relationship between age and participants’ post-training knowledge in groups A and B (p = 0.951). On the other hand, sex determined the results of the knowledge test, as women had a significantly higher score (Me = 90.0%) than men (Me = 80.0%) three months after receiving the training (p = 0.038). Short-term practical skills in the resuscitation procedures performed did not show sex-based relationship, yet it can be noted that men showed better depth of compressions (51.75 vs. 40.59) and overall quality of resuscitation (50.66 vs. 43.13) than women, while women had better compression rates (34.74 vs. 22.06) and relaxation (38.12 vs. 29.81). In the three-month evaluation, men were found to have significantly better depth of compressions (p = 0.029) and mean depth of compressions (p = 0.043), while women were found to have significantly better use of relaxation (p = 0.001). A series of analyses did not show a statistically significant relationship between place of residence and education in relation to acquired knowledge or skills in both short- and three-month assessments in both groups of respondents.

Discussion

Evaluating the most effective methods for teaching the principles of BLS and CPR proves to be a challenge for researchers around the world. The numerous educational programs, state-of-the-art technological solutions, and psychological aspects woven into medical simulations are designed to shorten the didactic process, increase participant satisfaction, and improve the long-term effectiveness of memorized activities. Participant satisfaction has a multifaceted impact on knowledge acquisition and readiness to put acquired skills into practice. In the study conducted, satisfaction analysis among trainees was divided into several aspects: overall satisfaction, usefulness and usability of the training, ease of use, technical issues, content and effectiveness. As a result, all respondents declared a very high level of satisfaction with the classes, especially with regard to the usefulness of the training (87.04% of people indicated the maximum rating). Participants perceive real value in the skills acquired at the training, which may influence their further willingness to take courses to improve and deepen the skills they have acquired.

During the evaluation of the training, respondents had the opportunity to post their written comments and feedback. It was noted that some respondents paid attention to the atmosphere in which the training was conducted and the aesthetic aspect of the training instructors. Another issue may be that training using virtual reality technology may have high interest and appeal especially among young people. Nevertheless, the respondents were least satisfied with the ease of training implementation (63.89%), and this aspect was rated worse by participants in VR classes. Some studies, e.g. as reported by Hubail et al., do not confirm this relationship, as the researchers indicated that participants in their study using VR were very satisfied with the ease of use [22]. Similarly, the ease of training use and overall satisfaction with attending the course were rated similarly highly by participants in the Issleib et al. study, Perron et al. [23, 24]. Presumably, this could have been due to technical problems that occurred during the training (temporary problems during calibration, trackers disconnecting during the practical task, or distorted images during the study (e.g., inadequate, impeding or distracting the participants’ perception of the virtual rescuer-instructor). Some VR trainees also reported a problem with visual defect that made it difficult for them to participate actively during the study without wearing contact lenses, therefore some of the subjects participated in the study with contact lenses over which they wore VR goggles and headphones. Assessment of training satisfaction based on participant self-assessments was only one element of course evaluation. It remains subjective and less reliable compared to the practical impact of the training, which was assessed by designated examiners.

The study found that the short-term quality of traditional training was significantly higher in both the quality of CPR and the BLS regimen, compared to training conducted using virtual reality technology. Participants in traditional training achieved higher overall quality of CPR, had a more correct technique for chest compressions, higher mean depth of compressions, better chest relaxation, and were more likely to correctly yell for help and clear the airways. This may have been due to the fact that the respondents had the opportunity for ongoing interaction with the instructor and a direct opportunity to correct mistakes right away during traditional training. The group implementing VR training only received feedback on chest compressions technique from phantom readings. The participant’s body position, the position of the wrist and finger weave, the words spoken when yelling and summoning help - these were aspects that the VR system did not verify. This relationship is somewhat confirmed by a study by Semeraro et al. which showed that VR trainees achieved similar chest compression rates and worse depth of compressions and overall CPR quality score compared to traditional trainees [25]. However, that publication did not report the absolute compression depths, and the observed difference between VR and conventional training was considerably less than in the present work, averaging 3.7 mm. The constant advantage of traditional training is the role of a vigilant instructor, which allows for continuous correction of errors made and their short-term memorization. According to the study authors, the problematic issues that have been mentioned can be addressed by further development of virtual reality and augmented reality technologies. The economic aspects of implementing the VR model should be carefully analyzed in comparison to the cost of traditional classes. Well-built software and optimally selected technical tools can prove to be more efficient and reliable as an element supporting traditional methods.

A completely new light was shed on the study by analyzing Stage 2, which is an assessment of participants’ three-month knowledge and skills. Three months after training Groups A and B, there were no significant differences between participants. Thus, both methods of teaching the BLS regimen were shown to have comparable three-month efficacy. The authors’ attention was also drawn to the upward trend in most criteria of practical skills in the VR group in the three-month evaluation. The result was not replicated among the representatives of traditional method training, as none of the parameters showed an upward trend, and two even dropped significantly. To confirm the observed effect, future studies should also include an evaluation of the effectiveness of training 6 months after the training. This aspect was investigated by researchers Castillo et al., Nas et al., whose study showed that 6 months after the course, no significant differences were seen in the quality of overall CPR and depth of chest compressions, among other things, between traditional and VR training [26, 27].

The foregoing aspects of short-term and three-month effectiveness comparing the two types of training, indicate that they are effective, but VR technology should be modified and developed to be at the same effectiveness level as traditional training, and in this form can constitute a more easily accessible, modern, excellent addition [28, 29], which stands out for its attractiveness especially among young people [30, 31].

Study limitations

The main limitation was the lack of some aspects in the software used in the study, namely ventilation and the ability to use an automated external defibrillator (AED). In such a situation, it would be appropriate to improve the software by adding both the ventilation aspect and the use of an AED, so that subjects can learn the entire basic resuscitation regimen along with cardiopulmonary resuscitation in a cycle of 30 compressions to 2 inhalations, which currently results in the best proven effectiveness in the context of unconscious and non-breathing persons. Another aspect was confounding factors that could affect the final outcome. This includes the study group, in which the use of e-learning materials on first aid prior to training cannot be excluded. The questionnaire survey only verified participation in on-site training. During the VR-based classes, some people reported problems with visual acuity. This was an aspect not previously anticipated by the authors of the questionnaire and the inclusion criteria. It can be assumed that people with visual impairment, which was previously unnoticed by the instructors, experienced discomfort when using the VR goggles, causing reductions in perception and didactic effect. 216 people took part in the survey, but only 205 participants were ultimately included, thus the undertaking should be considered a pilot study. The study focuses only on chest compressions. Key BLS elements like rescue breaths and AED use were not evaluated.

Conclusion

The obtained results allowed us to achieve the research goal of determining the short- and three-month effectiveness of VR-based BLS training compared to traditional training. The virtual reality teaching method achieved comparable three-month effectiveness to traditional training, despite the lack of confirmation of this conclusion in the short-term evaluation. The three-month evaluation of the VR training effectiveness indicated a significant increase in BLS quality across most of the assessed criteria, while the group trained using the traditional method did not demonstrate an increase in any of the parameters studied, and the results of two parameters significantly deteriorated. The authors found no significant differences in satisfaction between the groups. The need to improve the technical specifications of VR tools to make them more accessible to participants was emphasized. Considering the numerous benefits of using virtual reality in first aid training, their implementation should be considered as a promising alternative to traditional forms.
