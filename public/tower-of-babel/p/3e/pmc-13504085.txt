Needle-Assisted Minimally Invasive Repair of Achilles Tendon Rupture: The Namira Technique

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Current evidence suggests that minimally invasive and percutaneous repair techniques, including the Percutaneous Achilles Repair System (PARS; Arthrex) and ACHILLON System (Integra LifeSciences Corporation), achieve functional outcomes comparable to open repair while reducing wound-related complications and infections.1,2 However, these techniques require specialized instrumentation and jig systems, which may be costly and not readily available in resource-limited hospitals, rural centers, or low-resource surgical environments. 3

In geographically dispersed health care systems, access to advanced implants and dedicated surgical devices may be uneven. Surgeons practicing in such settings often require practical and reproducible techniques that preserve the principles of minimally invasive repair without depending on specialized equipment. 4 Therefore, an accessible technique using widely available instruments may help expand the feasibility of minimally invasive Achilles tendon repair.

This technical note describes the Needle-Assisted Minimally Invasive Repair of the Achilles, or NAMIRA, technique. The technique uses an 18-gauge intravenous catheter as a percutaneous suture-passing guide and standard nonabsorbable braided sutures, which were selected for their wide availability, to achieve minimally invasive repair of acute Achilles tendon rupture.

Methods

Indications

This technique is indicated for acute, closed midsubstance Achilles tendon ruptures with adequate tendon quality and repairable tendon ends, particularly in settings where a minimally invasive approach is desired but dedicated Achilles tendon repair systems are not available.

Contraindications

Contraindications for this technique include chronic Achilles tendon rupture, large tendon defects, insertional avulsion injuries, open injuries, active local infection, severe soft tissue compromise, poor tendon quality, revision cases, and reruptures requiring reconstruction or tendon augmentation. In such cases, alternative procedures such as open repair, graft augmentation, or tendon transfer should be considered.

Preoperative Evaluation and Positioning

Acute Achilles tendon rupture typically presents with a sudden popping sensation, posterior ankle pain, weakness during push-off, and a positive Matles and Thompson test. Although diagnosis is usually clinical, MRI may be obtained to evaluate rupture characteristics and tendon quality when planning minimally invasive repair.

The patient is positioned prone with appropriate padding. Following anesthesia and prophylactic intravenous antibiotics, a thigh tourniquet is inflated to 220 to 260 mm Hg. The rupture site, calcaneal insertion, and planned incision sites are marked preoperatively (Figure 1).

Preoperative marking of the Achilles tendon rupture site and planned mini-open incision. The palpable tendon defect and intended incision landmarks are outlined before surgical repair.

Surgical Technique

A transverse incision measuring approximately 2 to 3 cm is made over the palpable tendon defect. Dissection is carried carefully through the subcutaneous tissue, and the paratenon is incised longitudinally. The ruptured tendon ends are identified and mobilized. A periosteal elevator is used to create a plane between the tendon and surrounding peritendinous tissue both proximally and distally.

The NAMIRA technique employs an 18‑gauge intravenous catheter as a percutaneous suture‑passing guide. A 2‑0 monofilament polypropylene suture (Prolene; Ethicon) is inserted into the lumen of an 18‑gauge intravenous catheter. The free end of the suture is bent backward and held externally by the surgeon while the intravenous catheter is advanced transversely through the proximal tendon stump, remaining adjacent to the tendon substance and directed with minimal lateral deviation to reduce the risk of sural nerve injury, until its distal tip emerges on the opposite side with the suture forming a loop (Figure 2).

Percutaneous suture passage using the NAMIRA technique. A 2-0 monofilament polypropylene suture (Prolene; Ethicon) is advanced into the 18-gauge intravenous catheter lumen and bent backward, before being passed through the proximal tendon stump, forming a distal loop as a shuttle.

This loop serves as a shuttle for subsequent suture. A no. 2 braided polyester suture (Ethibond; Ethicon) is then introduced through this shuttle loop, after which the polypropylene suture is withdrawn from the opposite side, relaying the braided polyester suture across the tendon (Figure 3).

Tendon-suture relaying. A no. 2 braided polyester suture (Ethibond; Ethicon) is introduced through the polypropylene shuttle loop, which is then withdrawn from the opposite side to relay the braided polyester across the tendon. The maneuver is repeated at a second transverse level within the proximal stump to create paired suture limbs.

This maneuver is performed at 2 or 3 transverse levels in the proximal tendon stump to create paired suture limbs, which are subsequently retrieved using a mosquito clamp or right-angle clamp through the incision to maintain a fully internalized construct (Figure 4).

Internal retrieval of suture limbs. The externally exiting suture limbs are retrieved internally through the limited incision using a mosquito or right-angle clamp, ensuring that the final suture construct remains within the tendon substance.

The procedure is repeated in the distal tendon stump using the same catheter-assisted shuttle technique (Figure 5). After all sutures have been passed and retrieved internally, the tendon ends are approximated by sequentially tensioning the sutures (Figure 6). The corresponding proximal and distal suture limbs are then tied using simple knots with paired suture limbs on each side of the tenorrhaphy to achieve stable tendon apposition, resulting in a construct that follows the same fundamental principle as the Achillon technique.

The same percutaneous suture-passing technique is repeated through the distal tendon stump to complete the suture configuration.

Completed suture placement through the proximal and distal tendon stumps prior to final tensioning. Each corresponding suture pair is tied using simple knots to restore tendon continuity and apposition, after which the incision is closed in a standard fashion.

The repair is assessed clinically by restoration of resting ankle tension and improvement of the Matles and Thompson tests (Figure 7). 5 The paratenon and subcutaneous tissue are closed in layers when possible, using a braided absorbable suture (Vicryl; Ethicon) with simple knots, followed by skin closure. A sterile dressing is applied, and the ankle is immobilized in plantarflexion using a posterior splint.

(A) Preoperative and (B) postoperative clinical assessment demonstrates restoration of resting ankle tension after the NAMIRA technique.

Postoperative Protocol

The ankle is immobilized in plantarflexion and kept non-weight-bearing for 3 weeks, followed by progressive weight bearing in a walking boot until 6 weeks. Range of motion and strengthening exercises are initiated thereafter, with transition to regular footwear at approximately 8 weeks. Return to daily activities is generally allowed after 12 weeks and return to sports after 16 weeks, subject to clinical assessment.

Results

The described technique was performed in 3 patients, with 2 cases of acute Achilles tendon rupture and 1 rerupture case, which comprised our entire initial experience with this novel approach. At the latest follow-up, the 2 acute rupture cases were evaluated at 9 months postoperatively and demonstrated excellent functional outcomes, with ATRS of 98 and 95, respectively. The rerupture patient was a 36-year-old woman whose primary repair had been performed elsewhere using an open Krackow suture technique, with an ATRS of 77 at 28 months postoperatively. No rerupture following the index procedure, wound complications, or sural nerve–related symptoms were observed in any patient during the follow-up period.

Discussion

Minimally invasive Achilles tendon repair has been developed to reduce wound-related complications and soft tissue disruption while maintaining satisfactory tendon apposition and functional outcomes. 6 However, these techniques often require specialized instrumentation that increases cost and may not be readily available in some health care settings, including Indonesia, where such devices are not reimbursed by the national health insurance system, creating a need for an alternative minimally invasive technique using readily available instruments. 3 The NAMIRA technique addresses this need by using standard materials and avoiding instrument modification or direct tendon grasping, as described in the modified ring forceps technique. 7

The NAMIRA technique offers a simple, jigless alternative using an 18-gauge intravenous catheter for controlled percutaneous suture passage. A 2-0 polypropylene suture serves as a shuttle to deliver a no. 2 braided polyester suture through the tendon, allowing reproducible minimally invasive tendon repair with readily available operating room materials and without specialized instrumentation (Figure 8). 8 The suture construct employed in the NAMIRA technique is conceptually comparable to those used in established minimally invasive Achilles tendon repair methods such as PARS and Achillon. All 3 approaches involve multiple suture passes through the proximal and distal tendon stumps, creating several fixation points that help distribute tensile loads across the repair while limiting soft tissue disruption. Among these techniques, the NAMIRA configuration most closely resembles the Achillon method, which also uses multiple transverse suture passes through the tendon. However, whereas PARS and Achillon depend on specialized jig-based devices to guide and standardize suture placement, NAMIRA uses an 18-gauge intravenous catheter as a freehand suture-passing instrument together with standard nonabsorbable sutures. As a result, the final suture configuration can be adapted according to surgeon preference and tendon characteristics. However, this may be particularly advantageous in resource-limited settings where dedicated Achilles repair systems are unavailable.

Comparison with established minimally invasive Achilles tendon repair methods. The NAMIRA method follows the principle of minimally invasive repair and ultimately recreates the suture configuration illustrated on the right, which is characteristic of Achillon device–assisted repair.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the technique still involves semi-blind percutaneous suture passage, and the risk of sural nerve injury cannot be eliminated. Careful attention to suture trajectory, limited lateral soft tissue dissection, and controlled catheter placement are essential (Table 1). 9 Second, this report includes only 3 preliminary cases and should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical superiority. Third, no biomechanical testing was performed to determine the strength of the construct compared with established techniques. Finally, the reproducibility of the technique among surgeons with different levels of experience has not yet been evaluated.

Pearls and Pitfalls.

Further cadaveric, biomechanical, and comparative clinical studies are required to assess the safety, fixation strength, learning curve, and long-term outcomes of the NAMIRA technique. Formal cost and comparative analyses are beyond the scope of this Technical Note and warrant future investigation.

Conclusion

The NAMIRA technique is a simple and accessible method for minimally invasive repair of acute Achilles tendon rupture. This jigless technique may be useful in settings where dedicated Achilles repair systems are unavailable. Further biomechanical testing and larger clinical studies are needed to validate the safety, reproducibility, and long-term outcomes of this technique.
