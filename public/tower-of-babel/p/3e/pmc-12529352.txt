Doing it together: Qualitative study on barriers and facilitators to dyadic internet interventions for interrole conflict
Authors: Ewelina Smoktunowicz, Jan Maciejewski, Magdalena Lesnierowska, Justyna Ziolkowska, Marta Roczniewska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Some psychosocial challenges are inherently relational and may be addressed more effectively with dyadic rather than individual interventions. Although uptake and engagement of internet interventions have been widely studied, existing evidence focuses on individually oriented programmes, leaving their potential for dyadic phenomena—such as work–family conflict (WFC) and family–work conflict (FWC)—largely unexplored. To identify barriers and facilitators specific to a dyadic internet intervention aimed at reducing interrole conflict, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 20 heterosexual, dual-earner couples (N = 40). Thematic analysis revealed five key themes related to potential participation in such an intervention. Motivation for uptake was tied to recognized need, curiosity, and belief in efficacy. Involving both partners promised relational benefits yet introduced scheduling difficulties, highlighting the challenge of “doing it together.” Participants felt ambivalent about disclosing personal matters online, with subtle gendered differences in willingness to share. They also emphasized the importance of flexible, engaging, and personalized pedagogical design. Finally, participants viewed the online format ambivalently—while time constraints were often raised, they were frequently mitigated by the flexibility of access. These findings suggest that effective dyadic internet interventions for WFC/FWC must balance individual and joint activities, incorporate gender-sensitive communication pathways, and tailor content to different couple profiles (e.g., with or without children). Such efforts may enhance engagement and implementation feasibility.

Introduction

Internet interventions—whether delivered in clinical (e.g., Carl et al., 2019; Hedman-Lagerlöf et al., 2023; Karyotaki et al., 2021) or non-clinical settings (Carolan et al., 2017; Stratton et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2024)—have traditionally been developed for individual users, with a few notable exceptions (e.g., Le et al., 2023). This remains the case despite growing recognition that some psychological challenges are inherently dyadic in nature. One such issue is conflict between work and family roles, a well-documented occupational stressor that has garnered substantial attention over the past decades. As Greenhaus and Beutell (1985) define it, it is “a form of interrole conflict in which the role pressures from the work and family domains are mutually incompatible in some respects. That is, participation in the work (family) role is made more difficult by virtue of participation in the family (work) role” (p.77). This bidirectional conflict may stem primarily from either the family domain (family-work conflict; FWC) or the work domain (work-family conflict; WFC; Michel et al., 2011), but its consequences extend across all areas of life (Amstad et al., 2011; Nohe et al., 2015).

At the individual level, interrole conflict has been linked to job burnout (Recuero and Segovia, 2021), mental and physical health issues (Kaila-Kangas et al., 2023), and decreased satisfaction with both personal relationships (Cobb et al., 2024) and work (Yucel and Latshaw, 2020). For organizations, the associated costs are substantial, including increased long-term sickness absence, reduced productivity, higher turnover, and elevated intentions to quit (Anderson et al., 2002; Kaila-Kangas et al., 2023; Williams et al., 2016). Given the breadth and severity of these effects, interrole conflict is increasingly recognized as a public health concern (Chandler, 2021), necessitating targeted preventive and therapeutic efforts.

Organizational responses have often focused on flexible work arrangements designed to give employees greater control over when and where they work. However, such initiatives have shown mixed results (Allen et al., 2013). While work-related interventions remain a critical area of inquiry, efforts that engage the family domain are equally essential. Because life partners frequently share responsibilities across both spheres—and often influence each other's experiences—interventions may be more effective when they involve both members of the couple. This approach, referred to as dyadic intervention, acknowledges that interrole conflict is not just an individual experience, but one that is shaped and negotiated within relationships. In her seminal work on crossover effects, Westman (2001) emphasized how couples empathize with, communicate about, and co-experience the challenges of balancing work and family demands. Recognizing this, researchers have increasingly adopted dyadic research designs, often using longitudinal or diary-based observational methods (Bakker and Demerouti, 2013). A growing body of work has also examined the strategies couples use to manage WFC and FWC (e.g., Matias and Fontaine, 2015; Shockley et al., 2021). Yet, to our knowledge, no intervention has been specifically developed to address interrole conflict using a dyadic format.

Given their accessibility and flexibility, internet interventions may offer a promising avenue for addressing the dyadic challenges of WFC and FWC, as online delivery can help minimize additional strain on participants' time and resources. Uptake and engagement with internet interventions remain active areas of research (Beatty and Binnion, 2016; Boucher and Raiker, 2024; Smoktunowicz et al., 2024), however, current studies that explore factors influencing initiation, sustained engagement, and completion, predominantly focus on the individual level. Although rates of non-completion in interventions for couples appear similar to those in individual formats (Knopp et al., 2023), the dyadic context introduces distinct complexities. For example, a commonly cited barrier—time—may become even more salient when it involves coordinating two people's schedules instead of one. Furthermore, recent findings suggest a more nuanced picture of engagement in internet interventions: participation varies not only with the way engagement is operationalized but also with the intervention's content and format (Boucher and Raiker, 2024). In dyadic programmes, a further layer of complexity arises because disclosure must be negotiated between partners; deciding what and how much to share can create friction even when the issue itself is not overtly sensitive. Accordingly, interventions addressing WFC and FWC—where work and family tensions may already feel delicate—may face particular challenges in securing open participation. Finally, in heterosexual dyads, gender often emerges as a relevant consideration. Although women have traditionally been viewed as more likely to engage in internet interventions (Beatty and Binnion, 2016), gender differences in interrole conflict are generally considered small or negligible (Byron, 2005; Shockley et al., 2017). Still, some researchers argue that important nuances may have been overlooked. As Williams et al. (2016) note, when it comes to gender, “(…) one wonders what is getting bleached out in the wash” (p. 522). Indeed, in their meta-analysis, Shockley et al. (2017) found small but notable differences for the group of dual-earner couples—those in which both partners are professionally active. Specifically, men tended to report higher WFC, while women more frequently experienced FWC. These differences may influence how men and women perceive and engage with dyadic interventions targeting interrole conflict. In sum, before implementing such interventions, it is crucial to first identify the unique barriers and facilitators associated with the dyadic format.

Aim

This paper reports on the pre-implementation phase of developing a dyadic internet intervention intended to reduce interrole conflict among dual-earner couples. The primary objective of this phase was to identify perceived barriers and facilitators to intervention uptake and engagement. In addition, we sought to ground the intervention design in the lived experiences of potential users, thereby enhancing both its ecological validity and implementation feasibility. Specifically, we address the following research question: What potential barriers and facilitating factors do life partners recognize in participating in a dyadic internet intervention designed to reduce WFC and FWC?

Method

Design

The study is part of a larger research project that, in addition to examining barriers and facilitators to participation in a dyadic internet intervention, aims to explore the subjective experiences of WFC/FWC in couples, investigate whether and how these dimensions cross over between partners, and identify coping strategies at individual and dyadic levels. Full details of the aims and research questions are available in the published study protocol and Open Science Framework preregistration (Smoktunowicz et al., 2023; https://osf.io/8jymv?view_only=327ef41497ed493c9ea738fd0ba5dcfc). This article specifically focuses on results related to barriers and facilitating factors in participating in an internet intervention designed to reduce WFC and FWC (Research Question 4). We employed an experiential qualitative design utilizing semi-structured interviews within a dyadic framework (Willig, 2008). With a bottom-up perspective, the study was grounded in a post-positivist paradigm (Guba and Lincoln, 1994), meaning that we view knowledge as provisional and open to revision, while aiming to strengthen its reliability through reflexivity and rigorous methodological procedures. Ethical approval was granted by the departmental Ethical Review Board at SWPS University (32/2023). All participants provided informed consent prior to data collection. The study design and data reporting adhered to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR; O'Brien et al., 2014).

Participants

The study was conducted among dual-earner, heterosexual couples: N = 20 couples. The sample size was predetermined in accordance with the concept of information power (Malterud et al., 2015), outlined in the study protocol (Smoktunowicz et al., 2023). Couples were required to be employed at least part-time (20 h/week) and to have cohabited for a minimum of one year. No restrictions were imposed regarding age, relationship duration, or occupational sector. To enhance participation among couples facing the complexities of interrole conflicts, the recruitment materials emphasized the study's focus on WFC and FWC as shared relational experiences. The final sample comprised both couples with and without children and included participants from diverse geographical regions across the country, enhancing the breadth of perspectives and supporting the potential transferability of the findings. Exclusion criteria applied if either partner was under 18 years old, lacked fluency in Polish, or declined to participate. Demographic data, including age (M = 39.18, SD = 10.71), years of relationship duration (M = 12.20, SD = 10.82), job tenure (M = 17.35, SD = 10.33), and occupational sectors, were collected prior to interviews and are presented in Table 1. Recruitment was conducted in collaboration with a specialized research agency, which facilitated participant diversity, ensured anonymity, and managed initial contact and coordination. Procedure and data collection

Data collection took place in November and December 2023. Upon signing informed consent but prior to the interviews, participants completed a demographic questionnaire, and a Multidimensional Measure of Work-Family Conflict (Matthews et al., 2010). Subsequently, semi-structured interviews (Kvale and Brinkmann, 2009) were conducted by four members of the research team (JM, ES, ML, and JZ). To obtain each partner's independent perspective on the perceived barriers and facilitators to engage in a WFC/FWC-targeted dyadic intervention, interviews were conducted separately with each partner in the dyad. This methodological approach was purposefully designed to mitigate social desirability bias and foster an environment in which participants could candidly share their experiences and engage in self-reflection, free from the potential influence of their partner's responses.

The interviews were conducted online to ensure access to participants from diverse locations. We used the platform provided by the research agency, which constitutes a minor deviation from the protocol, where participants were to be offered to choose the platform, they were most comfortable using. This change was determined by safety and logistical considerations. Agency's platform was specifically designed for secure data collection, incorporating advanced encryption protocols and compliance with ethical and legal standards for data protection. These efforts aimed to enhance data quality by fostering a sense of security and engagement throughout the process. A total of 40 interviews were conducted; however, it was only possible to include 39 of these in the analysis, as the responses of one participant were not captured on the recording. The average interview duration was 48.03 min (SD = 9.25; range: 27–69 min).

Each interview was conducted using a structured guide developed through an extensive review of the literature and aligned with the study's objectives. The guide was designed to elicit in-depth, subjective narratives by incorporating open-ended questions, complemented by follow-up probes to deepen participant reflections. The interviews explored key dimensions of WFC and FWC, including participants' conceptualizations of the interrole conflict, perceived impacts on daily functioning, and coping strategies employed at both the individual and dyadic levels. A critical component of the interviews focused on participants' subjective perceptions of barriers and facilitators to engage in intervention targeting interrole conflicts. All interview recordings were transcribed verbatim and subsequently anonymized to protect participant identities. To maintain data confidentiality, all transcripts were securely stored with access restricted exclusively to the research team. Signed informed consent forms were stored separately from the interview data, ensuring that no identifying information could be linked to the transcripts. The section of the interview guide specifically relevant to the research question in the present study is provided in Appendix A (A.1).

Data analysis

Although couples were recruited as dyads, the present analysis addresses Research Question 4 and was prespecified as individual-level reflexive thematic analysis whereas dyadic analysis linking responses from both partners is reserved for RQs 1–3 (Smoktunowicz et al., 2023). Reflexive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2021) was employed, adhering to the six-phase framework (Braun and Clarke, 2006): transcription and familiarization with the data, initial coding, theme generation, theme refinement and definition, and final narrative synthesis. Data analysis was conducted using MAXQDA software (VERBI Software, 1989–2024). Two researchers independently coded the dataset—ES, specializing in WFC/FWC research, and JZ, an expert in qualitative methodologies—ensuring a rigorous, and interdisciplinary analytical approach. Any discrepancies in coding were addressed through discussion. Subsequently, a thorough revision of the preliminary themes was undertaken to ensure their congruence with the coded data and emergent patterns within the dataset, ultimately leading to the definition and refinement of overarching themes. The final themes and sub-themes were iteratively refined through comprehensive discussions involving the entire research team (ES, MR, ML, JM) to capture the core meaning of each thematic category (Braun and Clarke, 2021). In line with reflexive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2021), the importance of themes was judged by their conceptual salience and relevance to the research question, rather than by numerical frequency or software-generated metrics.

A comprehensive set of strategies was employed to enhance the credibility and depth of the study (Collaço et al., 2021). Reflexivity was maintained through ongoing critical discussions within the research team, acknowledging the interpretative character of the analyses and exploring how the researchers' perspectives shaped both data collection and theme development (Braun and Clarke, 2021). To promote transparency and reproducibility, documentation of all analytical procedures and decision-making processes was maintained throughout the research process. Additionally, descriptions and illustrative examples of participant narratives were incorporated to provide contextual depth, thereby strengthening the transferability of the findings beyond the research sample (Nowell et al., 2017). Furthermore, the pre-registered study protocol (Smoktunowicz et al., 2023) functioned as a critical peer debriefing mechanism from the earliest stages of the research process. By having the protocol reviewed prior to data collection, we strengthened the study's transparency and replicability while enhancing methodological rigor, minimizing potential biases, and reinforcing the coherence of the analytical framework.

Results

Our analysis of participants' responses to the research question about perceived barriers and facilitators to engaging in a dyadic intervention aimed at reducing interrole conflict revealed five main themes, each comprising its own set of subthemes (Fig. 1). The themes are presented in an order that reflects a logical flow through participants' accounts. We begin with motivation. This often provided the starting point for their reflections. Motivation to start

The first theme to emerge was the motivation required to uptake and engage with internet intervention. Participants identified a range of factors that could both enhance and undermine whether people uptake it and participate in it. When discussing their motivation, participants were also eager to propose their own solutions to the barriers they perceived. For example, lack of time is a classic barrier to interventions' uptake; yet, the participants seemed to downplay its role.

Time-need tension

Time constraints were often the first concern participants mentioned as barriers to actively engage in the intervention, quoting other commitments or general ‘business’ as barriers. However, this concern was immediately countered by the recognition that, despite competing demands, the urgency of resolving difficulties could take precedence. As one participant noted: “Probably sometimes time [is the barrier], because as I said, I work a lot (...). But I believe the biggest factor here is the willingness.” [ID21, M, 44].

Addressing actual needs

Experiencing genuine difficulties with interrole conflict was seen as a key motivator for engaging in internet intervention. As one person put it, “I think most of all, if I had any problems related to this, it would be a greater incentive—whether such a program would actually help me solve them.” [ID28, M, 57]. This perspective was further reinforced by participants who reported having no significant issues balancing work and life, either because they had never struggled with it or had already developed effective coping strategies. Their lack of a pressing need to address interrole conflict made them less inclined to join the intervention, highlighting the importance of perceived necessity as a facilitator for engagement. One person explained: “(...) looking at it very strictly on a personal level, it seems to me that we have a pretty well-established system for dealing with stress, and I'm just not sure how necessary it would be.” [ID25, F, 23].

Curiosity and openness

Some participants expressed general curiosity and openness toward the potential content of the intervention, showing a willingness to explore it. Even those skeptical about seeking help through psychological programs admitted they might consider joining if something genuinely captured their interest. Others were motivated by the opportunity to learn new strategies and gain additional insights, regardless of their existing need or ability to manage interrole conflict. As one person said: “For me, it's more about curiosity—what it could be, how it could work, what I could learn from it, and what I could unlearn. Because maybe there are things worth unlearning that I'm not even aware of yet, right? You never know. So, it varies, but... it's definitely something that brings something positive and interesting.” [ID34, M, 40].

Effectiveness incentive

Participants were more inclined to engage in the intervention if they felt assured of its effectiveness. These assurances could come from two main sources. On the one hand, participants sought credible evidence, such as scientific validation and reliable sources of information. For example, one person said. “I think it's encouraging if it's well-documented and not just thrown out as a simple slogan. If there were clear and detailed results described, I think that would also encourage me.” [ID26, M, 30]. On the other hand, personal recommendations from their social networks played a crucial role in shaping their willingness to participate, as described by this participant:”I think it would make it easier for them to (…) maybe identify with the problem or with someone who has benefited from it. Very often, couples don't want help, but when they see something like: ‘Look, this helped me, I had this and that problem, and it worked for me,’ it makes a difference. When they see an example of someone who used this kind of help and it worked, that becomes the factor that encourages them to take the step.” [ID23, F, 34].

On doing things together

As participants were asked to identify barriers and facilitators to an intervention involving both partners, the dyadic nature of such an intervention emerged as a dominating theme in their reflections. Participants described a spectrum of potential consequences: While the dyadic approach offered benefits, such as strengthening the couple's relationship, it also introduced logistical challenges by requiring the participation of both partners.

Challenges of interdependence

The dyadic structure of the intervention was perceived as potentially challenging due to several factors, such as differing levels of motivation to address interrole conflict, time constraints affecting one partner more than the other, and general difficulties in coordinating potential shared activities required by the intervention. Some participants also expressed concerns about potential asymmetry in interest and engagement in the intervention tasks, which could lead to tension. As one person shared: “Certainly, it would be a challenge if we had different perspectives on various philosophies and solutions. What I see as potentially effective, he might see differently, and that could cause some friction if he decides that it won't work.” [ID33, F, 34]. To make participation more appealing, several respondents highlighted the importance of flexibility. They suggested that while maintaining a dyadic structure, the intervention should allow for some activities to be completed individually or accommodate different levels of engagement. One participant proposed that “It would be nice if everyone had their own space, but there was also one shared space. Or alternatively, that everyone just has their own space. And for example, if someone feels ready to share, then, I don't know, maybe they click something and let it show in that shared space.” [ID6, F, 30].

Engagement through shared experiences

At the same time, participants saw the dyadic structure as a benefit. Scheduling time together and engaging in shared activities was perceived as an opportunity to strengthen their relationship. For some, it was seen as a chance to spend more time as a couple or a family as explicitly stated by this participant: “Maybe if it were things we were supposed to do together—with the child—it might make it easier [to join the intervention]. Probably the fact that these are activities we do together, not separately, that they connect us and help us bond more.” [ID33, F, 41]. For other participants, the dyadic format seemed to provide a structured way to address interrole conflict. In the words of one person, “The fact that these exercises are done as a couple, together, is definitely a plus because every element that allows us to do something together is important. We barely see each other throughout the day—work, responsibilities—so if this could be something that makes us sit down together on the couch and talk it over, then that would be really great.” [ID31, F, 36].

Sharing and confessing

The intersection between the subject matter of the intervention—namely, work-family and family-work conflict—and its online delivery, emerged as another theme. Participants noted that some individuals might feel apprehensive about acknowledging their experience of interrole conflict or sharing personal details in an online psychological program. Conversely, others viewed internet delivery as a means to be more open and honest. These perspectives gave rise to four subthemes.

Vulnerability

A key concern that emerged was the reluctance to acknowledge struggles with interrole conflict and one's role in it. One person admitted “So it's purely a matter of willingness—or fear, fear that I might find out I'm not quite as saintly, not quite as perfect as I thought.” [ID3, M, 53]. Additionally, participants anticipated that some individuals might feel uneasy about self-reflection, sharing personal details about their family, or receiving advice on how to manage their lives due to the deeply personal nature of interrole conflict: “And something like this would lead to reflection on their relationship, on themselves, and some people simply don't want that.” [ID30, F, 31].

Gender

Vulnerability often intersected with gender assumptions. Men were perceived as more reluctant to acknowledge or address interrole conflict. As one person observed: “I would also see reluctance, so to speak, from one of the partners as a potential issue. Because while I know that, for example, women tend to have a nature that allows them to open up about their problems more easily, I also know that men often don't even see those problems.” [ID23, F, 34]. This reluctance was seen as further amplified by the online format of the intervention, with men perceived as less likely to share vulnerabilities online, as admitted by another participant: “I personally am open to any suggestion, but I know men who would never go near a computer for this. ‘No, no, I'm not going to talk about what's happening at home, that would be crazy,’ right?”. [ID2, M, 55]. These observations were made by both men and women.

Need for human contact

Some participants expressed concern that the online format might feel impersonal—particularly given the intimate nature of the intervention's focus. As one person explained: “I need direct, physical contact—to see the other person's posture, for example. A microphone and webcam help a lot, but they don't give you everything, and I often felt like the person I was speaking to didn't quite understand what I meant (...) I think that when you're digging into things that are so private and personal, it really calls for contact—sorry—but face to face.” [ID22, F, 44]. Participants also expressed that a traditional, face-to-face format would allow for a more personalized approach: “However, the human factor, the conversations—I'm the kind of person who prefers talking to an actual person. In such conversations, sometimes you can even uncover more topics than you initially expected.” [ID9, M, 39].

Privacy supported by online interaction

Conversely, the online format was also seen as a way to mitigate concerns about vulnerability and reluctance to share intimate details about one's personal life. Participants noted that an internet-based intervention could reduce feelings of shame or judgment, as one would not need to confess to problems or discuss intimate details in a face-to-face conversation. As one participant put it: “What would encourage us is the fact that it's online, so we wouldn't feel like we're confessing to a stranger. Because, for some people, it's a challenge to openly talk to someone about their problems.” [ID30, F, 31].

Pedagogical design

Another theme focused on design-related features that could either encourage or hinder participant engagement with the dyadic internet intervention. Participants discussed specific elements such as the mode of content delivery, the structure and interactivity of the intervention, and the credibility of information sources—ranging from informal peer experiences to formal expert guidelines.

Engaging forms of delivery

Participants readily shared their ideas on the preferred format of the intervention, demonstrating ease in suggesting various approaches. They emphasized the importance of a diverse and engaging design, expressing interest in a multimodal approach that includes videos, podcasts, and both open- and closed-ended questions for reflection. As explained by one of the participants: “Some of the materials are in the form of videos and others in a regular written format to read, that's also okay.” [ID18, M, 44]. At the same time, they highlighted the value of combining online activities (such as reading and listening) with offline, interactive tasks completed together with a partner. One participant suggested that “If the tasks involved something like going to the kitchen, discussing something, then coming back and seeing the effect of that on the platform—whatever form this platform or tool would take—then that could be fine, right? I think that's actually a good direction.” [ID29, M, 29].

Personalization

Participants identified personalization as a key factor that could encourage them to join the intervention, in particular in the interventions' content. One participant provided an example of such personalization: “So if, let's say, a couple doesn't have much time together, then it would be about suggestions for how they could spend that time.” [ID35, F, 23]. Another emphasized that the intervention should be adapted to different life stages, as the challenges couples face vary significantly as a function of their circumstances. Specifically, they explained: “A young married couple with two children under the age of three will have completely different problems than a couple with two highly energetic teenagers. A couple our age, who are still fairly active but have children about to leave home, will face yet another set of challenges. And then there are those preparing for retirement, who will have different concerns altogether. So, I think this program would need to be tailored in a radically different way for each of these groups.” [ID22, F, 44].

Peer experience

One specific learning-related feature indicated by participants was the option to learn from others. This form of vicarious learning could involve examples of other couples navigating interrole conflict, offering new perspectives and potential coping strategies. In the words of one of the participants: “I think it would be worthwhile to take part in such a program because it would allow us to see how others approach things differently. It could provide a broader perspective. Maybe what I do seems good to me, but I could get insight from another couple who does things differently. It's not about making radical changes but perhaps combining approaches—taking something from someone else while also sharing something of my own.” [ID2, M, 55].

Tips and recommendations

Participants expressed a desire also for third-party recommendations on coping strategies and feedback on the couples' current situation. Specifically, they were looking for practical tips, ideally tailored to their context. One person suggested: “I mean, I think the idea of some kind of improvement or even having an outside person, someone from the outside, take a look at certain aspects is great—I think that would be helpful.” [ID9, M, 39], while another proposed: “Maybe some kind of takeaways from it, like how we're managing things, or some tips—because it could kind of be seen as a bit of couples therapy.” [ID17, K, 39].

Accessibility

The final theme that emerged in response to participants' identification of barriers and facilitators concerned the accessibility of the internet intervention. Although online delivery is generally regarded as more accessible, participants noted that time constraints remain a significant barrier. In contrast, accessibility in terms of location was viewed almost exclusively positively—particularly due to the dyadic nature of the intervention and the logistical challenges it presents.

Time demands

As previously mentioned, participants identified lack of time as the primary barrier to participation. While a pressing need to address interrole conflict could sometimes override this challenge, time constraints remained a significant obstacle, as expressed by this participant: “Well, obviously, most people we know struggle with a lack of time, and as I said, if both partners in a couple are working, especially at different hours, it becomes an even bigger problem, right.” [ID19, M, 52]. However, some participants viewed this as merely a perceived lack of time, suggesting that time could be managed more effectively to allow for participation in the intervention, as implied by this participant “But it's also a matter of time management skills—how, in reality, the more responsibilities we have, the more we start to see that we could actually handle even more over time.” [ID10, F, 39].

Time flexibility

In light of time constraints, the ability to engage with intervention at flexible times was seen as a major facilitating factor. Participants emphasized the importance of features such as flexible all-time access, the ability to pause and resume activities, and the freedom to choose when to engage with the intervention based on their own schedules. One person remarked: “I think this is the most encouraging aspect—that I can do it at any time of the day or night, whenever we have free time and when we're together. That's when we can work on solving some tasks. For me, at least, it's very encouraging that I'm no longer tied to a specific time. Instead, when we have time, we sit down with my wife and do it together.” [ID28, M, 57].

Place flexibility

Another factor that helped mitigate time constraints was the ability to participate in the intervention not only at any time but also from any location. This flexibility was seen as particularly important, allowing participants to integrate the program into their daily routines more easily, as explained by this person: “The fact that it's online, remote, meaning I can log in even during downtime at work, after coming home, or anywhere while on a business trip. I guess the most important thing for me is that it's flexible—not always tied to a specific hour—and that it's accessible everywhere.” [ID18, M, 44].

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the factors that may hinder or facilitate couples' uptake and engagement with a dyadic-format internet intervention intended to reduce WFC and FWC. Some of the themes we identified align with findings from previous studies on barriers and facilitators to engagement in individual internet interventions, including perceived usefulness, flexibility, privacy, and time as a barrier (Borghouts et al., 2021). Our findings also in part overlap with factors that shape uptake and engagement in traditional couples therapy. For example, practical considerations, such as time constraints, and preferences, such as choosing in-person versus online (i.e., via video platforms) formats, were noted in previous studies (Hubbard and Anderson, 2022; Williamson et al., 2019). The present data add a dyadic layer to this literature by examining factors unique to dyadic internet interventions—ranging from logistical considerations and attitudes toward online delivery to the readiness to be vulnerable and address interrole conflict as a relational issue within the couple.

Among practical challenges that affect engagement, one of the most commonly cited barriers is lack of time. However, our interviewees noted that it may be used as an excuse and not an actual impediment. This aligns with previous findings suggesting that claims of insufficient time often mask other barriers, such as low motivation, competing priorities, or technical difficulties (Busby Grant et al., 2024). All of these also emerged in our study. Nevertheless, the time barrier should not be overlooked, as it becomes even more complex in the context of dyadic interventions, where participation requires coordination between two individuals. In practical terms, dyadic internet interventions could address the time barrier as early as the recruitment phase by openly acknowledging it but also emphasizing the intervention's accessibility, flexibility in terms of time and place, and potential to help solve a real problem—ultimately supporting better outcomes for the couple.

This brings us to the theme of doing things together—a particularly noteworthy aspect, as it is unique to dyadic interventions. Some concerns about the logistics of participating alongside a partner were expected. Online delivery should mitigate these to some extent, allowing couples to participate from home with flexible timing for completing exercises. However, interviewees highlighted less straightforward challenges, such as potential frustration if one partner lags behind or is less interested and engaged in the intervention. Interestingly, participants themselves proposed solutions, emphasizing the need for a thoughtful balance between tasks that must be completed together and those that can—or even should—be done individually. These solutions could be developed and tested in pilot feasibility studies, such as living labs (Westerlund and Leminen, 2011), to ensure that the balance between individual and joint tasks effectively supports participant engagement. Moreover, personalization in dyadic interventions takes on an additional dimension, as it must account not only for individual needs but also for the couple as a unit. For example, interviewees recognized that the needs of couples may differ depending on relationship duration and parental status—those in long-term relationships versus newer couples, and those with children versus those without. To address these differing needs, future dyadic internet interventions may offer parallel tracks grounded in a shared theoretical framework (e.g., time, strain, and behavior dimensions of interrole conflict; Greenhaus and Beutell, 1985), with content and examples adapted to the demographic characteristics of participating couples.

Another theme unique to interventions focused on interpersonal dynamics is sharing and confessing. Partners may have conflicting perspectives, and interrole conflict can signal deeper family tensions, making it a particularly sensitive topic. In practical terms, creators of dyadic interventions are thus advised to carefully design exercise modules and perhaps provide guidelines for potential conflict resolution, in particular when the interventions are self-guided, that is delivered without human support (Andersson, 2014). Moreover, both male and female participants indicated that men might be more reluctant to address the problem of interrole conflict. One practical implication would be thus to acknowledge the importance of gender-sensitive approaches, particularly in how internet-based interventions are communicated. This might involve tailoring promotional materials to reflect differing attitudes—for instance, by emphasizing privacy and including testimonials from male users. At the same time, it is noteworthy that gender differences emerged solely in the theme of sharing and confessing, reinforcing the view that women and men largely share not only the experience of interrole conflict but also similar attitudes toward potential interventions aimed at addressing it.

Limitations

An important perspective should frame how we interpret these findings. First, most participants were unfamiliar with the concept of internet intervention, as the study was conducted in an emerging country in this field. Although we provided a detailed briefing on the planned intervention—a self-guided, web-based program— we primarily relied on their perceptions of what they thought would be important. This lack of familiarity also meant that participants' responses focused almost entirely on the uptake of the intervention rather than engagement with it. Thus, it is important to recognize that this type of study should be only the first step in the process of identifying the barriers and facilitators unique to the dyadic format. This topic needs to be revisited after pilot testing of the intervention, so participants can reflect on their actual experience with it. Second, during the interviews, participants reported experiencing low to moderate WFC and FWC. The absence of individuals with high interrole conflict may be attributable to the very nature of the issue—time constraints and resource depletion likely reduce their availability for voluntary research participation. Additionally, discomfort or shame associated with discussing these challenges may have contributed to their underrepresentation. Although it was not our goal to specifically recruit individuals with high interrole conflict, their absence limits the conclusions we can draw. Individuals experiencing high interrole conflict may identify different barriers or perceive certain barriers as more significant than those with low or moderate conflict. This pattern has important implications for recruiting participants in future dyadic internet interventions. It is likely that those most affected by interrole conflict will be the least able or willing to engage initially—a trend also observed in occupational interventions targeting issues such as job burnout, where the most severely affected individuals are often absent (Bes et al., 2023). Arguably, those most in need may first require broader systemic changes before they are ready to engage in family-oriented, dyadic interventions. This allows for formulating practical recommendations for subsequent interventions: We suggest that initial implementation efforts focus on couples most willing and able to participate, even if this excludes those with the most severe WFC and/or FWC. This approach will allow to assess preliminary efficacy, feasibility, engagement, and adherence, allowing for iterative refinement of the intervention. In time, a well-developed and effective program may prove more accessible and appealing to more vulnerable populations. Notably, participants in this study highlighted peer recommendations as a strong motivator, suggesting that positive feedback from early users could be instrumental in future recruitment efforts.

Another potential limitation is that we conducted individual interviews with participants, a methodological decision made to capture personal perspectives. However, future research could supplement our findings and interview couples together, as this may provide additional insights into their shared experiences and dynamics. Finally, as this study was part of a co-creation effort to develop a dyadic intervention addressing WFC and FWC, we emphasize that the identified barriers and facilitators may present differently in interventions focused on other topics.

Conclusions

While research on barriers and facilitators to engaging in internet interventions is extensive, it predominantly adopts an individual-level perspective. This study contributes to the literature by exploring these factors within a dyadic framework, focusing on couples navigating interrole conflict—a challenge with implications not only for individual well-being but also for organizational functioning and public health. This dyadic perspective from potential users offers valuable insights for developing more inclusive and ecologically valid internet interventions.
