DKK1 Targeting in Corneal Schwann Cells Rescues Axonal Regeneration and Mechanosensory Function After Corneal Injury
Authors: Michael Li, Christian A. Tallo, Mary Katherine Eddy, Aarush Kolli, Ricky Paramo, Paola Bargagna‐Mohan, Royce Mohan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStromal nerves regulate the sensory functions of the cornea, which can be disrupted by surgical, traumatic, or chemical injuries. Corneal Schwann cells (cSCs) ensheath axons to provide trophic support, but their role in axonal regeneration is still unexplored. We utilized the proteolipid protein 1‐enhanced green fluorescent protein (Plp1‐eGFP) reporter mice to investigate cSCs in two models of corneal nerve injury: the corneal micropocket injury (CMI) model, which causes focal stromal axonal severance, and acute exposure to nitrogen mustard (NM), which results in blunt damage across the entire cornea and limbal tissue. After CMI, the cSC network declined rapidly over 7 days post‐injury (dpi) but recovered to levels of uninjured controls by 14 dpi with sprouting at both the injury and collateral areas. Axons remained significantly lower than cSCs, compromising mechanosensory functions. The NM injury led to a sustained cSC and axonal deficit with persistent mechanosensory loss through 14 dpi. Previously, we identified Dickkopf‐related protein 1 (DKK1) as a novel candidate gene expressed in cSCs. In this study, we developed a micellar formulation RM4404 incorporating a small‐molecule DKK1 inhibitor for topical application and tested the therapeutic potential of this drug in both injury models. Application of RM4404 during the cSC regenerative phase (7–14 dpi) enhanced cSC repair and significantly improved axonal regeneration with restoration of mechanosensory function in both injury paradigms. These findings identify a promising DKK1‐targeted therapy that promotes cSC repair, enhances corneal nerve regeneration, and restores sensory function in models of corneal injury.

Introduction

The cornea is the most innervated tissue in the human body (He and Bazan 2016; Muller et al. 2003; Shaheen et al. 2014). Surgical procedures involving deep corneal stroma incisions, such as photorefractive keratectomy, radial keratotomy, and LASIK, as well as deep stromal injuries that sever stromal nerves, result in axonal degeneration and hypoesthesia (Belmonte 2007; Chao et al. 2014; Chen et al. 2014; Ferrari et al. 2011; Saika et al. 2013). These sensory deficits often persist for years, severely compromising ocular function and patient quality of life. Exposure to toxic chemicals, particularly vesicants, also causes severe corneal damage characterized by acute pain and neurotrophic keratopathy (Geraci 2008; McNutt et al. 2012; Panahi et al. 2017; Ruff et al. 2013). Despite the clinical severity of these conditions, effective therapeutic options are limited. A contributing factor is the incomplete understanding of the mechanisms driving corneal nerve degeneration and repair, particularly in the context of chemically induced injuries.

Corneal nerves have intrinsic regenerative properties, as evidenced by the partial restoration of innervation following vision corrective surgery. However, the deficits in sensory function remain a concern (Al‐Aqaba et al. 2010; Cruzat et al. 2011; Henriksson et al. 2009; You et al. 2000; Zander and Weddell 1951) because corneal nerve density is rarely restored to the pre‐surgical values (Bandeira et al. 2019; Escapini 1955; Madduri et al. 2009; Mathers et al. 1988; Niederer et al. 2007; Rao et al. 1985; Wilson and Ambrosio 2001).

The health and maintenance of the corneal sensory axons are supported by a specialized type of glial cell known as corneal Schwann cells (cSCs). While most regenerative strategies have focused on neurotrophic modulators (Conrad et al. 2009; Saika et al. 2013; Shaheen et al. 2014; Zhong et al. 1999), few studies have attributed these trophic effects to cSCs. Recent efforts to investigate disease‐related roles of cSCs illuminated that overexpression of ERK1/2 in this glial lineage results in corneal neurofibromas, revealing the plasticity of cSCs that allows them to enter oncogenic pathways through the formation of myofibroblasts (Bargagna‐Mohan et al. 2017). Thus, the conventional roles that cSCs play in physiological axon regeneration remain underappreciated in this regard.

Most of the knowledge regarding SCs derives from other peripheral nervous system (PNS) tissues, such as the sciatic nerve (Jessen et al. 2008; Jessen and Mirsky 2016; Sisakhtnezhad and Matin 2012; Wood and Mackinnon 2015). Following injury, axons undergo Wallerian degeneration and the quiescent SCs initiate a repair response (Arthur‐Farraj et al. 2012). Injury‐activated SCs undergo a reprogramming event in which they downregulate myelin genes, upregulate markers of immature SCs, de‐differentiate, and begin expression of de novo genes found only in repair SCs (Jessen et al. 2015; Jessen and Mirsky 2016). These programmed changes govern the SC repair phenotype (upregulation of trophic factors, surface proteins, and cytokines, and activation of the immune response) aimed to build regenerative tracks to direct axonal regeneration (Arthur‐Farraj et al. 2012; Napoli et al. 2012; Woodhoo et al. 2009). Repair SCs alter their gene expression and ultimately enter a promyelinating program to become mature myelinating SCs (m‐SCs). This repair phenotype is apparently also conserved in non‐myelinating SCs (nm‐SCs) (Jessen and Mirsky 2016). But of distinct relevance to the cornea is that unmyelinated axons of the central cornea are ensheathed by nm‐SCs, which would be an obligatory phenotype required for corneal transparency.

The canonical Wnt signaling pathway regulates development, regeneration, and inflammation (Ahn et al. 2011; Grigoryan et al. 2013; van Vliet et al. 2021). The Dickkopf (DKK) proteins modulate this pathway, with DKK1 acting as a soluble antagonist that inhibits β‐catenin signaling (Stanganello et al. 2019). DKK1 is involved in neural stem cell differentiation and regenerative processes, and its inhibition has shown promise for neural repair after spinal cord injury (Briona et al. 2015; Gonzalez‐Fernandez et al. 2024). In the eye, DKK1 contributes to ocular development and anterior segment homeostasis (Lieven and Ruther 2011), while DKK2 is also essential for corneal integrity (Song et al. 2018). Here, we focus on DKK1 and its role in cSC‐mediated axonal repair, which remains poorly understood.

Recently, we performed single‐cell RNA sequencing of tissue isolated from the central cornea of rabbits and identified a cluster of nm‐cSCs cells expressing classical glial markers such as Plp1, as well as novel candidate genes, including Dkk1 (Bargagna‐Mohan et al. 2021). Here, following up on this initial study, we have employed the Plp1‐egfp transgenic mouse line to investigate the regulation of cSC and axonal network in response to two corneal injury paradigms: corneal micropocket injury (CMI) and acute chemical exposure with nitrogen mustard. We have characterized shared and distinct features of injury induced cSC degeneration and regeneration, and by deciphering the temporal appearance of repair cSCs we used this information to test the hypothesis that pharmacological inhibition of DKK1 would enhance cSC‐mediated axonal repair. To evaluate this, we developed RM4404, a novel micellar formulation of a DKK1 inhibitor designed for topical ocular delivery. We identified a therapeutic window during which RM4404 significantly promoted cSC survival, enhanced axonal regeneration, and restored corneal mechanosensitivity following injury. These findings offer new insight into the glial biology of the cornea and suggest that modulation of Wnt signaling in cSCs may represent a promising strategy for promoting sensory nerve repair in the injured cornea.

Methods and Materials

Mice

Proteolipid protein 1‐enhanced green fluorescent protein B6; CBA‐Tg (Plp1‐eGFP)10Wmac/J (Jax #033357; RRID:IMSR_JAX:033357) transgenic mice and wild type C57BL/6J (Jax#000664; RRID:IMSR_JAX:000664) mice were purchased from Jackson Labs (Bar Harbor, ME). Homozygous breeding pairs of Plp1‐eGFP mice were used to establish multiple lines by breeding progeny with wild type C57BL/6J mice in‐house at the University of Connecticut Health Center in the Center for Mouse Genome Modification. Transgenic breeders were replaced every 6 to 7 months to ensure competent heterozygote breeding lines. This colony has been managed for over 2 years using this breeding scheme. Colonies of wild type C57BL/6J mice were also bred in‐house. Mice were housed in specific pathogen‐free cages in designated laboratory animal housing facilities at UConn Health. Mice were provided enrichment in their cages in the form of plastic cottages and tissue paper. Cages were cleaned two times a week, and animals were maintained group housed 3 to 5 per cage in an environment of 12‐h dark: light cycles and 66° Fahrenheit temperature within 40%–70% humidity. Mice of both sexes were typically used for injury experiments between 2 and 5 months of age. All experiments were conducted in accordance with procedures approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of Connecticut Health Center, and the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) Statement for the Use of Animals in Vision and Ophthalmic Research.

Corneal Micropocket Injury Model

The corneal stromal injury model is an adaptation of the corneal micropocket angiogenesis assay that was developed initially for mice and rabbits to study growth factor pellet‐induced corneal neovascularization (Kenyon et al. 1996; Mohan et al. 2000; Yeh et al. 2000). In our refinement of this model, we omit the incorporation of a growth factor pellet; thus, we refer to this injury as the CMI model and have exploited it to study the stromal axonal injury produced in vision corrective surgeries, such as laser‐assisted in situ keratomileusis (LASIK) performed in humans (Ivarsen et al. 2003). In brief, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane using a mouse anesthesia machine (RWD Life Sciences Sugare Land, TX) and provided topical Proparacaine for corneal anesthesia. The surgical procedure was conducted with the aid of a dissecting microscope, with mice placed on a warming pad after anesthesia. For the procedure, the investigator held the globe gently by the upper and lower eyelids, with a pair of blunt forceps with one hand, and using the other hand, an incision was made along the corneal midline using a #15 Bard‐Parker surgical blade. This central incision penetrated through the epithelial basement membrane and into part of the anterior stroma. Next, while still holding the eye, an ophthalmic microblade (Beaver Visitec International Ophthalmology, 15°, 5.0 mm depth) was used to pry apart the incision lip and gently create the intrastromal micropocket by extending the blade toward the peripheral cornea following the curved contour of the tissue. The cornea was maintained hydrated with sterile PBS during the surgical procedure. This U‐shaped stromal pocket severed deep stromal axons within a focal region of the cornea, without damaging the adjacent limbal region. After the procedure, atropine and tobramycin eye drops were applied once, followed by erythromycin eye ointment paste to prevent infections and maintain hydration. Mice were provided extended‐release buprenorphine (1.3 mg/mL; Ethiqa XR, Covetrus North America, Albany, NY) subcutaneously. Topical tobramycin drops were applied to the mouse corneas once per day for 2 days following the injury.

Corneal Nitrogen Mustard Injury Model

Nitrogen mustard (NM; mechlorethamine hydrochloride; bis (2‐chloroethyl) methylamine, HN2) was purchased from Selleckchem (Cat# S4252; lot S425202; CAS#: 55‐86‐7). The NM stock solution was prepared to a concentration of 10 mM in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) solution, and aliquots were stored at −20°C. Before use, the stock solution was diluted in PBS solution. Mice were anesthetized and prepared for injury, as described above. One cornea of the mouse was exposed by topical application of 5 μL to either 1% or 0.2% NM solution for 5 min, then eyes rinsed with 5 mL of PBS as previously reported (Umejiego et al. 2023). The fellow eye was not injured. After the procedure, atropine and tobramycin eye drops were applied once, followed by erythromycin eye ointment paste to prevent infections and maintain hydration. Mice were provided extended‐release buprenorphine (1.3 mg/mL; Ethiqa XR, Covetrus North America, Albany, NY) subcutaneously post injury. Topical tobramycin drops were applied to the mouse corneas once per day for 2 days following the injury.

Corneal In Vivo Imaging

Plp1‐eGFP mice were anesthetized using isoflurane and placed on the mouse holder while holding a nose cone adapter for maintenance of anesthesia. The mouse eye brought into focus to allow visualization of the cornea using a slit lamp attachment on the Micron III fundus camera system (Phoenix‐Micron Inc, Bend, OR). Images were collected for assessment of corneal clarity under bright light illumination and routinely examined to ensure eyes did not develop unintended fibrosis or neovascularization (Mohan et al. 2000; Yeh et al. 2000). To capture live images of cSCs, the blue filter was applied that allowed for capture of fluorescence from the cSC network. Images were imported into Photoshop to enhance brightness and improve quality.

Development of DKK1 Inhibitor as a Micellar Formulation RM4404 for Topical Application

Kolliphor HS‐15 (Polyoxyl 15 hydroxystearate; CAS# 70142‐34‐6; BASF; lot# 90878456P0) was solubilized in sterile 0.9% NaCl prewarmed to 37°C and gently stirred for 30 min at 37°C to afford a 0.5% (w/v) Kolliphor HS‐15 suspension (Leonardi et al. 2014). The small molecule DKK1 inhibitor WAY‐262611 (Cat# 317700, Sigma‐Aldrich; CAS# 1123231‐07‐1) was prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide at 10 mM and stored at −20°C. prior to use, a 10 mM stock solution of WAY262611 was diluted to 1 mM in 0.9% NaCl. For the 20 μM formulation, the diluted stock was then added to a Kolliphor HS‐15 suspension to reach the final concentration. For the 200 μM formulation, the 10 mM stock solution was directly diluted into the Kolliphor HS‐15 suspension to achieve the desired final concentration. The incorporation of the drug as a micellar suspension in Kolliphor HS‐15 was achieved by gentle stirring at room temperature for 30 min, followed by a period of rest at 4°C for 16 h without stirring. This micellar drug suspension, called RM4404, was filtered through a 45‐micron filter, and aliquots were stored at 4°C. Representative aliquots stored for 1 month at 4°C were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The micellar suspension was placed on a glow‐discharged 400‐mesh carbon‐ coated copper grid and negatively stained with 0.5% freshly prepared uranyl acetate. Images from different grids were obtained on a HITACHI H‐7650 transmission electron microscope. The micelle size was determined against an internal scale bar. For in vivo use, the RM4404 micellar formulation was routinely prepared 1 to 2 days before use, and aliquots were stored at 4°C. Kolliphor HS‐15 micellar suspension lacking the drug was similarly prepared and used as the vehicle control. Mice of both sexes subjected to the CMI or 0.2% NM injury model were provided 20 μL of the vehicle or RM4404 formulation by topical application to the injured cornea twice per day, separated by 7 to 8 h between dosing regimens. The modifications to the drug treatment protocol in individual experiments are detailed within the corresponding figures.

Corneal Mechanical Sensitivity Test

The Cochet‐Bonnet aesthesiometer (Luneau Technology Operations, Pont‐de‐l'Arche, France; 12/100 mm) was employed to perform corneal mechanical sensitivity testing in mice subjected to CMI or 0.2% NM exposure. This test measures the corneal response to mechanical stimulation produced by application of the nylon filament to the cornea. In brief, the mouse was gently restrained by hand and subjected to the test without the use of anesthesia with the aid of a dissecting microscope. Starting at position #6.0 (filament fully extended), the tip was contacted with the cornea, and gentle pressure was applied to cause the filament to slightly bend. The investigator ensured that the filament contacted the central aspect of the cornea and not the eyelid or eyelashes. A no‐blink response was considered negative, while an eye blink was recorded as a positive response. When a no blink was detected at the extended filament length, the filament length was shortened, and the test was repeated until a positive eye blink response was obtained. When the blink response was detected, the dial setting was used to confirm with five additional tests for a total of six sampling measurements. For multiple‐time‐point measurements, the mechanical sensitivity test was always performed in the morning before the scheduled drug application for that day.

Corneal Flatmount Immunohistochemistry Analysis

The mouse eyes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h at room temperature, and subsequently, the corneal epithelial cell layer was gently removed using a surgical blade. The cornea was surgically excised at the limbus and placed in a blocking/permeabilization solution (1× PBS solution, 5% goat serum, 1% BSA, 0.5% Triton‐X) for 48 h at 4°C. Corneal tissue was incubated for 48 h with different primary and secondary antibodies according to the experimental design (see primary and secondary antibodies used in Table 1). All antibodies were diluted into a background‐reducing buffer (Aligent DAKO, cat# S302281‐2) and then washed three times for 30 min each in 1× PBS solution. Primary antibodies were incubated for 48 h at 4°C, and secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 h at room temperature (RT), then washed in 1× PBS solution for an additional 16 h at 4°C. The corneas were flattened by making four spaced incisions and mounted on glass slides. Corneal flatmounts were imaged on a Leica Thunder microscope. Briefly, 10× tile scans of each whole‐mount cornea were obtained using a Leica LAS X Navigator microscope and images were assembled using the Leica software. From such composite images representing cSC and the axonal network, the summed lengths of fluorescent, contiguous structures internal to the limbus for each cornea were measured using FIJI software with the Neuroanatomy plugin. 10× tile scan images were imported into Adobe Photoshop 2025 and resized (96 or 300 pixels/inch resolution, 2048 × 2048 pixels width/height) and then cropped as circular images using the elliptical Marquee tool. Images were processed for analysis using Image J software (RRID:SCR_003070) as described (See Supplemental Methods in Data S2).

Corneal Histological and Immunohistochemical Staining Analysis

Mouse eyes were embedded in optimum cutting temperature compound (OCT; Tissue‐Tek, Radnor, PA), or quick frozen on dry ice and stored at −80°C for processing later. Immunostaining was performed as previously described (Umejiego et al. 2023). Cryostat sections 12 μm thick were collected and slides stored at −20°C. Before processing, slides were air dried for 30 min at room temperature and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde/PBS solution for 15 min, followed by three washing steps, each for 15 min in PBS solution. For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, sections were washed once with deionized water, then immersed into fresh Mayers hematoxylin for 3 min. Slides were rinsed in water and immersed in blueing agent (Fisher CS410‐4) for 30 s and washed again in water. Subsequently, slides were immersed in Eosin for 3 min and dipped quickly in water. Sections were dehydrated sequentially with two 10 s washes in 95% ethanol, and two 10 s washes with 100% ethanol, following 3 changes of Citrosol (Fisher 22‐143‐975) for 2 min each. Slides were mounted with Cytoseal 60 (VWR 48212‐154) using glass coverslips. Slides subjected to immunostaining were blocked and permeabilized in PBS solution that contained 0.5% Triton‐X, 5% goat serum, and 1% BSA. Blocking was performed overnight at 4°C. Samples were probed with primary antibodies diluted in DAKO background reducing solution overnight at 4°C. After three washes in PBS solution for 15 min each, slides were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature and washed two times in PBS solution for 10 min each followed by a longer wash for 3 h in PBS solution.

Data Analysis and Statistics

Experiments were designed for sample size of N = 8–10 mice/group based on power analysis (power 0.8 with significance 0.05) and results from our prior studies that tested for significant differences in injury‐related effects on corneal clinical endpoints (Umejiego et al. 2023; Mohan et al. 2000; Yeh et al. 2000). Injured mice were randomized into vehicle or RM4404 treatment cohorts to avoid experimental bias. In the CMI model, both eyes were subject to injury and thus each eye provides an independent measure of changes in cSC and axonal networks and mechanosensitivity, whereas in the NM injury model one mouse eye was injured due to humane reasons. Initial pilot experiments were used for investigator training to ensure unintended clinical outcomes (absence of corneal fibrosis or neovascularization after CMI) were absent, although corneal haze is anticipated in 0.2% NM injury (Umejiego et al. 2023). Corneas were inspected by biomicroscopy to visibly assess such outcomes were minimized as a measure of technical success in CMI. Corneas were also examined for damage during tissue processing steps ex vivo. In such cases, damaged corneal samples were not included for data analysis. These steps allowed for both training of multiple individuals and to ensure data consistency. Subsequent experiments were typically conducted with equal representation of male and female mice and entire experiments were repeated over different days to attain the sample size and normalize for batch effects. Imaging data was quantified by the original experimenter and both the image tracings and quantification results were randomly reexamined by a secondary uninvolved investigator blinded to the samples. The numbers of eyes in each study group are shown in the figures represented by data points within bar graphs and total numbers in each study are presented in the statistical analysis within the figure legends. Data for the comparisons of more than two sample groups were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis nonparametric test with Dunn's multiple comparisons using statistical hypothesis testing employing Prism statistical software Version 10 (RRID:SCR_002798). For comparisons of two sample sets, the Mann–Whitney nonparametric unpaired 2‐tailed test was applied. A difference with p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of the Plp1‐eGFP Transgenic Mice Line for Injury Studies

Using in vivo imaging, we visualized the cSC network in the Plp1‐eGFP corneas (Figure 1). While this approach enabled real time assessment of the cSC network, imaging of the central cornea was limited due to fluorescence emerging from the retina (Figure 1A, asterisk), and the SCs present in the iris (van Zyl et al. 2022) (Figure 1A, arrows). Despite these challenges, this transgenic mouse line can provide insights into cSC dynamics following corneal injury (Figure 1). Uninjured corneas revealed an extensive arborization of eGFP+ processes and punctate nuclei distributed throughout the stroma (Figure 1A). We next examined the corneas of mice subjected to the CMI (a representative histological section of the stromal penetrating injury is shown in Figure S1). The cSC network appeared reduced at 5 dpi, with visible thinning of cSC processes (Figure 1B). In the chemical injury model, the potent DNA‐damaging effects of NM (Goswami et al. 2016) rendered cSCs highly vulnerable to this chemical, resulting in a dose‐dependent loss of eGFP+ cells. At 5 dpi, 1% NM exposure had a devastating impact both in the central and limbal regions of the cornea, leading to near‐complete ablation of the cSC network (Figure 1C) (Umejiego et al. 2023) whereas 0.2% NM dose also caused substantial degeneration of both the cSC and axonal networks. However, we noticed that some peripheral structures remained intact (Figure 1D, arrows). Based on this partial preservation, we selected 0.2% NM for all our experiments, given that these remaining cSC structures could be potentially rescued by pharmacological treatment. Taken together, these results indicate that while in vivo imaging was effective for assessing the overall extent of cSC injury, quantitative analyses of network architecture and regeneration required immunohistochemical evaluation of flat‐mounted corneas.

Comparison of cSC and Axonal Networks in Mice Subjected to CMI

We performed corneal flat‐mount imaging using eGFP fluorescence to visualize cSCs and the axonal marker β‐III tubulin to label corneal axons in the CMI model (Figure 2). In uninjured control corneas, eGFP+ cells formed a uniform, interconnected network that overlapped with the β‐III tubulin+ axons (Figure 2A–C). At 2 dpi, the eGFP+ and axonal networks were significantly reduced (Figure 2D,E). This pattern of degeneration was novel, as we expected the focal injury to limit the loss of both cSCs and axons mainly in a focal injured region. By 7 dpi, the cSCs in the central cornea remained reduced based on gross morphology (Figure 2G), but axonal loss extended even further toward the peripheral regions (Figure 2H). Notably, merged images showed mid‐peripheral zones devoid of axons yet retaining eGFP+ cells (Figure 2I). At 10‐ and 14‐dpi, the cSCs appeared actively regenerating (Figure 2J,M), whereas axons remained blunted (Figure 2K,N). Quantitative fluorescence analysis confirmed divergent recovery dynamics where eGFP+ cells decreased initially but then increased significantly after 7 dpi (Figure 2P), whereas axons showed a progressive time‐dependent degenerative phenotype with minimal recovery (Figure 2Q). High‐magnification imaging further revealed evidence of early cSC sprouting in axon‐devoid regions as early as 2 dpi, with short projections extending into denervated zones (Figure 3D, arrows). This sprouting pattern persisted at 7 and 10 dpi, occasionally colocalizing with axonal sprouts (Figure 3I), and remained evident at 14 dpi within the arborized cSC network (Figure 3M, arrow), indicating ongoing network reestablishment. These data identify that cSCs dedifferentiate into repair cSCs early and produce elongated processes to reestablish a structural template ahead of axonal processes, with evidence of continued new sprouting occurring even at 14 dpi. To localize regions and quantify where such cSC sprouting was occurring, we examined the 10× tiled images of corneal flat mounts to count the number of these sprouting structures (Figure 4). Uninjured corneas revealed a sparse eGFP+ baseline sprouting (Figure 4A), and a nonsignificant increase at 2 dpi (Figure 4B,G). Starting at 7 dpi, there was a significant increase in sprouting structures (Figure 4C,G), and their numbers were higher at 10 and 14 dpi across injured and non‐injured areas of the cornea (Figure 4D,E,G), consistent with the overall pattern of cSC network restoration (Figure 2). Interestingly, sprouts varied morphologically and in length, ranging from single to multiple projections from individual cSCs (Figure 4F). These data suggest that while the intrinsic regenerative capacity of repair cSCs can effectively restore their network, the paracrine mechanisms through which cSCs support axonal regeneration appear to be compromised, leading to axonal deficits. In vivo corneal mechanosensitivity testing also showed a time‐dependent loss of mechanosensitivity (Figure 5). These functional impairments parallel histological findings of early axonal loss and limited regeneration, particularly in the central cornea (Figure 2).

Comparison of cSC and Axonal Networks in Mice Subjected to 0.2% NM Chemical Injury

We performed corneal flat‐mount imaging using eGFP fluorescence to visualize cSCs and the axonal marker β‐III Tubulin to label corneal axons in the 0.2% NM corneal injury model (Figure 6). At 5 dpi, the eGFP+ network was fragmented in both central and peripheral regions (Figure 6D) and significantly reduced (Figure 6J) compared to uninjured (Figure 6A,J). β‐III tubulin staining also revealed significant axonal degeneration (Figure 6K), with more pronounced loss in the central cornea (Figure 6E). By 14 dpi, in striking contrast to the CMI model (Figure 2), 0.2% NM‐injured corneas showed minimal cSC recovery (Figure 6G,J), and significant loss of axonal processes in all corneal areas (Figure 6H,K). This data indicates that NM injury significantly limits the capacity of damaged/degenerated axons to regenerate, due, in part, to widespread injury to both cSCs and axons. High‐magnification imaging confirmed degeneration in both peripheral and central regions at 5 dpi of both cSCs and axons (Figure 7G–L) compared to uninjured (Figure 7A–F).

DKK1 Is Highly Expressed in Mouse cSCs

Our prior findings revealed that rabbit nm‐cSCs express Dkk1 transcripts, whereas other corneal cell types from the central cornea did not, as reflected by scRNAseq analysis (Bargagna‐Mohan et al. 2021). Here, we confirmed DKK1 protein expression in cSCs, and co‐localization with several established cSC markers (Figure 8). In uninjured Plp1‐eGFP mice, DKK1 immunostaining overlapped with eGFP+ cells (Figure 8A–C). In uninjured WT corneas, DKK1 co‐localized with L1‐CAM, a previously described marker of this glial cell type (Figure 8D–F) (Bargagna‐Mohan et al. 2021). DKK1 was found in both the peripheral and central regions of the cornea (Figure 8G), suggesting a potentially broad functional role in cSC physiology. Furthermore, DKK1 immunostaining was observed in m‐cSCs at the periphery (Figure 8H) as well as in the slender processes of central corneal nm‐cSC (Figure 8K).

Development of RM4404 for Topical Inhibition of DKK1

After confirming DKK1 expression in uninjured cSCs, we hypothesized that injury‐induced DKK1 production by cSCs may limit corneal axonal regeneration by antagonizing Wnt signaling (Gonzalez‐Fernandez et al. 2024). To facilitate corneal topical delivery and improve drug retention, we developed a micellar preparation of WAY262611 that was called RM4404 (see details in Section 2). Micellar preparations of RM4404 were characterized by TEM (Figure 9) and shown to be stable even after 1 month of storage at 4°C, consistent with micelles formed with Kolliphor HS‐15 preparations (Hou et al. 2019). The topical delivery of RM4404 was timed to align with the kinetics of repair cSC sprouting (Figures 2 and 4), with the goal of targeting DKK1 during the active phase of SC regeneration, consistent with β‐Catenin‐Wnt signaling dynamics (van Vliet et al. 2021).

Delayed RM4404 Treatment Promotes cSC and Axonal Regeneration and Improves Corneal Sensory Function in the CMI Model

We evaluated the therapeutic effect of DKK1 inhibition in the CMI model using a delayed treatment paradigm (7–14 dpi as the therapeutic window; Figure 10A), based on the expectation that a substantial population of dedifferentiated repair cSCs arising from both injured and collateral regions (Figure 4) would be present during this period. We hypothesized that DKK1 inhibition could enhance trophic support and reverse the delayed axonal regeneration seen in this model (Figure 2). Mice were injured and separate cohorts subjected to topical RM4404 or vehicle treatment twice per day from 7 to 14 dpi or injured and left untreated for 14 dpi. Corneal flat‐mounts were used to visualize the eGFP+ SC network and stained with the axonal marker β‐III Tubulin (Figure 10). At 14 dpi, the eGFP+ network did not significantly change in the RM4404‐treated or the vehicle‐ treated groups (Figure 10B) compared to uninjured samples suggesting that neither the RM4404 nor vehicle produced affected the intrinsic regenerative function of repair cSCs. The axonal network was significantly greater in the RM4404‐treated corneas at 14 dpi compared to injured corneas at 14 dpi that were not treated, revealing a drug‐induced reversal in axonal degeneration in the CMI model. The vehicle‐treated group showed a small improvement in axonal degeneration, but this was not significantly different from the 14‐day injured untreated samples (Figure 10C). These findings showed that RM4404 did not alter cSC regeneration per se over this treatment period but produced a significant trophic effect that rescued the delay in axonal regeneration (seen in Figure 2).

To assess whether axonal rescue induced by RM4404 is sustained beyond the 7–14 dpi treatment window, mice were withdrawn from treatment and monitored until 30 dpi. This approach avoids chronic dosing, given that nerve repair can continue for months to years post‐injury (Niederer et al. 2007). In this second experimental arm (Figure 10A), injured mice received RM4404 or vehicle from 7 to 14 dpi as before, followed by no further treatment until 30 dpi. This design allowed us to evaluate whether cSCs and the axonal network return to the homeostatic baseline observed in uninjured mice. The eGFP+ cSC and axonal network in the vehicle‐treated corneas appeared reduced, whereas the axonal network in RM4404‐treated corneas appeared similar to those of uninjured corneas by gross examination (Figure 10D–I right panel). Quantitative measurements confirmed that the vehicle‐treated corneas had significantly reduced cSC network compared to the control uninjured group, with RM4404‐treated corneas showing a small increase, though not significant, in the cSC network at 30 dpi compared to uninjured corneas (Figure 10J). Quantification of the axonal network revealed vehicle‐treated corneas had significantly reduced levels compared to uninjured controls, whereas the RM4404‐treated corneas instead showed an increased axonal network reaching levels that were not significantly different from that of uninjured controls (Figure 10K). Together, these data suggest that RM4404 treatment during the 7 day period of drug treatment did not alter the growth of the cSC network remarkably from its inherent regenerative levels, and consequentially, the growth of the cSC network over the subsequent 16 day drug free period remained relatively unchanged. On the other hand, RM4404 treatment during the 7 day period restored axonal network expansion, which was also sustained over the subsequent 16 day drug free period.

We next investigated whether RM4404 treatment also altered corneal mechanosensory function in this novel treatment paradigm. Mechanosensory testing was performed on uninjured mice, injured untreated mice at 7 dpi, and after vehicle or RM4404 treatment at 14 dpi, and finally at 30 dpi (Figure 11). At 7 dpi, both female and male injured corneas showed a significant reduction in mechanosensory function compared to uninjured corneas (Figure 11A), which was consistent with previous analysis (Figure 5). No improvement was observed at 14 dpi in the vehicle‐treated male and female mice, whereas RM4404‐treated male and female mice showed a significant restoration of corneal sensitivity, suggesting a robust drug‐mediated effect (Figure 11B). To determine whether DKK1 inhibition through RM4404 produced sustained improvements even after drug withdrawal, we evaluated mechanosensitivity at 30 dpi. In either sex group, the 30‐dpi vehicle‐treated corneas still showed sensory deficiency, similar to those at 14 dpi, whereas RM4404‐treated mice regained mechanosensory function similar to levels of uninjured controls (Figure 11C), suggesting that RM4404 has a durable therapeutic effect that effectively improved corneal mechanosensitivity in both male and female mice.

Next, we tested whether acute RM4404 treatment in the NM injury model (Figure 14A) could also rescue cSCs and promote their regenerative potential to enhance axonal regeneration. NM‐injured mice were treated twice daily from the day of injury through 7 dpi and then left untreated till 14 dpi, when they were analyzed. RM4404 treatment showed significant improvement in the cSC network at 14 dpi over the vehicle‐treated group (Figure 14B,C). Interestingly, the axonal network was also improved by RM4404 treatment and Tub+ axons in RM4404‐treated corneas was significantly greater than the cSCs (Figure 14D). Corneal mechanosensitivity testing at 7 dpi revealed that RM4404‐treated mice were significantly improved compared to the vehicle‐treated group (Figure 14E). At the 14‐dpi time point following the 7‐ day drug‐free period, the RM4404‐treated mice retained significant improvement in mechanosensitivity compared to the vehicle‐treated mice (Figure 14F). This corneal functional recovery corroborates the improvement of axonal regeneration promoted by RM4404 treatment.

Delayed Treatment With RM4404 Antagonizes DKK1 In Vivo

To validate in vivo targeting of DKK1 by RM4404, we performed a dose response study to compare the effects of a low dose (20 μM) dose with a 10‐fold higher dose (200 μM) of the DKK1 inhibitor. A minor modification to the treatment regimen was introduced that preserved post‐injury dosing while incorporating a brief drug cessation period. This modified treatment protocol was designed to assess how DKK1 levels reset across treatment groups following drug withdrawal and whether this effect influences Wnt activation. This strategy was informed by our analysis of repair cSC sprouting kinetics (Figures 2 and 4) and the sustained effects following RM4404 treatment cessation in our earlier studies (Figures 11 and 14). In the modified protocol, mice subjected to injury were treated with either vehicle, low dose RM4404, or high dose RM4404 applied topically twice daily from 5 to 10 dpi, followed by a treatment‐free recovery period through study termination at 14 dpi (see Figure 16).

Delayed Treatment With RM4404 Improves Corneal Tissue Architecture in NM Injury

To assess for potential histopathological effects in mouse corneas following CMI and 0.2% NM injury, we performed hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining from 14 dpi samples of the modified RM4404 treatment protocol. Corneal sections from the CMI model showed no appreciable differences in epithelial or stromal thickness among the three treatment groups (Figure 18A–C). In contrast, corneas injured with 0.2% NM showed reduced corneal thickness in vehicle‐treated samples, consistent with the blunt nature of this chemical injury (Umejiego et al. 2023). This thinning was reflected in decreased dimensions of both the epithelial and stromal compartments compared with corresponding regions in corneas from mice treated with either dose of RM4404 (Figure 18F–H). These differences between injury models likely reflect distinct underlying mechanisms of corneal pathology associated with each insult; however, within each model, both RM4404 doses produced broadly similar gross effects. These data are consistent with our in vivo findings showing the two drug doses RM4404 restored mechanosensory function in both injury models (Figure S2) and improved corneal clarity that was deteriorated in vehicle treated corneas of 0.2% NM injured mice (Figure S3).

Delayed Treatment With RM4404 Increases Axin2 Levels In Vivo

Since RM4404 effectively targets DKK1 in vivo in both injury models, we next investigated whether DKK1 inhibition enhances β‐catenin‐Wnt signaling in mice subjected to the modified treatment protocol. Axin2, a well‐established direct target of β‐catenin, is widely used as a readout of β‐catenin pathway activation (Alldredge and Fuhrmann 2016; Lustig et al. 2002). Because initial attempts to localize Axin2 within cSCs were challenging, we considered whether DKK1 targeting in cSCs could potentially induce activation of Wnt signaling in a non–cell‐autonomous manner, resulting in increased Axin2 expression in other corneal cells, such as in corneal epithelial cells and wound fibroblasts. This rationale is supported by recent findings suggesting a trophic role for cSCs in epithelial regeneration following injury (Mirmoeini et al. 2023). To examine this mechanism in the context of dose‐related perturbation of DKK1, we analyzed Axin2 levels in corneal cryosections from vehicle, low and high dose RM4404 treatment groups from both injury models.

In the CMI model, vehicle‐treated corneas exhibited basal Axin2 cytoplasmic staining in epithelial cells (Figure 19A,B; panel a), with stromal cells showing cytoplasmic expression (Figure 19A,B; panel b). The low RM4404 dose caused increased cytoplasmic Axin2 staining in the corneal epithelium with no increases in the stroma (Figure 19C,D; panels c and d). The higher RM4404 dose induced a stronger cytoplasmic and nuclear staining for Axin2 (Figure 19E,F; panel e), alongside numerous stromal cells that showed staining in the cytoplasm (Figure 19E,F; panel f).

Taken together, the increase in Axin2 levels, and particularly its nuclear abundance at the high dose in both injury models, may reflect a potential feedback response aimed at modulating β‐catenin signaling under increased DKK1 inhibition.

Discussion

We defined the temporal dynamics of cSC regeneration after stromal injury and identified a therapeutic window in which DKK1 targeting in repair cSCs promotes axonal regeneration and restores mechanosensory function. This effect extended to chemical injury, where the DKK1 inhibitor RM4404 similarly enhanced axonal regrowth and recovery. These findings establish repair cSCs as therapeutically targetable across injury contexts and support a framework for corneal neuro‐regeneration.

In CMI, repair cSC sprouting occurred both locally and in collateral regions of the cornea with cSCs preceding structural axonal remodeling (Beuerman and Rozsa 1984), indicating a broad stress response. In contrast, the regenerative capacity of cSCs appears progressively impaired after NM injury and dose dependent. NM injury affected central and limbal cornea (Umejiego et al. 2023), damaging both nm‐ and m‐cSCs. This observation informed the use of a lower (0.2%) NM dose for therapeutic testing and motivated the use of the Plp1‐eGFP mouse to examine dynamic features of Schwann cell network remodeling during repair (Umejiego et al. 2023).

In CMI, DKK1 perturbation enhanced axonal regeneration without expanding the cSC network. Vehicle‐treated corneas showed limited axonal regrowth but no functional recovery, indicating that, in the absence of appropriate cSC‐derived guidance cues (Arthur‐Farraj et al. 2012; Clements et al. 2017; Jessen et al. 2008; Napoli et al. 2012), axons may be structurally present but functionally ineffective. RM4404 effects are likely primarily cSC‐mediated, although the possibility of non‐cell‐autonomous Wnt activation is supported by DKK1 redistribution and increased Axin2 levels.

In NM injury, early cSC depletion, likely due to vesicant cytotoxicity (Goswami et al. 2016; McNutt et al. 2012) and broader damage, may impair reprogramming and underlie persistent neurotrophic deficits (Geraci 2008; McNutt et al. 2012; Panahi et al. 2017). Consistent with post injury stage‐specific roles of Wnt signaling, delayed RM4404 treatment was more effective, suggesting benefit during a later trophic phase (van Vliet et al. 2021) and aligning with clinical priorities that initially favor tissue preservation over sensory restoration (Tervo et al. 1985; Yu and Rosenblatt 2007).

RM4404 also improved epithelial and stromal thickness after NM injury, suggesting broader regenerative effects. Given DKK1 expression in the limbal niche (Kulkarni et al. 2017), its inhibition may support epithelial function directly or indirectly via cSC‐derived trophic signals (Arthur‐Farraj et al. 2012; Jessen and Mirsky 2016; Mirmoeini et al. 2023). The increased presence of DKK1 beyond cSCs may reflect extracellular redistribution or vesicular export (Yamamoto et al. 2008). Notably, this stromal dispersion persisted after RM4404 withdrawal, indicating sustained alteration of DKK1 localization. As a Wnt antagonist, DKK1 blocks LRP5/6 and β‐catenin signaling (Ahn et al. 2011); its inhibition in the PNS is consistent with broader roles of Wnt signaling in Schwann cell plasticity and axonal regeneration (Grigoryan et al. 2013; Stanganello et al. 2019; Gonzalez‐Fernandez et al. 2024).

Our data support a model in which DKK1 perturbation by RM4404 promotes a phase‐ and dose‐dependent pro‐regenerative Wnt environment in the cornea, enhancing axonal regeneration and functional recovery without overt tissue dysregulation, likely through cSC‐derived trophic and guidance cues.

Lastly, several limitations should be noted, including reliance on mechanosensitivity as a functional readout for corneal sensation (Belmonte et al. 2004). Moreover, the technical demands of CMI (Mohan et al. 2000) could be restrictive for wider adoption. Although no sex differences were observed, larger cohorts are needed for confirmation. Future studies should define the molecular mechanisms by which RM4404‐mediated DKK1 antagonism modulates Wnt signaling across distinct cell populations and determine whether other Wnt activators yield similar improvements in corneal sensory recovery.
