Mechanism research on the treatment of gout with modified Simiao decoction based on network pharmacology and Mendelian randomization method
Authors: Jiaqi Guo, Weida Guan, Jiaming Liang, Yingqi Gao, Liyan Zhang, Xin Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The treatment of gout remains a huge challenge, and currently, the mainstream Western medicine treatment methods still have some drawbacks. This study aims to elucidate the potential mechanism of modified Simiao decoction (MSMD) in the treatment of gout through network pharmacology analysis, Mendelian randomization (MR), and molecular docking. We hope to contribute to the research on the targets of action of traditional Chinese medicine and the exploration of the mechanism of gout. We employed network pharmacology to screen the potential targets of MSMD in relation to gout. MR was utilized to further augment the potential targets and elucidate the causal associations between them and gout. Eventually, the identified targets were combined with active ingredients for the purpose of molecular docking. Through network pharmacology, we identified 44 potential targets. MR analysis identified 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR) as a potential causal risk factor for gout (odds ratio [95% confidence interval] = 1.192 [1.033–1.375], P = .016). Molecular docking predicted that TP53, IL6, TNF, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ, IL1B, and HMGCR exhibited favorable binding effects with the active ingredients. TP53, IL6, TNF, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ, and IL1B are central inflammatory targets of MSMD. Notably, HMGCR was identified as a causal risk factor for gout and a potential target of MSMD. We hypothesize that MSMD may exert therapeutic effects by inhibiting HMGCR activity, counteracting its risk effect. This hypothesis, along with the anti-inflammatory roles of other targets, warrants further experimental validation.

1. Introduction

Gout is a metabolic rheumatic disorder caused by purine metabolism dysfunction, leading to persistently elevated blood uric acid levels and the deposition of monosodium urate crystals in joints and surrounding tissues, which triggers intense inflammatory responses and tissue damage.[1,2] Current first-line Western treatments for acute gout attacks (including colchicine, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and glucocorticoids) are effective but often associated with significant adverse effects.[3,4] For instance, colchicine may cause gastrointestinal distress or even severe myelosuppression and organ damage.[5,6]

Given these limitations, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has gained increasing attention as an alternative or adjunctive therapy for gout.[7,8] Modified Simiao decoction (MSMD) is a classical TCM formula developed based on the principles of “clearing heat and promoting diuresis.” It has been widely used in clinical practice and has demonstrated efficacy in alleviating gout symptoms.[9–11] In TCM theory, gout is primarily attributed to “dampness-heat accumulation.” The therapeutic principle of MSMD, “clearing heat and promoting diuresis,” suggests its potential actions in modulating inflammatory responses (“heat”) and uric acid metabolism (“dampness”).[12,13] Therefore, MSMD may exert its effects by simultaneously targeting key inflammatory pathways and urate transporters. However, due to the multi-component nature of TCM formulas, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying MSMD’s anti-gout effects remain incompletely understood.[14] While previous network pharmacology studies have elucidated the mechanisms of the classical Simiao decoction in gout, our study focuses on a modified formulation (MSMD) that incorporates additional herbs such as “Chaihu and Banxia.”[15] These additions are intended to enhance the formula’s ability to “soothe the liver and resolve phlegm” in TCM terms, which may translate to broader immunomodulatory and metabolic regulatory effects. Therefore, the present study aims to identify not only the shared mechanisms with classical formulations but also the unique targets and pathways conferred by these modifications.

Although preliminary studies have explored the mechanism of Simiao-derived decoctions,[15] a comprehensive analysis that integrates causal inference via Mendelian randomization (MR) to prioritize targets is still lacking. Furthermore, the specific contribution of the modified components in MSMD remains to be determined. Network pharmacology, via high-throughput screening procedures, fabricates intricate connection networks that intricately interweave relevant drugs, targets, and diseases.[16,17] MR represents a highly efficacious statistical paradigm for probing the causal nexus between targets and diseases.[18,19] In recent years, it has occupied a central and pivotal position in the realm of drug target research.[20] This methodological approach exploits common genetic variants to decipher the causal linkages between the 2, thereby circumventing the confounding and bias factors that ubiquitously afflict “natural randomized trials.”[21] The amalgamation of network pharmacology and MR proffers a pragmatic conduit for exploring the pharmacological bedrock of traditional Chinese medicine prescriptions and evaluating the pharmacological modulation of gene targets.[22] Molecular docking can be judiciously employed to validate the interaction between active ingredients and key therapeutic targets.[23] Liu et al have embarked upon such investigative undertakings, leveraging network pharmacology, MR, and ancillary methodologies, and have successfully unearthed the molecular mechanisms by which Chinese herbal medicines ameliorate tinnitus.[24] Network pharmacology was first used to generate a holistic hypothesis by identifying potential active compounds and their candidate targets. However, associations derived from network pharmacology are observational and prone to confounding. To infer causal relationships between these candidate targets and gout, we subsequently employed MR, which utilizes genetic variants as instrumental variables to mitigate confounding. Finally, molecular docking was applied to simulate the binding affinities between the core active compounds and the prioritized causal targets, providing preliminary structural evidence for their interactions. This stepwise approach leverages the strengths of each methodology to move from correlation to causation.

Based on this rationale, we hypothesize that: MSMD alleviates gout through multi-component, multi-target interactions, primarily involving inflammatory, and metabolic pathways; the modified ingredients in MSMD confer additional therapeutic benefits by targeting unique causal genes related to immunomodulation and metabolic regulation.

2. Materials and methods

This study does not need ethical approval because there is no personal data involved.

2.1. Collection of active ingredients and targets of MSMD

MSMD is composed of the following ten herbs: huangbai (10 g), huangqin (10 g), chaihu (9 g), banxia (9 g), niuxi (10 g), cangshu (9 g), yanhusuo (10 g), yiyiren (30 g), tufuling (25 g), and gancao (10 g). With the aim of retrieving the constituent components of MSMD from the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology (TCMSP) platform, we strategically employed the names of Chinese herbal medicines as search keywords. Specifically, the terms “huangbai,” “huangqin,” “chaihu,” “banxia,” “niuxi,” “cangshu,” “yanhusuo,” “yiyiren,” “tufuling,” and “gancao” were precisely inputted for the retrieval operation. To screen for critical active compounds, oral bioavailability ≥ 30% and drug-likeness ≥ 0.18 were set as the screening criteria, as these are widely accepted thresholds in network pharmacology studies for identifying bioactive molecules with potential therapeutic utility.[25] By implementing this methodology, we conducted an in-depth exploration to identify the protein targets corresponding to the chemical constituents of “Jiawei Simiao Decoction.” Subsequently, we capitalized on the UniProt database (http://www.uniprot.org/) to append gene abbreviations to the ascertained targets and eliminated the redundant ones to execute standardization procedures.[26]

2.2. Identification of known therapeutic targets for gout

To amass the relevant targets of “gout,” we adopted a multi-database search approach. Specifically, we separately queried the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/), MalaCards database (https://www.malacards.org/), TTD database (https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/), and OMIM database (https://omim.org/)[27] using the disease keyword “gout.”

2.3. Network construction of targets between MSMD and gout

The “MSMD–active components–target genes–gout” network was constructed and visualized using Cytoscape software (version 3.10.2, Cytoscape Consortium, San Diego). In this network, nodes represent herbs, active compounds, targets, or the disease (gout), and edges represent the interactions between them.[28]

2.4. Construction of protein–protein interaction (PPI) network

A PPI network of the common targets was constructed using the STRING database (version 11.5), with “Homo sapiens” set as the organism and a minimum required interaction score of > 0.400 (medium confidence).[29] Disconnected nodes were hidden from the network. The resulting PPI data was then imported into Cytoscape for further topological analysis. The “cytoNCA” plugin was employed to calculate relevant network parameters, with the values of degree centrality, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, Eigenvector centrality, and median value is used to screen out core proteins.[30]

2.5. Exposure and outcome dataset

With respect to the exposure dataset, the eQTLGen Consortium provided a comprehensive collection of cis-expression quantitative trait locus (cis-eQTLs) data and allele frequency statistical information.[31] The 4463 genes included in the druggable gene list were sourced from a previous study.[32,33] The eQTLs analyzed in our study were restricted to single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) located within 1000 kb upstream or downstream of the endpoints of druggable genes. This selection criterion was based on the proximity to the target gene in the context of drug development research and the potential for more direct regulation of gene expression. Ultimately, eQTLs of 1450 druggable genes were successfully identified. For the outcome dataset, we utilized GWAS analysis of gout from the FinnGen database,[34] which incorporated data from 10,888 cases and 287,796 controls of European ancestry.

2.6. MR analysis

For MR analysis, the TwoSample MR (version 0.5.6) was utilized, which is an R package (Medical Research Council Integrative Epidemiology Unit [MRC-IE], Bristol, UK) specifically designed for conducting MR study.[35] Initially, the instrumental variables associated with exposure-related drugs were harmonized with the outcome dataset. Instrumental variables (IVs) for the exposure (gene expression) were selected according to the following rigorous criteria: SNPs significantly associated with the exposure (cis-eQTLs) were identified at a genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸); to ensure independence of the IVs, linkage disequilibrium clumping was performed with a stringent r² threshold of < 0.001 and a window size of 10,000 kb using the 1000 Genomes Project European reference panel; palindromic SNPs with ambiguous strand orientation were removed; to assess the strength of each IV, the F-statistic was calculated. All SNPs included had F-statistics > 10, indicating a low risk of weak instrument bias. Finally, the SNPs were harmonized with the outcome data to ensure the effect alleles were aligned.[36] Subsequently, a series of analytical methods were implemented, namely MR Egger, weighted median, inverse variance weighted (IVW), simple mode, weighted mode, and MR-PRESSO. Among these, the IVW method is the most prevalently adopted approach. The MR Egger and IVW techniques were employed to carry out heterogeneity tests. Cochrane Q value served as the metric for assessing the heterogeneity of genetic instruments; specifically, a P-value >.05 was indicative of the absence of substantial heterogeneity. The MR Egger regression equation was applied to evaluate the horizontal pleiotropy of genetic instruments, and a P-value >.05 signified the lack of horizontal pleiotropy. After eliminating outliers through the MR-PRESSO test, sensitivity analyses were reiterated. To guarantee that no single SNP exerted a significant influence on our research outcomes, the leave-one-out method was employed to successively remove each SNP. The results obtained from the IVW method were then compared with those of all variants. For the majority of druggable genes, multiple independent instrumental SNPs were available, allowing for the application of various MR methods. However, for the 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR) gene, only 2 independent SNPs (rs6453133 and rs7707394) meeting our stringent criteria were identified as suitable instrumental variables. When only 2 IVs are available, methods such as MR Egger and weighted median cannot provide stable or reliable estimates due to their inherent requirements for a larger number of variants. Therefore, the IVW method was employed as the primary analytical approach for HMGCR, as it provides the most reliable causal estimate under these circumstances. To assess the robustness of the IVW estimate, we additionally report the Cochrane Q statistic for heterogeneity. The absence of significant heterogeneity (P > .05) would increase confidence in the IVW result, as it suggests consistency between the causal estimates derived from each individual SNP.

2.7. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses

In order to comprehensively explore the potential mechanisms of action of the active components of MSMD in gout treatment and the functions of the common target genes, the Bioconductor and Cluster Profiler packages in the R-language platform were utilized. These tools were applied to conduct GO and KEGG pathway analyses on the target genes obtained from the previous step.[37,38] Specifically, the GO analysis mainly focused on the cellular component (CC), molecular function (MF), and biological process (BP) aspects of the potential target genes. This approach aimed to provide a more in-depth understanding of the underlying BPs and pathways involved in the therapeutic effects of MSMD on gout.

2.8. Molecular docking of active ingredients with core targets

Molecular docking was conducted for the acquired key active components and key targets. The key active components were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/).[39] Their 2D structures were then downloaded and imported into Chem 3Dsoftware to construct the 3D structural models of the key active components. During this procedure, the structure optimization function was utilized to achieve energy minimization, and the files were saved in the mol2 format. Subsequently, the 3D structures of the key targets that exhibited the highest degree of structural compatibility with the key active components were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/).[40] PyMOL (Schrödinger LLC, New York) was employed to perform operations such as water removal and separation of the original ligands. Thereafter, AutoDock Tools 1.5.6 (The Scripps Research Institute, La Jolla) was utilized to convert the files into the pdbqt format and identify the active binding pockets. Finally, molecular docking and visualization were accomplished using the AutoDock Vina software.

3. Results

3.1. Active ingredients in MSMD and targets screening

The constituents of MSMD were retrieved via the TCMSP platform. The chemical components of the aforementioned 10 traditional Chinese medicine compounds were designated as candidate active ingredients. The candidate active ingredients selected in the previous step were introduced into the TCMSP database to acquire protein targets. Subsequently, the UniProt database was utilized to convert gene names, thereby obtaining relevant protein targets that interact with the active ingredients (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q151). After merging and eliminating duplicates from the above-mentioned information, a total of 214 active ingredients and 255 gene targets were obtained.

3.2. Identification of known therapeutic targets for gout

Through databases such as Gene Cards (https://www.genecards.org/), the disease keyword “Gout” was searched. After removing duplicate values, a total of 623 gene targets related to “gout” were obtained (Fig. 1) (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q151). After mapping the potential drug targets with the disease-related gene targets, 44 common targets were obtained (Fig. 1).

(A) The Venn diagram of gout target genes. (B) The Venn diagram of MSMD–gout target genes. MSMD = modified Simiao decoction.

3.3. Construct a regulatory network for the traditional Chinese medicine compound

The active components of MSMD, target genes, and the disease of gout, which were obtained previously, were imported into the Cytoscape 3.10.2 software. Subsequently, a visualized network of “MSMD–active components–target genes–gout” was constructed (Fig. 2). In the process of analyzing the topological properties of the constructed network, based on the degree values of the drug active components, the top-5 core components were screened out. They are quercetin (degree 32), wogonin (degree 14), naringenin (degree 14), kaempferol (degree 12), and stigmasterol (degree 12). Analysis of the network topology reveals that a single chemical component often has multiple corresponding intersection gene targets. Conversely, 1 intersection gene target can also be associated with multiple chemical components simultaneously. Additionally, these chemical components are derived from different herbs within the formula. This complex network relationship indicates that MSMD exerts its therapeutic effects on gout through a multi-component, multi-target, and multi-pathway mode of action, which provides a theoretical basis for further exploring the underlying pharmacological mechanisms of this traditional Chinese medicine formula in treating gout.

3.4. PPI network

The obtained 44 common targets were imported into the STRING database for analysis (Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q151). The STRING database was applied to preliminarily construct a PPI network for the intersection genes (Fig. 3). The higher the degree of network connection indicates the closer the relationship between proteins. A total of 43 nodes (including 1 hidden node) and 345 edges are involved in the figure. Network nodes represent proteins and edges represent protein–protein associations. The interaction relationships between active components and common targets were imported into Cytoscape software and visualized (Fig. 4). Relevant network parameters were calculated using the “cytoNCA” plugin, and core targets were identified as those whose betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, degree centrality, and Eigenvector centrality values were all above the respective median values of the network. Finally, the top 5 targets screened out are TP53, IL6, TNF, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARG), and IL1B respectively (Fig. 5).

3.5. GO pathway enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment analysis was conducted on the 44 overlapping targets. GO enrichment analysis revealed that these targets were enriched in 127 MF terms, 1715 BP terms, and 45 CC terms. The top 10 results ranked by the adjusted P-value are presented. The results indicated that the genes were mainly enriched in the following pathways. In terms of BP, the functions were predominantly related to “response to xenobiotic stimulus,” “regulation of inflammatory response,” “response to lipopolysaccharide,” “response to molecule of bacterial origin,” etc. Regarding CC, the functions were mainly associated with “membrane raft,” “membrane microdomain,” “external side of plasma membrane,” etc. As for MF, the functions mainly included “cytokine activity,” “cytokine receptor binding,” etc (Fig. 6).

Enrichment analyses were respectively carried out for the 44 repeated targets. (A) The GO pathway enrichment analysis, and (B) the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. GO = gene ontology, KEGG = Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes.

3.6. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

The KEGG pathways of the core genes involved in the treatment of gouty arthritis by MSMD mainly include “lipid and atherosclerosis,” “IL-17 signaling pathway,” “PI3K–Akt signaling pathway,” etc (Fig. 6).

3.7. MR analysis

3.7.1. Candidate druggable genes for gout

This study examined the MR associations between 1450 proteins with accessible eQTL signals and the risk of gout. The results of this investigation can be found in the supplementary materials (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q151). After conducting MR analysis on the exposure and outcome, using a criterion of P < .05, 40 candidate druggable genes with strong MR evidence for gout were screened out. These were visualized and exported as a forest plot using R software (Fig. 7). As potential targets for the treatment of gout with MSMD, we selected the top 5 genes: HLA–DRB6, UBR1, ULK3, JUN, and HMGCR. Genetic prediction indicated that elevated levels of UBR1, ULK3, and HMGCR were associated with an increased risk of gout. Conversely, lower levels of HLA–DRB6 and JUN were correlated with an increased risk of gout, suggesting a negative correlation between these 2 proteins and the risk of gout (Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q151). Notably, for the HMGCR gene, only 2 independent instrumental SNPs were available. The IVW analysis indicated that genetically predicted HMGCR expression was associated with an increased risk of gout (odds ratio [95% confidence interval] = 1.192 [1.033–1.375], P = .016). Reassuringly, no significant heterogeneity was observed between the 2 SNPs (Cochrane Q = 0.747, P = .387), supporting the reliability of the IVW estimate.

The forest plot displays the results of 40 candidate druggable genes.

The 40 druggable genes selected through MR analysis were mapped against the 44 gene targets identified in network pharmacology. The mapping results were then visualized, yielding a Venn diagram. The only overlapping gene was found to be HMGCR (Fig. 8).

The top 3 active components ranked by degree value (quercetin, wogonin, and naringenin) were selected for molecular docking with the screened core genes (including HMGCR), and a heatmap of binding energy was generated (Fig. 9). The results indicated that there was a strong binding affinity between these active components and the targets, with binding energies of less than −5.0 kcal/mol. 4. Discussion

Given the limitations of conventional gout therapies, MSMD has emerged as a promising alternative. Our integrated analysis aimed to elucidate its potential mechanisms beyond the established anti-inflammatory effects of its parent formula.

First, by using network pharmacology, we identified 255 targets of the active components in MSMD and 623 potential targets for gout. There were 44 overlapping targets between them. This finding suggeststhat MSMD exerts its therapeutic effect on gout through a multi-component and multi-target pharmacological action. MSMD is a compound preparation composed of ten different herbs, and the key components for inhibiting or treating gout are quercetin, wogonin, and naringenin.

Furthermore, the CytoNCA plugin in the Cytoscape software was used to identify the potential therapeutic targets of MSMD for the treatment of gout. The potential targets are TP53, IL6, TNF, PPARG, and IL1B. Currently, numerous studies have confirmed that these targets, such as TP53, IL6, TNF, PPARG, and IL1B, are closely related to the inflammatory response. Some studies have shown that genetic variations in TP53 are associated with environmental adaptation.[41,42] It has been reported that TP53 can influence the stress responses of various cells and the expression of target genes, and induce cell senescence and apoptosis.[43] Activated TP53 can promote apoptosis, cell growth arrest, and DNA repair.[44] IL6 is a multifunctional cytokine that regulates inflammatory and immune responses[45] and is an important mediator of fever and the acute-phase response.[46] Some research indicates that the TNF superfamily plays a crucial role in many inflammatory and autoimmune diseases, such as driving the inflammatory response and stimulating lymphocyte activation.[47,48] As one of the most important pro-inflammatory cytokines, TNF-α can cause oxidative stress at the site of inflammation and is also involved in the formation of vasodilation and edema.[49] It has also been confirmed that the development of gout inflammation is positively correlated with the production of TNF-α.[50] IL6 can be continuously produced by fibroblasts stimulated by TNF-α and enhance the effects of TNF-α and IL-1β, thereby exacerbating the progression of inflammation.[51] PPARG inhibits inflammation by competitively inhibiting inflammatory signaling pathways and blocking the production of inflammatory mediators.[52] Su et al found that flavonoids can directly or indirectly bind to the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARG) protein, thereby regulating the activity of PPARG transcription factors.[53] And quercetin, one of the main active components in our study, belongs to the flavonoid family. IL-1β is produced by macrophages and can act on chondrocytes, inducing the degradation of the cartilage matrix and leading to cartilage damage. Inflammatory cytokines such as IL6, IL-1, and TNF-α can promote the inflammatory response and joint destruction in arthritis patients.[54–56] Research shows that gout is a typical inflammatory disease driven by the activation of the innate immune system, caused by the MSU-induced inflammatory response, mainly mediated by macrophages and neutrophils. The activation of the Nod-like receptor pyrin domain-containing protein 3 inflammasome and the release of IL-1β play a key role in the initiation of acute gout attacks, especially the activation of the Nod-like receptor pyrin domain-containing protein 3 inflammasome, which leads to the release of IL-1β and other inflammatory cytokines.[57–59] In addition, MSU can mediate the release of inflammatory factors, activate NF-κB to initiate and regulate the expression of genes related to TNF-α and inflammatory mediators and adhesion molecules involved in the inflammatory immune response, thereby accelerating the inflammatory response in gout. Our identification of well-established inflammatory targets such as IL6, TNF, and IL1B is consistent with previous network pharmacology studies on the classical Simiao decoction.[15] However, our study adds a causal layer to this association through MR analysis and explores the impact of additional herbs in the modified formula. While we had anticipated that these additions might reveal entirely novel targets, our results suggest their role may be to fine-tune the formula’s interaction with known inflammatory pathways or to contribute to the overall polypharmacological profile, rather than to introduce wholly distinct mechanisms.

Through MR analysis, we found that HLA–DRB6, UBR1, ULK3, JUN, and HMGCR were significantly associated with gout. Among them, UBR1, ULK3, and HMGCR are risk factors for the development of gout, while HLA–DRB6 and JUN are protective factors against gout. Moreover, HMGCR is one of the targets obtained from the mapping between MSMD and gout. Studies have demonstrated that HMGCR is involved in various autoimmune processes and is considered to participate in lipid and cholesterol metabolism.[60] The results of the KEGG enrichment analysis of the target genes in this study suggest that pathways such as “lipid and atherosclerosis,” “IL-17 signaling pathway,” and “PI3K–Akt signaling pathway” may be the potential action pathways through which MSMD treats gout.

Our MR analysis identified HMGCR as a potential causal risk factor for gout, where increased expression is associated with higher disease risk. Interestingly, this same gene was also identified as a target of MSMD through network pharmacology. This apparent paradox can be resolved by hypothesizing that the active compounds in MSMD (e.g., naringenin, wogonin) may function as inhibitors of HMGCR activity or expression. By antagonizing this risk factor, MSMD could potentially counteract its gout-promoting effects, thereby yielding a therapeutic benefit. This mechanism is analogous to statins, which are well-known HMGCR inhibitors, although the relationship between statins and gout risk is complex and not entirely straightforward. This intriguing hypothesis, generated by our multi-method approach, warrants explicit experimental validation in future studies to determine the exact directional effect of MSMD constituents on HMGCR function.

During the molecular docking process, we found that TP53, IL6, TNF, PPARG, IL1B, and HMGCR had good binding interactions with the active components of MSMD, such as quercetin, wogonin, and naringenin, and the binding energies were all less than −5.0 kcal/mol. The scores of chemical bond binding energy are used to interpret the docking results. The larger the absolute value of the negative score, the more stable the structure. According to the molecular docking results, PPARG had the lowest binding energy of −7.4 kcal/mol with quercetin. It is noteworthy that the binding energy we obtained for the quercetin–PPARG interaction (−7.4 kcal/mol) was stronger than that reported in a previous study of Simiao powder.[15] This discrepancy may arise from differences in docking software, parameters, or the specific protein conformation (Protein Data Bank ID) used for the analysis. Molecular docking scores are best interpreted as relative, rather than absolute, measures of binding affinity. Therefore, the key takeaway is not the precise value but the consistent prediction from both studies that a stable interaction is plausible. Based on the above analysis, PPARG inhibits inflammation by competitively inhibiting inflammatory signaling pathways and blocking the production of inflammatory mediators.[52] Quercetin has been proven to have a wide range of biological effects,[61] including anti-cancer, anti-inflammatory, and anti-viral activities, as well as the ability to attenuate lipid peroxidation, platelet aggregation, and capillary permeability.[62]

This study has several limitations that must be considered. First, the foundational network pharmacology approach is inherently associative and depends heavily on the completeness and accuracy of underlying databases and the chosen screening criteria (oral bioavailability/drug-likeness thresholds), which may introduce selection bias. Second, the minimal overlap between the targets identified by network pharmacology (44) and MR (40) (with only HMGCR in common) should not be interpreted as robust validation but rather as a complementary, hypothesis-generating convergence. Third, as discussed extensively, the HMGCR finding presents a conceptual challenge (a causal risk factor being a putative drug target) that we have proposed a mechanistic hypothesis for, but which requires definitive functional validation. Fourth, molecular docking only predicts binding affinity and cannot confirm whether the interaction is agonistic or antagonistic, nor can it account for complex in vivo conditions like compound metabolism, bioavailability, or off-target effects. Finally, our MR analysis relied on GWAS data from European populations, which may limit the generalizability of the genetic findings to other ethnicities.

5. Conclusions

This study employed network pharmacology, MR analysis, and molecular docking to explore the mechanism of MSMD in treating gout. The results indicated that quercetin, wogonin, and naringenin are the key bioactive compounds in MSMD for treating gout, while TP53, IL6, TNF, PPARG, and IL1B are the core targets of gout. Moreover, through MR analysis, HMGCR was identified as a potential risk factor, showing a positive correlation with the risk of gout. Molecular docking studies revealed that quercetin, wogonin, and naringenin had strong binding affinities with TP53, IL6, TNF, PPARG, IL1B, and HMGCR respectively. Among them, quercetin had the highest binding energy of −7.4 kcal/mol with PPARG. The docking results suggest the possibility of strong interactions between the above-mentioned compounds and the targets. The above research findings provide a basis for further investigating the potential of MSMD in treating gout and for further exploration of its molecular targets.
