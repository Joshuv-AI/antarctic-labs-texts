Changes in Gait After Training for Individuals With Cerebellar Ataxia
Authors: Affan Smani, Seonjoo Lee, Michael Spinner, Scott Barbuto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate gait changes for individuals who performed either home aerobic or balance training.DesignSingle-blind randomized clinical trial.SettingHome training and outcome assessments at large, tertiary, urban hospital.ParticipantsIndividuals with cerebellar ataxia (N=52).InterventionsIndividuals in the aerobic group trained 30 minutes per session, 5 times per week at up to 85% predicted maximum heart rate. Individuals in the balance group (control) performed 30 minutes of balance exercises of varying difficulty 5 times per week. Participants in both groups were expected to train for 1 year.Main Outcome MeasuresGait parameters (step length, step variability, stride width, gait velocity) were determined at 0, 6, and 12 months.ResultsOf the 52 individuals who agreed to participate, 24 individuals in each group had gait analysis performed. At 12 months, 20 and 19 participants had gait analysis performed in the balance and aerobic groups, respectively. Linear mixed effect model analysis was used to determine difference between individuals in the balance versus the aerobic group. Step length was the only parameter that showed statistically significant improvement with aerobic training over balance training (6mo: β, 4.1 cm; 95% CI, 0.76-7.42; P=.03; 12mo: β, 4.1 cm; 95% CI, 0.33-7.82; P=.04). We also found that individuals who hit training goals (either balance or aerobic) had statistically significant improvements in step length (6mo: β, 7.15 cm; 95% CI, 3.87-10.42; P<.001; 12mo: β, 7.16 cm; 95% CI, 4.20-10.13; P<.001), stride width (6mo: β, −2.72 cm; 95% CI, −4.33 to −1.11; P=.001; 12mo: β, −2.29 cm; 95% CI, −3.72 to −0.86; P=.002), and gait speed (6mo: β, 15.26 cm/s; 95% CI, 5.16-25.36; P=.004; 12mo: β, 16.85 cm/s; 95% CI, 7.89-25.80; P<.001) compared to those who did not hit training goals. Step variability was not improved with either training.ConclusionsThere was a minor improvement of increased step length in individuals with ataxia who performed home aerobic training compared to home balance training. However, individuals with ataxia who regularly performed either type of home training had statistically significant improvements in multiple gait parameters compared to those who did not train regularly.

Cerebellar ataxias are a group of neurologic disorders in which damage to the cerebellum leads to balance difficulties and incoordination.1 In addition to motor deficits, psychiatric symptoms and mood changes are also prevalent.2 For autosomal dominant forms, the disease often appears well into adulthood, but cases have occurred in children.3 There are now over 50 genetic ataxia subtypes, each with distinct phenotypic expressions.4 There are no drug treatments for most ataxia types,5 with exercise programs as the main method to improve motor symptoms and quality of life.6 Prognosis varies significantly among individuals with ataxia, but those with the most common forms can progress from symptom onset to wheelchair use within 10 years and death within about 20 years. Unfortunately, life expectancy and general morbidity are markedly lower than the average population.7, 8

Most research on exercise protocols for cerebellar ataxia have been conducted on balance training. In these studies, a variety of training approaches, including home training, inpatient training, and virtual reality-based training, have been used.9 Many of these programs have shown some level of effectiveness in alleviating symptoms of ataxia, especially if the training was supervised.10, 11, 12, 13 General measures of postural stability and dynamic balance were also shown to improve in a majority of these studies,14 although it is unclear if the benefit was of clinical meaningfulness.13 Changes in gait parameters are less often studied, although a few studies have shown balance training can improve step length and gait speed.10, 11, 12, 13

There is considerably less information on the impact of aerobic training on ataxia. The first study to examine its impact used a home-based cycling regimen for 4 weeks among 20 individuals with ataxia. Training only caused modest improvements in ataxia symptoms, and no gait metrics were examined.15 Because of the limited training performed in this study, our research team hypothesized that more rigorous aerobic training might lead to better improvements for individuals with ataxia. We first determined rigorous aerobic training, training 5 times per week for 30-minute sessions at 65%-85% maximum heart rate, was safe in this population.16 We next determined that home aerobic training improved ataxia symptoms more than home balance training, although improvements in measures of gait and balance were similar between groups.17

The mechanism in which training improves ataxia, balance, and gait parameters is unknown. Aerobic exercise with rat models of ataxia show it may slow disease progression.18, 19 Ataxic rats that performed wheel running had a 61.6% increase in cerebellar Purkinje cell survival and a 13%-14% increase in lifespan compared to their nontraining counterparts.18 Another study showed aerobic training caused an increase in proteins that are important in learning, memory, and neuroprotection, leading to improved coordination and slower neuronal loss.19 In addition, aerobic exercise enhances cardiovascular endurance, improves stability, and builds bone strength.20 Its repetitive and rhythmic elements may also improve neuroplasticity, leading to greater adaptation against cerebellar degeneration.21

Given the encouraging preliminary data, we conducted a larger (N=62) single blinded, randomized controlled trial comparing home balance to aerobic training that followed participants for a longer duration (1y). Training remained 5 times per week for 30-minute sessions at 65%-85% maximum heart rate. The Scale for the Assessment and Rating of Ataxia (SARA) was the primary measure with secondary measures of gait speed, Dynamic Gait Index (DGI), timed Up and Go (TUG), maximal oxygen consumption (VO2max), Fatigue Severity Scale, and World Health Organization Quality of Life-Bref. Individuals randomized to home aerobic training showed greater improvements in SARA, fatigue, and VO2max compared to individuals in home balance training. Both individuals in the aerobic and balance training groups demonstrated improvements in TUG, gait speed, and DGI, but there were no statistical differences between groups. Neither group showed improvements in quality of life including DGI.22

As part of the above study, individuals were asked to participate in a substudy in which they underwent a more detailed analysis of their gait. This analysis was not presented in the above-mentioned work. Consistent with our other findings, we hypothesized that home aerobic training would cause more improvements in gait parameters than balance training.

Methods

Study design

This is a substudy to an assessor-masked, single-center, randomized clinical trial of individuals with ataxia that compared home balance to aerobic training. Assessments were conducted at Columbia University Medical Center. The institutional review board for Columbia University approved the protocol, AAAT8388. All participants provided informed consent, and this trial followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials reporting guidelines.

Study participants

Participants with cerebellar ataxia were enrolled using convenience sampling from the ataxia clinic at the Neurological Institute of Columbia University Medical Center and the Coordination of Rare Diseases at Sanford registry during January 1, 2021 to September 1, 2023. Participants were eligible for inclusion in the trial if they had a clinical diagnosis of ataxia, were older than 18 years, could walk unaided, and had the ability to safely ride a stationary exercise bicycle. Exclusion criteria included poorly controlled or unstable cardiovascular disease, Montreal Cognitive Assessment score<23/30,22,23 disorders that would interfere with the ability to perform endurance exercise (eg, stroke, respiratory problems, traumatic brain injury, or neuromuscular disease), and/or evidence of serious arrhythmias or ischemic heart disease.

Randomization

Participants who met the criteria and agreed to participate were randomly allocated to home balance or home aerobic training in a 1:1 ratio through a computer-based permuted block randomization with a block size of 6 or 8. Block sizes were randomly generated. The randomization sequence was generated by an independent statistician (S.L.), and the details of the group allocation were concealed on cards placed inside sequentially numbered, sealed opaque envelopes.

Interventions

Aerobic training

Participants randomized to the aerobic group were given a stationary exercise bike (ProGear 225 Folding Magnetic Upright Exercise Bike with Heart Pulse) for home use. They were instructed to exercise 5 times per week. The regimen included a 5-minute warm up, 30 minutes of exercise at target heart rate, and 5 minutes of cool down. Subjects were instructed to monitor heart rate and adjust the exercise to remain in the target heart rate range (ie, by changing bike resistance or pedaling at different speeds). Individuals were instructed to train at 65%-85% age predicted maximum heart rate achieved during their baseline cardiopulmonary exercise testing testing. Participants were considered adherent to training if they achieve 80% of goals (training at least 24 minutes per session, at ≥65% max heart rate, 4 times/wk). Exercise fidelity was determined using either a Fitbit Charge 5 or Apple Watch Series 3.

Balance training

The balance training protocol has been published previously.24 Participants were given three levels of exercises: easy, moderate, and hard. At the initial assessment, the exercise physiologist evaluated the participants, instructed them on safe exercise performance, and recommended an exercise starting level. Participants were then instructed to perform 30 minutes of exercise, 5 times per week. Specifically, participants were asked to perform 6 different exercises, each for 5 minutes. Participants recorded training sessions on either a Fitbit Charge 5 or Apple Watch Series 3 to ensure exercise fidelity. Participants were considered adherent to training if they achieve 80% of their goals (training at least 24 minutes per session, with balance challenge score≥6,24 4 times/wk).

Gait assessments

Participants walked on a 6.10-m × 0.61-m instrumented walkway (Zeno Walkway, ProtoKinetics). They completed 4 passes over the instrumented walkway and were instructed to walk at their normal speed for overground walking conditions. Spatial and temporal characteristics of the gait were measured using the instrumented mat software PKMASa (ProtoKinetics Movement Analysis Software). The following gait parameters were analyzed: step length, step variability, stride width, and gait velocity. Step length is defined as the distance between the point of initial contact of one foot and the point of initial contact of the opposite foot. Stride width is defined as the lateral distance between the lines of progression of the left and right feet during walking. Step variability refers to the fluctuations in step length from one step to the next. Gait velocity is the speed the person walked over the gait mat in cm/s.

Sample size

The sample size for the main study was determined using pilot data comparing home aerobic to balance training on ataxia symptoms using SARA. In that study, we found a large effect size of 0.84 in favor of aerobic training.24 Assuming an attrition rate of 25%,24 a sample size of 48 participants with 24 in each arm was required to provide a 2-arm trial with 80% power to detect an effect size of at least 0.80 at a 5% level of significance. However, we were able to recruit more participants in this study over a 2-year period. Sample size was capped at 62 participants because of budgetary constraints for the original study.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted with R, version 4.02.b All variables were examined for illegitimate values, outliers, and inconsistencies. The distributions of variables were then examined and described in terms of means and standard deviations for continuous variables and proportions for categorical variables. The primary analysis was on the Intent-To-Treat principle. We accounted for dropouts by examining the primary outcome variables using (longitudinal) linear mixed effects models for continuous outcomes. Mixed effects models do not require complete measurement data to estimate the outcome variable. The inferences from analyses with missing data are valid under the missing at random assumption, which is not testable in most medical research and our study. Thus, we performed a sensitivity analysis by comparing the results from those of completers. For all analyses, we reported standardized effect sizes to guide future studies and meta-analyses.

Discussion

There are few treatment options for individuals with most types of cerebellar ataxia.25 Standard good practice is to recommend balance training to maintain function, but little is known about aerobic training. In this substudy, we wanted to compare changes in gait parameters between individuals who performed home aerobic verse balance training. In our past studies, we have found that home aerobic training provided more improvement in ataxia symptoms than home balance training.24 We expect this result is in part because of the difficulty for individuals with ataxia to adequately challenge themselves at home with balance training. Indeed, other studies looking at supervised balance training programs have shown more improvements than seen in studies where home balance training was conducted.26, 27, 28, 29 Thus, we expected home aerobic training to improve gait parameters more than home balance training.

To our surprise, we found only modest improvement in step length with aerobic training compared to balance training. There were no other statistical differences between gait parameters for the 2 groups. Although not tested in individuals with ataxia, aerobic training in stroke has shown to improve gait speed by enhancing (1) cardiovascular efficiency; (2) leg motor control; and (3) coordination by improving neuromuscular timing and limb loading patterns.30,31 In Parkinson’s disease, aerobic training enhances gait rhythmicity and reduces gait variability and fall risk.32,33 On the other hand, balance training can improve stride length by enhancing reactive balance, giving individuals more confidence to take longer steps without fear of falling.34 It also can enhance the ability to control the center of mass over the base of support, leading to more consistent and stable gait patterns.35 Thus, both types of training can lead to improvements, which may be why we did not detect differences between groups.

Consistent with the above rationale, we found that individuals in either group who training regularly had improved gait parameters when compared to individuals who did not hit training goals. Participants who met training goals had an increase in gait velocity of 15-17 cm/s, which is above the 10 cm/s difference outlined as clinically meaningful by Brach et al.36 Step length also increased by over 7 cm for those who trained regularly, which is also clinically meaningful.36 Finally, we saw that gait width improved on average by 0.65 cm for those who hit training goals compared to individuals who did not train having gait width worsen by 2.2 cm. These results stress the importance of training for individuals with cerebellar ataxia to maintain functional gait.

In the larger study, we found that these improvements in gait parameters echoed improvements in clinical scales.22 Although aerobic training caused more improvement in ataxia symptoms as measured by SARA than balance training, both training methods improved balance as measured by DGI and TUG as well as gait speed.22 These improvements in clinical measures will undoubtedly lead to improved functional abilities that will benefit individuals with ataxia. Interestingly, neither training method improved individual perception of quality of life.22 This may be because of other unaddressed concerns such as emotional well-being, social engagement, and finances. Further research will need to be conducted to follow these participants over a longer duration to determine if the functional improvements delay progression of disability. Future directions of work also include analyzing the relationship between the duration, frequency, intensity, and type of exercise to improvements in ataxia symptoms, balance, and gait to optimize training regimens for best impact.

Study limitations

There are several limitations to this study which must be highlighted. First, although one of the larger studies given the rarity of cerebellar ataxias, the sample size was still small. Moreover, individuals with different subtypes of spinocerebellar ataxias were allowed to participate to increase recruitment. Although this type of recruitment allows the study to be more representative of the ataxia population, it introduces heterogeneity into the study. Although relatively low for this 1-year exercise study, another limitation is that loss to follow-up could lead to attrition bias; participants who remained in the study potentially had healthier lifestyles or were more consistent with medical treatment, leading to better outcomes. Finally, the use of home training could have caused variances in training levels. We tried to compensate for this by using smartwatches to track training.

Conclusions

There was a minor improvement of increased step length in individuals with ataxia who performed home aerobic training compared to home balance training. However, individuals with ataxia who regularly performed either type of home training had statistically significant improvements in multiple gait parameters compared to those who did not train regularly.
