“External Fixation Tower” for Lower Extremity Microvascular Reconstruction With All-around Protection and Morphological Adaptability
Authors: Yu Kagaya, Minoru Takeji, Yuki Okawa, Natsumi Shirouzu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Summary:This ideas and innovations report addressed a novel method of “external fixation tower” for the postoperative management of lower extremity free flap reconstruction. Traditional splinting methods often fail to accommodate the complex shape of the reconstructed foot and maintain proper limb positioning, which is insufficient to secure flap safety, and can lead to complications such as equinus contracture and pressure ulcers. We present a case of a 73-year-old man with a diabetic gangrene over the right Achilles tendon and heel who underwent free latissimus dorsi myocutaneous flap transfer, using the Hoffmann 3 external fixation system for postoperative management. The external fixation tower provided complete circumferential protection of the foot, including vascular anastomotic site, total pressure offloading, and elevation of the flap. It simultaneously served multiple functions, including ankle joint fixation to prevent equinus contracture and heel offloading to prevent pressure ulcers, and provided a platform for gauze placement beneath the flap. The postoperative course was uneventful. As the postoperative flap perfusion stabilized, the configuration of the external fixator was progressively simplified, and rehabilitation was done while wearing it. In lower extremity reconstruction using free flaps, postoperative management with external fixation of Hoffmann 3 system provides all-around protection, allows for adaptive morphological modifications based on the clinical situation, and enables rehabilitation and ambulation while wearing it. The method is considered not only to reduce various postoperative complications and lessen the burden on doctors and nurses for postoperative care but also to produce favorable outcomes.

Takeaways

Question: Free flap reconstruction of the lower extremity carries various postoperative risks, including hemodynamic instability due to involuntary leg movements and equinus contracture or pressure ulcers resulting from prolonged immobilization.

Findings: The “external fixation tower” offers multiple advantages; it protects the vascular anastomotic site, provides complete offloading and elevation of the flap and foot, and prevents equinus deformity through ankle immobilization. The configuration can be gradually simplified during the recovery phase, and rehabilitation can be initiated while wearing it.

Meaning: In lower extremity reconstruction using free flaps, postoperative management with external fixation provides all-around protection and allows for adaptive morphological modifications based on the clinical situation.

CASE AND INNOVATION PRESENTATION

Free flap reconstruction of the lower extremity is generally considered to have a higher incidence of vascular complications than that in other regions, such as head and neck or breast.1,2 Several factors contribute to this increased risk, including the susceptibility of hemodynamic changes due to foot elevation or dependency,3 arterial calcification in peripheral artery disease, diabetics, and old age.4 Postoperative delirium is a well-recognized risk after free flap surgery,5 and delirium-induced involuntary movements can be more pronounced in the extremities—particularly the lower limbs, which are difficult to control because of their greater muscle strength.

To ensure postoperative immobilization and pressure offloading of the flap site, splints are commonly used after lower limb free flap surgery.6 However, splints alone are often insufficient in accommodating the complex shape of a reconstructed foot and maintaining specific limb positions necessary for complete pressure offloading. Additionally, prolonged postoperative immobilization can lead to equinus contracture and pressure ulcers caused by the splint itself.7 These complications may delay ambulation, necessitate additional treatments, or, in severe cases, result in a scenario where the limb is salvaged but the walking function is lost.

To address these issues, we used the Hoffmann 3 external fixation system (Stryker Inc.) for foot and lower leg free flap reconstruction. The Hoffmann 3 system offers both high fixation strength and morphological adaptability. Although the Hoffmann 3 system is typically used for open fracture fixation or correction, we have adapted its use by constructing a specialized configuration, referred to as an “external fixation tower” in this report, for postoperative management of free flap reconstruction.

Herein, we present a case of Achilles tendon and heel free flap reconstruction demonstrating the protective capacity and morphological adaptability of the external fixation tower in postoperative management after lower limb free flap reconstruction. The patient whose photographs were used in the present article provided informed consent for the report.

A 73-year-old male patient underwent free latissimus dorsi myocutaneous flap transfer for a diabetic gangrene over the right Achilles tendon and heel. The thoracodorsal artery and vein were anastomosed with the anterior tibial artery and vein (end to side). The calcaneus was affected by osteomyelitis, and a partial bone debridement was performed 4 weeks before the flap transfer. Intravenous ceftazidime (2 g) administration every 8 hours was started and continued for a total of 8 weeks until 4 weeks after flap surgery.

The Hoffmann 3 external fixation system was used, with four 5-mm half pins inserted into the tibia and four 3-mm half pins inserted into the metatarsal bones, forming an external fixation tower (Figs. 1 and 2). This external fixation tower provided complete circumferential protection of the foot including the vascular anastomosis site, ensured total pressure offloading and elevation of the flap, and simultaneously served multiple functions: ankle joint stabilization to prevent equinus contracture, heel offloading to prevent pressure ulcers, and provision of a platform for gauze placement beneath the flap.

No postoperative wound complications occurred, and the flap survived without any complications. From postoperative day (POD) 14, the external fixation structure was simplified and non-weight-bearing rehabilitation was initiated on the bed. Temporary limb lowering was permitted while ensuring complete protection of the flap (Fig. 3). On POD 21, the external fixation was further simplified to allow partial weight-bearing during rehabilitation, yet enabling flap offloading while in bed (Fig. 4). On POD 28, the metatarsal pins were removed to allow ankle mobility, and gait training was initiated with the use of an orthotic insole. At this stage, the external fixator was used solely for pressure offloading of the flap in the supine position. The external fixator was completely removed on POD 42, and both dorsiflexion and plantarflexion functions of the ankle joint were well preserved. DISCUSSION

External fixation has been used for open fractures of the lower leg in combination with free flap and has been reported as a microsurgical-friendly tool from the perspective of flap protection.8 Recent reports highlight its use for offloading after flap reconstruction of a diabetic foot.9 The current case presents a further advancement in the application of external fixation for lower extremity free flap reconstruction.

The external fixation tower’s structure was not based on a predesigned blueprint; rather, it emerged as a necessary form, assembled step by step with careful consideration of the areas requiring protection. During surgery, pins were inserted, and a simple kickstand-type frame was assembled. The full external fixation configuration was constructed postoperatively in the ward by multiple staff members working simultaneously. This workflow avoided unnecessary prolongation of operative time and helped to reduce anesthesia duration, thereby lessening the burden on the patient.

To achieve maximal stability and flap safety during the critical first 1–2 weeks—when flap perfusion remains at risk—a scaffold-like structure using multiple external fixation components should be constructed. This establishes conditions under which the safety of the flap and anastomosed vessels is maintained regardless of the patient’s orientation or foot movement, while preventing equinus contracture and pressure ulcers. These measures significantly reduce the postoperative management burden on surgeons and nurses. As flap perfusion stabilizes, components can gradually be removed, allowing for a balanced approach that ensures flap protection while simultaneously facilitating rehabilitation (Figs. 3 and 4). (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E441.)

Pin-site infection is a potential risk associated with the use of external fixation. although such infections can largely be prevented through diligent pin-site care and appropriate dressing techniques, pin removal in the event of a deep infection is typically sufficient to resolve it.10 Currently, limited evidence and no consensus exist regarding the optimal regimen for prophylactic antibiotic administration to prevent pin-site infections.10

In conclusion, the external fixation tower method using Hoffmann 3 provides all-around protection of the reconstructed foot and allows for adaptive morphological modifications based on the clinical situation. However, its safety and comparison with conventional methods require further investigation.
