Identification of biomarkers for non-invasive diagnosis and risk stratification in prostate cancer using NMR-based metabolomics and machine learning
Authors: Xi Zhang, Minjiang Chen, Binbin Xia, Binrui Liu, Xiaoya Lin, Hanyang Tao, He Wang, Tengfei Gu, Jie Li, Baijun Dong, Hongchang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The non-invasive diagnosis and risk stratification of prostate cancer (PCa) remain clinically challenging due to the limited specificity of prostate-specific antigen (PSA). In this retrospective study, we applied a comparative machine learning (ML) framework to rank and select biomarkers from serum 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)-based metabolomics, subsequently developing three sequential metabolite panels. These metabolite-based models effectively distinguished overall PCa from benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), clinically non-significant prostate cancer (cnsPCa) from BPH, and clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa) from cnsPCa. All models achieved areas under the curve (AUC) consistently above 0.9 in both discovery and validation cohorts, without incorporating clinical variables such as age or PSA. Decision curve analysis (DCA) further confirmed their superior clinical utility over the current PSA-based strategy. This study underscores the potential of ML-driven metabolomics for accurate, non-invasive diagnosis and effective risk stratification of PCa, which could significantly improve patient management and reduce unnecessary interventions.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the second most common cause of cancer-related death among men worldwide, accounting for 15% of all male cancers1. In 2020, there were approximately 1.4 million new cases of PCa, and this number is projected to reach 2.9 million by 20402. The often slow and subtle progression of PCa frequently leads to diagnosis at advanced stages, when clinical and pathological features become more evident3. Given the rising incidence, the development of early and accurate diagnostic systems is urgently needed4. Currently, PCa screening primarily relies on prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing, followed by digital rectal exams (DRE) and multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI) to guide biopsy decisions and targeting5. However, PSA levels can be elevated by conditions unrelated to PCa, such as benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)6, inflammation7, acute urinary retention, and infections8. As a result, although most individuals with elevated PSA levels have benign conditions, many still undergo invasive biopsies and potential overdiagnosis. Although mpMRI improves the detection of clinically significant disease, its widespread use is limited by availability, cost, and interpretation variability9. Thus, the current PSA-based screening paradigm and the subsequent confirmatory biopsy imposes a considerable social and economic burden on patients and healthcare systems, often leading to overdiagnosis and unnecessary biopsies and treatments10,11.

PCa treatment strategy depends on various factors, including tumor stage, grade (Gleason score, GS), PSA levels, and the patient’s age and health status12. Patients with a GS below 7 (clinically non-significant prostate cancer, cnsPCa) may be managed through active surveillance or watchful waiting. In contrast, those with a GS of 7 or higher (clinically significant prostate cancer, csPCa) require timely intervention due to a higher risk of progression and reduced survival rates13. At present, no accurate non-invasive biomarkers exist for predicting csPCa prior to biopsy. Consequently, there is an imperative for a diagnostic tool that can accurately stratify risk among patients with elevated PSA levels, precisely the cohort from whom PSA itself proves inadequate for guiding clinical management.

Metabolite profiling has shown promise in assessing PCa aggressiveness, guiding treatment decisions, and monitoring therapeutic responses14. Serum metabolite biomarkers offer a non-invasive, accessible, and cost-effective approach for early detection and disease monitoring15. Previous studies have demonstrated that serum metabolite alterations provided distinct biomarker signatures capable of discriminating PCa from BPH and healthy controls16–18. Moreover, metabolite biomarkers not only improve the specificity and sensitivity of PCa screening but also provide a systemic metabolic signature associated with tumor development and progression17–19. Although previous metabolomic studies have successfully identified metabolic disturbances associated with PCa progression20,21, the accurate initial triage of patients with elevated PSA into BPH versus overall PCa, and the subsequent critical risk stratification between cnsPCa and csPCa, remain major unresolved challenges.

Machine learning (ML), a prominent artificial intelligence (AI) technique, is increasingly applied across biomedical fields to autonomously process complex data22,23. It offers significant advantages in interpreting omics data, developing predictive models, identifying biomarkers, and stratifying patients for precision medicine24,25. Integrating serum metabolite biomarkers with ML holds promise for pinpointing critical metabolites for disease detection, reducing unnecessary biopsies, and ultimately advancing personalized treatment.

In this study, a 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)-based metabolomics approach was utilized to characterize the serum metabolite profiles from BPH, cnsPCa and csPCa patients. A key methodological advance is the systematic application and comparison of multiple ML algorithms to refine metabolite selection, moving beyond traditional statistical methods. An optimized biomarker panel was developed using the resulting discriminant metabolites and further validated in an independent cohort. This study aims to uncover metabolic mechanisms underlying PCa development and to establish three diagnostic models: one for the initial discrimination of overall PCa from BPH, a second for distinguishing cnsPCa from BPH, and a third for discriminating csPCa from cnsPCa. This comprehensive approach may provide new insights for diagnosis, management and improved risk stratification of PCa in clinical practice.

Results

In this study, a total of 270 participants with PSA levels ranging from 4 to 20 ng/mL were recruited, including 128 BPH patients, 95 cnsPCa patients, and 47 csPCa patients. These participants were divided into a discovery cohort (BPH = 67, cnsPCa = 49, csPCa = 26) and an independent validation cohort (BPH = 61, cnsPCa = 46, csPCa = 21). Serum metabolic segregations among BPH, cnsPCa, and csPCa patients

A 1H NMR-based metabolomics approach was utilized to analyze the metabolite profiles in serum samples from 128 BPH patients, 95 cnsPCa patients, and 47 csPCa patients. Supervised orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) model was applied to the discovery cohort to assess metabolic pattern changes and identify key metabolites using the variable importance in projection (VIP) score. As shown in Fig. 2B, a clear separation was observed between BPH and PCa patients. The model’s robustness was confirmed by a 100-times permutation test (R2Y = 0.899, Q2 = 0.769, p-value < 0.01; Fig. 2C). A threshold of VIP >1.5 was set to select key metabolites, LDL/VLDL, leucine, β-HB, lactate, acetone, acetoacetate, glutamine, citrate, creatine and formate were identified (Fig. 2D). Similarly, the OPLS-DA model effectively distinguished cnsPCa from BPH subjects (Fig. 2E), with validated parameters (R2Y = 0.932, Q2 = 0.543, p-value < 0.01; Fig. 2F). Key metabolites for this comparison included LDL/VLDL, leucine, valine, β-HB, alanine, acetone, acetoacetate, glutamine, citrate, and formate (Fig. 2G). Significant metabolic pattern changes were also observed between csPCa and cnsPCa patients (Fig. 2H). The model validity was confirmed (R2Y = 0.93, Q2 = 0.414, p-value < 0.01; Fig. 2I), and key metabolites including leucine, β-HB, lactate, lysine, acetate, pyruvate, glutamine, citrate, creatine, creatinine, phenylalanine, and formate (Fig. 2J).

Metabolites with VIP >1.5 and p-value <0.05 were selected for further analysis. Relative to BPH patients, PCa patients showed significantly decreased levels of acetone, β-HB, acetoacetate, creatine, leucine, glutamine and citrate, but increased formate level. Diagnostic performance of potential biomarker panel for distinguishing overall PCa from BPH patients

To further identify the optimal combination of potential biomarkers for distinguishing overall PCa patients from BPH subjects, three ML algorithms (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and random forest (RF)) were employed to rank and select the most significant biomarkers from the pre-filtered candidate pool (VIP > 1.5, p < 0.05). All three ML methods consistently selected the same six metabolites: glutamine, leucine, β-HB, citrate, formate and creatine (Fig. 4A, B for LASSO; Fig. 4C, D for RF; Fig. 4E, F for XGBoost). To quantitatively assess the risk of overfitting relative to our sample size, we performed a learning curve analysis on three ML algorithms. Supplementary Fig. 1 illustrates the learning curves of all ML model, where the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) was used to evaluate performance across different training sample sizes. The training set achieved consistently high AUC (close to 1), and the test set’s AUC gradually increased to align with the training set, demonstrating all model’s low overfitting risk and strong generalization capability (Supplementary Fig. 1A–C). Subsequently, to ensure a fair comparison for determining whether adding clinical variables to our metabolite panel could enhance diagnostic performance, we built and evaluated four distinct models (biomarkers; biomarkers_PSA; biomarkers_age; biomarkers_PSA_age) within the same statistical framework (standard logistic regression) on the discovery cohort. As detailed in Fig. 4G, H and Supplementary Table 2, ROC analysis illustrates that the model based solely on the six metabolites (AUC = 0.965, 88.0% sensitivity, 92.5% specificity) performed comparably to models additionally incorporating age (AUC = 0.972, 89.3% sensitivity, 95.5% specificity, p = 0.22), PSA (AUC = 0.964, 89.3% sensitivity, 91.0% specificity, p = 0.78), or both (AUC = 0.972, 86.7% sensitivity, 97.0% specificity, p = 0.22) (DeLong’s test, all p > 0.05), indicating that the metabolic profile captures the essential diagnostic information. Moreover, decision curve analysis (DCA) confirmed that the model based on the metabolites panel alone provided a net benefit comparable to the models incorporating clinical variables (PSA and/or age) across a wide range of threshold probabilities, as shown in Fig. 4I. Both the PSA-only model and the “treat all” or “treat none” strategies offered substantially lower net benefit, underscoring the superior value of the metabolite-based approach. The diagnostic potential of the serum biomarkers was then validated by an independent validation cohort of 128 serum samples (BPH = 61, PCa = 67). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2A–F, PCa patients exhibited higher formate levels and lower levels of β-HB, creatine, citrate, leucine and glutamine compared to BPH patients, indicating a high reproducibility of the discriminant biomarkers across both the discovery and validation cohorts. Moreover, the ROC analysis revealed that an integration of these six metabolites in the validation cohort had a 0.926 AUC value, along with a sensitivity of 94.0% and a specificity of 75.4% (Fig. 4J and Supplementary Table 3). Similarly, the DCA analysis of the validation cohort demonstrated an excellent clinical value, with the biomarker model showing a significant net benefit over PSA across a wide range of risk thresholds (Fig. 4K). Subsequently, the classification model trained on the discovery cohort was tested on the external validation cohort to assess its diagnostic performance. As shown in Fig. 4L, the model could correctly diagnose 63 cases of our 67 PCa patients with an accuracy of 94.0%. Therefore, the metabolite biomarker panel displayed a good diagnostic capability for differentiating overall PCa patients from BPH subjects.

Diagnostic performance of potential biomarker panel for distinguishing cnsPCa from BPH patients

The LASSO regression, XGBoost, and RF algorithms were also applied to identify biomarkers for distinguishing cnsPCa from BPH patients. As illustrated in Fig. 5A, B, LASSO selected 4 key metabolites (citrate, alanine, β-HB, glutamine) based on the lambda.min value. RF algorithm results indicated that a model comprising six metabolites achieved the highest accuracy of 83.9% (Fig. 5C), and the optimal biomarker combination was determined to include citrate, alanine, β-HB, glutamine, leucine, and valine (Fig. 5D). Using cross-validation and feature importance evaluation with the XGBoost algorithm, a diagnostic model comprising five metabolites (citrate, alanine, glutamine, leucine, and β-HB) was identified as the optimal biomarker combination (Fig. 5E, F). As detailed in Fig. 5G and Supplementary Table 2, ROC analysis demonstrated that the LASSO regression model achieved an AUC of 0.901, with a sensitivity of 87.0% and a specificity of 83.9%. The RF algorithm yielded an AUC of 0.922, a sensitivity of 88.9% and a specificity of 83.6%. The XGBoost algorithm achieved an AUC of 0.917, a sensitivity of 90.1% and a specificity of 73.0%. ROC analysis of the models on the discovery cohort showed high AUCs (LASSO: 0.901; RF: 0.922; XGBoost: 0.917), with no significant performance gain from the larger panels. Therefore, the concise 4-metabolite panel from LASSO (citrate, alanine, β-HB, glutamine) was selected. Learning curve analysis also indicates that the LASSO model exhibits low risk of overfitting, as the performance on the test set closely matched that on the training set (Supplementary Fig. 1D). As shown in Fig. 5H, I and Supplementary Table 2, adding age (AUC = 0.950, 87.8% sensitivity, 88.1% specificity, p = 0.18), PSA (AUC = 0.941, 91.8% sensitivity, 80.6% specificity, p = 0.42), or both (AUC = 0.952, 79.6% sensitivity, 97.0% specificity, p = 0.23) to this panel did not significantly improve the AUC compared to the biomarkers alone (AUC = 0.936, 93.9% sensitivity, 79.1% specificity; DeLong’s test, all p > 0.05). Diagnostic performance of potential biomarker panel for distinguishing cnsPCa from csPCa patients

ML algorithms were also applied to identify biomarkers for discriminating cnsPCa and csPCa patients, based on the key metabolites with VIP value >1.5 and p-value <0.05. LASSO selected a 5-metabolite panel (formate, citrate, creatine, leucine, glutamine; Fig. 6A, B), RF selected a 6-metabolite panel (leucine, glutamine, formate, citrate, creatine and pyruvate; Fig. 6C, D), and XGBoost selected a 4-metabolite panel (creatine, formate, leucine and citrate; Fig. 6E, F). The LASSO model achieved an AUC of 0.920, with a sensitivity of 76.2% and a specificity of 95.7%; the RF model an AUC of 0.932, with a sensitivity of 76.2% and a specificity of 86.9%; and the XGBoost model an AUC of 0.867, with a sensitivity of 71.4% and a specificity of 89.1% (Fig. 6G and Supplementary Table 2). As the RF and XGBoost models did not show a significant improvement over the more parsimonious LASSO model, the 5-metabolite (formate, citrate, creatine, leucine and glutamine) LASSO panel was chosen. Supplementary Fig. 1G indicates that the LASSO model exhibits low risk of overfitting, as the performance on the test set closely matched that on the training set. As detailed in Fig. 6H, I and Supplementary Table 2, the sequential addition of age (AUC = 0.939, 80.8% sensitivity, 95.9% specificity, p value of 0.36), PSA (AUC = 0.946, 84.6% sensitivity, 93.9% specificity, p value of 0.89), or both (AUC = 0.944, 80.8% sensitivity, 97.9% specificity, p value of 0.92) did not significantly enhance the diagnostic performance of the metabolic panel (AUC = 0.945, 76.9% sensitivity, 100% specificity; All DeLong’s test p > 0.05), indicating that the metabolite biomarker model captures the essential diagnostic information. As shown in Fig. 6J, DCA analysis revealed that the metabolite biomarker model yields a net benefit comparable to the models incorporating clinical variables (PSA and/or age) across a wide range of threshold probabilities. Discussion

The accurate non-invasive diagnosis and effective risk stratification of PCa remain major challenges in clinical practice. Traditional PSA testing alone cannot reliably distinguish between BPH, cnsPCa, and csPCa, and biopsies are associated with risks, including infection and overtreatment of indolent cancers26. Furthermore, existing imaging modalities offer limited predictive value for early csPCa27. ML has revolutionized disease diagnosis by offering advanced algorithms that can analyze complex, high-dimensional data28–31. In line with this paradigm, we developed a diagnostic strategy that integrates ¹H NMR-based serum metabolomics with a comparative ML framework. In this study, our comparative ML approach, employing three distinct algorithms (LASSO, RF, XGBoost), proved highly effective in refining parsimonious and robust biomarker panels from a pre-filtered candidate pool. This multi-algorithm strategy moves beyond traditional univariate statistics, ensuring the selection of optimal metabolite combinations for collective predictive performance. The most significant advancements of our study lie in its specific clinical focus, rigorous validation, and methodological approach. While many studies have identified metabolite panels32–34, a substantial number lack independent validation or do not specifically target the critical risk stratification between cnsPCa and csPCa. We developed and validated three distinct metabolite panels, each targeting a critical clinical decision point, thus forming a sequential diagnostic cascade. The resulting panels demonstrate a complementary diagnostic profile that directly addresses the well-documented limitations of PSA specificity, forming a coherent diagnostic cascade for sequential clinical decision-making.

First, for the initial triage of patients with elevated PSA, a six-metabolite panel (citrate, β-HB, leucine, glutamine, creatine, formate) was established to initially differentiate of overall PCa from BPH. It serves as a high-accuracy “rule-out” test to reduce unnecessary biopsies in patients with elevated PSA, achieving excellent performance in both discovery (AUC = 0.965, sensitivity 88.0%, specificity 92.5%) and validation (AUC = 0.926, sensitivity 94.0%, specificity 75.4%) cohorts. This performance profile is particularly valuable for initial screening. The high sensitivity ensures that most true PCa cases are identified, while the reasonable specificity helps to reduce unnecessary biopsies in men with BPH8,9. The increase in sensitivity from discovery to validation cohort (88.0%–4.0%) is particularly noteworthy, though the decrease in specificity warrants attention in future applications. The comprehensive diagnostic metrics, including positive and negative predictive values (PPV, NPV), are detailed in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3. The observed false positives, while leading to unnecessary biopsies under current standards, are a less critical error than missing a cancer, and our model’s primary aim is precisely to reduce this overall biopsy burden.

Second, to refine diagnosis among those suspected of PCa, the four-metabolite panel (citrate, alanine, β-HB, glutamine) further distinguishes cnsPCa from BPH, supporting the identification of indolent disease for active surveillance. This panel maintains strong diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.936 in discovery, 0.919 in discovery) with high sensitivity (93.9% in discovery, 80.4% in validation) and good specificity (79.1% in discovery, 90.2% in validation). The consistently high specificity across both cohorts is clinically important as it minimizes false positives when identifying cnsPCa, thereby strengthening confidence in recommending active surveillance for appropriate patients and helping to avoid overtreatment of indolent disease10,11. The maintained high specificity in the validation cohort underscores its reliability as a confirmatory test. The low false positive rate is key here, as incorrectly labeling a BPH patient as having cancer (even if indolent) could cause unnecessary psychological distress and clinical follow-up.

Third, for crucial risk stratification, the five-metabolite panel (formate, citrate, creatine, leucine, glutamine) discriminates csPCa from cnsPCa. This panel functions as a high-specificity “rule-in” test to prioritize high-risk patients for intervention. This sequential multi-model approach provides a comprehensive, non-invasive diagnostic solution aligned with the principles of personalized medicine. This panel demonstrated robust performance (AUC = 0.945/0.907) characterized by exceptionally high specificity (100% in discovery, 91.3% in validation) with moderate sensitivity (76.9% in discovery, 81.0% in validation). This performance profile is clinically rational for risk stratification, where a positive result strongly indicates csPCa, ensuring that high-risk patients are prioritized for timely intervention while minimizing unnecessary biopsies in those with indolent disease5,8. The trade-off is a moderate sensitivity, meaning some aggressive cancers (false negatives) might be misclassified as indolent. The improvement in sensitivity from discovery to validation cohort (76.9%–81.0%) while maintaining high specificity is particularly encouraging. However, the strong NPV confirms its utility in confidently identifying low-risk patients who may be candidates for active surveillance.

The clinical utility of this ML-driven metabolomic cascade was further confirmed. DCA demonstrated its superior net benefit over the PSA-only strategy. Notably, adding clinical variables (age, PSA) did not significantly improve performance, underscoring that the serum metabolic profile itself captures essential diagnostic biology. The progressive nature of these three panels creates a comprehensive diagnostic pathway: beginning with high-sensitivity triage (BPH vs. PCa), progressing to balanced performance for intermediate stratification (BPH vs. cnsPCa), and culminating in high-specificity risk assessment (cnsPCa vs. csPCa). This cascading approach mirrors the sequential clinical decision-making process in PCa management and offers a more nuanced alternative to the current PSA threshold strategy.

The metabolite panels identified through our ML approach revealed systematic alterations in serum metabolite profiles across the disease spectrum from BPH to cnsPCa and csPCa. These changes predominantly involved energy metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and ketone body metabolism, providing crucial insights into the metabolic reprogramming associated with PCa development and progression. The most prominent metabolic alterations centered on energy metabolism pathways. Our observation of progressively decreasing levels of citrate from BPH to csPCa is one of the most consistent findings in PCa metabolomics, aligning with the established knowledge of impaired citrate metabolism in prostatic malignancy35–37. Similarly, the observed decrease in glutamine levels in PCa compared to BPH, and its further reduction in csPCa relative to cnsPCa, reflects increased utilization of a source of nitrogen to support tumor growth through various biosynthetic pathways38,39. Creatine, included in two of the three diagnostic panels, displayed progressively reduced levels from BPH to PCa and further to csPCa. This consistent decline indicates a disturbance in cellular energy storage and transfer mechanisms that intensifies with disease advancement40. The ketone body metabolism pathway was represented by β-HB, which exhibited significantly decreased levels in cnsPCa and overall PCa patients compared to BPH. This contrasts with hypotheses suggesting that ketone bodies, as alternative energy substrates, may support tumor growth41,42. This divergence may be explained by the systemic nature of our serum measurements; the observed depletion could indicate an accelerated consumption of β-HB within the tumor microenvironment, a notion supported by findings that ketone body utilization can promote cancer progression43. This highlights the critical distinction between circulating metabolite pools and localized tumoral flux. Amino acid metabolism alterations were evidenced by changes in leucine and alanine. The observed reduction in leucine levels in PCa patients compared to BPH, with a further significant decrease in csPCa relative to cnsPCa, aligns with several serum metabolomics studies reporting decreased branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) levels in PCa patients44. While some tissue-based studies report intracellular accumulation, the reduction in systemic levels likely indicates accelerated catabolism to fuel the TCA cycle and meet the heightened energy demands of aggressive tumors38,39. This hypothesis is strongly supported by the significant negative correlation we observed between serum leucine levels and Gleason score. Alanine showed a distinct pattern with elevated levels in cnsPCa compared to BPH, potentially indicating enhanced glycolytic activity45, and this finding was corroborated with a previous study46. Formate demonstrated a unique behavior with increased levels in PCa compared to BPH but showing the most pronounced elevation in csPCa compared to cnsPCa, suggesting progressively dysregulated one-carbon metabolism with disease advancement47.

It is important to emphasize that these serum metabolite profiles represent systemic metabolic signatures that reflect the complex interplay between the tumor, host response, and other organs48. While these circulating biomarkers provide robust, non-invasive indicators of disease state, they should be interpreted as systemic reflections rather than direct measures of intratumoral metabolic flux49. The consistent identification of these metabolic alterations across both discovery and validation cohorts strengthens their reliability as diagnostic biomarkers and underscores their potential for clinical translation in PCa management.

Despite the promising findings, several limitations need to be addressed. First, the retrospective design and relatively small sample size from a single center may limit the generalizability of our results. This design also precluded the collection of systematic data on potential confounders (e.g., comorbidities and medications) and long-term follow-up to definitively confirm the initial biopsy-based diagnoses. Additionally, the Gleason scores of PCa patients are subject to biopsy sampling error, and the BPH group may include undetected cancers. Therefore, future large-scale, multi-center prospective studies with scheduled follow-up are essential for robust validation. Furthermore, while we defined csPCa according to established guidelines (GS ≥ 3 + 4), future studies with expanded cohorts should aim to investigate metabolic differences between Gleason 3 + 4 and 4 + 3 subgroups to enable more refined risk stratification. Second, while the use of serum samples aligns with our goal of minimal invasiveness, its reflection of systemic metabolism may obscure prostate-specific signals. Future studies correlating serum with simultaneous tissue and urine metabolomics are needed to confirm the origin of our identified biomarkers. Furthermore, although we established a correlation between metabolite alterations and Gleason score, the observational nature of our study cannot elucidate underlying mechanisms. Future functional studies are required to define the precise roles of metabolite changes in driving tumor aggressiveness. Finally, integrating additional multi-omics data, such as transcriptomics and proteomics, could further enhance the predictive power of our diagnostic model.

In conclusion, this study highlights the potential of integrating metabolomics with ML to identify non-invasive biomarkers for PCa diagnosis and risk stratification. We established a diagnostic cascade comprising three metabolite panels: (1) a high-sensitivity tool for initial discrimination of PCa from BPH; (2) a high-specificity test for refining the diagnosis to cnsPCa versus BPH; and (3) a high-specificity “rule-in” test for stratifying cancer aggressiveness by distinguishing cnsPCa from csPCa. The identified biomarker panels offer superior accuracy compared to traditional clinical markers and could significantly reduce unnecessary biopsies, minimize overtreatment, and improve patient outcomes. With further validation, this multi-model metabolomic approach could play a critical role in advancing precision medicine in PCa management.

Methods

Study participants

In this study, a total of 270 patients with serum PSA concentrations ranging from 4 to 20 ng/ml participated in the retrospective cross-sectional analysis. The discovery cohort comprised 142 serum samples collected between October 2021 and August 2022 at Renji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University. An independent validation cohort consisting of 128 serum samples, was collected from the same institution during a subsequent period (October 2022 to January 2023) to ensure temporal segregation and prevent any analytical bias. All participants were evaluated through a standard clinical pathway that included serum PSA testing, digital rectal examination (DRE), and pre-biopsy multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI). Patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) served as the controls. Individuals were excluded from the study if they had a history of prior PCa treatment, other active malignancies, or comorbid conditions known to influence metabolic pathways, such as heart disease, renal impairment, or diabetes mellitus. Following the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines50, all patients underwent a targeted biopsy guided by mpMRI-ultrasound fusion. This combined approach consisted of a systematic 12-core template biopsy alongside targeted sampling of any mpMRI-suspicious lesions (PIRADS ≥3). PCa was graded using the Gleason score (GS), based on the primary and secondary patterns of tumor growth. A GS of ≥7 was used to identify clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa), while a lower score was categorized as clinically non-significant prostate cancer (cnsPCa). The basic biochemical and clinical subject parameters were listed in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

Sample collection and preparation

All patients fasted overnight to eliminate dietary effects, and blood samples were collected the following day. Blood specimens were collected in centrifuge tubes without anticoagulants and allowed to clot at room temperature for 15 min. The samples were then centrifuged at 1024 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain serum samples. The serum samples were stored at −80 °C until NMR analysis. The serum samples were thawed at 4 °C and vortexed for 10 s, then mixed with 250 μL of phosphate buffer (0.2 mM Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4, pH = 7.4) and 50 μL of D2O. After vortexing, the mixture was centrifuged to remove impurities (4 °C, 10,000 × g, 15 min), and the supernatant (490 μL) was transferred to a 5 mm NMR tube.

1H NMR-based metabolomics analysis

1H NMR-based metabolomics analysis was performed using a Bruker Avance III 600 MHz NMR spectrometer equipped with a 5 mm inverse triple resonance probe (TXI) and the Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) pulse sequence. The acquisition parameters used in this study were based on those outlined in our previous work51, and were set as follows: data points = 256 K; relaxation delay = 4 s; spectral width = 12,335.5 Hz; acquisition time = 2.66 s per scan.

All CPMG spectra were processed using TopSpin 3.0 software (Bruker BioSpin, Rheinstetten, Germany) to obtain metabolite information. All spectra were corrected for phase and baseline, and the chemical shifts of metabolites were referenced to the lactate standard peak at 1.33 ppm. Metabolites were identified using Chenomx NMR Suite 7.7.2 software and the Human Metabolome Database (http://hmdb.ca). Subsequently, the icoshift algorithm based on spectral interval’s calibrations was applied to align NMR spectra in MATLAB (R2012a, The Mathworks Inc., Natick, MA, USA)52. The spectral region from 0.0 ppm to 9.0 ppm (excluding the water peak from 4.70 to 5.00 ppm) was segmented into bins of 0.01 ppm width for multivariate statistical analysis to reduce data dimensionality while preserving metabolic pattern information. For precise integration and univariate statistical analysis of individual metabolites, a finer bin width of 0.0015 ppm was applied. To minimize inter-sample variation, the concentrations of serum metabolites were calculated as relative units (r.u.) based on their peak area by relative to the total area of each NMR spectrum. The high reproducibility and stability of the NMR platform, combined with the use of total area normalization, served as the primary quality control measures, ensuring robust and comparable metabolite quantification across all samples53.

Multivariate analysis

The processed dataset (0.01 ppm binning) was subjected to multivariate metabolomics analysis using SIMCA 14.1 software (Umetrics AB, Umea, Sweden). An orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) model was employed to visualize the metabolic differences and identify important metabolites. Model robustness was evaluated using a 100-times permutation test, with higher R²Y and Q² values indicating better fit and predictive capability, respectively. The value of R2 and Q2 close to 1.0 indicate a good performance of OPLS-DA model. Variables with a variable importance in projection (VIP) score >1.5 were selected for further analysis. Potential diagnostic biomarkers were identified using a two-stage workflow: primary filtering selected metabolites with VIP >1.5 and a p-value < 0.05 from univariate analysis; these candidates were then further refined using three machine learning (ML) algorithms for feature selection and model building.

Machine learning (ML) for selection of optimal metabolite combinations

Machine learning techniques were implemented using R software (version 4.2.3) to identify potential diagnostic biomarkers and build predictive models. To ensure reproducibility, a fixed random seed (set.seed(123)) was used for all analyses. The analyses leveraged the following R packages for model construction and tuning: glmnet (v4.1.8) for Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, randomForest (v4.1.8) for Random Forest (RF) algorithm, xgboost (v1.7.11) for extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) algorithm, and caret (v7.0.1) to streamline the hyperparameter tuning and cross-validation process across all algorithms.

A LASSO regression model with a binomial distribution (family = “binomial”) was employed for feature selection. The model was fitted with alpha set to 1 to enforce the L1 penalty. The optimal penalty parameter (lambda) was selected from a sequence of 80 values (10−9–100) via 10-fold cross-validation repeated 3 times within the discovery cohort. The value of lambda that yielded the minimum cross-validation error (lambda.min) was chosen to define the most parsimonious model.

The RF algorithm was implemented to assess variable importance and construct a classification model. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using a 10-fold cross-validation repeated 3 times. The tuned parameters included: mtry (number of variables randomly sampled as candidates at each split, tested around the square root of the number of features), maxdepth (maximum depth of trees, tested from 2 to 5), nodesize (minimum size of terminal nodes, tested from 3 to 10), and ntree (number of trees, tested from 30 to 100). The final model with the lowest out-of-bag (OOB) error was selected.

The XGBoost algorithm was applied for its high predictive performance. A comprehensive grid search was conducted to optimize key hyperparameters through 10-fold cross-validation repeated 3 times. The tuned parameters encompassed: eta (learning rate; 0.05, 0.1), max_depth (maximum tree depth; 3, 4, 5), min_child_weight (3, 5, 7), gamma (minimum loss reduction; 0), alpha (L1 regularization; 0, 0.05, 0.1), lambda (L2 regularization; 1, 2, 3), and nrounds (maximum number of boosting iterations; 50, 80). To address the class imbalance in the cnsPCa vs. csPCa comparison, the scale_pos_weight parameter was set to the ratio of negative to positive class instances. The final model was chosen based on the best cross-validation performance.

The diagnostic potential of the metabolite panels identified by the ML algorithms was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the ROC curve (AUC), along with sensitivity, specificity, and the optimal cut-off point (determined by the Youden index), were calculated. To statistically compare the performance of different models and biomarker panels, the DeLong test was employed to compare AUC values, with a p-value <0.05 considered statistically significant. All ROC analyses and DeLong tests were performed using MedCalc software (version 20.0.22). To evaluate the potential clinical utility of the biomarker panels and to compare them against the PSA-only strategy, decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted. DCA calculates the net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities, thereby quantifying the clinical value of a diagnostic model by balancing true positives against false positives. The net benefit of each model was plotted against the risk threshold, with strategies of “treat all” and “treat none” serving as references.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed on the dataset binned at 0.0015 ppm using SPSS 22.0 software (IBM Corp, USA). Differences between two groups were assessed using the Student’s t-test (for normally distributed data) or Mann-Whitney U tests (for non-normally distributed data). Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Metabolites with VIP > 1.5 and p-value <0.05 were selected as significant differential metabolites.
