Direct and interaction effects of CEO academic experience and CEO duality on digital transformation
Authors: Yexin Liu, Weiwei Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We used regression analysis to examine the direct and interaction effects of CEO academic experience and CEO duality on digital transformation in order to explain why firms vary in their digital transformation. Data analysis of manufacturing firms listed on the A-share market of the Shanghai and Shenzhen stock exchanges from 2007 to 2022 reveals that both academic experience and duality have significant positive direct effects on digital transformation. Both variables also mutually interact to promote digital transformation. This study not only makes an important contribution to the research on digital transformation and upper echelons theory but also provides useful guidance for firms developing their digitization strategy.

Introduction

Businesses around the world have been metamorphosed through the action of digital transformation, and firms that fail to embrace this transformation are unlikely to maintain a competitive edge [[1], [2], [3]]. Digital transformation extends beyond the use of digital technologies in firms [4]; it entails the adoption of new business models to create and capture value [5]. It thus poses significant challenges for each firm, prompting scholars to explore the means of achieving digital transformation [[6], [7], [8], [9]].

The manufacturing industry is at the forefront of digital technology adoption, making it a suitable subject to help understand the mechanisms of such adoption. Yet, manufacturing firms may suffer a digitization paradox [10] that arises from the requirement of substantial changes in business operations [11]. In this study, we focus on Chinese manufacturing firms, which are critical contributors to the national economy. Improving their competitiveness is a factor of high-quality economic development [12]. Since Chinese manufacturing firms have undergone varying degrees of digitization, they comprise a rich sample pool for this study [13].

Digital transformation is a crucial strategic decision for a firm [14]. According to upper echelons theory, top managers, especially chief executive officers (CEOs), play a vital role in these strategic decisions [15]. This makes it crucial to identify how CEOs affect a firm's digital transformation.

The upper echelons theory predicts that the characteristics and experiences of a CEO are manifested in a firm's strategic decisions [16], which thus commands the study of the effects of CEO characteristics and experiences [17]. Most literature on the upper echelons theory highlights the effect of the observable characteristics of CEOs, such as gender [18,19], age [20], education [21,22], tenure [23,24], and experience [25], while academic experience, which is a unique characteristic, remains neglected. In fact, academic experience may equip CEOs with an explorative cognitive pattern that enables them to understand new opportunities and advanced technologies better than their peers [26]. Therefore, it is necessary to investigate the antecedent effects of a CEO's academic experience on digital transformation.

Among the upper echelon variables, the CEO's duality is a popular area of scientific inquiry with significant practical interest [27]. According to upper echelons theory, CEOs can more easily impose their personal preferences on firms' strategic decisions when they have more managerial discretion [28]. Duality is thus a common measure to assess managerial discretion [29,30]. Quite reasonably, a CEO with a dualistic character may more easily select and implement strategic decisions for digital transformation. The influence and impact of CEO duality itself are still unclear [31], which confirms the need to further study this variable, especially its effect on digital transformation. We thus examine the direct impact of duality and also determine the possibility of an interaction effect between the CEO's academic experience and duality.

Using the lens of upper echelons theory, we examine whether academic experience and duality, as characteristics of a CEO, have a direct effect on digital transformation. We also theorize and test the interaction between academic experience and duality. We consider this more fruitful than only examining the direct effect of academic experience or duality, as it allows us to better identify how executive characteristics affect a firm's strategic decisions. To this effect, we employ a sample of Chinese manufacturing firms listed on the A-share markets of the Shanghai and Shenzhen stock exchanges from 2007 to 2022. The results show that both academic experience and duality have a significant, positive, and direct effect on digital transformation, and the mutual interaction of the two variables further promotes digital transformation.

This study makes several important contributions to the literature on digital transformation and upper echelons theory: (1) While most prior research on the determinants of digital transformation typically focuses on the top management team [32,33], we show that academic experience and duality—two CEO-level characteristics—are also relevant to firms' digital transformation. (2) Several recent studies have investigated the impact of the CEO's academic experience [[34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39]], but the effect of duality remains debatable. We provide complementary evidence that both academic experience and duality promote digital transformation, suggesting that CEOs with these characteristics can perform better in the context of strategic change. (3) We respond to the call in upper echelons research to open the “black box” between executive characteristics and organizational outcomes. By revealing the interaction effect of academic experience and duality, we provide valuable insights into how firms can extract most value from top executive characteristics.

The remaining paper is organized as follows: Section 2 presents the relevant literature and research hypotheses. Section 3 describes the research methodology. Section 4 presents the research results. Section 5 discusses the results. Section 6 concludes.

Literature review and hypotheses development

Upper echelons theory

In the field of strategic management, upper echelons theory conjectures on the effect of the firm's top executives' characteristics and experiences on organizational actions and outcomes, and the mechanism thereof [40]. Its central argument is that these variables can influence top executives' interpretation of the strategic environment, and thus, influence their strategic choices, which, in turn, affect organizational actions and outcomes [41,42].

The CEO, as the most powerful actor in the organization, strongly influences the firm's strategic choices [43]. Building on upper echelons theory, a growing corpus of literature has examined how the CEO's characteristics and experiences influence organizational actions and outcomes [44]. Most of this focus has been directed toward gender, age, tenure, and experience. In particular, the heterogeneity in experiences of CEOs plays an even greater role in strategic decisions [45]. Academic experience, as a special experience, inevitably has a profound impact on the CEO's cognition, thus influencing their managerial decisions.

The CEO's duality is another variable of interest within upper echelons theory, which posits that managerial discretion is a key variable to explain how the different characteristics and experiences of top executives can interact to shape the processes of managerial decision making [46]. Duality occurs when the CEO is also a board chairperson. Holding both the position of the CEO and that of the chairperson of the board heightens the CEO's managerial discretion by allowing a wider span of control and weakening the relative control of the board [47,48]. Within the board, managerial discretion from a dual leadership structure allows the CEO to significantly influence the strategic decisions of firms [30].

Academic experience

The academic experience of the CEO has begun to receive more research attention. While its significant impact on firm value [34], firm growth [35], firm innovation [36,37], earnings management [38], and firm fraud [39] has been noted, its link to digital transformation is understudied. Academic experience especially differs from other characteristics, such as education [49], which simply reflects the development of the CEO's individual knowledge and skills. Academic experience, however, is more closely related to professional discipline [50].

CEOs with academic experience, which includes a teaching position at a university, working in a research institution, or a research position at an association, generally have three distinctive characteristics [49]: Because of their academic training, they tend to be more rigorous than their non-academic counterparts, making them better able to process complex information [51]; because they have been trained to think critically, they are also good communicators and able to convey information clearly to others [35]; and they tend to have higher ethical standards than their non-academic counterparts [37].

We accordingly argue that the CEO's academic experience has a significant positive effect on digital transformation for the following reasons. Such CEOs practice rigor in their work and are more inclined to formulate reasonable and logical decisions [51]. In the digital economy, the difficulty of manufacturing firms in maintaining a sustainable competitive advantage is further aggravated by a rapidly changing external environment. In these circumstances, more attention should be paid to digitization, which provides new opportunities for firms to upgrade [52]. Compared with their counterparts, CEOs with academic experience can better understand the importance of digitization in firm development [36], and thus, better promote digital transformation.

CEOs with academic experience also have better communication skills because of their experience with workshops and conferences, and presenting academic papers. They can facilitate consensus building within the top management team [53], which is an important skill, since digital transformation is a high-risk activity that may face resistance from other top executives. CEOs with academic experience may more easily convince top management teams to agree with digital transformation.

Finally, the high ethical standards that come from academic experience allow for long-term decision making rather than short-term behaviors [54]. Academic experience favors improvements to the quality of internal controls through bettering rules and regulations [51]. Long-term orientation and higher quality of control are also positively related to digital transformation. We thus propose—Hypothesis 1The CEO’s academic experience has a significantly positive effect on digital transformation.

The CEO’s academic experience has a significantly positive effect on digital transformation.

Duality

The upper echelons variable of CEO's duality is an important but controversial concept in business and management research [55]. Two competing theoretical perspectives dominate research on its effect: On the one hand, agency theory argues that duality increases the CEO's power over the board and hinders the board's independence, which may result in managerial entrenchment, and ultimately produce negative effects [56]. On the other hand, stewardship theory argues that duality promotes more focused and flexible leadership, which facilitates organizational effectiveness in a dynamic environment, and ultimately produce positive effects [57]. This finding also suggests that the impact of duality should be considered within a specific context [58].

The benefits of duality might be especially salient in complex environments [59], wherein its benefits outweigh its inherent costs, leading to a positive effect. We thus argue that, in the context of digitization, the CEO's duality may have a significantly positive effect on digital transformation for the following reasons: In the era of the digital economy, firms face highly dynamic and uncertain competitive environments. Under these conditions, digital transformation is an inevitable necessity for every firm [60]. However, it remains a high-risk and uncertain challenge as well. In this context, the CEO's duality offers strong, unified leadership structure; such clear leadership can increase a firm's responsiveness, especially in a rapidly changing business environment [61]. The benefits of duality may be most valuable in digital transformation [62], as this situation requires quick decisions and a clear strategic direction.

Dual leadership can further enhance the CEO's ability to secure the resources critical for digital transformation [63]. Indeed, duality favors the likelihood that the CEO will push their ideas and risk taking [64]. When the CEO is also the chairperson of the board, the CEO operates with more discretion in using internal and external social resources to advance the firm's digitization. We thus propose—Hypothesis 2The CEO’s duality has a significantly positive effect on digital transformation.

The CEO’s duality has a significantly positive effect on digital transformation.

Upper echelons theory argues that a firm's behavior reflects the choices of its CEO. Even when faced with the same situation, CEOs with the same characteristics are likely to make different choices. This implies that the CEO's academic experience and duality may interact with each other to affect digital transformation, as we explain in the next section.

Interaction of academic experience and duality

The CEO's duality enhances the impact of academic experience. Such CEOs have more managerial freedom to shape the direction of a firm. The impact of academic experience may be enhanced when CEOs gain more power, given their power to make more direct decisions in favor of digital transformation.

The dual governance structure allows the CEO more managerial freedom to mobilize resources for their own purposes. Under such conditions, if a CEO with academic experience is also dualistic, they may be best dispositioned to sanction and allocate more valuable resources toward digital transformation. Thus, academic experience can also enhance the impact of duality. That is, duality can help establish clear lines of authority and a unity of command. This allows the CEO to make and implement decisions for digital transformation despite internal and external opposition. Under such conditions, a dualistic CEO with rich academic experience can more easily translate their experience into digital transformation. We thus propose that both academic experience and duality, although independent, can positively affect digital transformation—Hypothesis 3The interaction between the academic experience and duality of the CEO has a significantly positive effect on digital transformation.

The interaction between the academic experience and duality of the CEO has a significantly positive effect on digital transformation.

Research method

Samples

Our analysis is based on data of manufacturing firms listed on the A-share markets of the Shanghai and Shenzhen stock exchanges, and obtained from the China Stock Market and Accounting Research (CSMAR), one of the most widely used business and management research databases in China. We first retrieved the digital transformation data provided by CSMAR; we then retrieved data on other variables from the CSMAR; at last, we merged the digital transformation data with data on other variables using stock codes and years as identifiers.

The timeframe used in this study ranges from 2007 to 2022. We chose 2007 as the starting year because it was the first year that the CSMAR had data on digital transformations. Following previous studies, we screened the sample data by first excluding special treatment firms (ST/*ST) because they are at risk of delisting [65]. We then excluded firms with only one year of observation and firm-year observations with incomplete information [66]. The final dataset consisted of 16,144 firm-year observations of 2365 firms.

Measurement

Dependent variable

Our dependent variable is digital transformation. Digital transformation involves many aspects of organizational change and such information is usually found in a firm's annual reports [67]. Usually, the use of certain keywords in a firm's annual reports reflects organizational change [39]. Digital transformation can be described by counting the frequency of keywords related to digital transformation in a firm's annual report [68,69].

Referring the CSMAR database and previous research [52,70], we used keywords related to digital transformation covering both the use of core digital technologies (artificial intelligence, blockchain, cloud computing, and big data technologies) and the applications of firms' digital technology. We used the frequency of these keywords to reflect a firm's level of digital transformation. To overcome the potential rightward bias problem of the data, we used the total number of keywords related to digital transformation plus one, and then took the natural logarithm to measure digital transformation.

Given that the effects of the independent and control variables on the dependent variables require some lag, we applied a one-period lag to the dependent variable (digital transformation) compared with other variables (CEO academic experience, CEO duality, and control variables). This also alleviated the endogeneity concern of reverse causality.

Independent variable

Our dependent variables are CEOs’ academic experience and duality. Following previous research, academic experience was measured by a dummy variable with a value of one if the CEO of a firm had taught in a university, worked in a research institution, or conducted research in an association, and zero otherwise [71]. Duality was measured by a dummy variable measuring 1 if the CEO was also the chairman of the board (duality) and 0 otherwise [72].

Control variables

Following the literature [32,73], we included a set of control variables at the firm, board, and CEO levels. We included firm size, firm age, state ownership, firm leverage, business growth, board size, female directors, and board independence, along with the CEO's gender, age, and education.

At the firm level, the firm's size and age were included as control variables because they may significantly affect its willingness toward digital transformation. Following the literature [[74], [75], [76]], firm size was measured as the natural logarithm of the firm's total assets, and firm age was measured as the number of years since the firm was established. Given that ownership may affect a firm's digital activities [77], we controlled for state ownership, which was measured by a dummy variable measuring 1 if the firm was state owned and 0 otherwise [78]. Firm leverage, measured as the ratio of total debt to total assets [79], was included as a control variable because firms with different leverage levels have different slack resources to invest in digital transformation. Business growth was measured using the growth rate of operating income [32]. The larger the value, the faster the growth rate of the firm's business income and the more it may be willing to carry out digital transformation.

At the board level, we controlled for board size, number of female directors, and board independence. We included board size as a control variable because a firm's board of directors is an important factor in resource dependence for digital transformation [80]. We controlled for female directors and board independence because female and independent directors have a significant impact on a range of organizational actions and outcomes [81,82]. Following the literature [83], board size was measured as the number of board members, female directors as the percentage of female directors to the total number of directors, and board independence as the percentage of independent directors to the total number of directors.

At the CEO level, we controlled for the gender, age, and education of the CEO because of the known impact of these variables on a firm's decision making [84]. Following the literature [43], gender was measured using a dummy variable with a value of 1 for male and 0 for female. Age was measured in years. Education is measured using an ordinal variable indicating the level of education received by the CEO, with a value ranging from 1 to 5, where 1 represented secondary school and below, 2 represented junior college, 3 represented bachelor's degree, 4 represented master's degree, and 5 represented doctorate. Thus, the higher the score, the more educated the CEO.

We have presented clear empirical evidence that the CEO's academic experience and duality are significantly and positively related to digital transformation. As the most powerful actors, CEOs can act on their own interpretations of the environment they face, and their experiences are valid proxies for their cognitions and, consequently, provide a powerful explanation for variations in their strategic choices [85,86]. Building on this perspective, recent scholarly work has begun to focus on the role of the CEO's experience in shaping a firm's decision making [87,88]. Despite fruitful findings on the impact of the CEO's experience, the role of their academic experience remains poorly understood. Following previous research, we confirm that CEOs with academic experience have a significantly positive impact on digital transformation.

Among CEO characteristics, duality is a controversial topic in corporate governance research. Two primary theoretical perspectives dominate the research on its effects: agency theory (costs) and stewardship theory (benefits). It is difficult to dispute either the costs or benefits of CEO duality [63], which depend on a specific context. Indeed, CEO duality is positive in environments with high dynamism, low generosity, and high complexity [55]. Digital transformation is risky and usually involves considerable effort in the long term, an duality is particularly valuable in this context, as it allows for a timelier response to digital transformations.

Given that almost no research has explored the interaction effect of academic experience and duality, we further investigated the interaction effect of these two variables in response to the call for a more systematic evaluation of the contingencies of upper echelons theory [40]. Hambrick (2007) suggests that the relationship between top managers' characteristics and organizational actions and outcomes can be influenced by managerial discretion, that is, the latitude top managers enjoy in making strategic decisions [89]. When managerial discretion is high, top managers’ characteristics are better predictors of organizational actions and outcomes than when managerial discretion is low. This logic underpins our analysis of the aforementioned interaction effect.

Our result indeed confirms the interaction effect—a result consistent with upper echelons theory. In verifying this relationship, we improve the understanding of how CEO characteristics affect organizational actions and outcomes.

Theoretical implications, practical implications and limitations and future research

Theoretical implications

We provide new insights into digital transformation and upper echelons theory based on our findings. First, our study contributes to the digital transformation literature by extending the understanding of the antecedent effects of the CEO's academic experience and duality on digital transformation. The results show that both academic experience and duality are beneficial for digitization. By providing new insights into the antecedents of digital transformation, this study sheds light on how firms can achieve digital transformation, and clarifies new avenues for digital transformation research.

Second, this study contributes to the upper echelons theory by enriching the understanding of the role of managers in firm digitization. We thus call for greater research into the effects of the academic experience and duality of CEOs. Our findings serve as clues for researchers to further explore how other CEO characteristics can be used to promote digitization.

Finally, this study contributes to upper echelon theory by responding to the call for greater attention to the theoretical and empirical specification of the conditions of CEO characteristics. The mixed findings on the impact of CEO characteristics have drawn scholarly attention to the contextual factors that moderate the influence of executive characteristics on organizational actions and outcomes. Responding to this call, we investigated the interactive effect of the CEO's academic experience and duality, especially confirm that both variables interact with each other to influence digital transformation. It is possible that the effects of CEO characteristics may be obscured by the main effects alone, and an interactive perspective may be more beneficial for further improving our understanding of their roles.

Practical implications

This study also has important practical implications for the digitization of firms. The positive relationship between the CEO's academic experience and digital transformation suggests that firms should hire CEOs with academic experience to achieve digital transformation. A similar positive relationship between duality and digital transformation means that firms could adopt a dual governance structure, subject to regulatory requirements, when crafting their digitization strategy. This is particularly important for CEOs with academic experience. Overall, this study provides empirical evidence that could guide firms' selection of appropriate CEOs for digitization.

Limitations and future research

This study has some limitations that need to be addressed in future research. First, we used only second-hand data as a proxy to measure digital transformation. Although this method is widely used, it may not be ideal. We suggest a more comprehensive measure of digital transformation.

Second, this study empirically tests the direct impact of the CEO's academic experience and duality on digital transformation. However, we do not test the underlying mechanism governing this relationship. One suggestion is exploring the variables that mediate this relationship.

Third, although we included many control variables, we could not control all the variables related to digital transformation because of data constraints. Future studies should include additional control variables with different data to validate the results of the present study.

Finally, the sample in this study was limited to listed Chinese manufacturing firms, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Future researchers should seek to replicate our findings in different cultural and industrial settings to enhance generalizability.

Despite these limitations, this study finds that both CEO academic experience and duality have a significant, positive, and direct impact on digital transformation. In addition, both these variables interact with each other to further promote digital transformation. This study is one of the first to focus on how CEO characteristics affect firms' digital transformation, providing new insights into the factors driving firms’ digitization.

Conclusion

In the digital economy, digital transformation is a strategic imperative for sustaining competitive advantages. It is thus crucial to identify the factors that drive digital transformation in firms. Moreover, how CEOs influence organizational actions and outcomes is a critical question that has long been addressed in strategic management research. We combined these two avenues of investigation to shed light on how CEOs influence firms' digital transformation. Specifically, we empirically investigated the direct effect of the CEO's academic experience and duality, as well as the interactive effect of these variables, on digital transformation. The results confirm the significant, positive, and direct effects of both variables, and their mutual interaction, on digital transformation. These results are further verified through a series of robustness and endogeneity tests.
