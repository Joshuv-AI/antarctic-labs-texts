Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells derived exosomes enhance the therapeutic efficacy of anti-miR-10b in glioblastoma
Authors: Saeideh Sadeghi Neshat, Masoud Soleimani, Zahra Madjd, Mehdi Dianatpour, Faezeh Vakhshiteh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) remains a highly aggressive brain tumor with limited therapeutic options. MicroRNA-10b (miR-10b) is consistently overexpressed in GBM and absent in normal astrocytes, making it a potential therapeutic target. In this study, we evaluated the feasibility of using human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell (hUC-MSC)-derived exosomes as delivery vehicles for Locked Nucleic Acid anti-miR-10b-5p (LNA-anti-10b) in U87MG glioblastoma cells. Exosomes were isolated by ultracentrifugation and loaded with LNA-anti-10b. Delivery efficiency was compared with lipofectamine-mediated transfection. The functional effects of exosome-loaded anti-10b (E-LNA-anti-10b) were assessed in both 2D and 3D culture systems. Cell viability and apoptosis were evaluated using Annexin V/PI, AO/PI, and MTT assays, while migration assays assessed cellular aggressiveness. The expression of validated miR-10b target genes and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related markers was analyzed by quantitative RT-PCR and Western blotting. hUC-MSC-derived exosomes efficiently delivered LNA-anti-10b to U87MG cells in both 2D and 3D culture models. E-LNA-anti-10b treatment was associated with enhanced reductions in cell viability, proliferation, migration, and expression of EMT-associated markers. Notably, naïve hUC-MSC-derived exosomes also exhibited intrinsic anti-proliferative and anti-migratory effects. Together, these findings indicate that hUC-MSC-derived exosomes possess inherent anti-tumor properties in GBM models and can functionally enhance the effects of LNA-anti-10b delivery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54058-2.

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is the most common, aggressive, and invasive primary brain tumor in adults and has the highest fatality rate among brain cancers1,2. GBM accounts for approximately 80% of primary malignant tumors of the central nervous system and is responsible for an estimated 225,000 deaths worldwide each year3. Despite aggressive multimodal treatment, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, patients have a median survival of only 12–15 months and a five-year survival rate of approximately 6.8%, largely due to rapid tumor progression, diffuse invasion, and resistance to standard therapies4,5. Over the past century, substantial research efforts have been devoted to improving survival outcomes for patients with glioblastoma; however, major challenges persist, including the identification of reliable biomarkers and the development of safe and effective drug delivery systems capable of selectively targeting tumor cells while minimizing damage to healthy brain tissue.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are short, non-coding RNA molecules approximately 21–23 nucleotides in length that regulate gene expression by modulating the stability and/or translational efficiency of specific messenger RNAs6–8. miRNAs play a critical role in cancer biology by influencing key cellular processes, including proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. Depending on their targets and cellular context, miRNAs may function as oncogenes or tumor suppressors, thereby contributing to tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic resistance.

Among these, microRNA-10b (miR-10b) is one of the most prominently upregulated and evolutionarily conserved miRNAs reported in glioblastoma. It is highly expressed in high-grade GBM while being virtually absent in normal brain tissue9˒10. miR-10b has been reported to be overexpressed in approximately 90% of GBM tumors across all four molecular subtypes, proneural, neural, classical, and mesenchymal, suggesting a broad association with glioblastoma pathobiology¹. Inhibition of miR-10b has been associated with reduced proliferation of glioblastoma cells and glioblastoma stem cells, indicating that miR-10b may contribute to the uncontrolled cellular growth characteristic of this malignancy11˒12. Moreover, suppression of miR-10b has been reported to decrease glioblastoma cell invasion and migration, whereas its overexpression enhances these aggressive behaviors, supporting its functional involvement in tumor progression13. In vivo studies further demonstrate that miR-10b inhibition attenuates the growth of stem cell–derived orthotopic glioblastoma xenografts, underscoring its biological relevance within the tumor microenvironment14.

In addition, multiple studies have reported that targeting miR-10b, either alone or in combination with chemotherapeutic agents, induces glioblastoma cell death and enhances the efficacy of chemotherapy and radiotherapy15–18. Given its reported oncogenic role in both glioblastoma cells and glioblastoma stem cells, therapeutic inhibition of miR-10b has been proposed as a strategy to suppress the stem cell population implicated in tumor initiation, recurrence, and treatment resistance. Collectively, these findings support miR-10b as a promising candidate for therapeutic targeting in glioblastoma.

Once a therapeutic molecule has been identified, an effective and reliable delivery system with appropriate biological characteristics is required. In recent years, growing interest has focused on exosomes, small extracellular vesicles secreted by a wide range of cell types. These nano-sized vesicles, typically ranging from 30 to 160 nm in diameter, play an important role in intercellular communication and have been implicated in numerous physiological and pathological processes19–22. Exosomes are being explored as a potential drug delivery platform due to their ability to penetrate biological barriers, offering a strategy for targeted cancer therapy21. They are capable of encapsulating and transporting diverse nucleic acid cargos, including DNA, messenger RNA (mRNA), miRNAs, and small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), to recipient cells. Several intrinsic properties make exosomes attractive as delivery vehicles23,24, including their biocompatibility, natural origin, loading capacity, circulatory stability23, low immunogenicity and toxicity, ability to cross biological barriers, and efficient cellular uptake25–28. Collectively, these features position exosomes as a promising platform for nucleic acid–based therapeutic delivery.

The selection of an appropriate exosome source is critical for the development of effective delivery systems. While many cell types secrete exosomes, human mesenchymal stem cell (hMSC)-derived exosomes are of particular interest due to their production yield, biosafety profile, and reported tropism for tumor cells. Among hMSC sources, human umbilical cord-derived MSCs (hUC-MSCs) offer several advantages, including higher exosome yields, lower immunogenicity, and minimal ethical concerns29,30.

In this study, we investigated the feasibility of using hUC-MSC-derived exosomes as a delivery vehicle for Locked Nucleic Acid anti-miR-10b-5p (LNA-anti-10b) and evaluated their functional effects on proliferation and aggressiveness in U87MG glioblastoma cells.

Materials and methods

Cell line and culturing conditions

The U87MG cell line was obtained from the Iranian Biological Resource Center (Tehran, Iran). U87MG cells were maintained in DMEM (low glucose) (BioSera, Germany) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA), 1% non-essential amino acids (Gibco, USA), 100 U/ml penicillin, 100 µg/ml streptomycin (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, China), and 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco, USA). The cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and 95% humidity. The culture medium was replaced every 2–3 days.

Tumor spheroid 3D culture

U87MG spheroids were generated by the free-floating method on poly-HEMA (poly-2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate)-coated dishes. Single-cell suspensions were seeded into poly-HEMA–coated dishes at 8 × 10⁵ cells/mL in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% glutamine, and 1% non-essential amino acids. The plates remained untouched for 5 days in a 37 °C incubator. To prepare non-adherent culture plates, 1.2 g of poly-HEMA (Sigma, USA) was dissolved in 100 mL of 99% ethanol by overnight rotation to ensure complete polymer dissolution. The resulting solution underwent centrifugation at 400 × g for 30 min to eliminate any remaining undissolved particles, followed by filtration using a 0.22 μm filter. Tissue culture dishes were subsequently coated with the poly-HEMA solution (1.2 mL per well for a six-well plate or 2.5 mL per T25 flask) within a biosafety laminar flow hood at room temperature. The solution was allowed to evaporate completely overnight to remove ethanol. Subsequently, the plates were rinsed with PBS and stored at 37 °C for future applications31,32.

Isolation of hUC-MSCs

Human umbilical cord samples were acquired post-delivery from the hospital, with the cooperation and consent of the infants’ parents, under the ethical guidelines approved by the Shiraz University of Medical Sciences. Proximal to the placenta, the UC was segmented into 1.5 cm lengths and underwent multiple washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 2% penicillin/streptomycin. Subsequently, the segments were subjected to 1 mL of collagenase type II (0.014 g/mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min with mechanical agitation via vortexing every 10 min for 30 s. Following this, FBS was added to inhibit collagenase activity, and the mixture was then centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was transferred to a 75-cm² cell culture flask (SPL Life Sciences, Korea) containing DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. After 72 h, the culture medium was replaced. Cell colonies were then dissociated with 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA and transferred to new flasks.

Characterization of hUC-MSCs

For hUC-MSC characterization, cells were trypsinized, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with antibodies (CD73, CD105, CD90, CD34, CD45), and subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry (FACSCalibur system; BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Data were processed using FlowJo software (Tree Star Inc., Ashland, USA). For multipotency assessment of hUC-MSCs, differentiation into osteogenic and adipogenic lineages was evaluated. Osteogenic differentiation was induced by culturing cells in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS, 50 µg/mL L-ascorbic acid-2-phosphate, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, and 0.1 nM dexamethasone for 3 weeks, starting at 80% confluence. Adipogenic differentiation was achieved using DMEM/F12 containing 10% FBS supplemented with 10 ng/mL insulin (Sigma-Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany) and 10 nM dexamethasone over the same duration. Media were refreshed twice per week for both differentiation protocols. Differentiation was confirmed via Alizarin Red staining (Sigma-Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany) for osteogenesis and Oil Red O staining (Sigma-Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany) for adipogenesis.

Extraction and identification of exosomes

To isolate exosomes, hUC-MSCs were cultured in 175 cm² flasks until they reached more than 80% confluence, at which point the medium was replaced with DMEM containing 10% exosome-free FBS (obtained by ultracentrifugation of FBS twice at 100,000 × g for 2 h). Every 48 h, the medium was collected and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min, 2,000 × g for 10 min, and 10,000 × g for 30 min to remove cells and debris. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm sterile filter and ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 70 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was washed with PBS and ultracentrifuged again at 100,000 × g for 70 min to obtain exosomes.

Electron microscopy

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Leo 906, Germany) at 80 kV was employed to examine the exosomal morphology. Twenty microliters (20 µL) of exosomes were mixed with 50 µL of 2% agarose gel, fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 12 h and 2% osmium tetroxide for 30 min, and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (20–100%). The samples were then impregnated with an 812 Resin kit (TAAB, UK), embedded, and polymerized. Ultrathin Sect. (60 nm) were stained with 2% uranyl acetate for 3 min and 0.5% lead citrate for 5 min before visualization with a transmission electron microscope.

Exosome size measurement

Dynamic light scattering (DLS, Zetasizer, Malvern, UK) was used to evaluate the size distribution of the purified exosomes. The exosome samples were first diluted in PBS at a ratio of 1:3 (v/v), followed by gentle mixing for 20 s. Subsequently, measurements were analyzed using Malvern software v7.11 (Zetasizer, UK).

Exosome protein concentration

In order to evaluate the protein concentration of purified exosome samples, the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein quantification assay kit (Bio Basic Inc., Canada-Takara) was employed. The process involved preparing nine serially diluted solutions of bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS as standards. Subsequently, the working reagent was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following this, 25 µL of BSA or exosome samples and 75 µL of BCA working reagent were combined in a 96-well plate, thoroughly mixed, and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The optical density (OD) was measured at 562 nm using an ELISA reader system (BioTek, USA). The protein concentration of isolated exosome samples was determined using the standard curve.

Western blot analysis

CD9, CD81, and CD63 (Abcam, USA) were analyzed by Western blot as positive Extracellular vesicles markers, while calnexin was assessed as a negative marker to evaluate potential contamination by endoplasmic reticulum-derived proteins. Protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with anti-rabbit secondary antibody at a 1:1000 dilution for 1 h. An ECL kit (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech, Piscataway, NJ) was used to detect the specific bands.

Loading the exosomes with FL-oligo/LNA-anti-10b using electroporation

To load exosomes with either miRCURY LNA miR-10b Inhibitor (LNA-anti-10b) (ACAAATTCGGTTCTACAGGGT) (Qiagen, catalog no. Y104100557-ADA), miRCURY LNA miRNA Inhibitor Control/scramble (LNA-SCont) (AGAGCTCCCTTCAATCCAA) (Qiagen, catalog no. Y100199007-ACA), or fluorescent-labeled oligonucleotide (FL-Oligo) (Bioneer, Korea), electroporation was employed. The electroporation solution was prepared by suspending exosomes (0.5 µg/µL) in a pre-chilled buffer solution consisting of 137 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 21 mM HEPES, 6 mM dextrose, 0.7 mM Na₂HPO₄·7 H₂O, and distilled water, in a 1:1 (v/v) ratio. Subsequently, LNA-anti-10b or LNA-SCont oligonucleotides were added at a final amount of 100 pmol per 100 µg/ml of exosomes30. After a 2-minute incubation on ice, the mixture was transferred to a cold 0.4 cm electroporation cuvette, and electroporation was carried out at 0.400 kV using an Eppendorf AG 22,331 (Germany). Following electroporation-mediated loading, exosome suspensions were re-analyzed by DLS to determine particle size distribution and evaluate possible vesicle aggregation or structural alterations induced by the loading procedure.

Evaluation of FL-oligo transfer into U87MG cells

The transfer efficiency of FL‑Oligo was evaluated by comparing two delivery methods: exosome‑mediated delivery and lipofectamine‑mediated transfection using Lipofectamine 3000 (Lipo‑3000; Invitrogen). In addition, the potential effects of FBS and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) on transfection efficiency were assessed. Twenty‑four hours prior to transfection, U87MG cells were seeded in 96‑well plates at a density of 15,000 cells per well in antibiotic‑free culture medium. Transfection was performed when cells reached approximately 70% confluence. FL‑Oligo (0.2–1 µg) was introduced into the cells using Lipofectamine (0.25-5 µL), according to the experimental conditions. After 24 h of incubation, transfection efficiency was evaluated by detecting green fluorescence (excitation 495 nm, emission 520 nm) using a fluorescence microscope (Axio Scope A1, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Exosomal delivery of LNA-anti-10b to U87MG cells

U87MG cells were treated with exosome‑loaded oligonucleotides to evaluate the delivery of LNA‑anti‑10b. Briefly, cells were seeded in 6‑well plates and cultured until reaching approximately 60–70% confluence. The culture medium was then replaced with serum‑free medium, and cells were exposed to 100 µg/mL of MSC‑derived exosomes loaded with either LNA‑anti‑10b (E‑LNA‑anti‑10b) or scrambled control oligonucleotide (E‑SCont) for 48 h. Control groups included untreated cells and cells treated with unloaded naïve exosomes (N‑Exo). Following the 48‑h incubation period, cells were collected for subsequent analyses.

Cellular uptake of PKH26-exosomes

PKH26-labeled exosomes were used to assess cellular uptake. Purified exosomes were labeled with PKH26 fluorescent dye (Sigma‑Aldrich) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, exosome suspensions were incubated with PKH26 for 15 min at 37 °C, and the labeling reaction was terminated by the addition of an equal volume of serum. Unbound dye was removed by ultracentrifugation (100,000 × g, 2 h, 4 °C), and the labeled exosome pellet was washed once in PBS and recentrifuged to minimize free dye contamination. For uptake experiments, U87MG cells were seeded in 35‑mm dishes and incubated overnight to allow attachment. PKH26‑labeled exosomes were then added to the culture medium and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. Following incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed in 70% ethanol for 15 min, and counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (10 µg/mL, 10 min, room temperature) to visualize nuclei. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a fluorescence microscopy. Images are presented as merged views together with separate Hoechst and PKH26 channels to illustrate exosome internalization.

MTT assay

To assess cell viability, U87MG cells were seeded at a concentration of 1.5 × 10³ cells per well in a 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The following day, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing E-LNA-anti-10b, LNA-anti-10b, LNA-SCont, E-SCont, or N-Exo at concentrations of 25, 50, or 100 µg/ml. Cells were further incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Subsequently, MTT reagent was added to each well, and cells were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. After incubation, DMSO was added to solubilize the formazan crystals, and the optical density (OD) was measured using a plate reader (BioTek, USA).

Live/dead assay

Cell viability was evaluated using a dual-staining approach with Acridine Orange (AO) and Propidium Iodide (PI). Following treatment with E-LNA-anti-10b, N-Exo, or PBS (control), U87MG cells were trypsinized, harvested, and washed twice with PBS by centrifugation at 300 × g. The AO/PI solution (10 mg/mL) was prepared under light-protected conditions and mixed with the cell suspensions. Analysis was performed using fluorescence microscopy (Axio Scope A1, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) with a 10× objective. Viable cells fluoresced green (AO-positive), whereas non-viable cells fluoresced red (PI-positive), allowing quantitative assessment of cell mortality.

Apoptosis assay

Cell apoptosis in U87MG cells was evaluated using the Annexin V/Propidium Iodide (PI) apoptosis kit (BioLegend, San Diego, USA). For 2D culture, 7 × 10⁴ U87MG cells were seeded in a 24-well plate and maintained at 37 °C for 24 h. For 3D spheroid culture, 8 × 10⁵ cells/mL U87MG cells were cultured in poly-HEMA-coated T25 flasks for 5 days. Following this, cells in 2D culture or spheroids were treated with E-LNA-anti-10b or N-Exo for 48 h. Treated cells were then collected, washed with PBS, and stained with 5 µL Annexin V and 10 µL PI, followed by gentle vortexing and incubation at 25 °C for 20 min in the dark. Apoptosis was analyzed using flow cytometry (FACS Calibur, BD Biosciences, San Diego, CA, USA) and fluorescence microscopy (Axio Scope A1, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Scratch wound-healing migration assay

U87MG cells were seeded in 6 well plates and allowed to reach approximately 80% confluence. A linear scratch was created in the cell monolayer using a sterile 10 µL pipette tip, and detached cells were gently removed by washing with PBS. To minimize the contribution of proliferation to wound closure, cells were maintained in low serum medium (1% FBS) throughout the assay. Cells were then treated with N Exo or E LNA anti 10b. Images of the wound area were captured at 0 and 24 h using an inverted microscope. Wound closure was quantified using ImageJ software and expressed as the percentage reduction in wound area relative to baseline (0 h). Each condition was analyzed in at least three independent experiments.

Functional assessment of miR-10b target genes

The functional impact of LNA‑anti‑10b delivery was assessed by quantifying transcript levels of validated direct miR‑10b targets, CDKN1A and HOXD10, following exosome- or lipofectamine‑mediated delivery. LNA‑SCont and E-SCont were used as the respective negative controls.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR assay

Total RNA was extracted from U87MG cells using the RNA Extraction Kit (Yektatajhiz, Iran) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were measured with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). Subsequently, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Yektatajhiz, Iran). Expression of CDKN1A and HOXD10 was quantified by qRT-PCR. GAPDH was used as the endogenous control, and relative expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Scrambled oligonucleotide-loaded exosomes served as additional controls. All experiments were performed in at least three independent replicates. Primers used for all targets are listed in Table 1.

Expression of EMT-associated genes

For functional assessment of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), U87MG cells were treated with E-LNA-anti-10b or N-Exo. The expression of EMT-associated genes CDH1, SNAI1, and SNAI2 was measured by qRT-PCR using GAPDH as the endogenous control, and relative expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Scrambled oligonucleotide-loaded exosomes served as additional controls.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed to analyze the expression of miR-10b downstream target proteins in U87MG cells, using N-Exo and untreated cells as control. Total protein was extracted by lysing cells in 500 µL of lysis buffer containing 62.5 mM Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 1 mM sodium vanadate, and 1 mM sodium fluoride. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against the target proteins. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:1000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection kit (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech, Piscataway, NJ).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in at least three independent replicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired t-tests, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by appropriate post hoc tests. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of hUC-MSCs

The hUC-MSCs exhibited a spindle-shaped fibroblastic morphology (Fig. 1a). The cells multipotent nature was confirmed by differentiating into osteoblasts and adipocytes using specific culture media (Fig. 1b and c). Phenotypic marker analysis revealed that the isolated MSCs were positive for CD73 (99.6%), CD90 (88.4%), and CD105 (82.4%) while were negative for CD34 (1.15%) and CD45 (0.207%) (Fig. 1d). These data collectively validated the successful isolation of hUC-MSCs.

Characterization of hUC-MSCs exosomes

After isolation via ultracentrifugation, the size of hUC-MSC-derived exosomes was evaluated using DLS, and their structure was visualized by TEM. DLS analysis showed a single, sharp peak at 101.3 ± 2.7 nm, with over 94% of particles between 80 and 120 nm, indicating a homogeneous population with minimal aggregation (Fig. 1e). TEM revealed spherical vesicles with uniform morphology and diameters predominantly within the 80–120 nm range, consistent with the DLS results (Fig. 1f). Western blot analysis demonstrated the presence of canonical exosomal markers CD9 (~ 24 kDa), CD63 (~ 26 kDa), and CD81 (~ 22 kDa) in the isolated vesicle fraction. In contrast, the endoplasmic reticulum marker calnexin was not detected in the exosome preparation but was clearly present in the whole‑cell lysate control, indicating that the vesicle preparation was not detectably contaminated with endoplasmic reticulum–derived proteins (Fig. 1g). These data support the successful isolation of a homogeneous and functionally suitable exosome population from hUC-MSCs.

Cellular uptake of exosomes

To assess the functional delivery of exosomes to U87MG cells, isolated hUC-MSC-derived exosomes were fluorescently labeled with PKH26 membrane dye according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After a 4-hour incubation, fluorescence microscopy revealed red fluorescent spots within the cytoplasm of U87MG cells, indicating exosome internalization (Fig. 2b). Hoechst 33,342 staining was used to visualize nuclei, providing a spatial reference for exosome localization (Fig. 2b). The observed cytoplasmic red fluorescence demonstrates that U87MG cells can efficiently take up hUC-MSC-derived exosomes under these conditions.

2D and 3D spheroid culture

U87MG cells in 2D culture displayed typical epithelial morphology. For 3D culture, Poly-HEMA–coated plates were used to generate spheroids under non-adherent conditions, allowing the formation of stable, free-floating aggregates. As shown in Fig. 2a, U87MG cells formed round, compact 3D spheroids in Poly-HEMA–coated dishes. After 5 days of incubation at 37 °C, spheroids were imaged using inverted and fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 2a). The spheroids exhibited smooth surfaces with an average diameter of 145.3 ± 32.7 μm, and most measured between 100 and 200 μm, consistent with previously reported glioblastoma spheroid sizes31–33. These observations indicate that U87MG cells can form uniform and compact spheroids suitable for downstream functional assays.

Transfection efficiency in U87MG cells

To assess the uptake of FL-Oligo by U87MG cells, fluorescence microscopy was used to compare exosome-mediated delivery with Lipo-3000. A higher intracellular fluorescence signal was observed in cells treated with FL-Oligo–loaded exosomes (0.03 µg) compared with cells transfected using Lipo-3000 (0.2 µg) (Fig. 2d). These results indicate that exosomes can serve as an effective delivery system for FL-Oligo, potentially due to their biocompatibility and natural uptake mechanisms. The influence of serum presence during transfection was also examined; omitting FBS did not substantially alter FL-Oligo internalization for either delivery method. Addition of DMSO (0.9%) increased FL-Oligo uptake but also led to U87MG cell death. Particle size analysis by DLS revealed no significant changes before and after oligonucleotide loading, suggesting that exosome integrity was maintained throughout the loading process.

Viability of U87MG cells

The MTT assay was performed to evaluate the effects of E-LNA-anti-10b delivery on U87MG cell viability. Treatment with N-Exo led to a noticeable reduction in cell viability, although lower concentrations had minimal impact. In contrast, treatment with free LNA-SCont or LNA-anti-10b alone did not substantially alter cell viability. Exosome-associated treatments, including E-LNA-anti-10b and E-SCont, as well as N-Exo at higher concentrations, were associated with a dose-dependent decrease in U87MG cell viability (Fig. 2c; p < 0.0001). These observations suggest that exosome delivery, with or without loaded oligonucleotides, can influence U87MG cell viability under the experimental conditions tested.

Live/dead assay

Fluorescence microscopy assessments in 2D and 3D culture showed reduced viability in the E-LNA-anti-10b-treated group compared with N-Exo-treated and untreated control groups (Fig. 2e). These results suggest that E-LNA-anti-10b treatment is associated with increased cytotoxic effects in U87MG cells. Notably, N-Exo–treated groups also displayed a modest increase in dead cells relative to controls, indicating that hUC-MSC-derived exosomes may exert intrinsic effects on cell viability under both 2D and 3D culture conditions.

Apoptosis assay

Flow cytometry was used to evaluate the apoptotic rate of U87MG cells following treatments. Analysis showed an increase in apoptotic cells in 2D cultures treated with E-LNA-anti-10b compared with control groups (Fig. 2f). In 3D spheroid cultures, the increase in apoptosis was less pronounced, which may be due to the compact structure of spheroids reducing the efficiency of exosome or oligonucleotide delivery to the core cells (Fig. 2f). These results indicate that E-LNA-anti-10b treatment is associated with enhanced apoptosis in U87MG cells, while also highlighting potential differences in treatment penetration between 2D and 3D culture systems.

Wound healing migration assay

To evaluate the impact of N-Exo and E-LNA-anti-10b on the migratory capacity of U87MG cells, a wound-healing assay was performed. To minimize the potentially confounding contribution of cell proliferation to wound closure, the assay was conducted under low-serum conditions (1% FBS). Treatment with both N-Exo and E-LNA-anti-10b was associated with a visible reduction in wound closure compared with untreated controls (Fig. 3a). Quantitative analysis demonstrated that exosome-mediated delivery of LNA-anti-10b resulted in a significantly greater reduction in wound closure compared with the N-Exo and control groups (9-fold and 14-fold reduction, respectively; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3b). These results suggest that E-LNA-anti-10b treatment effectively modulates cellular migration, although a minor influence from altered proliferative capacity under these conditions cannot be entirely excluded.

Functional effects of LNA-anti-10b in U87MG cells

To assess the effects of LNA‑anti‑10b in U87MG cells, we analyzed the expression of validated miR‑10b target genes following exosome- and Lipofectamine-mediated delivery. E‑LNA‑anti‑10b treatment resulted in a significant increase in CDKN1A expression, whereas HOXD10 expression remained unchanged. A comparable pattern was observed with Lipofectamine-mediated delivery, while scrambled control oligonucleotides produced no similar effects. In addition, EMT‑associated genes were modulated, with upregulation of CDH1 and downregulation of SNAI1 and SNAI2. Collectively, these findings indicate that delivery of LNA‑anti‑10b, by either exosomes or Lipofectamine, is associated with gene expression changes consistent with functional target modulation (Fig. 3c and d).

Western blot validation of protein expression

Western blot analysis was performed to complement the qRT-PCR results and examine EMT-related protein expression. Following E-LNA-anti-10b treatment, protein levels of the tumor suppressors E-cadherin and PTEN were increased, while EMT markers SNAI1 and SNAI2 were decreased compared with N-Exo and untreated control groups. CDKN1A, a reported miR-10b target, also showed increased expression, whereas HOXD10 levels remained unchanged (Fig. 3e). These results are consistent with the qRT-PCR data and indicate that exosome-mediated delivery of LNA-anti-10b is associated with modulation of EMT-related proteins, potentially contributing to reduced cell migration and invasion in U87MG cells.

Discussion

Numerous studies have firmly established that miR-10b is overexpressed in multiple malignancies, including glioblastoma, colorectal cancer, breast cancer, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and melanoma34–38. This overexpression is associated with enhanced tumor proliferation, invasion, and metastatic potential. Gabriely et al. (2011) reported markedly elevated miR-10b expression across several glioblastoma cell lines, while expression was negligible in normal brain tissue, neural progenitor cells, and differentiated neurons39. This tumor-specific expression pattern highlights miR-10b as a potential therapeutic target, offering opportunities for selective interventions that may spare healthy brain tissue.

In this study, we employed exosomes derived from hUC-MSCs40 to deliver LNA-anti-10b to U87MG glioblastoma cells and evaluated both delivery efficiency and biological effects. Our results demonstrate enhanced oligonucleotide uptake via exosomal delivery compared with Lipo-3000, even at lower oligonucleotide concentrations, likely reflecting the natural cell-penetrating properties of exosomes and their ability to protect cargo from enzymatic degradation41.

Functionally, exosome-mediated delivery of LNA-anti-10b (E-LNA-anti-10b) was associated with reduced proliferation, migration, and modulation of EMT-related markers in U87MG cells. Notably, naïve hUC-MSC-derived exosomes also exhibited intrinsic anti-proliferative and anti-migratory effects, highlighting the contribution of exosome-intrinsic activity. Target gene analysis showed increased expression of CDKN1A, whereas HOXD10 remained unchanged, consistent across both exosome- and lipofectamine-mediated delivery.

EMT-related genes were also modulated, with increased CDH1 expression and decreased SNAI1 and SNAI2, and Western blot analysis confirmed these changes at the protein level. Collectively, these observations indicate that the phenotypic effects of E-LNA-anti-10b are functionally associated with miR-10b targeting while also reflecting intrinsic exosome activity42–48.

Although CDKN1A and EMT-associated genes were responsive to miR-10b inhibition in our model, HOXD10 expression remained unchanged following LNA-anti-10b treatment. Accumulating evidence indicates that the regulatory relationship between miR-10b and HOXD10 is highly tumor type– and context-dependent. In epithelial malignancies such as breast and colorectal cancers, miR-10b directly suppresses HOXD10, thereby promoting invasion through downstream effectors including RhoC and uPAR49,50. In contrast, glioblastoma appears to exhibit a distinct regulatory landscape. Gabriely et al. demonstrated that miR-10b inhibition in multiple glioma cell lines resulted in derepression of cell-cycle and apoptosis-related genes such as Bim, TFAP2C, p16, and p21, while HOXD10 expression remained unchanged39. Similarly, Guessous et al. were unable to experimentally confirm HOXD10 regulation in GBM stem cells despite repeated validation attempts11. Although certain glioma models (e.g., U251 and LN229) have shown HOXD10 responsiveness, these findings appear to be model-specific rather than universal15. Collectively, these studies suggest that HOXD10 is not consistently a dominant functional target of miR-10b in glioblastoma. Therefore, the modest and statistically non-significant change observed in U87MG cells likely reflects GBM-specific regulatory heterogeneity and differential target hierarchy, rather than inadequate miR-10b inhibition.

We also observed increased expression of PTEN, a key tumor suppressor and negative regulator of PI3K/AKT signaling51,52, and CDH1 (E-cadherin), a critical epithelial marker53,54. The modulation of these genes, along with CDKN1A, aligns with the observed reductions in migration and EMT-associated phenotypes, underscoring the functional impact of E-LNA-anti-10b treatment. These results support the potential of miR-10b-targeted interventions as an adjunctive strategy to modulate glioblastoma cell aggressiveness43,44,55. Our findings extend prior work on miR-10b by introducing exosome-mediated delivery as a functional approach14,41. The choice of hUC-MSC-derived exosomes is supported by their superior anti-tumor activity and delivery efficiency compared with other MSC sources40,56,57.

Furthermore, the high permeability of hUC-MSC-derived exosomes across the blood-brain barrier and their intrinsic therapeutic properties highlight their potential as a delivery platform for nucleic acid-based therapies58,59. The comparison between 2D and 3D culture systems further emphasizes the relevance of functional evaluation. While 2D cultures showed pronounced apoptosis following treatment, 3D spheroids exhibited more modest responses, likely due to enrichment of therapy-resistant, stem-like cells60,61. This highlights the importance of evaluating functional effects across complex tumor models.

Despite the functional insights obtained from exosome‑ and Lipofectamine‑mediated LNA‑anti‑10b delivery, this study has several limitations. Notably, we did not directly quantify miR‑10b levels or activity following LNA‑anti‑10b treatment. Instead, miR‑10b inhibition was inferred from modulation of validated downstream targets, including CDKN1A and HOXD10, together with associated cellular phenotypes. While these findings are consistent with functional inhibition of miR‑10b, they represent indirect evidence and do not provide a direct empirical readout of miRNA suppression. Importantly, we did not perform rescue experiments or orthogonal specificity controls; therefore, specificity and causality cannot be conclusively established, and off‑target contributions to the observed phenotypes cannot be excluded. In addition, LNA‑modified anti‑miRs may inhibit miRNA function through sequestration rather than degradation, such that changes in total miRNA abundance may not fully reflect functional inhibition. Future studies incorporating direct approaches, such as qRT‑PCR–based quantification of miR‑10b and/or miR‑10b–responsive luciferase reporter assays, will be important to more definitively confirm miR‑10b inhibition and strengthen mechanistic conclusions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, hUC-MSC-derived exosomes exhibit intrinsic cytotoxic and anti-migratory activity against U87MG glioblastoma cells. Delivery of LNA-anti-10b via exosomes further enhanced these functional effects, resulting in increased tumor cell death and modulation of EMT-related markers. These findings suggest that exosome-mediated delivery of LNA-anti-10b can functionally augment the anti-tumor activity of hUC-MSC-derived exosomes, representing a promising strategy for glioblastoma therapy that warrants further investigation.
