Protrec2: tissue-specific network-based missing protein recovery method
Authors: Weijia Kong, Wilson Wen Bin Goh, Limsoon Wong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite technological advances, missing proteins remain a challenge in proteomics, obscuring proteins that are biologically or clinically important. We present Protrec2, a probabilistic framework that integrates tissue-specific protein complex annotations with Bayesian inference to recover unreported but biologically present proteins. We benchmarked Protrec2 on HeLa and A549-derived proteomes under “upper-bound” and “lower-bound” scenarios, reflecting distinct but complementary real-world use cases. In upper-bound evaluations, Protrec2 consistently outperformed state-of-the-art methods such as PROTein RECovery, Functional Class Scoring, Hypergeometric Enrichment, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis, achieving the highest recovery rates: up to 98.4% in A549 and 96.5% in HeLa and validating 650 and 453 proteins, respectively. In lower-bound evaluations, Protrec2 maintained superior precision, validating over 90% of its predicted proteins in the A549 dataset and 74.6% in HeLa, while other methods exhibited significant performance drops. We applied Protrec2 to six matched lung tumor–normal pairs and validated predictions against CPTAC. Over 85% of predicted proteins were supported, with cancer-specific proteins mostly upregulated and normal-exclusive ones downregulated. Frequently recovered proteins (e.g. P4HA3, SNX1, HIP1R, NOS2) are known to play key roles in lung cancer, highlighting the biological and clinical relevance of Protrec2. These findings establish Protrec2 as a robust, biologically grounded tool for missing protein recovery, with broad applicability in discovery proteomics and translational research.Keywords: missing proteins, proteomics, protein complex, Bayesian inference, tissue specificity

Introduction

Proteomics, the large-scale study of the complete set of proteins expressed or modified by an organism or cell type, has emerged as a cornerstone of modern biological research. Advances in mass spectrometry (MS) technologies have propelled the field beyond simple protein cataloging toward a deeper understanding of protein dynamics, interactions, and functions across diverse biological contexts, including health and disease. However, despite these technological strides, persistent data quality challenges―particularly the presence of missing values (MVs) or “missing proteins”—continue to limit the reliability and comprehensiveness of proteomic datasets. These data gaps undermine the potential of MS-based proteomics to deliver accurate biological insights, posing significant hurdles to downstream analysis.

Network-based approaches are ideal for inferring undetected proteins and improving protein coverage. By leveraging prior biological knowledge and protein interaction networks, these methods enhance data reliability and facilitate more comprehensive protein identification. Previously, we developed PROTREC (PROTein RECovery), a network-based algorithm designed to enhance missing protein prediction [1]. PROTREC operates on the principle that “absence of evidence is not evidence of absence” and utilizes protein complexes to infer the presence of undetected proteins, demonstrating outstanding results.

While PROTREC has demonstrated significant improvements in missing protein recovery, it has limitations. A key challenge is its heavy reliance on stringent filtering thresholds for identifying protein complexes of interest. Highly stringent filtering criteria render PROTREC highly precise, with predicted protein recovery rates as high as 90%. However, this also has the impact of reducing sensitivity, preventing us from confidently identifying missing proteins that reside in lower-scoring complexes. Furthermore, PROTREC depends primarily on known protein complexes cataloged in databases such as CORUM. However, as CORUM only contains 694 human protein complexes above the size threshold (≥5 proteins), this limits its coverage of the broader human complexome and proteome [2]. This narrow scope inevitably excludes many biologically relevant but less characterized complexes. Moreover, PROTREC suffers from not accounting for protein expression variations across different tissues, preventing it from reporting missing but phenotypically important differential proteins. These limitations constrain PROTREC’s application to real-world problems, thus motivating the development of an upgraded version.

We developed Protrec2, an enhanced protein recovery framework that integrates tissue-specific protein complex annotations with a Bayesian inference mechanism. Protrec2 introduces a probabilistic framework that updates the likelihood of protein presence based on its co-occurrence in experimentally curated complexes and its tissue relevance. Unlike PROTREC, which uses a one-size-fits-all threshold, overlooking tissue-specific expression differences, Protrec2 addresses this by integrating tissue-aware complex annotations and updating protein likelihoods in a context-specific manner, capturing biological heterogeneity across tissues. The Protrec2 package can be found in GitHub: https://github.com/miaomiao6606/Protrec2.

Methods

Protrec2 algorithm

We provide a summarized description of Protrec2 and elaborate in the sections below. Broadly, the Protrec2 workflow comprises three steps: protein complex assembly, tissue-specific protein complex probability assignment, and Bayesian-based protein-level probability estimation (Fig. 1). Protrec2 initially compiles protein complexes from curated databases and expands them using biologically plausible criteria. Each complex is assigned a tissue-specific confidence score, combining literature-based evidence with Gene Ontology (GO)–based functional coherence. These scores are integrated into a Bayesian inference framework that refines protein-level probabilities, allowing the model to infer the presence of unobserved proteins without the need for network topology. This probabilistic updating scheme captures protein complex membership logic and contextual evidence, yielding high-confidence recovery of biologically relevant missing proteins.

Protrec2 workflow. The Protrec2 algorithm integrates observed proteomic data with comprehensive tissue-specific annotated protein complexes. Protein- and complex-level priors are initialized based on sample-specific detection and curated tissue annotations. Through Bayesian inference using co-complex support and logit-based integration, Protrec2 infers the posterior probabilities of missing proteins in a sample-specific manner. Final predictions are made by thresholding the normalized probabilities.

Comprehensive protein complex generation

To construct a comprehensive and biologically relevant protein complex dataset, we integrated data from multiple authoritative sources and implemented a multi-step expansion and refinement strategy.

Initial construction and expansion

The foundation of the dataset was built using the CORUM database, a curated repository focused on mammalian protein complexes with real-world experiment support [2]. Complexes with a size greater than or equal to five proteins were used. The threshold of five proteins was chosen to ensure sufficient confidence in the biological relevance and stability of predictions. However, plenty of complexes are smaller than the threshold mentioned, and not adding them limits protein coverage.

To address this limitation and increase coverage of biologically relevant but less characterized protein complexes, we systematically expanded the CORUM-derived complex set using high-confidence protein–protein interaction data from the STRING database (api version 0.1.5) [3] . We applied a stringent combined interaction confidence score threshold of >780 (on STRING's 0–999 scale), which represents a conservative choice exceeding the commonly used threshold of 700 that is widely adopted in network biology studies [4]. This conservative threshold was selected to ensure that expanded complexes maintain biological plausibility and functional coherence comparable to experimentally curated CORUM complexes while minimizing the inclusion of spurious interactions.

For complex expansion, we implemented a conservative criterion: a candidate protein was added to an existing CORUM complex only if it exhibited high-confidence interactions (score > 780) with ALL proteins in the original complex, ensuring that expanded assemblies maintained biological plausibility and structural coherence. This “all-or-nothing” requirement prevents spurious expansion from isolated high-scoring interactions while enabling discovery of bona fide complex members that were absent from CORUM due to incomplete curation.

Integration with additional databases

We further enriched the dataset by integrating information from the Complex Portal, an European Molecular Biology Laboratory – European Bioinformatics Institute (EMBL-EBI) resource providing manually curated, experimentally validated data on protein complexes across various organisms [5]. The integration process involved standardizing the data by removing redundant characters (such as parentheses and square brackets) and ensuring consistent formatting. This step further expanded the dataset from 1592 to 1731 complexes.

To increase coverage, protein–protein interactions derived from Reactome were included [6]. While Reactome primarily organizes biological processes into pathways, its interaction data provide high-confidence associations between proteins. Although not all interactions necessarily represent stable protein complexes, we retained those that comprised more than five proteins, with at least 80% of the components having valid UniProt identifiers [7]. We aim to enrich the dataset with biologically relevant groupings that could approximate functional complexes. The integration of Reactome data further expanded the dataset from 1731 to 3885 unique complexes.

Tissue-specific confidence annotation and Gene Ontology–based refinement

To assign tissue-specific relevance, we mapped complexes to literature-supported expression profiles and empirical tissue datasets [8]. Tissue relevance score was assigned based on metadata and textual descriptions provided in the original complex records, including source-specific annotations and expression tags. Given a specific tissue, we assigned a value of 1 to complexes with explicit tissue support, 0 to those explicitly nonrelevant, and 0.5 to entries with ambiguous or unspecified tissue association. This scoring was performed separately for each tissue type, resulting in tissue-specific relevance profiles across 27 distinct tissues. The tissues included in this analysis are: bile, bladder, bone, brain, breast, cervical, colorectal, esophageal, gallbladder, gastric, head and neck, kidney, leukemia, liver, lung, lymphoma, myeloma, neuroblastoma, ovarian, pancreatic, prostate, rhabdoid tumor, sarcoma, skin, testis, thyroid, and uterine. For entries lacking strong tissue evidence, we incorporated GO annotation similarity [9]. Although GO annotations are not tissue-specific, we used them to capture internal functional consistency. We reasoned that such coherence may indicate biological relevance in the absence of direct tissue annotation. Specifically, for each complex, we identified GO terms directly annotated to its protein members and computed the number of terms common to all members inside the complex. This value was then divided by the number of proteins in the complex, with the numerator constrained using the minimum of the complex size and the number of shared GO terms to avoid score inflation. The resulting normalized coherence score served as a fallback proxy in cases where tissue annotations were unavailable. These values were averaged with tissue relevance scores to produce the initial probability . The resulting complex list was normalized, deduplicated, and standardized by unifying protein identifiers (e.g. removal of isoform suffixes), producing a final panel of tissue-informed protein complexes with quantitative priors.

Tissue-specific complex probability and Bayesian inference

After assembling the panel of protein complexes, each complex was assigned a tissue-specific probability reflecting its biological relevance with respect to the specific tissue in question. These scores incorporated literature-supported tissue expression as well as functional coherence previously computed from shared GO terms.

This approach updates protein-level probabilities based on their presence in curated complexes and the confidence levels of those complexes.

Benchmark data

To evaluate Protrec2, we conducted benchmark experiments on cell line data (A549 and HeLa cell lines) and clinical data (lung cancer patients). The evaluation involved using an initial protein acquisition method (e.g. Data-Dependent Acquisition mass spectrometry (DDA)) that is less sensitive to identify an initial set of observed proteins, running Protrec2 with these proteins as input, and validating the output by integrating datasets derived from more sensitive acquisition platforms or confirmation from open proteome expression databanks including CPTAC (Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium) for matched tumor proteome data from lung cancer patients, and a diverse set of public resources such as ProteomeXchange repositories (e.g. PRIDE, MassIVE), which aggregate large-scale diaPASEF datasets, high-coverage Astral datasets, and multi-omics proteomics studies.

For A549, we employed DDA-PASEF data comprising 24 samples, organized as three biological replicates, each with four technical replicates [13]. Validation of predicted proteins was achieved by cross-referencing with its corresponding diaPASEF counterpart [13]. This dataset offered a well-controlled design with clearly defined replicates, minimizing biological variability. As such, it served as an ideal testbed to evaluate baseline recovery performance under controlled experimental conditions, and to establish a reference point before applying Protrec2 to more heterogeneous or literature-integrated contexts such as HeLa.

For HeLa, we used the DDA-PASEF data as input. This dataset comprises three samples [14]. Given the limited replicates and the widespread use of HeLa in proteomics literature, we leveraged complementary datasets to provide a robust and diverse validation framework. We incorporated multiple external validation datasets. Initially, we integrated diaPASEF data from two distinct sources to complement one another [14]: the first involved multi-omics measurements that not only captured protein abundance but also reflected transcriptomic and metabolic heterogeneity across HeLa samples [15]; the second was a large-scale meta-analysis of 41 publicly available HeLa proteomics datasets, aimed at recovering overlooked post-translational modifications and consistently underrepresented proteins [16]. This enabled cross-validation of protein predictions in both high-resolution focused experiments and broader, aggregated data landscapes.

To demonstrate our approach also works on other proteomic technologies, we included an external dataset derived from the Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer, known for its exceptional sensitivity and proteomic coverage [17]. Specifically, we obtained a publicly available Astral HeLa dataset, comprising three independent DIA-MS runs of whole-cell HeLa lysates, which previously demonstrated the Astral analyzer’s capability for near-complete proteome coverage [17]. By combining proteins identified across these replicates, we formed a comprehensive external validation set consisting of 8796 unique proteins. Utilizing this high-sensitivity Astral reference allowed us to rigorously assess the predictive accuracy of Protrec2, independently confirming predictions and ensuring that our evaluations were not biased by the initial experiment conditions.

Clinical relevance was established using DIA-MS clinical data from lung cancer samples (ProteomeXchange ID: PXD000603) [18]. A subset of six matched tumor–normal pairs was selected from this larger dataset and analyzed using a high-resolution Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) platform. The samples were derived from paired lung tissue specimens collected under matched clinical conditions. This dataset was particularly valuable for evaluating whether Protrec2 can recover context-specific proteins that are differentially missing in cancer versus normal conditions.

Comparative evaluation of missing protein recovery

To evaluate the performance of Protrec2 in recovering missing proteins, we conducted a systematic comparison against four baseline methods: PROtein RECovery (PROTREC) [1], Functional Class Scoring (FCS) [19], Hypergeometric Enrichment (HE) [20], and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) [21]. The descriptions of these methods can be found in the original PROTREC paper [1]. All methods were assessed based on each algorithm’s ability to predict unobserved proteins within a sample and to match those predictions against independently confirmed reference datasets, including large-scale HeLa and A549 proteomes, multi-omics datasets, and metabolomics resources, as described in the Benchmark Data section.

To capture both conservative and inclusive recovery potential, we defined two validation modes, lower-bound and upper-bound, corresponding to different input assumptions during prediction. The lower-bound and upper-bound validation strategies are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S1. Both scenarios are compared using a 95% precision cutoff, with P-value-based and probability-based methods mapped to precision as outlined in previous literature [22].

In the lower-bound scenario, each sample is analyzed using only the proteins originally observed in that sample as the input. The algorithm predicts missing proteins based solely on this limited sample-specific information. Proteins predicted with high confidence (probability ≥.95 or adjusted P-value ≤.05) and not observed in the sample are considered predicted [22]. If a predicted protein is found in the external validation set, it is counted as validated.

In the upper-bound scenario, each sample is analyzed independently. However, when estimating the probability of an unobserved protein x, the algorithm is allowed to use all other proteins observed in any samples. Specifically, it uses this broader protein pool to compute and . This setting evaluates the maximal recoverability of each method. To infer whether a protein x, which is unreported in a given sample S, is present in the sample S, the model can use all other proteins reported in any replicate of S as supporting evidence. If the inferred existence probability of protein x exceeds the confidence threshold, it is considered predicted. This setup enables the model to incorporate broader evidence from the sample’s replicates. As in the lower-bound scenario, only proteins absent from the current sample and passing the same confidence threshold are treated as predictions. Validation follows the same principle: a predicted protein is considered validated if it is present in the external reference.

These two evaluation modes reflect distinct but complementary real-world use cases in proteomics. The upper-bound scenario mirrors standard experimental practice where investigators acquire multiple technical or biological replicates (typically two to four per condition) and leverage cross-replicate information to maximize protein recovery, a routine analytical approach based on the reasonable assumption that proteins observed in one replicate are likely present but stochastically missed in others. The lower-bound scenario represents the most conservative setting where predictions must be made from a single sample without cross-replicate support, relevant for pilot studies, samples with limited material, or clinical specimens with high inter-patient variability. Together, these scenarios provide a comprehensive evaluation of method performance across the range of data availability conditions encountered in proteomics research.

In earlier work, recovery was defined at the PSM level or based on heuristics such as the two-peptide rule [1], without distinguishing whether a protein was conditionally missing across replicates. In contrast, Protrec2 leverages higher-confidence, curated external protein references and evaluates recovery at the protein level. This allows for stricter, more biologically meaningful validation, while the dual-scenario design captures both conservative and inclusive prediction settings. The design takes advantage of improved availability of and accessibility to large-scale proteomic datasets, enabling a realistic assessment of missing protein prediction.

Results

Protrec2 outperforms all other methods consistently

In the A549 cell line, Protrec2 consistently demonstrated superior performance across both lower- and upper-bound recovery scenarios. Under the lower-bound scenario, where only a single protein list was used for prediction, Protrec2 achieved a notably high mean recovery rate of 90.8%. This substantially outperformed PROTREC (81.8%), FCS (82.8%), HE (80.1%), and GSEA (73.8%) (Fig. 2). Beyond its strong performance in missing protein recovery, Protrec2 also produced a substantial number of predictions per sample (56.1 on average), with the vast majority successfully validated, reflecting unprecedented sensitivity. In contrast, PROTREC produced limited numbers of predictions—5.0 on average per sample—resulting in lower absolute validation counts despite their relatively high recovery rates. Meanwhile, FCS and GSEA predicted broader sets of proteins, averaging 129.1 and 188.9 predictions per sample, respectively, but exhibited a noticeably lower recovery rate than Protrec2. Lastly, HE underperformed across both evaluation metrics, with a limited number of predicted proteins (44.9) and a correspondingly low recovery rate. These findings suggest that Protrec2 not only identifies a broader range of potentially novel proteins but also maintains a significantly higher validation accuracy than methods that prioritize quantity over confidence.

In the upper-bound scenario, recovery rates increased across all methods due to the comprehensive nature of the input. Protrec2 scored a high mean recovery rate of 98.4%, closely matching PROTREC’s 99.7% and clearly surpassing FCS (87.4%), HE (86.9%), and GSEA (74.6%) (Fig. 3). However, Protrec2’s distinguishing advantage is in the scale and reliability of its predictions. It identified ~660 proteins not reported in the original screen per sample, with over 650 of these subsequently validated. This not only highlights Protrec2’s robustness in exploiting expanded input but also underscores its capacity to uncover a substantial number of biologically relevant proteins that are missed by conventional screening approaches. Notably, although PROTREC achieved a marginally higher recovery rate, Protrec2 validated >56.6% as many proteins than PROTREC (415.0 on average), highlighting a dramatic advantage in absolute discovery power.

The upper-bound scenario is not idealized or unrealistic; it is designed to reflect routine proteomics practice where investigators pool observations across replicates of the same biological condition to maximize protein identification coverage. The lower-bound results presented earlier demonstrate that Protrec2 maintains robust performance even in the most conservative single-sample scenario, while the upper-bound results confirm its ability to effectively leverage replicate information when available, as researchers would in actual data analysis workflows.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Protrec2 not only performs competitively in permissive scenarios but also maintains high precision and discovery power under conservative constraints. Its ability to produce large, biologically validated protein sets positions it as a powerful tool for missing protein recovery across a spectrum of experimental conditions.

Protrec2 is platform-agnostic

To rigorously assess the cross-platform robustness of Protrec2, we evaluated its performance on the HeLa cell line using two independent validation frameworks: (i) an integrated reference constructed from high-resolution diaPASEF-based multi-omics HeLa measurements combined with a large-scale meta-analysis dataset and (ii) the ASTRAL reference set derived from a well-curated spectral library of high-confidence protein identifications. These complementary references allowed for a robust evaluation across both data-rich, diverse sources and stringent, curated validation platforms.

Under the lower-bound scenario, where predictions were made from a single observed protein list and validated against these external references, Protrec2 demonstrated consistently superior performance. Using the integrated diaPASEF reference, it achieved a mean recovery rate of 74.6%, closely matching PROTREC (75.5%) and notably higher than FCS (68.7%), HE (62.6%), and GSEA (63.4%) (Fig. 4). Crucially, Protrec2 predicted an average of 59.7 proteins per sample with 44.7 successfully validated, which is seven times more than PROTREC, indicating a strong balance between prediction scope and reliability. The ASTRAL reference further reinforced these findings. Although more conservative in scope, ASTRAL validation confirmed a recovery rate of 69.0% for Protrec2 with 41.7 proteins validated, markedly higher than PROTREC (58.4% with 5 proteins validated) and GSEA (59.8% with 149.7 proteins validated) and marginally higher than FCS (68.8% with 84.7 proteins validated) and HE (68.3% with 28 proteins validated) (Fig. 5).

In the upper-bound scenario, where the full observed protein list was used as a reference during individual protein probability prediction, Protrec2 sustained high performance on both platforms. It achieved a recovery rate of 96.5%, closely tracking PROTREC (99.3%) but significantly exceeding FCS (75.7%), HE (72.3%), and GSEA (63.4%) (Fig. 6). More critically, Protrec2 predicted an average of 469.5 proteins per sample, of which 453 were validated, which was nearly double that of PROTREC (277.7 validated proteins) and far exceeded the validated predictions of FCS (131.3), HE (34.0), and GSEA (184.7). These results demonstrate that Protrec2 not only maintains high accuracy when full input data are available but also greatly expands the number of biologically supported discoveries, striking an optimal balance between prediction scale and reliability under permissive conditions.

Together, these results illustrate the platform-agnostic excellence of Protrec2. Its ability to deliver consistently high recovery rates and validated outputs across both heterogeneous, high-throughput references and conservative, high-confidence libraries demonstrates a rare combination of scalability, precision, and biological validity. Unlike other methods that exhibit volatility across platforms, Protrec2 remains remarkably stable, reinforcing its potential as a generalizable tool for missing protein recovery in diverse experimental and clinical proteomics settings.

Dissecting the dual strengths of Protrec2: complex integration and Bayesian inference

To evaluate the individual contribution of Protrec2’s key design elements, namely, its integration of expanded protein complex with tissue-specific annotations and its Bayesian inference framework, we conducted a controlled analysis using the ASTRAL HeLa dataset under matched validation conditions. Earlier, Protrec2 was run using its full design—both the enriched multi-source complex set and the Bayesian inference mechanism—while baseline methods used only the canonical CORUM complex list. Here, to isolate the contribution of Bayesian inference, we standardized the complex inputs: All methods, including Protrec2, were given access to the same comprehensive multi-source complex set. This unified-access configuration controls prior knowledge advantage and allows comparison of inference strategies on equal footing.

In the upper-bound scenario, Protrec2 maintained its superior performance with a recovery rate of 96.0%, validating 450.3 proteins per sample (Fig. 8). Although PROTREC maintained an excellent recovery rate of 98.4%, it produced only 424 validated proteins. Notably, FCS, HE, and GSEA produced far larger prediction sets under the expanded complex input, with GSEA predicting over 1700 proteins per sample. However, this increase in prediction volume did not translate into superior performance. FCS validated only 615.0 of its 1007.0 predictions (61.0%), HE validated 379.7 out of 568.7 (66.7%), and GSEA validated just 646.0 out of 1759.3 predictions (36.7%). These results highlight a sharp drop in validation precision, particularly for GSEA, indicating a tendency toward overprediction without corresponding biological support.

These findings indicate that while all methods benefit numerically from access to an expanded complex set, only Protrec2 translates that advantage into accurate, high-confidence protein recovery. Its Bayesian inference mechanism selectively amplifies informative cocomplex signals while avoiding false-positive propagation. In contrast, other methods using enrichment- or rank-based algorithms tend to inflate predictions indiscriminately, sacrificing validation precision for breadth. This demonstrates that Protrec2’s performance advantage is not merely quantitative but qualitative, deriving from a well-grounded inference process that remains stable across prediction scales.

Predictions from Protrec2 reveal biological significance

To highlight the clinical relevance and biological significance of proteins identified by Protrec2, we implemented an experiment design using a clinically relevant DIA-MS dataset consisting of six paired patient-derived lung cancer and adjacent normal tissue samples from a lung cancer study (ProteomeXchange ID: PXD000603). To capture patient-specific expression differences, each tumor–normal pair was analyzed individually.

We defined cancer-specific recovered missing proteins as proteins recovered by Protrec2 only in the tumor tissue and absent in both the paired adjacent normal tissue from the same patient and the original screen. Conversely, we defined normal-exclusive recovered missing proteins as proteins recovered only in the adjacent normal tissue and not in the paired tumor tissue and absent from the original screen.

To evaluate these expectations, we validated our results against the CPTAC lung cancer proteomic datasets (PDC000234 and PDC000153). First, we assessed whether the recovered missing proteins could be independently supported by CPTAC evidence. Second, we examined the directional consistency of these proteins by comparing their expression trends in CPTAC. We assessed whether cancer-specific proteins were upregulated in tumors (logFC >0) and whether normal-exclusive proteins were downregulated (logFC <0) relative to adjacent normal tissues.

Across all samples, 87.18% of cancer-specific and 85.45% of normal-exclusive recovered proteins were independently validated in the CPTAC lung cancer proteomic datasets. This high validation rate demonstrates that most Protrec2-predicted missing proteins are indeed present in large-scale clinical proteomic cohorts. Detailed validation rates per sample and group are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2.

The expression trends of predicted proteins reveal a clear and distinct asymmetry in regulation direction for the two groups. Cancer-specific proteins predominantly exhibit upregulation (logFC >0), while normal-exclusive proteins show a strong tendency toward downregulation (logFC <0) across all sample pairs. On average across the six sample pairs, 68.5% of cancer-specific proteins were upregulated and 90.0% of normal-exclusive proteins were downregulated in tumor versus normal tissues. Detailed percentages for each individual sample are provided in Fig. 10. This clear directional separation underscores the biological relevance of Protrec2’s predictions, indicating that the recovered proteins are not only statistically detectable but also functionally aligned with tumor-specific expression patterns observed in independent clinical cohorts.

To further explore the biological relevance of recurrent predictions, we focused on six cancer-specific proteins: Q7Z4N8, Q13596, O75146, Q15018, P35228, and P54821, which were consistently recovered in over 50% of cancer-specific patient samples.

Most of these six proteins exhibit strong literature and database support for functional relevance in lung cancer. P4HA3 (Q7Z4N8) has been shown to mediate TGF-β-dependent extracellular matrix remodeling and to promote epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [23]. SNX1 (Q13596) regulates Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR) trafficking and protein kinase B (AKT) pathway signaling, and has been implicated in chemoresistance mechanisms in lung cancer [24]. HIP1R (O75146) is associated with tumor immune modulation, with evidence linking its expression to PD-1/PD-L1 pathway regulation and immunotherapy response in NSCLC [25]. NOS2 (P35228), encoding inducible nitric oxide synthase, is frequently upregulated in lung tumors and contributes to KRAS-driven tumorigenesis and inflammatory microenvironment remodeling [26]. PRRX1 (P54821) functions as a transcriptional regulator of EMT, promoting tumor cell invasion and metastasis in lung cancer models [27]. Although ABRAXAS2 (Q15018) is not directly studied in lung tumors, it plays a critical role in DNA double-strand break repair through the BRISC complex, a process widely implicated in cancer cell survival and genome instability [28].

Collectively, the level of concordance between prediction, biological function, and disease relevance supports the utility of Protrec2 not only for data recovery but also for hypothesis generation and target nomination in precision oncology. The recurrence of these proteins across multiple patient samples, combined with their independently established roles in lung cancer, underscores the power of Protrec2 to uncover context-specific, biologically relevant molecular signals beyond the detection limit of conventional screens.

Discussion

Protrec2 represents a substantial advancement in the field of proteomics analysis, offering a robust, biologically informed framework for identifying missing proteins across diverse cellular and clinical contexts. Unlike traditional imputation methods or earlier network-based strategies, Protrec2 integrates an expanded and biologically diverse set of protein complexes with tissue-specific information, enabling it to recover proteins that are both structurally plausible and biologically meaningful.

At its core, Protrec2 employs a Bayesian inference model that integrates three complementary layers of evidence—prior detection probability, cocomplex structural support, and tissue-specific complex confidence—into a unified posterior probability for each unobserved protein. This probabilistic formulation not only stabilizes inference through logit-space smoothing but also enables nuanced recovery under sparse or noisy data conditions, outperforming stringent and deterministic strategies such as the original PROTREC. This distinction is particularly evident in lower-bound evaluations (Fig. 9), where Protrec2 recovered over three times as many biologically validated proteins as PROTREC using the same complex definitions.

Unlike enrichment-based methods such as FCS, HE, and GSEA, which assign scores at the complex level and propagate them uniformly to all member proteins. Protrec2 performs protein-level inference directly. For instance, FCS assesses complex activity by comparing observed overlaps against random protein sets but lacks the granularity to distinguish condition-specific or low-abundance proteins within enriched complexes. In contrast, Protrec2 yields individualized, probabilistically weighted predictions, allowing it to recover functionally meaningful proteins that are overlooked by binary enrichment approaches.

Threshold sensitivity analysis further validates Protrec2's probabilistic framework (Supplementary Figs S3 and S4). Unlike baseline methods whose recovery rates plateau across thresholds, Protrec2 demonstrates monotonic improvement from cutoff 0.50 to 0.95, confirming that its superior performance derives from fundamentally better uncertainty quantification rather than threshold-specific optimization.

The design of Protrec2 also positions it well for future extensions. Its architecture is compatible with the integration of single-cell transcriptomics and spatial proteomics data, which would allow even finer-grained recovery of proteins based on microenvironmental context. Furthermore, its probabilistic output can be directly adapted for downstream tasks such as network propagation, drug target inference, or molecular subtyping.

Nonetheless, certain limitations remain. The tissue-specific annotations used were derived from population-level data and might not fully reflect interpatient heterogeneity. Additionally, Protrec2 currently operates on a binary presence/absence basis and does not yet incorporate protein abundance, modification status, or subcellular localization, which may further refine prediction specificity. Our protein complex repository, while comprehensive, remains incomplete for novel or condition-specific complexes. Furthermore, validation performance is constrained by proteomics dataset coverage, with reported recovery rates representing lower bounds on true performance. Addressing these gaps will require deeper integration with quantitative proteomics, structural biology, and multi-omics datasets.

In summary, Protrec2 is not merely an imputation tool—it is a biologically grounded discovery engine. By unifying structural priors, tissue context, and probabilistic reasoning, it advances the precision and interpretability of missing protein recovery, making it well suited to support both discovery proteomics and translational biomedical research.

Conclusion

Protrec2 advances missing protein recovery by unifying priors, tissue-specific complex annotations, and Bayesian inference into a biologically grounded predictive framework. Through systematic benchmarking in A549 and HeLa cell lines, Protrec2 consistently outperformed existing methods across both constrained and permissive validation settings, achieving higher recovery rates and greater numbers of biologically validated predictions. Crucially, its outputs were confirmed across heterogeneous validation sources, demonstrating that Protrec2 not only predicts statistically plausible proteins but also recovers those with meaningful biological context and tissue specificity. These results establish Protrec2 as a robust and generalizable framework for biologically meaningful protein recovery across diverse proteomic landscapes.
