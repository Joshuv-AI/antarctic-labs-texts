Neighborhood disadvantage and general anesthesia utilization in cesarean delivery: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Andrea J Ibarra, Hannah Campion, Cecilia Canales, Brittany N Burton, Alejandro Munoz, Robert S White, Runjia Li, Goundappa K Balasubramani, Janet M Catov
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeighborhood disadvantage, a social driver of health (SDOH), has been associated with adverse perinatal outcomes; yet little is known about its association with anesthetic choice.ObjectiveThe purpose of this study is to assess the association of neighborhood disadvantage and anesthetic choice for cesarean deliveries. We hypothesize that people from the most disadvantaged neighborhoods are more likely to receive general anesthesia for cesarean deliveries compared to those from the most advantaged neighborhoods.Study designThis single-center retrospective cohort study identified index cesarean deliveries performed between 2008 and 2017. People were categorized into no, low, moderate, and high disadvantage neighborhood using the area deprivation index. The odds of receiving general anesthesia versus neuraxial anesthesia (epidural, spinal, or combined spinal-epidural) were compared using logistic regression models.ResultsOf the 16,351 people with cesarean deliveries, 96.0% received neuraxial versus 4.0% general anesthesia. The rates of general anesthesia were 6.3%, 4.2%, 3.1%, and 2.4% for the high, moderate, low, and no disadvantage groups (P<.001), respectively. Indications for general anesthesia by obstetric indication were different by neighborhood disadvantage (P<.001), but no differences were observed by contraindications of neuraxial anesthesia (P=.091). Compared to the no disadvantage group, the high disadvantage group had higher odds of general anesthesia (aOR 2.0, 95% CI (1.5 to 2.7), P<.001). Results were unchanged after evaluating people in labor only.ConclusionsPeople from disadvantaged neighborhoods are more likely to receive general anesthesia for cesarean deliveries, even after considering clinical features. The general anesthesia rate is a meaningful benchmark in obstetric anesthesia that may contribute to disparities.Key words: anesthesia disparities, cesarean delivery, general anesthesia, neighborhood disadvantage

Introduction

In the United States, approximately 32% of childbirths occur via cesarean delivery, with around 3%–6% of these deliveries performed under general anesthesia.1,2 Anesthetic choice has been linked to maternal and neonatal complications.3 Neuraxial anesthesia is the preferred option for cesarean deliveries due to a lower risk profile. Conversely, the use of avoidable general anesthesia has been associated with elevated risks for systemic complications, including pulmonary and cardiac events.4 Despite substantial evidence supporting the benefits of neuraxial anesthesia, disparities in anesthetic utilization for cesarean deliveries persist.5

Social drivers of health (SDOH) play a significant role in contributing to healthcare disparities, including those related to obstetric anesthesia outcomes. Individual and community socioeconomic characteristics have been identified as crucial factors impacting these outcomes. At the individual level, race—a social and power construct has been shown to influence the choice of anesthetic modality, with Black people less likely to receive neuraxial anesthesia and people with a below high school educational level associated with less utilization of neuraxial anesthesia.5,6

At the community level, the neighborhood deprivation index serves as a comprehensive measure of SDOH, encompassing factors such as housing, poverty levels, and access to resources. A previous study addressing the impact of neighborhood deprivation on anesthesia modalities for childbirth reported no difference in neuraxial anesthesia use by deprivation index; however, this association was examined in a country with universal health coverage.6 Liu et al. showed that people from disadvantaged neighborhoods had decreased neuraxial anesthesia use in vaginal deliveries.7 Research examining the relationship between the neighborhood deprivation index and the use of general anesthesia for cesarean delivery, however, is scarce in settings not providing universal health coverage. Our study aimed to investigate the association between neighborhood disadvantage and the use of general anesthesia in cesarean deliveries. We hypothesized that people residing in high disadvantage neighborhoods are more likely to receive general anesthesia during cesarean delivery compared to those residing in neighborhoods with no disadvantage.

Methods

This study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board (STUDY20030130) and followed all STROBE guidelines.8 This single center, retrospective, cohort study utilized data from an institutional registry of electronic health records (EHR) of all people delivering at UPMC Magee-Women's Hospital (MOMI database).9

Study population

The registry included data on people who lived in Pennsylvania and underwent cesarean delivery from January 1, 2008 to December 31, 2017. If people had more than one delivery during the study period, only the first (index) delivery was included in the final analysis.

Using ICD-9, ICD-10 codes and admission data from the EHR we extracted demographic data including age, gestational age, body mass index (BMI) at delivery, primiparity, heart disease, renal disease, and diabetes (gestational, type 1, or type 2), smoking during pregnancy, previous cesarean delivery, preeclampsia, eclampsia or HELLP, and multiple gestation. Self-reported race was categorized as Black, White, Asian, Hispanic, and Others. We categorized “Others” as those who identified as Pacific Islander, Alaska Native, Hawaiian, and with missing information because of the low representation in the cohort.

We also extracted cesarean delivery and characteristics such as in labor before cesarean delivery, neonatal outcomes (i.e., 5-minute APGAR scores<7, neonatal intensive care unit [NICU] admission) and maternal outcomes including prolonged length of stay defined as more than 3 standard deviations above the mean length of stay, and medical intensive care unit (MICU) admissions.3

Using prior studies, we categorized obstetric indications that may warrant general anesthesia including abnormal fetal heart rhythm, fetal distress, blood transfusions, hysterectomy, placenta abnormalities (accreta, increta, percreta, previa, or abruption), uterine rupture, umbilical cord prolapse, and contraindications to neuraxial anesthesia such as hematological conditions (disseminated intravascular coagulation, thrombocytopenia), sepsis and shock, fever during labor, and chorioamnionitis.4,5

State birth records were linked to data in the EHR registry to include additional individual SDOH characteristics such as categorizations of maternal and partner education, receipt of food assistance in addition to number of prenatal visits and maternal death within 1 year of delivery. We also included neighborhood SDOH characteristics such as distance to the hospital, food deserts, and living environment using the Food Access Research Atlas.10 We were interested in characterizing nutritional status by food deserts and food assistance because these variables may influence perioperative outcomes, correlate with lower socioeconomic status and can provide strategies to mitigate disparities.11,12

Area deprivation index

The primary predictor variable of interest was neighborhood disadvantage, a community-level SDOH variable. From the EHR, we extracted the home address for each person who met our inclusion criteria and transformed it into coordinates using ArcGIS Pro (Environmental Systems Research Institute, ESRI). People with unavailable addresses or addresses that could not be geocoded were excluded from the analyses (N=5,030). To calculate the distances between each individual's address and UPMC Magee Women's Hospital, we used network analysis functions available in ArcGIS Pro. The network analysis tools also allowed us to determine the most efficient route considering road networks, providing accurate distance measurements based on travel time constraints and road conditions. Federal Information Processing Standards census block tract data, made publicly available by the United States Census Bureau (2020), was utilized to delineate geographical boundaries and aggregate demographic information at the census block tract level.13

Using the neighborhood atlas, we linked the 2015 and 2020 area deprivation index (ADI) to the census block tract level of each person. For deliveries performed between 2008–2015, we used the 2015 ADI because it was constructed using the 2011–2015 five-year estimates from the US Census American Community Survey. For deliveries performed after 2015, we used the 2020 ADI, which covered 2016–2020 estimates.14 ADI is a validated neighborhood deprivation measurement created by Kind and Buckingham at the University of Wisconsin.15 ADI combines 17 different census indicators, including poverty, education, employment, and physical environment (e.g., housing quality), which measure neighborhood socioeconomic status. These indicators are incorporated into a composite ranking of neighborhood disadvantage ranging from 1, which represents the lowest neighborhood deprivation (least disadvantaged), to 100, which represents the highest (most disadvantaged). We used the median ADI from our sample as a reference to categorize people into quartiles: no disadvantage, low disadvantage, moderate disadvantage, and high disadvantage.16

Outcome of interest

The outcome of interest was the likelihood of general anesthesia use for cesarean delivery. Anesthesia type was reviewed by the first author (A.I.) as reported in the database and manually categorized as neuraxial (i.e., epidural, spinal, combined spinal-epidural) or general anesthesia. Patients who received both general and neuraxial anesthesia were classified as general anesthesia.

Statistical analysis

Maternal and obstetric characteristics and clinical indications for general anesthesia were reported and compared by anesthesia mode: general versus neuraxial groups. Clinical indications for general anesthesia were grouped as in prior studies.1,4 For continuous variables, means and standard deviation (SD) were presented, and group comparisons were conducted using student t-test. Frequency and percentage for categorical variables are reported and compared using Chi-square test.

Univariate logistic regression was performed to analyze the association between general anesthesia and risk factors. We fit a multivariable logistic regression model that included risk factors. Backward and forward elimination was employed sequentially first by removing the least significant variable from the full model (backward selection) and then by adding variables sequentially (forward selection). Variables considered were age, race/ethnicity, gestational age, BMI at delivery, diabetes, previous cesarean delivery, preeclampsia, eclampsia or HELLP, Medicaid, maternal education, distance to hospital, prenatal visits, cesarean delivery description, and obstetrical indication). We used a P-value threshold of 0.2 to ensure that important variables were not removed prematurely. The final model included neighborhood disadvantage, gestational age, BMI at delivery, previous cesarean delivery, preeclampsia, eclampsia or HELLP, Medicaid, prenatal visits (<10) before delivery, and obstetrical indications. Goodness-of-fit, model performance, and multicollinearity tests were performed to assess model fit using Hosmer–Lemeshow P-value, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the variance inflation factor (VIF). Additionally, we assessed the association of general anesthesia and neighborhood disadvantage among people in labor and among the all the deliveries during the study period. A two-sided P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analysis was performed using STATA, version 15.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Results

Within the study timeframe, there were 97,704 deliveries, and of those, 29.8% (N=29,126/97,704) were performed via cesarean delivery. We excluded nonindex births during the study period (N=7,089) and individuals without an ADI (N=5,030) and those with missing anesthesia type (N=656). The final analytic sample included 16,351 people who had cesarean deliveries for their index delivery hospitalization under neuraxial (96.0%) or general anesthesia (4.0%) (Figure 1).

Overall distribution of ADI

We observed a wide range of ADI values. The median ADI was 65 (IQR: 45–85). The overall ADI distribution showed that a high proportion of our cohort lived in the most disadvantaged neighborhoods (Supplementary Figure 1). Our hospital is in Pittsburgh, Allegheny County, Pennsylvania—a region with pronounced maternal and neonatal disparities. Study population

The mean age of our sample was 29.6 (SD 5.8) years old. Of the total sample, 73.6% self-identified as White, 17.6% as Black, 4.7% as Asian, 3.1% as Others and 1.0% as Hispanic. There were baseline differences by anesthesia mode. People who received general anesthesia were more likely to have heart, lung and renal conditions; preeclampsia, eclampsia or HELLP; Medicaid; education less than high school; a partner with less than high school education; and food assistance (Table 1). Among those who received general anesthesia, the rate of this procedure rose in tandem with increasing neighborhood disadvantage (no: 15.6%, low: 19.1%, moderate: 27.0%, high: 38.3%).

We reported baseline and cesarean delivery characteristics by neighborhood disadvantage (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). The rates of general anesthesia were 6.3%, 4.2%, 3.1%, and 2.4% for the high, moderate, low, and no disadvantage groups (P<.001), respectively. Additionally, when exploring the association between individual SDOH and anesthesia mode, we observed no difference in the likelihood of general anesthesia by race, maternal education, or food assistance; however, Medicaid (P=.001), and partner education less than high school (P=0.018) had higher odds of general anesthesia (Supplementary Table 3). Among those who were in labor only, people who came from moderate (aOR 1.6 [1.0 to 2.3]) and high neighborhood disadvantage (aOR 2.6 [1.7 to 3.8]) had higher odds of general anesthesia (Supplementary Figure 2). When including all the deliveries during the study period (Supplementary Table 4), the adjusted odds of general anesthesia in the high disadvantage compared to the no disadvantage group was 2.2 ([95% CI 15–3.3; P=.001]).

Comment

Principal findings

Our findings link neighborhood level-social drivers of health in the peripartum period to the likelihood of receiving general anesthesia. Our study is novel and expands prior findings by demonstrating an association between general anesthesia and neighborhood disadvantage in a cohort that does not have universal health care. These results support the use of general anesthesia as meaningful quality maker for obstetric anesthesia.

Results in the context of what is known

Our results are consistent with those from a study on vaginal deliveries that found an association between low-income and low education neighborhoods and the use of neuraxial anesthesia in a cohort with national health care coverage.7 However, a different study including people with universal health care demonstrated no relationship between neighborhood deprivation and neuraxial use when adjusting for preanesthetic evaluation and other characteristics, highlighting the benefit of affordable access to health care. Although health care is available for pregnant women in the US, eligibility still depends on income, immigration status and state of residence.

Because general anesthesia is associated with adverse maternal outcomes, national societies, including the Society of Obstetric Anesthesia and Perinatology, have established a benchmark of less than 5% of cesarean deliveries using general anesthesia.17 In our study, we observed a 4.0% rate of general anesthesia. While our rate of general anesthesia was less than 5%, the rate varied by neighborhood disadvantage, those from high neighborhood disadvantage had a rate as high as 6.3% compared to 2.4% from the no disadvantage (Supplementary Table 2). The increased risk of general anesthesia in this high disadvantage group was persistent even when accounting for obstetric indications that included fetal distress or abnormal fetal heart rhythm and prenatal visits. Our hospital is a referral center that cares for high-risk pregnancies and therefore not only clinical but also social risk factors may contribute to this difference in rate. Our study highlights the importance of further investigating disparities in general anesthesia by accounting for structural factors that may affect people living in disadvantaged neighborhoods.

Clinical implications

The excess odds of general anesthesia observed in our study may also reflect interpersonal aspects of systemic bias associated with clinical care such as implicit bias. Implicit bias affects communication, trust, and adherence to treatment. Those elements may be associated with the decision to administer neuraxial anesthesia during childbirth. Acknowledging the whole person, their life history, and social structure can improve physician-patient rapport and promote successful counseling and guideline adherence.18

Research implications

Another structural component of deprived neighborhoods is the lack of access to optimal nutrition that can be measured by food deserts and food assistance. Food deserts have been independently linked to pregnancy morbidity.11 Limited access to supermarkets can pose significant barriers to achieving healthy nutrition and appropriate weight gain during pregnancy. In our study, we observed that the most disadvantaged areas had less access to supermarkets (31.9% compared to 0.3%). While nutrition status does not affect the decision for general anesthesia administration, future studies can consider using this additional marker of overall health and socioeconomic status that can potentially elevate the risk of requiring general anesthesia during childbirth.

Our results emphasize the need for advocacy, including health system, policy, and community-level public health initiatives aimed at reducing disparities in the utilization of general anesthesia. These efforts have the potential to address healthcare inequalities at their root, benefiting pregnant people, and improving maternal and neonatal outcomes in cesarean deliveries.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has strengths and limitations. First, our study responds to recent efforts to characterize environmental exposures through the life course. The socio-exposome paradigm has been postulated and urges researchers to measure environmental exposure as precisely as gene expression.19 Uncovering geographic locations with increased risk of using general anesthesia for cesarean delivery may promote health system changes and reallocation of resources. A mapping of needs also helps clinicians and hospitals understand where their services are in demand. Increasing access to neighborhood healthcare resources may influence the utilization and optimization of preconception health.20 We also included individual and neighborhood-level SDOH variables, allowing us to capture unmeasured individual level variation in the odds of general anesthesia for cesarean delivery.

We acknowledge that ours was a single center, retrospective study, and this may limit generalizability. Our study used the deidentified MOMI database and therefore manual chart review to confirm the rate of labor epidural in situ was not possible. We were also unable to manually extract cesarean delivery urgency; however, we included obstetric indications that may warrant the use of general anesthesia as reported in prior studies.4,5 Causality cannot be inferred; however, randomizing people to an anesthetic technique is not considered ethical. The lack of racial disparities in anesthesia type observed in our study may be limited by our sample size; however, using ADI—an actionable SDOH variable allowed us to detect socioeconomic associations with anesthetic use. Future studies should include additional confounders (e.g., individual health literacy level) of general anesthesia use that were not included in the present study. Causal analysis methods may also reveal additional elements of the neighborhood (e.g., segregation) as mediators, confounders, or moderators that may explain the excess risk of general anesthesia associated with neighborhood disadvantage observed in our study.

Conclusions

In summary, people residing in disadvantaged neighborhoods had a higher likelihood of receiving general anesthesia during cesarean delivery, even when clinical indications and maternal characteristics were considered. Efforts to eliminate anesthesia disparities should focus on addressing neighborhood social drivers of health factors in the peripartum period.

Condensation

Compared to no disadvantage, people who live in high disadvantaged neighborhoods exhibit high risk of general anesthesia during cesarean delivery.
