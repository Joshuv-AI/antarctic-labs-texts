Incidence and associated risk factors of anti-tuberculosis drug induced liver injury among TB patients
Authors: Vincent Nyangwara, Ziyaad Waja, Roslyn Thelingwani, Raadhiya Osman, Ziska Pretorius, Keitumetse Majoro, Tom Boyles, Bekiwe Ncwana, Ananyo Choudhury, Collen Masimirembwa, Neil Martinson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis (TB) is a global health challenge. Use of anti-TB drugs to treat TB is associated with high prevalence of side effects that include anti-TB drug induced liver injury (ATDILI). The aim of this study was to determine the incidence and associated clinical risk factors of ATDILI among South African patients on treatment for TB disease.MethodsThis was an ambispective case-control study of patients on treatment for TB disease receiving drug sensitive and drug resistant treatment regimens. The retrospective and prospective studies were done from January 2021 to June 2024, involving a total of 610 patients on treatment for TB disease from whom 13 had ATDILI. From the retrospective and prospective cohorts, we extracted 13 ATDILI cases and 276 controls. Additionally, 44 severe ATDILI cases were directly recruited from the hospital to enrich the number of cases in the study. Prospective patients were followed for up to 6 months, while retrospective participants had a single visit with follow-up durations determined by their parent studies. Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify clinical and demographic factors associated with the development of ATDILI).ResultsIn the studied cohorts, the incidence of ATDILI was 2.1% (13/610). The ATDILI cases and controls consisted of 215 (64.6%) male patients; 57 patients were diagnosed with hepatotoxicity, 44 from the hospitalized cohort,12 from the retrospective cohort and 1 patient from the prospective cohort. The median time from the initiation of treatment to the onset of hepatotoxicity was approximately 30 days. Univariate logistic regression revealed significant differences (p < 0.05) in sex (p = 0.003), HIV status (p = 0.002), BMI (p = 0.038), hypertension (p = 0.047) smoking (p = 0.006), and alcohol consumption (p = 0.001) in relation to ATDILI. Multivariate analysis further revealed that female sex (p = 0.041), HIV status (0.022) and alcohol consumption (0.048) were independently associated with an increased risk of ATDILI.ConclusionsThe incidence of ATDILI in this study was 2.1%, lower than the previously reported range of 3–36% and highlights the need for standardized definitions of phenotypes across studies. In univariable analysis, female sex, HIV status, BMI, hypertension, smoking, and alcohol consumption were identified as potential risk factors for ATDILI. Background

Tuberculosis (TB) is a global health challenge with an estimated 10.8 million people with TB in 2023, a slight increase from 10.7 million in 2022. South Africa, with an estimated annual incidence rate of 468 per 100,000 population accounts for 3% of global patients with TB cases [1]. According to current World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations, first-line treatment of drug-sensitive TB (TB sensitive to all first-line anti-TB drugs) is a combination regimen of four TB drugs with an initial intensive 2-month phase of isoniazid (INH), rifampicin (RIF), pyrazinamide (PZA), and ethambutol (EMB) followed by a 4-month continuation phase of INH and RIF [1]. Current treatment regimens for drug-resistant TB range from 6 to 20 months, with longer durations used selectively but increasingly discouraged [2].

Despite the effectiveness against TB, this polychemotherapy may cause anti-TB drug-induced liver injury (ATDILI), estimated to occur in 3–36% of patients with drug-susceptible tuberculosis [3], often leading to early treatment withdrawal to allow recovery, prolonged treatment duration, treatment failure, and selection of drug resistance [4]. ATDILI may result from direct toxicity of the primary compound, from a metabolite, or from an immunologically mediated response affecting hepatocytes, biliary epithelial cells and/or liver vasculature [4–6]. The incidence of ATDILI when INH is combined with RIF appears higher than for INH alone, with addition of PZA the risk increases further by 3-fold [7]. Because mechanisms of ATDILI are poorly understood, ATDILI is difficult to predict and avoid [8]. Therapeutic responses to ATDILI requires temporary cessation of all TB treatment for days to weeks with re-challenge of TB treatment once liver enzymes have normalized. This may result in re-escalation of liver enzymes warranting change in TB therapy. Changes in therapy are difficult as primary care clinics often do not stock alternative agents to the routine four-drug, fixed dose combination tablet for drug sensitive TB treatment. Should TB treatment be continued in the face of elevated liver enzymes, severe liver necrosis and death may result [5, 9]. Although predicting in whom, and when ATDILI will occur is challenging, some patient characteristics increase risk. These risk factors include co-infection with Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), hepatitis B/C, high alcohol intake, smoking, advanced age, female sex, pre-existing chronic liver disease, malnutrition, and genetic factors [6, 10, 11]. Several studies reported that advanced age as a potential risk factor for ATDILI [6, 10] but, a study in Ethiopia showed that the incidence of ATDILI was higher in younger patients [12]. Chronic alcohol intake is a predisposing factor for ATDILI [13].Some studies suggest that female sex is an independent predictor of ATDILI [14, 15]. However, a recent report suggested that males have a higher risk of developing ATDILI [3, 5, 10]. Pre-existing chronic liver disease and malnourishment have been associated with ATDILI [10, 16]. Differences in study design, study population, ATDLI definitions and monitoring practices restricts understanding of ATDILI.

In South Africa Costiniuk et al. reported ATDILI incidence of 12% in treatment re-challenge in HIV co-infection with TB from 2005 to 2013 at King Edward VIII Hospital in Durban [17]. Although previous studies such as those by Costiniuk et al. have explored aspects of ATDILI in South African TB populations, data is limited on the combined incidence and detailed clinical risk factors using harmonized diagnostic criteria. Our ambispective case-control study aims to address this gap. We therefore aim to determine the incidence and associated clinical risk factors of ATDILI among South African TB patients.

Methods

Study design

This was an exploratory, ambispective case-control study of patients on treatment for TB disease receiving drug-sensitive and drug-resistant treatment regimens. We recruited participants from the following four retrospective studies: the India-South Africa collaboration on pharmacokinetics in drug resistant TB patients (ISA-DRPK) [18], the Adjunctive Aspirin and Ibuprofen for tuberculosis (SMA-TB) [19], the Bedaquiline, Pretomanid and Linezolid Clinical Access Program (BPAL) study [20] and Statin Adjunctive Therapy for TB (STAT-TB) study [21]. Patients with TB who received access to the Bedaquiline–Pretomanid–Linezolid (BPaL) regimen in South Africa were primarily individuals with rifampicin-resistant TB (RR-TB). Controls were defined as patients receiving standard anti-TB therapy who did not develop ATDILI throughout the course of treatment, as evidenced by normal liver function test results on routinely scheduled testing and the absence of clinical signs of liver injury documented in their medical records. Patients with ATDILI and controls were harmonized across studies by applying the same ATDILI definition and the same exclusion criteria, and time points for liver enzyme evaluation relative to TB treatment initiation. Pharmacogenomics of hepatotoxicity in the treatment of drug sensitive and drug-resistant TB in Africans (PGx@DILI) study recruited a prospective cohort of TB patients in Gauteng, and North West, provinces in South Africa. All participants who had laboratory confirmed TB were started on TB treatment; patients with ATDILI developed biochemical evidence of liver injury during their patient follow up attributable to TB drugs after initiating TB therapy. Controls did not develop biochemical liver injury during TB therapy during the patient follow up, despite undergoing repeated screening. The study was conducted in clinics and hospitals at existing research sites where well-established cohorts were already in place, facilitating participant recruitment and data collection (Fig. 1). In prospective studies, ATDILI causality was assessed by study clinicians using Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) measurements. For retrospective cohorts and hospitalized cases, clinical records and laboratory data were reviewed to confirm ATDILI. Prospective patients were followed for up to 6 months, while retrospective participants had a single visit with follow-up durations determined by their parent studies. Two controls were matched to each case of ATDILI based on their TB drug regimen, age, gender, alcohol consumption, and HIV/ART status. Where feasible, we prioritized the selection of controls from the same study or sub-study as the ATDILI cases. The current analysis integrates data across these cohorts to examine the incidence and risk factors associated with ATDILI. By leveraging standardized clinical, demographic, and biochemical data, this approach enables a comprehensive assessment of ATDILI in a real-world, multicenter context.

The study comprised three arms: prospective, retrospective, and extreme phenotype cohorts. Extreme phenotype were patients who reported to the hospital with a suspected ATDILI case

Sample size estimation

Sample size to assess genetic associations for the risk for developing ATDILI was determined using the CaTS power calculator (http://csg.sph.umich.edu/abecasis/gas_power_calculator) developed by Skol et al. 2006 [22]. In the sample size estimation, odds ratios of 2.5 to 3.5 were assessed. A sample size of 160 cases and 320 controls was estimated to have 80% power to detect the effect of SNPs with a MAF of 0.05 for an OR of 3.5. The sample sizes and odd ratios (ORs) for this study were within ranges used and observed in a meta-analysis of risk factors for the association of the NAT-2 slow acetylator status with drug induced liver injury [23]. Moreover, studies have also found association in ATDILI case-control study with fewer samples [24, 25] suggesting that our study is powered enough to found clinical associations.

Patient inclusion/exclusion criteria

Participants were enrolled from clinical trials, observational studies, or directly by study staff. Inclusion criteria were: age > 18 years; self-identified Black South African; laboratory-confirmed Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection (Xpert Ultra or culture); ≤1 week on TB treatment at enrolment; baseline ALT within normal limits; and written informed consent. Additional consent was obtained for HIV testing (unless already documented), sample storage (blood, urine, oral swab, sputum), genomic analysis, and national/international data sharing. Exclusion criteria included: active psychiatric illness or substance abuse affecting consent or adherence; pre-treatment liver injury; hepatitis B/C infection; use of hepatotoxic drugs (other than TB); undocumented concomitant medications; refusal of HIV testing; poor TB treatment adherence; or investigator-assessed unsuitability. Participants not meeting ATDILI criteria post-enrolment were withdrawn and excluded from analysis. Hospitalized ATDILI cases were included if they met the study case definition and had clinical evidence excluding other causes of liver injury. They also consented to HIV testing, biospecimen storage, and hepatic biopsy retention. Exclusion criteria for hospitalized patients included inability to consent, critical illness with poor prognosis (< 48 h), or an alternative diagnosis for liver injury. More information on the study inclusion and exclusion criteria has been extensively addressed on the protocol paper which has been submitted for publication on a separate journal.

Anti-TB drug induced liver injury diagnosis

For the purposes of this study, patients were screened for elevated ALT using a definition of ATDILI defined as alanine transaminase (ALT) level > 120 IU/L and symptomatic (nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, jaundice); or ALT level > 200 IU/L and asymptomatic as described by the Drug‑Induced Liver Injury Network (DILIN) [26]. In the prospective arm of the study ALT, were assessed at three predefined time points: at baseline, on day 15, and on day 30 of treatment. These time points were selected based on existing evidence indicating that the majority of ATDILI cases manifest within the first months of therapy [3, 4]. For the retrospective arm, LFT data were extracted from routine clinical records and hospital databases. In this study, we categorized ATDILI cases into two subgroups for descriptive purposes: Standard ATDILI were participants who developed liver injury during the course of anti-TB treatment, confirmed through routine monitoring and clinical evaluation while severe ATDILI were participants who presented to the hospital with severe liver injury while on anti-TB therapy, often requiring hospitalization and immediate clinical intervention. Both groups were included in the ATDILI case definition, and all met the biochemical thresholds for ATDILI based on ALT elevation as per the study criteria. All the participants were put on standard four-drug regimen for drug-susceptible TB (isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol). Patients later diagnosed with drug-resistant TB were transitioned to the BPaL regimen (bedaquiline, pretomanid, and linezolid). All participants provided written informed consent and the study was approved by the University of the Witwatersrand’s Human Research Ethics Committee (Wits HREC: M240904).

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using STATA (version 18; Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA) and R Statistical Software (version 3.4.3, https://www.r-project.org/). The incidence of ATDILI was defined as the number of new ATDILI cases observed during anti-TB treatment divided by the total number of individuals who were followed up in each individual study. In the pooled dataset, cumulative incidence was calculated by dividing the number of participants who developed ATDILI by the total number who met the inclusion criteria and had complete follow-up data. Patients hospitalized with severe ATDILI, were excluded from incidence calculations because we did not have a clearly defined denominator. Comparisons across subgroups such as sex, ALT, HIV status, BMI category, smoking and alcohol use were performed using non-parametric tests i.e. Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis test where appropriate. To identify factors associated with ATDILI, we conducted univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses. The outcome variable was the occurrence of ATDILI. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to quantify associations between clinical characteristics and ATDILI risk. Variables with a p-value < 0.20 in univariable analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. Backward elimination, guided by likelihood ratio tests (p < 0.05), was applied to derive the final model.

Among 610 participants from the prospective and routine cohorts, 13 developed ATDILI during treatment, yielding a cumulative risk of 2.1% (13/610) in patients who started TB treatment with apparent normal liver function at baseline. The 44 severe ATDILI cases were excluded from this estimate due to lack of a denominator. In our study, the average time from the initiation of anti-TB treatment to the development of ATDILI was 30 days.

A total of 333 patients with TB infections were enrolled, of whom 215 (64.6%) were male. The median age was 39 years (IQR: 30.00–46.00); however, patients in the severe ATDILI group were older on average (42 years) than those in the standard ATDILI (41 years) and non-ATDILI (40 years) groups. ALT levels varied significantly across the groups, participants with severe ATDILI exhibited a median ALT of 478.88 U/L (IQR: 265.00–626.00), markedly higher than the standard ATDILI group (389.06 U/L; IQR: 189.00–729.00) and the non-ATDILI group (21.53 U/L; IQR: 12.00–28.00).

HIV infection was a major comorbidity, affecting 59.2% of the entire cohort. The proportion of HIV-positive individuals differed by ATDILI severity—56.5% in non-ATDILI, 69.2% in standard ATDILI, and 84.1% in severe ATDILI. BMI across groups was generally similar, with all means approximately 23 kg/m². In terms of sex distribution, the standard ATDILI group had a greater proportion of female participants (61.5%, n = 8) than the non-ATDILI group (19.2%), while the severe ATDILI group had a more balanced sex composition (52.3%,). Unemployment was common across all groups ranging from 63.6% − 66.4%.

Although the overall prevalence of diabetes was low (1.8%), it was considerably higher in the severe ATDILI group (9.1%). A similar trend was observed with hypertension, which was more common in the standard (23.1%) and severe (13.6%) ATDILI groups compared to the non-ATDILI group (5.1%). Smoking was also prevalent (71.5% overall), with current smoking common in the standard ATDILI group (53.9%) compared to the non-ATDILI group (42.8%). Alcohol use was widespread (67.6%) but declined with ATDILI severity: 80.8% among non-ATDILI participants, 69.2% in the standard ATDILI group, and 52.3% in the severe ATDILI group.

Clinical symptoms were more pronounced in patients with severe ATDILI. Jaundice was observed in 36.4% (n = 16, N = 44) of patients in this group, compared to 7.9% (n = 1, N = 13) in the standard ATDILI group and 7.6% (n = 21, N = 276) in the non-ATDILI group. Similarly, abdominal pain and dark urine were most frequent in the severe ATDILI group (43.2% and 52.3%, respectively), followed by the standard (30.8% for both symptoms) and non-ATDILI groups (31.2% and 21.0%, respectively).

All TB patients who developed anti ATDILI had documented adherence to their prescribed anti-TB treatment regimens throughout therapy. Adherence was measured using pill counts, clinician assessment, or patient self-report, depending on the study. For hospitalized cases, adherence was inferred from medical records prior to admission. Similarly, all patients living with HIV were initiated on the standard fixed-dose combination therapy comprising Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), Lamivudine (3TC), and Dolutegravir (DTG) (TLD regimen), in line with international treatment guidelines.

Table 3 summarizes the univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses of factors associated with ATDILI. In the univariable analysis, female sex was significantly associated with higher odds of ATDILI (OR = 3.29, SE = 0.37, p = 0.003, 95% CI: 1.16–3.69). HIV-positive status was also significantly associated with increased risk (OR = 3.24, SE = 0.44, p = 0.002, 95% CI: 1.09–3.55). Higher body mass index (BMI) showed a borderline protective effect (OR = 0.94, SE = 0.04, p = 0.038, 95% CI: 0.87–1.01). Hypertension was significantly associated with increased odds of ATDILI (OR = 0.62, SE = 0.69, p = 0.047, 95% CI: 1.17–2.88). Smoking (OR = 3.76, SE = 0.47, p = 0.006, 95% CI: 1.53–3.87) and alcohol consumption (OR = 3.21, SE = 0.37, p = 0.001, 95% CI: 1.57–3.69) were also significantly associated with increased risk. In the multivariable analysis, sex remained a significant independent predictor, with females continuing to show higher odds of ATDILI (OR = 2.31, SE = 0.43, p = 0.041, 95% CI: 0.23–1.19). HIV status also remained significant (OR = 0.35, SE = 0.46, p = 0.022, 95% CI: 0.13–0.82). Alcohol use was independently associated with increased risk of ATDILI (OR = 2.20, SE = 0.40, p = 0.048, 95% CI: 1.00–4.83). However, BMI, hypertension, and smoking were not statistically significant in the multivariable model. Age was not significantly associated with ATDILI in either model and was excluded from the final multivariable analysis.

Discussion

In this ambispective case-control study, we found ATDILI incidence of 2.1% among patients on treatment for TB disease in South Africa. Female sex, HIV status and alcohol use were independent clinical risk factors significantly associated with ATDILI in multivariable analysis, while in univariable analysis, HIV status, BMI, alcohol use, hypertension, and smoking were associated. Our cohort included a subset of drug-resistant TB patients who were transitioned to the BPaL regimen, none of them developed ATDILI. This supports emerging evidence that newer regimens such as BPaL reduces hepatotoxicity risk in DR-TB patients [28]. We retained these individuals in the analysis for completeness and transparency, while noting this distinction in treatment exposures.

The incidence of ATDILI shows wide variation across ethnic and geographic populations, ranging from around 3% to 36% [29, 30]. The variation in reported incidence of ATDILI globally may be attributed to differences in underlying comorbidities, variations in the definitions used for diagnosing ATDILI, and differences in treatment practices, including different regimens [7, 31, 32]. We defined ATDILI based primarily on elevations in alanine aminotransferase (ALT). While the DILIN criteria recommend evaluating multiple liver enzymes, including AST and ALP [26], in this study ALT was selected as the primary marker due to its higher specificity for hepatocellular injury and because it was used for all the included studies. As such, ALT elevations are more reliably attributed to liver injury, particularly in the context of drug-induced hepatotoxicity [4].

A global meta-analysis by Wang et al. (2022) reported a pooled ATDILI incidence rate of 11.5% across 160 studies, while a recent Indian meta-analysis (2024) found an incidence of 12.6%, and a multi-center cohort study in Korea (2023) reported an incidence of 7.6% [29–34]. In our current study, a higher cutoff for alanine aminotransferase (ALT) values was used for case definition than those used in the Indian and Korean studies, which could partly explain the lower ATDILI incidence observed. Our adherence to the DILIN [26] definition is an important contribution towards a well-defined case-control cohort that can be used to investigate the clinical risk factors for ATDILI although direct comparisons with previous ATDILI studies should be interpreted cautiously due to differences in population characteristics and definitions of ATDILI.

The clinical presentation of ATDILI is varied and non-specific, ranging from asymptomatic liver dysfunction to severe acute hepatitis or even acute liver failure [3, 35]. The mechanism of ATDILI primarily involves hypersensitivity reactions, which can present with symptoms such as dark urine, jaundice, fever, fatigue, and abdominal pain. In our study, the most commonly reported symptoms among participants who developed ATDILI were dark urine, jaundice, and abdominal pain, observed in approximately 65% of symptomatic cases. ATDILI can sometimes be fatal if not diagnosed and managed promptly [2]. Therefore, it is advisable to routinely monitor liver function in patients receiving anti-TB drugs.

In our study, the mean time from the initiation of anti-TB treatment to the development of ATDILI was approximately 30 days, similar to previous reports of 24–30 days reported by [29, 34, 36]. Several factors have been shown to influence the severity of ATDILI, including advanced age, sex, smoking, alcohol consumption, HIV/AIDS, and body mass index [16, 34, 36]. We found significant association between sex of patients and ATDILI. Studies have reported that women are at increased risk of developing hepatotoxicity when receiving anti-tuberculosis treatment [6].

In our study, the proportion of women with ATDILI was higher than that of men, suggesting that women receiving anti-tuberculosis drugs may be more prone to liver damage or even liver failure. Kumar et al. [34]. conducted a study in India, reporting that approximately 70% of anti-TB drug-associated liver failure cases occurred in female patients. In contrast, among hospitalized patients with severe ATDILI, the proportion of females was almost the same as that of males but, in the standard ATDILI group, females were also more prevalent accounting for 61.54% of cases. We found no significant difference in age of patients and risk for ATDILI. There are few studies that have reported an association between age and ATDILI [36], although some studies have reported an association between advanced age and ATDILI [6, 37].

Hypertension, alcohol consumption, and smoking were also identified as risk factors for ATDILI in the univariable analysis. These findings suggest that female sex, higher BMI, HIV infection, hypertension, smoking, and alcohol use could contribute to elevated liver enzyme levels and may be considered potential risk factors for ATDILI. Given that tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), lamivudine (3TC), and dolutegravir (DTG) (TLD) is typically well tolerated and associated with a low incidence of hepatotoxicity, it is likely that the observed risk of ATDILI in this cohort was primarily driven by the anti-TB drug regimens rather than the TLD therapy [29]. Additionally, we consistently observed that all patients appeared to have good adherence to their anti-TB therapy, which could have contributed to an increased risk of ATDILI due to higher cumulative drug exposure. Good adherence was defined as completion of ≥ 90% of prescribed doses during the treatment period under review. Our study found no significant association between the presence of diabetes and the occurrence of ATDILI. Several studies have similarly reported no significant association between diabetes and ATDILI risk [6, 38].

In this study, stratification of ln(ALT) levels by participants demographic and behavioral variables revealed notable trends. Participants younger than 65 years, particularly those less than 36 years of age, exhibited higher ln(ALT) values compared to those aged ≥ 65 years. This may reflect age-related differences in hepatic enzyme activity or susceptibility to hepatocellular injury. Female participants had marginally higher ln(ALT) levels than males among those who developed ATDILI, possibly reinforcing that female sex confers increased ATDILI risk [9]. Additionally, higher ln(ALT) levels were noted among current smokers and alcohol users, indicating possible additive hepatic stress [5, 39]. These findings underscore the need for liver function monitoring in high-risk groups during anti-TB therapy.

Even though this study focused on non-genetic risk factors for ATDILI, DNA samples from cases and controls have been collected and are currently undergoing genomic analysis to investigate genetic biomarkers associated with ATDILI. This future work aims to complement our current findings and contribute to the development of precision risk prediction tools.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is the ambispective design, including both retrospective and prospective data of patients from several studies ensuring a diverse and representative sample of ATDILI cases that enhances external validity and generalizability across patient subgroups. Additionally, by leveraging existing data and biological samples from participants who have already consented, the study maximizes efficiency and avoids unnecessary duplication of effort. The deliberate focus on patients with severe ATDILI phenotypes, especially from major hospitals, enriches the dataset with cases likely to provide deep insights into risk factors and mechanisms underlying severe liver injury. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the relatively low number of ATDILI cases may have limited the statistical power to detect significant associations in multivariable analysis. Second, the use of ALT alone to define hepatotoxicity, without incorporating other LFT parameters such as AST and ALP, may have underestimated the true incidence of ATDILI. Another limitation of the study is data heterogeneity and selection bias, as it combines data from diverse study designs and clinical settings, which may result in inconsistencies in data quality, laboratory testing, and diagnostic criteria. The other limitation of our study is the lack of comprehensive data on concomitant medications that may influence liver function. In particular, in the SMA-TB study, aspirin was administered as part of the intervention and may have contributed to hepatotoxicity in some patients. We did not adjust for the use of such non-TB medications in our regression models, which could have introduced confounding.

Conclusion

This ambispective case-control study found an incidence of ATDILI at 2.1%. While several factors such as HIV status, BMI, hypertension, smoking, and alcohol consumption were associated with ATDILI in univariable analysis, only female sex, HIV status and alcohol consumption remained significantly associated after multivariable adjustment. These findings suggest that female patients, individuals living with HIV, and those who consume alcohol may be at increased risk of developing ATDILI and could benefit from closer monitoring during tuberculosis treatment. Future studies incorporating genetic and pharmacokinetic data are recommended to improve risk prediction and prevention strategies.
