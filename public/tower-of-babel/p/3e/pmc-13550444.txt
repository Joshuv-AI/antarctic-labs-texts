Molecular identification of trichomonads in captive sugar gliders and tortoises with diarrhea: a case study
Authors: Subin Lee, Joohyung Kim, Kihwan Yang, Godfrey Sammy Mwakyusa, Seongjun Choe, Min-Jeong Kim, Dong-Hyuk Jeong, Seung-Hun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Trichomonads are widely distributed protozoa; however, studies on these organisms in captive sugar gliders and tortoises kept as companion animals remain limited. This study investigated trichomonads in four captive companion animals presenting with diarrhea—two sugar gliders (Petaurus breviceps), one red-footed tortoise (Chelonoidis carbonaria), and one Horsfield’s tortoise (Testudo horsfieldii)—using conventional PCR and 18S rRNA gene metabarcoding. Light microscopic examination of fresh fecal samples at a veterinary clinic confirmed the presence of motile protozoa morphologically consistent with trichomonads in all animals. To identify the species, fecal samples were collected from each animal, and DNA was extracted for PCR and metabarcoding targeting the 18S rRNA gene. Trichomonads were not detected in any of the four samples by screening conventional PCR. In contrast, subsequent metabarcoding targeting the V9 region of the 18S rRNA gene successfully identified Trichomitus batrachorum in both tortoises and low abundance trichomonads in one sugar glider. Subsequent PCR using alternative primer sets confirmed the presence of trichomonads in all four animals. Phylogenetic analysis identified T. batrachorum in both tortoises, Simplicimonas sp. in one sugar glider, and Pentatrichomonas hominis in the other sugar glider. These findings expand current knowledge of the host range of trichomonads in captive sugar gliders and tortoises and suggest their possible clinical relevance in these animals presenting with diarrhea.

Introduction

Parabasalids belong to the phylum Parabasalia and are distinguished by the presence of a parabasal apparatus, cryptopleuromitosis, and hydrogenosomes, which are involved in anaerobic energy metabolism1,2. Through the integration of morphological and molecular analyses, parabasalids have been classified into six classes: Hypotrichomonadea, Trichomonadea, Tritrichomonadea, Cristamonadea, Trichonymphea, and Spirotrichonymphea2. Parabasalids are commonly found in the distal digestive tract of animals, where they can occur as symbionts, commensals, or parasites1. Species within the classes Hypotrichomonadea, Trichomonadea, and Tritrichomonadea are particularly important in the fields of medicine and veterinary science3–5.

Trichomonads are flagellated parabasalid protists that thrive in warm, moist environments and primarily inhabit the gastrointestinal and urogenital tracts of diverse vertebrate and invertebrate hosts6. Several species of trichomonads are known for their pathogenicity, including Trichomonas vaginalis in humans3, Tritrichomonas foetus in cattle4, and Trichomonas gallinae in birds5. In contrast, some trichomonads are nonpathogenic or have poorly defined pathogenic potential. For instance, Tetratrichomonas gallinarum is generally considered a commensal organism7. However, studies have reported that this parasite may induce lesions in the ceca and livers of avian hosts, including domestic fowl and turkeys, and pathogenic manifestations associated with T. gallinarum infection continue to be reported7,8. Nevertheless, experimental infections have not provided evidence of its pathogenicity, indicating that the pathogenicity of this parasite is controversial7,9.

Because the pathogenic potential of trichomonads varies among species, identifying the species is important for understanding their clinical significance. Traditionally, species identification has been performed using morphological characteristics under light microscopy; however, this method is often challenging due to the morphological similarity among trichomonads10. To overcome these limitations, molecular methods such as conventional PCR-based detection of the 18S rRNA gene, ITS1–5.8S rRNA–ITS2, and some protein-coding genes have been widely used11,12. While PCR is a powerful tool for pathogen detection, it requires known primer sets and may fail to detect unexpected or low-abundance taxa in mixed samples due to primer specificity. In contrast, metabarcoding based on next-generation sequencing (NGS) targeting the V9 region of the 18S rRNA gene enables identification of rare or novel pathogens that may be difficult to detect using conventional methods13. Additionally, this method can detect organisms present at low abundances and allows for the simultaneous identification of a broad diversity of eukaryotes.

Over the past decades, exotic companion animals such as sugar gliders and tortoises have gained increasing popularity worldwide, although their history as companion animals is shorter than that of dogs and cats14,15. Despite their growing popularity, the molecular characterization of their associated trichomonads and investigations of their pathogenic potential are limited. Parasitological surveillance in these animals is therefore important for establishing baseline data on the host range and possible clinical relevance of trichomonads.

Therefore, this study investigated trichomonads in two sugar gliders, one red-footed tortoise, and one Horsfield’s tortoise with diarrhea in the Republic of Korea (Korea) using conventional PCR and metabarcoding.

Methods

Ethical statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chungbuk National University (CBNUA-26–0064-03). The experiments were performed using feces that the animals naturally excreted for diagnostic purposes without restraint or forced fecal sampling for the experiment. Verbal informed consent was obtained from the owners before fecal sample collection. The owners were informed that the fecal samples collected during diagnostic examination would be used for this study. All procedures were performed by licensed veterinarians. None of the treatments were harmful or detrimental to animal welfare.

Clinical examination and microscopic screening

Four privately owned companion animals presenting with diarrhea and anorexia were brought to a veterinary clinic in the Republic of Korea: two sugar gliders (Petaurus breviceps, sample IDs EA-4 and EA-16), one red-footed tortoise (Chelonoidis carbonaria, sample ID EA-13), and one Horsfield’s tortoise (Testudo horsfieldii, sample ID EA-14). To screen for gastrointestinal parasites, light microscopy was used to examine each fecal sample. Motile protozoan organisms morphologically consistent with trichomonads were observed in all four cases. However, definitive species identification was not possible.

After diagnosis, all animals received symptomatic treatment supplemented with an additional 20–30 mg/kg dose of metronidazole. A re-examination two weeks later confirmed their recovery, and a microscopic examination confirmed the absence of trichomonads in feces.

DNA extraction and screening conventional PCR

To identify the species of trichomonads, fecal samples were collected from each animal and transferred to the College of Veterinary Medicine, Chungbuk National University, Korea. Genomic DNA was extracted on the day of arrival using a QIAamp® Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted DNA was stored at -20 °C until further use.

Conventional PCR was performed using two primer sets for trichomonad detection. For amplification of the ITS1 region, the forward primer (5′-GTAGGTGAACCTGCCGTTG-3′) and reverse primer (5′-ATGCAACGTTCTTCATCGTG-3′) were used 16. PCR amplification was performed at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 58 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. For amplification of the ITS1–5.8S rRNA–ITS2 region, TFR-3 (5′-CGGGTCTTCCTATATGAGACAGAACC-3′) and TFR-4 (5′-CCTGCCGTTGGATCAGTTTCGTTAA-3′) were used 4. PCR amplification was performed at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 67 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR was performed using AccuPower HotStart PCR Premix Kit (Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea).

Metabarcoding of 18S rRNA and 16S rRNA genes

Four fecal DNA samples used for conventional PCR were individually sent to Macrogen in Daejeon, Korea, for metabarcoding. Metabarcoding was performed for two purposes. First, trichomonads were screened by targeting the V9 region of the 18S rRNA gene of eukaryotes. Second, pathogenic bacteria were screened using the bacterial 16S rRNA gene V3–V4 region.

For the V9 region of the 18S rRNA gene, PCR was performed using the universal primer sets 1380F (5′–CCCTGCCHTTTGTACACAC–3′) and 1510R (5′–CCTTCYGCAGGTTCACCTAC–3′) 17. For the V3–V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene, universal primers 341F (5'-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3') and 805R (5'-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3') were used 18. PCR was performed under identical conditions: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 25 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The resulting PCR products were purified with AMPure beads (Agencourt Bioscience, Beverly, MA). The purified PCR products were then used for a second PCR with Nextera XT Index primers to add index sequences required for final library construction. A second PCR was performed under the same conditions as the first, except with 10 cycles. Amplified products were purified using AMPure beads, quantified using the VICTOR Nivo™ (Perkin Elmer) with PicoGreen reagents, and qualified using the TapeStation D1000 ScreenTape (Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany). The libraries were normalized, pooled, and quantified by qPCR following the qPCR Quantification Protocol Guide (KAPA Library Quantification Kits for Illumina sequencing platforms). Paired-end sequencing (2 × 300 bp) was conducted on the Illumina MiSeq i100 platform using MiSeq i100 Series Reagent Kits (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Bioinformatic analysis

Adapter and primer sequences were removed from the raw reads using Cutadapt (v3.2), with forward and reverse reads truncated to 200 bp and 180 bp for the V9 region of 18S rRNA gene, and to 250 bp and 200 bp for the V3–V4 region of 16S rRNA gene, respectively 19. Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated through error correction, merging, and denoising using the DADA2 (v1.18.0) algorithm20. Paired-end reads were merged using an overlapping approach, and chimeric sequences were removed using the consensus method with the removeBimeraDenovo function in DADA2. ASVs shorter than 100 bp for the V9 region of the 18S rRNA gene and 350 bp for the V3–V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene were filtered out using R (v4.0.3). Taxonomic assignment was performed using QIIME (v1.9.0)21. Each ASV from the V9 region was compared against NCBI_NT_20250606, the NCBI nucleotide database generated on 6 June 2025, using BLAST + (v2.9.0; query coverage > 85% and identity > 85%)22. For the V3–V4 regions, each ASV was aligned to the organism with the highest similarity in the corresponding reference database (NCBI_16S) using the Bayesian classifier algorithm (DADA2 v1.18.0; confidence value = 50).

Confirmatory conventional PCR, cloning, and phylogenetic analysis

After trichomonads were identified in samples by metabarcoding, additional conventional PCR was performed to confirm the trichomonads species identified. PCR targeting the 18S rRNA gene of trichomonads was conducted using alternative primer sets and the same kit described above. For amplification of the 18S rRNA gene, T18SF (GGAAGCACACTTCGGTCATAG), T18SR (CGTTACCTTGTTACGACTTCTCC), T18SRi (CCTTCCGTCAATTCCTTCAA) and T18SFi (AGGGTTTCTGTCGATCAAGG) were used16. PCR amplification was performed at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min, 56 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 2 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. All positive samples were sent to Macrogen (Daejeon, Korea) for bidirectional sequencing. In cases of EA-16, for which direct sequencing failed, the positive PCR product was cloned into E. coli DH5α using the pGEM-T Easy Vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), cultured, and sequenced.

The obtained sequences were aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm in MEGA 11. Species identification was based on search of BLAST against the NCBI GenBank database using sequence identity and query coverage. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the maximum likelihood method based on General Time Reversible model with 1,000 bootstrap replicates in MEGA 11.

Results

Taxonomic identification of trichomonads by metabarcoding

For the V9 region of the 18S rRNA gene, the total reads obtained from each sample were as follows: EA-4, 379,559; EA-13, 467,919; and EA-14, 237,793. After quality trimming and filtering, 369,926 reads were retained for EA-4, 456,919 for EA-13, and 229,181 for EA-14. NGS could not be performed for the other sugar glider (EA-16) due to failure in library construction.

Taxonomic classification of ASVs from the V9 region revealed a broad range of eukaryotic groups, including parasites, fungi, and plants (Fig. 1A). Among the ASVs assigned to the trichomonad group, Trichomitus batrachorum was identified in a red-footed tortoise and a Horsfield’s tortoise with abundances of 10.20% and 22.88%, respectively (Table 1). Taxonomic identification of bacteria by metabarcoding

For the V3–V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene, the total reads obtained from each sample were as follows: EA-4, 84,958; EA-13, 97,438; EA-14, 101,914; and EA-16, 125,276. After quality filtering, 34,724 reads were retained for EA-4, 40,651 for EA-13, 43,214 for EA-14, and 50,235 for EA-16.

To identify bacterial taxa potentially associated with diarrhea in each animal, taxonomic classification of ASVs from the V3–V4 region was performed (Fig. 1B). Among the four animals analyzed, an ASV assigned to Escherichia fergusonii, which has previously been isolated from goats and dairy cattle with diarrhea23,24, was detected exclusively in the two sugar gliders (Table 1). To our knowledge, no pathogenic bacterial taxa associated with diarrhea were identified in the red-footed tortoise or the Horsfield’s tortoise.

Conventional PCR, cloning, and phylogenetic analysis of trichomonads

Screening conventional PCR was first performed using two primer sets to test for the presence of trichomonads in fecal samples from four animals. However, all samples tested negative (Table 1).

Based on the trichomonad taxa suggested by the metabarcoding analysis, confirmatory PCR and cloning targeting the 18S rRNA gene were performed. These analyses confirmed the presence of trichomonads in the feces of all four animals. T. batrachorum was identified in a red-footed tortoise and a Horsfield’s tortoise, and Simplicimonas sp. was identified in one sugar glider by direct sequencing. In the other sugar glider, Pentatrichomonas hominis was identified by cloning and sequencing of the 18S rRNA gene amplicon.

The obtained 18S rRNA gene sequences were compared with reference sequences in the GenBank database using the BLAST algorithm. The T. batrachorum sequences exhibited 98.66–99.06% identity with T. batrachorum sequences from iguanas (JX565072) and 96.67–97.46% identity with T. batrachorum sequences from pigs (KC953861). The Simplicimonas sp. sequence exhibited 95.51% identity with an S. similis sequence from a human (MH053427). The P. hominis sequence showed 99.61% identity with a P. hominis sequence from a bull (DQ412640) and 99.60% identity with a P. hominis sequence from an Angola colobus (HQ149968).

Phylogenetic analysis based on the 18S rRNA gene revealed distinct separation among the orders Hypotrichomonadida, Tritrichomonadida, and Trichomonadida within the phylum Parabasalia (Fig. 2). Additionally, the analysis confirmed the presence of T. batrachorum and P. hominis. Regarding Simplicimonas, the sequence obtained in this study was most closely related to S. similis; however, the two showed distinct divergence. Based on sequence similarity and phylogenetic relationship, the isolate was designated Simplicimonas sp. Discussion

This study investigated trichomonads in captive sugar gliders and tortoises presenting with diarrhea and suspected of trichomonad infection based on light microscopic examination in the Republic of Korea, using conventional PCR and metabarcoding approaches. Motile protozoa were observed in all animals at the veterinary clinic; however, conventional PCR initially failed to detect trichomonads in fecal samples from four of the animals. However, subsequent metabarcoding analysis revealed the presence of trichomonads and enabled species-level identification in two tortoises, as well as the detection of low-abundance trichomonads in one sugar glider. These results suggest that the screening PCR failure was likely due to primer-template mismatches or inefficient amplification conditions rather than the true absence of trichomonads25. Based on these findings, confirmatory PCR targeting the 18S rRNA gene was performed on all four samples. This confirmed the presence of trichomonads and identified trichomonad species through Sanger sequencing and phylogenetic analysis. These results demonstrate that metabarcoding is a valuable approach for screening and detecting trichomonads, and that subsequent PCR can confirm the presence of trichomonads and enable species-level identification.

Among the identified trichomonad species, Trichomitus batrachorum belongs to the order Hypotrichomonadida, the family Hypotrichomonadidae, and is known to exhibit trophozoite, pseudocyst, and cyst stages26. This parasite has been reported in amphibians26, reptiles6,27, and mammals such as pigs28, primates and humans29. In the present study, T. batrachorum was identified in a red-footed tortoise and a Horsfield’s tortoise via conventional PCR. Additionally, 18S rRNA gene metabarcoding revealed relatively high abundances of T. batrachorum in both tortoises (10.20% and 22.88%, respectively), indicating that it was the predominant trichomonad. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the obtained sequences clustered with T. batrachorum sequences from iguana (JX565072) and pig (KC953861). While previous studies have not reported definitive clinical signs associated with T. batrachorum infection, both tortoises in this study exhibited gastrointestinal symptoms, including diarrhea and anorexia6,26–29. Because T. batrachorum was predominant in the 18S rRNA gene metabarcoding data from both tortoises, it may have contributed to their clinical signs. However, clinical improvement after metronidazole treatment alone is insufficient to determine whether T. batrachorum was the etiological agent, as metronidazole is active against both anaerobic bacteria and protozoa30. Furthermore, a previous report of T. batrachorum in non-human primates and their keepers suggests possible zoonotic potential29. This highlights the need for further investigations into its pathogenicity and epidemiological significance in tortoises.

Pentatrichomonas hominis is a flagellated protozoan belonging to the order Trichomonadida and the family Trichomonadidae. It is regarded as a commensal that inhabits the large intestine of various mammals31. However, P. hominis has also been associated with diarrhea in humans, dogs, non-human primates, and cats10,32–38. The presence of P. hominis in a boa, a scops owl, goats, and pigs suggests an expanded host range beyond mammals27,37,39,40. In the present study, P. hominis was identified in a sugar glider via cloning and sequencing analysis. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the obtained sequence was closely related to the P. hominis sequence from the goat (JX565028). To our knowledge, this is the first report of P. hominis infection in sugar gliders. Notably, the sugar glider in this study exhibited gastrointestinal signs that resolved following metronidazole treatment. However, as noted above, clinical improvement after metronidazole treatment alone is insufficient to determine whether P. hominis was the etiological agent. In addition, 16S rRNA gene metabarcoding detected E. fergusonii, a bacterium previously isolated from diarrheic animals; therefore, its possible contribution to the clinical signs cannot be excluded41. Overall, the etiological significance of P. hominis and E. fergusonii in this case remains uncertain and requires further investigation. Nevertheless, the detection of P. hominis in a sugar glider expands the known host range of this trichomonad.

Several species currently classified within the genus Simplicimonas were formerly known as the genus Monocercomonas. However, a previous study reassigned these species to the genus Simplicimonas and renamed them (e.g., S. moskowitzi, S. cuniculi, S. axostylis, S. molae, S. caviae, S. pistillum, and S. lori)2. A novel species, S. similis, belonging to the order Tritrichomonadida and the family Simplicimonadidae, was first described in the feces of a lined leaf-tailed gecko (Uroplatus lineatus)2. Subsequent studies have also reported its presence in carabaos and humans, though no definitive association with clinical signs has been established28,42. In the present study, BLAST analysis revealed that the Simplicimonas sp. identified in a sugar glider shared only 95.51% identity with S. similis isolated from humans (MH053427). Phylogenetic analysis showed that the obtained sequence diverged from S. similis. However, V9 region metabarcoding analysis failed to detect Simplicimonas. Due to the limited research on Simplicimonas and the scarcity of reference data, the organism could not be definitively identified at the species level. Clinically, the sugar glider exhibited diarrhea that resolved following metronidazole treatment. In this animal, 18S rRNA gene metabarcoding revealed a high relative abundance of Blastocystis sp., a gastrointestinal protozoan parasite that has been associated with diarrhea in humans43. Therefore, Blastocystis sp. may also have contributed to the diarrhea in this sugar glider, although its pathogenic role could not be determined in this study. Meanwhile, 16S rRNA gene metabarcoding detected E. fergusonii, indicating that a bacterial contribution also cannot be excluded. Therefore, the diarrhea observed in this sugar glider may have involved multiple organisms, and the specific role of Simplicimonas sp. remains uncertain. These findings suggest that sugar gliders may be a previously unrecognized host of Simplicimonas, though its clinical significance in this species is still unknown.

Metabarcoding, an application of next-generation sequencing (NGS), enables the simultaneous detection of multiple eukaryotic taxa within a single sample. This includes rare or potentially novel pathogens, which are often difficult to identify using conventional methods, such as microscopy, culture, and conventional PCR44. However, metabarcoding results can be affected by primer sets, target regions, and reference databases13. In the present study, Simplicimonas sp. was identified in a sugar glider by conventional PCR but not by 18S rRNA gene metabarcoding, which showed only P. hominis (0.01%) and T. augusta (0.07%) at low abundances. Additionally, 10.75% of ASVs could not be assigned to any known taxa, suggesting that some taxa may have remained unidentified. Although the NCBI database includes Simplicimonas sequences, available Simplicimonas reference sequences are relatively short and provide limited coverage of the V9 region targeted in this study, which may have contributed to the failure to assign Simplicimonas sp. by metabarcoding. This discrepancy may also be explained by differences in primer sets: conventional PCR used trichomonad-specific primers that showed 100% identity to the corresponding Simplicimonas reference sequence, whereas the V9 metabarcoding forward primer showed 94.74% identity. Additionally, one sugar glider sample could not undergo metabarcoding analysis due to failure during library construction. These findings highlight the need for further studies using optimized primer sets, improved library preparation protocols, and expanded reference databases to improve detection accuracy.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to identify T. batrachorum in red-footed tortoise and Horsfield’s tortoise, P. hominis and Simplicimonas sp. in sugar gliders, thereby contributing to a better understanding of trichomonad host associations in these animals. The results also suggest that metabarcoding can detect diverse trichomonads, though full-length 18S rRNA gene sequencing remains essential for confirming their identity and achieving reliable species-level identification. Given the limited research on trichomonads in sugar gliders and tortoises, and their detection in animals presenting with diarrhea, further studies are needed to understand their potential pathogenic role, host range, and transmission routes. Furthermore, the sequences obtained in this study provide valuable data that highlight the need to expand sequence databases to facilitate the more accurate identification and classification of trichomonads.

Conclusion

This study used molecular approaches to identify trichomonads in captive sugar gliders and tortoises presenting with gastrointestinal signs. Although motile protozoa were observed by light microscopy, trichomonads were not detected in any sample by the initial screening PCR. In contrast, 18S rRNA gene metabarcoding detected trichomonads that were not amplified by the screening PCR, and subsequent targeted PCR and phylogenetic analysis identified T. batrachorum in two tortoises, P. hominis in one sugar glider, and Simplicimonas sp. in another sugar glider, representing the first molecular identification of these trichomonads in Korea. These findings also suggest that trichomonads may be clinically relevant in sugar gliders and tortoises with gastrointestinal signs, although their pathogenic role requires further investigation. Overall, metabarcoding may be a useful addition to conventional PCR for detecting and characterizing diverse trichomonads, while expanded sequence databases and further studies are needed to clarify their host range, pathogenic role, and transmission routes.
