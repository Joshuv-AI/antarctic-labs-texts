Successful Immunosuppressive Treatment of Spinal Segmental Myoclonus in GlyR Antibody–Associated Stiff-Person Spectrum Disorder/PERM Spectrum
Authors: Yi Yang, Yuanhe Zhang, Pei Xiao, Ting Zeng, Xianwen Zhang, Yufeng Tang
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Spinal segmental myoclonus (SSM) is a rare involuntary movement disorder characterized by myoclonic jerks originating from the spinal cord and involving muscles innervated by specific spinal segments [1]. The etiologies of SSM are diverse, including vascular, inflammatory, infectious, postinfectious, demyelinating, degenerative, paraneoplastic, and space-occupying lesions [2]. It has important localizing value and often indicates an underlying spinal cord lesion. We report a rare case of anti-glycine receptor (GlyR) antibody-positive stiff-person spectrum disorder/progressive encephalomyelitis with rigidity and myoclonus (PERM) spectrum in which the patient initially presented with SSM and conus medullaris syndrome, with marked clinical improvement following aggressive immunosuppressive therapy.

A previously healthy 66-year-old man presented to the emergency department with frequent myoclonus and muscle rigidity in both legs for 3 days, rendering him unable to stand or walk. Two weeks before admission, he experienced saddle anesthesia, bladder/bowel dysfunction, and impotence. No eyelid ptosis, diplopia, facial numbness, trismus, dysphagia, or dysarthria were reported. One week after admission, the patient developed dizziness, limited mouth opening, and masticatory muscle spasm. Neurological examination revealed synchronized myoclonus in the lower limbs, occurring spontaneously or provoked by tapping below the T10 level (Supplementary Video 1), with saddle area hypoesthesia, absence of the anal reflex, and slight hypertonia in the legs. His muscle strength was normal, and bilateral Babinski signs were negative.

Laboratory examinations revealed unremarkable results, including complete blood cell counts; liver and renal function; electrolytes; autoimmune panels; and syphilis, HIV, and vitamin B12 levels. Electroencephalography revealed normal background activity without epileptiform discharges. Electromyography revealed no evidence of peripheral nerve injury in the extremities. Brain and spine MRI revealed a T2-hyperintense conus medullaris lesion (Figure 1). Cerebrospinal fluid analysis revealed a normal white blood cell count (2 cells/μL), protein level (25 mg/dL), and negative oligoclonal bands. Tests for myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein and aquaporin-4 antibodies were negative. Antibodies associated with stiff person syndrome spectrum disorders (GAD65, GAD67, GlyR, GABAaR, DPPX, amphiphysin, and ganglionic AchR) revealed the presence of GlyR antibodies in both serum (titer, 1:100) and cerebrospinal fluid (titer, 1:100) using a cell-based assay.

Sagittal T2-weighted MRI showed T2 hyperintensity at the conus medullaris (A), and axial T2-weighted MRI revealed that the lesion is diffuse and symmetrical (B).

The patient was diagnosed with GlyR antibody-associated stiff-person spectrum disorder/PERM spectrum. The bilateral leg myoclonus was considered an SSM, with the conus medullaris lesion as a possible generator. He received oral clonazepam (1 mg three times daily) and baclofen (10 mg three times daily) for symptomatic treatment; however, no significant clinical improvement was observed. After high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1 g daily for 5 days), the dose was gradually tapered, and marked clinical improvement was observed. After 14 days of treatment, he was able to walk independently without myoclonic episodes (Supplementary Video 1). He was discharged on maintenance oral prednisone (20 mg daily) combined with mycophenolate mofetil (0.5 g twice daily). At the 6-month follow-up, serum GlyR antibodies were negative.

GlyR antibody-associated stiff-person spectrum disorder/PERM spectrum was first described in 2008 [3]. It commonly presents with muscle rigidity, brainstem signs, trismus, myoclonus, spasms, or hyperekplexia, with SSM being a rarely reported symptom [4]. It is a rarely reported case of SSM caused by a conus medullaris lesion in the disease. SSM is an uncommon movement disorder characterized by involuntary, brief, rhythmic muscle contractions confined to a limited spinal cord region and is often indicative of an underlying spinal cord lesion, with nearly all categories of spinal cord pathology reported. The pathophysiology of SSM is unclear but may involve loss of local inhibitory interneurons, hyperexcitability of anterior horn neurons, aberrant axonal re-excitation, and reduced suprasegmental inhibition [5]. In this patient, SSM may have resulted from dysfunction of segmental spinal cord circuitry due to disruption of inhibitory glycinergic transmission by GlyR antibodies, which mediate fast inhibitory neurotransmission in the spinal cord and brainstem [6]. Neurologists should consider underlying spinal cord lesions in patients who present with SSM. Management focuses on treating the underlying etiology when identified, while symptomatic therapies include clonazepam, valproic acid, and levetiracetam; botulinum toxin injections may benefit selected cases [7]. This case highlights GlyR antibody-associated stiff-person spectrum disorder/PERM spectrum as a potential and potentially reversible cause of SSM with appropriate immunosuppressive treatment.
