Berberine ameliorates acute hyperglycemia and hypoinsulinemia induced by ketamine/xylazine in rats: role of alpha2 adrenergic receptors, oxidative stress and inflammatory suppressing mechanisms
Authors: Rusul Abdulhameed Kadhim, Amir Erfanparast, Esmaeal Tamaddonfard, Davoud Amirkashani, Mehdi Imani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundXylazine is a common veterinary drug used for sedation, anesthesia, muscle relaxation and analgesia. Berberine (C20H18NO4+) is an alkaloid compound found in different plant species with a wide range of biological and pharmacological activities. To date, no studies have examined the effects of berberine on acute hyperglycemia. Therefore, the present study was designed to investigate the effects of berberine on acute hyperglycemia and low insulin levels caused by ketamine/xylazine (K/X) administration. To clarify the involved mechanism, yohimbine (C21H26N2O3, an α2-adrenergic receptor antagonist) was used. To further elucidate the underlying mechanisms, insulin concentration, SOD activity, TAC, MDA, TNF-α, and IL-1β levels in serum were also determined.MethodsBerberine (1.25, 5, and 20 mg/kg) and yohimbine (0.5 and 2 mg/kg) were injected intraperitoneally (IP) 25 and 20 min before acute hyperglycemia induction, respectively. IP administration of a cocktail of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) caused acute hyperglycemia. The tail blood glucose levels and their average (30–120 min) were measured. After the last blood glucose level measurement, blood samples were taken, and the serum insulin concentration, total antioxidant capacity (TAC), superoxide dismutase (SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) levels were evaluated.ResultsK/X significantly (P < 0.05) increased blood glucose at 30, 60, 90, and 120 min, decreased insulin levels, and induced oxidative stress, which was accompanied by an increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines. Berberine (5 and 20 mg/kg) and yohimbine (2 mg/kg) significantly reduced blood glucose and increased insulin levels. Berberine (5 and 20 mg/kg) treatment also improved the decreased TAC content and SOD activity and restored the increased serum levels of MDA, TNF-α and IL-1β. Furthermore, coadministration of ineffective doses of berberine (1.25 mg/kg) and yohimbine (0.5 mg/kg) significantly prevented hyperglycemia and hypoinsulinemia. The synergic effect between berberine and yohimbine was observed.ConclusionOverall, berberine helps counteract K/X-induced hyperglycemia and hypoinsulinemia. Anti-hyperglycemic synergy between berberine and yohimbine suggests that berberine works partly by blocking α2 adrenergic receptors. In addition, oxidative stress and pro-inlammatory mediators reduction and anti-oxidative activity elevation might be involved in these metabolic effects of berberine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05017-y.

Introduction

Xylazine is a common veterinary drug used for sedation in cattle, sheep, goats, horses, cats and primates. It may also produce mild to moderate analgesia in most animals [1, 2]. On the other hand, xylazine and ketamine can be recommended as anesthetic combinations in companion rabbits, and particularly in rodents, owing to their adequate analgesic effect, deep sedation and duration of surgical anesthesia [2, 3]. Xylazine use, however, is associated with a few unfavorable side effects. For example, xylazine has been found to produce severe hypoxia in sheep [4]. Additionally, xylazine has been reported to cause a pronounced increase in the serum glucose concentration [2, 5].

Acute hyperglycemia is characterized by a transient increase in plasma glucose levels. It can occur due to various factors, such as stress, physical trauma, certain medications, infections, sepsis or hormonal disorders [6, 7]. In nondiabetic individuals, it is often referred to as “stress hyperglycemia” and it can be observed in acute care settings, where it is associated with poor outcomes in conditions such as myocardial infarction and stroke [6].

Berberine (C20H18NO4+) is an alkaloid present in different plant species, including barberry (Berberis vulgaris L.). The chemical structure of berberine is shown in Fig. 1 [8]. Berberine has a wide range of biological and pharmacological activities, making it a promising compound for therapeutic applications. Antimicrobial, antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, hepatoprotective and cholesterol-lowering effects of berberine have been reported [9, 10]. Additionally, berberine exhibits significant antidiabetic effects through multiple mechanisms [11]. In this regard, berberine increases insulin receptor expression and enhances insulin signaling in human cell lines [12]. In addition, berberine ameliorates oxidative stress, inflammation and apoptosis in diabetes through several signaling pathways, including nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), AMP-activated protein kinases (AMPK) and nuclear factor erythroid-2 related factor 2 (Nrf2) [13, 14]. On the other hand, in streptozotocin- and high-carbohydrate/high-fat diet-induced diabetic rats, berberine may provide therapeutic effects by preserving β-cell mass and improving insulin secretion [15]. These findings suggest that berberine may offer therapeutic benefits in diabetes by targeting oxidative stress, inflammation, β-cell preservation, and glucose utilization. However, although the effects of berberine on diabetes have been extensively studied, its impact on acute hyperglycemia has not been examined to date.

Ketamine/xylazine (K/X)-induced hyperglycemia is a well-documented phenomenon in various animal species, particularly in rodents. Although the exact molecular mechanism is not fully clear, K/X-induced hyperglycemia is primarily attributed to suppression of insulin secretion via α2-adrenergic receptor signaling, peripheral insulin resistance, and the possible role of other regulatory hormones [5, 16]. Additionally, the hyperglycemic response involves modulation of glucoregulatory hormones, including decreased insulin, ACTH, and corticosterone, and increased glucagon and growth hormone levels [5]. The α2-adrenergic receptor stimulation reduces tissue insulin sensitivity and glucose uptake [16]. Among the α-adrenoceptor subtypes (A, B, and C), only α2A- and α2C-adrenoceptor mRNAs were detected in β-cells of rat islets and it is suggested that the inhibition of insulin secretion occurs via the α2A-adrenoceptor subtype [17]. The activation of these receptors was associated with inhibition of cAMP formation and hyperpolarization of β-cells [17]. The ADRA2A gene, which encodes the α2A-adrenoceptor, is potentially a valuable lead for diabetes therapy because it can be manipulated pharmacologically [17]. The blood glucose-increasing effect of xylazine (an α2 adrenergic receptor agonist) was inhibited by the previous injection of yohimbine (an α2 adrenergic receptor antagonist) [18, 19].

Various drugs commonly used to sedate, tranquilize, or anesthetize rodents, such as α2 receptor agonists, can significantly influence their physiological status and alter hematological and biochemical parameters [3, 20]. The present study was designed to demonstrate the effect of berberine on acute hyperglycemia caused by K/X administration. To clarify the mechanism involved, yohimbine was used. At the end of each experiment, blood samples were collected to determine the superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, malondialdehyde (MDA), and total antioxidant capacity (TAC) levels in serum. Additionally, pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) levels, were assessed. The necessity of this research is based on the premise that if berberine causes a change in K/X-induced acute hyperglycemia, at least its mechanism related to the adrenergic system or decreasing oxidative system and pro-inflammatory mediators could be clarified.

Materials and methods

Animals

Adult Wistar rats (260–300 g) were supplied by the rat house of the Laboratory of Physiology, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Urmia University. In addition, because hormonal fluctuations during the estrous cycle may influence glucose metabolism and insulin dynamics, only male animals were used in this study [21, 22]. Animals were housed under standard conditions (22 ± 0.5 °C; 12–12 h dark‒light cycle) with free access to food and water. All the experimental procedures were conducted between 9:00 AM and 2:00 PM. This study was designed and reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines [23] and the Veterinary Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine at Urmia University.

Chemicals

Chemicals, including berberine chloride hydrate and yohimbine (an antagonist of an α2 adrenergic receptor), were purchased from Sigma‒Aldrich Chemical Co., St. Louis, MO, USA. Ketamine (10%, Alfasan, The Netherlands) and xylazine (2%, Alfasan, The Netherlands) were purchased from native veterinary pharmacies. Chemical solutions were freshly prepared 15 min before intraperitoneal (IP) administration.

Acute hyperglycemia induction

Acute hyperglycemia was induced using a previously described method [5, 18], by the IP injection of a combination of ketamine and xylazine (100 and 10 mg/kg body weight, respectively). Thirty minutes and immediately before injection and at 30, 60, and 120 min after K/X injection, blood glucose was measured with a glucose meter (Freesense, Freertab, Iran). For blood collection, the tip of the animal’s tail was pricked with a 30-gauge needle, the blood sample (20 µL) was placed on the device strip twice, and the average number obtained was recorded. The accuracy of the glucometer was evaluated via a standard glucose quantification kit (Pars Azmoun, Iran).

Assessment of biochemical parameters and insulin

The serum levels of total antioxidant capacity (TAC), malondialdehyde (MDA), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity were quantified via commercially available assay kits (Arsam Fara Zist Kit; Arsam Fara Zist Co., Urmia, Iran).

The TAC levels were measured based on the ABTS method. In this method, TAC was determined using the ABTS [2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)] radical cation decolorization assay. Antioxidants in the sample reduce the blueish-green ABTS⁺ radical to a colorless form, and the decrease in absorbance is measured spectrophotometrically at 414 nm. The results are expressed as nmol/mL [25].

The SOD activity was assayed based on its capacity to inhibit the autoxidation of pyrogallol in an alkaline environment. The enzyme activity, inversely proportional to the formation of pyrogallol-quinone measured at 325 nm, is reported as U/L [26].

The MDA levels, which reflect lipid peroxidation, were assessed via the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) method. The MDA-TBA adduct formed was quantified colorimetrically at 532 nm. The results are expressed as nmol/mL [27].

Serum contents of TNF-α and IL-1β were measured by ELISA according to the kit instructions (Bioscience, Santa Clara, USA). The TNF-α and IL-1β contents of serum were expressed as pg/mL.

Serum insulin concentrations were measured via a species-specific enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Rat Insulin ELISA Kit, Mercodia, Sweden) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Insulin levels are reported as µg/L.

Statistical analysis

Graph Pad Prism 8.2.1 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was used to analyze the obtained data. The time-point-related results were analyzed via two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (analysis of variance) and then with Bonferroni correction. The results obtained from the average blood glucose and insulin levels and biochemical parameters were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test. All the data are presented as the means ± SEMs. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all the results.

Results

As shown in Figs. 3a and 4a, and 5a, blood glucose levels at 30 min before normal saline or K/X injection did not differ between the study groups. In the vehicle group, there were no significant changes before or after IP injection of normal saline at the measured times (P > 0.05). In the K/X-treated group, blood glucose levels increased significantly (P < 0.001) at 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after injection. In addition, the average blood glucose levels (30–120 min) of the normal saline- and K/X-treated groups were significantly different (P < 0.001) from those of the other groups (Figs. 3b and 4b, and 5b). On the other hand, as shown in Figs. 3c and 4c, and 5c, the serum insulin levels were significantly (P < 0.001) lower in the K/X mixture-treated group than in the normal saline-treated group.

IP injection of berberine (1.25 mg/kg) had no significant effect on K/X-induced hyperglycemia at 30, 60, 90, or 120 min. Berberine (5 and 20 mg/kg) significantly reduced K/X-induced hyperglycemia (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01, respectively) at 30, 60, 90, and 120 min (Fig. 3a). The average blood glucose levels (30–120 min) also confirmed the significant reduction (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01, respectively) in K/X-induced hyperglycemia caused by berberine at doses of 5 and 10 mg/kg (Fig. 3b). On the other hand, berberine at doses of 5 and 20 mg/kg significantly inhibited the decrease in serum insulin levels caused by K/X (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01, respectively) (Fig. 3c).

Discussion

In this study, blood glucose levels and serum insulin levels were significantly greater and lower, respectively, after the IP injection of K/X than before the injection and in the normal saline-treated group. Ketamine is an antagonist of NMDA glutamate receptors that is used for the induction and maintenance of anesthesia, pain management and psychiatric disorders [28]. Xylazine, an α2 adrenergic receptor agonist, is used in animals for its sedative and muscle relaxant effects in combination with anesthetics such as ketamine and isoflurane [2, 29]. Xylazine, alone or in combination with ketamine, has been reported to increase plasma glucose concentrations and decrease insulin levels in dogs, cattle, monkeys, and rats [5, 16, 18]. The pancreas is extensively innervated by the sympathetic system, and electrical stimulation of these nerves significantly inhibits insulin release [30]. In this context, it has been suggested that α2 adrenergic receptors are postsynaptic in pancreatic beta cells and that their activation reduces insulin release and consequently increases blood glucose levels [17, 30, 31]. However, the results of this study are consistent with those of other researchers, in which xylazine was able to increase blood glucose levels.

In this study, berberine inhibited hyperglycemia and hypoinsulinemia induced by K/X. The findings that berberine can inhibit both hyperglycemia and hypoinsulinemia induced by K/X align with increasing evidence supporting its multifaceted antidiabetic effects. The hypoglycemic properties of berberine have been demonstrated in various diabetic experimental models, including those caused by high-fat diets and streptozotocin [15, 32]. In addition, berberine can lower blood insulin levels by enhancing insulin sensitivity [11]. The ability of berberine to counteract xylazine-induced hyperglycemia suggests that its action is not limited to a single diabetic pathway but may involve broader mechanisms of glucose homeostasis.

In our study, K/X-induced hyperglycemia and hypoinsulinemia were inhibited by prior administration of yohimbine. Yohimbine is a selective adrenergic antagonist that has a broad spectrum of pharmacological activities [33]. Various studies have reported that peripheral and intracerebroventricular injections of yohimbine can inhibit xylazine-induced hyperglycemia [5, 18, 34]. It has also been reported that in dogs, hyperglycemia and hypoinsulinemia induced by intravenous injection of dexmedetomidine, an α2 adrenergic receptor agonist, are inhibited by the α2 antagonist MK-467 [35].

In our study, coadministration of berberine and yohimbine at low doses prevented K/X-induced hyperglycemia compared with their separate administration at the same doses. In other words, a synergistic effect was observed between berberine and yohimbine. Berberine interacts with human platelet α2 adrenergic receptors in a complex manner, exhibiting both agonist and antagonist properties depending on the biological context. For example, both berebrine and yohimbine suppressed the inhibitory effect of clonidine, an α2 adrenergic partial agonist, on electrical stimulation-evoked contraction of the prostatic portion of the rat vas de deferens [36]. In addition, berberine was found to competitively inhibit the specific binding of [3 H]- yohimbine and the displacement curve was parallel to those of clonidine, epinephrine, and norepinephrine [37]. On the other hand, in the presence of L-epinephrine, berberine blocked the inhibitory effect of L-epinephrine, which behaves as an α2 adrenoceptor antagonist [37]. These properties are similar to those of clonidine on human platelets, suggesting that berberine is a partial agonist of platelet adrenoceptors. However, more studies are needed in this area.

In the present study, K/X administration induced acute hyperglycemia accompanied by oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, as evidenced by increased serum MDA, TNF-α, IL-1β and reduced antioxidant enzyme activity (SOD, TAC). The aim of assessing MDA, SOD, and TAC in clinical and research settings is to evaluate oxidative stress status and antioxidant defense capacity in various diseases and metabolic conditions [38, 39]. Furthermore, TNF-α and IL-1β are pro-inflammatory cytokines, which have gained much attention in inflammatory situations [40]. Our observations align with a previous report indicating that administration of ketamine in combination with xylazine and acepromazine resulted in a rise in TNF-α levels within 30 min, and peaked at 2 h, followed by a rapid decline [41]. On the other hand, the administration of K/X induced acute hyperglycemia may indirectly contribute to an increase in oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory markers by disrupting glucose metabolism and insulin signaling. Elevated blood glucose can aggravate inflammation and the oxidative stress response, as evidenced by elevated circulating nitrotyrosine (a marker of oxidative stress) levels in normal individuals, and an acute increase in inflammatory cytokine concentrations [42, 43]. Experimental models have demonstrated that short-term hyperglycemia induced by glucose infusion can provoke oxidative stress in the liver and trigger inflammatory responses [44]. Human studies showed that plasma levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β rose significantly within 2 h of induced hyperglycemia in both normal and impaired glucose tolerance subjects [43]. These findings suggest that acute hyperglycemia can rapidly trigger oxidative and inflammatory responses, contributing to a potential tissue injury even in nondiabetic conditions. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of monitoring and managing the oxidative balance in acute hyperglycemia to prevent potential metabolic and cellular damage.

Our results showed that berberine administration ameliorated biochemical alterations, decreased the MDA content and restored the SOD and TAC levels. Additionally, berberine alleviated the increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β) caused by K/X administration. These findings support the known anti-oxidative and anti-inflammatory properties of berberine, which include the ability to scavenge free radicals and increase the activity of endogenous antioxidant enzymes. The protective effect observed here aligns with previous research on the efficacy of berberine in reducing oxidative damage and inflammation [8, 45]. Studies of diabetic animals and cells cultured with high glucose-containing media have shown that berberine inhibits oxidative stress [46, 47]. The antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms of berberine are complex, involving multiple cellular kinases and signaling pathways, such as AMPK, MAPKs, Nrf2, and NF-κB. In addition, berberine directly scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) and stabilizes mitochondrial function [48].

Notably, the protective effects of berberine against K/X-induced hyperglycemia, oxidative stress, and inflammation could be further investigated in larger animal models. This research is particularly relevant because acute hyperglycemia poses significant clinical challenges, particularly in emergency and hospital settings [6]. Such studies could provide valuable translational insights into the therapeutic potential of berberine for managing acute hyperglycemia and its associated oxidative and inflammatory complications.

Finally, this study has several limitations. First, only male animals were used, limiting generalizability to females. Second, mechanistic interpretations rely on indirect evidence. In addition, selective antagonists were not used, restricting insight into site-specific mechanisms. Therefore, future studies should include both sexes, consider estrous cycle effects, directly examine the Nrf2 pathway and α2A involvement, and use selective antagonists to enhance mechanistic and translational relevance.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that the IP administration of K/X induces significant hyperglycemia, primarily through the activation of α₂-adrenergic receptors, which suppress insulin secretion. Our research revealed that berberine effectively inhibits both hyperglycemia and hypoinsulinemia induced by K/X, and the combination of berberine and yohimbine at low doses has a synergistic effect, offering increased protection against xylazine-induced glucose dysregulation. On the other hand, K/X-induced acute hyperglycemia contributes to increased oxidative stress, as evidenced by elevated MDA levels and decreased SOD activity and TAC. However, further research is necessary to elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying the observed effects of berberine and to explore the broader clinical implications of these findings.
