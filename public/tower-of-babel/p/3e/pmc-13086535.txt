Epidemiology, Management, and Alarmingly High Mortality of Burn Patients: A Retrospective Analysis From a Resource‐Limited Tertiary Centre in Syria
Authors: Lorieh Domat, Adel Azar, Ahmad Alkheder, Diana Mohammad, Jean Domat, Hanady Zwaraa, Rawnak Almidani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBurn injuries pose a significant challenge in resource‐limited settings, particularly during humanitarian crises. This study examines the epidemiology, management, and outcomes of burn patients at a tertiary hospital in Damascus, Syria, from 2021 to 2024. A single‐centre retrospective cohort study was conducted on 500 admitted burn patients. Data on demographics, injury characteristics, management, complications and mortality were analysed using descriptive statistics, chi‐square tests, Mann–Whitney U tests and binary logistic regression. The lethal area 50% (LA50) was estimated using the logistic regression equation: LA50=−constant+∑bixibTBSA. The mean patient age was 20.5 ± 17.9 years, with a male predominance (55%). Flame burns (72.4%) at home (87.6%) were most common. The mean total body surface area burned was 30.99% ± 21.31%, with 68.6% being full‐thickness injuries. The complication rate was 91%, with infection being most prevalent. The overall mortality rate was 39%, with sepsis (56.4%) as the leading cause. Logistic regression identified larger burn area (OR = 1.152, p < 0.001), full‐thickness depth (ref. partial thickness, OR = 0.281, p < 0.001), older age and female gender as independent predictors of increased mortality. Deceased patients had significantly shorter hospital stays. LA50 was 42.63% for a 40‐year‐old male with a partial‐thickness flame burn and a 10‐day hospital stay; for a female with a full‐thickness flame burn under the same conditions, it was 27.43%. This study reveals an alarmingly high burden of burn injuries in a crisis‐affected setting, with extreme complication and mortality rates. The findings highlight the critical impact of extensive burns and sepsis, underscoring severe systemic constraints on care. Urgent context‐specific interventions to strengthen acute burn management, infection control, and prevention strategies are imperative.Keywords: burn care, burns, humanitarian crisis, mortality, risk factors, Syria

Introduction

Burns remain a formidable public health issue worldwide, exacting a heavy toll in terms of morbidity, mortality, and economic strain, especially in low‐ and middle‐income countries where over 90% of burn‐related deaths occur [1]. Globally, around 9 million burn incidents severe enough for medical care occurred in 2019, leading to 111 000 fatalities and 7.5 million disability‐adjusted life years, with welfare losses of about $112 billion or 0.09% of gross domestic product [1]. In Europe, the incidence of severe burns ranges from 0.2 to 2.9 per 10 000 people, with a downward trend; flames, scalds, and contact burns are the primary causes, mostly affecting males (60%), with nearly half the cases involving those under 16 years [2]. In low‐ and middle‐income countries, burns predominantly happen in domestic settings (43%–79%), with hot liquids and flames as leading causes, though occupational burns are notable in Dhaka and Kathmandu (32% and 43%); intentional injuries account for up to 32% in Nairobi [3]. Prevention efforts in these regions, through education, environmental changes or combined strategies, have shown promise in boosting safety knowledge, yet few have directly measured reduced burn rates, underscoring the need for robust evaluations [4]. In the Middle East, including Saudi Arabia, epidemiological patterns reveal scalds as the most frequent mechanism in paediatric cases (54%), with 59.2% involving total body surface area under 10%; factors like female gender, larger burn area and extended hospital stays heighten risks of morbidity and death [5]. A systematic review of burn injuries in Saudi Arabia confirms these trends, noting higher vulnerability among children and the importance of tailored interventions [6]. In Ethiopia, 40.9% of burn patients were discharged with complications, linked to pre‐hospital interventions, scald aetiology, and lack of fluid resuscitation, highlighting how resource limitations amplify poor outcomes [7]. A meta‐analysis of paediatric burns identifies key mortality risks: larger total body surface area, age under 4 years, female gender, flame burns, inhalation injury, mechanical ventilation need, rural residence, non‐accidental burns, and prolonged hospital stays [8]. In Syria, amid a decade‐long crisis crippling healthcare, a retrospective study at Al‐Mouwasat University Hospital from 2017 to 2021 found flames the chief cause (61.3%), with children comprising 58.8% of 641 cases; mortality was tied to high abbreviated burn severity index scores and complications occurred in 32.6% [9]. Extending this work to 2021–2024 at the same facility enables examination of potential shifts in injury factors, management, complications, mortality predictors, and causes of death among 500 patients, to guide context‐specific improvements in burn care.

Methods

Study Design

A single‐centre, retrospective cohort study was conducted at Al‐Mouwasat University Hospital in Damascus, Syria. The study period spanned from October 2021 to October 2024. The primary objectives were to identify factors associated with burn injuries, describe management strategies, evaluate complications, assess mortality rates, determine causes of death and analyse predictors of mortality among burn patients.

Ethical Consideration

The study protocol received approval from the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (BMREC) at Damascus University (MD‐261125‐565). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the research.

Patient Selection

A comprehensive review of the hospital archives identified 555 potential patient records. The inclusion criteria encompassed patients of all ages who were admitted with burn injuries. Specifically, we included: adults with > 15% Total Body Surface Area (TBSA) burned; children and elderly patients (> 60 years) with > 10% TBSA; infants with > 5% TBSA; burns involving special areas (face, hands, feet, genitalia) regardless of size; and all cases of electrical, chemical, inhalation, or circumferential burns.

Exclusion criteria were defined as: (1) patients who did not require hospitalisation and (2) patients transferred from other hospitals or centres who were not admitted to our facility directly from the time of injury.

After applying these criteria, 55 records were excluded due to failure to meet inclusion standards or insufficient data. Consequently, the final analytical cohort consisted of 500 patients.

Statistical Analysis

Calculations were performed using SPSS version 26. Categorical variables were summarised with counts and percentages, particularly for complications and mortality, using Pearson's Chi‐Square and Fisher's Exact tests (for cells with expected counts less than 5 or when 20%–25% of cells had low expected frequencies) to assess associations. Percentages were calculated for columns. Non‐normally distributed data, such as age, burn area and hospital stay, were compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. Binary logistic regression was employed to identify predictors of mortality.

The lethal area 50% (LA50) was estimated using the logistic regression equation: logit(p) = constant + b1x1 + b2x2 + … + bixi, where p represents the probability of death, and x1, x2, …, xi are the explanatory variables. Comorbidities and Past Surgical History

Conditions such as asthma, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, heart abnormalities, VSD, hypothyroidism, and schizophrenia were recorded at low frequencies (ranging from 0.4% to 2.8%) and showed no significant association with complications or mortality, except for diabetes mellitus.

Past surgical histories included cholecystectomy (0.2%), caesarean sections (0.6%), hysterectomy (0.2%) and hernia repairs (0.4%).

The majority of patients were under 5 years old. The incidence of complications was highest in the 11–20 and > 60 age groups, with a statistically significant relationship (p < 0.05). Injury Location and Cause

Most burns occurred at home, with the highest complication rates observed in burns resulting from traffic accidents or street injuries, but this was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Mortality was highest in burns sustained at home, followed by those from workplace and street injuries ( p < 0.05), Table 3. This trend may be attributed to increased exposure among vulnerable populations (children or elderly), more severe mechanisms of domestic burns such as flames in enclosed spaces, or delays in initial management. Conversely, work‐related injuries appeared to be relatively protective, being associated with lower complication and mortality rates.

Injury Distribution

Injury locations included head (332, 66.4%), trunk (343, 68.6%), upper limbs (406, 81.2%), lower limbs (363, 72.6%), genital region (158, 31.6%) and multiple sites (465, 93%). The most affected areas were the upper extremities (81.2%) and lower extremities (72.6%).

Multiple‐site injuries significantly increased complication rates. Mortality was higher with multiple injuries but was not statistically significant.

Complications and mortality rates tend to rise as the extent of burn distribution widens, involving multiple anatomical sites, Table 6. Burns affecting the trunk, lower limbs, and genital area are particularly strong indicators of adverse outcomes, probably because they involve larger surface areas and are more prone to deep tissue damage. While burns limited to the upper limbs may not independently predict poor prognosis, they can contribute to increased mortality when part of a more extensive burn injury. Additionally, head burns may elevate the risk of death due to potential airway compromise or proximity to critical structures.

Procedures and Antibiotics

Out of 500 patients, 228 (45.6%) did not require surgery, while 272 (54.4%) underwent procedures such as escharotomy (180, 36.0%), debridement with skin grafting (146, 29.2%), fasciotomy (16, 3.2%) and amputation (14, 2.8%). Surgical management was important in patients with complications.

Topical antibiotics included fusidic acid and sulfadiazine; some patients received nystatin, while polymyxins and metronidazole were rarely used. The use of sulfadiazine is associated with higher patients without complications and more survivors (Table 8). Microbiology

Bacterial cultures were conducted in 73 cases (14.6%), with all results being positive. The most frequently isolated pathogens included Klebsiella (30, 41.1%), Pseudomonas (21, 28.8%) and Enterobacter (14, 19.2%). Most cultures detected a single bacterium (79.5%), with some cases showing two (19.2%) or three (1.4%) organisms.

Outcomes and Final Management

Good outcomes were observed in 160 patients (32%); moderate outcomes (deformities or retractions) in 131 (26.2%); and poor outcomes (suffered necrosis and required amputation or died) in 209 (41.8%).

All results were favourable when no complications arose. However, among complication cases, 209 (45.9%) had poor outcomes, including mortality, amputation, or necrosis; 115 (25.3%) had good outcomes and 131 (28.8%) experienced moderate results such as malformations or retractions.

In survived cases, 14 (4.6%) had poor outcomes, 160 (52.5%) good and 131 (43.0%) moderate.

Conclusion

This study involved 500 patients (55% male) with a mean age of 20.5 ± 17.9 years. The majority of burns were caused by flames (72.4%) and occurred at home (87.6%). The mean burn surface area was 30.99% ± 21.31%, with 68.6% involving full‐thickness burns. Multiple body areas were affected in 93% of cases. Over half of the patients (54.4%) required surgical intervention, and 88.2% received systemic antibiotics. The mean hospital stay was 12.24 days. Complications were observed in 91%, with sepsis being the most common complication (88.8%), and the overall mortality rate was 39%. Final outcomes were favourable in 32%, moderate in 26.2%, and poor in 41.8%.

Binary logistic regression was employed to evaluate the associations between mortality and variables including age, gender, burn area and depth, hospital stay and flame. Inspection of standardised residuals identified five outliers (standardised residuals > 2.5), which were subsequently removed from the analysis. The final model was statistically significant, χ 2 (7, n = 495) = 361.197, p < 0.001, and explained between 51.8% (Cox & Snell R Square) and 70.4% (Nagelkerke R Square) of the variance in mortality. The model correctly classified 87.5% of cases.

Regarding the predictors, the odds of mortality increased by 1.015 times with each additional year of age. Conversely, being male was associated with 0.411 times lower odds of mortality compared to females. An increase in burn area was associated with 1.152 times higher odds of death, while a decrease in hospital stay was linked to a 0.939 times reduction in mortality risk. Additionally, a decrease in flame injury was associated with a 0.32 times lower likelihood of mortality. Furthermore, partial thickness burns were 0.281 times less likely to result in mortality compared to full‐thickness burns (Table 11).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study provides a critical analysis of burn injury epidemiology, management, and outcomes at a single tertiary centre in Damascus, Syria, between 2021 and 2024—a period marked by a protracted humanitarian crisis. The findings depict a stark and severe burden of disease, characterised by an exceptionally high complication rate of 91% and a mortality rate of 39%. These figures stand in sobering contrast to reports from more stable settings and underscore the profound impact of systemic healthcare degradation on burn care [2, 7, 11]. The study delineates key demographic, injury‐related and management factors influencing outcomes, confirming universal themes in burn pathophysiology while highlighting unique, crisis‐exacerbated vulnerabilities that demand context‐specific interventions.

The demographic and etiological profile of our cohort aligns with, yet intensifies, patterns observed in many low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs). The overwhelming predominance of domestic injuries (87.6%) and flame burns (72.4%) is consistent with reports from other resource‐constrained regions [3, 6, 12]. This pattern reflects hazardous domestic environments where unsafe cooking, heating, the use of unrefined fuel, testing gas cylinders with a lighter instead of using soap foam, and lighting with open flames are prevalent, a reality exacerbated by overcrowding and poverty, and acutely persistent in conflict settings. The clinical severity of the cohort was substantial, with a mean Total Body Surface Area (TBSA) burned of 30.99% and a high prevalence of full‐thickness (68.6%) and multi‐site (93%) involvement. As robustly demonstrated in global burn literature and reaffirmed here, both the extent and depth of the burn were the most powerful correlates of adverse outcomes [2, 7, 8, 11, 13, 14]. The mean burn area in non‐survivors (48.93%) was more than double that of survivors (19.51%), a dose‐dependent relationship that forms a cornerstone of burn prognosis.

Our multivariate analysis quantified the independent predictors of mortality, yielding critical insights. The increased odds of death with larger TBSA (OR = 1.152) and full‐thickness depth are universally acknowledged physiological truths [2, 8, 13]. The elevated risk associated with advancing age (OR = 1.015 per year) aligns with the well‐established frailty and reduced physiological reserve in older patients [11, 13, 15]. However, several findings warrant deeper contextual scrutiny. The pronounced and statistically significant female gender disparity in mortality is a concerning and consistent finding across our analyses. While biological differences may play a role, this disparity likely points to intersecting socio‐cultural and crisis‐related vulnerabilities, such as gender‐specific domestic exposures, delays in care‐seeking behaviour, or differential access to resources within households—a trend noted in other regional studies [5, 8, 16]. This necessitates urgent qualitative investigation to inform equitable care strategies.

The estimated LA50 in our cohort further contextualises the gravity of our findings. Using the derived logistic regression model, the LA50 for a 40‐year‐old male with a partial‐thickness flame burn and a 10‐day hospital stay was 42.63% TBSA. This value is considerably lower than the LA50 reported in many high‐resource settings, where mortality often reaches 50% only at burn areas exceeding 60%–70% TBSA [17]. The lower LA50 in our population reflects not only the severity of injuries but also the limited capacity for aggressive resuscitation, early surgical intervention, and infection control—a reality imposed by years of healthcare system strain. Notably, for a female patient with a full‐thickness burn under the same assumptions, the LA50 dropped to 27.43% TBSA, underscoring the heightened vulnerability of female patients in our setting and reinforcing the gender disparity observed in our multivariate analysis. These LA50 estimates serve as a practical tool for risk stratification and highlight the urgent need for targeted improvements in early burn care and resource allocation.

The potent risk associated with flame aetiology, and the counterintuitive strong association of wet burns (scalds) with mortality in one model, illuminate specific injury mechanisms in this setting. Flame burns, often involving flammable liquids in confined spaces, typically cause deeper and more extensive damage, explaining their strong predictive power [12]. The high mortality from scalds, more commonly associated with better outcomes in adults, may indicate specific high‐risk scenarios in our population, such as large‐volume scalds in vulnerable groups (children, elderly) or co‐existing injuries in complex domestic accidents. It may also reflect particular challenges in managing scald wound infections in this environment.

The clinical course was overwhelmingly dominated by sepsis, which was the most frequent complication (88.8%) and the leading cause of death (56.4%). This rate is alarmingly high compared to the 2%–14% of burn deaths attributed to sepsis in better‐resourced centres and exceeds even the elevated rates reported from other LMICs [2, 15, 18]. This crisis‐level sepsis burden is a direct consequence of the lethal synergy between extensive wounds, compromised host defenses, and a crippled infection control infrastructure, particularly the lack of isolation rooms in both critical care and general units, along with inconsistent use of protective clothing by healthcare workers and family members. Limitations in early excision and grafting, shortages of topical and systemic antimicrobials, and overcrowded wards inevitably fuel cross‐transmission. The microbiological profile, dominated by Klebsiella and Pseudomonas, is typical of nosocomial gram‐negative infections in burn units and aligns with patterns seen in other settings where antibiotic resistance is a mounting concern [3, 15, 18]. The near‐universal use of broad‐spectrum systemic antibiotics (88.2%), including agents like vancomycin and carbapenems, was a necessary but double‐edged strategy, highlighting the pervasive infection threat while raising serious concerns about antimicrobial stewardship and escalating resistance, especially in children—where paediatricians often initiate broad‐spectrum antibiotic coverage from the first day of admission, particularly when the burn area exceeds 25%.

The management landscape revealed a system adapting to profound scarcity. The high rate of surgical intervention (54.4%), primarily escharotomy and skin grafting, indicates an attempt at definitive management despite constraints. The association of topical sulfadiazine with better outcomes among survivors underscores the vital role of any consistent, accessible topical antimicrobial prophylaxis in the absence of alternatives. A poignant and revealing finding was the inverse relationship between hospital length of stay and mortality, where deceased patients had significantly shorter admissions. This starkly reflects the fulminant course of severe burn shock, early multi‐organ dysfunction, and rapid‐onset septicemia, which claimed lives before prolonged supportive care could be implemented—a phenomenon powerfully indicating gaps in early resuscitation and critical care capacity [1, 16].

Electric burns, although few in number, were mostly high‐voltage, leading to limb amputation due to the difficulty of transporting patients from remote areas within the necessary timeframe for fasciotomy and limb salvage. However, the amputations ultimately saved the lives of many patients suffering from high‐voltage electrical burns.

This highlights the importance of improving access to medical care in remote areas, where timing can be a critical factor in treatment outcomes. It also illustrates the difficult balance between saving lives and making tough surgical decisions, emphasising the need for effective protocols to address such cases in the future.

When contextualised within the broader literature, the extremity of our outcomes becomes clear. The mortality rate of 39% far exceeds the 6.9% reported in a Saudi Arabian systematic review, the 1.35% in a Riyadh paediatric study, the 6.6% from Addis Ababa, and even the 17% average for LMICs reported in a recent WHO‐based analysis [5, 6, 10, 15]. It is, however, comparable to the 33.9% mortality found in the earlier (2017–2021) cohort from the same Syrian hospital, indicating a persistently dire situation over time [9]. This disparity is not merely a function of injury severity but a stark metric of the cascading failures in a healthcare system fragmented by over a decade of conflict: shortages of specialised staff, equipment, and intensive care resources; disruptions in supply chains for essential medications and fluids; and likely, delays in presentation and initial resuscitation. This stark contrast illustrates that burn outcomes are not dictated by injury severity alone. Rather, burn pathophysiology is not a fixed biological constant but is deeply influenced by the environment in which care is delivered. The findings scream for a dual‐pathway response.

First, immediate clinical and operational strengthening is crucial. Winning the battle against sepsis must be the paramount priority, requiring reinforced infection prevention and control protocols, reliable access to basic topical and systemic antimicrobials and strategic antibiotic stewardship. Strengthening early resuscitation capabilities—ensuring adequate fluid and electrolyte replacement—is fundamental, as evidence suggests this can dramatically reduce complications [7]. Second, and equally critical, is a long‐term commitment to prevention, the most cost‐effective and humane strategy. Our data, consistently identifying the home as the epicentre of severe flame injuries, call for targeted, culturally adapted, community‐based prevention programs. Global evidence suggests that successful prevention requires a multi‐pronged approach: educational campaigns on safe cooking and first aid delivered through trusted channels, coupled with environmental modifications like promoting safer stove designs or temperature regulators for water heaters [1, 4]. Legislative measures for product safety, though challenging in crisis, remain an ultimate goal.

Given the predominance of domestic flame injuries, relying solely on educational campaigns may prove inadequate; evidence indicates that combining education with the distribution of safety devices, such as safer energy alternatives, yields superior hazard reduction in low‐resource settings. Moreover, while other developing nations report declining mortality through modernised treatment concepts, our sustained high mortality emphasises how infrastructure collapse specifically negates such clinical advancements [19, 20].

This study has limitations inherent to its retrospective, single‐centre design in a crisis setting, and the absence of a validated, context‐specific severity score also represents an area for future research.

This study illuminates the devastating impact of burn injuries within a complex humanitarian emergency. The extraordinarily high mortality is multi‐factorial, rooted in the severity of injuries (extensive, deep flame burns) and catastrophically exacerbated by systemic constraints on care delivery. The identified risk factors provide a clear triage framework for prioritising the most vulnerable. To break this cycle, concerted efforts are urgently needed: bolstering acute burn care capacity with a focus on infection control and resuscitation; implementing and evaluating locally relevant burn prevention strategies; and establishing prospective surveillance to enable ongoing quality improvement. Investing in burn care and prevention in crisis‐affected areas like Syria is not only a moral imperative but a strategic investment in human welfare and future stability.

Conclusion

This study underscores the severe burden of burn injuries in a crisis‐affected setting, with alarmingly high complication (91%) and mortality (39%) rates. Larger burn area, full‐thickness depth, older age, and female gender were independently associated with increased mortality. The predominance of sepsis as the leading cause of death highlights critical gaps in infection control and resuscitation capacity. These findings call for urgent, context‐specific interventions to strengthen acute burn care and implement effective prevention strategies, emphasising the need for sustainable healthcare support in humanitarian crises.
