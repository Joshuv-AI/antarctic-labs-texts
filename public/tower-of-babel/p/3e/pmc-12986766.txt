Synergy of diagnosis coding between administrative claims and electronic health records of large patient populations across multiple healthcare organizations
Authors: Minqi Christelle Xiong, Harlan Pittell, Christopher Kitchen, Elyse C Lasser, Hadi Kharrazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study evaluates the completeness of diagnostic information captured in electronic health records (EHRs) compared to administrative claims data across multiple U.S. healthcare organizations between 2010 and 2022.Materials and MethodsDeidentified claims and EHR data of ∼17 million patients across 56 health care organizations were linked. For each Clinical Classification Software (CCS) diagnostic group, proportions of patient-years were computed for diagnoses recorded exclusively in administrative claims, in EHR, or in both sources. Agreement metrics were calculated for high‐level CCS diagnostic groups. Trends in the EHR-claims diagnosis coding gap were estimated using linear regression.ResultsThe completeness of diagnosis data captured in the EHR data, as compared to administrative claims, improved ∼10% from 2010 to 2019. Nonetheless, ∼45% of person-diagnosis data were only captured in claims, and were missing in EHRs, across all years. The missingness of diagnosis data in EHRs, as compared to claims, varied across CCS diagnostic groups with some categories being missed more often than others. The EHR-claims gap of diagnostic codes also affected comorbidity measures such as the Charlson Comorbidity Index, which narrowed significantly from 2010 to 2019, but did not meaningfully change from 2020 to 2022.DiscussionCapturing comprehensive diagnosis data is essential for accurate risk adjustment, yet EHR data systematically under-document multimorbidity compared with claims. Improving EHR interoperability can enhance completeness of EHR-derived diagnosis data and potentially narrow the EHR-claims gap of diagnostic codes.ConclusionsEHRs capture significant amounts of diagnostic data, but increased interoperability of EHRs and integration of claims feeds are essential to achieving comprehensive risk stratification capability.

Introduction

Electronic Health Records (EHRs) have experienced rapid growth and widespread implementation in the U.S., with adoption rates reaching 88% among office-based physicians and 96% among hospitals in 2021.1,2 EHRs have enabled extensive capture of diverse data, including diagnoses at point of care, administrative and billing data, and medical history.3 From clinical, administrative, and research perspectives, usage of the EHR information have supported various applications, such as clinical decision support,4,5 patient risk stratification,6–8 and population health surveillance.9,10

Value-based care has been incentivized by policies that promote improving patient outcomes while reducing costs among healthcare systems.11,12 To achieve these goals, value-based models have increasingly tied reimbursement to risk stratification and quality measurement.6,13,14 These risk stratification efforts traditionally rely on comprehensive administrative claims to identify patients at high risk of utilization.15,16 While claims data remain the reference standard for risk stratification and comorbidity ascertainment, their adjudication delays and siloed availability limit the timeliness and clinical integration of claims data for healthcare providers.17,18 Consequently, the expanding adoption and integration of EHRs across health systems is now considered a viable substitute for claims data to play a key role in value-based efforts such as risk adjustment.14,19

Diagnostic information is commonly used to estimate comorbidity levels of patient populations and calculate risk of utilization outcomes.20,21 Diagnosis data are also routinely collected and captured in EHRs during health care visits, making EHR data repositories a suitable data source to measure comorbidity and utilization risk scores.6,8 However, despite this potential usage, EHR diagnostic data quality could be undermined by several factors.3,22–24 Most importantly, EHRs are often limited to data captured locally within a health system, and often miss information captured in other health systems due to the lack of interoperability among EHRs.24,25 Additionally, limited integration of health systems with health information exchange (HIE) organizations exacerbates the missingness of diagnostic codes captured elsewhere.26 Accordingly, clinicians and health system analysts who rely solely on EHRs may overlook important diagnoses or comorbidities that occur outside their institutions thus limiting the use of EHRs for value-based risk adjustment.8,27,28

Administrative claims, in contrast to EHRs, capture all billed diagnostic encounters across healthcare settings, making them the reference standard for comorbidity ascertainment and utilization analytics.6 However, the retrospective nature and lack of integration of claims data into clinical workflows constrain real time usage of claims data by healthcare providers.17,18 Prior comparisons of claims versus EHR completeness have been limited to single health systems and select disease areas.29–32 These studies have not examined the value of EHRs compared to claims in capturing diagnosis and comorbidity information across multiple large healthcare organizations over an extended period.

To address this gap, an evaluation of diagnostic information completeness was conducted by linking claims and EHR data from diverse health systems over a 13-year span (2010–2022). We quantified, for each diagnostic category and year, the proportions of patient-years recorded exclusively in claims, exclusively in the EHR, or in both sources. We also calculated agreement metrics (i.e., sensitivity, specificity, Cohen’s κ, and PABAK) as well as temporal trends between diagnostic codes captured in EHRs and claims. Our analysis reveals how capture of diagnostic information in EHRs has evolved and informs the feasibility of leveraging EHR data for disease management, comorbidity measurement, and risk stratification.

Materials and methods

The goal of this retrospective data analysis was to evaluate the completeness of diagnostic information between claims and EHRs of large patient populations across several different healthcare organizations and over an extended period. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.33 The study was reviewed and approved by the Johns Hopkins School of Public Health’s Institutional Review Board (IRB00005784).

Data

This study used de-identified administrative claims and EHR data from the Optum Labs Data Warehouse (OLDW).34 The database contains longitudinal health information on enrollees and patients, representing a mixture of ages and geographical regions across the United States. The claims data in OLDW includes medical and pharmacy claims, laboratory results and enrollment records for over 200 million commercial and Medicare Advantage enrollees. The EHR-derived data includes a subset of EHR data that has been normalized and standardized into a single database.34 Diagnostic data are captured using International Classification of Disease, ninth (ICD-9) and tenth (ICD-10) editions. This study involved a retrospective analysis of a subset of OLDW data (2010–2022) containing patients with both claims and EHR data.

Sample identification

For each year in the study period, we identified enrollees in a commercial or Medicare Advantage plan with a diagnosis in claims and EHR. As patients in OLDW may have varied follow-up durations, eligibility was captured in patient-year units, which denotes enrollee’s record for a given calendar year. This unit enables consistent year-over-year comparisons across organizations and accommodates enrollment churn and variation. Commercial enrollees over the age of 64 were excluded from our analysis as such individuals may have incomplete claims data if they were covered by a supplementary source of insurance such as Medicare (not captured in OLDW). For a similar reason, we also omitted Medicare Advantage enrollees ages 64 and younger from the analysis. Patient-year combinations in which enrollees had gaps in insurance coverage or exhibited conflicting data were excluded. Coverage lapses were removed because of missing diagnostic information and potential data conflicts, such as discrepant demographic fields (e.g., different year of birth) between claims and EHR. Multiple criteria/steps were applied to finalize the sample identification process across all years (Appendix 1 & 2).

Outcome measures

Outcome measures were based on diagnosis codes in claims and EHR. We limited our analysis to diagnoses derived from structured data and excluded rule-out, suspected, or provisional diagnoses. More specifically, from claims, we omitted diagnoses from clinical laboratory, diagnostic imaging, and durable medical equipment providers as such diagnoses are provisional. From EHR data, we omitted diagnoses originating from unreliable settings such as phone encounters and diagnoses coded as “possible diagnosis of”. Natural language process (NLP) derived diagnoses from EHR medical notes were also excluded as validity and reliability of NLP methodology used to extract such data was not assessed consistently across the years. We also omitted diagnoses from the EHR that were coded as present on admission but never confirmed. Diagnoses from the problem lists were included, which only slightly increased the total number of patient-year diagnoses in the EHR.

We rolled up similar diagnoses into broader diagnosis groups using AHRQ Clinical Classification Software (CCS)35 to match diagnoses between claims and EHR. Eighteen high-level CCS diagnostic categories were indexed 1 to 18 as follows: (1) Infectious and parasitic diseases; (2) Neoplasms; (3) Endocrine, nutritional, and metabolic diseases and immunity disorders; (4) Diseases of the blood and blood-forming organs; (5) Mental illness; (6) Diseases of the nervous system and sense organs; (7) Diseases of the circulatory system; (8) Diseases of the respiratory system; (9) Diseases of the digestive system; (10) Diseases of the genitourinary system; (11) Complications of pregnancy, childbirth, and the puerperium; (12) Diseases of the skin and subcutaneous tissue; (13) Diseases of the musculoskeletal system and connective tissue; (14) Congenital anomalies; (15) Certain conditions originating in the perinatal period; (16) Injury and poisoning; (17) Symptoms, signs, and ill-defined conditions and factors influencing health status; and (18) Residual codes, unclassified, all E codes [259. and 260.]. For each diagnosis group, we counted the number of patients with a recorded diagnosis for that condition in claims, EHR, or both sources. We calculated discrete chronic conditions by using the AHRQ CCS tool to identify diagnosis codes indicative of a chronic condition and then mapped diagnoses to diagnosis groups using the CCS categories. We also constructed two measures of comorbidity: number of chronic conditions and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI).20 Each measure was constructed using claims records and EHR records separately. CCI was calculated using the R comorbidity package v1.1.0.36

Control variables

Control variables included age (i.e., 0–18, 19–44, 45–64, 65+), census region (i.e., Midwest, Northeast, South, West), insurance type (i.e., Medicare Advantage or commercial), sex (i.e., Female, Male), race (i.e., Asian, Black, White, Unknown), ethnicity (i.e., Hispanic, Non-Hispanic, Unknown), size of the health system (i.e., 2.5 million+, 1–2.5 million, <1 million), and affiliation with an integrated delivery network (IDN) (i.e., Yes or No). An IDN is a coordinated system of healthcare providers and organizations that work together to deliver a comprehensive range of healthcare services to patients within a specific area or community.37

Statistical analysis

Descriptive characteristics were measured for all years and each year separately. Summary statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Where ranges are shown, they reflect variability across healthcare organizations or CCS diagnosis groups, as specified. To further analyze diagnostic information in the study cohort, for each CCS diagnosis group, multiple occurrences of the same condition within a calendar year and source were collapsed into a single event. The annual counts and proportions of patient-years were calculated within each diagnosis group, which were extracted from claims only, EHRs only, or in both sources (Appendix 3).

Patient-years represent unique patients with at least one recorded diagnosis in a CCS group during that calendar year. Repeated occurrences of the same condition within the year are collapsed to a single event. Proportions expressed in patient-years should be interpreted as annual person-level prevalence per source rather than counts of encounters or services (i.e., the percentage of enrollees with at least one diagnosis in that CCS group recorded in claims, EHR, or both).

The patient-year proportions were visualized using stacked bar charts. The charts were stratified using high-level CCS diagnosis groups and the health care organizations (HCOs) represented in the underlying data. For patient‐year comorbidity burden in the study cohort, counts of chronic conditions (0, 1, 2, ≥ 3) and CCI scores were computed separately from claims and EHR data. Results

A total of 16,991,756 patient-years were observed across all years (Appendix 1). Age, gender, geography, and insurance type were derived from claims. IDN status, health system size, race, and ethnicity were drawn from EHRs. The sample spanned all age ranges, included more females (58%), was predominantly commercially insured (83%) and mostly lived in the Midwest (57%). Also, majority of these patients were White (63%), non-Hispanic (67%), and treated within IDNs (78%) (Table 1). Study samples extracted for individual years (e.g., 2019) showed a similar pattern of population characteristics (Appendix 4).

Over the study period, the proportion of diagnoses appearing only in claims steadily declined from 51% to 41% by 2020, while the fraction captured in both claims and EHR rose from 46% to 56% (Table 2, Appendix 5). This trend was reversed from 2020 to 2022 with the share of diagnosis data captured in both data sources decreasing by 18%. Given the common use of CCS diagnosis categories in population health analytics, share of patient-diagnosis counts were calculated for each of the high-level 18 CCS categories (Figure 1, Appendix 6) as well as the 88 mid-level CCS categories (Appendix 7). The share of patient-diagnosis counts for high-level CCS categories were also calculated and visualized for individual study years (Appendix 8).

Overall, most patient-diagnosis cases across high-level CCS categories appeared in both claims and EHRs, averaging 51% across all categories. However, depending on the diagnosis group, the share of patient-diagnosis varied considerably across data sources (i.e., 25–66% in claims only, 1–10% in EHR only, 24–74% in both data sources). For example, 74% of patient-diagnosis mapping to CCS category 17 (i.e., signs, symptoms, and ill-defined conditions influencing health status) were captured in both claims and EHRs, while claims-only and EHR-only shares were 25% and 1%, respectively (i.e., 99% captured in all claims and 76% in all EHRs). For CCS category 15 (i.e., conditions originating in the perinatal period), patient-diagnosis shares were 24% in both data sources, 66% for claims-only, and 10% for EHR-only data (i.e., 90% captured in all claims, but only 34% in all EHRs). On average, across all CCS diagnosis categories, 55 ± 10% of patient-diagnosis cases were captured in all EHRs while this rate was much higher at 96 ± 2% for all claims (Appendix 6).

Agreement statistics were calculated to measure the percentage of diagnosis extracted from and matching between all claims and all EHRs. Overall, the agreement reached a specificity of .98 (indicating few patient-diagnoses in EHRs not found in claims) and a sensitivity of .57 (indicating a moderate number of patient-diagnoses in claims that were also found in EHRs). Kappa and PABAK scores reached .6 and .65, accordingly, confirming moderate to substantial agreement between claims and EHRs beyond chance across all high-level CCS diagnostic groups (Appendix 6). Similar to the descriptive comparisons of patient-diagnoses across high-level CCS categories (Figure 1), CCS category 15 received the highest specificity (1.00) and the lowest sensitivity (.27), while CCS category 17 received the lowest specificity (.88) and the highest sensitivity (.75). Kappa and PABAK scores also confirmed the variation of agreement levels across CCS categories (Appendix 6).

The variations in the distribution of diagnosis codes across claims and EHRs were also observed at mid-level CCS categories (Appendix 7). Highly prevalent diagnoses groups, such as hypertension and diabetes mellitus without complication demonstrated strong capture by both data sources (66% and 56%), whereas musculoskeletal, skin, and sprain diagnosis groups exhibited less than 40% capture rate by both data sources. EHR-only rates remained low across mid-level CCS categories (mostly 1–10%) limiting the added value of diagnosis codes in EHRs when added to claims (Appendix 7).

Variations in the capture rate of diagnostic information across data sources (i.e., claims and EHR) were further analyzed at the healthcare organization level (Figure 2). The relative proportions of patient-diagnosis pairs found only in claims, only in the EHR, or in both data sources were calculated and visualized across each of the 56 healthcare organizations. While capturing diagnostic information in both data sources remained the modal category at most sites, pronounced heterogeneity was observed in how much each healthcare system relies on claims versus EHR. Overall, the share of diagnosis data captured in total EHRs ranged between 37% and 72%, with an average of 55 ± 8% across all healthcare organizations. The share of EHR-only diagnoses data ranged between 1% and 7%, with an average of 3 ± 1%, indicating the limited value of EHR-derived CCS-grouped diagnosis information if claims data are available (Figure 2). The size of the healthcare organizations did not considerably impact the share of EHRs in capturing the diagnosis information when compared to claims. Similarly, the stratification of the healthcare organizations based on their IDN status did not result in a notable difference in the distribution of diagnosis information across claims and EHR data (Appendix 9).

CCI scores showed a similar trend to the chronic condition counts, with claims data having a higher share of patient-years with higher CCI scores. For example, patient-years with a CCI of 3+ (i.e., highly comorbid) constituted 9% of patients captured in claims while this share was only 4% for EHRs. However, the percentage of patients with a CCI of 0 (not comorbid) showed a reverse trend with identifying fewer patients in claims than EHRs (68% vs 79%). Overall, using claims data resulted in calculating a higher CCI (0.74 ± 1.46) compared to EHRs (0.39 ± 0.99) for the same patient population (Table 3). Replicating this analysis across individual years showed similar trends with claims data resulting in a higher level of comorbidity counts/scores compared to the EHRs (Appendix 10).

The gap in comorbidity levels was regressed against years of data to measure significant trends in claims and EHRs capturing diagnoses/comorbidity data. When regressed across the entire study period (2010–2022), the results showed a significant trend in the increasing share of patients with 1 or 2 chronic conditions in the EHR data. For example, the share of patients with 1 chronic condition increased 0.43 percentage points in the EHR data compared to claims annually (Table 4). Similar results were found for CCI, with the share of patients with CCI of 0 increasing 0.58 percentage points in EHRs compared to claims annually. However, the share of patients with CCI of 3+ decreased 0.65 percentage points in EHRs compared to claims, showing the increasing value of claims in capturing higher CCI patients over the study period (Table 4). Nonetheless, when regressed across the pre-COVID years (2010–2019), the results show a significant trend in the increasing share of patients with a CCI of 3+ in the EHR data. Indeed, the share of patients with CCI of 3+ increased 0.34 percentage points in EHRs compared to claims annually, thus showing the improving ability of EHRs in capturing higher CCI patients in pre-COVID years (Appendix 11 & 12).

Discussion

EHRs have achieved high adoption rates across U.S. healthcare providers,1 offering an opportunity for value-based providers to utilize EHR data for risk stratification and quality measurement.6,8,14 Limited interoperability of EHRs and lack of HIE integration, however, have resulted in value-based providers not having access to clinical information, such as diagnosis codes, that are captured outside of their healthcare organizations.2,26 This has further restricted the use of EHRs for populations health management and risk stratification efforts in which complete diagnostic data across time is required.38,39 To measure the extent and variations of this limitation across healthcare organizations, this study evaluated diagnostic data completeness by linking claims and EHR data across 56 healthcare organizations from 2010 to 2022.

Our study revealed the heterogeneity in EHR diagnoses completeness across diagnostic categories (Figure 1), health organizations (Figure 2), and several years (Table 2). These findings are consistent with prior studies of encounter heterogeneity in multisite EHR networks,40 institutional processes,41 and calendar years.29 Our study also revealed differences in the chronic condition counts and the CCI score when using diagnostic data captured in claims versus EHRs (Table 3). These findings are aligned with prior studies showing a lower rate of chronic conditions captured in EHRs versus claims at single integrated health systems.6,30,31 The gap analysis of the CCI score showed an increasing difference between claims and EHRs among patients with high CCI scores from 2010 to 2022; however, this trend decreased from 2010 to 2019 (Table 4 & Appendix 11).

At the high-level CCS diagnosis grouping, the share of patient-diagnosis varied across data sources with 25–66% identified in claims only, 1–10% in EHR only, and 24–74% in both data sources (Appendix 6). Using the high-level CCS grouping, on average, 96 ± 2% of patient-diagnosis were identified in the entire claims data while only 55 ± 10% were identified in all EHR data (Appendix 6). Mid-level CCS diagnosis groups exhibited even a wider difference across data sources, with the percentage of identified patient-diagnosis varying 19–77% in claims only, 1–70% in EHR only, and 10–69% in both data sources (Appendix 7). On average, using the mid-level categories, 95 ± 8% of patient-diagnosis were found in all claims data and 47 ± 10% in all EHR data (Appendix 7). These results indicate that risk stratification efforts should use claims data, if available, to extract diagnostic information; however, if EHRs are the only source of data, diagnostic data should be treated with caution.6,8 Moreover, CCS groupings showed that increasing the granularity of the diagnosis codes could lead to EHRs missing more patient-diagnosis cases compared to claims data (e.g., mid-level CCS groupings identified fewer patient-diagnosis cases in EHRs than high-level CCS groupings when compared to claims data). Thus, to use EHR data more effectively, especially when claims data are lacking, risk stratification algorithms should consider the effect of various diagnosis groupings on the completeness of the extracted data.

Expectedly, our results showed that the proportion of diagnoses data captured in claims or EHRs varied markedly across healthcare organizations (Figure 2). However, we did not find a correlation between healthcare organization size (i.e., number of patients) or type (i.e., IDN or not IDN) and the completeness of diagnosis codes captured in EHRs when compared with claims (Appendix 9). For example, some large health systems missed over 40% of diagnoses in their local EHR, while certain smaller networks achieved capture rates exceeding 60%. This heterogeneity likely reflects differences in local documentation workflows, coding policies, and EHR configurations regardless of the size or type of the healthcare organization.42,43 Consequently, in risk stratification efforts, reliance on EHR data for comprehensive diagnostic ascertainment should not be solely based on the size or type of healthcare organization as it may yield different results due to unknown system-level factors.

Overall, the study results show an initial increase in the share of EHRs in identifying patient-diagnoses cases (2010–2019; pre-pandemic years); however, this trend decreases in the remaining years (2020–2022; pandemic years) (Table 2). Even though the dispersion of diagnosis completeness is consistent with prior studies,6,29 EHR’s share of diagnostic information improved remarkably over the pre-pandemic decade (2010-2019), with EHRs capturing 10 additional percentage points at the end of this period (Table 2). This gain likely reflects multiple factors such as expanded HIE participation,44,45 strengthened interoperability standards that reduce semantic and technical barriers between systems,46 and the incentives of Meaningful Use and value‐based programs, which have driven more consistent, structured clinical documentation workflows.47–49 Additionally, as patient‐panel assignments and system integrations have stabilized over the years, longitudinal continuity of care should have been improved, further enhancing EHR completeness for diagnostic information recording.50

In contrast to the pre-pandemic years, the decreasing completeness of diagnosis information identified in EHRs during the pandemic years (2020–2022) may reflect some contextual factors. For instance, health systems rapidly expanded telehealth services, which initially lacked interoperability with local EHRs thus missing diagnostic codes captured during telehealth visits;51 health systems may have demoted the encoding of diagnostic codes which were deemed not high priority during the COVID-19 pandemic due to resource constraints;52 and, health systems with fragmented EHRs may have limited reporting of complete data to existing multi-institute data warehouses such as OLDW. Nevertheless, despite not being captured in EHRs as structured diagnosis codes, such information could still be extracted from telehealth databases and EHR’s free text and then billed through administrative channels thus showing up in claims data (Table 2).

Our study findings carry several important implications for policy and data governance, especially for population health management programs. First, although completeness of diagnosis codes captured in EHRs has improved, persistent under‐capture of complex comorbidities highlights the need for stronger interoperability mandates and incentives to ensure that providers have comprehensive diagnostic visibility, such as real-time claims-to-EHR feeds under the 21st Century Cures Act.53 Second, payers and health systems should collaborate on data‐sharing agreements that enable secure, bidirectional exchange of claims and clinical data, closing gaps that currently leave critical information unnoticed or mislaid.54 Finally, robust data governance frameworks, incorporating standardized coding conventions, routine completeness audits, and integration of external encounter data, are essential to maintain and further advance the quality of EHR diagnostic information for all patient‐severity levels.55

Several factors might limit generalizability of this study findings. First, our analysis is based on 56 healthcare organizations that opted into the OLDW platform, which may not represent all facility types or geographic regions. Second, we focused exclusively on diagnosis codes; other clinically relevant signals such as laboratory values, medications, and unstructured clinical notes were not evaluated but could further inform completeness assessment for risk stratification purposes.56–61 Third, EHR systems and coding standards evolve over time, and periodic updates to underlying data models or workflows may have influenced temporal trends independently of true changes in documentation completeness. Fourth, although our cohort includes diverse populations, most organizations were clustered in the Midwest, potentially limiting generalizability to regions with different demographic profiles. Fifth, while claims data are generally more complete than EHR data, they may still be incomplete when patients change insurers, carry secondary coverage, or encounter benefit carve-outs. Adjudication delays and denials can also result in missing claims. Consequently, using claims as the reference does not ensure clinical diagnostic accuracy. Indeed, in this study, claims served as a pragmatic analytic reference for completeness rather than a clinical gold standard. Sixth, we assessed raw diagnosis codes captured in EHRs and claims, rather than validated computable phenotypes, which may offer more robust definitions for complex conditions and merit examination in future work.62–64 Seventh, our age stratification was restricted to Medicare Advantage enrollees aged 65 and older, excluding the broader traditional Medicare population. In this scenario, gaps in chronic‐conditions and CCI scores capture may be even larger among the older adults and frail populations.65 Finally, our study assessed the completeness of diagnosis codes in EHRs for risk stratification purposes. Although these results may have applications in other contexts too, the quality of EHR data should always be assessed depending on the research or operational aims of such data in practice.

Future studies should investigate how the completeness of diagnostic data captured in EHRs is affected by various internal or external variables. For example, future research can assess how different aspects of healthcare organizations may improve or decrease the quality of diagnostic data in EHRs. Future studies should also evaluate if the quality of EHR’s diagnostic data varies across different population groups. For instance, future work may examine potential disparities of EHR diagnostic data quality across minority populations.66 Additionally, among uninsured populations and in the absence of claims data, new approaches are needed to measure the value of EHRs in capturing diagnostic data. Given the anticipated increase of uninsured populations, such studies will enable health systems to better risk stratify and manage the health of such growing populations using EHR data.67 Lastly, the findings of this study primarily inform population-level analytics and risk stratification efforts. Future research should assess the effect of EHR data quality on point-of-care clinical decision-making at the patient-level.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that EHRs captured incomplete diagnostic information compared to claims but quality of such data significantly improved from 2010 to 2019. Our multi‐system analysis shows that EHR-claims agreement has improved for healthier patients and some high‐burden groups, yet multi-comorbidity populations remain under-ascertained in EHRs. Poor data quality for diagnostic information can negatively impact population health management decision-making that are based on disease risk-adjustment models incorporating comorbidity and chronic conditions. To fully leverage EHRs for value-based care and research, health systems must strengthen interoperability and integrate claims data as seen fit.
