LiteDriveNet for driver distraction classification using a lightweight multi-scale convolutional neural network
Authors: Abirami Namachivayam, Nirmala Paramanandham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDistracted driving is a critical global concern and a major factor in numerous road collisions. To mitigate these issues, this article introduces a novel model, LiteDriveNet, a resource-efficient 16-layer convolutional neural network with a compact size of 1.71 MB, designed for precise image identification and behavioral analysis. The architecture incorporates multi-scale receptive blending, progressive feature enhancement, and hierarchical feature aggregation to achieve robust feature extraction. To assess the model performance, a dataset named DistractedDrivingSet_v1 is also proposed, comprising 6075 outdoor images across 8 distinctive classes, captured under real-world lighting conditions such as sunlight and shadows. LiteDriveNet’s efficacy was further verified using two publicly available benchmark datasets, the State Farm Distracted Driver Detection (SFD3) dataset and the American University in Cairo (AUC) version 2. Experimental findings show that LiteDriveNet consistently surpasses both accuracy and computational efficiency compared to existing state-of-the-art, lightweight, and neural architecture search-based (NAS) models. LiteDriveNet achieves average validation accuracy improvements of 16.53% on DistractedDrivingSet_v1, 6.70% on SFD3, 10.91% on AUCv2 Camera1, and 28.97% on AUCv2 Camera2. An ablation study also proves that LiteDriveNet is an eminent lightweight model for distracted driver recognition, as it is highly suitable for real-time environments compared to state-of-the-art techniques.Keywords: Advanced driver assistance system, Artificial Intelligence, Convolutional neural networks, Distracted driver classificationSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Distracted driving has become a major concern worldwide, contributing to a high number of traffic accidents yearly. In 2024, about 178,000 people in India lost their lives in traffic accidents, with about 60% of those deaths occurring among people between the ages of 18 and 341. According to the survey, the most vulnerable road users are motorized two-wheeler riders, cyclists, and pedestrians2. Abnormal and distracted driving significantly increases the risk of traffic accidents, and it has become a serious issue in road safety. Accordingly, identifying drivers who exhibit abnormal driving behavior is essential for implementing future preventive measures to avoid traffic accidents3. Nowadays, smartphones and entertainment devices are installed in cars, having an increasing impact on modern drivers. It is essential to develop efficient solutions, as the number of distractions increases for drivers to manage these dangerous habits. With the development of AI, automated driving systems are trying to reach levels 4 and 5 in advanced driver assistance systems (ADAS)4. Driver behavior can be classified into different categories based on psychological, external, and visual data. The physiological data-based approaches concentrate on gathering physiological data from drivers, including electrooculography (EOG), electrocardiograms (ECG), and electroencephalograms (EEG). The external data is based on environmental gathering from the steering wheel, GPS, and cameras. Visual data is collected from the camera, which is positioned towards the driver’s upper body, face, hand, and head to capture and analyze features based on the driver’s actions. However, due to hardware implementation costs, individual behavior, physiological differences, and the impact of environmental temperature and dew on the sensors, these approaches are frequently challenging to generalize and implement5. Due to these limitations, the previously mentioned techniques are not suitable for distracted driver behavior classification (DDBC).

Deep learning techniques have led to advancements in the ADAS and driver monitoring systems. Visual data-based driving behavior identification has advanced widely and produced better outcomes. Some of the key categories that road accidents fall into are aggressive driving, such as speeding, tailgating, and ignoring traffic signals and signs; distracted driving, such as mobile phone usage (texting & calling), eating or drinking while driving, and talking to passengers; impaired driving, such as drunk driving and driving while fatigued or drowsy; and risk-taking behavior, such as driving at excessive speeds, ignoring pedestrian crossings, and running stop signs6. CNN-based deep learning methods are a trending area in ADAS technology. However, existing deep learning classification models still suffer from a huge number of model parameters and poor classification accuracy, with limited resources in vehicle platforms. To address these limitations, the work’s novelty lies in the design of a compact, well-structured, and lightweight CNN, namely LiteDriveNet. It is designed to have fewer model parameters, resulting in faster training time and reduced loss. This proposed model’s performance is compared with other famed pre-trained models such as VGG16, VGG19, ResNet50, MobileNetV2, InceptionV3, and DenseNet121, and other state-of-the-art techniques. The work’s novelty lies in the design of a compact, resource-efficient CNN tailored for distracted driver behavior detection, the integration of advanced feature extraction techniques, and the creation of a dedicated dataset, all of which collectively outperform existing state-of-the-art (SOTA) models on multiple benchmarks.

Methodology

This framework aims to improve road safety by rapidly categorizing driver behaviors and avoiding distractions. Advancements in AI and sensor technologies are continuously enhancing the accuracy and effectiveness of systems by reducing the risks associated with distracted driving. It is developed to identify and recognize distractions that a driver may experience while driving. The general framework for recognizing distracted driving behavior using a novel LiteDriveNet is shown in Fig. 1. This framework contains data acquisition, data splitting, preprocessing, and classification based on driver distraction categories. In the first stage, three different dataset resources are utilized, including a proposed dataset for distracted driver behavior, named DistractedDrivingSet_v1. This dataset contains 8 classes, including eating or snacking, speaking on earphones, single-hand driving (left), looking somewhere, adjusting the mirror, reading a paper or a receipt, single-hand driving (right), and not wearing a seatbelt. The AUC Distracted Driver Dataset was created by the American University in Cairo43,44. The State Farm Distracted Driver Detection dataset is available on Kaggle45. Those datasets are categorized into 10 classes, including normal driving, texting on the right-hand side, texting on the left-hand side, drinking liquids, reaching behind, hair and makeup, chatting to passengers, chatting on the right side, talking on the left side, and operating the radio.

Overview of the driver behavior classification framework.

Next, data splitting is done by dividing the dataset into subsets for training, validation, and testing purposes. The ratio of data splitting may vary depending on the total number of images available. Commonly used distributions such as 70% for training, 20% for validation, and 10% for testing (70:20:10); 80% for training and 20% for testing (80:20); or 90% for training and 10% for testing (90:10). The training set is used for model training; the validation set is used for evaluating the model’s performance to avoid overfitting by fine-tuning the hyperparameters; and the testing set is used for testing the trained model by using unseen data. In the preprocessing stage, the images are resized to standard resolution, normalized, and then encoded using the one-hot method for the cross-entropy loss function. After feeding the data into the model for training, it extracts the necessary features for classification. The hyperparameters are used to validate the model performance, such as learning rate schedulers and early stopping. Early stopping terminates the training process when the validation accuracy begins to decrease, thereby avoiding overfitting to the training data. In the final stage, the model’s performance is examined using accuracy, recall, confidence score, precision, and F1 score to classify distracted actions.

Proposed LiteDriveNet architecture

LiteDriveNet uses efficiently compact CNN architecture designed manually to achieve an optimal trade-off between computational efficiency and accuracy, unlike the NAS-based method and the ensemble approach. LiteDriveNet is a 16-layer custom CNN designed with three blocks, consisting of a hybrid block, a refine block, and a fusion block, as shown in Fig. 2. The overall feature extraction in the model consists of seven convolutional layers and five max pooling layers. The convolution layers in the hybrid block use different sizes of filters (5 × 5 and 3 × 3) to fuse the receptive fields. For the refine block, the 3 × 3 layers are stacked on top of each other to improve the feature enhancement. Finally, the 3 × 3 layers are stacked together in the fusion block to combine hierarchical features. In the classification stage, the model consists of one global average pooling layer, two dense layers, and a SoftMax layer. The main objective of designing LiteDriveNet is to minimize trainable parameters while maintaining high classification accuracy. The pseudocode for the model is depicted in the algorithm.

The proposed LiteDriveNet model architecture.

Hybrid convolutional block

The LiteDriveNet is implemented with an input shape of 224 × 224 and a total of seven convolutional layers, consisting of one kernel layer and six kernel layers. Unlike traditional models, which use repeated 3 × 3 kernels throughout, LiteDriveNet introduces a 5 × 5 convolution in the very first layer, enabling a larger receptive field early on to capture contextual body posture and hand-object interactions, which are crucial in distracted driving. As illustrated in Fig. 3(a), the proposed architecture performs multi-scale receptive blending in the hybrid block, which combines one kernel layer with integrated kernels in the early layers.

A 5 × 5 kernel at the first layer captures these coarse spatial relationships immediately, before spatial dimensions are reduced by pooling. This is computationally justified because the first layer has only 3 input channels, so the additional cost of a 5 × 5 kernel (1,216 parameters) over a 3 × 3 kernel (448 parameters) is minimal relative to the representational benefit gained. LiteDriveNet employs a deliberate method of progressive growth of filters, such as 16 in Block 1, 32 in Block 2, and 56, 128, and 128 in Block 3. The idea behind such asymmetric growth serves two purposes. Firstly, by maintaining relatively small filter sizes such as 16 and 32 in early layers when the feature map sizes are large (224 × 224 or 112 × 112), the computational load is kept small. Secondly, filter size 128 is used in later layers, where feature maps have been down-sampled to 14 × 14 and 7 × 7 by max-pooling, making it possible to efficiently construct high-level discriminative feature representations at low computational cost. This is fundamentally different from ensemble-based methods, which fuse features from three large pre-trained networks simultaneously, a strategy that achieves high accuracy but requires having a large number of parameters. Before the activation function, every convolutional layer in LiteDriveNet is followed by batch normalization. It accelerates convergence by reducing internal covariate shift, acts as a regularizer, reducing dependence on dropout, and stabilizes training across the varied image conditions.

Dense and output block

LiteDriveNet employs a global average pooling (GAP) instead of flattening. Flatten converts entire feature maps into one-dimensional vectors, often resulting in thousands to millions of parameters, which leads to huge memory space. In contrast, GAP computes the average of each map to a single value and aggregates spatial information. It reduces the number of parameters and memory and often prevents overfitting. Replacing the traditional flattened layer prior to the dense layers leads to the greatest parameter reduction in LiteDriveNet. Following the fifth max pooling operation, the spatial dimensions of the feature map are 7 × 7, and it has 128 channels. The result of a flatten operation would be a vector of 6,272 values, resulting in a dense (512) layer with 3,211,776 parameters in that transition alone, over 7 times the total number of parameters in LiteDriveNet. GAP then averages each of the 128 feature maps spatially to give a 128-dimensional output. The dense (512) layer then requires only 66,048 parameters. This one design decision not only decreases transition parameters by a factor of about 48x but also increases generalization by compelling the network to generate spatially averaged, globally representative features instead of position-specific features.

Design rationale and comparison with existing lightweight approaches

LiteDriveNet architectural design is driven by the attainment of an optimum balance between feature representation and computational efficiency, especially in real-time embedded applications. Architecturally, existing distracted driver detection models are mainly based on multi-stage detection pipelines or complex feature modelling, which increases the computational cost. Detection-based methods, including the EfficientDet-based method41, follow a two-stage architecture that uses a backbone network (EfficientNet) with an object detection module (EfficientDet) to localize areas of interest such as hands, face, and objects, then classifies the localized region. It is an explicit region-based reasoning that increases the spatial interpretability, but it also has additional computational costs of bounding box regression, feature pyramid networks, and multi-scale feature fusion, which in turn increase latency and memory usage. Similarly, ensemble-based architectures42 can be regarded as an extension of this complexity, in which multiple deep networks (DenseNet, ResNet, and GRU) are combined with each other through feature fusion and voting mechanisms, which result in duplicate feature acquisition and high-cost inference. Moreover, hybrid CNN-Transformer models like MAViT5 can improve the interaction of global and local features with an attention mechanism. However, they add extra computational cost and inference time because of the transformer computations.

In contrast to NAS-based models like detection-driven NAS-based architectures that further augment the complexity of combining Faster R-CNN with deep NASNet40 backbones of more than 1200 layers, which is associated with high computational cost and low scalability. In contrast, LiteDriveNet is based on a manually designed and structured CNN architecture to prevent the extra cost of search and the runtime complexity of automation architecture search and dynamic convolution. It can achieve multi-scale feature representation by utilizing a lightweight hybrid convolutional approach (using 5 × 5 and 3 × 3 kernels in the early layers) to allow the model to learn both global contextual (e.g., driver posture) and fine-scale local (e.g., hand-object interactions) information without making use of attention. Also, progressive feature refinement and hierarchical feature aggregation are used to guarantee efficient feature learning at depth, with low parameter complexity. Moreover, the most significant design option is the application of global average pooling (GAP) as an alternative to traditional flattening operations. Unlike many existing lightweight CNN models that still rely on dense fully connected layers, GAP drastically decreases the number of parameters and alleviates overfitting by enforcing spatially aggregated feature representations. This leads to a large drop in model complexity and maintains discriminative capability. As a result, LiteDriveNet offers an easier but efficient alternative to the existing state-of-the-art lightweight architectures, achieving competitive performance or better with much fewer parameters and improved real-time suitability.

Experimental settings and performance metrics

An Intel Xeon E-2236 CPU with 128GB RAM, along with TensorFlow and Keras version 2.15.0, is used to conduct experiments. The training process is carried out for 50 epochs; early stopping is used to end the training process if the validation loss does not improve consecutively after 10 epochs. The Adam optimizer is used with a learning rate of 0.001, momentum of 0.9, and second-order momentum of 0.999. ReduceLROnPlateau is used to reduce the learning rate by a factor of 0.1 when validation accuracy does not improve after 5 consecutive epochs. The categorical cross-entropy loss function is utilized, and the training is performed with a batch size of 32. To enhance the robustness and generalization capability of the model without affecting the original driving behavior characteristics, a light-weight data augmentation pipeline was used to augment the training images. In particular, the slight camera orientation variations were simulated in two ways: by randomly rotating the scene (± 5°) and by randomly scaling the scene (0.95, 1.0). Small adjustments in brightness (0.1), contrast (0.1), saturation (0.05), and hue (0.02) were applied through color jittering to handle illumination variations and environmental changes. In order to have a fair comparison, all the models considered in this study were trained in the same experimental conditions. The performance analysis of the LiteDriveNet is compared with conventional pre-trained models and state-of-the-art models based on accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and confidence scores, which are defined in Eqs. (16, 17, 18, 19, and 20), respectively. The accuracy calculates the correctly predicted classes out of the total number of sample images. Precision measures the accuracy of true positive predictions by avoiding negative classes predicted as positive classes. Recall calculates that the actual positive classes are identified correctly as positives. The F1 score measures the balance between precision and recall. The confidence score is the ratio of correct predictions to the total predictions.

Results and discussions

LiteDriveNet is designed as a single-stage, end-to-end CNN model with less parameter count and reduced computational complexity. This makes it favorably suitable for deployment on resource-constrained systems like embedded systems and edge devices deployed in ADAS. The findings show that LiteDriveNet achieves a superior balance between accuracy and efficiency, which is more feasible in real-life applications than the current state-of-the-art methods. Existing techniques, like NAS-based optimization or ensemble learning, try to make models more robust by making them more complex, but this makes them less efficient. Beyond standard performance, the model’s generalizability in real-world driving environments is a critical consideration. Although benchmark datasets such as SFD3 and AUCv2 primarily capture scenarios from controlled or semi-controlled environments. The proposed DistractedDrivingSet_v1 dataset is a collection of images taken in outdoor environments, with light conditions such as sunlight, shadows, and illumination variation.

Nonetheless, real-world deployment may imply further problems, including changes in light conditions (day and night cycles), weather conditions (rain, fog, and low visibility), and moving background disturbances. It can impact the quality of the visual feature and subsequently influence classification performance. To address real-world adaptability, prior literature has emphasized facial expression recognition models being very sensitive to changes in lighting and other environmental distractions and has suggested methods like data augmentation, attention, and multi-scale feature extraction to enhance resilience in uncontrolled settings. Even with these improvements, there is an ongoing problem of attaining uniform performance in various real-life scenarios. Future efforts will focus on increasing practicality by adding illumination-invariant preprocessing, weather-based augmentation plans, and better feature learning architectures. Also, it can be extended to multi-view facial inputs and incorporated with multimodal cues to enhance system reliability in real-world situations.

Conclusions and future directions

LiteDriveNet, a novel 16-layer lightweight CNN with 446,498 trainable parameters and a compact model size of 1.71 MB, is designed for real-time classification of distracted driver behavior. The architecture integrates multi-scale receptive blending, progressive feature enhancement, and hierarchical feature aggregation across three purposefully designed blocks, achieving high classification accuracy without attention mechanisms, neural architecture search, or ensemble complexity. Experimental results across three datasets demonstrate consistent superiority over existing methods, achieving 99.84% on DistractedDrivingSet_v1, 99.40% on SFD3, 98.63% on AUCv2 Camera 1, and 99.26% on AUCv2 Camera 2, with 99–100% precision, recall, and F1 score. Moreover, it resulted in an inference latency as low as 1.7 ms per image, 0.538 GFLOPs, and an efficiency ratio of 0.900. LiteDriveNet is well-suited for embedded ADAS deployment, the best among all compared models. The ablation study further validates its robustness across varied hyperparameters, input resolutions, and cross-validation folds, confirming the adaptability of LiteDriveNet. In 5-fold cross-validation, the model achieves consistent performance across datasets: 98.92%–99.81% on SFD3, 97.19%–99.02% on AUCv2 Camera 1, 98.61%–99.80% on AUCv2 Camera 2, and 99.46%–99.79% on DistractedDrivingSet_v1, with inference latency below 7 ms per image, ensuring real-time performance. Hyperparameter evaluation shows that Adam (0.001) performs best, reaching 99.69% on SFD3 with dropout (+ 6.99% over baseline), while maintaining stable performance across different learning rates, indicating low sensitivity and strong generalizability. Across input resolutions from 32 × 32 to 128 × 128, LiteDriveNet sustains high accuracy, achieving up to 99.50% on SFD3, 99.26% on AUCv2 Camera 2 at 96 × 96, and 99.72% on DistractedDrivingSet_v1 at 128 × 128, proving flexibility for deployment under changing hardware constraints. Future research will focus on extending the dataset to include night-time and hostile weather scenarios, which will further improve the model’s generalization ability for real-world ADAS implementation.
