Leveraging Digital Innovations for Universal Oral Health Coverage: Evaluation of a Teledentistry Programme among Indonesian adults
Authors: Manu Raj Mathur, Shehnaz Khan, Ali Golkari, Vaidehi Ketkar, Tri Ari Estoetti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs the global burden of oral diseases rises, cost-effective solutions are needed to overcome accessibility barriers in oral healthcare. In Indonesia, geographical challenges further limit access to dental services. Teledentistry has emerged as a promising intervention to bridge these gaps by enhancing access to professional guidance and promoting better oral hygiene practices. This paper evaluates a Teledentistry initiative that aims to support progress towards achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC) for oral health.MethodsThis mixed-methods study evaluated the effectiveness of Unilever Oral Care’s Teledentistry initiative (the Tanya Pepsodent “Talk to a Dentist” programme) rolled out in Indonesia. The quantitative component compared oral health outcomes between individuals who voluntarily engaged with the Teledentistry service after scanning a QR code printed on Pepsodent product packaging (test group) and those who participated via an online consumer panel and were demographically matched to the test group (control group). The qualitative component involved in-depth interviews with General Dentists to assess the app’s usability and feasibility in providing dental care.ResultsQuantitative findings showed that post-test group respondents increased their brushing frequency by an average of 1.06 times per week (i.e., cumulative weekly brushing frequency) and brushing duration by 0.15 min. They also experienced fewer difficulties with chewing and speech, and 62% reported intending to reduce the interval between dental visits. Qualitative findings revealed that dentists viewed the teledentistry service as an efficient alternative due to its flexible availability, including on weekends. They also noted that it helped reduce dental anxiety among patients. However, some challenges were identified, including long wait times and usability difficulties.ConclusionsThe Teledentistry service led to improvements in average weekly brushing frequency and oral health awareness but did not improve perceived control or self-rated oral health. These mixed results suggest that while the service supports behaviour change, sustained impact may require integration with in-person care and continued professional support.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06830-5.Keywords: Teledentistry, Oral health, Awareness, Digital health, Access, Universal health coverage

Introduction

The staggering burden of oral diseases affecting 3.5 billion people globally [1] has resonated across the landscape of health research, drawing long-overdue attention to this crisis. Recent evidence indicates that this number continues to rise [2] highlighting persistent structural barriers in coverage, availability, affordability, and geographic accessibility [1]. Among the key drivers of the global oral disease burden is the enduring inequity in access to appropriate oral healthcare services. However, the rapid pace of technological advancement, coupled with declining costs, has generated growing interest in the application of telemedicine to help address these disparities. In particular, Teledentistry presents notable potential to improve the delivery of oral healthcare in rural and remote regions, where conventional services remain limited or difficult to access [3].

The WHO Global Oral Health Action Plan identifies digital technologies as a critical enabler of accessible and equitable oral health services. It underscores the role of digital interventions in strengthening prevention and promotion strategies through enhancing oral health literacy at the individual and community levels. It also emphasises the potential of digital health technologies in improving early detection, remote consultation, and referral pathways for the timely management of oral diseases [4]. The 2024 Bangkok Declaration on Oral Health further reinforces the importance of scalable, cost-effective digital solutions, urging governments and stakeholders to integrate innovative Teledentistry models into national oral health strategies [5].

Telemedicine, which is the use of digital technologies to provide remote healthcare services, has been widely adopted to improve access to medical care. In dentistry, this approach was introduced to address accessibility challenges and empower individuals with the knowledge and guidance needed to maintain better oral health at home in initiatives generally known as Teledentistry [6]. Evidence indicates that Teledentistry offers significant benefits for both patients and dentists, improves access to oral health services, enhances the efficiency of care delivery, and demonstrates strong potential as a cost-effective solution for expanding dental care, particularly in resource-limited settings [3, 7–11]. Additionally, Teledentistry has been recognised as a means of bridging the gap between urban and rural healthcare [7]. The term “Teledentistry” was introduced in 1997 by Cook, who defined it as “the practice of using video-conferencing technologies to diagnose and provide treatment guidance remotely.” [12] The WHO further refines this definition by emphasising the role of digital platforms in enabling collaboration between oral health professionals or direct engagement between patients and providers, regardless of distance [13].

Indonesia’s oral health crisis is shaped by multiple structural barriers. With a population of over 270 million spread across 17,500 islands, the country, with its unique topography, faces significant challenges in delivering equitable oral healthcare [14, 15]. Despite efforts to integrate oral health into national health policies, 88.8% of the population suffers from dental caries, and 74.1% experience periodontal disease [15]. Alarmingly, 57.6% of Indonesians report oral health issues, yet only 10.2% seek treatment [15], reflecting low awareness, economic constraints, and logistical difficulties in accessing care. The shortage and maldistribution of dental professionals exacerbate accessibility issues, with only 33,652 dentists registered as of 2021—most of whom are concentrated in urban centres [16]. As a result, entire communities in rural and remote regions lack direct access to professional dental care, leading to the progression of preventable oral diseases into more severe conditions. While the Universal Health Coverage (UHC) Scheme has made progress in expanding general healthcare access in Indonesia, its impact on dental care remains constrained by economic limitations, workforce shortages, and logistical barriers [14].

The COVID-19 pandemic placed immense pressure on global healthcare systems and severely disrupted dental services. Given the close physical contact involved in dental procedures, routine care was widely suspended, and only emergency treatments were allowed during the height of the crisis [17]. This interruption in service delivery highlighted the fragility of traditional dental care models and emphasised the need for alternative approaches to ensure continuity of care in challenging circumstances. Although the pandemic is no longer the primary concern, it served as a turning point for the broader adoption of telemedicine. In Indonesia, the crisis accelerated the use of digital health platforms, demonstrating the feasibility of remote healthcare delivery and setting the stage for more permanent digital integration [18]. As digital transformation redefines healthcare accessibility, Teledentistry has the potential to revolutionise oral healthcare delivery, ensuring that quality dental care is no longer determined by geographical or financial constraints but is an accessible right for all [13, 15]. While the Indonesian government’s digital health transformation agenda recognises the strategic importance of telemedicine, its practical implementation, particularly in the context of remote healthcare delivery, continues to face systemic and infrastructural challenges. In addition, the integration of telemedicine into oral healthcare remains limited. This study examines the feasibility of a targeted digital oral health intervention, the Pepsodent Teledentistry service, as a potential solution to Indonesia’s unique accessibility barriers and as a contribution to the national objective of achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC) that includes oral health [17].

The Pepsodent Teledentistry service was launched in Indonesia in April 2022 as a digital health initiative to improve access to dental consultations. This study aims to evaluate its effectiveness in enhancing oral health awareness, motivating behaviour change, and driving measurable improvements in oral hygiene practices in Indonesia 3 years after its launch.

Methods

This study adopted a mixed-methods approach involving both (1) a quantitative analysis comparing oral health outcomes between users (test group) and non-users (control group) and (2) a qualitative study involving in-depth interviews with general dentists to explore their perspectives on the platform’s usability and feasibility in dental care delivery.

Within this Teledentistry service, patients initiate their journey by scanning a QR code or dialling a designated number found on product packaging and advertisements. This directs them to a WhatsApp channel (Fig. 1), where they undergo an onboarding process. Initially, patients are required to complete an onboarding form providing personal details, including their name, date of birth, address, and relevant medical information, along with the reason for their consultation. Upon registration, they are placed in a virtual queue and matched with an available dentist. A 15-minute engagement rule was implemented to optimise efficiency, wherein patients who did not receive a response were reassigned, and unresponsive users had their sessions closed. The service involved a WhatsApp-based model, and the consultations were primarily carried out via text messaging, with minimal use of voice, images, or video calls. When necessary, dentists requested images for further assessment. The sessions concluded once all the patient’s concerns were addressed, after which the users were directed to a feedback system to rate their experiences.

For the quantitative evaluation, a longitudinal survey was conducted across six major Indonesian cities- Jakarta, Bandung, Surabaya, Medan, Semarang, and Makassar- targeting individuals aged 18 to 55 from LSM 4 + socioeconomic groups. The LSM (Lifestyle Segmentation Model) 4 + includes individuals from lower-middle to upper-class backgrounds, characterised by their access to modern amenities and exposure to digital health solutions. Participants were recruited through two distinct methods. The test group (n = 300) consisted of first-time users who voluntarily engaged with the Teledentistry service after scanning a QR code printed on Pepsodent product packaging. This method enabled direct access to the service and the associated survey. In contrast, the control group (n = 300) was recruited via an online consumer panel. The test group was completed first, and its demographic profile (age, gender, LSM 4+) was used as a reference to quota-match the control group during recruitment. Post-recruitment, both groups were reviewed to ensure demographic comparability. Participants across both groups met the inclusion criteria of aged 18–55 and LSM 4 or above. A two-phase measurement approach (pre- and post-intervention) was used for both groups, with structured surveys administered before engagement and again after a 21-day cooling period. Over-recruitment was done to account for attrition, and participants were followed up with via calls and SMS reminders to reduce dropouts.

The questionnaire used in this study was developed specifically for this research and was not adopted from a pre-existing validated instrument (see Supplementary File 1). The design process was iterative and involved multiple rounds of review and feedback from internal stakeholders, local implementation teams, and academic partners. At each stage, the draft was revised with particular focus on ensuring clarity, cultural appropriateness, and content alignment with oral health behaviours. Face validity, content validity, and construct validity were assessed during these iterative reviews. No pilot study was conducted to formally test the instrument’s psychometric properties, and the repeated expert consultation process was used to establish conceptual and practical validity. Reliability was measured based on the final sample sizes. The margin of error was approximately 6% for the test group and 5% for the control group, at a 95% confidence level, which indicates acceptable statistical reliability for group-level inferences. The surveys captured self-reported oral health behaviours, awareness, and hygiene practices, with quotas applied for age, gender, and socioeconomic background to ensure comparability between groups.

To complement the quantitative findings, qualitative, semi-structured, in-depth interviews were conducted with six empanelled General Dentists who had participated in the Teledentistry services. The interviews, held online in Bahasa Indonesia, lasted one hour and included dentists from Greater Jakarta, Bogor, and Kepulauan Seribu regions. Discussions explored their professional experiences with the platform, overall satisfaction, patient engagement levels, and the perceived impact of Teledentistry on oral care behaviours. Additionally, dentists provided feedback on onboarding, participation barriers, and potential improvements to enhance adoption for both practitioners and patients.

This study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee for Health Research, Faculty of Dentistry, Universitas Trisakti (Ethical Clearance No: 041/S3/KEPK/FKG/12/2023).

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics summarised demographic characteristics and baseline oral health behaviours, while comparative analysis measured changes in oral health knowledge, motivation, and practices pre-and post-intervention. Our study employed a difference-in-differences (DiD) framework with matched intervention and control groups observed pre- and post-intervention. This design inherently accounts for any confounding factors that are constant over time by focusing on the relative changes in outcomes rather than absolute levels. We also matched participants on key demographics to ensure baseline comparability between groups, further mitigating potential confounding at the outset. Given this setup, we considered the essential confounder control to be achieved through the matched DiD design itself, reducing the added benefit of an additional multivariate regression for time-invariant factors. Chi-square tests were applied to analyse categorical outcomes, while independent samples t-tests were used for continuous variables. Statistical significance was determined at the p < 0.05 level. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 28.

The sample size, while sufficient for our DiD comparisons, was relatively modest for conducting a fully powered multivariable regression. Incorporating multiple covariates in a regression model with a limited sample can substantially reduce statistical power and yield unstable estimates. In small-sample settings, adding many variables risks overfitting the model and may cause significant effects to appear statistically non-significant purely due to insufficient power. Since a multivariate regression under our sample constraints could produce unreliable or uninterpretable results, we opted for the simpler analytic strategy described above to ensure the findings remained robust and interpretable given the data available.

A net impact calculation was performed on key outcomes, including average brushing frequency, dental visit frequency, oral health issues, self-rated oral health, and perceived control over oral health, to quantify the intervention’s effectiveness.

The qualitative analysis employed a structured coding approach to extract insights from in-depth interviews with six dentists. Transcribed and translated data were systematically examined using a directed content analysis, where responses were categorised under predefined macro information areas. These included barriers and challenges (e.g., digital literacy gaps), technical and feature improvements (e.g., optimising communication tools), app usability and perceived experience (e.g., engagement levels and effectiveness), and patient profiles and disease patterns (e.g., oral health awareness). This approach allowed for an in-depth exploration of both user and provider experiences while ensuring alignment with key research objectives.

Results

Of the initial 300 participants in each group, 241 test respondents and 308 control respondents completed the general survey questions. Geographically, the respondents of the quantitative survey were 30% from Jakarta, 15% from Medan, 12% from Semarang, 3% from Makassar, 20% from Bandung, and 20% from Surabaya (Fig. 2). The demographic composition of both groups was broadly similar, with a majority aged 18 to 34 years, and a relatively even distribution across LSM segments 5 to 8. Gender was also balanced across groups (see Table 1). Among test respondents, 49% used the service for general consultation, while 51% sought care for pain or dental issues. Female test respondents were significantly more likely to use Teledentistry for pain-related concerns, whereas male test respondents primarily sought general consultations. While the quantitative results highlight gender differences in service usage, qualitative insights from dentists suggested that service utilisation was higher among female patients.

Qualitative results

Dentists recognised this Teledentistry service as a valuable bridge between self-care and professional treatment, offering patients a flexible and accessible alternative to in-person visits. In their opinion, many patients appreciated the option of remote consultations, finding them less intimidating. The platform’s flexibility was further highlighted by usage peaking on Fridays and around holidays, indicating that patients valued the convenience of digital consultations. Additionally, the Teledentistry service played a role in improving oral health awareness, with urban users often seeking second opinions or clarification on online information.

Despite these benefits, dentists identified certain challenges that limited the platform’s effectiveness. The long-drawn onboarding procedure was identified as a usability hindrance, especially for patients in pain. Some users also experienced longer waiting times (> 15 min), which led to dissatisfaction with the service. Dentists also faced some obstacles, including prank users disrupting workflow and a lack of clear guidelines for unsupervised young users accessing the service. While most dentists adapted well to the platform, older practitioners with limited digital proficiency, found certain features such as image uploads, voice calls, and video consultations, more challenging. Consultations were primarily conducted through text-based chat, as both patients and dentists preferred this format for its efficiency, privacy, and reliable connectivity. While chat allowed for multitasking, it limited the dentists in providing detailed explanations, and video calls remained underutilised due to technical difficulties and prank users. The image-upload feature was widely used but it limited the dentists’ ability to provide accurate diagnoses due to poor image quality. While digital consultations provided a foundation for improved oral care behaviours, dentists emphasised that their long-term impact was limited without integration with in-person services, such as community health centres. Overall, the most significant barriers identified were the long onboarding process, limited diagnostic accuracy due to poor image quality, and disruptive behaviours from prank users.

Discussion

This study evaluated the efficacy of a Teledentistry service in Indonesia using a mixed-methods approach, integrating quantitative analysis of patient engagement with qualitative insights from general dentists. The findings highlighted Teledentistry’s role in addressing both general oral health concerns and pain management. A notable gender-based trend emerged in service utilisation, with female test respondents being more likely to use Teledentistry for pain-related concerns, whereas male respondents primarily sought consultations for general dental concerns. These insights could inform more targeted engagement strategies, such as promoting pain-relief or emergency consultation features more prominently for female users. For male users, messaging may focus more effectively on general dental check-ups or maintenance consultations to align with their usage patterns. The qualitative insights from dentists suggested that female patients were more proactive in engaging with the platform. This finding aligns with results from a study on dental care utilisation in Indonesia between 1999 and 2009, which found that although the number of male respondents was higher, female respondents were more likely to perceive a need for and seek dental care. This trend may be due to women’s greater concern with their health and aesthetics, as well as a higher prevalence of dental caries in females [19].

The quantitative findings also revealed improvements in average weekly brushing frequency and duration among test respondents, potentially due to an increase in oral health awareness post-intervention. Additionally, the frequency of oral health problems decreased, and the intended gap between dental visits was reduced, suggesting that Teledentistry played a role in promoting proactive oral care behaviours. The qualitative analysis reinforced these findings, as dentists observed that some patients, particularly those in rural or underserved regions, relied on Teledentistry as a crucial source of professional guidance. Patients who were self-conscious about their dental condition or hesitant to attend in-person visits valued the ability to seek expert advice. Despite these positive outcomes, test respondents reported a decline in their overall oral health condition. Furthermore, both test and control respondents demonstrated a decline in the perceived control over their oral health, indicating that increased awareness alone may not be sufficient to foster a sense of agency in managing oral health concerns.

The qualitative findings highlighted multiple barriers faced by both dentists and patients based on the dentists’ perspective in using the platform. Dentists expressed concerns over the quality of patient-submitted images, as ensuring proper angling and lighting remained challenging despite providing the best possible guidance. While many dentists found the system user-friendly, digital skill inconsistencies emerged as a major barrier. Older practitioners in particular struggled to navigate features like image uploads and video consultations. These findings align with broader research on Teledentistry adoption, where Indonesian dentists have reported concerns over technological unfamiliarity, diagnostic inaccuracy, and the absence of direct patient contact [20]. Prior studies have also noted apprehension regarding data security and the time required for clinical image capture, further contributing to low acceptance of Teledentistry among dental professionals in Indonesia [21]. Similar challenges have been reported across ASEAN member states, where diagnostic limitations and the lack of standardised training protocols continue to hinder wider Teledentistry implementation [17].

This study helped highlight key challenges that impact not only the effectiveness but also the scalability and long-term sustainability of the Teledentistry service, particularly regarding usability, provider confidence, and patient engagement. On the patient side, the onboarding process posed a significant barrier, as users were required to provide extensive personal details, such as name, date of birth, address, and medical history, before accessing a consultation. This lengthy process, particularly for those in pain, discouraged engagement. Additionally, the absence of technical features, such as a ‘typing’ indicator, sometimes resulted in longer perceived waiting times at the beginning and middle of conversations, contributing to user dissatisfaction. These findings can be further supported by existing research on telemedicine uptake, which highlights that system usability is a key determinant of sustained patient engagement [22]. Individuals are more likely to use digital health platforms when they feature intuitive navigation, clear instructions, and minimal technical friction [22, 23]. The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) provides a useful lens to understand these usability barriers, as it identifies performance expectancy (perceived usefulness), effort expectancy (ease of use), social influence, and facilitating conditions as critical factors influencing technology adoption [24]. In this study, patients’ engagement with Teledentistry was shaped by their perception of its utility in addressing their oral health concerns, as well as the ease or difficulty of navigating the platform. Similarly, dentists with lower digital literacy struggled with advanced features, reinforcing the need for structured training to ensure seamless adoption. Addressing these challenges will be essential to maximising the long-term effectiveness of the Teledentistry service.

Since the findings show that the intervention increased average weekly brushing frequency and oral health awareness, but it did not improve patients’ perceived control over their oral health, this suggests a disconnect between knowledge acquisition and the ability to take meaningful action. The Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) suggests that individuals engage in behaviours when they perceive favourable outcomes and social reinforcement. However, it assumes that behaviours are entirely under individual control [25]. The Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) refines this by introducing perceived behavioural control (PBC), which posits that an individual’s ability to act on their intentions is influenced by external constraints [25]. In line with this, the observed decline in the perceived control over oral health for the test group, despite improved habits, indicates that while patients gained knowledge of oral health practices, the realisation that deeper dental issues such as cavities and gum disease require interventions beyond self-care may have reinforced a sense of powerlessness. This perspective is supported by the qualitative findings, where dentists described Teledentistry as an effective tool for education and prevention but not a substitute for in-person treatment. These insights emphasise the need for stronger integration between Teledentistry and in-person dental care.

To enhance the effectiveness of Teledentistry, the dentists involved in the study survey recommended several technical improvements, including a streamlined onboarding process, a countdown timer, a typing indicator, automated text shortcuts, and image annotation tools. Beyond these enhancements, one of the recommendations for making Teledentistry sustainable involved liaising with the Puskesmas (Community Health Centers) [26], to provide structured referral pathways. While the Puskesmas present a promising avenue for referrals, it remains a challenge due to disparities in infrastructure, internet access, and digital literacy [27]. Additionally, the dentists suggested integrating educational video content within the platform, as this could help address the prevalent dental fear and misinformation in Indonesia [28]. By providing accessible resources on common procedures, oral hygiene practices, and myth-busting facts, these videos could reinforce professional guidance and encourage informed decision-making. Drawing from successful telemedicine models [29], such content could encourage positive health-seeking behaviours and improve patient confidence in oral care.

Several limitations must also be acknowledged. The quantitative analysis relied on self-reported data, which is subject to response bias and potential social desirability bias, and the short follow-up period of 21 days restricted the ability to assess long-term behavioural change. The study’s cross-sectional design and relatively small sample size also limit the generalisability of the findings and may reflect some degree of sampling bias. Formal reliability testing (e.g., Cronbach’s alpha) was not performed, which limits the psychometric strength of the survey instrument. While the DiD framework offers quasi-experimental value, no multivariate regression or control for confounders was implemented, limiting the ability to isolate intervention effects more rigorously. The qualitative component, based on a small sample, may not fully capture the diversity of provider experiences. Furthermore, concerns surrounding data privacy and confidentiality remained a barrier to adoption, as some patients hesitated to disclose sensitive health information via digital platforms.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the effectiveness of a Teledentistry service in Indonesia using a mixed-methods approach, highlighting both its potential benefits and the challenges that hinder its long-term sustainability. The findings demonstrate that Teledentistry increased oral health awareness and improved brushing behaviours among users, reinforcing its value as an accessible digital health intervention. While the service encouraged proactive oral care behaviours and led to shorter intended intervals between dental visits, it did not significantly improve patients’ perceived control over their oral health, and self-rated oral health condition showed a slight decline in the test group. However, this suggests a growing awareness among users of the need for continued efforts and professional support to achieve better oral health outcomes. Since knowledge acquisition alone is insufficient to empower individuals in managing their oral health, this emphasises the need for stronger integration between digital and in-person dental services. Additionally, persistent usability barriers such as long onboarding processes, technical limitations, and remote diagnosis challenges highlight the need to refine the platform. These improvements will be essential to ensure effective delivery of care in future implementations.

Future research should prioritise robust methodologies to mitigate potential biases and provide a more comprehensive evaluation of Teledentistry’s impact. Additionally, further studies should examine Teledentistry’s role in rural and underserved communities to better understand its potential in addressing oral healthcare inequalities. Given the growing role of digital health in oral care, future evaluations could also apply the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) framework to systematically assess factors influencing Teledentistry adoption and engagement. Clinically, findings from future research could inform integration models for hybrid care, while educationally, they may guide digital literacy training for both patients and dental professionals.
