Mothers’ perceptions of bonding with their newborns in different care settings: a descriptive qualitative study
Authors: Raymonde Gagnon, Diane St-Laurent, Amélie Garban, Carl Lacharité
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPregnancy and birth prepare the mother to enter into a relationship with and care for her baby, both through expectation of the child and the intense physiological hormonal changes associated with these processes. The birth environment likely plays a role in establishing the mother’s bond with the newborn. The aim of this study is to document the bonding process in ordinary birth situations (without medical complications) by describing the representations and experiences of primiparous mothers regarding the development of the bond with their newborn within a continuum of temporality from birth to the first two postnatal months, according to type of birth (natural or epidural) and context of care (medical-nursing team or midwives).MethodsIn this interpretative descriptive qualitative study, interviews were held with 26 primiparous Quebec women, divided into three groups according to purposive sampling: (1) birth with epidural and medical-nursing team in hospital (n = 10); (2) natural birth with medical-nursing team in hospital (n = 6); and (3) natural birth with midwife team in a birth center, home or hospital (n = 10). A vertical, horizontal and cross-sectional analysis was conducted.ResultsThe results were divided into two parts. The first examined how mothers described the bond with their babies within a temporal continuum: (1) pregnancy: love and connection; (2) birth: spontaneous love; (3) first moments: discovery and sense of belonging; (4) first 24 h: feeling of responsibility; (5) first week: love that develops over time. The second part addressed when the bond emerged and how it evolved according to the context of care and type of birth, within the same temporal continuum until two months after birth. A delay was observed in bonding. The bond developed during pregnancy and birth for women who gave birth naturally, and later for those who gave birth with an epidural. “Felt experience” appeared to contribute to bonding. The delay seemed to disappear after the first few weeks.ConclusionsEncouraging mother-centered practices that enhance self-efficacy and self-confidence, with a focus on “felt experience,” regardless of the birth context, may help mothers establish a bond with their child from birth.

Background

Central to the complex process of the transition to parenthood, birth is a significant and transformative human event, for parents and newborns alike. The first encounter with the child represents a key moment in the development of the mother-child relationship. The process of creating this relationship brings into play the concept of bonding. This concept, introduced by the research of Rubin in 1967 [1, 2] and developed by Klaus and Kennell [3, 4], is defined as a process initiated by the mother toward her newborn and refers to the emotional bond that the mother develops with her baby [5, 6]. It is described as a dynamic emotional state created during pregnancy or at birth [5, 7], that continues to develop during the first months of the child’s life [7–9]. Bonding includes the mother’s feelings and emotions towards her baby. This concept has given rise to debates and objections (overemphasis on the importance of immediate physical proximity between mother and newborn, and excessive pressure on mothers). However, other studies have since nuanced these debates, provided a better understanding of this concept, and assessed its relevance [10, 11]. Bowlby’s attachment theory offers an integrative framework for understanding the bonding process. According to Bowlby, from birth, children are predisposed to develop an attachment with the person who gives them care and affection. Similarly, parents are also biologically prepared to form an emotional bond with their newborns, protect them, offer them comfort and provide the care necessary for their survival and wellbeing (referred to as the caregiving system) [12, 13]. While several studies on bonding have examined associations with maternal mental health [14–16], few have focused on the relational processes in ordinary situations of bonding. There is a paucity of research exploring how mothers describe and develop an emotional bond with their baby within a continuum of temporality (timeframes) from birth up to the first few months postpartum, and the factors that may influence this process, such as type of birth.

Pregnancy and birth prepare the mother to establish a relationship with and care for her child. The necessary conditions come together for this process to occur, including the expectation of the child and the intense hormonal and physiological changes that activate maternal impulses and influence sensory acuity [17]. These major changes prepare the woman for the transition to motherhood. The birth experience also plays a role [18, 19]. During labor, hormonal mechanisms are triggered to facilitate birth and prepare the fetus to adapt to life outside the womb [20, 21]. The birth itself and the mother’s interpretation of the birth may interact, positively or negatively influencing the emotional bond she develops toward her newborn [19, 22].

At birth, discovery is the interaction process whereby the mother gets to know her infant, allowing her to better understand the expression of her infant’s needs [23, 24]. This process of mutual discovery, which underlies bonding, is prominent during the perinatal period [25]. The first step of discovery is the detection of the child’s signals [26, 27], which relies on the mother’s attention. She must be physically and psychologically available [27]. The first few hours after birth represent a key moment: the mother’s sensitivity is heightened, especially as a result of the hormones that are released [28].

The crucial moments in the initiation of mothering depend on a wide range of factors. In addition to the personal characteristics of parent and infant, social and contextual characteristics [29–31] also play a role in the experience and exercise of parenthood [32]. The environment of the birth is prominent in how mothers will connect with their children in the first moments of life. The individuals present additionally play an important role during this crucial transition period [33–35].

A study conducted across 13 high-income countries showed that epidural rates vary from 10 to 63% [36]. Epidural use is on the rise in Canada, from 53.2% in 2006–2007 to 65% in 2023, and more than 77% in Quebec [37]. Epidural is often trivialized by both health professionals and women, since the associated medical complications appear to be thoroughly controlled, despite the documented effect of the accompanying cascade of procedures [38–40].

Yet epidural administration diminishes the release of endogenous oxytocin [41–43], a hormone that plays an important role during birth. Previous research has focused mainly on the physical effects of endogenous oxytocin on the production of uterine contractions, the fetal and placental ejection reflex after birth, and the ejection of breast milk [44]. This hormone is also secreted during intercourse, orgasm and lactation. Sometimes called the love hormone [43], oxytocin has been found to have psychological effects such as alleviating pain [45] and promoting attachment and calm [44, 45]. Postnatally, skin-to-skin contact with the baby, eye contact and touch increase oxytocin production [44]. Associations have been found between oxytocin and bonding [46]. When no disturbance is present in the mother, the release of this hormone helps promote a positive relationship and create an optimal bond with the infant [47]. Oxytocin acts on the maternal brain by playing a role in the initiation of the mother-child bond and maternal emotional responses toward the child [48].

During epidurals, synthetic oxytocin must be administered to offset the lack of natural oxytocin, yet its effects have been little studied [49]. Some research indicates that synthetic oxytocin may interfere with successful breastfeeding [50–53] and that it affects newborns’behavior during the first hour [54]. Other hormones are also secreted during natural childbirth. Endorphins in particular can trigger feelings of pleasure and euphoria [20], and play a role in mother-infant attachment, as they do in mammals [55]. In addition, the low cortisol levels associated with epidurals may have negative impacts on bonding [56].

When mothers give birth with an epidural, the environment and context surrounding birth are also quite different and can undoubtedly influence the first contact between mothers and their babies. When the mother is under epidural anesthesia, the pain disappears, but also her uterine and vaginal sensations, as does her ability to move about. She is placed under medical supervision and fetal monitoring and receives intravenous medication. A medical team works with her to let her know when she is having contractions and how she should push to deliver her baby, unlike in physiological childbirth, where her bodily sensations take precedence.

Much remains to be done to better understand whether and how obstetric interventions and professional attitudes can influence these precious moments and their postnatal repercussions. Is the mother’s experience the same regardless of the birth context? Does this affect the bonding process?

The structure of services in Quebec offers an opportunity to shed light on this issue in distinct contexts. The public health network system authorizes birth either in a hospital with or without an epidural in the presence of a medical-nursing team (an obstetrician-gynecologist or general practitioner, and nurses), or with midwives in a birth center, at home or in hospital. Quebec’s birth centers are part of the public network and are essentially under the clinical responsibility of midwives. Midwife-assisted births are natural, and if an epidural is required, the woman is transferred to a hospital’s medical-nursing team. In hospital births with an obstetrician-gynecologist or general practitioner, nurses provide care to the woman during labor, birth and the immediate postnatal period.

In this exploratory study we seek to better understand how the bonding process unfolds according to the type of birth and the context of care. Our main objective was to describe the representations and experiences of primiparous mothers regarding the development of the bond with their newborn within a continuum of temporality from birth up to two months thereafter, according to type of birth (natural or epidural) and context of care (medical-nursing team or midwives).

Methods

Study design and participants

This qualitative study explored mothers’ individual perceptions of their first contact with their newborn, according to type of birth and care setting, as well as the emotional bond they developed during the first two months after birth. The choice to meet with primiparous mothers allowed us to study the establishment of the first mother-child relationship. Based on the principle of purposive sampling [57], we recruited primiparous participants for three groups: (1) birth with epidural and a medical-nursing team in a hospital center (MnTP); (2) natural birth with a medical-nursing team in a hospital center (MnTN); and (3) natural birth with a midwife team in a birth center, at home or in a hospital (MT). Medical-nursing team refers to a team made up of doctors (gynecologists-obstetricians or general practitioners) and nurses.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants in this study had to be healthy primiparous women over 18 years of age whose birth had taken place between 37 and 41 weeks with a medical-nursing team or with midwives. To be eligible for the study, they must not have had a medically induced labor, an instrument-assisted birth (forceps or vacuum extractor), a cesarean section, or a complication that, under the care of a midwife, would have required transfer to a doctor.

Data collection

Participants were recruited through social media, community organizations in perinatal care and posters put up in two hospital centers and a birth center (a setting where follow-up with a midwife is provided, regardless of the place of birth). The two hospitals and the birth center where recruitment took place belonged to the same regional health and social services center. All participants who contacted the research team were included in the study if inclusion and exclusion criteria were met.

Each participant signed a consent form to participate in the study, from which she was free to withdraw at any time. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Université du Québec à Trois-Rivières [CER-22-284-10.02] and the Ethics Committee of the Centre intégré universitaire de santé et de services sociaux de la Mauricie-et-du-Centre-du-Québec (CIUSSS-MCQ) [certificate number: CER-2022-571]), the regional health and social services center that collaborated in the recruitment.

Individual interviews were conducted with the participants two months after birth. We collected their perceptions of their first contact with their newborn, their experiences of childbirth, their perceptions of the relationship established with their child during the first two postnatal months and, more broadly, their comments on their pregnancy. Prior to the in-person interview, they completed a socio-demographic questionnaire online, as well as the Postpartum Bonding Questionnaire (PBQ) [58, 59]. The PBQ consists of 25 items regarding the mother’s feelings toward her child (e.g., my baby makes me feel anxious; I enjoy playing with my baby; I feel distant from my baby; my baby irritates me; I feel happy when my baby smiles or laughs). The information provided by the mother was not compiled as questionnaire data, but was rather used as a basis for discussion during the interview. Each interview included a semi-structured section with open-ended questions focusing on their experience, and mainly on the first contact with their newborn, their childbirth and their relationship with the baby in the first two months.

The interviews also included a structured section, during which questions from the PBQ were used to further explore the mother’s relationship with her child. These items were presented to the mother, who told us which ones best reflected her experience with her baby. The mothers’ comments surrounding the items of the PBQ were analyzed as an integral part of the interview. The PBQ psychometric scale itself was not analyzed; the focus of the study was on the content of the interview, including the comments regarding the PBQ. The copy of the birth record was used to complete the data (e.g., administration of synthetic oxytocin). The use of these various instruments and sources of information contributed to data triangulation [60].

The interviews (approximately 60 to 90 min) took place face-to-face at the mothers’ homes, with one exception (by videoconference). They were conducted by the principal researcher and two research assistants with prior training in qualitative interviewing. Each interview was audio recorded. Field notes were also written after each interview.

More than half had a post-secondary education and were employed in the healthcare, administration or teaching sectors. For half of the participants, family income was equal to or higher than the median annual family income in Quebec (i.e., CAD 86,000) [61], and for the other half, it was lower. The average duration of pregnancy ranged from 39.2 to 40.4 weeks, and labor lasted approximately seven hours. Nine out of the 10 participants who gave birth with an epidural received oxytocin, mainly after the epidural. All but one of those who gave birth naturally with the medical-nursing team received oxytocin after birth, while eight out of the ten women who gave birth with a midwife did not. Complications during labor and birth were present in nine out of ten cases for the MnTP group, two out of six cases for MnTN women, and three out of ten cases for MT women. Similarly, procedures on babies were more frequent in the MnTP group than in the other two groups (nine MnTP, one MnTN and two MT). All but two mothers (one MnTP and one MnTN) breastfed.

Data analysis

After the interviews were transcribed, we conducted a six-step thematic analysis of the data: (1) pre-analysis or familiarization with the data corpus (initial reading and identification of general ideas and first impressions); (2) open and inductive coding (segmenting into meaning units and assigning codes); (3) categorization (grouping codes into coherent sets and creating thematic categories); (4) continuous thematization [62] (grouping categories into central themes), which we also organized in a temporal logic; (5) establishing relations among themes; and (6) interpretation.

The interviews were subjected to vertical and horizontal thematic analysis [60, 62]. NVivo software was used to facilitate data coding, categorization and comparison between each group. We used matrices to cross-reference the thematic codes of the three groups. The temporality (timeframes) analysis was conducted based on the content of the participants’ discourse. The timeframes were pregnancy, labor, delivery, birth, first moments (i.e., the first two hours after birth), the first 24 h (2 h to 24 h after birth), the first week at home, the first two months, and, finally, after 2 months. Then, a cross-sectional analytical approach was taken with respect to the themes describing the relationship established by the mother with her baby, in order to reveal convergences and divergences in experiences.

Finally, in a subsequent step, the analytical questioning approach [63] was used to highlight the main explanatory factors for the differences observed, with one question leading to another. How did the relationship with the newborn develop at the moment of birth in each group? In the first few hours? The first few weeks? The first few months? Was there a difference after two months? How was the contact with the baby during labor and birth ? What emotions were expressed at the different timeframes? This questioning allowed us to describe the mother-infant bond at different timeframes and for each group. The results of this initial analytical process led us to further investigate the moment at which each participant expressed the beginning of the bond with her baby. We then put this information in a table according to temporality (timeframes) and group. We also indicated when there were special cases (e.g., a variation in the bond for some mothers). As we delved deeper, we realized that women regularly mentioned their pregnancies, leading us to look into this phase to gather data, which we had not initially intended to explore. While the sample size was relatively small, the analysis indicated that data saturation had been attained, as further accounts did not add substantially novel insights beyond those already identified.

The principal investigator (RG) and a doctoral research assistant (LRL) conducted an initial phase of coding and thematic analysis, which was subsequently completed and analyzed by the principal investigator (RG) and a second doctoral research assistant (AG) from a discipline different from that of the other researchers. The codes, categories, and themes were discussed regularly at each stage of the analysis and validated during debriefing meetings with the two other researchers (CL and DS). Whenever it was deemed necessary, we returned to the interview data to verify the context of the excerpts. The reflexive stance adopted by the research team members made it possible to consider different perspectives and ensure the necessary distance required for data interpretation. The research’s strong grounding in the data, illustrated by numerous participant verbatim excerpts, as well as the detailed analysis of the data, contributed to the credibility of the results presented.

The analytical framework was based on the bonding process [5, 6]. This process helped shed light on mothers’ representations of their babies and understand how they perceived themselves as women and as mothers in relation to the development of the affective bond with their infant. Through this analytical lens, we examined mothers’ perceptions within a continuum of temporality from birth up to two months postpartum, and the factors that might influence the mothers’ bond, such as the context and type of birth.

Results

Results are divided into two parts. The first describes how mothers talked about and described the bond established with their baby within a continuum of temporality, from pregnancy to the first week after birth. The second presents the moment when the bond first manifested itself and how it evolved from birth to two months postpartum, according to context of care and type of birth in each of the three groups of participants.

Mothers’ descriptions of the bond within different timeframes

This section presents how mothers represented and described the bond with their baby (feelings, emotions) within a continuum of temporality, from pregnancy to the first week at home following birth.

During pregnancy: love and connection

Several participants mentioned a feeling of love starting from pregnancy. For some, it was instantaneous and characterized by wonder: “Seriously, in that moment, the moment I saw ‘pregnant.’ It was just a magical moment. It’s really like a Wow. You feel so happy.” (P20-MT). This was also the case for P14-MnTN who did not want to get attached to her baby for fear of miscarriage, and who ultimately mentioned that this was impossible for her. She was already “into what was coming” and developed an instant bond as soon as she had her positive pregnancy test. Conversely, it could also be a process that evolved over time: “The love grew with time” P1-MnTP. Others projected themselves into the future through the baby: “We imagined a little baby you know super uh…. We’re kind of relaxed in life, so a relaxed, quiet little baby who likes people…” (P5-MnTP)1.

Other participants who expressed a bond during pregnancy described it as a connection, in particular MnTN and MT participants. The connection was very much present for P24-MT: “My feeling with her in my belly, I feel like we were just connected.” For P26-MT, being attentive and attuned to her baby’s movements made her feel connected: “It’s really been a beautiful pregnancy. I felt connected to him.” Another MnTN participant described a special time of closeness that she felt with her daughter. Another mentioned a strong connection, yet an abstract one, because of the difficulty imagining her baby:

Well, actually, for me it was pretty abstract, that is, before I gave birth, I knew I was pregnant, but I didn’t really have an image of the baby or anything. So I felt connected, but I still had trouble imagining.” (P27-MnTN).

Well, actually, for me it was pretty abstract, that is, before I gave birth, I knew I was pregnant, but I didn’t really have an image of the baby or anything. So I felt connected, but I still had trouble imagining.” (P27-MnTN).

P20-MT spoke of a feeling of trust in herself and her baby: “From the beginning, I would say: I have confidence in my daughter and I have confidence in my body. They know best what needs to be done.” Two MT participants developed a bond that they described as special by being able to nourish and protect the baby inside their womb, which also created for one mother a feeling of belonging. In contrast, one participant did not develop an attachment bond during pregnancy, as she considered that her baby “wasn’t there”. (P9-MnTP).

During labor, delivery and birth: spontaneous love

For other participants, the bond was more likely to appear spontaneously at birth. Giving birth allowed P11-MT to establish contact with her baby: “Giving birth, basically, it’s when you make contact.”; and it allowed P17-MT to create a connection: “When she came out, she’s there, she’s with us, she is her own little person, and the real connection was made there, before even seeing her.”

Two other women, who had established a bond during pregnancy, reported feeling like they formed a team with their baby during birth: “I’d say we were both very strong to give birth. We made a good team during the birth, both of us.” (P12-MT). Another mother, P16-MnTN, realized that her baby was an individual in his own right, who was also experiencing this moment: “You know, your baby also goes through the trauma of birth, and he works hard to get out as much as you work hard to help get him out.”

At birth, participant P29-MT reported feeling proud that her newborn resembled her. Two participants mentioned a love that continued to develop gradually after pregnancy, including P12-MT: “I was moved, but I wasn’t yet in love like I am today. Usually when something new happens, it takes a few days for it to sink in for me. Then I go, ‘Oh Wow! We had a baby and I just gave birth.”

First moments after birth: discovery and sense of belonging

The first moments are a time of discovery for mothers: discovery of their baby and their first breastfeeding. One MT participant mentioned a feeling of connection (P8-MT) in the first moments after giving birth. Another mother, P13-MnTP, expressed the beginning of a bond that manifested itself as a look of judgment that she perceived from her baby: “She was judging me.… She looked at me with so much judgment. That little eyebrow. It was strange, but at the time I was like, does she love me?”. Other MnTP and MT participants who had already created a bond reported a feeling of belonging and possession in the early moments after birth, and another mentioned that love came from caring for and breastfeeding her baby.

First 24 h after birth: feeling of responsibility

It seemed that during the first 24 h (at the birth center or at home for MT participants and during the hospital stay for MnTP and MnTN groups [birth with medical-nursing team]), most mothers developed a feeling of responsibility toward their baby. For several women in the MnTP and MnTN groups, this could be manifested in the form of concern for the baby’s health and safety. This concern, both real and hypothetical, was due in part to medical protocols and the feeling of not being in control of things due to a lack of information. However, such fears were not mentioned by the MT participants. One of them, faced with a real health problem for her child, remained confident in managing the situation.

First week at home: love that develops over time

Two participants in the MnTP group talked about developing a bond with their baby during the first week. They described it as a love that developed and improved over time. One of them, P7-MnTP, explained with hindsight that she felt in love from day one, and that this love only grew with time: “We had the impression that we already loved him to the max from day one, but now I know that wasn’t the case in retrospect. It wasn’t quite as strong.” P18-MnTP mentioned that, although her baby was a “stranger,” she felt a sense of belongingand a feeling of love, which developed during the first few weeks: “I immediately felt a sense of belonging and love for this baby, even if it was a bit of a stranger at first. … And to this very day, it only gets stronger and better.”

Regarding this phase, however, mothers in all groups talked less about the bond with their baby, even for those who had already established one. This seemed to be linked to a necessary adjustment period upon returning home, where they were more likely to be searching for their bearings. We now turn to how mothers first referred to a meaningful bond with their baby and how this bond evolved over time, depending on the type of birth and the context of care.

In each group, we recorded the moment when each participant first mentioned a significant bond with her baby (see Table 3). We examined only the timeframes when the mothers in each group talked about a bond with their baby (emergence and evolution).

It is worth noting that “baby blues” can prevent the development of the bond before the first few months. Mother-reported baby blues refer to a transient emotional state in which the mother often feels anxious, vulnerable and prone to mood swings. This differs from postnatal depression, which is based on a diagnosis [64]. This was the case for participant P10-MnTP, who mentioned a bond with her baby only during the first months after birth. She was aware of the baby’s presence during pregnancy, but regarded him as a “stranger” and did not mention any particular connection with him during this period. Only in the first few months after birth did she begin to have fun with her baby and develop a relationship. “I still think in hindsight it looks like a post-traumatic issue . It’s as if little bits of myself have come together and I’m able to have fun.”

P2-MnTN, who also reported experiencing baby blues, began to build a bond with her baby after 2 months: “At first it was a lot of questioning, and ups and downs. But you know, as soon as that whole phase was over, that’s when we really created something. You know, I really feel a good bond together.” She mentioned that the development of a bond only began after two months, as she became more at ease with her baby: “When I became more confident and was able to start going out and everything. That’s when…. Well, not feeling like a prisoner anymore, that really strengthens the bond. (P2-MnTN).

Participants who gave birth with epidural and a Medical-nursing Team (MnTP)

In the MnTP group, two participants mentioned creating a bond with their baby from pregnancy. For these women, the bond began when they felt the baby through their belly: “I already had a strong bond with her. You know, I would stroke my belly… and you know I felt her clearly” (P4-MnTP); “When we felt movements I would say, now he’s doing yoga, he’s practicing soccer, we tried to imagine him.…” (P5-MnTP). It should be noted that these two MnTP participants, who expressed a bond starting from pregnancy, stand out from the other MnTP participants. One was heading to a natural birth in a birth center and ended up having to have an epidural (breech baby). The other mentioned from pregnancy that she wanted a non-medicalized birth, and she was therefore supported accordingly.

During labor and delivery, the MnTP participants did not really express a bond with their fetus. Most of them were not focused on the birth. Only one mentioned being connected to herself during the delivery phase while following the instructions and encouragement of the staff and her husband (“Go! Go! Go!”). P5-MnTP thought about the fact that she was about to give birth to the child she had been expecting for nine months, and imagined this moment as the “most beautiful thing in the world.” She felt both excited and feverish. Most of them slept through labor until the delivery. Participants P4-MnTP and P13-MnTP mentioned a feeling of strangeness that may have been linked to not really realizing what was going on with their baby. This might also be due to the birth context, as they were focused more on their appearance (nudity and preoccupation with modesty), which could cause discomfort (P6-MnTP). One participant expressed a particular experience of imagining herself in her baby’s place and seeing herself being “pushed toward the light” (P10-MnTP). Finally, during the delivery, there was an alteration of the bond created during pregnancy for P4-MnTP. She explained that not having felt anything gave her the impression that the baby lying on her was not hers; she did not feel a sense of belonging.

Optimal conditions did not appear to be in place to encourage the development of the bond at the time of birth in the MnTP group. These women described it as an event in which they were passive: the newborn was placed on the mother, positioned quickly for breastfeeding. In addition to this passivity, mothers talked about how, under anesthesia, they struggled to realize that their baby was born: “I had the epidural and it’s like I was still a bit buzzed.” (P19-MnTP).These participants reported emotions linked to a situation of “strangeness” generated by this context. They appeared to need extra time to come to terms with the birth and become fully conscious of it.

Regarding the first moments after birth, few women talked about the emotional bond with their baby. Some spoke of fuzzy memories: one mentioned difficulty recalling this moment, linked to medication, and another referred to the epidural. Some expressed the difficulty of realizing the baby’s presence. The context of childbirth (medical protocol, medical staff, hospital rhythm and routines, care for the baby, etc.) may have interfered with the mother’s spontaneity and the baby’s rhythm, and therefore with the development of their bond. For example, one participant reported being shocked to discover her baby, but was rushed because of the organization of care at the hospital. The same lack of spontaneity can be seen during first-time breastfeeding. MnTP participants had to help their babies latch on, or ask for assistance, which they either received (one woman explained how the nurses “plugged” the baby to the breast) or did not. Thus, they experienced mixed feelings of joy, satisfaction and relief (at having had a good pregnancy and birth) on the one hand, and worry about the child’s health or possible complications that could arise (e.g., baby not crying enough) on the other.

This impression persisted for the first 24 h (which took place in the hospital). For mothers who talked about this timeframe, contact with the baby was established visually, and some paid critical attention to their baby’s appearance. P1-MnTP spoke of an interaction with her baby, which she considered to be one-way: “I don’t think he knew he was alive. He didn’t know he was in the world. We never heard the baby, never. Never, never. I could hear other people’s babies crying and mine wasn’t.” This is in contrast with P5-MnTP, who had established a bond during pregnancy, and who mentioned an ongoing bond with the baby that was inside her and that she could now meet.

For P10-MnTP and P13-MnTP, during the first few days, their baby remained a stranger that they needed to get to know. These mothers mentioned the need to be close to their child during their stay at the hospital (the needed to have the baby with them, to create a “cocoon,” to be alone with the baby to create a relationship). This was also reflected in their emotions; they could express their joy more once they were in the room with their partner.

From the first week at home, what allowed P7-MnTP and 18-MnTP to develop a connection, which had not really been created before, was to care for their baby: “The reflexes kicked in. Having to take care of my baby, of everything, yes.” (P7-MnTP).

The first month appeared to have been a pivotal moment for the MnTP participants in establishing a bond with their child (see Table 3). It was during that period that MnTP participants most developed a bond with their baby through interactions. For P19-MnTP, this began with the baby’s smiles: “Then when she started making her first smiles. That’s love right there. It’s crazy. I don’t remember… I’ve been trying to figure out when was the first time she smiled, but it was very early. That really strengthened the bond.” Another participant, P6-MnTP, explained that love came with the care she provided to her baby. She reported having been lucky to establish a relationship in the first few months, which she believed was helped by having a “lively” baby who slept through the night: “I also feel privileged to have been able to develop a relationship with her so quickly, considering that she sleeps through the night, that she’s a lively baby and everything. So I think that made it easier.” For another, experiencing good times with her baby contributed to the relationship: “We’re able to have fun together, and what’s really helpful is that the more the days or even hours go by, the more she’s interacting. … So there you go, I think it really helps.” (P10-MnTP).

After two months, interactions with the baby reinforced the development of the bond for MnTP mothers, through gaze, facial expressions, smiles and laughter in response to the mother’s promptings.

Participants who gave birth naturally with a Medical-nursing Team (MnTN)

For MnTN participants, the bond was established mainly during pregnancy. This bond was forged in a variety of ways. For one mother, it was during the ultrasound: “That’s when you realize. Because you know, yes, there’s a living being there, and she’s going to arrive. So, from then on, I started talking to her more, rubbing my belly more, trying to have a little connection with her.” (P16-MnTN). For P25-MnTN and P14-MnTN, the trigger was feeling the baby’s movements. Touching her belly, massaging it, and talking to the baby helped to maintain the bond, or even create it: “I used to talk to her when she was in my belly…, I was quite happy. We would take photos at a few weeks, a few months and then see how the belly was evolving.” (P25 MnTN).

For P15-MnTN, it was when she felt her baby descend during delivery that she created an attachment bond: “I felt I had worked hard to have the baby. It’s like… I physically felt that I had pushed the baby. It felt like a direct attachment. You know, you’re the one who came out of there” (P15-MnTN). During the delivery, other MnTN mothers were very focused on themselves and what they were experiencing (fatigue, pain, giving birth too quickly). Several also mentioned their baby’s participation in the delivery phase and in the birth. Some expressed a strange sensation linked to what was taking place in their body at the time of delivery, and to their relation with time and reality. Contractions allowed them to “communicate with their baby” (P27-MnTN). Contact was made by “feeling” the baby, which seemed to play an important role in making birth concrete for mothers, who became aware that their child was being born.

Participants who mentioned emotions during birth referred to love, whether instantaneous or gradual, joy and pride, as well as their connection with the baby despite the environment which, as for the women in the MnTP group, may not have been the most favorable to developing their bond. Some participants said that feeling everything contributed to their relationship with their baby. Conversely, for P25-MnTN, who had developed a bond during pregnancy, this contact was difficult to maintain given the swiftness of the birth and the context, which underlines the importance of feelings and sensations:

I wasn’t really aware that it had taken place in two hours, but I remember it was strange. You feel like your body doesn’t belong to you anymore. I don’t know, it’s kind of weird. You’re getting stitches, but you’ve got this little curled-up ball lying on you. All warm. At the same time. But I don’t know. It’s like it happened super fast.

The first moments following childbirth passed quickly; without having had time to fully realize what had taken place, mothers were already on to the next stage in the flow of things at the hospital. And yet, several participants mentioned the need to “collect themselves.” The physical relationship with the baby (appearance, contact with the breast / breastfeeding, discovery of the baby’s body), through the sensations it provided (look and touch), could trigger an emotional response: “It was when I had my first breastfeeding, the two hours after giving birth, that it became clear that I was the mother.” (P15-MnTN). For several MnTN participants, discovering and meeting their baby was a highlight. They mainly expressed joy and pride in the first moments after birth. The following elements were mentioned by mothers and seemed to reinforce their feeling of confidence: being proud to have felt the baby come out, feeling proud and strong to have given birth naturally, to have done this together with the baby, and that the baby latched on by themselves.

During the first 24 h (which took place in hospital), participants in the MnTN group mentioned the need for intimacy and closeness to support the development of a bond. This bond was created in different ways for different mothers: for one mother it was when she was providing care to her baby, for another it was once the excitement had subsided around her, and for a third it was once she was reassured of the absence of complications.

After two months, MnTN participants mentioned greater closeness and reciprocity as contributing to strengthening the bond with their baby.

Participants who gave birth naturally with a Midwife Team (MT)

For the MT participants, the bond with their baby mainly began during pregnancy. “Right away, I went to touch my belly. I touched [baby’s name] and I was like, ‘My baby!’” (P20-MT). For two others, love developed through protecting and nourishing their baby within their body: “I sort of took care of her a lot…. The moment is a highlight for mothers, a moment of encountering their baby, described as being “beyond words and wonderful” (P23-MT). Data analysis showed that the women’s experiences of bonding varied according to the context of care and type of birth, with a different temporality of bonding in each group. Whereas most of MT and MnTN participants mentioned a bond with their baby between pregnancy and the first moments after birth, MnTP participants mainly mentioned it when they returned home and in the ensuing weeks. Discussion

In this exploratory study, we sought to better understand the unfolding of the bonding process according to type of birth and context of care. The main objective was to describe the representations and experiences of primiparous mothers concerning the development of the bond with their newborn within a temporal continuum from birth up to two months postpartum depending on the type of birth (natural or epidural) and the care context (medical-nursing team or midwives).

The birth experience and its subjective interpretation play an important role in the creation of the bond between mothers and their baby [65, 66]. Study participants talked about the first moments spent with their babies, as well as their pregnancy, the birth and the relationship they developed with their baby in the first two months after birth. Few studies have examined how mothers describe and develop their emotional bond with their babies within a continuum of temporality from birth up to two months postpartum and the factors that may be associated. The intent of our study was to help fill this gap.

Some mothers described the bond with their baby in terms of love and connection during pregnancy, while others spoke of spontaneous love during labor and birth. In the moments immediately after birth, mothers reported a sense of belonging, whereas in the first 24 h, they talked about developing a feeling of responsibility toward their child. Finlayson et al. [67] explain that the weight of responsibility, perceived as anxiety-provoking, can cause the bond with the infant to emerge in the first 24 h without the mother feeling immediate love. Among the mothers we met with, love continued to develop over time during the first weeks at home, and they mentioned more their attachment bond through interactions with the baby from the first month onwards (see Fig. 1).

Analysis of mothers’ descriptions of the bond in the interviews uncovered elements that converged or diverged between the three groups of mothers, leading to the conclusion of timing differences in the creation of the bond between mothers and their baby, depending on the context of birth. MnTN and MT participants talked about the emergence of a bond with their baby during pregnancy, birth and the first moments after birth. Conversely, MnTP women said almost nothing about bonding during these periods; rather, it was in the weeks following birth that they mentioned the development of a bond with their child.

Results suggest that the mothers in the three groups experienced different care paradigms where oxytocin and epidural administration may play a role. Felt experience also appeared to be an important dimension of the first contact with the child. In addition, the care context and type of birth may have had a role to play in mothers’ sense of confidence and self-efficacy, which in turn might have played a role in the establishment of the bond with their baby.

Bonding and the birth care paradigm

Our study highlights the link between mothers’ representations, the way they relate to the baby, and the care paradigms that they may have experienced. Technocratic, humanistic and holistic models [68] translate into different approaches to pregnancy and birth, for both practitioners and the women or pregnant people who are culturally socialized into these visions [69, 70]. For example, Behruzi et al.’s [70] study conducted in a specialized hospital in Quebec, highlighted the influence of components of organizational culture on the humanization or medicalization of childbirth practices.

Our findings indicated that participants who developed a bond during pregnancy and birth seemed to have experienced a care context more in line with a holistic or humanistic paradigm. This manifested itself in mothers touching their belly, talking to the baby, and feeling the baby’s movements during pregnancy, which was encouraged by the midwife, for those who had one. The practice of midwifery in Quebec is rooted in the holistic or at least humanistic model [71], which was reflected in the discourse of our participants. Even though the birth process seemed to have led MnTN and MT mothers to be very focused on themselves, and to experience fatigue and pain, they reported teaming up with their baby, and that their baby participated in their own birth. They fully experienced the birth by being totally involved and immersed in the moment. They felt a sense of pride and accomplishment in having brought the baby into the world themselves.

By contrast, MnTP participants’ attention seemed to be shifted away from their babies and mainly focused on the risks and the external environment, to the detriment of creating an early bond with the baby. They were focused on medical aspects and seemed disconnected from their childbirth, as evidenced by an absence of felt experience and the presence of outside distractions. We may assume that they were in a care environment where pregnancy and birth are seen as potentially pathological, and which emphasizes the risks for the unborn baby. This corresponds to the medical or technocratic model first described by the anthropologist Davis-Floyd [72], but also found in recent studies, for example in Sweden [73], Ireland [74] and Finland [75]. Goldkuhl et al. [73] highlighted the power imbalance between institutional requirements and women’s needs, emphasizing the crucial role of birth philosophy in fostering safe birthing environments. Likewise, Huschke [74] underscored how technocratic hospital cultures and risk-oriented discourses surrounding childbirth often undermine informed decision-making during pregnancy and labor.

Our results are coherent with previous research which suggests that experiencing the events of pregnancy, birth and the first moments with the newborn in a humanistic or holistic environment appears to optimize bonding conditions, unlike having to follow the pace imposed by the hospital and lacking the intimate conditions conducive to the development of the emotional bond with the infant [73, 76]. Our findings are also in line with Gaboury et al. [77] who emphasized that hospital routines and physical environment were not consistent with parents’ expectations. Respectful, person-centered perinatal care is important for improving the birth experience and strengthening the mother’s relationship with her baby, from the beginning of pregnancy [78, 79].

Felt experience and bonding

The results of our analysis underscore the importance of mother’s felt experience in creating a bond with the baby. In our view, this term has a physical and bodily connotation, as well as a psychological and emotional one. Felt experience can be conceptualized as a form of subjective consciousness that links sensations to emotions. This notion refers to what Fire [80] (p.1) describes, based on Gendlin’s (1962) work, as a concrete internal flow of “something present, directly referred to, and felt’ (p. 243). It is that ‘inner sense’, which can be located when attention is directed inwardly”. Felt experience is a notion closer to the phenomenology of feelings than to the concept of embodiment (i.e., the body is linked to the mind, that is, sensory perceptions and actions influence the mind; Chen et al. [81] or the concept of sensoriality (i.e., the sensory dimension linked to the senses, which is both socially and culturally constructed [82]. In the felt experience, the body, mind, sensory experience and emotions are interconnected and contribute to the development of the maternal bond toward the child, from the beginning of pregnancy. The role of felt experience in the development of the bond with the baby was expressed by the participants in a way that evolved according to the different temporalities, or timeframes, of their pregnancy (feeling their baby move inside their womb, kicking, touching their belly) and at birth (feeling the baby move and descend, the contact of the baby’s head in the expulsion stage, etc.), as well as in the first moments after birth (touching, having the baby lie on them, etc.). Felt experience was also expressed in more abstract manner (feeling “attached,” creating a “connection,” etc.). These were all elements that helped create or strengthen the bond with the baby, because the “moment of birth is a moment of passage, both physical and psychological.” [83].

In our study, felt experience during birth was also manifested through emotions. Mothers who had developed an earlier bond with their baby, that is during pregnancy or birth, reported a strong feeling of strong, connection and attachment – which constitutes a subjective experience. When the mother experiences an intense and meaningful positive feeling of the birth and feels emotionally connected to her baby, she is more likely to develop a strong bond and become emotionally invested in her baby [84, 85].

Because skin-to-skin contact promotes attachment [86], it is practiced systematically for two hours in Quebec hospitals in the first moments after birth. For some women, this may contribute to the bond with their child. However, MnTP participants did not mention developing a bond at that time. Thus, the results of our study showed that it was not enough to place the baby on the mother (skin-to-skin) for this bond to be created. Mothers must be willing and available to engage in this bodily contact, feel ready, and have the right conditions in place, all at once. The comments of certain MT and MnTN participants illustrate this: they explained that they needed to take a few minutes for themselves in order to be ready for skin-to-skin contact with their baby. Both physical and psychological availability are important elements in the creation of a bond [87, 88].

Early touch is also an important aspect of the bond between the mother and her newborn. Neuroscience provides a biological explanation for the impact of touch on the construction of the individual. Initiation of early contact and communication between mother and child at birth often relies on touch [89], which is also used to sooth and regulate the newborn [90]. The bond can then be perceived as both intuitive and sensory from the first hours and days after birth [67]. Early contact is also supported by attachment theory and research, which emphasize that contact allows the mother and baby to develop a more positive relationship, with effects that last throughout childhood [86]. In addition, rudimentary types of touch (e.g., static touch, stroking, tapping and grasping) play a role in the development of mother-infant synchrony, contributing to the development of their relationship and a secure bond from the earliest moments [91].

Oxytocin, epidural and the birth context

In our study, we found that mothers who developed a later bond with their baby had received synthetic oxytocin in labor or after the epidural. As previously mentioned, in most cases, epidural use requires the administration of synthetic oxytocin.

The relation between natural oxytocin and bonding has previously been highlighted, notably by Feldman et al. [46]. Moreover, other studies indicate that synthetic oxytocin inhibits natural oxytocin [41–43], which plays a role in attachment and maternal behaviors [44, 45, 46]. In the absence of disruptions, this hormone contributes to developing a positive relationship and creating optimal bonds between mother and baby [47]. According to Walters et al. [48], oxytocin acts on the brain and plays a role in initiating bonding as well as in the mother’s emotional responses to her newborn. While the disruptive effects of prenatally administered synthetic oxytocin have been demonstrated with respect to breastfeeding initiation [51, 92], our results suggest that it does not appear to be conducive to the mother’s initiation of this bond either.

The administration of epidural goes hand in hand with the injection of synthetic oxytocin, which disrupts the natural biological course of hormone release during labor and birth. Yet, Haratipour et al. [93] established that the level of hormones present in the mother’s blood at the time of birth makes for a “sensitive period” conducive to the creation of a bond and attachment with her baby [93]. Moreover, by inhibiting the sensations of birth, epidurals prevent women from benefiting from this natural process. This was the case for P4-MnTP, who had developed a bond with her baby during pregnancy, but experienced a disruption of this bond in the first moments after birth because she had felt nothing during birth. The role and importance of felt experience throughout birth is further supported by a specific case, P25-MnTN who, even though she gave birth without an epidural, no longer expressed a bond at the moment of birth because of the swiftness of the birth. She felt nothing and, as in the case of P4-MnTP, did not realize that her child was born. This phenomenon may be explained neurologically, by the chain of responses provoked by physically feeling the birth of the baby, which generates a neurological stimulus that, in turn, creates an emotional response in the mother [94, 95]. It may also be explained by the fact that contact generates the experience that leads to awareness of the phenomenon [96], in this case, birth. This may in part explain the delay in bonding of mothers who gave birth with epidurals in our study, compared to those who gave birth naturally.

Our study results also indicate that mothers who gave birth with an epidural talk more about post-birth fatigue than others, which is likewise supported by the literature [97] Thus, they were not in an ideal condition to develop a bond with their newborn. This may have contributed to the fact these mothers did not mention a bond initiated between the first 24 h and their return home.

Finally, results suggest that the birth context also played a role in the temporal variations observed in the initiation of bonding. Unnecessary procedures during birth can inhibit the baby’s physiological processes and behaviors that may contribute to the bond between mother and newborn [98]. In our study, mothers who have birth in hospital reported being subjected to the pace (routines) of the hospital and institutional protocols, leaving little room for quality moments of physical contact, which may also have contributed to the variations observed in the timing of bonding between the different groups of mothers. These findings have implications for public health policies, such as promoting birth environments that support the establishment of the mother-child bond. They would need to take greater account of the rhythms of the mother and the baby. In this regard, the WHO has published recommendations for respectful and safe childbirth, emphasizing personalized, less medicalized care that should be applied in all birth settings [99].

For mothers in the MnTP group, the bond with their baby developed later, after returning to their room or home. The bond developed through daily care of the baby—washing, dressing, feeding, etc.—with touch and proximity playing a pivotal role. This observation echoes the study by Rankin et al. [100], in which mothers reported that their sense of bonding strengthened over the weeks with repeated interaction and daily care.

Furthermore, “bodily accessibility,” that is, how the mother perceives herself to be available for contact and physical proximity to her newborn, also influences bonding [101]. It may be assumed that mothers who give birth naturally, in a context that fosters closeness, intimacy and the pace of both mother and baby, pay greater attention to the tactile aspect of this first contact, and may also be more receptive to felt experience for the establishment of the bond with the child from birth.

The mother’s self-efficacy and confidence contribute to bonding

During the transition to parenthood, self-efficacy refers to the parent’s sense of confidence in successfully taking on the role of parent [102]. Parental self-efficacy, defined as the ability to take care of one’s child, is a key factor associated with the well-being of both parents and children [103]. Overall, self-efficacy is a process whereby the individual has the conviction that they are able to successfully perform the required behaviors in a situation considered to be difficult, and also that this will lead to a favorable outcome [104, 105]. Self-efficacy is linked to confidence [106]. According to Kadiroglu et al. [106], supporting the maternal bond may enhance self-efficacy. Perez et al. [107] suggest that these two variables appear to be bidirectional: a strong bond is associated with greater confidence and vice versa, a pattern that was also observed in our study. Perez-Ayala [108]showed that the feeling of self-efficacy, a positive birth experience and positive interaction with the newborn were closely related.

Our study suggests that more confident mothers develop a bond with their babies more easily and quickly, a finding that is in line with existing literature [107]. Several MT mothers mentioned a feeling of confidence in their pregnancy and their unborn child, which may have contributed to the early initiation of a bond. Other research has shown that mothers who are in touch with their baby from pregnancy and attentive to the baby’s signals are more likely to maintain this attitude after the birth [109]. However, some mothers may form a precise – and somewhat rigid – mental representation of their baby during pregnancy, rather than remaining attentive and open. Such detailed projections may lead to a sense of disconnection after birth [107]. In our study, we observed that maternal projections onto the child (e.g., perceiving a judgmental gaze) may have negatively affected the early stages of the relationship.

Furthermore, MT participants expressed greater confidence in their ability to bring their child into the world. This finding is consistent with the results of a study conducted across four Canadian provinces (British Columbia, Manitoba, Ontario and Quebec), which revealed that women attended by midwives, compared to those attended by obstetricians, had greater self-efficacy relative to health and were more confident in the birth process [110].

During birth, MT participants and some MnTN participants expressed having a strong feeling of self-efficacy. They were proud to have actively brought their child into the world on their own and felt a sense of accomplishment. Most established a connection with their newborn in the period surrounding birth, which also developed their confidence in being able to care for the baby [111].

The results of our study also suggest that self-confidence may be difficult to build or maintain during highly medicalized prenatal care, or to rebuild following a difficult childbirth, as also noted by Perez et al. [107]. Among some MnTN mothers, the reported experience seemed to convey greater empowerment, but suggested a more mixed pattern in terms of confidence. Some MnTN and several MnTP women mentioned a fear of complications in their pregnancy, which in some cases may have hindered the creation of a bond with their baby, given their fear of becoming attached and losing the baby. They seemed to focus more on the risks and problems that could occur during pregnancy, rather than positive outcomes in the future. They therefore sought a more medicalized environment, which they considered safer. According to social cognitive theory, a perceived lack of efficacity in mastering a potentially risky event leads to anxiety and fear [112]. Childbirth is often accompanied by a great deal of uncertainty, particularly for primiparous women, and in our North American society, where control is important, it can be perceived as threatening by many people [105, 113, 114]. For some mothers, as we have seen, the fear of complications is omnipresent and undermines their ability to believe that they will be able to cope with birth, and these fears also reverberate during the first moments after birth. This finding is similar to results from Souto et al.’s study [114] showing that some women develop fears that prevent them from attributing a positive meaning to their childbirth experience and undermine their ability to cope with it. These fears also extend into the early postpartum period. As a result, these mothers adopt a more passive stance, waiting for outside help that they believe to be more competent than themselves.

Paquet [115] pointed out that more Quebec women prefer to turn to obstetrician-gynecologists, who specialize in pathologies, because they believe their clinical skills to be superior. Their own lack of knowledge, or even ignorance, about the possibility of choice, and the lack of information available about it, condition their decision-making process, as do their perceptions of pregnancy and birth. Paquet [115] showed how the women themselves see these events, either as physiological processes or, conversely, as a pathological state with a high risk of complications, which also influences the use of various services; as a result, mothers’ experiences can vary in these different care contexts. The care and support are different, and in particular vary between monitoring risk in medical follow-up, and taking into account the overall needs of women and pregnant people, which is more present in midwife care [116].

In short, our results suggest that self-confidence and feeling of self-efficacy may help mothers connect with their babies and develop a feeling of attachment. This finding is consistent with that of Stoodley et al. [117], who propose that maternal confidence is essential to the development of the bond, because the more competent the mother feels, the more solid the bond becomes. Indeed, the more they are in an environment where they can exercise their power to act (feeling able to give birth to and take care of their child), the more favorable are the conditions for developing an earlier bond with their baby. The study by Mercuri et al. [89], conducted with mothers who had given birth naturally, also showed that those who had established contact the earliest after birth had a better relationship with the child afterwards.

Study strengths and limitations

Our study sheds light on how the mother’s first contact with her child is established at birth and in the immediate moments after. This was done using a nuanced and extensive analysis of temporality that included distinct timeframes not examined in previous research on bonding: segmenting birth into labor, delivery, delivery-birth, first moments after birth, first 24 h after birth and considering the first weeks after birth. Felt experience appears to be an important element in bonding, a fact that has been little documented previously. Felt experience is shaped by the conditions and environment surrounding birth. Whereas previous research has shown links between oxytocin and breastfeeding, our study suggest possible associations between oxytocin and epidural during birth and the establishment of the mother-infant bond. A unique contribution of our study was to explore the experiences of mothers who gave birth with or without an epidural in different care contexts, that is, in the hospital environment with a medical-nursing team, and in birth centers, at home or even in hospital with midwives. By examining a diversity of care settings, our study points to conditions favorable to the establishment of the emotional bond between the mother and her newborn.

The limited diversity of care conditions and services related to the provision of medical-nursing and midwifery services constitutes a limitation of our study. Another one is the degree of homogeneity of the participants, since all of them came from a semi-urban area and most of them were white and middle-class, fairly well educated, heterosexual, and lived in a couple. This makes it difficult to extrapolate the results to other types of primiparous mother populations. Additionally, the data collected was based on the mothers’ representations of the initial contact and first moments with their child, leaving room for subjective experience, an essential element of the experience, but a complementary in situ observation could have documented other elements important to our understanding. In addition, the characteristics of the interviewers (a midwife and a doctoral student in psychology) may also have influenced the way the interviews were conducted.

Conclusion

The results of our study help to highlight how mothers describe the bond they feel toward their babies, from the moment they feel connected with them and how it unfolds overtime, starting in pregnancy to the first two months after birth. We also gained a better understanding of the bonding process as a function of type of birth (natural or epidural) and care contexts (with a medical-nursing team in hospital or with a midwife team in a birth center, home or hospital) within a temporal continuum. Women’s experiences of bonding varied according to the context of care and type of birth, with a different temporality of bonding in each group. The bond developed during pregnancy and birth for women who gave birth naturally, and later for those who gave birth with an epidural. “Felt experience” appeared to contribute to bonding and the delay observed in bonding seemed to disappear after the first few weeks.

Establishing the early bond that develops between mother and baby is an important aspect in the development of the relationship. However, even if our research shows that the delay is resolved later on, the first moments remain important in establishing the mother-infant relationship. This said, bonding is a complex process influenced by a combination of individual, social and cultural factors.

Far from placing all the responsibility for this process solely on the parent, our study allows for a deeper understanding of this phenomenon as a function of the birth context. Further research is necessary to better understand the different mechanisms at play in the development of the bonding process and improve environmental and social contexts in order to foster favorable conditions supporting the birth experience.
