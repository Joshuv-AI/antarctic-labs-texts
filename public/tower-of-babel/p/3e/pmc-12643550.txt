Battle beyond membrane: flagella as a conduit for phage DNA entry and a trigger for bacterial defense in Yersinia enterocolitica
Authors: Zijing Wang, Wei Chen, Enming Wang, Shuai Le, Wenyu Han, Shuai Yuan, Jingmin Gu, Bing Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacterial flagella are known for facilitating motility to support nutrient acquisition and predator evasion, but can also serve as receptors for phages. Here, we characterize a dual role of flagella during infection of Yersinia enterocolitica by phage X1: functioning as a conduit for phage DNA entry and as a trigger for bacterial defense. X1 employs a “contraction-driven” mechanism, using its contractile tail to inject DNA into Yersinia flagella. Unlike other characterized flagellotropic phages that rely exclusively on flagella or less efficiently on secondary surface receptors, X1 can infect cells via lipopolysaccharides with even higher efficiency. Furthermore, certain Yersinia strains can detect X1 invasion and activate a flagellum-dependent toxin–antitoxin (Flag-TA) system, which requires the flagellar motor stator proteins MotAB to activate the abortive infection response. Our findings reveal a novel phage infection strategy and uncover a new bacterial defense that are both based on the flagellar machinery.

Introduction

Many bacteria possess the ability to move actively within their environment, typically using surface structures such as flagella or pili [1, 2]. It allows bacteria to move toward or away from specific stimuli, therefore offers bacteria the advantage of exploring their surroundings, acquiring nutrients, and avoiding hostile conditions. In Yersinia enterocolitica (Y. enterocolitica), a pathogenic bacterium responsible for causing yersiniosis, motility plays an important role in its ability to colonize hosts and survive in diverse environments [3]. And its motility is primarily achieved by polar flagella, whose existence is temperature-dependent: the bacterium exhibits high motility at lower temperatures with flagella but significantly reduced motility at higher temperatures with arrested expression of genes that are responsible for flagellum synthesis [4]. The flagellum facilitates environmental survival, colonization, and possibly the early stages of infection; however, it can be a target for host immune system once settled inside humans and animals [5]. As the length of Y. enterocolitica flagellum normally exceeds the length of the cell, it is therefore a potent inducer of host immunity. As an adaptive strategy, the flagellum is then abandoned by Y. enterocolitica to evade immune responses and promote other virulence mechanisms, including biofilm formation. Generally, pathogenic Yersinia either repress flagella expression under host conditions, such as in Y. enterocolitica, or have permanently lost this capability through mutation, as seen in Yersinia pestis (Y. pestis) [3].

Although phages primarily bind to bacteria via specific receptors on the bacterial surface, flagella can also be the attachment site [6]. Flagellotropic phages can initiate their infection cycles by attaching to the flagella of their motile hosts [7]. In fact, due to the size and mobility of the flagellum, phages that use flagella as receptors may have an advantage over those that rely on lipopolysaccharides (LPS) or outer membrane channels in flagellum-producing bacteria under certain conditions [8]. Among these phages, some are strictly flagellotropic, for which flagella are essential for initiating infection. Phage χ is a classic example of strict flagellotropic phage, which recognizes and binds to the flagellar filament, and utilizes the rotation of the flagellum to “travel” down to the bacterial surface, where it injects its DNA. This movement is thought to resemble a nut spinning down a bolt (the “nut-and-bolt” model) [9, 10]. Bacteria, including Escherichia, Salmonella, and Serratia, carrying mutations that result in the absence of flagella (e.g. ∆fliC) or paralyzed flagellar motors (e.g. ∆motA) are resistant to phage χ. Phage iEPS5 is another flagellotropic phage that infects Salmonella by targeting the flagellum as its receptor [11]. It has been proposed that the phage DNA is first injected into the flagellum and then uses the rotational motion, which somewhat resembles the nut-and-bolt model, to reach the cell body. Notably, iEPS5 possesses a long non-contractile tail, morphologically similar to χ. However, since phage χ does not inject its DNA into the flagellum, the exact mechanism by which iEPS5 breaks the flagellum structure to deliver its DNA remains unclear. Notably, counterclockwise (CCW) rotation of the flagellum is essential for infection by both χ and iEPS5, as shown by the resistance of the ∆cheY mutants. Furthermore, some flagellotropic phages like F341, ϕCb13, and ϕCbK can recognize secondary surface receptors on non-motile hosts, though with significantly lower infection efficiency [12, 13]. Although Y. enterocolitica possess flagella at low temperatures, their flagellotropic phage has not been described.

Faced with survival challenges, bacteria have evolved various anti-phage defense systems, including the well-known CRISPR–Cas, toxin–antitoxin (TA), and restriction–modification (RM) systems, alongside many newly identified mechanisms [14]. TA systems are small genetic loci found throughout bacteria that, in some cases, provide antiphage defense. Due to the cytotoxicity of the toxin, usually, TA systems mediate abortive infection (Abi), leading to bacterial death in response to phage infection [15]. As a result, the phages are removed, and the bacterial colony is protected. Recent advances in the study of phage defense have uncovered numerous novel TA systems that are activated under specific stress conditions and operate through diverse mechanisms [16]. The type II TA system is one of the most extensively studied, consisting of a stable toxin and a protein antitoxin that neutralizes the toxin under normal conditions [17]. The toxin typically disrupts essential cellular functions, such as protein synthesis or DNA replication. A representative example is RelE, a toxin that cleaves bacterial messenger RNA to trigger Abi [18]. Bioinformatic tools such as TAfinder [19] have been developed to rapidly predict and compare type II TA loci in newly sequenced bacterial genomes. While membrane-associated defense systems such as Zorya and Kiwa have been characterized [20, 21], no bacterial defense system has yet been reported that is specifically activated by phage attachment to the bacterial flagellum.

Here, we report Yersinia phage X1 as a unique flagellotropic phage, distinct from classical examples such as phage χ. Unlike previously characterized flagellotropic phages that depend on the flagellum for infection, X1 is not strictly flagellum-dependent and demonstrates even higher infection efficiency through the cell surface receptor, LPS. Intriguingly, the flagellum appears to impede rather than facilitate X1 infection, suggesting an alternative evolutionary trajectory in phage-host co-evolution. Remarkably, X1 employs an active, contraction-driven “screwdriver–screw” mechanism to inject its DNA into the flagellum, in contrast to the passive “nut-and-bolt” model described for other phages. Moreover, we identify a flagellum-dependent toxin–antitoxin (Flag-TA) system that functions as an additional bacterial defense strategy, with indispensable roles played by the stator unit MotAB. Together, our findings reveal that the evolutionary arms race between bacteria and phages extends beyond the cell membrane, positioning the flagellum as both a portal for infection and a sensor for activating bacterial defense.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and phages

The bacterial strains utilized in this study are detailed in the Supplementary Table S1. Escherichia coli (E. coli) was cultured in Lysogeny Broth (LB), which consists of 1% tryptone (OXOID, UK), 0.5% yeast extract (OXOID, UK), and 1% NaCl (OXOID, UK). The culture was maintained with shaking at 180 rpm or on LB 1.5% agar plates at 37°C. To preserve the multiflagellated morphology, Y. enterocolitica was grown in TG broth, containing 1% tryptone (OXOID, UK) and 25 mM glucose (Tianjin Kemio Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd, China) [22], under shaking conditions at 25°C. Overnight cultured Y. enterocolitica was collected and coated on TG 0.65% agar plates for further growth [22]. Antibiotics, including 100 μg/ml ampicillin, 20 μg/ml gentamicin, or 20 μg/ml chloramphenicol, were supplemented into the medium as required.

After 5 min of adsorption, the bacterial and phage suspensions were transferred into 8 ml of 0.35% semi-solid agar TG broth, which was subsequently poured onto plates that had been prepared with an initial layer of solid medium. A single plaque was isolated from the semi-solid agar TG broth and added into TG broth containing 100 μl of fresh logarithmic phase host bacteria, followed by incubation for 6 h at 37°C. Supernatants were collected after centrifugation at 6000 × g and 4°C, and subsequently filtered through a 0.22 μm filter (Millipore, USA).

Whole-genome sequencing of Y. enterocolitica

The whole-genome DNA of Y. enterocolitica was extracted by Tiangen Bacterial Genome DNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen, China). The whole-genome DNA of Y. enterocolitica was sequenced using the PacBio RS and Illumina MiSeq platforms (Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China). The PacBio sequence reads were assembled using HGAP4 and CANU (version 1.6) and subsequently corrected with Illumina MiSeq data using Pilon (version 1.22). The prediction of open reading frames (ORFs) and their annotations was conducted using Glimmer 3.0.

Serial dilution plaque assay

Cultures of Y. enterocolitica in the logarithmic growth phase were mixed with 0.35% TG soft agar and then poured onto agar plates. Subsequently, 2 μl of each serially diluted phage X1 suspension, ranging from 10³ to 10⁸ PFU/ml, were spotted onto the TG double-layer agar plates containing Y. enterocolitica lawn. The plates were incubated at either 25°C or 37°C, depending on the experimental conditions.

Bacteriophage adsorption assay

Yersinia enterocolitica was cultured to the logarithmic growth phase (OD600 0.6–0.8) and subsequently washed gently three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, consisting of 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, 1.8 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.4). The bacteria were then mixed with phage X1 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1. The mixture was incubated at either 25°C or 37°C for 15 min with shaking at 180 rpm, followed by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The titer of the unadsorbed phage in the supernatant was subsequently determined using the double-layer agar method. All experiments were conducted independently three times.

Identification of phage receptor genes in Y. enterocolitica LJYL201 at 37°C

Yersinia enterocolitica strain LJYL201 was cultured to logarithmic phase (OD600 = 0.6–0.8) and plated onto 0.6% semi-solid LB agar. Phage X1 (10⁸ PFU/ml) was serially diluted, and 10 μl of each dilution was spotted onto the bacterial lawn. Plates were incubated at 37°C, and the emergence of phage-resistant colonies within lysis zones was monitored. Candidate phage-resistant LJYL201 mutants were re-streaked and cultured for three successive generations to confirm the genetic stability of the resistance phenotype. To verify resistance, both wild-type and mutant LJYL201 strains were cultured to mid-log phase and challenged with phage X1 at varying titers. The adsorption efficiency of phage X1 to wild-type and resistant mutant strains was quantified using standard phage adsorption assays. Genomic DNA was extracted from confirmed adsorption-resistant mutants, and whole-genome sequencing was performed. Comparative genomic analysis between wild-type and mutant strains was conducted using SnapGene software to identify mutations associated with phage resistance. Candidate receptor genes were knocked out, and the resulting strains were tested for phage adsorption efficiency to validate their role in X1 susceptibility.

Gene editing using the CRISPR/Cas system

CRISPR/Cas9 knockout plasmids pPTCPLS were utilized to knockout genes 1817, rfaQ, flhC&flhD, motA, motB, motA&motB, and 1817&1816 [23]. Complementary spacer oligonucleotides were annealed and subsequently inserted into pPTCPLS, which had been digested with Eco31Ⅰ, to construct pPTCS. The 1 000 bp upstream and downstream fragments of gene 1817 were amplified using primer pairs 1817-UF/1817-UR and 1817-DF/1817-DR, respectively. These upstream and downstream polymerase chain reaction (PCR) fragments were then ligated into the SalI-XhoI site of pPTCS through seamless DNA cloning to generate pPTCS-△1817. Following this, the plasmid pPTCS-△1817 was transformed into Y. enterocolitica via electroporation. To identify positive knockout clones, PCR identification was performed using the primer pairs 1817-knockout-F/1817-knockout-R. Gene knockout experiments for rfaQ, flhC&flhD, motA, motB, motA&motB, and 1817&1816 were conducted following the same methodology as described earlier.

Negative-stain transmission electron microscopy

Bacterial flagella and phage adsorption were visualized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) [24]. The Y. enterocolitica cultures were incubated overnight at 25°C or 37°C and subsequently spotted directly onto semisolid TG agar medium containing 0.65% agar. These plates were then incubated at 25°C or 37°C for 24 h. A disposable sterile inoculating loop was used to gently pick up Y. enterocolitica colonies, which were then immersed in PBS to dissolve the bacteria in it. The samples were placed on a copper grid and prepared using negative staining with 1% phosphotungstic acid before observation with TEM (Hitachi HT-7800, Japan).

For phage adsorption visualization, samples were prepared by first glow discharging 200-mesh copper, carbon-coated grids (zxbairui, Beijing) for 1 min. Phage X1 was added to the bacterial cultures at an MOI of 2, and the cultures were incubated statically for 5 min at either 25°C or 37°C. The samples were then dropped onto the copper grid and incubated for 1 min on the grid, which was subsequently stained with 2% uranyl acetate before observation with TEM (Talos L120C, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

DNase I protection assay

The genomic DNA of Y. enterocolitica phage X1 was extracted by MiniBEST Viral RNA/DNA Extraction Kit (Takara Bio, China). The genomic DNA was treated with various concentrations of DNase I (Worthington, USA) for 15 min at 37°C, and digestion was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. Yersinia enterocolitica strain LJYL256 was cultured to the logarithmic growth phase (OD600 0.6–0.8) and subsequently washed gently three times with PBS. The bacteria were then mixed with phage X1 at an MOI of 2. The mixture was incubated with a final concentration of 30 pm DNase Ⅰ at 37°C for 5 min. Subsequently the mixture was incubated at 25°C for 30 min with shaking at 180 rpm, followed by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The titer of the supernatant was subsequently determined using the double-layer agar method. All experiments were conducted independently three times.

SYBR Gold staining and high-resolution confocal laser microscopy observation

Phages were stained using SYBR Gold (Invitrogen, USA) as previously described [11]. SYBR Gold (10 000×) was diluted with PBS. Phage X1, at the concentration of 1010 PFU/ml, was stained overnight in the dark at 4°C with a 1000× SYBR Gold solution (final concentration). The phage solution was then diluted with PBS and ultra-filtrated through an Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter with a molecular weight cutoff of 30 000 (Millipore, USA) at 2500 × g at 4°C to remove free SYBR Gold particles. The ultrafiltrated liquid was washed five times with 15 ml PBS and re-ultrafiltrated at 4000 × g to 200 μl. An overnight culture of Y. enterocolitica was plated onto TG medium containing 0.65% agar and incubated at either 25°C or 37°C for 24 h. Subsequently, one milliliter of PBS was added to the colonies, which were allowed to stand for 5 min before carefully aspirating the bacterial suspension. SYBR Gold-labeled phages were added into the bacterial suspension at an MOI of 10, and the mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 5 min and then in the dark at 25°C for 1, 15, and 30 min, respectively. The samples were then observed immediately under a high-resolution laser confocal microscope, capturing green fluorescence signals (excitation wavelength: 450 nm, emission wavelength: 550 nm) and bright field images. The average fluorescence intensity was measured using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health) and compared quantitatively.

Efficiency of center of infection assays

The efficiency of center of infection (ECOI) of phage X1 was determined as previously described [25]. Yersinia enterocolitica was cultured to the logarithmic growth phase (OD600 0.6–0.8) and gently washed three times with PBS. The bacterial pellet was then mixed with phage X1 liquid at an MOI of 0.1. The mixture was incubated with shaking for 15 min at either 25°C or 37°C, followed by centrifugation for 5 min at 5000 × g and 4°C. The pellet was washed twice with PBS and subsequently resuspended in PBS to an equal volume as the supernatant fraction. The titer of the phage in the suspension was determined using the double-layer agar method. The ECOI was calculated as the ratio of PFU/ml at 25°C to PFU/ml at 37°C. All experiments were performed independently three times.

Efficiency of plating assay

Nineteen strains of Y. enterocolitica were cultured to the logarithmic growth phase (OD600 0.6–0.8) and gently washed three times with PBS. After a 5 min adsorption, the bacteria and phage suspensions were transferred into 8 ml of 0.65% semi-solid agar TG broth, which was subsequently poured onto plates prepared with an initial layer of solid medium. The plates were inverted and incubated at either 25°C or 37°C for 16 h. The number of plaques observed after incubation at 25°C was compared to that at 37°C. The experiments were conducted independently in triplicate.

Protein expression and purification

All primers utilized in this study are detailed in Supplementary Table S2. The pETDuet1 expression system was employed for co-expression experiments. DNA fragments of genes 1817 and 1816 were amplified using Y. enterocolitica LJYL201 genomic DNA as a template, with primer pairs 1817-F/1817-R and 1816-F/1816-R, respectively. The PCR products were analyzed via agarose gel electrophoresis (1%), and the target products were purified using the E.Z.N.A.® Gel Extraction Kit (Omega, USA). The 1817 and 1816 fragments were subsequently cloned into the BamHI–HindIII and KpnI–XhoI sites of the pETDuet1 vector, respectively, using the Seamless Cloning Kit (Tsingke, China). The seamless cloning product was transformed into E. coli DH5α competent cells, from which single colonies were selected and identified by using Sanger DNA sequencing. The same methodology was employed to construct the vectors pETDuet1-1817-1816, pETDuet1-1817, and pET-SUMO-1816. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed by back-to-back PCR amplifications using forward primers bearing the mutation to introduce. The mutation primers were designed by the online NEBaseChanger (New England Biolabs, https://nebasechanger.neb.com), and then the plasmid pETDuet1-1817(H323A) was obtained using Q5 Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (New England Biolabs, E0552S).

The vector pBAD33 was utilized to construct the overexpression plasmids for 1817, 1817–1816, and EGFP-1817–1816-mko2. DNA fragments of 1817 were amplified via PCR using the primer pairs BAD1817-F and BAD1817-R. Additionally, DNA fragments containing the 1817 and 1816 genes were amplified using the primer pairs BAD1716-F and BAD1716-R. EGFP and mko2 genes were synthesized by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. DNA fragments of EGFP, 1817–1816, and mko2 were amplified via PCR using the primer pairs EGFP-F/EGFP-R, 17–16-F/17–16-R, and mko2-F/mko2-R. The three fragments were fused by an overlap PCR using primer set EGFP-F/mko2-R. The resulting fragments were subsequently inserted into the KpnI–HindIII sites of the pBAD33 vector by employing the seamless ligation cloning extract technique.

The recombinant vectors pETDuet1-1817–1816 and pET-SUMO-1816 were transformed into competent E. coli BL21(DE3) cells, respectively. A single colony was cultivated in 5 ml of LB containing 100 μg/ml of ampicillin or kanamycin overnight, after which the culture was transferred to 1000 ml of LB and incubated at 37°C with shaking at 180 rpm. While the M9 minimum medium containing 15NH4Cl was used for isotopic labeling of 1816 for nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments. Upon reaching the logarithmic phase of growth (OD600 0.6–0.8), 0.1 mM isopropyl-beta-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added, and the culture was cultivated for an additional 16 h at 18°C. The bacterial cells were harvested via centrifugation (5000 × g for 10 min at 4°C) and subsequently washed with a phosphate buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM NaH2PO4, 20% glycerol, pH 8.0) before being subjected to sonication to ensure bacteria disruption. After centrifugation (20 000 × g for 30 min at 4°C), the proteins in the supernatant were purified using Ni-affinity chromatography. The 6× His-SUMO tag was removed using SUMO protease Ulp1 and separated by Ni-affinity chromatography. Analytical gel filtration chromatography was performed on an ÄKTA FPLC system (GE Healthcare, USA). The purity of the protein and its apparent molecular weight were assessed using sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE).

The recombinant vector pETDuet1-1817 or pETDuet1-1817(H323A) was transformed into competent E. coli BL21 (DE3) pLysS cells. The steps prior to the addition of IPTG were consistent with those described in the previous paragraph. After inducing protein expression for 5 h at 18°C, the bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation (5000 × g for 10 min at 4°C) and washed with phosphate buffer [300 mM NaCl, 50 mM NaH2PO4, 20% glycerol, 1 mM PMSF, 1 mM protease inhibitors (Beyotime Biotechnology, China), pH 8.0]. The process for purifying protein 1817 or 1817(H323A) followed the same protocol as described previously. The purified proteins were concentrated using a 30 kDa ultrafiltration tube (Millipore, USA) to eliminate imidazole.

Bacterial growth curves

The constructed overexpression plasmids, pBAD33-1817, pBAD33-1817–1816, and the empty vector pBAD33, were introduced into Y. enterocolitica LJYL201 via electroporation, respectively. Cells transformed with the empty vector pBAD33 served as a negative control. Yersinia enterocolitica LJYL201 containing pBAD33-1817, pBAD33-1817–1816, or pBAD33 were incubated until OD600 reached 0.2–0.3. Subsequently, l-arabinose was added to one aliquot at a concentration of 0.2%, while the other aliquot remained without arabinose. A volume of 150 μl from each group was then transferred to clear 96-well plates. Kinetic measurements of bacterial growth were conducted in the 96-well plates using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA) at 25°C. Each experiment was performed independently three times.

Yersinia enterocolitica strain LJYL201 was cultured until it reached the logarithmic growth phase (OD600 0.6–0.8) and subsequently diluted 100-fold. Phage X1 was added into the LJYL201 cultures at MOIs of 0.1 and 10, respectively. A bacterial culture without the addition of phage X1 served as the negative control. The resulting mixtures and bacterial cultures were then transferred into 96-well plates, with 150 μl dispensed per well. Growth curves were established by measuring OD600 at 0.5 h intervals at 25°C using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA). Each experiment was conducted independently three times to ensure reproducibility.

Bacterial survival after phage infection

Yersinia enterocolitica strain LJYL201 was cultured to the logarithmic growth phase (OD600 0.6–0.8), after which phage X1 was added into the culture at an MOI of 2. A bacterial culture without the addition of phage X1 served as a negative control. The mixture was shaken and incubated for 15 min at either 25°C or 37°C. Samples were collected every 5 min, diluted, and plated on TG agar to determine colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/ml). All experiments were conducted independently three times.

Confocal laser microscopy observation

For live/dead staining, the Live/Dead Fixable Aqua Dead Cell staining kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was utilized [25]. Yersinia enterocolitica strain LJYL201 was cultured to the logarithmic growth phase (OD600 0.6–0.8), after which phage X1 was added at an MOI of 2. The mixture was shaken and incubated at 25°C, and 100 μl of the mixture was sampled at 15 min, followed by staining according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Bacterial cultures without the addition of phage X1 and heat-killed bacteria (70°C) served as controls. Slides were examined under a confocal laser microscope (Olympus CX-41; Olympus America, Center Valley, PA, USA), and fluorescence signals were observed and captured: green (indicating intact cell membranes, excitation wavelength: 490 nm, emission wavelength: 515 nm) and red (indicating ruptured cell membranes, excitation wavelength: 535 nm, emission wavelength: 617 nm).

The constructed overexpression plasmid pBAD33-EGFP-1817–1816-mko2 was introduced into Y. enterocolitica LJYL256Δ1817&1816 via electroporation. LJYL256Δ1817&1816 was cultured to the logarithmic growth phase (OD600 0.6–0.8) and gently washed three times with PBS. The bacterial pellet was then mixed with phage X1 liquid at an MOI of 2. The mixture was incubated with shaking for 15 min at 25°C, followed by centrifugation for 5 min at 5000 × g and 4°C. The supernatant was discarded carefully, and the precipitate was resuspended in 50 μl of fresh PBS. A bacterial culture without the addition of phage X1 served as a negative control. Slides were examined under a confocal laser microscope (Olympus CX-41; Olympus America, Center Valley, PA, USA), and fluorescence signals were observed and captured: green (indicating intact cell membranes, excitation wavelength: 490 nm, emission wavelength: 515 nm) and red (indicating ruptured cell membranes, excitation wavelength: 535 nm, emission wavelength: 617 nm).

RNA-seq and bioinformatics analysis

Yersinia enterocolitica was cultured to the logarithmic growth phase (OD600 0.6–0.8). Then phage X1 was added into the culture at an MOI of 2. An equal volume of PBS was added to the control group. The mixture was shaken and incubated for 15 min at either 25°C or 37°C. Following centrifugation at 5000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, the cells were washed multiple times with PBS. Total RNA was extracted using a Bacterial RNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The integrity of the total RNA was evaluated using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, USA). For complementary DNA (cDNA) library preparation, 1 µg of total RNA was utilized for cDNA synthesis and amplification with the Illumina TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit (Agilent Technologies, USA), adhering to the manufacturer’s guidelines. The library preparations were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. Each RNA-seq library underwent quality verification using FASTQC v0.52. The filtered sequencing reads were then subjected to genomic localization analysis with Bowtie2 (allowing 2 mismatches, while other parameters were set to default). Newly predicted transgenic regions were annotated and commented on using software available at (http://cs.wellesley.edu/∼btjaden/Rockhopper/). Read counts mapped to each gene were determined using HTSeq v0.6.1, from which the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads for each gene were calculated based on gene length and read counts. Differential expression analysis between the two conditions/groups was conducted using the DESeq2 R package (version 1.20.0), designating genes with an adjusted P-value < .05 as differentially expressed. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes, correcting for gene length bias, was performed using the GOseq R package. Additionally, gene set enrichment analysis was conducted for both GO and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) datasets.

RT-PCR and qRT-PCR

Yersinia enterocolitica was cultured to a plateau density of OD600 1–1.2 and subsequently washed three times with PBS at 5000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. Total RNA was extracted using a Bacterial RNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed using a reverse transcription kit (Takara, China). Bacterial DNA was extracted from the culture broth utilizing a bacterial genomic DNA kit (Tiangen, China). PCR and reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) were carried out using purified total DNA or cDNA as templates, with primer pairs 1817-F/1817-R, 1816-F/1816-R, and 1817-F/1816-R, respectively.

Yersinia enterocolitica was cultured to the logarithmic growth phase (OD600 0.6–0.8), and phage X1 was added at an MOI of 2. The mixture was shaken and incubated for 15 min at either 25°C or 37°C. Subsequently, the mixture was gently washed three times with PBS at 5000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. A bacterial culture without the addition of phage X1 served as a control. RNA from these samples was extracted and reverse-transcribed to cDNA as previously described [26]. Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq II kit (Takara Bio, China) with the primer pair 1817-Q-F/1817-Q-R. The 16S rRNA gene was utilized as an internal reference gene. All experiments were conducted independently three times.

qRT-PCR was conducted to detect the transcript levels of several phage X1 genes, including DNA methyltransferase, single-stranded DNA binding protein, and long tail fiber protein. Strains LJYL201 and LJYL201Δ1817 were cultured to the logarithmic growth phase (OD600 0.6–0.8), and phage X1 was added at an MOI of 2. The mixture was shaken and incubated for 1, 3, 5, and 15 min at 25°C, respectively. RNA was extracted from these samples and reverse-transcribed to cDNA. qRT-PCR was performed using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq II kit (Takara Bio, China) with the primer pairs DM-Q-F/DM-Q-R, SSB-Q-F/SSB-Q-R, and TF-Q-F/TF-Q-R, respectively. All experiments were performed independently three times.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed as described previously [27]. The samples were separated using SDS–PAGE gel electrophoresis at a constant voltage of 80 V. Subsequently, the bands were transferred onto a 0.22 µm PVDF transfer membrane (Thermo, USA) via a wet transfer method. The PVDF membrane was blocked with EveryBlot blocking buffer (Bio-Rad, USA) for 5 min, followed by overnight incubation with rabbit polyclonal anti-1817 (AtaGenix Laboratories Co., Ltd, China) or mouse polyclonal anti-1816 (ABclonal Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China) at 4°C, respectively. After this, the membrane was shaken and incubated at room temperature for 30 min, with 10-min intervals. The membrane was then incubated with anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Bio-Rad, USA) at room temperature for 40 min, followed by another shaking incubation at room temperature for 30 min, also with 10-min intervals. Then, membranes were visualized using ChemiDoc™ MP imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA).

DNase and RNase activity assay

The genomic DNA of Y. enterocolitica LJYL201 and phage X1 was utilized to conduct the DNase activity assay. The whole-genome DNA of Y. enterocolitica was extracted by Bacterial Genome DNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen, China). The genomic DNA of Y. enterocolitica phage X1 was extracted by MiniBEST Viral RNA/DNA Extraction Kit (Takara Bio, China). A concentration gradient of purified proteins 1817 was incubated with the substrate (100 ng) in varying proportions. DNA treated with buffer served as a control. Reactions were incubated at 37°C or 25°C as indicated and then terminated at 100°C for 5 min. Samples were visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis in a 0.5% agarose gel.

The total RNA extracted from Y. enterocolitica LJYL201 was utilized to conduct the RNase activity assay. The total RNA of Y. enterocolitica LJYL201 was then extracted with Bacterial Total RNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen, China). A concentration gradient of purified proteins 1817 was incubated with the substrate in varying proportions. A premix was prepared to incubate proteins 1817 and 1816 simultaneously. RNA treated with buffer served as a control. Reactions were incubated at 37°C or 25°C for 5 min and then terminated at 65°C for 5 min. Additionally, RNase activity was evaluated in the presence of RNaseOUT™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Samples were visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis in a 1% agarose gel.

Nuclear magnetic resonances

Purified 1816 protein (for 1H-NMR) or 15N-labeled 1816 protein (for 1H–15N HSQC) was prepared at a concentration of 0.5 mM in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM TCEP, and 5% D2O (pH 7.4). All NMR spectra were collected on a Bruker 800 MHz (Avance III HD) spectrometer equipped with cryoprobe at 298 K. The data were acquired and processed using Topspin 4.1.4 (Bruker). The phase and baseline of the spectra were corrected manually.

Structure prediction and modeling

All structure prediction and modeling were conducted by the online AlphaFold Server (https://alphafoldserver.com/). In brief, the protein sequences of 1816 and 1817 were individually or collectively input, thereby generating the independent structures of 1816 and 1817, as well as the structure of the 1816–1817 complex. Subsequently, the predicted structures were aligned and analyzed with the homologous structures, EsaD (PDB ID: 8GUN), EsaG (PDB ID: 8GUP), and EsaD–EsaG complex (PDB ID: 8GUO) [28], to derive the final structural, domain, and interaction information. The analysis and visualization of these structures were conducted using PyMOL (https://pymol.org).

Homology analysis

All sequences examined were aligned against the NCBI database by BLASTn or BLASTp with an E-value threshold of 0.05. The distance trees of results were produced using BLAST pairwise alignments using the tree method of Fast Minimum Evolution with a Max Seq Difference of 0.75 (for genome) or 0.9 (for protein). The results underwent further taxonomic analysis and were then plotted using the Phylogenetic Tree tool of the online ChiPlot platform (https://www.chiplot.online/) [29].

Bioinformatics analysis of the phage X1 genome

RAST 2.0 combined with BLASTP/BLASTN was used to predict potential ORF [30]. Gene function block diagrams were mapped using CLC Main Workbench version 7.7.3 software (CLC Bio-Qiagen, Aarhus, Denmark).

Statistical analysis

Statistical data presented in this study were processed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). GraphPad Prism 6 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was utilized for chart generation. P-values < .05 were considered statistically significant. Error bars represented the standard error of the mean.

Results

X1 is a flagellotropic phage that infects both mobile and nonmobile Y. enterocolitica

Phage X1 has been reported as a broad-host-range lytic phage with a contractile tail, capable of infecting multiple Y. enterocolitica strains at 37°C [31]. To identify the bacterial surface receptor utilized by phage X1, we first isolated three independent phage-resistant Y. enterocolitica mutants and performed whole-genome sequencing. All three mutants harbored point mutations in the gene encoding LPS core heptosyltransferase RfaQ, suggesting that LPS may serve as the phage receptor (Supplementary Fig. S1A). To validate this hypothesis, we constructed an rfaQ knockout strain and observed a severe reduction in phage adsorption compared to the wild-type control (Supplementary Fig. S1B). These results confirm that LPS is the receptor for X1 at 37°C.

However, since Y. enterocolitica is known to produce multiple flagella at temperatures ranging from 22°C to 30°C, which may act as physical barriers, obstructing phage access to its presumed cell surface receptor. In contrast, we observed that the infection efficiency of X1 at 25°C remained relatively unchanged, or slightly reduced compared to 37°C, as demonstrated using Y. enterocolitica strain LJYL256 (Fig. 1A and B). To assess whether flagella interfere with phage attachment, we compared X1 adsorption efficiency at 25°C and 37°C. Surprisingly, adsorption was unaffected by flagella production at the lower temperature (Fig. 1C). One possible explanation is that X1 is a flagellotropic phage capable of recognizing both flagella and LPS. However, this would be atypical, as previously reported flagellotropic phages using both receptors generally show reduced infection efficiency via the surface receptor [12, 13]. We then used TEM to verify the hypothesis. At 25°C, bacteria produced multiple flagella, and phages were observed attaching to these filaments but not seen on the surface at an MOI of 2 (Fig. 1D). In contrast, at 37°C, when no flagellum was produced, phages were observed attaching directly to the cell surface (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Notably, two distinct phage morphologies were observed on the flagella: one with an intact head and extended tail, and the other with an empty head and contracted tail (Fig. 1E). The contracted sheath was ~50% shorter than its initial length, consistent with sheath contraction observed during phage T4 infection [32]. Together, these findings suggest that X1 appears to inject its DNA into the flagellum during infection, reminiscent of the observation reported for the flagellotropic phage iEPS5.

To verify this hypothesis, we labeled phage DNA with SYBR Gold and monitored the infection process using confocal epifluorescence microscopy. This technique enabled high-resolution visualization of SYBR-labeled phage DNA at multiple time points. At 1-min post-infection (p.i.), phage DNA appeared as distinct fluorescent puncta localized outside the bacterial cells. By 10 min p.i., elongated, flagella-like fluorescent structures were observed, suggesting that phage DNA had entered these appendages (Fig. 1F). At 30 min p.i., fluorescence signals were predominantly confined to the bacterial cytoplasm, with no detectable flagella-like traces remaining. These dynamics closely resemble those reported for the flagellotropic phage iEPS5 and suggest that phage X1 similarly injects its DNA through the flagellar filament. Notably, the flagella-like fluorescent structures at 10 min varied in length and curvature, consistent with variability in the distance between phage attachment sites and the bacterial surface, as well as the inherent flexibility of flagella. Additionally, fluorescence intensity increased ~20-fold from 10 min p.i., rising from ∼1000 to ∼20 000. This phenomenon has also been observed in other SYBR Gold-labeled phage studies [33, 34], as SYBR Gold gains greater accessibility to DNA once the compacted phage genome is released from the capsid. And the unpacking of X1 genome results in a dramatic increase in fluorescence intensity, indicating DNA delivery during infection.

To further investigate this phenomenon, we deleted the flhDC genes in LJYL256, which are essential for flagella production (Supplementary Fig. S1D) [22], and repeated the infection assays. In the absence of flagella, phage DNA remained as discrete surface-associated dots at 1 min p.i., and no flagella-like traces were observed at 10 min p.i. (Fig. 1G). Phage DNA was detected inside the cytoplasm as early as 10 min p.i., earlier than in wild-type, flagellated bacteria. By 30 min p.i., the cells had lysed, consistent with productive phage infection.

Together, these results demonstrate that X1 is atypic flagellotropic phage capable of infecting both motile and non-motile Y. enterocolitica, utilizing both the flagellum and surface LPS to initiate infection. Moreover, the flagellum may act as a conduit or guide for DNA delivery during the motile stage.

X1 infects mobile host in a contraction-driven “screwdriver–screw” model via flagella

While both X1 and iEPS5 utilize flagellar filaments for DNA delivery, their infection mechanisms appear to differ substantially. X1 possesses a contractile tail, in contrast to the non-contractile tail of iEPS5. For iEPS5, CCW flagellar rotation is essential for successful infection, as deletion of motA, which encodes a stator protein critical for flagellar rotation, abolishes infectivity. In contrast, as a myovirus, X1 may rely on the mechanical force generated by tail sheath contraction to drive its DNA into the flagellar filament. To test this hypothesis, we constructed an LJYL256ΔmotA mutant and evaluated X1 infectivity. TEM confirmed that motA deletion did not impair flagellum assembly (Supplementary Fig. S2). Furthermore, plaque assays revealed that X1 infection was not only unimpaired in the ΔmotA mutant but was even enhanced relative to the wild type (Fig. 2A and B), paralleling the increased infection efficiency observed at 37°C compared to 25°C (Fig. 1A and B), when flagellar production differs. These findings suggest that flagellar movement is not required for X1 infection, unlike χ and iEPS5. Further supporting this, confocal epifluorescence microscopy demonstrated that DNA injection still occurred via the flagellum in the ΔmotA mutant, as evidenced by the presence of flagella-like SYBR Gold-labeled DNA traces at 10 min post-infection (Fig. 2C). Collectively, these results indicate that X1 infection is independent of flagellar rotation and that sheath contraction alone is sufficient to drive DNA translocation via the flagellum.

As phage DNA left a flagellum-like trace during infection, flagellum should serve as a pathway for DNA delivery. The DNA could either travel externally along the appendage or through its internal hollow core. To check between these possibilities, we tested whether extracellular DNase I could degrade the DNA during infection. First, we evaluated DNase I activity on purified X1 genomic DNA in vitro and determined that complete digestion was achieved within 5 min at a molar ratio of 5 × 10−6 (DNase I to X1 genome, Fig. 2D). Considering that, at OD600 = 0.6 with an MOI of 2, the concentration of phage genomes in the bacterial culture is ~2 pM, we applied 30 pM DNase I, representing an approximate 5 000 000-fold excess compared with the in vitro assay, to ensure effective degradation during infection. Results showed that the addition of excess DNase I did not affect phage infectivity (Fig. 2E), and flagellum-like fluorescent structures were still observed (Fig. 2F).

Based on these findings, we propose the following infection model for X1 (Fig. 2G). Upon binding to the host flagellar filament, phage X1 contracts its tail sheath, driving the tail tube into the filament in a manner analogous to non-flagellotropic phages that penetrate the bacterial cell envelope. This contraction-driven “screwdriver–screw” mechanism enables the phage to overcome structural barriers and actively translocate its DNA through the flagellum. This model is supported by the assembly of flagellum via a type III secretion system (T3SS), a transmembrane export apparatus that forms the extracellular filament [35]. Since the diameter of the T3SS-like export channel exceeds the ∼2 nm width of phage DNA, the channel could theoretically accommodate DNA translocation (Fig. 2H). However, the efficiency of this process may be limited, as several factors can impede successful infection. These may include, but are not limited to, the need for sophisticated mechanisms to penetrate and inject DNA through a narrow channel, as well as the presence of export apparatus proteins that may obstruct the reverse movement of DNA. This contraction-based model contrasts mechanistically with phage iEPS5, which relies on CCW flagellar rotation for successful infection.

Yersinia enterocolitica is protected by a flagellum-dependent defense system

Interestingly, the host range of phage X1 varies significantly between 25°C and 37°C: at 37°C, 15 out of 19 tested Y. enterocolitica strains are susceptible to X1 infection, whereas only 5 strains remain susceptible at 25°C (Fig. 3A). Although phage adsorption is unaffected in 10 of the resistant strains at 25°C (Supplementary Fig. S3A), efficiency of plating (EOP) assays reveal a marked reduction in infection rates in these strains at the lower temperature (Supplementary Fig. S3B). These results indicate the presence of a low-temperature-activated bacterial defense system protecting against X1 at 25°C.

Using strain LJYL201 as a model, the unchanged adsorption efficiency but significantly reduced ECOI at 25°C suggests that this putative defense mechanism is not due to compromised absorption (Fig. 3B). Plaque assays further support the presence of this defense system, and cell survival measurements within 15 min post-infection show reduced survival at 25°C, consistent with an Abi system that triggers cell death upon phage infection (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, growth curves of LJYL201 infected with X1 at two different MOIs and both temperatures further corroborate the presence of a temperature-dependent Abi-like defense mechanism, which protects Y. enterocolitica from phage X1 infection specifically at 25°C (Fig. 3D).

However, as the flagellum serves as the receptor for X1 at 25°C, the defense mechanism could be flagellum-dependent rather than directly activated by temperature. To test this, we used the LJYL201ΔflhDC strain, which lacks flagella, and found that the deletion abolished protection against X1 at 25°C (Fig. 3E). This demonstrates that LPS cannot activate the Abi system, regardless of temperature, and that the defense mechanism is indeed flagellum-dependent, with its temperature sensitivity arising from the requirement of low temperatures for flagellum expression. To further dissect this defense system, we compared sequences of major flagellar components, including stator units, the C-ring complex, and the export apparatus responsible for phage DNA translocation, across protected and unprotected strains. Surprisingly, no conserved sequence differences were identified (Supplementary Fig. S4A–H), suggesting the defense is not mediated by flagellar structural variation.

Although no flagellum-dependent defense system has been previously described, we drew parallels to the recently identified Zorya system [20], in which the membrane-embedded proteins ZorA and ZorB (ZorAB) are structural homologs of MotAB and are involved in sensing phage attachment and triggering defense signaling. ZorAB senses phage presence and signals the downstream nuclease via the cytoplasmic tail of ZorA. Structural alignment of MotAB and ZorAB revealed a notable difference: MotA lacks the very long cytoplasmic tail (∼70 nm, partially shown in the structure) that is present in ZorA, which is essential for signal transduction and effector recruitment (Supplementary Fig. S5A–D). Importantly, deletion of motA, motB, or both (motAB) in the LJYL201 strain abolished its resistance to phage X1 (Fig. 3F), while retaining flagellar attachment (Fig. 3G). It is plausible that MotAB may similarly function as a sensory module, detecting the reverse movement of phage DNA through the flagellar channel, reminiscent of the export of flagellar components during assembly, and signaling a downstream Abi. While our data highlight the essential role of the stator unit in activating this defense, the precise molecular mechanism remains to be elucidated (Fig. 3H).

Yersinia activates a downstream toxin for defense

To further investigate the nature of the putative Abi defense, we first examined whether membrane integrity was compromised upon activation of the defense response. Live/dead staining performed 15 min post-infection showed that bacterial membranes remained largely intact during defense activation (Fig. 4A). Consistent images revealed that some bacteria exhibited a less electron-dense cytoplasm, indicating signs of degradation (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that the Abi system does not rely on lytic membrane damage but instead triggers internal cellular deterioration.

To identify the downstream effector responsible for this defense, we compared transcriptomic profiles of LJYL201 during X1 infection under conditions where the Abi system is active (25°C) versus inactive (37°C). Notably, fewer genes were differentially expressed at 25°C (Fig. 4C), consistent with early termination of infection by Abi, whereas widespread transcriptional changes were observed during productive infection at 37°C (Supplementary Fig. S6). Among the genes specifically upregulated during Abi activation, we identified a putative nuclease gene, GM0001817 (hereafter referred to as 1817), which showed a significant increase in expression at 25°C. Gene context analysis and RT-PCR assay revealed that 1817 is co-transcribed with an upstream gene, GM0001816 (1816), suggesting they form a bicistronic operon regulated by a shared promoter, which is upregulated during X1 infection as shown by the RT-qPCR result (Fig. 4D). Deletion of 1817 abolished resistance to phage X1 at 25°C as demonstrated in the plaque assay, while overexpression of 1817 in Yersinia severely inhibited bacterial growth (Fig. 4E). TEM analysis of 1817-overexpressing cells showed preserved membrane structures but a markedly less electron-dense cytoplasm, consistent with cytoplasmic degradation (Fig. 4F). These results indicate that 1817 functions as a downstream toxin, activated in response to phage infection as part of the flagellum-dependent Abi-like defense system. The adjacent gene 1816 is likely to encode a cognate antitoxin that neutralizes 1817 under non-stress conditions.

The 1816–1817 operon encodes a novel flagellum-dependent type II toxin–antitoxin system

To determine whether the 1816–1817 operon encodes a functional TA pair, we first recombinantly expressed both proteins and assembled the complex in vitro. Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) coupled with SDS–PAGE analysis demonstrated that 1816 and 1817 form a stable complex, supporting the notion of direct binding (Fig. 5A and B). Functionally, co-overexpression of both 1816 and 1817 did not impair bacterial growth (Fig. 5C), in contrast to the cytotoxic effect observed upon overexpression of 1817 alone (Fig. 4E). These findings show that 1816 neutralizes the toxicity of 1817, consistent with a type II TA module. To test whether this TA system is activated during phage infection, we generated custom polyclonal antibodies against 1816 and performed western blotting following X1 infection (Fig. 5D). Upon infection at 25°C, conditions under which the Abi system is active, 1816 protein levels decreased markedly. In contrast, under non-permissive conditions for Abi activation (37°C), 1816 levels remained relatively stable. These results, in conjunction with transcriptomic and RT-qPCR data showing operon upregulation at 25°C, suggest that activation of the Abi system involves selective degradation of the antitoxin 1816 while allowing accumulation of the toxin 1817.

To examine subcellular localization and dynamics of the TA pair, we fused 1817 to green fluorescent protein (GFP) and 1816 to red fluorescent protein (RFP), and visualized their distribution via fluorescence microscopy. In uninfected cells, 1817-GFP and 1816-RFP colocalized into distinct foci that did not overlap with DAPI-stained nucleoids, suggesting that the TA complex is not nucleoid-associated (Fig. 5E). Upon X1 infection at 25°C, 1817-GFP remained visible, whereas the 1816-RFP signal was lost, consistent with degradation of the antitoxin during activation of the defense response. These data collectively support that the 1816–1817 operon encodes a functional, phage-inducible type II TA system, which contributes to Abi by toxin activation following antitoxin degradation. Importantly, in ΔflhDC and ΔmotAB mutant strains, which lack flagella or flagellar motor function, levels of 1816 increased following X1 infection (Fig. 5F). This suggests that activation of the TA system is dependent on flagellar stator unit, linking it mechanistically to the flagellum-dependent Abi-like defense described earlier. As this TA system is not detected by standard bioinformatic tools such as TA-Finder and appears to represent a previously uncharacterized class, we designate it the Flag-TA system.

To gain insight into its molecular architecture, we used AlphaFold to predict the structure of the Flag-toxin. The model revealed three distinct domains, including a C-terminal ββα-metal HNH nuclease domain (residues 245–408), a hallmark of nucleases commonly found in type II TA toxins (Fig. 5G). The remaining domains lacked clear overall homology to known structures, likely suggesting uncharacterized functionality. In the predicted Flag-TA complex, the Flag-antitoxin binds in close proximity to the HNH domain and appears to interfere with the conserved nuclease motif, potentially blocking catalytic activity.

Flag-toxin is an RNase that degrades host and phage RNA to mediate Abi

To determine whether the Flag-toxin functions as a DNase or RNase, we performed in vitro nuclease assays using purified 1817 protein and various nucleic acid substrates. Genomic DNA from phage X1 and Y. enterocolitica remained intact following incubation with Flag-toxin at both 25°C and 37°C, indicating no detectable DNase activity (Fig. 6A and B). In contrast, 23S and 16S rRNA from Y. enterocolitica were rapidly degraded by the toxin under the same conditions, confirming its RNase activity (Fig. 6C). To validate these findings in vivo, we monitored phage RNA abundance following infection using RT-qPCR. In the Δ1817 mutant strain of Y. enterocolitica (lacking the Flag-toxin), transcripts of several phage genes, including DNA methyltransferase, single-stranded DNA-binding protein, and tail fiber protein, were readily detected throughout infection at 25°C (Fig. 6D). However, in the wild-type LJYL201 strain, these transcripts were undetectable, consistent with degradation by Flag-toxin-mediated RNase activity. These results suggest that the toxin cleaves both host and phage transcripts during infection, thereby activating the Flag-toxin for an Abi to protect the broader bacterial population.

Using AF3, we built a Flag-toxin (HNH domain)-RNA model, which demonstrated that the RNA directly contacts conserved key residues within the catalytic motif (Supplementary Fig. S7A). Site-directed mutagenesis of histidine 323 to alanine (H323A) completely abolished RNase activity in vitro (Fig. 6F), confirming that the HNH motif is essential for RNA cleavage and toxin function. Although the Flag-TA system lacks overall similarity to previously characterized TA modules, sequence homology analysis of its RNase domain suggested resemblance to the YeeF/YezG TA module from Bacillus subtilis [36] (Supplementary Fig. S7B and C). AlphaFold 3 predictions indicated that the Flag-toxin differs markedly from YeeF, except for the conserved nuclease domain (Supplementary Fig. S7D). The distinct architecture of the Flag-toxin, including its three-domain organization, may enable signal transduction from MotAB, although the specific transducer remains unidentified. The Flag-antitoxin adopts a similar fold as YezG but contains additional helices (Supplementary Fig. S7E). Interestingly, AlphaFold predicts that the Flag-antitoxin forms a well-folded tertiary structure, in contrast to the intrinsically disordered nature typical of many canonical type II antitoxins [37]. Consistent with this prediction, the 2D ¹H–¹⁵N HSQC NMR spectrum confirmed that the Flag-antitoxin is folded in isolation.

Together, these findings demonstrate that Flag-toxin is a nonspecific RNase that targets host and phage RNA during infection, thereby mediating a flagellum-dependent, temperature-sensitive Abi defense in Y. enterocolitica.

Conservation of the flag-TA defense system

As the Flag-TA module does not resemble classical type II toxin–antitoxin (TA) systems and is not readily identified by standard bioinformatic tools such as TA-Finder, we next explored its evolutionary conservation. To this end, we constructed separate phylogenetic trees for the phage tail fiber protein (presumed to recognize the flagellum), the Flag-toxin, and its cognate antitoxin to assess whether these components exhibit conserved evolutionary patterns. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the phage tail fiber protein is highly conserved among flagellated members of the Yersinia, Salmonella, and Enterobacter genera (Supplementary Fig. S8), consistent with a shared flagellum-mediated mode of infection. In contrast, the Flag-antitoxin exhibited greater sequence diversity across species than its corresponding toxin (Supplementary Fig. S9A and B), suggesting that the antitoxin component may have undergone more extensive evolutionary divergence or modular adaptation.

While these phylogenetic relationships are not definitive, they provide important clues about the evolutionary dynamics of the Flag-TA module and suggest a potential functional linkage between the flagellar apparatus and activation of this defense system. Given that the N-terminal and central domains of the Flag-toxin remain uncharacterized, it is plausible that these regions participate in sensing or transmitting signals from the flagellar machinery to initiate toxin activation. These domains may serve as adaptors or scaffolds within a broader, flagellum-dependent defense pathway, a hypothesis that warrants further biochemical and structural investigation.

Discussion

Our study reveals Yersinia phage X1 as a novel flagellotropic phage with a dual infection strategy and a unique DNA injection mechanism, offering new insights into the complex dynamics of phage-host interactions. Unlike the prototypical flagellotropic phage χ, which strictly depends on the flagellum for infection and follows a passive nut-and-bolt model [9, 38], phage X1 can utilize both the flagellum and LPS as receptors for infection. This dual strategy suggests an alternative evolutionary trajectory in phage-host coevolution and places X1 and related phages in a previously unrecognized clade within the flagellotropic phage group, allowing effective infection across diverse bacterial physiological states, particularly in response to temperature-dependent changes in motility.

One of the striking features of X1 is its contraction-driven DNA injection mechanism. Phage iEPS5 appears to rely on flagellar rotation to passively travel toward the cell surface [11]. This mechanism raises important questions about how a long-tailed phage establishes a translocation channel within a thin, dynamic appendage like the flagellum and whether flagellar composition or mechanical state influences DNA entry efficiency. However, X1 actively injects its DNA into the flagellar structure, bypassing the need for such motility-dependent transport. Furthermore, genome annotation of X1 (Supplementary Fig. S10) revealed multiple tail protein genes, many of which remain uncharacterized. Determining a high-resolution structure of the phage particle will be critical to elucidate how these proteins coordinate alternative infection pathways.

Another intriguing feature is the Flag-TA system, which functions as a temperature-dependent bacterial defense mechanism against phage X1 infection. While temperature-dependent nuclease activity has been described in the HREX system [39], in the case of Flag-TA, temperature-induced flagellum production is the key factor. The involvement of the stator unit in activating this defense highlights a previously unrecognized sensory role for the flagellum. While canonical TA systems are typically triggered by intracellular stress signals [26, 40–44] or transcriptional changes [45–47], the Flag-TA system appears to be uniquely responsive to structural perturbation or recognition events occurring at the flagellar level. This adds a new layer of complexity to the arms race between bacteria and phages, expanding our understanding of bacterial immune strategies beyond the cell body to include motility apparatus as both a phage receptor and immune sensor. It is also worth noting the similarity between ZorAB of the Zorya defense system and MotAB, the motor protein complex involved in flagellar rotation [20]. The membrane-embedded ZorAB of the Zorya defense system shares significant structural similarities with MotAB [48–50]. This resemblance raises the possibility that flagellar motor proteins serve dual functions in motility and phage sensing. Despite these intriguing parallels, the molecular mechanism linking MotAB-mediated sensing to Flag-TA activation, along with the potential involvement of a dedicated signal transducer, remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Together, our findings redefine the bacterial flagellum as a multifunctional organelle: not only a locomotive apparatus and phage receptor, but also a critical node in the innate immune response. The dual role of the flagellum, facilitating phage infection while triggering abortive defense, reflects a delicate evolutionary balance. Phages such as X1 have evolved sophisticated strategies to exploit flagellar structures, while bacteria have, in turn, adapted these vulnerabilities into phage surveillance systems. This arms race underscores the versatility of both phages and their bacterial hosts and highlights the flagellum as a central player in microbial conflict.
