Introduction

When we, Wangari Kinyanjui and Amy Patel, started our partnership while both at our respective medical schools, neither of us was oblivious to the challenges faced by low- and middle-income country (LMIC) researchers conducting research. This collaboration between the University of Nairobi (Wangari Kinyanjui) and the University of Oxford (Patel) was a tool to overcome some of these challenges. However, there were unexpected challenges which surfaced as we tried to conduct our research, which have resulted from decades of a research system which has disadvantaged LMIC interests.

Here, we outline our model for research collaboration and highlight some of the challenges which persist when conducting systematic reviews in LMICs.

An equitable and ethical model for high-income country (HIC) involvement in low- and middle-income country (LMIC)

How HIC researchers conduct research in LMICs has changed. However, LMICs continue to bear the burden of a lingering power dynamic which has allowed HIC-led research, under the guise of the innocent pursuit of knowledge, to predominate. In reality, HIC motivations have included research being cheaper to conduct in LMICs and requiring less stringent ethical approval.1

Rethinking research in LMICs is not as simple as withdrawing HIC input altogether. Many HICs are former colonial powers and are answerable for the economic and political instability that has prevented decades of institutionalised medical research and training from being established. HICs should actively support and invest in strengthening LMIC research systems, while LMICs, in turn, should engage by shaping research agendas, contributing local expertise and sustainably fostering long-term systems that enable independent research leadership.

Our answer is collaboration, where HIC researchers join LMIC-led research to share knowledge, experience, resources and, most importantly, funding, much more easily acquired as an HIC researcher. Often, this involves HIC and LMIC researchers publishing jointly as first authors. However, although this practice achieves equality, it does not achieve equity, where equity stands to offer someone with less privilege more and someone with more privilege less. As an established HIC researcher, it is vastly easier to be published, progress in your career and receive funding. Although we must incentivise HIC researchers to support LMIC research, levelling the playing field means establishing LMIC ownership of research and independent first authorship which will increase exposure and future funding irrespective of HIC involvement.

Long-term research relationships, even if striving to be collaborative, may have imbalances of power which must be interrogated. Often the HIC ends up deciding which research areas and which LMIC partnerships to invest in, when instead LMIC researchers should be choosing both the research questions and the HIC researchers that they trust to work with. Redundancy describes the practice of HICs collaborating on LMIC research while simultaneously establishing sustainable training and infrastructure in the LMIC such that the HIC becomes superfluous. We must question whether HIC researchers are practising ethically if they are not actively pursuing their own redundancy and striving for true collaboration with long-term equitable outcomes.

The role of HIC-determined standards in research inequity

In our current research system, power is held by journals and funders, mostly from HICs. Therefore, LMICs are forced to conform to HIC set standards to receive funding and publish their research.

An example of an HIC set standard is that systematic reviews are the highest tier of evidence,2 but this standard should not be assumed to hold the same importance in LMICs. An LMIC researcher wishing to publish a systematic review must conduct it using HIC-determined protocols. These protocols make assumptions about the contexts in which LMIC researchers are operating. For example, that all valid, generated data have been published and that the journals in which data have been published are registered on main databases.3

We came across this issue when conducting a literature search on appendicitis in LMIC settings. When we searched large databases such as MedLine, Scopus and Embase, the search did not yield results representative of the current knowledge. However, when we began to search the grey literature, materials and research that are produced outside of traditional publishing and academic channels, we found research relevant and important to the topic we were reviewing.

LMIC authors: the publishing dilemma

To publish in open-access journals, hefty publishing fees must be paid which, without access to funding, may not be possible. The alternative to subscription-access journals is equally unattractive, requiring expensive subscription, without which the intended audience cannot access the literature. Furthermore, even when LMIC researchers choose to submit to journals, editorial representation is not always diverse, increasing implicit bias risk.4

Some LMIC authors opt to publish in smaller, alternative journals operating a consumer-per-page fee model providing a compromise between affordability and accessibility.5 Alternative journals are often labelled as ‘predatory’, insinuating they exploit authors in need of somewhere accessible to publish.6 These journals offer less rigorous peer review, leading to the assumption that their literature is of low quality. This literature is rarely included on main databases and therefore in systematic reviews.

Together, this presents a dilemma for LMIC authors where they are either facing the inaccessibility of publishing in international journals or their research being discredited if they choose the only realistic and affordable option when publishing in independent, alternative journals.

As young doctors and researchers, we must consider our research relationships with our LMIC partners. We must collaborate and focus on how best to be impactful to our LMIC partners—HICs may have a role to play in developing research capability through supporting with funding and education. In addition, we must reflect on how to make our research practices more equitable, such as by thoroughly searching grey literature and alternative journals when conducting systematic reviews in LMIC settings.
