Establishment and development of the gut microbiota in dairy goats during the early life
Authors: Zhannur Niyazbekova, Kuanysh Kassen, Yuecai Jiang, Dengliang Li, Yuxu Qi, Damir Khussainov, Zhanat Batanova, Dekun Chen, Wentao Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe bacterial population in the gut microbiota plays a crucial role in animal health and productivity. Despite this importance, the establishment and development of a microbial community in the Saanen dairy goat during the early stages of life have not been thoroughly quantified or functionally investigated. Therefore, the present study considered the initial establishment of the gut microbiota in the goat kids and the role of maternal factors, including goat milk and maternal gut microbiota as a source of these microorganisms.ResultsIn this study, a total of 81 samples were collected from healthy dairy goats, consisting of 27 milk samples and 54 fecal samples (27 from maternal goats and 27 from their kids). The samples were collected on days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 14 postpartum, with 5–6 samples taken each day from milk, maternal feces, and kid feces. To analyze microbial communities, 16 S rRNA gene sequencing was performed. The maternal milk exhibited significantly higher microbial richness and diversity compared to fecal samples. Beta diversity analysis revealed distinct microbial compositions between maternal fecal and kids’ meconium samples on the day of birth. Significant microbial sharing was observed in meconium, with taxa such as Atopostipes suicloacalis (p ≤ 0.01), and Escherichia, Turicibacter sanguinis, and Romboutsia sedimentorum (p ≤ 0.05). These findings suggest that the maternal gut and the kids’ meconium have distinct microbial compositions on the day of birth, despite sharing some dominant bacterial families. According to taxonomy data, Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae, and Bacteroidaceae were the most prevalent bacterial groups in all maternal milk and maternal and kid feces. In addition, maternal milk and gut microbiota significantly influenced the composition of kids’ gut microbiota in early life.ConclusionsThese findings provide valuable insights into the microbial dynamics during the early stages of life and highlight the significant role of maternal factors, such as milk and gut microbiota, in shaping the microbial communities of offspring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-04958-8.

Introduction

The gut microbial population is critical for animal growth, including immune response maturation, metabolism, nutrient digestion and utilization, and the prevention of pathogenic colonization [1–3]. In ruminants, the establishment of gut microbiota follows a well-defined succession, progressing through three stages: initial colonization, transition, and maturation [4, 5]. The early composition and diversity of the gut microbiota are influenced by several factors including maternal influence, feeding management, and environmental exposure [6–8]. For instance, close maternal contact facilitates the transfer of microbes through the mother’s skin, milk, saliva, and feces, which contributes significantly to shaping the neonate’s gut microbiome [7]. Nutrition and host phylogeny also play important roles in determining microbial richness, structure, and function in young animals [9, 10]. The early-life gut microbiota is typically dominated by four phyla, including Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, and Bacteroidetes [11, 12]. Within this community, bacterial families such as Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae, which belong to the phylum Firmicutes, have been commonly detected in goat kids [13].

Recent studies have shown that the dietary supplementation of methionine in the maternal diet in the late stages of pregnancy in dairy cows has a beneficial impact on small intestinal functionality and health [14]. Additionally, maternal milk serves not only as a nutritional source but also as a carrier of diverse microbial species [15]. This includes beneficial and potentially probiotic bacteria such as Streptococcus, Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Faecalibacterium, and Acinetobacter, which colonize the neonate’s intestine during the feeding [16].

Despite these findings, relatively few studies have investigated how maternal factors influence the overall diversity and functional capacity of the intestinal microbiota in dairy goat kids. In particular, little is known about how microbial communities cooperate and compete during the early stages of gut ecosystem development. To expand our understanding of gut microbial development during early life in dairy goats, we evaluated the composition of bacterial communities in the maternal milk, maternal gut, and infant gut using 16 S rRNA sequencing analysis, aiming to identify potential maternal sources that contribute to early microbial colonization in goat kids. Materials and methods.

Animals and experimental design

The study was carried out at the Saanen dairy goat breeding center in Shaanxi province, China, after approval by the Ethical Committee for Animal Experimentation of Northwest Agriculture and Forestry University (approval no. 2021NWAFU0114). In the present study, twenty-seven pregnant goats aged between 2 and 5 years were randomly selected, and their living quarters were a large farm barn with other goats. None of the trial’s goat participants had any antibiotic or other medical treatments within 3 months before sampling.

Adult goats were fed with a total mixed ration (TMR), and diets were offered twice daily and water was freely available. Feed ingredients and the chemical composition of diets were described in the supplementary material (Supplementary Table S1). After birth, the kids were kept in small pens to prevent them from contact with other adult goats. The kids fed on maternal milk through natural suckling and did not receive any supplementary feeding material. Colostrum feeding began within half an hour of birth and was repeated four to five times a day.

Sample collection

We collected a total of 81 samples, including fresh milk samples (MM, n = 27), fresh maternal fecal samples (MF, n = 27), and kids’ fecal samples (KF, n = 27), at 1, 3, 5, 7, and 14 days after delivery. Meconium (the dark green newborn’s first feces) samples were collected within the few hours after birth, before the first suckling of the kids. Fresh fecal samples were collected directly from the rectum of goats using sterile gloves and forceps to ensure minimal contamination. Milk samples, including colostrum and mature milk, were collected from each animal (1 mL per sample) before the milking. Udder teats were cleaned with wipes soaked in 70% ethanol before collection. Sampling was carried out in accordance with the normative guidelines in the Laboratory Handbook of the National Mastitis Council [17].

After collection, all samples were transferred to sterile plastic tubes and kept in an icebox for a short time before being transported to the lab, then subsequently frozen at − 80 °C and stored until the next processing.

Genomic DNA extraction

The DNA was extracted from 100 to 150 mg of milk samples using the DNeasy PowerFood Microbial Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Nordrhein-Westfalen, Germany) and fecal samples using the MagPure Stool DNA KF kit B (Magen, Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations [18, 19]. DNA concentration was quantified using a Qubit fluorometer with the Qubit double-stranded (ds) DNA BR (Broad-Range) test kit (Invitrogen, Headquarters, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). DNA quality was assessed by visualising bands on a 1% (w/v) agarose gel. The DNA samples were stored at 4 °C until library preparation.

Library preparation and sequencing

The V3 and V4 regions of 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes from each sample were amplified with 341 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806R (5’- GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’) PCR primers [20]. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification was performed in a 50 µl reaction containing PCR primer, master mix, and 20 ng of genomic DNA. PCR thermal cycling was performed at 94 °C for 3 min of initial denaturation, followed by 30 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s of denaturation, 56 °C for 45 s of annealing, and 72 °C for 45 s of elongation in each cycle, with a 10-minute final extension at 72 °C. The duplicated PCR products were purified using AmpureXP beads, eluted in the elution buffer, and quantified with an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The amplified V3 and V4 regions of the 16 S rRNA gene were then sequenced as 300-bp paired-ends reads on an Illumina MiSeq platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China) using Illumina’s standard workflows.

Bioinformatical and statistical analysis

Paired-end reads were merged using the Fast Length Adjustment of Short Reads program (FLASH, version 1.2.11) [21]. High-quality clean reads were produced by performing quality filtering on the raw tags under filtering conditions in the QIIME program (version 1.8.0). Chimeric sequences were verified and removed using the UCHIME algorithm (Undetected chimeras, version 4.2.40 http://www.drive5.com/usearch/manual/uchime_algo.html) [22, 23]. Furthermore, the sequencing analysis was performed using UPARSE software (version 7.0.1090 http://drive5.com/uparse/), where sequences sharing ≥ 97% similarity were grouped into operational taxonomic units (OTUs). The Ribosomal Database Project (RDP, version 2.2 https://sourceforge.net/projects/rdp-classifier/) classifier was used to taxonomically classify the same OTU sequences. To generate an OTU abundance statistics table for each sample, the USEARH global was used to compare all tags to the original OTUs [24].

Representative OTUs with a high number of occurrences and annotation were selected for taxonomic metadata using the Mothur algorithm (version 1.31.2) [20]. The Shannon (http://www.mothur.org/wiki/Shannon) and Chao (http://www.mothur.org/wiki/Chao) indices were calculated by QIIME to investigate alpha diversity [25], and were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, displayed with R software. For beta diversity analysis, Bray-Curtis distance and un-weighted Unifrac distance were used to determine the relationships between milk, maternal, and kid fecal intestinal microbiomes, and the Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) was used for visualization [26]. PERMANOVA and ANOSIM were performed to examine significant differences between various groups. Normality of taxonomic abundance data was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value (* 0.01 < p ≤ 0.05, ** 0.001 < p < 0.01, *** p ≤ 0.001). The Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States (PICRUSt) program was used to analyse the metagenome functions of milk and maternal and kids’ gut microbiome compositions. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) databases were used to identify functional metagenome pathways [27].

Results

Sequencing summary

In this study, we analyzed 81 samples, including 27 maternal milk (MM), 27 maternal fecal (MF), and 27 kid fecal (KF) samples collected from dairy goats in Shaanxi Province, China, using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. As previously stated, we found 17,380,500 reads from the sequencing platform. For the next study, we processed 4,262,707 high-quality read sequence reads, with an average length of 433 bases, after quality-filtering (as detailed in the methods).

Microbial diversity of goat milk, maternal and kids’ feces

After quality filtering with the coverage of 97% sequence similarity, the 16 S rRNA amplicon data set produced 4,262,707 high-quality reads. In total, the sequences were clustered into 5417 OTUs from all MM, MF, and KF samples, of which 1936 were core OTUs, respectively. The core OTUs account for 35.7% of the full OTUs. Moreover, the 4804, 2271, and 3829 OTUs were detected in MM, MF, and KF samples. Among them, 1,326 OTUs were unique to MM, 63 OTUs were unique to MF, and 477 OTUs were unique to KF. Additionally, 199 OTUs were shared between MM and MF, 1,343 OTUs between MM and KF, and 73 OTUs between MF and KF. (Fig. 1A). In addition, the core number of OTUs between the MM and KF samples (1343 OTUs) was higher than the number between the MF and KF samples (73 OTUs). According to these results, the number of OTUs in kids is remarkably similar to that in maternal milk.

In the alpha diversity analysis, the Shannon and Chao1 indexes were designed to examine the richness and diversity of the microbiota in the MM, MF, and KF samples. The Shannon index showed significant differences between the MF and KF (p ≤ 0.001) and in the MM and KF (p ≤ 0.001) samples, but no significant differences between the MF and MM samples. (Fig. 1B). However, significant differences in Chao richness were detected between the MF and MM (p ≤ 0.01), MF and KF (p ≤ 0.001), and MM and KF (p ≤ 0.01; Supplementary Figure S1). The microbial diversity of milk, maternal and kids’ feces all significantly differed from each other in diversity and richness level comparisons of alpha diversity. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test confirmed that the variety of the maternal (MM, MF) microbiome was significantly higher (p ≤ 0.001) than the kids’ (KF) microbiome (Fig. 1B). These results suggest that the microbial diversity differs between the udder milk, maternal, and fecal samples of dairy goats.

To compare the similarities among the bacterial communities between the milk and maternal-kid stool samples, the principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) was associated with the OTU abundance of each sample and was created on the unweighted unifrac distance metric (Fig. 1C). The PCoA analysis revealed significant differences among MM, MF, and KF samples (PERMANOVA, F = 16.25, R² = 0.294, p < 0.0001). The two principal coordinates explained 31.39% (PCoA1) and 14.13% (PCoA2) of the total variation. The maternal milk influenced the kids’ gut microbial structure. For example, some samples showed distinct clustering with maternal milk based on unweighted unifrac distance.

Nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) was performed to further clarify the difference among the bacterial population of the MM, MF, and KF samples, using the Bray-Curtis similarity at the OTU level for all samples. As shown in Supplementary Figure S2, KF samples formed distinct clusters, while MM samples were very close to MF samples (ANOSIM, p < 0.0001).

According to MM, MF, and KF samples, the amplicon-sequencing results revealed the top five bacterial phylum-level contributions to the microbiota. In MF, Firmicutes (72%) and Bacteroidetes (20%) were the most abundant phyla. In MM, the dominant phyla were Firmicutes (30%), Proteobacteria (20%), and Bacteroidetes (17%). Similarly, in KF, Firmicutes (37%), Bacteroidetes (28%), and Proteobacteria (28%) were the most prevalent. Additionally, Actinobacteria, Cyanobacteria, and Spirochaetes were detected as predominant phyla in all samples collected from samples MM, MF, and KF (Fig. 1D).

The dynamics of microbial communities in goat milk, maternal and kids’ feces

To characterize microbial diversity in early life, we collected 27 goat kids’ fecal samples from goat kids at birth (meconium) and 3, 5, 7, and 14 days of age. Additionally, we collected 27 goat milk samples and 27 maternal stool samples at kidding and at 3, 5, 7, and 14 days postpartum. The Shannon index was used to assess the evenness and richness of microbial diversity. On day 5, the Shannon index was higher in maternal milk and fecal samples compared to days 1, 3, 7, and 14. Over time, microbial richness increased from day 1 to day 5, and then decreased from day 7 to day 14 in MM samples. However, there were no considerable differences over time. Interestingly, we discovered that the microbial community in the kids’ meconium (first feces of a newborn) on day 1 exhibited significantly higher alpha diversity compared to days 3–14 (Wilcox, p < 0.05; Fig. 2).

To analyze the taxonomic composition of the microbial population in this cohort, we performed amplicon-sequencing targeting the V3 and V4 variable regions of the bacterial 16 S rRNA genes and constructed species-level composition profiles for all 81 samples. At the family level, Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae, and Bacteroidaceae were the dominant communities detected in the MF, MM and KF microbiota (Fig. 3A, C). The relative abundance of Ruminococcaceae was stable in MM and MF samples (Fig. 3A, B), but decreased in KF samples from day 1 to 14 (Fig. 3C). Nevertheless, the abundance of Lachnospiraceae increased in KF samples from day 5 to day 14 (Fig. 3C). Interestingly, during this period, Lachnospiraceae in maternal milk suddenly decreased, while their abundance in fecal samples remained stable (Fig. 3A, B). Moreover, the high abundance of Enterobacteriaceae in KF dramatically increased from birth to day 5, and then stabilized until 2 weeks (Fig. 3C). In contrast, Enterobacteriaceae abundance presented an opposite trend in MM samples with significant reduction from day 1 to day 5, and was not detected in MF samples (Fig. 3A, B). Bacteroidaceae were significantly enriched in KF samples from day 5 to day 7 (Fig. 3C), whereas in MM and MF samples, their abundance gradually declined from day 1 to day 14 (Fig. 3A, B).

At the genus level, Bacteroides, Clostridium, and Escherichia were the dominant genera in MM samples on the 1 st day after birth, with average abundances of 12.34%, 2.68%, and 2.02%, respectively. The gut microbiota of KF was primarily dominated by Bacteroides and Clostridium over time. Escherichia (p ≤ 0.01) was significantly enriched from meconium to day 5, then stabilized until day 14. The abundances of Bacteroides (p ≤ 0.01) and Clostridium (p ≤ 0.05) significantly increased between days 5 and 14 in KF samples after birth. Initially, Corynebacterium was not detected in MF samples on days 3 and 5, but a small amount (0.0025%, no significance) was found on day 7. By day 14, it was no longer detected in any MM, MF, and KF. Some bacterial genera were detected only on specific days. For example, in the MM samples Prevotella, Pantoea, and Treponema (p ≤ 0.01) were dominant on day 7, while Brucella was detected on days 5 (p ≤ 0.01) and 7 (p ≤ 0.01). In MF samples, Oscillibacter (p ≤ 0.001) and Alistipes were dominant on day 1. In KF samples, Parabacteroides (p ≤ 0.01) and Fusobacterium were dominant on day 14 (p ≤ 0.05; Supplementary table S2). The relative abundances at the genus level exhibited individual-specific differences and were subject to intra-individual differences over time.

The gut microbiota differentiates between the mother and the kids on the day of birth

To discover potential transfer of microbial stains from the mother goat and their subsequent establishment in the kid’s intestine, we compared the maternal fecal (MF) and the kids’ meconium samples on the first day after birth. A total of 3837 OTUs were identified from MF and meconium samples, of which 1794 were present in all groups and defined as core OTUs. The core OTUs comprised approximately 46.7% of the total OTUs. In addition, 168 OTUs were uniquely identified in MF, while 1875 unique OTUs were found in meconium (Fig. 4A). According to diversity analysis, the microbial ecology of maternal feces (MF) was significantly lower (p ≤ 0.001) than that of the meconium samples (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, we analyzed the relative abundance profiles of bacterial species found in maternal (MF) and kids’ meconium samples. Phascolarctobacterium succinatutens and Succinivibrio dextrinosolvens were detected in both MF and meconium, showing similarity but no significant differences. As expected, microbial community analysis revealed significantly high frequency sharing of Atopostipes suicloacalis (p ≤ 0.01), as well as Escherichia, Turicibacter sanguinis, and Romboutsia sedimentorum (p ≤ 0.05) in meconium samples (Fig. 4C). These findings indicate a distinct and diverse microbial community in kids’ intestines at birth compared to maternal feces. Functional prediction of goat milk, maternal and kids’ feces.

To investigate the microbiome of maternal milk (MM), maternal (MF), and kids’ (KF) feces, we used PICRUSt (Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States) to estimate metabolic activities based on 16sRNA genes [28]. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to determine the statistical significance of gene distribution among MM, MF, and KF samples. At the first level of KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, http://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.html) pathway classification, six subcategories were identified: “Organismal systems”, “Metabolism”, “Human diseases”, “Genetic information processing”, “Cellular processes”, and “Environmental information processing”. Among all MM, MF, and KF samples, the metabolic pathway (79%) had the highest relative abundance (Supplementary Figure S3A).

At the second level of KEGG pathway analysis, pathways associated with carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid metabolism, metabolism of other amino acids, replication and repair, and energy metabolism were enriched in all MM, MF, and KF samples, with average abundances of 13.8%, 12.6%, 7.2%, 5.8%, and 5.5%, respectively, with no significant differences among the groups. However, the transport and catabolism, environmental adaptation, endocrine system (0.2%), and infectious diseases: bacterial (0.4%) pathways were detected at low abundances. In KF samples, metabolism of cofactors and vitamins (p = 0.02), biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites (p = 0.04), glycan biosynthesis and metabolism (p = 0.004), and signal transduction (p = 0.002) were significantly increased. In contrast, metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides (p = 0.0003) was significantly enriched in MF samples, while lipid metabolism (p = 0.0002) was notably increased in MM samples (Fig. 5A).

The level 3 KEGG pathway analysis presented that the microbiota of MF samples was enriched with functions related to amino acid metabolism, including leucine, isoleucine, and valine biosynthesis (2.4%), D-Glutamine and D-glutamate metabolism (2.3%), and lysine biosynthesis (1.8%), cysteine and methionine metabolism (1.4%). Among all groups, the biosynthesis of ansamycins was the most abundant metabolic function, particularly in the MF and KF, representing 5.2% and 4.8%, respectively. In addition to ansamycins, other antibiotic biosynthesis pathways were also detected, such as streptomycin biosynthesis and biosynthesis of vancomycin group antibiotics (Supplementary Figure S3B). Moreover, we detected a high frequency of functional community interactions across milk and maternal, and kids’ feces (Fig. 5).

A total of 25 clusters of orthologous groups (COG) were identified from the 16 s RNA gene sequencing data using PICRUSt. Among these, 8 COG genes showed significant differences among MM, MF, and KF samples. Specifically, signal transduction mechanisms (p = 0.0002), defense mechanisms (p = 0.002236), nucleotide transport and metabolism (p = 4.70E-05), cell motility (p = 1.00E-06), and amino acid transport and metabolism (p = 6.90E-05) were significantly enriched in MM samples. In contrast, cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis (p = 0.009), intracellular trafficking, secretion, and vesicular transport (p = 0.0002), and extracellular structures (p = 1.30E-05) were significantly enriched in MF samples. Functionally, the relative abundance of COG gene families was higher in MM and MF samples than in KF samples (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the microbial composition of maternal milk and fecal samples, as well as the relationship between maternal and kids’ microbial communities and their functional prediction during early life. To investigate bacterial diversity, we examined goat milk, maternal feces, and corresponding kid fecal samples at different time points postpartum using 16 S rRNA sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq sequencing platform.

Various factors, including maternal milk, living conditions and geographic location, and antibiotic therapy, influence the establishment and development of a neonate’s intestinal microbiota in early life [7, 29]. Maternal milk serves as a complex of commensal and potential beneficial bacteria, contributing to the initial colonization of the neonatal gut and supporting immune maturation in the early life stage of development [30]. Several studies have reported that maternal milk exhibits greater microbial diversity compared to the maternal and neonatal intestinal microbiome [31].

In our study, microbial diversity (Shannon and Chao indices) was lower in newborn intestinal samples, whereas maternal milk and fecal samples exhibited higher microbial diversity. Previous research has shown that the bacterial composition in maternal milk changes with lactation stage, from colostrum to mature milk. Our first study also confirmed that microbial diversity in the milk of Saanen dairy goats changed across different lactation periods. The most prevalent genera in colostrum included Lactobacillus, Variovorax, Rothia, Novosphingobium, Psychrobacter, Brachybacterium, Yersinia, Coxiella, Roseateles, Sanguibacter, Cloacibacterium, Sphingobacterium. In contrast, mature milk contained Aeromonadales, Herbaspirillum, Yaniella, Planomicrobium, Turicibacter, Cupriavidus, Cloacibacterium, Azospirillum, Rhodobacteraceae, Peptostreptococcus, Bacteroidales, and Georgenia [32]. Consistent with previous studies on human and bovine milk [33, 34], we found that Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Firmicutes were the predominant bacterial phyla in goat milk. Firmicutes were also dominant in the maternal intestine, with their relative abundance increasing over time. Interestingly, the bacterial composition of caprine milk fluctuated throughout early lactation: richness increased from day 1 to day 5 but declined from day 7 to day 14. Among individual bacterial taxa, Escherichia, Bacteroides, and Clostridium were abundant in maternal milk on the first day postpartum but decreased over time. This could be due to the milk microbiota not being stable at the beginning of lactation or period of the change from colostrum to mature milk [32, 35]. Interestingly, we found a relative abundance of Brucella only on days 5 (p ≤ 0.01) and 7 in maternal milk (p ≤ 0.01). While milk and fecal microbiota are inherently different, our goal was to assess the role of milk as a potential microbial source for early gut colonization. Although this has been demonstrated in other livestock species [36, 37], such dynamics remain underexplored in goats during early life.

The maternal gut microbiota in sheep and goats was dominated by Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae [13, 38]. We also found that the phylum Proteobacteria, which includes facultative anaerobes such as the Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae families, dominated fecal samples collected from mothers. Notably, Enterobacteriaceae decreased considerably in maternal milk from day 1 to 14, while Bacteroidaceae and Clostridium increased in the kids’ gut. The first microbial components that establish the gastrointestinal environment as totally anaerobic during the initial stage of the gut microbiota are anaerobic bacteria, which include numerous members of the Enterobacteriaceae, Lachnospiraceae, and the Fusobacterium families [39, 40].

Bacterial diversity has been reported to increase with age in ruminants, such as calves and goats [13, 41–43]. In the present study, we observed the opposite trend: microbial diversity in kids’ meconium samples (newborn’s first fecal) was higher than in maternal milk and maternal fecal samples on the first day after birth, but decreased over time. Based on these results, we hypothesized that the bacterial taxa may be transmitted directly from the maternal gut to the kids’ gut during birth [8, 44]. Another likely route of early microbial transfer is maternal licking, which occurs immediately after birth and may expose the newborn to the mother’s oral and skin microbiota [45]. To investigate this, we individually compared maternal and kid feces on the first day. Interestingly, we observed that microbial diversity and taxonomic composition were significantly different between the kids’ meconium and maternal fecal samples on the first day after birth. As observed in this study, meconium samples were significantly dominated by bacterial taxa such as Escherichia, Romboutsia sedimentorum (p 0.05), and Atopostipes suicloacalis (p 0.01), all of which have been previously described in fecal samples from different populations [46–48]. Romboutsia sedimentorum was originally detected in the sediment of an alkaline-saline lake, where it ferments glucose to generate acetic acid, ethanol, isobutanoic and isovaleric acids, all of which have potential health benefits [48, 49]. However, there is a scarcity of studies on its physiological functions. The high diversity observed in meconium samples may result from direct or indirect transmission through maternal and environmental factors during or after birth [8, 50]. Additionally, Phascolarctobacterium succinatutens and Succinivibrio dextrinosolvens, both previously isolated from feces, may be transferred from the mother’s intestine to the kids’ and colonize their intestine. Although no in-depth assessment of this mechanism has been conducted, vertical transfer of gut microbes from mother to offspring is considered a major pathway for microbiota colonization in neonates [8, 51]. In this study, we also found that some bacterial genera were common in the kids than in maternal milk and their feces, which could be vertically transmitted. However, our data also demonstrated that microbial strains such as Parabacteroides and Fusobacterium, which are present in maternal milk and the gut, may not serve as a source of microbial strains for the kids’ intestine. Further studies are needed to determine the microbial communities of the amniotic fluid, placenta, fetal membranes, and other locations that may influence the composition of the kids’ gut microbiota via the intestinal-fetal or oral-fetal-placental routes. In addition, future research focusing on later developmental stages and incorporating rumen fluid sampling would offer a more comprehensive understanding of archaeal populations and rumen-specific microbial contributions.

We explored the function of bacteria using PICRUSt based on the 16 S rRNA gene data to better understand the impact of the microbial community in caprine milk, as well as in maternal and kids’ feces. The gastrointestinal tract contains a significant number of microorganisms that play essential roles in various physiological processes, including metabolic and nutritional balance, immune system maturation and activation, and brain function [52, 53]. Comparative analysis of gut microbial metabolic function capacities indicated that KEGG pathway analysis indicated an increase in the metabolism of terpenoids, polyketides, and lipids (p = 0.001) in the maternal fecal and goat milk samples, whereas pathways related to the biosynthesis of other secondary cofactors, and vitamins (p = 0.05), metabolism of signal transduction, and glycan biosynthesis (p ≤ 0.01), were more abundant in the KF samples. The dynamic shifts in gut microbiota at different taxonomic levels may be associated with these changes in functional profiles. For example, in the present study, carbohydrate metabolism emerged as an important source of nutrients and energy in the host gut microbiota, being enriched in milk and maternal and kids’ feces. Previous research has linked the metabolism of carbohydrates to a higher proportion of Lachnospiraceae, Lactobacillaceae, and Ruminococcaceae [38]. In addition, the innate immunity of the kid gut microbiota is modulated by the metabolism of amino acids and carbohydrates [54]. Moreover, vitamins generated by the intestinal microbiota may help prevent oxidative stress and inflammation, which are important for maintaining intestinal homeostasis and have been associated with increased food intake during pregnancy in mice [55, 56]. Previous researchers found that the metabolism of polyketides, terpenoids, and lipids was more active in the gut microbiota of mice when conjugated linoleic acid was used [57]. Lipid metabolism is a crucial and complex metabolic process, and the higher comparative abundance of Bacteroides in the kid’s intestine may help control body weight and fat content [58]. Pathways for the biosynthesis of ansamycins, vancomycin group antibiotics, and streptomycin were identified in both maternal and kid gut microbiota. These secondary metabolites have broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties, highlighting the gut microbiota’s potential to produce bioactive compounds that support microbial balance and host defense [59].

Conclusion

Our study characterized the establishment and development of goat kids’ intestinal microbiota during the first two weeks of life. The most common bacterial phyla in the maternal milk, maternal and kid feces were Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria. Additionally, our findings showed that the same bacterial genera of Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae, and Bacteroidaceae were present in maternal milk and their fecal samples, as well as in matched kid feces. These results suggest the existence of microbiota specific to each mother-kid pair that may provide unique benefits to the offspring. Moreover, diversity analysis revealed significant differences between the gut microbiota of kids and mothers, with bacterial species such as Escherichia, Turicibacter sanguinis, Romboutsia sedimentorum (p ≤ 0.05), and Atopostipes suicloacalis (p ≤ 0.01) being more prevalent in kids’ feces during the first day after birth. Further research is needed to fully understand the interactions between kids’ gut microbiota and maternal factors such as amniotic fluid, placenta, fetal membranes, and other maternal and environmental factors. Additionally, KEGG pathway classification predicted that metabolic pathways such as carbohydrate metabolism (13.8%), amino acid metabolism (12.6%), other amino acid metabolisms (7.2%), repair and replication (5.8%), and metabolism of energy (5.5%) were enriched in the caprine milk, maternal feces, and kid fecal samples. Overall, our study highlights that maternal milk and gut microbial composition may play a crucial role in the establishment and development of the gut microbiota in goat kids.
