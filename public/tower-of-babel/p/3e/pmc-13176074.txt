Oral low-dose methotrexate improves infliximab pharmacokinetics, immunogenicity, and endoscopic outcomes in Crohn’s disease: a propensity score-adjusted real-world study
Authors: Shangzhan Huang, Dongyan Li, Tingting Qin, Feng Tang, Runzhao Ma, Yinxian Shen, Haoying Liu, Jin Gong, Qionghui Huang, Meng Ke, Li Wang, Anqi Dai, Juan Li, Jiazhi Liao, Fang Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe effectiveness of infliximab in Crohn's disease is often compromised by immunogenicity. Oral low-dose methotrexate may offer a promising strategy to optimize infliximab therapy, yet its real-world effectiveness and safety remain uncertain.AimThis study aimed to compare the effectiveness and safety of infliximab plus oral low-dose methotrexate versus infliximab monotherapy.MethodIn this single-center retrospective cohort study, 119 patients with Crohn's disease received either infliximab monotherapy (n = 99) or combination therapy with oral low-dose methotrexate (10–15 mg/week; n = 20). The primary outcome was endoscopic remission (Simple Endoscopic Score for Crohn’s Disease ≤ 2) at week 26. Propensity score methods, including nearest-neighbor matching and inverse probability of treatment weighting, were used to adjust for confounding. Conditional and weighted logistic regression were used to estimate treatment effects.ResultsAfter propensity score adjustment, combination therapy was associated with a significantly higher rate of endoscopic remission (aOR 3.70, 95% CI 1.16–11.75; P = 0.027). No significant differences were observed in endoscopic response or clinical outcomes. In a pharmacokinetic subgroup, combination therapy resulted in numerically higher infliximab trough concentrations and no detectable anti-drug antibodies (0% vs. 16.7%). Higher trough concentrations were associated with endoscopic remission (P = 0.033). The incidence of elevated liver enzymes was higher in the combination group (20.0% vs. 4.0%), but this difference was not statistically significant after adjustment (P = 0.058).ConclusionIn patients with Crohn's disease, oral low-dose methotrexate combined with infliximab was associated with significantly improved endoscopic remission at week 26 compared to infliximab monotherapy, without a statistically significant increase in adverse events.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11096-026-02098-z.

Impact statements

Immunogenicity remains a major challenge in infliximab therapy for Crohn's disease, leading to therapeutic failure in a significant proportion of patients.Low-dose oral methotrexate in combination with infliximab has shown promise in reducing anti-drug antibody formation and improving drug exposure, potentially enhancing therapeutic outcomes.Implementing low-dose oral methotrexate as an adjunct to infliximab in clinical practice could offer a safer, more accessible strategy for optimizing treatment effectiveness and long-term remission in patients with Crohn's disease.This approach may reduce reliance on more toxic immunomodulators, minimize the risk of immunogenicity, and provide a cost-effective, patient-centered alternative to enhance mucosal healing and improve clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Infliximab (IFX), a tumor necrosis factor (TNF) antagonist, has significantly improved therapeutic outcomes in Crohn’s disease (CD) [1]. Yet, approximately 43.3% [2] of patients receiving anti-TNF therapy eventually develop loss of response, often attributable to immunogenicity, namely the formation of anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) that reduce drug levels and efficacy [3, 4]. To reduce immunogenicity and maintain higher IFX trough levels, IFX is often co-administered with immunomodulators such as azathioprine (AZA) or methotrexate (MTX) [5]. The SONIC trial showed that IFX combined with AZA resulted in higher steroid-free remission rates at week 26 compared to IFX monotherapy (56.8% vs. 44.4%) [6]. Nevertheless, long-term AZA use is associated with an increased risk of lymphoma, particularly in young males, as well as opportunistic infections [7]. Furthermore, the higher prevalence of NUDT15 variants in East Asian populations, including Chinese patients, significantly elevates the risk of AZA-induced myelosuppression [8], thereby prompting the search for safer adjunctive immunomodulators.

As safety concerns continue to limit the long-term use of AZA, MTX has increasingly gained attention as a feasible alternative immunomodulator in combination with IFX. The Combination of Maintenance Methotrexate-Infliximab Trial (COMMIT), which administered 25 mg of subcutaneous MTX weekly, demonstrated that combination therapy significantly reduced the incidence of ADAs compared to IFX monotherapy (4% vs. 20%, P = 0.01) [9]. Although no corresponding improvement in clinical efficacy was noted, the immunomodulatory effect of MTX was confirmed. Pharmacokinetic studies have shown that subcutaneous MTX achieves higher plasma bioavailability and earlier accumulation of intracellular MTX polyglutamates (MTX-PG) than oral administration; however, MTX-PG levels eventually converge between routes of administration with prolonged exposure [10, 11]. Furthermore, several studies suggest comparable clinical efficacy between oral and parenteral MTX when combined with IFX in CD [12, 13]. Given its convenience, lower cost, and avoidance of injection site reactions, oral MTX may offer long-term adherence in real-world practice [13] and is increasingly recognized as a practical adjunct to IFX particularly in maintenance strategies.

When considering oral MTX as an adjunctive strategy, low-dose regimens are generally preferred due to higher oral bioavailability at doses ≤ 15 mg [14] and a lower risk of cumulative toxicity [15]. In routine clinical practice, the decision to add low-dose oral MTX to IFX therapy is individualized and typically based on perceived immunogenicity risk, suboptimal IFX exposure, contraindications, safety concerns, and patient preference. Real-world evidence suggests favorable immunomodulatory effects of this approach. A cross-sectional real-world study involving patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) reported that concomitant oral methotrexate use (mean dose 11.6 ± 5.1 mg/week) was associated with a lower prevalence of anti-infliximab antibodies (7.0% vs. 23.1%) and higher infliximab trough concentrations [16]. Additionally, a retrospective study found no significant difference in clinical outcomes between high-dose and low-dose MTX when combined with IFX in CD [17], further supporting the use of low-dose regimens. However, a critical evidence gap persists regarding whether these immunological benefits translate into improved objective endoscopic remission compared with IFX monotherapy in real-world populations. Accordingly, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to evaluate the clinical effectiveness, pharmacokinetics, immunogenicity, and safety of IFX monotherapy versus IFX combined with low-dose oral MTX (≤ 15 mg/week) in Chinese patients with CD.

Aim

This study compared the effectiveness and safety of IFX monotherapy versus IFX plus low-dose oral MTX in moderate-to-severe CD, specifically evaluating whether MTX co-administration improves endoscopic remission and drug exposure by reducing immunogenicity.

Method

Study design and participants

Patients with moderate-to-severe CD receiving IFX, with or without low-dose oral MTX, between June 2020 and January 2025 were retrospectively enrolled from Tongji Hospital (Wuhan, China). Eligible patients were ≥ 14 years old, diagnosed with moderate-to-severe CD (Crohn's Disease Activity Index [CDAI] ≥ 220, or Simple Endoscopic Score for Crohn’s Disease [SES-CD] ≥ 7, or ≥ 4 for isolated ileal disease). Exclusion criteria included: (1) incomplete endoscopic assessment data at baseline or week 26; (2) ongoing or anticipated surgical intervention; (3) active severe infections, malignancy, pregnancy or lactation; (4) prior biological therapy; (5) prior MTX use or other immunosuppressants within one month before IFX initiation; and (6) other investigator-determined unsuitability. The final cohort included 119 patients, assigned to either IFX monotherapy (n = 99) or combination therapy (IFX + MTX; n = 20). Treatment protocol and data collection

All patients received intravenous IFX (5 mg/kg) at weeks 0, 2, 6, and then every 8 weeks. Combination therapy patients also received oral MTX (10–15 mg/week) starting between weeks 2 and 6, with folic acid the following day. Baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory variables, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), Montreal classification, perianal involvement, prior perianal surgery, intestinal obstruction history, and laboratory indicators (hemoglobin [Hb], C-reactive protein [CRP], serum albumin [ALB]), were retrospectively collected. SES-CD and CDAI scores were assessed at baseline.

Outcome measures

Outcomes assessed at week 26 included primary efficacy outcome (endoscopic remission: SES-CD ≤ 2) and secondary outcomes: endoscopic response (≥ 50% SES-CD reduction), clinical remission (CDAI < 150), clinical response (≥ 70-point CDAI reduction), and liver enzyme abnormalities (alanine aminotransferase [ALT] or aspartate aminotransferase [AST] ≥ 1 × upper limit of normal [ULN]).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized as n (%) or median (IQR). To address confounding, propensity score (PS) methods were used to estimate the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT). The PS was estimated via logistic regression incorporating age, sex, BMI, Montreal classification (location and behavior), perianal involvement, prior perianal surgery, and history of intestinal obstruction. Fecal calprotectin and baseline CRP were excluded due to missing data (> 55%) and potential imputation bias, respectively. We applied 1:2 nearest-neighbor matching (NNM) and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), with covariate balance confirmed by a standardized mean difference (SMD) ≤ 0.1. Primary and secondary outcomes were analyzed using conditional (for NNM) and weighted (for IPTW) logistic regression. Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Exploratory subgroup analyses for endoscopic remission were performed using IPTW. Continuous laboratory variables were compared with Welch’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Week-14 IFX trough concentrations were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and the incidence of ADAs was analyzed with Fisher’s exact test. All analyses were two-tailed with P < 0.05. Analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.3) using MatchIt, cobalt, and survey packages.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No. TJ-IRB202506001; approved on November 6, 2024).

Comparative effectiveness of combination therapy versus monotherapy

After propensity-score adjustment, IFX combined with low-dose MTX demonstrated significantly improved mucosal healing outcomes compared to IFX monotherapy, although differences in clinical and biochemical outcomes were not statistically significant in Table 2. Specifically, combination therapy was associated with significantly increased odds of endoscopic remission (NNM OR = 3.70, 95% CI 1.16–11.75, P = 0.027; IPTW OR = 3.65; 95% CI 1.15–11.54, P = 0.029). Although estimates for endoscopic response (≥ 50% reduction in SES-CD) numerically favored combination therapy (NNM OR = 3.71; IPTW OR = 2.54), wide confidence intervals and non-significant P values (> 0.10) indicated limited statistical precision, preventing definitive conclusions. Subgroup analyses for endoscopic remission

In an exploratory subgroup analysis using IPTW, detailed in Online Resource 2, IFX plus low-dose oral MTX was associated with significantly higher odds of achieving endoscopic remission in patients with perianal disease (adjusted OR = 4.14, 95% CI 1.16–14.81; P = 0.032) and in male patients (adjusted OR = 4.10, 95% CI 1.14–14.74; P = 0.034). No statistically significant differences in achieving endoscopic remission were detected in the remaining subgroups, including patients without perianal involvement, those in different BMI categories (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 or ≥ 18.5 kg/m2), or female patients.

Given the observed numerical difference and clinical importance of liver enzyme abnormalities, propensity-score adjusted analyses were specifically focused on this outcome. After 1:2 NNM, the risk of liver dysfunction was numerically higher with IFX + MTX (adjusted OR = 2.91; 95% CI 0.68–12.45; P = 0.151). IPTW sensitivity analysis yielded a similar, though imprecise, estimate (adjusted OR = 4.38; 95% CI 0.97–19.84; P = 0.058).

Infliximab exposure, immunogenicity, and endoscopic outcomes

In the propensity-score–matched cohort, 43 of 60 patients (71.7%) had evaluable serum IFX trough concentrations (Fig. 3c) and ADA status (Fig. 3e) assessed immediately prior to the scheduled infusion at week 14. Of these, 30 patients received IFX monotherapy and 13 received IFX combined with low-dose MTX. The median IFX trough concentration was higher in the combination group (6.0 µg/mL; IQR 3.5–8.3) than in the monotherapy group (4.5 µg/mL; IQR 1.7–7.1), although the difference was not statistically significant (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P = 0.508). ADA positivity was detected in 16.7% (5/30) of patients receiving IFX monotherapy, while no ADA-positive cases were identified in the combination group. However, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.147). To assess the relationship between drug exposure and mucosal response, patients were stratified by endoscopic remission status at week 26 (Fig. 3d). Those who achieved remission (n = 22) had significantly higher median IFX trough levels (6.15 µg/mL; IQR 3.55–9.05) compared to non-responders (n = 21; 4.2 µg/mL; IQR 1.1–6.0; P = 0.033), indicating that higher serum concentrations are associated with better endoscopic outcomes.

Discussion

Although AZA is an established strategy to improve IFX effectiveness [18, 19], its clinical use is often constrained by adverse effects, particularly myelotoxicity [20–22]. This well-documented limitation underscores the need for safer alternative combination strategies. To our knowledge, this study represents the first real-world, propensity score–adjusted analysis to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of combining low-dose oral MTX (10–15 mg/week) with IFX in patients with CD. We demonstrated that concomitant low-dose oral MTX was associated with a significantly higher rate of endoscopic remission (SES-CD ≤ 2) at week 26. Importantly, this enhancement was not associated with a statistically significant increase in adverse events during the 26-week observation period. Collectively, these findings provide preliminary real-world evidence supporting low-dose oral MTX as a feasible immunomodulatory alternative for optimizing IFX therapy.

A key clinical implication of our study is that low-dose oral MTX (10–15 mg/week) appears sufficient to optimize IFX therapy when used as an adjunct to anti-TNF treatment. Traditionally, MTX has been administered parenterally at higher doses (15–25 mg/week) as a monotherapy in IBD, utilizing either subcutaneous or intramuscular injection routes [23]. However, when immunomodulators are used to optimize IFX therapy, lower doses may be adequate to achieve immunomodulatory effects. In this context, low-dose AZA has been shown to yield clinical outcomes comparable to higher doses while reducing dose-related toxicity [24, 25]. As highlighted in a recent review by Demase et al. [14], the absorption of oral MTX is a saturable, dose-dependent process, resulting in higher bioavailability at doses of ≤ 15 mg. Moreover, accumulating evidence suggests that the clinical benefit of MTX in combination with anti-TNF therapy plateaus at relatively low doses, with no additional gain at higher doses [26]. Similarly, Borren et al. reported comparable effectiveness between low-dose (≤ 12.5 mg) and higher-dose MTX when combined with anti-TNF therapy in patients with IBD [17].

Building on the rationale that low-dose oral MTX can exert clinically relevant immunomodulatory effects, our study provides real-world evidence describing its association with improved endoscopic outcomes when combined with IFX. The principal observation of this study is that low-dose oral MTX (10–15 mg/week) in combination with IFX was associated with a more than threefold increase in the odds of achieving endoscopic remission at week 26 (OR = 3.70) after adjustment for baseline confounders. Endoscopic remission was selected as the primary outcome in accordance with the treat-to-target strategy endorsed by the STRIDE-II consensus [27]. This objective endpoint has been consistently associated with improved long-term outcomes, including sustained remission, reduced hospitalization, and lower surgical risk [28, 29]. In contrast, earlier pivotal trials evaluating MTX in combination with IFX did not assess endoscopic remission as a primary endpoint [9, 16], relying instead on symptom-based indices such as CDAI. These indices are inherently subject to patient-reported variability and may not reliably reflect underlying mucosal inflammation, potentially underestimating disease activity [30–33]. Differences in endpoint selection may therefore partly explain the discrepancy between the absence of clinical benefit observed in prior studies and the endoscopic benefit observed in the present study. Nevertheless, residual confounding related to treatment selection and real-world care dynamics cannot be fully excluded. Collectively, these findings suggest that low-dose oral MTX may represent a practical adjunct to IFX, with the potential to enhance mucosal healing.

The enhanced mucosal healing with combination therapy is likely attributable, at least in part, to its favorable effects on IFX exposure. In the propensity score-matched cohort, we observed a numerical trend toward higher median IFX trough levels and a lower incidence of ADAs in the combination group compared with IFX monotherapy. Although these trends did not reach statistical significance, they are consistent with prior clinical studies demonstrating that concomitant MTX reduced ADA formation and improves IFX trough concentrations [9, 16, 34, 35]. Notably, the median trough level observed in the combination group approximated the proposed therapeutic window of 6–10 µg/mL for mucosal healing [36], whereas this threshold was not reached in the monotherapy group. Consistent with this observation, patients who achieved endoscopic remission had significantly higher trough levels than non-remitters, reinforcing the well-established association between adequate drug exposure and mucosal healing [37–40]. These findings suggest that the benefit of combination therapy may be mediated, at least in part, through improved pharmacokinetic exposure.

Despite improved endoscopic remission, combination therapy did not yield additional benefit in symptom-based outcomes, consistent with findings from prior randomized trials [9, 41]. This limited clinical advantage may reflect the high baseline symptomatic response already achieved with IFX induction in biologic-naïve patients [42, 43]. In exploratory subgroup analyses, combination therapy was associated with numerically higher endoscopic remission rates in male patients compared with IFX monotherapy; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited sample size. This aligns with pediatric data suggesting greater IFX durability in boys treated with MTX [44]. Additionally, patients with perianal disease appeared to benefit more from combination therapy, indicating potential value in this high-risk subgroup, although confirmatory studies are required.

In this study, patients receiving combination therapy showed greater reductions in CRP and improvements in ALB at week 26. These improvements may be explained, at least in part, by higher IFX trough concentrations, but may also reflect a potential synergistic anti-inflammatory effect, as previously suggested in the PANTS study [45].

In terms of safety, IFX combined with low-dose oral MTX was generally well tolerated over the 26-week follow-up period. However, the combination group exhibited a numerically higher, though non-significant, incidence of liver enzyme abnormalities, indicating a potential hepatic safety signal that warrants attention. While this difference was not statistically significant after propensity-score matching, a similar trend was observed under IPTW, likely reflecting limited statistical power. This observation is consistent with the COMMIT trial [9], in which the majority of liver enzyme abnormalities occurred in the combination therapy arm. Moreover, prior meta-analyses have demonstrated a higher risk of MTX-associated hepatotoxicity in patients with IBD compared with those with rheumatologic diseases [15]. Importantly, liver enzyme abnormalities occurred later in the combination group, underscoring the need for ongoing liver function monitoring, particularly during prolonged therapy. Larger, multicenter prospective studies with extended follow-up are required to validate these findings and to better define the long-term benefit–risk profile of low-dose oral MTX in combination with IFX.

This study has important limitations. Its retrospective, single-center design is inherently susceptible to selection bias and residual confounding, despite rigorous propensity score adjustment. The relatively small sample size, especially in the combination group, limits the precision of effect estimates and the ability to detect uncommon or delayed adverse events. Finally, the 26-week follow-up period precludes assessment of long-term remission durability and cumulative toxicity. Larger, multicenter prospective studies with extended follow-up are required to validate these findings and to better define the long-term benefit–risk of hepatotoxicity associated with long-term combination therapy.

Conclusion

In this propensity score-adjusted cohort of patients with CD, low-dose oral MTX combined with IFX is associated with improved endoscopic remission, without a statistically significant increase in adverse events. These findings support low-dose oral methotrexate as a practical adjunct to optimize infliximab therapy.
