Physiotherapists’ pro-growth experiences in past and current workplaces: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Koji Ikeda, Yasutomo Jono, Yoshiyuki Yoshikawa, Yuki Noda, Koji Takimoto, Ryo Mikami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract [Purpose] This study aimed to explore the workplace climate that supports self-growth among physical therapists (PTs) by comparing their past growth-promoting experiences with conditions in their current workplaces. [Participants and Methods] An online survey was conducted with 357 Japanese PTs. Data were collected on demographics, self-growth experiences, and workplace climate. Workplace climate was evaluated using a four-point Likert scale across 10 factors: bright, exciting, warm, strict, honest, open, diligent, passionate, cooperative, and autonomous. Past growth-promoting environments were compared with current workplace conditions. [Results] Significant differences were found in 8 of the 10 factors between past growth-promoting environments and current workplaces. The largest differences were observed in strictness, honesty, and diligence. [Conclusion] Workplace climate appears to play an important role in PT self-growth. Creating a balanced environment that promotes discipline, honesty, and diligence while avoiding excessive stress may help foster professional development among PTs.Key words: Workplace climate, Self-growth, Physiotherapists

INTRODUCTION

Individuals engaged in specialized professions, such as physiotherapists, develop and grow through experiential learning1). In this study, “develop and grow” refers to both professional growth (e.g., acquiring new clinical skills and enhancing professional competency) and personal growth (e.g., improving empathy and emotional regulation within professional contexts). Some experiences markedly promote self-growth in both areas2, 3).

For example, struggling to build effective relationships with patients or receiving critical feedback from senior colleagues can trigger both professional learning and personal growth. Efficient learning from such experiences depends on professional belief in and commitment to one’s work4). Ikeda et al.4,5,6) investigated Japanese physiotherapists and identified fifteen types of growth-promoting experiences and three professional beliefs: (1) practices that emphasize building patient relationships, (2) practices carried out with pride, responsibility, and enthusiasm, and (3) practices conducted with awareness of treatment outcomes and social benefits. Because physiotherapists are influenced by organizational culture, workplace climate, and atmosphere7, 8), their growth is also likely shaped by these factors. However, there is limited research on the relationship between workplace climate and personal self-growth, leadership, or management7). In particular, few studies have examined workplace climate in relation to physiotherapists’ self-growth from the perspective of growth-promoting experiences across time.

Although the definitions of terms such as organizational culture and workplace climate are not yet established, they can be regarded as the totality of an individual’s subjective perceptions (awareness and emotions) of the workplace9, 10). For consistency, this paper uses the term “workplace climate” to encompass organizational culture, climate, and atmosphere. A positive workplace climate generally enhances staff performance and quality of life9,10,11,12). Previous studies on healthcare professionals indicate that workplace climate can influence work–life balance12), moral standards12), stress13, 14), safety and accidents11, 13, 14), burnout13,14,15), turnover13, 16, 17), performance12, 18), quality of care13), mental health19), self-efficacy20), and job satisfaction15, 21). In Japan, workplace climate has also been linked to burnout in medicine and long-term care22,23,24), work–life balance25), turnover and retention24,25,26,27), stress24, 28), and satisfaction28).

Studies on physiotherapists show that job satisfaction correlates positively with the organization’s ethical climate8), and that leadership helps foster a culture of evidence-based physical therapy7). Improving workplace climate appears to be one of the most important measures to enhance satisfaction and reduce resignations29). Among rehabilitation professionals in Japan, Nishigori et al.28) reported relationships among organizational culture, occupational stress, and job satisfaction, while Shinohara and Kato26) found that mutual respect reduces turnover. Yamashita et al.30) and Ikeda and Ikeda31) also noted associations between workplace climate and work motivation. Despite these insights, little is known about how workplace climate supports physiotherapists’ personal self-growth. Growth is multifaceted and comprehensive, making it difficult to define or measure. Ikeda et al.32) defined physiotherapists’ self-growth as “a sense of self-growth” and attempted to identify the workplace climate that fostered it. The findings showed that physiotherapists perceive self-growth when the learning environment is positive, particularly when diligence and strictness are present32). Given the multifaceted nature of growth, further investigation is needed to clarify the workplace climate characteristics that promote and encourage self-growth.

There are more than 200,000 physiotherapists in Japan33), and education and human resource development have become major challenges. These challenges do not stem from the sheer number of physiotherapists but from the increasing diversity in clinical roles, levels of expertise, and educational needs across workplaces. The Japan Physical Therapy Association34), a public interest incorporated association, has established guidelines for newcomers, created a certification system, and promotes lifelong education. Because implementation is left to each workplace, workplace climate likely plays a critical role in physiotherapists’ development.

In this study, we define growth-promoting experiences as pro-growth opportunities, referred to as “ba”, which denote a shared relational space that fosters professional learning and development. Rather than re-identifying experiences already described in previous research, this study focuses on clarifying the characteristics of workplace climates that support and sustain such opportunities. These pro-growth opportunities are temporally situated experiences, and workplace climate is interpreted as a subjective perception at that time. Unlike previous studies that focus on general workplace characteristics, this study uniquely examines workplace climate from the perspective of growth-promoting experiences, thereby identifying climate features that specifically support physiotherapists’ self-growth.

Therefore, the purpose of this study is to investigate the workplace climate characteristics that promote physiotherapists’ perceived self-growth, encompassing both professional and personal aspects, by comparing the climates of past pro-growth opportunities with those of current workplaces. Specifically, we try to answer the following question: what aspects of workplace climate are associated with physiotherapists’ perceptions of growth-promoting experiences?

PARTICIPANTS AND METHODS

The participants were recruited from March to May 2023 via physiotherapists’ networks and social media and were directed to an online survey. A total of 357 Japanese physiotherapists provided informed consent and participated. Inclusion criteria were licensed physiotherapists currently employed in Japan, with no restrictions on years of experience, workplace type, or age. Exclusion criteria were unemployment, lack of a valid license, or missing data on key variables. All 357 questionnaires were complete and valid; therefore, all were included in the analysis.

This study extended a previous investigation, which examined the types of experiences and professional beliefs that promote physiotherapists’ growth32). The present study used the same sample but addressed a different hypothesis and conducted independent analyses focusing on workplace climate associated with growth-promoting experiences.

We designed a cross-sectional survey using a self-administered online questionnaire (Google Forms) (Table 1). Given that workplace climate can be considered the subjective view of an individual employee (i.e., the structuring of awareness and emotions)35, 36), the participants evaluated their workplaces based on their own perceptions. The questionnaire items were: (a) basic attributes (age, sex, years of experience as a physiotherapist, and type of workplace); (b) the growth-promoting experiences they had encountered and the climate of the workplace at that time; and (c) the climate of their current workplace. For (b), we presented 15 experiences previously identified by Ikeda et al.5), asking respondents to choose the one they felt most contributed to their growth. The respondents then rated the climate of that workplace using ten descriptors commonly employed in Japan to describe workplace climate: bright/cheerful, exciting, warm, strict, honest, open, diligent, passionate, cooperative, and autonomous. They rated both their most growth-promoting workplace and their current workplace on a four-point scale. “Growth” was not newly defined in the survey; rather, we relied on a previous definition, in which self-growth was conceptualized as “a sense of self-growth perceived by physiotherapists in their workplace learning environment6)”. Self-growth was treated as a subjective sense embedded within growth-promoting experiences rather than as an independently defined construct. Workplace climate was assessed as a subjective perception at a specific point in time. The climate of past growth-promoting experiences reflects participants’ retrospective perceptions of the workplace at that time, whereas current workplace climate reflects their present perceptions. Therefore, the comparison represents differences in perceived climate across time points rather than differences between fixed workplaces.

Questionnaire

We calculated the proportion of respondents selecting each growth-promoting experience and included an open-ended option for additional responses. Next, we compared ratings for the 10 climate descriptors between pro-growth opportunities (growth settings) and current workplaces. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were applied, and effect size (r) was calculated as r=Z/√N. Statistical significance was set at 5%. Analyses were conducted using BellCurve for Excel (Social Survey Research Information Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

This study received approval from Naragakuen University Research Ethics Review Board (approval number: 4-H033). It was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration, with all participants providing informed consent.

RESULTS

Of the 357 participants, 263 (73.7%) were men, 91 (25.5%) were women, and 3 (0.8%) declined to respond. The mean age was 35.3 ± 8.2 years and the mean number of years of experience as a physiotherapist was 12.1 ± 7.3. Regarding the workplace type, 81 (22.7%) worked in acute medical care, 88 (24.6%) in recovery, and 29 (8.1%) in convalescence. In long-term care, 61 (17.1%) worked in daycare or home care, 18 (5.0%) in care facilities, 46 (12.9%) in education or research institutions, and 34 (9.5%) in other settings.

DISCUSSION

Researchers have reported that the climate of Japanese physiotherapists’ workplaces affects occupational stress, job satisfaction, and turnover28), and that improved evaluation and treatment systems may enhance motivation and contribute to a favorable climate30). However, no studies have examined how workplace climate influences physiotherapists’ self-growth in both professional and personal domains.

The growth of physiotherapists is difficult to define, as no objective measurement criteria exist for this process. Similarly, there are no scales that directly evaluate the climate that promotes personal growth and development. This led us to employ a semantic differential method that captures meaning attribution and subjective impressions36, 37). This study structured workplace climate using 10 items commonly used in Japan—bright or cheerful, exciting, warm, strict, honest, open, diligent, passionate, cooperative, and autonomous. Structuring these dimensions makes it easier to interpret workplace climate and track changes over time.

We also compared our findings with those of Ikeda et al5). The four top-ranked growth-promoting experiences were the same across both studies, but the fifth differed: in our study, it was “receiving advice from senior physiotherapists”, while in the study by Ikeda et al.5), it was “providing therapy to severe patients (multidisciplinary collaboration)”, with “negative experiences” ranked sixth. Differences may stem from participant characteristics, as our sample covered a broader range of workplaces, or from methodology, as the previous study used qualitative data whereas we used a self-administered online survey. Educational and workplace reforms in Japan, including expanded lifelong education programs promoted by the Japan Physical Therapy Association, may also have influenced physiotherapists’ perceptions. Further research is needed to clarify these factors.

In this study, we framed growth-promoting experiences as “pro-growth opportunities” and compared them with current workplace climate across the ten items. Eight items—excitement, strictness, honesty, openness, diligence, passion, cooperation, and autonomy—were significantly stronger in pro-growth opportunities. Effect sizes exceeded 0.45 for strictness, honesty, and diligence, indicating substantial differences. These findings extend those of Ikeda et al.32), who showed that diligence and strictness were key elements of a favorable learning environment. The present study demonstrates that a broader set of climate features, including honesty, autonomy, and cooperation, are more pronounced in pro-growth opportunities than in current workplaces. Together, these findings suggest that both interpersonal experiences and workplace climate are essential for fostering physiotherapists’ professional and personal growth. Importantly, the lower scores observed in current workplaces should not be interpreted as a deterioration of workplace climate. Rather, the findings suggest that growth-promoting experiences tend to occur in environments with distinctive climate characteristics. This implies that aligning current workplace climates more closely with such characteristics may facilitate physiotherapists’ self-growth.

Strictness emerged as one of the most influential climate items. A certain degree of strictness may be necessary for healthcare professionals to master their work, but excessive strictness can increase stress38, 39). Honesty not only reduces deviant behavior40) but also enhances motivation, engagement, and loyalty41). Diligence fosters high performance but, when excessive, risks creating a competitive, exhaustion-prone climate42). Thus, while strictness and diligence are essential, they must be balanced with honesty, autonomy, cooperation, excitement, openness, and passion. It should be noted that stress was not directly measured in this study, and the above interpretation is based on findings from previous research. Autonomy reduces stress and drives organizational performance39, 43), while cooperation improves service quality, enhances workforce performance, and supports retention44, 45). Altogether, these elements form the climate most conducive to growth.

In recent years, many younger workers in Japan have left jobs they perceived as overly demanding46). At the same time, talented staff sometimes leave workplaces they find too loose or lacking in growth opportunities46). Because perceptions of “strict” versus “loose” are subjective, they are difficult to address with a single approach. As work-style reform and work–life balance initiatives advance, it is increasingly challenging for institutions to compel staff to participate in training. Our study suggests that, even under these conditions, climates characterized by strictness, honesty, and diligence—together with other supportive factors—remain crucial for fostering growth. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as differences in perceived workplace climate across time rather than direct comparisons between different workplaces.

Organizational climate and its attributes vary greatly45). Organizations consist of structures, systems, and cultures, with corporate culture influencing performance and leadership shaping workplace climate47). Climate is dynamic and often reflects leaders’ values and behaviors. However, change should not rely on a single leader but instead involve collective effort by the team, leveraging the strengths and personalities of all members. Creating climates that promote growth thus requires teamwork. Our study contributes by conceptualizing workplace climate as the sum of subjective impressions and demonstrating its role in shaping physiotherapists’ development. These findings reflect perceptions of Japanese physiotherapists and may not generalize to other healthcare professions or cultural settings.

This study has limitations. The sample does not represent all physiotherapists, and self-report data may be affected by recall bias. In addition, we did not exclude participants who evaluated both past and current climate within the same workplace; therefore, the findings may partly reflect within-workplace temporal perceptions rather than differences between distinct workplaces. Another limitation is the lack of a direct definition of “self-growth” for respondents. We relied on the definition from our previous study, in which self-growth was described as “a sense of self-growth” perceived in the workplace. This reliance on prior conceptualization may have led to variability in how participants understood the term.

Despite these limitations, this study highlights the significance of structuring workplace climate to facilitate understanding and potential change. Future research should examine the characteristics of individual workplace climates and determine whether climates modeled on pro-growth opportunities can be created and sustained, and to what extent they support professional and personal development. At the same time, growth is not guaranteed by climate alone; each physiotherapist must also meet individual educational challenges. Workplace climate provides indirect support by fostering conditions conducive to growth.

It has been demonstrated in the literature that physiotherapists’ self-growth—professional and personal—is promoted through specific experiences and professional beliefs. This study complements the literature by showing that such growth-promoting experiences are nurtured by particular workplace climate conditions characterized by strictness, diligence, honesty, and related attributes.

These findings suggest that fostering a favorable workplace climate can indirectly support physiotherapists’ sense of self-growth, contributing to motivation, retention, and quality of care. For managers and educators, the results emphasize the importance of cultivating climates that balance technical learning opportunities with cooperation, autonomy, openness, and passion.

Future research should explore how targeted interventions to shape workplace climate influence physiotherapists’ growth and whether these approaches can be applied across healthcare settings and cultures. Longitudinal studies are also needed to clarify the causal pathways linking workplace climate with professional and personal development.
