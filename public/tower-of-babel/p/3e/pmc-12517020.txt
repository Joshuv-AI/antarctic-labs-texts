Identifying and prioritising technical and non-technical skills for simulation-based curriculum in anaesthesiology: a Delphi-based needs assessment
Authors: Divya Vincent, Priya Mitali, Antony Eapen, Lulu Sherif Mahmood, Gandeeva Ramesh Mudigere
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo identify and prioritise technical and non-technical procedures that should be integrated into a simulation-based training curriculum in anaesthesiology using the Delphi method.Study designA needs assessment using a three-round Delphi process was completed among leaders in anaesthesiologists across medical colleges in our country. The responders were mostly alumni of our institution and their colleagues. Delphi round 1 identified technical and non-technical skills after brainstorming sessions with experts in the field. Round 2 involved collecting data through questionnaires about the technical skills best suited for simulation-based training and topics of importance using Copenhagen Academy for Medical Education and Simulation-Needs Assessment Formula (CAMES NAF) score. Round 3 included final elimination and reprioritisation. The topics were narrowed down by steering research members based on data saturation in the open-ended questions (qualitative), as well as statistical data (CAMES-NAF) for the quantitative questions.ResultsIn Delphi round 1, we identified 22 technical skills, 10 non-technical skills and 21 scenarios. Response rates in the Delphi round 2 averaged to 85.5% and responses were prioritised using CAMES NAF score. Open-ended questions reached data saturation for non-technical skills and scenarios. In round 3, we were able to narrow it down to 10 technical skills, 5 non-technical skills and 8 scenarios after elimination and reprioritisation.ConclusionsWe identified and prioritised 8 scenarios, 10 technical skills and 5 non-technical skills in anaesthesiology that are suitable for simulation and may be used as a guide for developing simulation-based curriculum in anaesthesiology.Keywords: Simulation, Anaesthesia, Medical education, Delphi Technique

Introduction

The adoption of simulation as a teaching and training tool is rapidly gaining recognition across various healthcare disciplines, despite the early stage of research in this area. Stakeholders increasingly demand empirical evidence demonstrating the efficacy of simulation in achieving desired learning outcomes. These outcomes encompass not only the supplementation of clinical experience but also the enhancement of patient care quality and safety through simulation-based training (SBT). The advancement of simulation methodologies for the benefit of patients and healthcare practitioners depends heavily on robust research efforts.1

The integration of SBT into the Anaesthesiology residents’ training curriculum entails identifying both technical and non-technical skills best suited for simulation-based instruction. This endeavour can be facilitated by optimising resource utilisation to enrich students' learning experiences. Today’s tech-savvy generation of students often finds traditional teaching methods less engaging, gravitating towards active participation and experiential learning facilitated by technology and innovation, particularly within the boundaries of SBT.2

While traditional medical training followed the Halsted apprenticeship model of ‘See one, Do one, Teach one’, this model may not be appropriate, given the risks to patients during students’ learning curves before competency attainment.3 4 SBT offers learners the opportunity for repetitive, deliberate practice within a safe environment, mitigating the potential for harm during the learning process.

Using a needs assessment survey from all medical colleges within the context of the National Medical Council (INDIA), Competency Based Medical Education guidelines in Anaesthesia, we can narrow down and target training areas that are often neglected or challenging to achieve during the residency period.5

Using this information, we plan to develop, implement and evaluate a Simulation-Based curriculum that can be integrated into the existing Anaesthesiology residency programme.6 To develop such a curriculum starts with a needs assessment (first step of Kerns’ six-step approach to curriculum development) which is a systematic process of identifying gaps, challenges and learning objectives to determine the educational needs of learners.7 8 The aim of this study was to conduct a national general needs assessment using a Delphi process to identify and prioritise technical and non-technical skills for inclusion in a SBT curriculum for anaesthesiology trainees.

Materials and methodology

Study design

It is a descriptive study where both quantitative and qualitative data were analysed using the Delphi technique. The Delphi technique allows involvement of participants from across different locations (figure 1). This method has been used to develop curriculum for SBT in various specialties like cardiology, ophthalmology, vascular surgery, urology and others.9,14

Delphi process for needs assessment. CAMES-NAF [Copenhagen Academy for Medical Education and Simulation Needs Assessment Formula]

Participants

All participants were practising anaesthesiologists and key opinion leaders in anaesthesiology education, affiliated with the Indian Society of Anaesthesiologists (ISA). The cohort was well-represented across various academic ranks: 30.3% were residents, 14.3% assistant professors, 9.1% associate professors and 16.9% professors. A significant proportion (40.3%) had over 10 years of clinical experience. Regarding simulation training, 56.6% had not undergone any, while 32.2% regarded it as essential for resident education.

The Delphi survey was distributed electronically via Google Forms, accompanied by a participation information sheet, to medical colleges, members of the ISA, and alumni of our institution, selected for their accessibility and relevance to the research focus. The survey remained open for 2 months, with two reminder notifications sent to encourage participation. In the first round, 90 anaesthesiologists from 32 institutions participated. Subsequent rounds (second and third) were administered to all participants, regardless of their involvement in previous rounds, to ensure comprehensive feedback. Participation was voluntary, with all responses treated confidentially.

Patient and public involvement

No patients participated and there was no public involvement in the study.

Data collection

A research group was formed to facilitate data collection and analysis. It consisted of five members, all investigators and coauthors of the study. The primary investigator was responsible for organising the data, ensuring that participant responses remained confidential from the steering group. All suggestions provided by participants were thoroughly considered and included unless consensus was not achieved.

Delphi round 1: brainstorming phase

The process started with a brainstorming phase where the participants (experts in the field of anaesthesiology) suggested technical procedures, scenarios and non-technical skills which an anaesthesiologist who had just completed specialist training should be able to perform. Participants were also asked to mention problems of simulation and challenges. The steering committee meticulously analysed and summarised the data from the brainstorming sessions to compile a comprehensive list.

Delphi round 3: elimination and prioritisation

In the third round of the Delphi process, participants were provided with the preliminary prioritised list of technical procedures developed in round 2 and list of scenarios and non-technical skills from round 1. They were tasked with reviewing this list and eliminating procedures they deemed unsuitable for SBT. Criteria for elimination included:

Inappropriateness for simulation: Procedures that were not conducive to being taught in a simulated environment.

Additionally, participants had the opportunity to reprioritise the remaining procedures based on their relevance and importance for SBT.

Data analysis

In Delphi round 1, we grouped similar skills together where the procedures could be combined and trained in one training programme. We separated the non-technical skills from the technical and put them in a separate category. We also made a separate category for scenarios. In Delphi round 2, the mean score for each factor was calculated and the CAMES NAF score was used to produce a preliminary list of technical procedures. The qualitative data (open-ended questions) for scenarios and non-technical skills were collected and ranked according to most suggested to least suggested. In Delphi round 3, we calculated the number of occurrences for each response using frequency analysis and applied a degree of agreement of ≥70% to establish consensus. The skills and scenarios that had <70% support from the participants were eliminated from the final list. The final list was developed following a focused group discussion among the steering research members.

Discussion

Our needs assessment successfully identified and prioritised key technical and non-technical skills and scenarios that should be integrated into a simulation-based curriculum for anaesthesiology residents. The high response rate of anaesthesiologists from 32 institutions and broad representation across different designations—from residents (30.3%) to experienced faculty (28.9% with >15 years of experience)—demonstrates strong support within the field and enhances the generalisability of our findings.

Similar to findings in paediatric training, our study identified critical procedural skills that are high-stakes but may have limited exposure during routine training.16 The top priorities identified in our assessment align with essential competencies in anaesthesiology, particularly focusing on airway management, vascular access and emergency response scenarios. This mirrors the emphasis on core life-saving procedures found in other specialties’ simulation curriculums like that of paediatrics and emergency medicine.15 16

A notable finding from our study was the strong emphasis on emergency scenarios. Failed airway management, circulatory failure, anaphylaxis, malignant hyperthermia and maternal/fetal collapse emerged as critical scenarios requiring simulation training. This aligns with the growing recognition that high-risk, low-frequency events are particularly well-suited for simulation-based education, as highlighted in recent medical education literature.17 Hence, the skill like fibreoptic intubation has been added as a skill, although according to CAMES-NAF, it was a low priority. The same goes for local anaesthesia systemic toxicity and malignant hyperthermia, as they can be learnt only by simulation, as it is very rare and comes with a high mortality rate.

The identification of non-technical skills, particularly interprofessional and hierarchical communication, as crucial areas for simulation training reflects an evolution in medical education. This finding emphasises that modern anaesthesiology training must go beyond technical proficiency to include essential communication and teamwork skills.18 19 While achieving a consensus view, findings are still based on professional opinion.

However, our study provides specific context for these challenges within anaesthesiology training, allowing for more targeted solutions.

A key strength of this study is the participation of anaesthesiologists from 32 different colleges, which demonstrates strong support within the field and enhances the generalisability and credibility of the findings. Furthermore, our questionnaire underwent peer review and assessment for face validity, which increases its reliability.

The mixed-methods approach, combining quantitative data with qualitative insights, provided a comprehensive understanding of training needs. The use of Google Forms and modern analytical tools, including AI assistance for data interpretation, represents an innovative approach to needs assessment methodology. A noteworthy limitation of this study is that the needs assessment was conducted at a national level, which may pose challenges for direct implementation in countries with differing professional standards and traditions. Looking ahead, it would be valuable to conduct a needs assessment in other countries and compare the results. Participants in this study included alumni and their professional networks, which could raise concerns about potential bias. Given that the study centred on anaesthesiology practices and skills, the affiliation with the institution was unlikely to introduce bias in their responses. While participation bias is an inherent aspect of research involving human subjects, efforts were made to mitigate its impact by ensuring a diverse and representative sample of participants. Alumni and their professional networks were used for their accessibility and relevance to the research focus.

Conclusions

This needs assessment successfully identified and prioritised key areas for SBT in anaesthesiology. The findings provide a framework for developing comprehensive simulation curriculum that addresses both technical and non-technical skills. By focusing on high-priority procedures and scenarios, training programmes can optimise resource utilisation while ensuring residents develop essential competencies in a safe learning environment.
