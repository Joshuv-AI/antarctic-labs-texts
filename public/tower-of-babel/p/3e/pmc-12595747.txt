Factor structure and measurement invariance of the Short Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale (SWEMWBS) among adolescents and youth
Authors: Kiira Karoliina Sarasjärvi, Marko Elovainio, Kaija Appelqvist-Schmidlechner, Pia Solin, Nina Tamminen, Sebastian Therman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe 7-item Short Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale (SWEMWBS) is widely used for measuring mental well-being due to its strong psychometric properties. However, its suitability for adolescents, particularly across gender and age groups, has not been established. The aim of the study was to assess the psychometric properties, factor structure, and measurement invariance of the SWEMWBS in Finnish youth.MethodsThe data from the 2017 School Health Promotion Survey included 132,828 young people (aged 13 to 20; 48.0% cis-female, 46.5% cis-male) from Finnish lower and upper secondary schools. The study employed exploratory factor analysis (EFA), confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), McDonald’s omega (ω), and measurement invariance.ResultsA single-factor model with good internal consistency (ω = 0.962) was confirmed via CFA, with minor residual correlations included in the model. Full scalar invariance was established across age, perceived health, chronic illness, loneliness, relationship status, and having at least one friend. The results indicate that the scale is suitable for detecting true differences in mental well-being across these groups. However, scalar invariance was only partial for gender and mental ill-health, suggesting some variability in the measurement properties of the SWEMWBS.ConclusionsThe SWEMWBS is sufficiently unidimensional and suitable for measuring mental well-being among Finnish adolescents. The scale is appropriate for population-level monitoring, as well as in intervention studies targeting the mental well-being in youth. However, comparing scores with the adult population or between specific groups (e.g., those with or without mental health conditions) requires caution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03571-6.

Background

There is a global need for easy-to-administer mental well-being assessment tools. According to the World Health Organisation, mental well-being is a ‘state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.’ [1]. In this framework, mental well-being is defined as ‘feeling good and functioning well’, covering hedonic, eudemonic, and sometimes the social aspects of mental well-being [2]. However, most existing mental health scales merely measure mental ill-health, such as depressive symptoms. Fewer assessment tools are designed solely to capture the aspects of positive mental health or mental well-being. This leaves a gap in the assessment of complete mental health. Because mental health is more than just the absence of mental illness, existing mental health scales should reflect that.

The term ‘mental health’ is often misused interchangeably with ‘mental ill-health’, ignoring positive mental health and well-being. The dual continua model distinguishes mental health and mental illness as separate concepts [3]. One continuum measures the presence or absence of mental well-being (flourishing vs. languishing); the other measures the presence or absence of mental illness [4, 5]. This means that high positive mental health can coexist with mental illness, and the absence of mental illness does not equal complete mental health. The model was first tested in American adults (25–74 years old) and adolescents (12–18 years old) and has since been validated across diverse populations and age groups, including clinical and non-clinical samples from both Western and non-Western societies [4, 6, 7].

Mental health disorders are common worldwide and often have a serious impact on individuals throughout their lifespan. Between 10 and 19% of adults, and one in four to five young people worldwide, experience mental disorders annually [8, 9]. Many mental health disorders begin in adolescence or young adulthood (12–24 years of age), and have proven to have both short-term and long-term effects on individuals’ health and well-being [8–10]. Therefore, addressing both positive and negative aspects of mental health is crucial when treating these conditions and preventing long-term adverse outcomes.

In Finland, studies have examined adolescent mental health trends before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic [11, 12]. Large population-based surveys from 2013 to 2023 were used to assess the trends in anxiety, depression, and well-being in lower and upper secondary schools using multiple mental health and ill-health scales. The prevalence of generalised anxiety among girls rose from 15.5 to 19.7% (pre-pandemic) to 30.2% in 2021, while the prevalence among boys increased from 5.3 to 5.9% to 7.5% [11]. Anxiety, depression, and social anxiety remained elevated in 2023, with well-being scores remaining stable or declining [12]. These findings highlight the need for continued monitoring of the youth mental health at the population level to inform future policies and interventions, along with ensuring that the instruments’ psychometric validity is up to date.

Including both aspects of mental health has many benefits. This approach can provide a more comprehensive understanding of mental health and can be used to guide more effective interventions and targeted health promotion campaigns. In fact, individuals who are ‘flourishing’ have a lower risk of mental health disorders, suicidal thoughts and problems with overall psychological functioning, such as low resilience, intimacy issues, and learning difficulties [13–15]. Loneliness is considered a key risk factor, while higher socioeconomic status, social support, and better physical health are protective factors in both youth and adult populations [16–22]. For instance, Lyyra and colleagues [19] found loneliness to be strongly associated with low mental well-being and self-esteem, particularly among girls, but also boys, in Icelandic and Finnish youth [19]. On the other hand, boys generally reported higher mental well-being [19]. Another study revealed a strong relationship between loneliness and social isolation for both physical and mental health among Danish youth [16]. Steinhausen and Metzke [22] highlighted gender-specific risk factors such as performance stress and authority pressures, while protective factors included self-esteem, coping skills, and social support among Swiss youth. Understanding how personal, medical, and social factors influence each other is essential for developing effective treatments, as well as prevention and health promotion interventions.

However, most of the existing mental health scales focus only on mental health symptoms rather than on positive mental health. Similarly in Finland, the validated positive mental health scales are limited, with most focusing on mental ill-health [23–33] or psychosocial health profiles and inpatient/outpatient assessment tools [34–39]. The scales that focus on positive mental health tend to assess only a certain aspect of positive mental health and well-being, such as resilience [40–42]; efficacy/empowerment [43]; and mental strengths and abilities, such as the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ; [44]) or the Behavioural and Emotional Rating Scale (BERS-2; [45]). Alternatively, these scales might be validated in very specific groups, such as athletes [46] or older individuals [47].

The 14-item Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale (WEMWBS) and its short 7-item version, the SWEMWBS, were developed in response to the need to measure the full range of mental health [48, 49]. Unlike many other scales, the (S)WEMWBS is normally distributed in the general population and employs only positively worded items covering hedonic (e.g., I’ve been feeling cheerful), eudemonic (e.g., I’ve been thinking clearly) and social aspects (e.g., I’ve been feeling loved) of mental well-being [49]. Both versions have good face validity and high internal consistency and are widely used worldwide in various populations [50–58].

As a brief questionnaire with a single summary score is used, the (S)WEMWBS is assumed to measure a unitary construct. The scale has been translated into multiple languages, and its psychometric properties have been evaluated across children, adolescents, and youth [53, 56, 57, 59–63]; students [49, 64–69], both in general populations [48, 49, 51, 54, 64, 70–78] and other population cohorts [50, 68],]. Most studies have reported a single-factor structure [49–94]. However, there are deviations from these overall results, with some studies indicating alternative results, such as weak factor loadings or poor fit of the single-factor structure [53, 57, 61, 65–67, 71, 73, 85, 91, 95–97]. Some studies have included several non-negligible residual correlations or even removed some of the items to achieve adequate model fit across both versions of the (S)WEMWBS [52, 53, 62, 65, 67, 70, 71, 73, 81, 89, 96, 97]. Although including residual correlations is common in the (S)WEMWBS literature, the practice is generally not recommended in psychometric research; if used, including correlations of error terms should always be justified [98, 99]. Most misfit issues involve the social well-being items. Because the shorter 7-item SWEMWBS does not include the most challenging items (only item 9 ‘I’ve been feeling close to other people’), it may be more suitable than the WEMWBS for measuring mental well-being in the general population. There is also some evidence that the SWEMWBS has better properties, especially among adolescents [53, 62] but also in the adult population [97].

In Finland, the factor structure of both (S)WEMWBS versions has been assessed in adults [97]. Various factor models were explored, with the WEMWBS fitting a three-factor model (separate hedonic, eudaimonic, and social well-being factor) [97]. The 7-item SWEMWBS showed a bi-factor model with a weaker eudaimonic residual factor [97]. The findings suggest that social well-being may be a distinct construct, making the SWEMWBS a more reliable overall measure. However, RMSEA values indicated poor model fit, leaving its one-dimensional nature open to debate. Validating the SWEMWBS in adolescents will ensure its applicability in this age group, enabling population-wide comparisons, life-course research, and improved mental health promotion and prevention.

Another way to assess psychometric properties is to evaluate measurement invariance. Measurement invariance is a statistical method used to assess how a scale behaves across groups or over time. Simply put, measurement invariance ensures that the items measure the same construct across different groups or time points. Several studies have assessed the measurement invariance of the (S)WEMWBS, ranging from basic socio-demographic group and country comparisons to romantic relationship or foster care status [54, 56, 75, 100–102]. However, due to cultural and language differences as well as varying sample sizes, cohorts, and methodological approaches, comparisons between these studies are rather challenging, and generalisability of the findings is unclear. To our knowledge, no one has investigated the measurement invariance of the (S)WEMWBS in the Finnish population. Assessing measurement invariance across multiple groups—not just gender and age—ensures that the scale functions are consistent in diverse groups. Socioeconomic status, physical or mental health status, and social relationships can influence how individuals interpret and respond to well-being measures. By testing invariance across these groups, we can confirm that differences in scores indeed reflect ‘true’ variations in well-being, rather than measurement bias. This ensures fair comparisons and equitable use of the scale in research, policy, and practice.

Thus, the present study assesses to investigate whether the shorter 7-item SWEMWBS is suitable for measuring mental well-being among Finnish adolescents. To accomplish this goal, the present study examines factor structure and measurement invariance using a comprehensive sample of 134,902 Finnish adolescents aged between 13 and 20 years.

The current investigation has two specific aims. The first aim is to determine whether the factor structure aligns with that of the Finnish adult population, confirming the applicability of the SWEMWBS across the life course in Finland. The second aim is to evaluate measurement invariance across various socio-demographic, health, and social factors to ensure consistent and fair use across different groups. Methods

Study sample

The School Health Promotion (SHP) study is a nationwide, census-based, biannual cross-sectional health survey by the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare [103]. The study collects anonymous and voluntary responses from students via a classroom-administered questionnaire (either paper or online) that covers topics such as living conditions, schoolwork, health, health-related behaviour, and access to school health services. The data collection has been conducted since 1996. The SHP is based on total sampling. The target groups are pupils (grades 4–5 and 8–9) of a comprehensive school, as well as students (years 1–2) in general upper secondary schools and vocational education institutions.

The 2017 data used in the present study encompassed 134,902 students from lower (grades 8 and 9) and upper secondary schools (years 1 and 2) across Finland [104]. Coverage of the target population within those respective age groups in Finland was substantial, with 63% of the students in Grade 8 or 9 (lower secondary school) and 54% in Year 1 or 2 (upper secondary school) participated the study.The coverage for vocational institution students cannot be reliably estimated. In the 2017 SHP the language options were Finnish, Swedish, Russian, English, and Northern Sami. The data collection was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board of the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare (THL) on several occasions, ensuring ethical compliance.

In the present study, 48.0% of the participants self-identified as cis-females, 46.5% as cis-males, 3.9% as other, while 1.6% were missing responses to either sex or gender. Of the participants, 54.2% were secondary school students, and 45.7% were upper secondary school students (including ones from vocational education institutions). In total, the sample provides a good representation of Finland’s geographical distribution.

Measures

SWEMWBS

The shorter version, the SWEMWBS, contains seven items (1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 9, and 11) from the original 14-item WEMWBS [48, 49] (Table 1). The items are positively worded and use a 5-point rating scale ranging from ‘1 none of the time’ to ‘5 all of the time’. The total scores ranged between 7 and 35 (SWEMWBS), with higher scores indicating greater mental well-being. A linear transformation from the raw score to ‘metric scores’ should be used to ensure comparability between the scales [48]. For more information regarding the registration and licenses, as well as the translation process related to the (S)WEMWBS, please visit the website: https://warwick.ac.uk/services/innovations/wemwbs.

Socio-demographic variables

Gender: ‘Cis-male’ and ‘cis-female’ are used for students whose self-reported gender identity corresponds with their self-reported official sex/gender (boy, girl). ‘Other’ is used for those whose gender identity (boy, girl, both, neither it varies) does not correspond to their official sex/gender (boy, girl). Note that there aren’t separate terms for sex and gender in Finnish.

Age

Age was assessed based on the student’s grade level. Students in grades 8 and 9 were grouped as ‘lower secondary school’ (M = 15.4, SD = 0.6; range 13–18 years old). Older students between 15 and 20 years old who reported being in either upper secondary school or vocational school were coded as ‘upper secondary school’ (M = 17.4, SD = 0.8).

General health items

Perceived health

Perceived health was assessed by asking ‘How is your health in general?’. Due to the small fraction of responses being ‘fairly bad or very bad’ (2.9%), these responses were combined with ‘average’ (16.1%) and categorised as ‘average or poor’ in our dichotomisation. The remaining categories, ‘fairly good’ and ’very good’, are categorised as ‘good’.

Chronic illness

Students were asked about any chronic illnesses or health problems diagnosed by a physician. The answer options were ‘yes’ or ‘no’.

Mental ill-health items

Anxiety

The 7-item Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) scale is used to identify probable cases of GAD and to assess symptom severity. The scale consists of seven items assessing symptoms over the previous two weeks (‘0 not at all’ to ‘3 nearly every day’), with total scores ranging from 0 to 21 [105]. The internal consistency and test-retest reliability of the original GAD-7 study are excellent: Cronbach’s α = 0.92 and the intraclass correlation = 0.83, respectively (72). The GAD-7 has also been validated in the 2015 iteration of the SHP, supporting a unidimensional factor structure and showing good internal consistency among Finnish adolescents (Cronbach’s α = 0.91) [31]. A cutoff score of ≥ 11 has excellent sensitivity and specificity for detecting moderate anxiety in both adults and adolescents in a clinical context [105, 106]. For example, in a study of anxious adolescents, a GAD-7 score ≥ 11 optimally balanced sensitivity (97%) and specificity (100%) for detecting moderate or greater anxiety [106]. This cutoff is therefore used in the present study to code for probable anxiety disorder.

Depression

The developers of the GAD-7 also created the 2-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-2) for major depression [107]. Like the GAD-7, the PHQ-2 assesses depression symptoms over the past two weeks using the same scoring tool (‘0 not at all’ to ‘3 nearly every day’) but using only two items: depressed mood and anhedonia [107]. A cutoff score of 3 is the most suitable for detecting major depression in clinical adult and youth samples [107, 108]. For example, a PHQ-2 score ≥ 3 showed 74% sensitivity and 75% specificity for DSM-IV major depression (structured interview) when assessing 499 youths (aged 13–17 years) who were enrolled in an integrated health care system [108]. A cutoff of three or greater is also used in the present study to indicate probable major depression.

Social well-being items

Loneliness

Students were asked if they ever feel lonely. The answers ‘never’ (29.8%) and ‘very rarely’ (36.0%) were coded as ‘no’, whereas the answers ‘sometimes’ (23.7%), ‘fairly often’ (6.9%), and ‘all the time’ (2.3%) were coded as ‘yes’.

Romantic relationship

Students from upper secondary school were asked ‘Are you dating at this moment?’ with the response options ‘yes’ and ‘no’.

Having at least one friend

Students were asked if they had close friends with whom they could talk confidentially about almost everything concerning themselves. The answers were dichotomised as ‘no’ or ‘yes’, indicating whether the student reported having at least one such friend.

Statistical analysis

SPSS Version 29.0.2.0 [109] was used for data pre-analyses and for managing and testing non-parametric group differences (Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon U test and Kruskal‒Wallis test). Effect sizes were calculated using an online calculator and the non-parametric results [110, 111]. Mplus version 8.7 for Linux [112] was utilised for all EFAs and CFAs to assess the construct and measurement invariance. As a default, all available data were used to estimate the EFA and CFA models under the assumption of random missingness, while missingness was not allowed for the observed covariates.

Implausible, likely mischievous, and missing responses

Due to the known facetious response patterns of the SHP, a total of 2,074 cases (1.5%) were excluded because they had at least two highly improbable responses (Supplementary Tables S1-4) [11, 113]. Among the non-facetious 132,828 respondents, the frequency of missing SWEMWBS data was negligible: all the responses were missing for 1,949 respondents (1.5%), and another 3,056 had at least one missing item (2.3%). Students with facetious response patterns (1.5%) or completely missing SWEMWBS items (1.5%) were excluded from the analyses, yielding a total sample size of 130,879 participants.

Factor structure

Several factor structures were explored. First, the previous factor models reported, which were used to assess the factor structure among the Finnish general adult population [97], were used to assess the similarity of the factor structure between youth and adult populations. However, the predetermined analysis plan was to first conduct the factor analyses from the previous study [97]. If the model fits were unsatisfactory, data-driven exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses (EFA, CFA) would be performed for one-, two-, and bi-factor models. To form a factor, at least three items per factor are required, with minimal cross-loadings, and all factor loadings must be statistically significant and above 0.3 [114, 115]. For bi-factor models, a minimum loading of 0.30, but ideally 0.50 or above, on at least one of the general or residual factors are required for an item to be included in the model [116].

Model fit was assessed using the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) < 0.05, Comparative Fit Index (CFI) >0.95, and Standardised Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) < 0.08 fit index limits [117]. To improve model fit, successive residual covariances between the items were included in the model based on the highest modification indices until an absolute change in CFI of less than 0.005 was obtained. However, the relationship between the items had to be justified [98]. The choice of model was determined based on the combination of fit values (RMSEA, CFI, SRMR); assessing multiple indices is encouraged when evaluating results [99, 118, 119].

The Weighted Least Square Mean and Variance Adjusted (WLSMV) estimator, the Mplus default, was used in all factor analyses with theta parameterisation. This estimator is suitable for normally distributed latent factors with ordinal indicators [120]. The WLSMV also provides standard fit indices and outperforms the maximum likelihood (ML) or weighted least squares–mean (WLSM) estimators with ordinal data when latent distributions are approximately normal [120, 121]. The WLSMV algorithm utilises all available pairwise data to estimate the polychronic correlation matrix, preventing case loss due to missing data under the assumption of random missingness. Latent means and variances were fixed at 0 and 1, respectively, while other parameters were freely estimated. The oblique Geomin rotation was used in EFA bi-factor models, which allows factor intercorrelations and ensures robust recovery of a simple factor structure in both empirical and simulated data [114, 122]; it is also the default rotation in Mplus [112]. Example syntaxes of the EFAs and CFAs are provided in Appendix A.

Measurement invariance

We investigated measurement invariance across the following groups: gender (cis-female vs. cis-male; cis-gender vs. other), age (lower vs. upper secondary), general health (perceived health, chronic illnesses), mental ill-health (anxiety, depression), and social well-being (loneliness, romantic relationships, friendships).

Configural, metric, and scalar invariance were used to assess measurement invariance [118]. Configural invariance compares the similarity of the construct (i.e., latent factors) between the two groups; metric invariance (weak invariance) evaluates the equivalence of the factor loadings; scalar invariance (strong invariance) assesses the equivalence between intercepts or thresholds across the groups. If the scale is noninvariant, groups may differ in their factor structures (configural non-invariance), items may vary in their informativeness across groups (metric non-invariance), or some items may exhibit differences in level between groups (scalar non-invariance). In such cases, the scale should be used with caution when making any group comparisons.

If any correlated residuals (error terms) were included in the factor models, the same error terms were fixed to be the same across the groups when assessing measurement invariance. For example, the single-factor model had three residuals that were allowed to correlate (items 1 and 2; 6 and 7; 7 and 11).

In configural invariance, both loadings and thresholds were freely estimated, whereas the latent factor mean and variance were set to 0 and 1, respectively, in both groups. The invariance criterion for configural invariance was adequate CFA model fit indices: RMSEA < 0.05, CFI >0.95, and SRMR < 0.08. For metric invariance, the loadings between the groups are set to be equal. The mean remained fixed at 0 in both groups, but the variance was freely estimated in the reference group. The model with constrained factor loadings (metric model) was then compared to the configural model. For scalar invariance, both the variance and mean were freely estimated, while both loadings and thresholds were constrained between the groups. The scalar model was then compared to the metric model. The scale was considered noninvariant if it resulted in an overall worsened model fit compared to the nested model. The non-invariance criteria for metric and scalar invariance were a minimum of − 0.01 change (ΔCFI; CFI ≥ − 0.01; [123, 124]), a change of 0.015 (ΔRMSEA; RMSEA ≥ 0.015; [124]), a change of 0.030 in metric (ΔSRMR; SRMR ≥ 0.030; [124]), and a change of 0.010 in scalar (ΔSRMR; SRMR ≥ 0.010; [124]) when comparing the two nested models [118, 124]. This combination of Δ values was chosen since it has proven to be particularly informative when the sample has equal group sizes, adequate power, and mixed invariance [124]. In the present study, the χ2 test was not used since it is known to be overly sensitive to sample size [118, 123, 125–128] and because the type I error for χ2 test increases when the WLSMV estimator is used [129].

If the full metric or scalar invariance is not met, partial metric and scalar invariances are assessed. To assess partial invariance, the thresholds (metric) or intercepts (scalar) are released one by one, based on the highest corresponding modification indices obtained, until the desired change in model indices is achieved. An example syntax of the configural, metric, and scalar invariance testing can be found in Appendix B.

Internal consistency

McDonald’s omega (hierarchical, ωh and total, ωt) as presented by Reise [130], was used to test reliability, with scores above 0.7 indicating good reliability [131]. Since McDonald’s omega considers the actual factor loadings, unlike Cronbach’s alpha, which assumes equal item contributions and tau equivalence, McDonald’s omega is often a more flexible and accurate measure [132–134]. McDonald’s omega was chosen since it has been proven to be a more appropriate reliability measure for SEM-based studies, and is suitable for assessing the reliability of bi-factor models [130, 131]. In the present study, for unidimensional and multifactor structures, we employ a method that McNeish [134] calls omega total (ωt), which specifically assumes that the scale is unidimensional. For any bi-factor models, we employ omega hierarchical (ωh), which indicates the share of total score variance explained by a single general factor common to all items [135]. Factor structure of the SWEMWBS

The SWEMWBS was normally distributed, with low to moderate (ranging from 0.41 to 0.68) item-item correlations indicating that the scale is suitable for factor analysis (Supplementary Figures S1-2, Tables S6). Several EFA and CFA models were then evaluated to assess the factor structure of the SWEMWBS. The results of each model are presented in Table 3.

None of the models from the previous study [97] met the RMSEA cut-off of 0.05. However, the other fit indices CFI (>0.95) and SRMR (< 0.080) showed good model fit (Table 3). Due to the RMSEA misfit, data-driven EFA models were constructed to improve the fit; however, they showed even worse model fit than the predefined models (Table 3). In the EFAs, neither the two-factor nor the bifactor models had three items with loadings above the 0.3. In the EFA two-factor model, items 1 (0.657) and 2 (0.954) formed a strong second factor, with the third highest loading being item 3 (0.268); in the bi-factor model, only item 2 (−0.380) reached the required limit, followed by items 7 (0.279) and 1 (−0.220). These models were then further investigated, adding the next items by highest loadings until the minimum of three items per factor was reached. However, none of these models performed better than the previous models reported by Sarasjärvi and colleagues [97]. Due to the poor model fits of the EFA models, these were not considered further. In contrast, the previously reported one-factor model (error terms 1–2, 6–7, 7–11) by Sarasjärvi and colleagues [97] was considered to have the best model fit (RMSEA = 0.090, CFI = 0.992, SRMR = 0.016). More detailed information on the factor analyses is presented in the Supplement (Tables S7–11).

Despite its poor RMSEA value, the one-factor model from Sarasjärvi and colleagues [97] had the best model fit and strong item loadings (0.69–0.84; Fig. 1). The error terms between the first two items (0.152), along with two of the eudemonic items, 6 and 7 (0.095) and 7 and 11 (0.102), were allowed to correlate according to the previous model [97]. McDonald’s omega was high (ω = 0.962), indicating good internal consistency. This single-factor structure was later used to assess measurement invariance.

Measurement invariance

Measurement invariance was evaluated across several socio-demographic, general and mental ill-health, and social well-being items (Tables 4 and 5). Although the RMSEA did not reach an acceptable fit of < 0.05 in any configural invariance models, other model fit indices showed adequate model fit (CFI > 0.95, SRMR < 0.08), supporting configural invariance. For metric and scalar invariances, most models achieved full scalar invariance. Partial invariance was detected for gender (cis-female vs. cis-male) and across both mental illness groups (anxiety and depression). The cut-off values for ΔCFI and ΔSRMR suggested scalar invariance across all models, but the ΔRMSEA did not meet the acceptable threshold of 0.015.

Only partial scalar non-invariance was found between cis-males and cis-females (Table 4); item 9, ‘feeling close to other people’, seemed to be the most problematic item. To overcome this issue, the two highest thresholds of item 9 were released, as they had the highest modification indices. Based on the results, cis-males were less likely to obtain higher mental well-being scores on item 9 compared to cis-females with the same level of mental well-being. However, the magnitude of the non-invariance between the thresholds was small (threshold 3: cis-male: −0.27 [standardised − 0.018]; cis-female: −0.72 [std. −0.54]; threshold 4: cis-male 1.38 [std. 0.91]; cis-female 0.82 [std. 0.61]; Supplementary Table S12; Figure S3).

We also compared the measurement invariance between cis-gender individuals (both cis-male and cis-female) and other gender identities (Table 4). The results indicated full scalar invariance, demonstrating that the scale is suitable for assessing actual group differences in mental well-being across this group comparison.

Age (lower vs. upper secondary school) also reached full scalar variance, meaning that the scale is suitable for detecting differences in mental well-being between lower and upper secondary students (Table 4).

In additional analyses, measurement invariance was investigated across several health and social well-being groups (Table 5). Both perceived health (good vs. average or poor) and existing chronic illnesses, as well as all the social well-being items (loneliness, relationship status, and having at least one close friend), reached full scalar measurement invariance. However, only partial scalar invariance was found for both anxiety and depression. For anxiety, item 3 (‘feeling relaxed’) results in some issues, whereas for depression, item 11 (‘been able to make up my mind about things’) was problematic. To achieve partial scalar invariance, the thresholds for both items were adjusted according to the largest modification indices. The second lowest threshold (‘rarely – some of the time’) of item 3 and the highest threshold (‘often – all of the time’) of item 11 were released. Although the items did not reach full scalar invariance, the non-invariance between the groups remained moderate, with standardised threshold differences of 0.38 for anxiety and 0.81 for depression (Anxiety (item 3, threshold 2): No −1,64 [std. −1.05] vs. Yes − 0.89 [std. −0.67]; Depression (item 11, threshold 4): No 0.85 [std. 0.57] vs. Yes 0.04 [std. 0.03]; Supplement Tables S13–14 and Figure S4–5).

Discussion

The present study investigated the psychometric properties of the SWEMWBS among 132,828 Finnish adolescents. Our study had two primary aims. First, we sought to determine whether the factor structure of the SWEMWBS in Finnish adolescents aligns with that observed in the Finnish adult population. Second, we examined whether the SWEMWBS demonstrates measurement invariance across multiple group comparisons among Finnish adolescents.

Based on our results, the SWEMWBS appears mostly unidimensional and appropriate for assessing mental well-being among Finnish adolescents across different subgroups. However, the factor structure varied from that of the Finnish adult population, indicating possible age-related differences in response patterns. Overall, a few exceptions related to the interpretation of specific items should be considered when applying the scale in future research.

While our results support the a priori single-factor structure from Sarasjärvi and colleagues [97], they remain inconclusive. First, none of the RMSEA values reached the desirable cut-off of 0.05 when assessing the factor structure. Second, the change criterion of 0.015 in the RMSEA was not always met in the measurement invariance analyses. Third, a few residual correlations (items 1 and 2; 6 and 7; 7 and 11) needed to be included when applying the single-factor model. This suggests that there are some issues with the measurement model, despite the small estimates of these free parameters.

Factor structure

Despite these issues, the adjusted single-factor structure had the best overall model fit compared to other models. The internal consistency was also excellent (ω = 0.962), supporting the applicability of the SWEMWBS in this population.

Also, contrary to our expectations, the bi-factor model showed the poorest model fit among Finnish adolescents (Table 3), despite being the most optimal solution for the Finnish adult population [97]. These results suggest that interpretations of certain mental well-being factors may differ across younger and older populations.

However, similar age-related differences have also been observed in other cultural contexts. For example, Fung and colleagues applied the same bi-factor model to the Chinese general population [136]. They were only able to partially replicate the findings. The bi-factor model demonstrated good model fit and satisfactory factor loadings among Chinese university students [136]. However, although the model fit indices also supported the bi-factor model among adolescents and working adults, some factor loadings failed to reach the recommended threshold of 0.30. Consequently, the authors chose to report a single-factor model instead [136]. Overall, more research is needed to determine whether the bi-factor model is suitable for assessing mental well-being across populations and in which contexts it offers advantages over a single-factor structure.

Overall, the current study supports the one-dimensionality of the SWEMWBS amongst Finnish youth, which is in line with the current literature and most common results worldwide. However, a major concern in our study was the poor RMSEA values across analyses. However, our findings closely mirror previous work on the (S)WEMWBS in the Finnish adult population [97]. suggesting these discrepancies reflect scale properties or cultural factors rather than measurement bias. To better understand our study’s limitations, simulation studies evaluating the performance of different fit indices in SEM may provide more relevant insights.

Model misfit

Though our models had good fit by the CFI and SRMR fit indices, RMSEA values were poor. Model misfit can be influenced by sample size, factor loadings, model complexity (e.g., degrees of freedom, residual correlations, numbers of indicators, factor cross-loadings), and the estimator used [99, 118, 119, 127, 137–148]. Considering our sample was unusually large (>130,000 adolescents), with strong factor loadings (>0.65), moderate degrees of freedom (11–14), and relatively complex models (single-factor with correlated residuals), some of these characteristics may help explain the poor RMSEA values observed in our study.

Sample size and model fit

Although the CFI/TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR are more robust than χ², they still depend on χ² and can be biased by sample and model size [118, 123, 128, 139, 143]. For example, Chen et al., [139] showed that RMSEA cut-offs are rarely met in small (n < 300), misspecified samples, while large samples (n < 800) may lead to over-accepting the models. Although it is unclear how directly these findings translate to our study, their work highlights the high inconsistency of RMSEA values across varying sample sizes and degrees of model misspecification. To test this, we reran our analyses in subsamples of approximately 0.5% (n = 650), 1% (n = 1,300), and 10% (n = 13,000) of the original sample. However, the results were unchanged, suggesting the elevated RMSEA was independent of sample size (See Appendix C; one-factor (residual covariances 1–2, 6–7, 7–11): 0.5% RMSEA = 0.087–0.100.087.100, 1% RMSEA = 0.081–0.095.081.095, 10% RMSEA = 0.087–0.094.087.094).

Additionally, Shi and colleagues [119] found that in large samples with ordinal data using 5-point rating scales, the RMSEA almost invariably reject models, while the SRMR has acceptable error rates across tested conditions. Given that SWEMWBS uses a 5-point scale, SRMR may thus be a more informative index. However, Shi and colleagues [119] used different estimators (ULS/ULSMV) from ours, and their findings are not directly applicable. For example, the RMSEA is calculated slightly differently depending on the estimator used [119].

Factor loadings and model fit

Better measurement quality (that is, high factor loadings) can sometimes result in poorer RMSEA values [141]. Paradoxically, a conventionally acceptable RMSEA value of 0.06 can indicate poor measurement quality if the standardised factor loadings are low (around 0.40), while even an RMSEA as high as 0.20 may indicate an acceptable fit when loadings are high (around 0.90) [141]. This may partly explain our results, since all loadings exceeded 0.65 while RMSEA remained above 0.07.

Model size and model fit

Model size also affects fit indices. The model size, or complexity, often reflects the number of indicators, factors, residual correlations, factor intercorrelations, cross-loadings, degrees of freedom, and/or their combinations. In fact, a study found that the RMSEA is not recommended for small degrees of freedom (df < 10) and small sample sizes (n = 50) due to misleading values [137]. Given our large sample (n ≈ 100,000) and moderate degrees of freedom (df = 11–14), this is unlikely to explain the poor RMSEA values in our study. However, Savalei and colleagues [142] found that RMSEA can also be prone to misfit in nested models with large df, and suggested the alternative RMSEAD index, which performs better in such cases. Although exploring alternative fit indices was beyond the scope of this study, this could be valuable in future research.

Shi et al., [143] concluded that, regardless of sample size, strong factor loadings (e.g., 0.80) combined with a small number of indicators in the model (p ≤ 10) are likely to yield biased RMSEA values. Excluding residual correlations from the model worsens the misfit even further. Our model had 7 items, strong factor loadings, which may partly explain the RMSEA misfit, despite allowing some residuals to correlate in our models. Furthermore, Montoya and Edwards [144] found that CFI and RMSEA are sensitive to correlated residuals, which can favour multi-factor models over one-dimensional scales in EFA. In our study, however, single-factor structures had better fit than multi-factor models.

Estimator and model fit

The model estimator may also influences the model fit [129, 145–147]. We used WLSMV for its suitability with ordinal data [121], although it has limitations. For example, compared to ML/MLR, WLSMV is less reliable for Δχ² invariance tests with small samples, weak non-invariance, or if the data is ordinal, and has a high volume of missingness [129, 146]. Fit values also vary by estimator: RMSEA is sensitive to thresholds and categorisation, while SRMR is robust when using ordinal data [145–148]. Consequently, conventional cut-offs may be inappropriate when the data is categorical [147]. According to the Sass et al. [146], caution is needed when interpreting the results, as the WLSMV does not perform well in misspecified models. In general, researchers should be aware that RMSEA and CFI may vary with the estimator, particularly for data that can be treated as continuous or ordinal (≥ 5 categories) [145]. To conclude, some of the misfit in our study may be due to the combination of a 5-point rating scale and the use of the WLSMV estimator. However, the impact of our large sample size, minimal missingness, and single-factor model remains unclear. For instance, Sass et al. [146] simulated a two-factor model with unspecified cross-loadings, while others [145, 147] used other estimators than we did.

In summary, several conditions appear to contribute to our poor model fit. As Groskurth and colleagues noted regarding goodness-of-fit indices (GOFs): ‘The sensitivity of GOFs to model misspecification varies greatly across simulation scenarios. Moreover, GOFs are susceptible to various data and analysis characteristics.’ [138]. Thus, we cannot fully determine the cause of our poor RMSEA values, but simulation studies suggest they may result from the combination of a large sample, strong loadings, few items, residual correlations, and the use of WLSMV with ordinal data. Under these conditions, CFI and SRMR often appear more reliable than RMSEA for assessing model fit.

Model fit cut-offs

In general, fixed cut-offs for model fit indices have been increasingly criticised for their potential to mislead researchers. Importantly, there is no consensus on what constitutes a “good” fit [99, 118, 126, 139, 140]. Therefore, these should be treated as guidelines rather than strict measures of psychometric quality. Also, each index has strengths and weaknesses: RMSEA includes a correction for parsimony, while CFI does not; SRMR tends to be robust across different conditions [144–148]. However, the benefits of RMSEA remain unclear, and some recommend omitting it entirely for measurement invariance and relying on ΔCFI and ΔMc instead [128].

Also, the recommendations of fixed cut-offs differ [128, 144, 150]. Even Chen [124], whose recommendations we followed, suggested different cut-offs for small and unequal samples (total N ≤ 300) with uniform patterns of non-invariance. Also, in practice, more relaxed cut-offs are often used (RMSEA ≤ 0.08, CFI ≥ 0.90, TLI ≥ 0.90, SRMR ≤ 0.08) [151]. These, more relaxed cut-offs, may be more suitable in our case, given the conditions affecting model fit in our study. Ultimately, cut-offs should be decided case-by-case, considering model specification, degrees of freedom, and sample size [139].

In fact, recent recommendations suggest abandonment of fixed cut-offs entirely, and favour more ‘dynamic’ thresholds that account for study design, sample size, and model complexity [138, 152, 153]. Others even suggest that indices such as ΔCFI and ΔRMSEA should not be used at all [154]. Instead, goodness-of-fit indices may be more appropriately treated as descriptive tools, with complementary reliance on χ²-based tests or likelihood comparisons [154]. Also, alternative indices, such as the RMSEAD index, have been proposed for higher stability, particularly with single-factor models or many indicators [142, 155].

Given that SWEMWBS is unidimensional with only seven items, future research could explore the use of alternative indices like the RMSEAD or more dynamic, context-sensitive thresholds to provide more stable and informative assessments of model fit, rather than relying solely on conventional RMSEA or fixed cut-offs. Any single index provides an incomplete picture of model fit, and the field is moving away from rigid cut-offs toward a more nuanced evaluation. Best practice is to report multiple indices and metrics, such as factor loadings and cross-loadings, while considering study design and sample characteristics. By combining indices, researchers can better assess trade-offs between model complexity, fit, and interpretability. Given the acceptable fit of the other model indices (CFI and SRMR), strong factor loadings, and low cross-loadings between the factors and the small residual correlations between the items, our results can be deemed reliable.

Modifications to the factor model

In our study, several error terms of the eudemonic items (items 6, 7, and 11) and the first two items (items 1 and 2) were allowed to correlate to reach an acceptable model fit. Even though this is a common procedure, the rationale behind the correlation must be justified [98, 99]. Allowing the eudemonic items (items 6, 7, and 11) aligns with the theoretical framework of mental well-being; thus, allowing these error terms to correlate is reasonable. However, it is unclear why the first two items (1: ‘feeling optimistic’; 2: ‘feeling useful’) are more related than explained by overall well-being.

Researchers have previously tried to explain this finding by order bias, translation issues, or older age [62, 73, 81, 97]. Specifically, item pairs such as 1 and 2 are likely to share measurement-specific variance due to their sequential nature rather than conceptual overlap. Additionally, the wording of items 1 and 2—as well as other items—is very similar, with most items beginning with ‘I’ve been feeling.’. Consequently, the error terms may also be correlated due to shared method variance, specifically due to similar wording. However, this has not yet been confirmed and could be an interesting avenue for future research.

Furthermore, a previous study investigated the face validity of the original 14-item WEMWBS among high school students in the UK [59]. They found several items, which were also part of the shorter 7-item SWEMWBS, that students found difficult to understand or interpret. The most challenging SWEMWBS items were the first two items and two of the eudemonic items (items 6, ‘dealing problems well’, and 7, ‘thinking clearly’). Therefore, students’ difficulties understanding or interpreting these items may also explain the highly correlated error terms in our study, as they are less distinguishable.

In general, including residual item covariances can improve the model fit. However, the validity of this method is still a topic of ongoing debate. We argue that this method can sometimes provide valuable insights into how specific items relate to each other, which may be meaningful when assessing item dependencies, redundancy, or measurement-specific variance in psychometric and scale validation. Therefore, in a scale validation study, it is more informative to include and report potential residual covariances, as this can help uncover issues with the measurement model.

Measurement invariance

In the present study, measurement invariance was examined across various groups. The group comparisons of SWEMWBS scores yield invariant results in most comparisons, with relatively small threshold non-invariance in specific group comparisons, mainly in mental ill-health and gender groups.

Sex, gender, and relationship groupings

Partial scalar non-invariance was detected between the cis-male and cis-female groups. At equal overall mental well-being levels, cis-females were more likely to respond more often (‘Often’ and ‘All of the time’) to item 9 (‘feeling close to other people’) than cis-males. The different response patterns associated with item 9 could be explained by differences in item interpretations.

Previously, focus group interviews revealed that high school girls in the UK were more confident in interpreting item 9 than boys were at the same year [59]. The researchers also reported that the social well-being items, including item 9, were often associated with sexual or romantic relationships, causing misinterpretation, hilarity, or embarrassment among high school students [59]. Similar misinterpretation or difficulties in translating item 9 were also reported in a recent meta-analysis [58].

Also, the response patterns of adult individuals on the 14-item WEMWBS differ based on their relationship status [101]. Being in a relationship was related to higher scores on items 9 (‘feeling close to other people’) and 12 (‘feeling loved’) compared to individuals who reported the same level of mental well-being but were single [101]. In the present study, several groups related to social well-being (including relationship status), were investigated for measurement invariance. However, we were unable to replicate the results of Goh and colleagues [101] among Finnish adolescents. In contrast, our study found that all social well-being groups achieved full scalar invariance, indicating that the SWEMWBS is suitable for assessing mental well-being, regardless of relationship status and loneliness, in this age group.

Another significant finding in our study was the establishment of full scalar invariance between cis-gender and other gender identities. Reaching full scalar invariance indicates that the differences in the reported means of the SWEMWBS between these groups are independent of item-specific effects. This is especially important since students who do not identify as cis-gender experience significantly worse mental well-being than their peers. Similar results have been reported elsewhere, with the LGBTQIA + community being at greater risk for poor mental health and suicidal ideation than their peers [156, 157]. These mental health challenges that the LGBTQIA + community is facing are significant and require more focused attention and support from society and the community.

General health groupings

Full scalar invariance was also found across general health indicators. This suggests that the youth are interpreting the items in a similar manner, regardless of any existing health concerns. These findings are unexpected, as we anticipated that these sub-groups would exhibit variations in their interpretation of the eudemonic SWEMWBS items. However, confirming that the SWEMWBS can accurately capture the group mean difference between these cohorts is encouraging. Future research could utilise the SWEMWBS to investigate the relationship between mental well-being and long-term health conditions among adolescents.

Mental ill-health groupings

In contrast to general health, only partial scalar invariance was established across mental ill-health groups. Students experiencing anxiety or depression showed different scores on items 3 and 11 compared to peers with the same level of mental well-being. Adolescents who were experiencing anxiety symptoms felt less relaxed and were more likely to respond ‘None of the time’ and ‘Rarely’ to item 3 ‘feeling relaxed’, than their peers. For depression, the groups differed based on the higher intercepts of item 11 (‘being able to make up my own mind’). Adolescents who were likely to be depressed reported having more difficulties making decisions. They were less likely to respond ‘Often’ or ‘All of the time’ to that item than their peers. Difficulties relaxing or making decisions are directly a part of the symptomology of anxiety and depression, and this moderate non-invariance can be expected. Although full scalar invariance was not achieved, the results do not affect the overall interpretation of mental well-being scores between the groups. However, the SWEMWBS might be prone to misinterpretation if the research question focuses explicitly on relaxation or decision-making related to anxiety or depression.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

This study is based on a large, well-representative population sample, enhancing the generalisability of the findings. More than half of the 8th and 9th graders (63%) and general upper secondary school students (54%) in the national school-aged population were represented in the study. However, vocational school students in upper secondary education are less well represented.

Another key strength is the thorough examination of multiple factor structures, ensuring a robust evaluation of the (S)WEMWBS dimensionality. Additionally, subgroup measurement invariance analyses were conducted, providing insights into potential variations across different populations and enhancing the scale’s usability in future research.

However, our study also faced several limitations. The true dimensionality of SWEMWBS data remains uncertain. The need to release several residual correlations to achieve a single model fit raises some concerns about overfitting. Furthermore, the reason for the poor RMSEA values remains unclear, despite our efforts to clarify potential causes in our discussion. Despite there being a lot of simulation studies already published, most of them utilise relatively small sample sizes (up to 1,000) [e.g., 123, 126, 144], and rarely include the WLSMV estimator in the simulation, although this estimator is known to be the most suitable for ordinal and categorised data. Thus, more research involving simulation studies, including ordinal data, WLSMV estimator, and large sample sizes (over 100,000), is necessary when evaluating the effectiveness of model fit indices.

However, choosing the right fit indices and their cut-offs, as well as the most optimal model estimator, is always a trade-off. As a whole, more research is needed to refine approaches for assessing scale dimensionality and measurement invariance. Method-based studies that systematically compare and analyse the impact of different estimators, rotations, sample sizes, and cut-offs when assessing factor structure or measurement invariance are warranted and needed in our field. However, researchers need to recognise the limitations of simulation studies, as their effectiveness and findings are constrained by the specific conditions under which they are conducted and may not fully reflect real-world data. Unlike empirical applications, simulation studies are built on a known latent structure and invariance, which is not the case with ‘real-life’ settings. This makes it difficult to determine whether the RMSEA misfit in our study arises from model misspecification, the known sensitivities of RMSEA under certain conditions in correctly specified models, or a combination of both. Future simulation work would benefit from incorporating more ‘real-life’ data, with its inherent complexity, missingness, and overall messiness, to enhance ecological validity.

Since our primary aim was to examine whether the factor structure of the SWEMWBS in Finnish adolescents aligns with that observed in the Finnish adult population, a more detailed comparison of estimators and methods was beyond the scope of this study. In future studies, it would be prudent to use multiple methods and fit indices to assess the psychometric properties of population-based surveys.

Future research could compare different analytical frameworks to determine the most appropriate methods for evaluating the latent structure and measurement consistency of the SWEMWBS across groups. For example, factor analyses and measurement invariance could be supplemented with item response theory (IRT)-based analyses, or using exploratory structural equation modelling (ESEM) that allows cross-loadings between the factors. Additionally, removing problematic items may improve the scale’s performance, at least in the Finnish adolescent population. Using only a single method or model index always has its limitations, and therefore, a mixed approach is warranted to avoid any over-simplification and to provide a more nuanced understanding of the construct, strengthening both validity and interpretability. Finally, some researchers have suggested reporting the effect size when assessing invariance [149]. While this is beyond our study scope, it offers a solution in future studies to evaluate the power of the results that are otherwise sensitive to sample size.

Conclusions and implications

The SWEMWBS demonstrates a mostly unidimensional structure, making it suitable for measuring mental well-being among Finnish adolescents. The scale has been part of large school-based health and well-being assessments since 2017. The scale’s ability to capture overall well-being, rather than just the absence of mental ill-health, makes it a potential and desirable instrument for population-level monitoring and evaluating well-being-promoting interventions in schools and community programs. However, certain item interpretations require careful consideration, particularly when examining mental well-being across cis-gender groups or varying levels of mental health symptoms.
