Effects and Complications of Hip Arthroplasty After Failure of Internal Fixation in Stable and Unstable Intertrochanteric Femoral Fractures
Authors: Juncheng Li, Canhong Zhang, Lan Lin, Nanxin Zhang, Jiexin Huang, Zida Huang, Huangfeng Lin, Hongxiang Wei, Haiqi Ding, Shaopeng Lin, Wenming Zhang, Xinyu Fang, Jiagu Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveConversion to hip arthroplasty (cHA) is a widely utilized and effective surgical intervention for addressing the failure of internal fixation in intertrochanteric femoral fractures (FIF‐INF). Although previous studies have confirmed that the failure rate of internal fixation is higher in unstable intertrochanteric femoral fractures, but whether the efficacy and complications of cHA after failure differ from those in stable fractures remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate and compare the clinical and radiological outcomes, as well as the incidence of complications associated with hip arthroplasty over a minimum follow‐up period of 3 years after the failure of internal fixation in both stable and unstable intertrochanteric femoral fractures.MethodsThis multicenter study retrospectively analyzed patients who underwent hip arthroplasty subsequent to the failure of FIF‐INF from December 2012 to December 2020 at various participating research centers. Cases demonstrating excellent and acceptable quality fracture reduction, as defined by the criteria established by Chang et al., were included. According to AO/OTA classification criteria of intertrochanteric fractures, the fractures were classified into stable fractures (31‐A1) and unstable fractures (31‐A2, A3). There were 47 patients with stable fractures and 56 patients with unstable fractures. Clinical and radiological evaluations were conducted for all patients. This study employed independent samples t‐tests, χ 2 tests or Fisher's exact test, and both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses.ResultsA total of 103 patients were analyzed. The HHS in the stable group improved from a preoperative mean of 47.08 ± 5.50 to 89.13 ± 4.75 at the final follow‐up, whereas that in the unstable group increased from 45.43 ± 6.36 to 83.87 ± 4.67. The improvement scores for the stable and unstable groups were 42.05 ± 4.69 and 38.81 ± 3.06, respectively, with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.0001). VAS scores decreased from the preoperative levels of 7.13 ± 0.92 and 7.61 ± 0.82 to 2.36 ± 0.87 and 2.91 ± 0.79, respectively, indicating a significant reduction in pain in both groups; however, the unstable group reported more severe postoperative pain (p = 0.001). The incidence of postoperative complications following cHA was significantly greater in the unstable group (28.57%) than in the stable group (10.64%) (p = 0.047).ConclusioncHA is an effective treatment modality for the failure of internal fixation in intertrochanteric femoral fractures. Compared with stable fractures, patients with initial unstable fractures that have failed experience a greater incidence of postoperative complications, relatively poorer joint function, and more pronounced pain following cHA.Keywords: failure of internal fixation, hip arthroplasty, intertrochanteric femoral fractures

Introduction

Intertrochanteric femoral fractures frequently occur in elderly patients, and with increasing age, their incidence is increasing [1]. Fracture reduction and internal fixation surgery is the preferred treatment method, as it allows patients to mobilize early after the procedure, which is beneficial for functional recovery and reduces complications and mortality [2]. However, due to severe osteoporosis [3, 4], and poor bone quality, the failure rate of internal fixation for intertrochanteric femoral fractures ranges from 3% to 12% [5, 6, 7].

The forms of internal fixation failure for intertrochanteric femoral fractures primarily include penetration of the internal fixation device, nonunion or malunion, traumatic arthritis, and avascular necrosis of the femoral head. Internal fixation failure can result in significant joint pain and functional impairment [8]. Salvage measures following internal fixation failure include internal fixation revision and total hip arthroplasty [9, 10]. Owing to the presence of severe osteoporosis and reduced local bone volume in elderly patients, internal fixation revision is challenging and associated with a high rate of refailure. Currently, conversion to total hip arthroplasty is the preferred method for managing all types of intertrochanteric femoral fractures in elderly patients with internal fixation failure [11, 12, 13].

Compared with primary total hip arthroplasty patients, patients with intertrochanteric fractures often have underlying health conditions that hinder satisfactory recovery of physical function following the initial surgery. Osteoporosis increases the risk of intraoperative and postoperative fractures. Additionally, revision surgery is more challenging, typically resulting in longer operation times and greater blood loss, which increases the degree of perioperative risk. Consequently, total hip arthroplasty performed after the failure of internal fixation for intertrochanteric femoral fractures is more complex, with a higher incidence of intraoperative and postoperative complications, and functional recovery is often less favorable than anticipated [14, 15, 16, 17]. Furthermore, the characteristics of the initial fracture, such as its stability, may also influence the therapeutic outcomes and complications associated with conversion to hip arthroplasty. Although existing literature has demonstrated that the failure rate of internal fixation for unstable intertrochanteric fractures of the femur is significantly higher than that for stable intertrochanteric fractures [18], conducting a multicenter study comparing postoperative complications after conversion to arthroplasty following internal fixation failure in stable versus unstable fractures is a research topic worth exploring.

Therefore, this multicenter study aims to retrospectively analyze the data of patients who underwent joint arthroplasty following the failure of intertrochanteric fractures. This study compared the clinical outcomes, radiological results, and incidence of complications associated with hip arthroplasty in patients with at least 3 years of follow‐up after fixation failure in both stable and unstable fractures. Additionally, the impact of fracture stability on the therapeutic effectiveness of conversion to hip arthroplasty should be examined.

Methods

Patient Selection Criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 65 years; (2) history of intertrochanteric fracture with failure of internal fixation due to screw breakage or dislodgement, nonunion, avascular necrosis of the femoral head, or inadequate initial fixation; (3) fracture reduction quality classified as excellent or acceptable according to the criteria established by Chang et al. [19]; (4) Normal cognitive function; (5) ability to walk independently without the use of assistive devices prior to the fracture; (6) any associated internal medical conditions have been resolved or are in a controlled state; (7) underwent hip arthroplasty (HA) for revision surgery; (8) availability of complete preoperative and postoperative x‐ray films, as well as postoperative three‐dimensional CT and other imaging data.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pathological fractures; (2) severe internal medical conditions or multiple traumas that pose a risk to life or impair postoperative functional exercises; (3) surgery on the same‐sided lower limb, fractures of the opposite hip, or any other revision surgeries; (4) fracture reduction quality classified as unacceptable according to the criteria established by Chang et al. (as it may independently increase complication rates).

This retrospective analysis included cases from December 2012 to December 2020 at six medical centers involving patients who underwent hip arthroplasty due to failed internal fixation of intertrochanteric femoral fractures. In order to further evaluate whether the experimental samples can support our assessment of the contrast between stable and unstable fractures after surgery, we used the PASS software to evaluate the data. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (IEC/IRB number: MRCTA, ECFAH of FMU [2020]338), and informed consent was obtained from all patients. Grouping criteria: According to the AO/OTA classification [20], fractures were categorized into stable fractures (31 A1) and unstable fractures (31 A2, A3). When grouping, the influence of diseases such as osteoporosis is not taken into account. After grouping, an analysis of the differences among groups for these factors is conducted.

Surgical Methods and Data Collection

All patients with intertrochanteric femoral fractures who experienced fixation failure underwent hip arthroplasty (HA) performed by senior joint surgeons at each center. All HA procedures were conducted via a posterior‐lateral approach, which involved full exposure and dislocation of the joint, followed by removal of the original internal fixator to prevent further fractures. The procedures of femoral neck osteotomy, acetabular and femoral medullary cavity preparation, and prosthesis implantation were subsequently carried out sequentially. When necessary, internal fixation of the greater trochanteric fracture fragment was performed. X‐rays were obtained on the day of surgery or the first postoperative day. Patients were protected from weight‐bearing and ambulation 24 h after surgery, depending on the stability of the prosthesis.

All preoperative clinical, laboratory, and imaging data were recorded, including the initial fracture type, internal fixation method, conditions of internal fixation failure (mode of failure and time from surgery to first detection of failure), and details of hip arthroplasty surgery (surgical duration, blood loss, and type of prosthesis). Additionally, intraoperative and postoperative complications related to hip arthroplasty surgery were documented, along with preoperative and postoperative Harris Hip Scores and visual analog scale (VAS) scores [21]. The assessment of intraoperative complications includes fractures of the greater trochanter and fractures around the prosthesis. Postoperative complications include periprosthetic fracture, dislocation, stem loosening or subsidence, and prosthetic joint infection. The femoral prosthesis has loosened. According to the Gruen classification (the femoral stem is divided into 7 zones), continuous transparent lines appear at the interface between the prosthesis and the bone or the prosthesis and the bone cement, with a width of ≥ 2 mm and spanning ≥ 2 consecutive zones, indicating prosthesis loosening. In addition to the transparent lines, if the prosthesis sinks by more than 5 mm or the internal rotation/external rotation angle of the femoral stem increases by more than 3°, it also indicates prosthesis loosening. A lower limb length discrepancy (LLD) was defined as a difference in limb length greater than 0.5 cm. The measurement methods for the length of lower limbs are all the “True Leg Length” method, which is the measurement from the anterior superior iliac spine to the medial malleolus (the protruding point on the front outer side of the pelvis) to the medial malleolus of the foot (the protruding point on the inner side of the ankle). Postoperatively, patients were monitored through outpatient visits, phone calls, and emails. Each patient underwent at least one outpatient follow‐up, during which x‐rays were taken to evaluate the condition of the prosthesis and joint function scores, and a standardized case report form (CRF) was completed (Figure 1).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted via SPSS version 21.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). For continuous data, normally distributed data are reported as the means ± standard deviations (x̄ ± s), whereas skewed distributions are reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). To compare groups for continuous data, an independent samples t‐test was employed for normally distributed data, and the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was utilized for nonnormally distributed data. Categorical data were analyzed via the χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test. Logistic regression analysis is used to assess the impact of various factors on the occurrence of postoperative complications. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic Characteristics Between the Stable Fracture Group and the Unstable Fracture Group

This study ultimately included 103 patients, comprising 60 females and 43 males. According to the AO/OTA classification, there were 47 patients in the stable group and 56 in the unstable group, with average ages of 73.71 ± 12.79 years and 76.88 ± 10.13 years, respectively. 48 cases occurred on the left side, and 55 occurred on the right side. The average follow‐up durations were 60.62 ± 14.23 months and 58.80 ± 13.09 months, respectively. We meticulously recorded the demographic parameters of the patients (Table 1).

A statistical analysis of the data in Table 1 revealed no significant differences between the stable and unstable groups in terms of age, sex, affected side, body mass index (BMI), or fracture fixation method. However, the time to internal fixation failure in the unstable group was significantly shorter than that in the stable group (p < 0.05), with a median failure time of 9 months for the stable group and 4 months for the unstable group. Additionally, the mode of internal fixation failure and the reasons for revision were significantly different between the two groups (p < 0.01). In the unstable group, a greater proportion of patients experienced internal fixation breakage or displacement, whereas in the stable group, a greater proportion of patients experienced femoral head necrosis or collapse.

Surgical Information Regarding Hip Arthroplasty After Failure of Intertrochanteric Fracture Fixation

During revision surgery, there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups regarding surgical methods or the selection of acetabular and femoral prostheses (Table 2). However, a greater proportion of patients in the unstable group required greater trochanter fixation (p = 0.007). The unstable group experienced a longer surgical duration (146.42 ± 42.20 min vs. 127.68 ± 23.80 min, p = 0.029), greater intraoperative blood loss (802.70 ± 343.58 mL vs. 572.30 ± 284.35 mL, p = 0.002), and a greater incidence of intraoperative complications, including greater trochanter fractures and periprosthetic fractures (Figure 2) (28.57% vs. 8.51%, p = 0.012).

The Impact of Fracture Classification on Postoperative Function and Complications

In the postoperative follow‐up study, the preoperative hip function score (HHS) of the unstable group was slightly lower than that of the stable group (45.43 ± 6.36 vs. 47.08 ± 5.50, p = 0.154). However, at the final follow‐up, the HHS of the stable group was significantly better than that of the unstable group (89.13 ± 4.75 vs. 83.87 ± 4.67, p < 0.0001), and the improvement in HHS (ΔHHS) was greater in the stable group. Pain levels improved significantly in both groups after surgery, with the stable group reporting a lower postoperative pain VAS score than the unstable group did (2.36 ± 0.87 vs. 2.91 ± 0.79, p = 0.001). However, there was no statistical significance in the comparison of ΔVAS between the two groups.

Regarding postoperative complications, the unstable group had a higher incidence of limb length discrepancies. There were 23 cases in the unstable fracture group and 10 cases in the stable group (p = 0.032). Complications requiring additional surgical intervention in the stable and unstable groups included periprosthetic fractures, joint dislocations, prosthesis loosening, and periprosthetic joint infections. A total of 16 postoperative complications occurred (28.57%) in the unstable group, significantly higher than the 5 complications in the stable group (10.64%) (p = 0.047) (Table 3). Six periprosthetic fractures occurred in the unstable group and two in the stable group. All eight patients underwent revision surgery followed by weight‐bearing training, with all fractures healing during follow‐up. Prosthesis dislocation occurred in two patients in the unstable group and one in the stable group. After reduction and bracing, one patient in the unstable group experienced a redislocation (Figure 3), with no further dislocations in others. Femoral stem loosening or subsidence occurred in four patients in the unstable group and one in the stable group; all five underwent revision arthroplasty without further loosening. Four PJI cases occurred in the unstable group and one in the stable group. These were treated with targeted antibiotics and revision, with no infection recurrence.

Discussion

Intertrochanteric fractures of the femur are a relatively common type of fracture in clinical practice. Currently, there have been numerous reports on the clinical research of intertrochanteric fractures of the femur. Conversion from internal fixation of intertrochanteric fractures to total hip arthroplasty (THA) is a relatively common surgical procedure. There have been many studies on the improvement of the Harris Hip Score (HHS) and the reduction of postoperative complications after conversion to THA. In a 3‐year retrospective study, Zhao et al. found that conversion to THA was a relatively common procedure. Whether in the PFNA group or the DHS group, the HHS score significantly increased by more than 30 points after conversion to THA. In a meta‐analysis, Liu et al. discussed the complication rates of conversion to THA following failed internal fixation (IF) of femoral neck fractures (FNFs) versus primary total hip arthroplasty (pTHA). Compared to pTHA, conversion to THA following failed IF of FNFs was associated with higher rates of deep infection, periprosthetic fractures, and reoperation [12, 22]. The results of this study indicate that, compared with patients with stable intertrochanteric fractures, those with unstable intertrochanteric fractures who underwent conversion to hip arthroplasty following internal fixation failure experienced relatively poorer clinical outcomes. Patients in the unstable fracture group demonstrated significantly less improvement in the Harris Hip Score, reported greater levels of postoperative pain, and had a greater incidence of complications than did those in the stable fracture group. These findings align with those of previous studies [14, 15, 16] and underscore the substantial impact of initial fracture stability on the outcomes of patients with cHA.

The Impact of Osteoporosis on Hip Revision Arthroplasty Following Intertrochanteric Fracture

Unstable intertrochanteric femoral fractures are frequently associated with significant bone damage and osteoporosis [23]. After failure of internal fixation, the quality of the bone deteriorates further, which may be a primary factor contributing to the relatively poor functional recovery and elevated pain levels experienced by patients in this cohort who underwent conversion to hip arthroplasty. Local bone destruction and osteoporosis in unstable patients often lead to the instability of the prosthesis. Bone destruction leads to the loss of bone structural integrity, weakens the support for the prosthesis, and increases the risk of loosening and displacement. Meanwhile, the fretting between the prosthesis and the bone interface increases, hindering bone growth and further affecting the stability [24]. Additionally, unstable fractures generally involve more extensive anatomical destruction at the fracture site, complicating the process of joint revision surgery. Moreover, elderly patients often present with a greater burden of comorbidities, which may further increase the incidence of complications.

Measures to Prevent Complications

For these patients, a comprehensive preoperative assessment of bone quality should be conducted, and bone density tests should be performed as necessary. During surgery, every effort must be made to preserve adequate bone volume to enhance the stability of prosthesis fixation. If needed, measures to augment bone volume can be implemented, such as autologous or allogeneic bone grafting or bone cement augmentation. Strengthening perioperative management is essential to prevent complications, including infection and deep vein thrombosis (DVT). Postoperatively, active functional exercises should be performed to promote the recovery of joint function [25].

Recent Advances in Research

In recent years, numerous researchers have introduced novel treatment strategies aimed at improving the prognosis of patients suffering from unstable intertrochanteric femoral fractures. Zhao et al. reported that the proximal femoral bionic nail presents considerable advantages in the management of these fractures, particularly among elderly patients, owing to its enhanced stability and safety. This innovative technique may serve as a viable alternative for addressing unstable intertrochanteric femur fractures [26]; AO/OTA type 31‐A3.3 intertrochanteric femoral fractures are acknowledged as the most unstable variant and are characterized by a high incidence of postoperative complications and implant failure. Compared with the proximal femoral nail, the proximal femoral totally bionic nail has superior capabilities in reconstructing the lateral wall's functionality, restoring physiological mechanical conduction, augmenting postoperative stability, and ultimately mitigating the risk of postoperative cutout and implant failure in the treatment of A3.3 intertrochanteric femoral fractures. This approach may represent a more favorable option for managing A3.3 intertrochanteric femoral fractures [27]. Furthermore, the modular femoral stem facilitates the effective treatment of failed femoral cephalomedullary nails and adverse variations in femoral anatomy, allowing for simultaneous correction of leg length, offset, and version, thereby alleviating pain, restoring function, and establishing a durable prosthetic‐to‐host composite [28]; some researchers also advocate for the consideration of short‐stem total hip arthroplasty (THA) in cases where THA conversion is necessitated by internal fixation failure, provided that patients are appropriately selected [29].

Analysis of Additional Contributing Factors

In the course of treatment, in addition to focusing on bone status, the preoperative status of joint function plays a significant role in the postoperative recovery outcomes of cemented hip arthroplasty. Parvizi et al. reported that patients with a limited preoperative range of motion tend to experience relatively poor functional recovery postsurgery [30]. Therefore, the implementation of targeted joint function training prior to surgery may enhance overall prognostic outcomes. Functional training before operation can enhance the strength of muscles around joints and lay a good foundation for postoperative rehabilitation. Preoperative rehabilitation training can increase joint flexibility and reduce the risk of postoperative stiffness. Good preoperative condition can make the operation process smoother, reduce the operation time, and the risk of complications; strong muscles and good bone mass contribute to the initial fixation of the prosthesis and improve its stability. Good physical condition and immune function before surgery can reduce the risk of postoperative infection and can also reduce the occurrence of complications such as blood clots [31].

Study Limitations

This investigation is characterized as a retrospective analysis, which is inherently subject to certain selection biases. The sample size is relatively small, potentially limiting its ability to accurately represent the broader context of complications associated with cemented hip arthroplasty following various types of fracture internal fixation failure. The ability to detect rare complications, such as loosening, dislocation, and other complications, is limited. Additionally, the duration of follow‐up is relatively brief, complicating the assessment of long‐term efficacy and the incidence of complications, such as osteolysis, aseptic loosening, and so on.

Clinical Significance

The findings of this study indicate that, in the management of patients experiencing internal fixation failure of intertrochanteric femur fractures, a thorough evaluation of the initial stability of the fracture is essential. In cases of unstable fractures, it is imperative to prioritize the assessment and optimization of preoperative bone quality and to implement more cautious surgical strategies and supportive care measures. These actions are aimed at enhancing therapeutic outcomes and mitigating the risk of complications. Furthermore, long‐term follow‐up studies are warranted to gain a comprehensive understanding of the clinical outcomes associated with cHA following internal fixation failure across different fracture types.

Conclusion

In patients who undergo joint arthroplasty subsequent to internal fixation failure of intertrochanteric fractures, those with unstable fractures exhibit poorer clinical outcomes and a higher incidence of complications than their stable counterparts do. Therefore, it is critical to emphasize the assessment and optimization of bone quality in the treatment of these patients, alongside the implementation of targeted preventive strategies to increase surgical efficacy and minimize complications. Additionally, individualized treatment approaches should be adopted, taking into account the varying underlying conditions and preoperative functional statuses of patients. Future research should focus on larger sample sizes and long‐term prospective studies to more thoroughly evaluate the clinical implications of diverse treatment modalities.
