Differences in breast cancer outcomes amongst Caribbean born women in Florida, USA—a population-based analysis
Authors: Alex P Sanchez-Covarrubias, Priscila Barreto-Coelho, Jovanka Ravix, Maurice Chery, Danielle Cerbon, Joseph Bernard, DuVaughn Curling, Dwight Lowe, Adrian Puello, Matthew Schlumbrecht, Isildinha M Reis, Judith Hurley, Sophia HL George
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundBlack women face the highest global burden of breast cancer mortality. Caribbean countries also experience the greatest burden of breast cancer mortality worldwide. In this study, we examined how sociodemographic and clinical factors shape presentation and overall survival among Caribbean born immigrants with breast cancer in Florida.MethodsWe performed a population-based cohort study of breast cancer cases diagnosed between 1981 and 2020 from the Florida Cancer Data System (FCDS). Eligible women were diagnosed with invasive breast cancer and categorized as Black women born in The Bahamas, Cuba, Dominican Republic, Haiti and Jamaica; US-born Black women (USBB) and US-born White women (USBW). Multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression estimated adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) for all-cause overall survival. Models were adjusted for age, insurance, estrogen receptor status, stage, grade, receipt of surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, hormonal therapy, and race/country of birth (nativity).FindingsAmong 179,992 women (mean [SD] age, 62.84 [13.90] years), there were 17,345 (9.6%) US-born Black women; 358 (0.2%) Bahamian-born; 241 (0.1%) Cuban-born; 182 (0.1%) Dominican-born; 2267 (1.3%) Haitian-born; 2335 (1.3%) Jamaican-born and 157,264 (87.4%) US-born White women. In multivariable Cox regression, US-born Black women had shorter survival compared to Cuban-born women (aHR:0.49 95% CI [0.33–0.71]), Dominican-born women (aHR:0.61 95% CI [0.39–0.95]), US-born White women (aHR:0.73 95% CI [0.70–0.77]) and Jamaican-born women (aHR:0.81 95% CI [0.72–0.92]) and there was no difference when compared to Haitian-born women (aHR:0.92 95% CI [0.82–1.03]). In estrogen receptor positive tumors, US-born Black women had shorter survival compared to Cuban-born women (aHR:0.61 95% CI [0.39–0.95]) and Dominican-born women (aHR:0.59 95% CI [0.36–0.97]). In estrogen receptor negative tumors, US-born Black women had shorter survival compared to Cuban-born women (aHR:0.22 95% CI [0.07–0.68]) and Jamaican-born women (aHR:0.78 95% CI [0.64–0.96]).InterpretationBlack women in Florida-USA, present significant heterogeneity in breast cancer outcomes based on nativity. There are significant differences in survival within the Caribbean Black immigrant population in Florida. US-born Black women had shorter survival overall and in estrogen receptor positive breast cancer, while Jamaican-born women had longer survival in estrogen receptor negative breast cancer. These findings underscore the need for disaggregated data, culturally tailored interventions and precision approaches in prevention and treatment to reduce disparities within the diverse Black population diagnosed with breast cancer globally.FundingJovanka Ravix reported receiving grants from the National Institutes of HealthNIMHDF31MD020275. Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed for cohort studies describing sociodemographic and clinical characteristics and survival outcomes amongst subpopulations of Black women diagnosed with breast cancer. We used the following terms: (“Black women” OR “Black”) AND (“breast cancer” OR “breast carcinoma” OR “breast neoplasm”) for which three studies had a similar context, one of them informed that, in the USA, Caribbean immigrants diagnosed with breast cancer have longer overall survival compared with US born patients. Another study found that, analyzing the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program, cancer specific survival was longer in Black Hispanic women compared to Black non-Hispanic women diagnosed with non-metastatic triple negative breast cancer. However, evidence on cancer outcomes in the Caribbean immigrant subpopulations in the USA remains limited.

Added value of this study

This study is to our knowledge the largest study to evaluate survival disparities in breast cancer amongst Caribbean immigrant subpopulations, US-born Black and US-born White women. We analyzed the Florida Cancer Data System (FCDS), which is not represented in national cancer registries such as SEER. Our study found that US-born Black women had the shortest overall survival compared to other subgroups; furthermore, stratification by molecular subtype showed that, specifically, US-born Black women had the shortest survival in estrogen receptor positive breast cancer, while Jamaican-born women had the longest survival in estrogen receptor negative breast cancer. The study documents health disparities within the Black population in Florida which can be attributed to genetic and social factors.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our study highlights significant differences in survival through different layers of the Black population in Florida. Understanding the complex interplay of social factors, cancer genetics and molecular drivers of tumorigenesis of each population subgroup is essential to develop targeted interventions addressing the disparities documented in the present investigation.

Introduction

Breast cancer continues to be the most frequently diagnosed cancer among women worldwide, and its incidence is rising. In 2025, there were over 319,750 new breast cancer cases in the United States, leading to more than 42,680 deaths, and constituting approximately 7% of all cancer-related fatalities.1,2 Whereas the Caribbean faces the highest breast cancer mortality rates, specifically The Bahamas, Barbados, and Jamaica experience the world's highest mortality.3 Despite advancements in reducing breast cancer mortality over the past four decades, these have not been uniformly distributed across all demographic groups.4 There is a notable disparity between Black and White women, with Black women facing a higher risk of breast cancer, and African American women exhibiting the highest death rate compared to other racial and ethnic groups in the USA.4

The observed disparities in breast cancer outcomes can be attributed to multiple factors, including variations in tumor biology and socio-economic determinants, such as limited access to healthcare, resulting in delayed diagnoses and presentations at more advanced stages of disease.5,6 Notably, Black women are more susceptible to develop aggressive triple-negative breast cancer and tend to be diagnosed at younger ages.1,7

The US Black population is characterized by its rich diversity, with approximately 47.9 million individuals identifying as Black as of 2022; of this population, 12% is foreign-born.8 Among Black immigrants, those originating from Caribbean countries outnumber their counterparts from Black African nations. Fifty-seven percent (57%) of all Black immigrants in the USA were born in Jamaica, Haiti, or Trinidad and Tobago, with a significant concentration residing in the states of New York and Florida.6,8,9 The Black immigrant population is genetically admixed, and there are known differences in European, Asian, South Asian and African ancestral contributions depending on the country of origin.10

In Florida, 76% of foreign-born individuals migrated from Latin America and the Caribbean. These immigrants constitute 21% of the state population. Given the heterogeneous demographic landscape seen in Florida, it is feasible to explore cancer outcomes through the lens of race and Caribbean nativity.11 In this study, we aim to investigate disparities within the Black population in the USA and their impact on breast cancer outcomes; we present the largest population-based study to date, delving into nativity-based disparities within the Black community.

Methods

Study design and setting

The Florida Cancer Data System (FCDS) is the state of Florida mandated cancer surveillance system. All newly confirmed diagnosed cancer cases are reported to FCDS since 1981. FCDS is a cancer registry that gathers data from 230 hospitals across the state of Florida. FCDS was queried for cases of breast cancer. We conducted a retrospective analysis of the queried cases after eligibility criteria were applied (Supplementary Figure S1). The study was completed under an approved institutional review board (IRB) for coding and abstraction of the data. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.12

Study population

Eligibility criteria included women with a known country of birth, self-identified as White (including White Hispanics) or Black and diagnosed with invasive breast cancer (International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition [ICD-O-3] codes: C50.X) from 1981 to 2020. White women (including White Hispanics) born outside the USA, trans women and men were excluded from the analyses. The categories in each variable that were used to include/exclude records in the study are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Other countries with low case counts (less than 100) were also excluded (Supplementary Figure S1).

Data source and variables

Data is reported to FCDS following the North American Association of Central Cancer Registries (NAACCR) guidelines. Captured variables collected and used in the analyses included: Age at diagnosis, insurance status, smoking status, stage (localized, regional and distant), estrogen receptor status, hormone receptor status (which included combined estrogen receptor and progesterone receptor status), Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2/Neu) status, triple negative receptor status, tumor grade, receipt of surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy and hormone therapy, sequencing of systemic treatment, sequencing of radiation and type of surgery (collapsed categories are provided in Supplementary Table S1).

Initially, the cohort was divided into three groups based on race and country of birth to assess patient characteristics and all cause overall survival (referred as “survival”) defined as elapsed time in years from date of breast cancer diagnosis to date of death or date of last follow-up, survival was the outcome of interest. The initial three groups included: US-born Black women, Caribbean-born Black women and US-born White women. For additional analysis, Caribbean-born Black women were classified by country of birth/nativity: Bahamian-born, Cuban-born, Dominican-born (from Dominican Republic), Haitian-born and Jamaican-born. Given our focus on the heterogeneity of the Black population and effect on survival outcomes, US-born Black women were chosen as the reference group to assess the differences among Black women subgroups by country of birth. Clinical and sociodemographic variables extracted from the cancer registry were identified a priori of the statistical analyses.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were expressed as mean and standard deviations. Categorical data were expressed as counts and percentages. A one-way ANOVA was used to assess differences in continuous variables. The chi-square test was used to assess differences in percentages across groups. Survival curves were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method. The log-rank test was performed to detect differences in survival across groups defined by explanatory variables including country of birth. The effect of explanatory variables on survival was assessed by univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazard analyses. Variables significant in the univariable analysis were included in multivariable models. Specifically, the following variables were included: age, insurance, ER status, grade, receipt of surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, hormonal therapy, stage, and race/country of birth. A complete case analysis approach was used to address missing data in the multivariable analyses. The statistical threshold for statistical significance was a two-sided p-value <0.05. Analyses were conducted using the SAS software 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) and R software 4.2 from August to December 2024.

Role of the funder/sponsor

The funding organizations had no role in the design and conduct of the study; collection, management, analysis, and interpretation of the data; preparation, review, or approval of the manuscript; and decision to submit the manuscript for publication.

Results

Description of the entire cohort

After applying eligibility criteria, a total of 179,992 female patients diagnosed with invasive breast cancer were included in the study (Supplementary Figure S1). Overall, mean (standard deviation [SD]) age at breast cancer diagnosis was 62.84 years (13.90), and most women never smoke (59.2%) and had private insurance (38.4%). Among tumors with reported immunohistochemistry, 81.4% were estrogen receptor positive, and 84.9% were HER2 negative; triple negative tumors accounted for 8.5%. Treatment modalities varied significantly among subgroups. Surgical interventions were lower in Haitian-born Black women (71.6%) and higher in US-born White women (90.4%), Jamaican-born Black women (83.8%) and US-born Black women (83.6%) (p < .0001). Radiation therapy was higher in Dominican-born Black women (41%), US-born White women (38%), Jamaican-born Black women (36.3%) and US-born Black women (34.2%), while Haitian-born Black women (29.7%) and Bahamian-born Black women (28.5%) received the lowest rates (p < .0001). Chemotherapy use was highest among Dominican-born Black women (51.2%), Haitian-born Black women (50.5%), Jamaican-born Black women (51.0%), Bahamian-born Black women (48.4%) and US-born Black women (46.5%) but lower in Cuban-born Black women (34.7%) and US-born White women (29.9%) (p < .0001).

Survival outcome based on nativity

During a median follow-up of 10.7 years, 93,098 all cause deaths (events) occurred. US-born Black women had shorter survival (median 8.02 years [95% CI 7.80–8.30]) compared to Caribbean-born Black women (median 8.82 years [95% CI 8.33–9.37]) and US-born White women (median 10.99 years [95% CI 10.91–11.07]) (Fig. 1A) (p < .0001). Breakdown of Caribbean-born Black women by country of birth (Fig. 1B), showed that Haitian-born Black women (median 7.37 years (95% CI 6.89–8.02)], Cuban-born Black women (median 7.11 years [95% CI 5.90–9.34]) and US-born Black women (median 8.02 years [95% CI 7.80–8.30]) had shorter survival while Jamaican-born Black women (median 10.89 years [95% CI 9.65–11.44]) and Bahamian-born Black women (median 10.38 years [95% CI 9.09–15.05]) had longer survival (p < .0001).

Stratification by molecular subtype showed that in estrogen receptor positive tumors (Fig. 1C), Jamaican-born Black women (median 11.60 years [95% CI 10.38–12.68]) and US-born White women (median 11.09 years [95% CI 10.95–11.21] had longer survival compared to US-born Black women (median 8.88 years [95% CI 8.61–9.43]) who had shorter survival (p < .0001). In the estrogen receptor negative tumor group (Fig. 1D), US-born White women (median 9.22 years [95% CI 8.87–9.78] had longer survival compared to US-born Black women (median 6.31 years [95% CI 5.74–7.16]) and Haitian-born Black women (median 5.25 years [95% CI 4.01–7.22]) who had shorter survival (p < .0001). In HER2 positive tumors (Fig. 1E), US-born White women (median 11.54 years (95% CI 10.32–15.09]) had longer survival compared to US-born Black women (median 8.08 years [95% CI 6.74–8.73]) who had shorter survival (p < .001). Similarly, in triple-negative breast cancer (Fig. 1F) US-born White women (median 6.94 years [95% CI 6.34–7.46] had longer survival compared to US-born Black women (median 5.53 years [95% CI 4.41–6.73]) who had shorter survival (p = .017).

In this cohort, univariable Cox regression analyses showed that known clinicopathologic factors such as tumor grade, stage at diagnosis, treatment type and molecular subtype directly impacted survival independent of nativity (Supplementary Table S2). Specifically, private insurance, hormone receptor positivity, localized stage, lower grade, receipt of surgery, chemotherapy, radiation and hormone therapy were associated with lower likelihood of death. In multivariable Cox regression, among all breast cancer cases, relative to US-born Black women, Jamaican-born Black women (aHR 0.81 [95% CI 0.72–0.92]), Cuban-born Black women (aHR 0.49 [95% CI 0.33–0.71]) and US-born White women (aHR 0.73 [95% CI 0.70–0.77]) had significant lower risk of death (or survival was longer). In patients with estrogen receptor positive tumors, there was lower risk of death in US-born White women (aHR 0.73 [95% CI 0.69–0.77]) and Cuban-born Black women (aHR 0.61 [95% CI 0.39–0.95]) compared to US-born Black women. In patients with estrogen receptor negative tumors, there was lower risk of death in Jamaican-born Black women (aHR 0.78 [95% CI 0.64–0.96]), US-born White women (aHR 0.78 [95% CI 0.72–0.84]) and Cuban-born Black women (aHR 0.41 [95% CI 0.19–0.86]) compared to US-born Black women. For patients with HER2-positive tumors, compared to US-born Black women, there was lower risk of death in US-born White women (aHR 0.71 [95% CI 0.60–1.29]). Finally in triple negative breast cancer, compared to US-born Black women, Cuban-born Black women had lower risk of death (aHR 0.22 [95% CI 0.07–0.68]) (Fig. 2). Discussion

Race is a socially constructed classification system, yet it remains a significant factor in health disparities due to its intersection with social, environmental, and genetic influences. Our findings reveal significant differences in breast cancer survival outcomes among Black women based on nativity, highlighting the heterogeneity within the Black population in Florida and emphasizing the importance of evaluating cancer outcome disparities within these subgroups.

Our research examines breast cancer data from 1980 to 2020 in Florida, a period that witnessed remarkable improvements in early detection, systemic therapies, and supportive care, leading to a 44% decline in mortality since 1989 and more than 500,000 averted deaths in the USA alone.1 However, these advances have not been experienced equally across populations. Black women continue to face a disproportionately high burden of breast cancer mortality, with a 40% higher death rate compared to White women (27.6 vs. 19.7 per 100,000, 2016–2020) despite a slightly lower incidence rate (127.8 vs. 133.7 per 100,000). This inequity is even more striking among women under 50, where the death rate for Black women is nearly double that of their White counterparts (12.1 vs. 6.5 per 100,000), underscoring persistent gaps in outcomes despite overall progress.4

Our findings align with previous research efforts exploring cancer incidence and outcomes within the global Black diaspora residing in the USA.13,14 Llanos et al. found a lower age-adjusted cancer incidence in Jamaican-born and other Caribbean-born females compared with African-born and Haitian-born females.15 Amongst sub-groups of Black women diagnosed with invasive breast cancer, US-born Black- and Haitian-born Black women persistently had worse survival compared to other groups, a finding that persisted across all molecular subtypes.

Most patients included in the FCDS were diagnosed with estrogen receptor positive breast cancer, which is consistent with known population estimates.16,17 In univariable analyses, among estrogen receptor positive breast cancer patients, Black Jamaican-born and White US-born women exhibited longer survival. However, after adjusting for factors in the multivariable model, Cuban-born Black, Dominican-born Black and US-born White women demonstrated longer survival; of note, US-born Black women had shorter survival in either scenario.

Unlike differences seen in estrogen receptor positive breast cancer groups, among HER2-positive tumors, the only significant difference in survival was between US-born White and US-born Black women, which has been previously documented.18,19 Previous studies highlight disparities in response to treatment related to HER2+ positivity with Black women being less like to complete therapy.20 Other studies have reported higher proportions of HER2+ tumors in the Black breast cancer population,21,22 and shorter 5-year survival in the Black and the American Indian/Alaska Native populations compared to their White counterparts.4

Black women have an increased risk for estrogen receptor negative tumors, and our cohort reflects this, showing higher proportion of estrogen receptor negative tumors in the Black population, consistent with previous reports.23,24 The higher proportion of estrogen receptor negative breast cancer can be partly explained by the higher percent of African ancestry, which has been linked to genetic variants associated with aggressive tumor biology, and immune microenvironment differences.25,26 In a previous analysis comparing breast cancer tumors from Nigerian women, who have a high proportion of West African ancestry, against tumors from White women, those from Nigeria had a higher rate of genomic instability and increased intra-tumoral heterogeneity.27 Tumor heterogeneity is a strong predictor for resistance to therapy and therefore shorter survival. This finding has implications for our cohort, as we report outcome differences by nativity within a socially defined group with significant genetic admixture.

Forced migration through the transatlantic slave trade shaped the genetic diversity of the African diaspora in the Caribbean as enslaved Africans from West and Central Africa were transported to different islands, where colonial history and migration patterns influenced ancestry proportions. For example, Haiti has the highest African ancestry in the region due to the large influx of enslaved Africans and minimal European admixture.28 In contrast, Cuba and the Dominican Republic have greater European and Indigenous genetic contributions.29,30

However, genetics alone cannot fully explain our observed outcomes. It is more likely that the reported differences result from the substantive influence of social determinants of health, including healthcare access, socioeconomic status, structural racism, and environmental exposures, actively shaping breast cancer risk, treatment, and survival. Previous research has highlighted various factors, including social barriers and mistrust of the healthcare system, which can impact outcomes in Black women living in the USA.31,32 Social determinants of health affecting this population include immigration stress, structural racism, neighborhood segregation and socioeconomic status which have been reported to directly influence survival in Black women. Palmer et al. demonstrated that African American women, particularly those under 40 years old with a pathogenic BRCA1 variant, were more likely to be diagnosed with estrogen receptor negative breast cancer, as the BRCA1 gene exhibits high penetrance in Black women.33 The Bahamas is known for having the highest rates of BRCA1 pathogenic variants globally, which is associated with an increased incidence of breast cancer in young women.34 Specifically, the mean age at breast cancer diagnosis in Bahamian women in a previous cohort of breast cancer women was 44.9 years,35 while on our study it was 52.0 years; in both cases the youngest mean age at breast cancer diagnosis was observed in Bahamian women. Despite having the youngest age at diagnosis in our cohort and facing a more aggressive type of breast cancer, with 59% presenting with high-grade tumors and the highest rates of triple-negative breast cancer at 30.7%, their survival was not shorter than other groups. This finding likely reflects higher rates of genetic testing in the USA, which leads to timely diagnosis and treatment.36 Although genetic data were not available in this study, these outcomes may be explained by the population's higher proportion of homologous recombination deficiency.37,38 However, overall study findings suggest that other factors beyond tumor biology may contribute to longer survival among Black Bahamian-born women. For example, improved survival among Bahamian-born Black women and other English speaking Caribbean immigrants could be due to access to the healthcare system and potential adherence to care.39

Our group has previously reported that Caribbean Black women had longer survival when compared to US-born Black women when diagnosed and treated in the USA, and this pattern was corroborated in our study and consistent across different countries in the Caribbean, except for Haiti.13 Haitian-born Black women had the highest percentage of metastatic disease at diagnosis compared to other groups (15%), despite having a higher proportion of hormone receptor-positive tumors (69%). Haitian immigrants may experience higher levels of migration-related stress compared to other immigrant groups (such as Jamaican-born and Bahamian-born women) due to their unique position as triple minorities—predominantly Black, immigrant, and Haitian Creole/non-English speakers.13,40 There are biases in healthcare systems that further drive the disparities observed in breast cancer outcomes within the Black population.41 Haitian-born Black women in the USA face a myriad of stressors such as discrimination, language barriers, socioeconomic challenges, fear of deportation, and conflicts with cultural beliefs.42,43 To be sure, Haiti is the poorest country the Americas and has suffered from prolonged political instability compared to others in the region. Factors such as historical ties, political instability in Haiti, and hostile reception in the USA further exacerbate their stress levels.44,45 The link between increased migrant-related stress and poor quality of life outcomes such as mental and physical health are well documented.46, 47, 48 In 2023, a study examining the unique causes of migration stress for Haitian immigrants, language barrier was ranked as the most instrumental/environmental stressor and a key difference between life in the USA and Haiti for Haitian immigrants.40

Cuban-born Black women were shown to have a comparable survival rate to the US-born White women on our multivariate analysis—contrary to what was seen in the univariable analysis. Although Cuban-born Black women represented 0.1% of the study population, this change can be accounted to factors that include the insurance variable in the multivariable analysis and the well-known healthy immigrant effect.49 The healthy immigrant effect, a phenomenon observed in immigrants from developing countries migrating to developed nations, wherein they exhibit better health outcomes compared to their native-born counterparts.50 Several potential explanations for this phenomenon have been elucidated in the literature, including (1) health screening or selection processes conducted by immigration officials of the host country; (2) adoption of healthier lifestyle behaviors by new immigrants in their host country; (3) self-selection among immigrants. Additionally, the Cuban population in Florida is known to have high median wealth and, as we could see in our reports, was found to have the higher proportion of patients with Medicare/Medicaid insurance—which is probably related to The Cuban Adjustment Act in Florida. Through this Act, Cuban nationals can become legal, permanent USA residents and access healthcare.39,51 These factors can collectively contribute to the observed differences in health outcomes between immigrant and native-born populations.52,53

Of note, our research compares to similar published research exploring disparities among individuals from African ancestry living in the USA, for example Pinheiro et al. highlighted how survival differences may be shaped by a combination of socioeconomic factors, healthcare access and structural inequities.54 Another previous study has also documented survival outcomes in African immigrant populations in the USA, emphasizing differences by nativity and migration-related factors in cancer research and prevention strategies.55 Previous studies have also documented differences in individuals from distinct populations, such as South Asian, East Asian and Native Hawaiians or Pacific Islanders. Individuals from these backgrounds faced 10% (East Asian) and 22% (Native Hawaiians or Pacific Islanders) higher mortality, from pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma compared to South Asian individuals.56 These findings highlight the substantial heterogeneity within broader racial categories. Despite this diversity, cancer registries and epidemiologic reports frequently combine East Asian, South Asian and Native Hawaiians or Pacific Islanders populations into a single category, which can obscure important subgroup differences.57 Capturing variables such as nativity, ancestry, and language are essential for accurately identifying patterns of inequity and at the same time adding opportunities for intervention, yet many cancer databases lack this level of detail. Without these data, opportunities to uncover and address meaningful differences in care and outcomes are limited58,59 Taken together, these studies highlight that our findings are consistent with previously identified disparities across immigrant and minority.

The study has limitations that should be acknowledged. First, temporal bias is present due to the nature of the cancer registry, particularly regarding the time required for data inclusion. The dataset used in this study includes information only up to the year 2020, and HER2 status has only been included in the registry since 2010. As a result, there is general information on HER2 status for many individuals, which may impact the completeness of our findings. This is the case for other variables such as estrogen receptor or hormone receptor status, which were collected since 2002. Furthermore, missing data from other variables represent a small percentage of the total subjects; however, there is the possibility that the missingness is not at random and could impact the results reported in this study. Additionally, information bias may be present due to reliance on registry data, which may not capture all relevant clinical and sociodemographic variables comprehensively.

All-cause survival was used as the primary outcome, which includes mortality from conditions beyond breast cancer. Certain individuals included in the analysis may have succumbed to causes unrelated to cancer, which may be inherently linked to age-related factors or other comorbidities, making it difficult to isolate breast cancer specific survival.

Furthermore, not all Black women in Florida diagnosed with breast cancer are represented in our analysis. Certain populations, such as women born in Africa, were underrepresented, likely due to immigration patterns into Florida, with African-born women comprising less than 1% of the dataset. Additionally, some Caribbean subpopulations, particularly Cuban-born and Dominican-born women, were relatively small, limiting our ability to draw definitive conclusions for these groups. Future studies with larger sample sizes and prospective data collection will be necessary to further validate these findings.

Despite these limitations, several strengths contribute to the robustness and relevance of this study. It utilized a large and comprehensive statewide dataset from Florida, which includes a diverse immigrant population from Cuba, Haiti, and Jamaica. This dataset allows for a detailed examination of breast cancer outcomes among Caribbean immigrant populations—a group not well-represented in other cancer registries such as SEER. This is also the largest study to date evaluating disparities in breast cancer outcomes within the Black population in Florida.

Our study findings shed light on the complex interplay of different variables influencing breast cancer outcomes within different subgroups of the Black immigrant population in Florida. Further investigation into the contributing factors that may lead to better survival in Black Jamaican-born women compared to Black US-born women with estrogen receptor positive breast cancer is needed. By recognizing the uniqueness of each subpopulation, we establish a foundation for developing targeted interventions that can meaningfully reduce disparities. Achieving this requires first understanding the biological, clinical, and social factors that drive population-level differences in outcomes. This comprehensive understanding is a critical prerequisite to designing interventions that are both effective and equitable.
