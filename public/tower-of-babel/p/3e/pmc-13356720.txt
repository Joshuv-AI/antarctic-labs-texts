Effects of in ovo dextrose, niacin, and retinoic acid administration on embryonic metabolism, antioxidant defense, and hepatic gene expression in long-stored broiler breeder eggs☆
Authors: Sinan Vicil, Fatma Tülin Özbaşer Bulut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveProlonged storage of broiler breeder eggs may aggravate the naturally limited carbohydrate availability of the avian egg and increase oxidative pressure during late embryogenesis. This study evaluated the effects of in ovo administration of dextrose (D-glucose), niacin, and retinoic acid on embryonic metabolism, glycogen deposition, antioxidant defense, and hepatic gene expression in broiler breeder eggs stored for 14 days.MethodsAfter 14 days of storage and E18 candling, a total of 640 fertile Ross 308 hatching eggs were included in the in ovo injection phase. Experimental groups consisted of control, dextrose (DEX), niacin (NIA), retinoic acid (RA), dextrose + niacin (DEX+NIA), and two RA-containing combination groups (RA+DEX+NIA-L and RA+DEX+NIA-H). At hatch, serum biochemical parameters, serum and liver antioxidant indicators, liver and muscle glycogen concentrations, and the relative hepatic expression of genes associated with energy metabolism and redox regulation were evaluated.ResultsThe findings revealed two distinct yet partially complementary response patterns. DEX, NIA, and especially DEX+NIA were more consistently associated with higher serum glucose, increased liver and muscle glycogen deposition, and gene-expression patterns consistent with metabolic support. Among serum biochemical variables, glucose was the most responsive parameter and was significantly increased in the DEX, NIA, DEX+NIA, RA+DEX+NIA-L, and RA+DEX+NIA-H groups compared with the control group. In contrast, RA was more clearly associated with antioxidant regulation, showing marked upregulation of NRF2 together with a more pronounced hepatic antioxidant enzyme response, particularly in CAT and GPx activities. Overall, antioxidant modulation was more evident in liver tissue than in serum. These treatment-specific profiles suggest that the embryonic response to prolonged storage may involve more than one physiological axis.ConclusionIn ovo metabolic interventions may help modulate the physiological burden imposed by prolonged egg storage, although the dominant physiological profile appears to depend on the compound administered. Under the present conditions, DEX/NIA-based treatments were more closely related to energy reserve support, whereas RA was more closely related to antioxidant defense. These findings provide a physiological basis for further studies on in ovo strategies aimed at improving resilience in long-stored broiler breeder eggs.Keywords: In ovo feeding, Niacin, Retinoic acid, Prolonged egg storage, Embryonic metabolism

Introduction

The storage of hatching eggs for defined periods is a common practice in commercial broiler production to facilitate flock scheduling and hatchery management. However, storage periods exceeding the optimal range are consistently associated with impaired embryonic development, reduced hatchability, and lower physiological robustness during incubation and hatching (Fasenko, 2007; Kouame et al., 2021). The adverse effects of prolonged storage are not limited to changes in egg quality alone, but may also influence embryonic energy balance, substrate availability, and metabolic resilience during late development (Givisiez et al., 2020; Özbaşer Bulut et al., 2025). Prolonged egg storage is not only a hatchery management issue, but also a biologically relevant source of embryonic metabolic stress that may compromise physiological readiness for hatch.

Throughout development, the chicken embryo depends entirely on nutrients deposited in the yolk and albumen (Wong and Uni, 2021). Although the egg is rich in lipids, its carbohydrate content is extremely limited. Total carbohydrates account for less than 1% of egg contents, and free glucose represents only a very small fraction of this pool (Campos et al., 2011). During late embryogenesis, however, glucose demand increases markedly because of intensified muscular activity, preparation for hatching, and restricted oxygen availability. Under these conditions, the embryo relies increasingly on glycogen mobilization and gluconeogenesis from amino acids to sustain energy production (Moran, 2007; Uni et al., 2005; Dayan et al., 2023). Prolonged storage may therefore aggravate an already constrained carbohydrate economy and increase metabolic pressure on the embryo (Fasenko, 2007; Givisiez et al., 2020). From a poultry physiology perspective, the late embryonic period represents a metabolically vulnerable window in which limitations in carbohydrate availability and oxidative balance may have disproportionate effects on embryonic resilience.

A second important dimension of this physiological burden is oxidative stress. The late embryonic period is characterized by high metabolic activity and elevated reactive oxygen species generation (Yang et al., 2018). In long-stored eggs, the metabolic adjustments required to compensate for limited energy availability may further intensify oxidative pressure. Thus, embryos derived from prolonged egg storage are likely challenged by a dual constraint: restricted energy substrate availability and increased oxidant burden (de Oliveira et al., 2008; Givisiez et al., 2020).

In ovo feeding has emerged as an important strategy for delivering biologically active compounds to the embryo during critical developmental stages. This approach has been used to support embryonic energy status, intestinal development, nutrient utilization, and physiological adaptation (Uni et al., 2005; Retes et al., 2018; Hou et al., 2018; Das et al., 2021; Kpodo and Proszkowiec-Weglarz, 2023). In the context of prolonged egg storage, in ovo interventions may provide a biochemically meaningful means of improving embryonic metabolic flexibility and physiological resilience (Akhlaghi et al., 2013). Therefore, interventions that support embryonic metabolic stability during this period may be relevant not only for understanding developmental physiology, but also for improving the biological robustness of long-stored hatching eggs.

In the present study, dextrose (D-glucose) was selected as a directly utilizable carbohydrate source to help support the embryonic energy deficit. Previous studies have shown that in ovo carbohydrate administration may improve late-embryonic energy status, increase hepatic glycogen reserves, and support post-hatch adaptation (Uni et al., 2005; Hou et al., 2018; Retes et al., 2018). Niacin was evaluated not merely as a vitamin supplement but as a precursor contributing to the NAD and NADP pools that are central to embryonic energy metabolism, mitochondrial redox reactions, and antioxidant defense. Earlier studies demonstrated that the developing chick embryo can synthesize nicotinic acid from tryptophan and that niacin biosynthesis occurs during chick embryonic development (Schweigert et al., 1948; Wilson and Henderson, 1960). In addition, metabolomic evidence indicates that yolk amino acid availability, including tryptophan, changes during broiler embryogenesis (Liu et al., 2021). Therefore, in the present study, niacin administration was primarily considered a direct strategy to support NAD/NADP-dependent metabolic and redox capacity in embryos from long-stored eggs.

Retinoic acid, the active metabolite of vitamin A, was included not as a direct energy substrate but as a redox-active regulatory molecule with potential relevance to oxidative balance. Beyond its established roles in differentiation and developmental regulation, all-trans retinoic acid has been shown to interact with NRF2-related signaling in a concentration-dependent manner, activating NRF2 and inducing its downstream antioxidant target genes, including the rate-limiting enzymes of glutathione biosynthesis, at the higher concentrations relevant to the present design (Tan et al., 2008). Because NRF2 is a principal transcriptional regulator of antioxidant and cytoprotective responses (Hammad et al., 2023), retinoic acid was considered a candidate intervention for modulating the oxidative component of storage-associated embryonic stress, complementary to the energy-substrate support provided by dextrose and niacin. Rather than treating these compounds as interchangeable in ovo supplements, the present study was designed to determine whether they preferentially modulate distinct adaptive axes — energy metabolism versus antioxidant defense — of embryonic physiology under prolonged storage conditions.

To examine these responses at the molecular level, a targeted hepatic gene panel was selected to represent the two physiological axes addressed by the study hypothesis — energy metabolism and redox regulation. To capture substrate handling and energy provision, ACACA and CPT1A were included as indicators of lipogenesis and fatty acid β-oxidation, respectively (Sanders and Griffin, 2016); IDH3A and OGDH as markers of tricarboxylic acid cycle flux; and G6PC as an indicator of hepatic glucose output, a pathway of particular relevance given the limited carbohydrate economy of stored eggs (Hutton and O'Brien, 2009). To capture the redox dimension, IDH2 was included as a marker of mitochondrial NADPH-linked redox capacity, providing a functional bridge between energy metabolism and antioxidant defense (Chen and Ding, 2023), and NRF2 as the principal transcriptional regulator of the antioxidant response (Tan et al., 2008). Together, these genes provide a focused framework for evaluating whether prolonged storage-associated adaptation in the embryonic liver is biased toward energy provision or antioxidant defense.

In light of this information, the primary physiological problem in long-stored eggs is not merely reduced hatchability, but also limited carbohydrate availability, increased glucose demand during late embryogenesis, depletion of energy reserves, and the accompanying oxidative burden. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of in ovo administration of dextrose, niacin, and retinoic acid in long-stored broiler breeder eggs on serum biochemistry, antioxidant defense systems, glycogen stores, and the expression of genes associated with energy metabolism. The main hypothesis of the study was that dextrose could provide direct energy/substrate support, niacin could support metabolic flux and redox capacity, and retinoic acid could regulate the antioxidant defense axis, thereby mitigating the adverse effects of prolonged storage on the embryo.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval

The study was conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and was approved by the Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee of Tekirdag Namik Kemal University (Approval No. 2024/279; Date: 08 January 2024).

Chemicals and commercial kits

All-trans retinoic acid (Cayman Chemicals, Ann Arbor, MI, USA; Cat. No. 11017) was used as the retinoic acid source, nicotinic acid (Bio Basic Canada, Markham, ON, Canada; Cat. No. NB0660) as the niacin source, and anhydrous D-glucose (Bio Basic Canada; Cat. No. GB0219) as the carbohydrate source. Glycogen concentrations in liver and muscle tissues were determined using a Glycogen Colorimetric Assay Kit (Elabscience, Houston, TX, USA; Cat. No. E-BC-K073-S). Antioxidant enzyme activities were evaluated using chicken-specific Catalase (CAT), Superoxide Dismutase (SOD), and Glutathione Peroxidase (GPx) ELISA kits (BT Lab, Shanghai, China; Cat. Nos. E0118Ch, E0011Ch, and E0298Ch, respectively). Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were measured using a thiobarbituric acid (TBA)-based colorimetric assay kit (Elabscience; Cat. No. E-BC-K025-M). For molecular analyses, total RNA was isolated using the EZ-10 Spin Column Total RNA Mini-preps Kit (Bio Basic Canada; Cat. No. BS1361/SK8655), cDNA was synthesized using the OneScript Plus cDNA Synthesis Kit (ABMgood, Richmond, BC, Canada; Cat. No. G236), and quantitative PCR was performed using BlasTaq 2X qPCR MasterMix (ABMgood; Cat. No. G891).

Eggs, storage, incubation, and experimental groups

Eggs obtained from 38-week-old Ross 308 broiler breeder hens were individually weighed using a balance with 0.01 g sensitivity, numbered, and stored for 14 days at 16°C and 65% relative humidity. At the end of the storage period, eggs were reweighed before incubation, and the mean egg weight immediately before incubation was 59.75 ± 0.58 g. Eggs were then transferred to VGS490 setter incubators operated at 37.5°C and 65% relative humidity. Each setter incubator had a capacity of 490 eggs, and two machines were used during incubation/hatching. Eggs were turned every 2 h during incubation. On embryonic day 18 (E18), eggs were candled, infertile eggs were excluded, and a total of 640 fertile eggs were included in the in ovo injection phase of the study. The final number of injected fertile eggs corresponded to seven main groups with 80 eggs per group and two sham groups with 40 eggs per group. After injection, fertile eggs were transferred to the hatcher, where temperature and relative humidity were adjusted to 36.5°C and 75%, respectively. Eggs from each treatment group were distributed between the machines as evenly as possible to minimize potential machine-related and positional effects. Machine or tray position was not treated as an experimental factor. The biological replicates reported for biochemical, tissue, and gene-expression analyses refer to individual chicks sampled at hatch.

The experiment included the following groups: (1) control (non-injected), (2) Sham 1 (200 µL of 0.9% NaCl), (3) Sham 2 (200 µL of vehicle solution containing DMSO equivalent to that used in the RA group), (4) dextrose group (DEX; 200 µL of 500 mOsm dextrose solution), (5) retinoic acid group (RA; 200 µL of approximately 90 µM all-trans retinoic acid), (6) niacin group (NIA; 200 µL of 80 mM niacin), (7) dextrose + niacin group (DEX+NIA; 200 µL of 500 mOsm dextrose + 40 mM niacin), (8) combination 1 group (RA+DEX+NIA-L; 200 µL of 90 µM RA + 500 mOsm dextrose + 40 mM niacin), and (9) combination 2 group (RA+DEX+NIA-H; 200 µL of 90 µM RA + 900 mOsm dextrose + 80 mM niacin). Sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.2 (effect size = 0.56, α = 0.05, power = 0.80), and groups were planned as n = 80 for the control and main treatment groups and n = 40 for the sham groups. The injection volume and osmolarity levels were selected based on previous in ovo feeding literature and practical tolerability considerations. A volume of 200 µL was used because it remains below volumes reported to negatively affect hatchability in carbohydrate-based in ovo applications. The 500 mOsm dextrose solution was selected to remain within the generally recommended osmolarity range for carbohydrate-based in ovo solutions, whereas the 900 mOsm dextrose-containing combination was included as a higher-osmolarity comparison within previously evaluated ranges (Tako et al., 2004; Zhai et al., 2011; Retes et al., 2018). Niacin was included as an NAD/NADP precursor, and retinoic acid was used as a low-dose regulatory compound targeting developmental and redox-related signaling rather than as an energy substrate.

Two sham groups were included in the broader experimental design to evaluate whether the injection procedure and vehicle solutions produced overt toxic effects that could impair hatchability. Hatchability percentages in Sham 1 and Sham 2 were comparable to the untreated control, indicating that neither the saline injection nor the DMSO vehicle prevented hatch. Because the present manuscript focuses on metabolic and molecular responses in the main treatment groups, and because sham groups were used primarily to assess hatch-related vehicle tolerance, sham data are not presented in the main tables.

Preparation of treatment solutions and in ovo injection

All treatment solutions were freshly prepared on the day of injection, warmed to 37°C before administration, and sterilized by passage through a 0.22 µm syringe filter. Because retinoic acid is light-sensitive, RA-containing solutions were prepared with minimal light exposure and kept protected from light until injection. The RA stock solution was prepared at 3.33 mM and diluted to obtain a final working concentration of approximately 90 µM. The amount of DMSO used in the Sham 2 group was kept equivalent to the carrier amount present in the RA treatment. Dextrose solutions were prepared to provide either 500 or 900 mOsm osmolarity, and niacin solutions were prepared at final concentrations of 40 or 80 mM depending on group design.

In ovo injection was performed on E18. The broad end of each egg was disinfected with 70% ethanol, and 200 µL of the corresponding solution was injected into the amniotic region at an approximate depth of 2.49 cm using a 25-gauge cannula. After injection, the opening was sealed with paraffin and the eggs were returned to the hatcher.

Experimental scope and sampling

The present manuscript represents the metabolic and molecular component of a broader experimental project. Although hatchability, chick quality, and growth-related parameters were also evaluated within the overall design, the present study specifically focuses on serum biochemical parameters, serum and liver antioxidant status, liver and muscle glycogen concentrations, and hepatic gene expression related to energy metabolism and redox regulation, with the aim of resolving treatment-related differences in embryonic physiological adaptation at hatch. Accordingly, the endpoints presented here were selected to characterize embryonic metabolic status, tissue antioxidant response, and hepatic transcriptional adaptation under prolonged storage-associated stress. For biochemical and tissue analyses, 7 biological replicates per group were used. For gene expression analyses, 3 biological replicates per group were analyzed, and each biological replicate was run in 3 technical replicates. Statistical analyses of qPCR data were based on biological replicates. The qPCR component was designed as an exploratory molecular assessment, and three biological replicates per group were selected in line with comparable avian embryo and liver gene-expression studies using three biological replicates (Xie et al., 2018; Farzaneh and Azizidoost, 2025).

Sample collection

At hatch, blood, liver, and pectoral muscle samples were collected from chicks selected for biochemical and molecular analyses. Serum was separated from blood samples by centrifugation and stored at −20°C until analysis. Liver and muscle samples used for glycogen determination were rapidly collected and preserved appropriately for tissue biochemical analysis. Liver samples allocated for qPCR were immersed in RNAlater solution (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA) and stored at −80°C until RNA isolation.

Serum biochemical analyses

Serum concentrations of total protein, albumin, glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol, fructosamine, non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA), β-hydroxybutyrate (BHBA), total bile acids, and uric acid were determined using appropriate commercial kits and/or standard biochemical procedures. Results were expressed on a group basis as mean ± SEM.

Glycogen analysis in liver and muscle tissues

Glycogen concentrations in liver and pectoral muscle tissues were measured using the Elabscience Glycogen Colorimetric Assay Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, tissue samples were homogenized and processed in accordance with the kit protocol, and glycogen-derived glucose was determined by a colorimetric reaction after the required pretreatment steps. Absorbance values were converted to concentration values using the kit-based calibration procedure, and results were expressed as mg glycogen per g tissue.

Antioxidant enzyme activities and lipid peroxidation analysis

CAT, SOD, and GPx levels in serum and liver samples were determined using chicken-specific ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. These assays were based on an antibody-mediated enzyme-linked detection principle specific for each analyte. Optical density values obtained from the reactions were interpreted using the kit protocols, and final values were calculated accordingly. Serum and liver MDA concentrations were measured as an indicator of lipid peroxidation using a TBA-based colorimetric assay kit. In this method, MDA reacts with thiobarbituric acid to form a colored complex, and the resulting signal was used for quantitative evaluation according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from liver tissues using the EZ-10 Spin Column Total RNA Mini-preps Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. This method is based on the purification of total RNA using a silica membrane spin-column system. Briefly, liver tissues were homogenized in lysis buffer, RNA was bound to the silica membrane, contaminants were removed through washing steps, and purified RNA was eluted with elution buffer. RNA quantity and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically, and suitable samples were used for cDNA synthesis.

Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from equal amounts of RNA using the OneScript Plus cDNA Synthesis Kit following the manufacturer’s recommendations. Quantitative PCR was performed using BlasTaq 2X qPCR MasterMix on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The amplification program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min. Amplification specificity was verified by melt-curve analysis from 65°C to 95°C with 0.5°C increments. Relative hepatic expression of ACACA, CPT1A, IDH3A, OGDH, G6PC, IDH2, and NRF2 was analyzed using ACTB as the reference gene. Relative expression values were calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences and amplicon characteristics are presented in Table 1. Standard curves were generated for each primer pair using serially diluted cDNA. Amplification efficiencies ranged from 90% to 98%, with R² values between 0.980 and 0.992, indicating acceptable amplification performance for the target and reference genes. Amplification specificity was confirmed by melt-curve analysis.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SEM. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using the Brown–Forsythe test. When these assumptions were satisfied, differences among groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. For serum biochemical and antioxidant parameters, Dunnett’s post hoc test was used with the untreated control as the reference because the primary objective was to compare each in ovo treatment with the control group. For glycogen and gene-expression endpoints, Tukey’s post hoc test was applied because comparisons among treatment groups, including single-agent and combination treatments, were also biologically relevant. For RT-qPCR, relative expression values were calculated by the 2^-ΔΔCt method, but statistical analyses were performed using ΔCt values. Technical replicates were averaged prior to analysis, and all statistical comparisons were based on biological replicates. In the tables, superscript letters indicate post hoc differences versus the control according to Dunnett’s test. In the figures, different letters indicate significant pairwise differences among all groups according to Tukey’s test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Hatchability of injected fertile eggs

Hatchability of injected fertile eggs ranged from 88.75% to 96.25% across the experimental groups. The untreated control group showed a hatchability of 91.25%, whereas Sham 1 and Sham 2 showed hatchability values of 95.00% and 92.50%, respectively. These values indicated that neither saline injection nor the DMSO vehicle produced an overt hatch-limiting effect. Hatchability data are presented descriptively only; no statistical analysis was performed on hatchability in this manuscript. Detailed hatchability, chick quality, and post-hatch performance outcomes will be reported separately.

Serum biochemical parameters

Serum biochemical findings are summarized in Table 2. Among the evaluated serum parameters, glucose concentration showed the most consistent treatment-related response. The control group had the lowest mean serum glucose level (181.30 ± 4.67 mg/dL). Serum glucose concentrations were significantly higher in the DEX, NIA, DEX+NIA, RA+DEX+NIA-L, and RA+DEX+NIA-H groups than in the control group (P < 0.05), whereas the increase observed in the RA group did not reach statistical significance. The highest glucose concentration was observed in the DEX+NIA group (199.20 ± 2.85 mg/dL).

Serum antioxidant enzyme activities and MDA levels

Serum antioxidant data are presented in Table 3. Serum CAT activity was significantly increased in the RA, RA+DEX+NIA-L, and RA+DEX+NIA-H groups compared with the control group (P < 0.05). The highest serum CAT activity was observed in the RA+DEX+NIA-L group (24.31 ± 1.54 U/mg), followed by the RA (22.35 ± 1.15 U/mg) and RA+DEX+NIA-H (20.90 ± 1.65 U/mg) groups. The increases observed in the DEX, NIA, and DEX+NIA groups were not statistically significant.

Taken together, the serum antioxidant response was limited, with CAT emerging as the most responsive marker at the systemic level.

Discussion

The present study showed that in ovo interventions applied to broiler breeder eggs stored for 14 days did not produce a uniform physiological response, but rather elicited distinct yet partially complementary response patterns related to energy metabolism and antioxidant defense. Overall, the data suggest that DEX-, NIA-, and especially DEX+NIA-containing treatments were more closely associated with glycogen deposition and gene-expression patterns consistent with metabolic support, whereas RA-containing treatments were more strongly associated with antioxidant regulation, particularly in liver tissue. This overall pattern is consistent with the physiological context of late embryogenesis, during which glucose demand increases and oxidative balance becomes increasingly critical (de Oliveira et al., 2008; Givisiez et al., 2020). The relevance of these findings lies in showing that prolonged storage-related embryonic stress is not physiologically uniform and that different in ovo interventions may bias the adaptive response toward different functional priorities at hatch.

Among the serum biochemical findings, glucose was the only parameter that showed a clear and consistent response across multiple treatment groups. The significantly higher serum glucose concentrations observed in the DEX, NIA, DEX+NIA, RA+DEX+NIA-L, and RA+DEX+NIA-H groups, relative to the control group, indicate that in ovo metabolic support may influence systemic energy availability at hatch. Previous studies have shown that in ovo carbohydrate supplementation can improve the energy status of late-stage embryos and increase glycogen reserves (Uni et al., 2005; Retes et al., 2018). The present results are broadly in line with those observations and suggest that metabolic interventions based on carbohydrate and niacin support may help maintain glucose availability under prolonged storage conditions. In contrast, the remaining serum biochemical parameters did not differ significantly among groups, indicating that the systemic biochemical response was relatively selective rather than global under the conditions of this study.

One of the most notable physiological findings was the increase in tissue glycogen, particularly in the DEX+NIA group. Liver glycogen was significantly higher in the DEX+NIA and RA+DEX+NIA-L groups than in the control group, whereas muscle glycogen was significantly increased only in the DEX+NIA group. Because liver and muscle glycogen represent critical energy reserves during the peri-hatch period, these findings support the view that combined dextrose and niacin treatment may have contributed to embryonic energy economy more effectively than either component alone under the present experimental conditions (Moran, 2007; de Oliveira et al., 2008). At the same time, this interpretation should remain within the limits of the measured endpoints, since the present study did not directly assess dynamic flux through carbohydrate pathways or post-hatch performance traits within this manuscript. Within this framework, DEX+NIA appears to be the treatment most consistently associated with energy reserve support, as reflected by its combined effects on serum glucose, tissue glycogen deposition, and metabolic gene expression patterns.

The gene expression data were generally compatible with this metabolic pattern, although they should be interpreted cautiously. In particular, the upregulation of ACACA, CPT1A, IDH3A, OGDH, and IDH2 in DEX-, NIA-, and DEX+NIA-related groups suggests transcriptional responses consistent with altered substrate handling, tricarboxylic acid cycle-associated metabolism, and mitochondrial redox-related functions. The prominence of DEX+NIA across both glycogen deposition and several metabolic genes may indicate that combined substrate and cofactor support was more strongly associated with hepatic gene-expression responses than single-agent treatments. However, because these conclusions are based on mRNA expression rather than direct enzyme flux measurements or protein-level validation, they should be interpreted as gene-expression patterns consistent with altered metabolic and redox-related regulation, rather than as direct evidence of pathway activation or metabolic flux.

Within this framework, the niacin-containing groups are particularly noteworthy. Niacin (nicotinic acid) is a precursor of NAD+ and NADP+, the redox cofactors that accept and donate electrons in glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, and that also sustain the NADPH supply required for glutathione- and thioredoxin-based antioxidant systems. An increased niacin supply may therefore expand the NAD/NADP pool available for these reactions. Consistent with this, the relatively strong expression of the TCA-cycle-related genes IDH3A and OGDH, and particularly of the NADP+-dependent IDH2, in the NIA and DEX+NIA groups is compatible with a state in which mitochondrial energy-linked and redox-related pathways are more strongly supported, since IDH2 contributes to mitochondrial NADPH generation and thereby links energy metabolism to antioxidant capacity (Chen and Ding, 2023). Likewise, the marked increase in G6PC expression in the DEX group, and to a lesser extent in the NIA group, may reflect an adaptive hepatic response related to glucose handling, although it could equally represent a counter-regulatory or stress-associated response. Nevertheless, these interpretations should remain conservative, as the present data do not directly resolve flux through gluconeogenic or mitochondrial pathways. Therefore, whether increased G6PC expression supports glucose output, reflects increased gluconeogenic capacity, indicates futile cycling, or represents a stress-related transcriptional response cannot be determined from mRNA data alone, and future studies should directly measure gluconeogenic flux.

In contrast to the metabolic profile associated with DEX/NIA-based treatments, the clearest antioxidant-related molecular signal was observed in the RA group. Among the analyzed genes, NRF2 showed a marked and treatment-specific increase only in this group. NRF2 is a principal transcriptional regulator of antioxidant and cytoprotective responses, and previous studies have reported that retinoic acid may interact with NRF2-related signaling in a context-dependent manner (Tan et al., 2008; Hammad et al., 2023). In the present study, the strong NRF2 response in the RA group, together with the more pronounced liver antioxidant enzyme response, is compatible with the interpretation that RA treatment was more closely associated with antioxidant defense than with the metabolic reserve-support pattern observed in DEX+NIA-related groups. However, this should be interpreted as an association supported by the present dataset rather than as a fully resolved mechanistic pathway. By contrast, the RA-associated profile was less defined by substrate-related endpoints and more clearly characterized by NRF2-linked antioxidant responses, particularly at the hepatic level.

The antioxidant enzyme data further reinforce this distinction between systemic and tissue-level responses. At the serum level, CAT was the only marker showing a clear significant increase, mainly in RA-, RA+DEX+NIA-L-, and RA+DEX+NIA-H-containing groups, whereas serum SOD, GPx, and MDA did not differ significantly. In liver tissue, CAT and GPx responses were more pronounced, whereas SOD activity did not differ significantly among groups. This pattern suggests that the antioxidant effects of the tested interventions were more evident at the hepatic level than in the circulation. The absence of significant differences in MDA despite changes in enzymatic antioxidant responses may indicate that the treatments influenced defensive capacity more clearly than steady-state lipid peroxidation under the present experimental conditions. These findings add to the view that embryonic responses to hatchery-associated stressors may be better interpreted as coordinated physiological profiles rather than single-parameter changes.

Taken together, the results support a model in which prolonged egg storage imposes both metabolic and oxidative constraints, but different in ovo interventions may preferentially influence different adaptive axes. Dextrose- and niacin-based treatments, particularly DEX+NIA, appear more closely related to substrate availability and glycogen reserve support, whereas RA appears more closely related to antioxidant regulation. The combination groups partly bridged these patterns, especially RA+DEX+NIA-L, which showed both antioxidant and glycogen-related responses. Rather than indicating a single unified mechanism, the dataset supports the interpretation that the response to prolonged storage may involve multiple, partially overlapping physiological axes.

From the perspective of the developing embryo and the day-old chick, these two axes are physiologically complementary rather than independent. During the peri-hatch period, the embryo must simultaneously mobilize energy reserves for the hatching effort and contain the oxidative burden that accompanies intense metabolic activity in long-stored eggs. In this context, the dextrose- and niacin-based treatments, which were associated with higher serum glucose and greater liver and muscle glycogen deposition, may help secure the carbohydrate and cofactor supply needed for hatching and early post-hatch metabolism, whereas the retinoic acid-associated NRF2 response may strengthen the antioxidant defense that protects newly formed tissues during this transition. The combination groups, particularly RA+DEX+NIA-L, partially expressed both profiles, suggesting that delivering substrate, cofactor, and redox-regulatory support together could, in principle, address both constraints at once. However, because the present manuscript did not measure hatchability beyond a summary level, chick quality, or post-hatch growth in detail, the extent to which these biochemical and transcriptional differences translate into improved chick development remains to be established and should be addressed in studies that integrate these endpoints.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the present study focused on selected biochemical, antioxidant, glycogen, and hepatic transcriptional endpoints and therefore does not capture the full spectrum of embryonic responses to prolonged egg storage. Second, gene-expression analyses were based on three biological replicates per group, selected in line with comparable exploratory avian embryo and liver gene-expression studies; although each biological sample was analyzed in technical triplicate, this limited biological sample size may reduce statistical power and warrants cautious interpretation of the transcriptional findings. Confirmatory studies with larger biological replication are therefore needed. Third, mRNA expression changes should not be interpreted as direct evidence of protein abundance, enzyme activity, or metabolic flux, and future studies should include protein-level validation and pathway-specific functional assays. Fourth, downstream NRF2 target genes such as GCLC, NQO1, and HO-1 were not assessed; therefore, the functional activation of the NRF2 antioxidant pathway could not be fully confirmed. Finally, although hatchability, chick quality, and post-hatch performance outcomes were evaluated within the broader experimental project, only a brief hatchability summary is presented here, and those production-related outcomes will be reported separately.

Although the present manuscript does not directly resolve production-level outcomes, the observed treatment-specific physiological profiles may still be relevant for designing more targeted in ovo strategies for long-stored broiler breeder eggs.

Overall, the study indicates that metabolic interventions applied in ovo may help modulate the physiological burden associated with prolonged egg storage, but that the nature of this modulation depends on the compound used. Under the present conditions, DEX+NIA was more consistently associated with glycogen deposition and energy metabolism-related transcriptional responses, whereas RA was more clearly associated with NRF2-linked antioxidant regulation. These findings provide a focused physiological basis for future studies integrating molecular, biochemical, and hatch-related endpoints in long-stored broiler breeder eggs.

Conclusion

In ovo administration of dextrose, niacin, and retinoic acid to broiler breeder eggs stored for 14 days produced distinct yet partially complementary effects on embryonic and hepatic physiology. Under the present experimental conditions, dextrose- and niacin-based treatments, particularly DEX+NIA, were more consistently associated with higher glycogen deposition, increased serum glucose, and gene-expression patterns consistent with metabolic support, whereas retinoic acid was more clearly associated with NRF2-related antioxidant regulation and a more pronounced hepatic antioxidant enzyme response. From an applied poultry perspective, these findings suggest that the biological effects of in ovo interventions in long-stored eggs may depend not only on whether supplementation is provided, but also on the physiological axis that is preferentially targeted.

Because the present manuscript focuses on biochemical, glycogen, and transcriptional endpoints, further studies integrating hatchability, chick quality, post-hatch performance, and protein-level validation will be needed to better define the biological and practical significance of these responses. Future studies should also prioritize functional NRF2 target genes, including GCLC, NQO1, and HO-1, to determine whether the observed NRF2 upregulation reflects functional activation of the NRF2-mediated antioxidant pathway. Nevertheless, the current findings provide a focused physiological basis for future work on in ovo strategies tailored to the specific physiological constraints imposed by prolonged egg storage.
