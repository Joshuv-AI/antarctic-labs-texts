G3BP2 confers venetoclax resistance by stabilizing ELF1-mediated MCL1 transcription in acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Zhixiang Chen, Yuanhua Cai, Yaping Wang, Xiaolin Lai, Xiongbin Yan, Xuechun Wang, Fuquan Tu, Wanting Wu, Huiyin Gao, Yi Zheng, Yingying Zeng, Yang Li, Jinggang Li, Lili Pan, Peiyao Wang, Wei Ji, Lin Xu, Shaoyuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Venetoclax is a promising treatment for acute myeloid leukemia (AML) patients, but resistance occurs following the upregulation of MCL1. The RNA-binding protein G3BP2 is upregulated in various solid tumors, and targeting G3BP2 has been shown to restore chemosensitivity. However, the role of G3BP2 in AML development and venetoclax resistance remains unknown. Herein, an integrated analysis revealed that G3BP2 confers venetoclax resistance and correlates with poor outcomes in AML. G3BP2 deficiency inhibits AML proliferation and induces apoptosis in AML cells and xenograft mice. G3BP2 expression aligns with MCL1 levels across AML cohorts, and the regulatory effect of G3BP2 on MCL1 was validated in AML cells. Notably, G3BP2 and MCL1 do not interact directly; instead, G3BP2 interacts with ELF1 mRNA to increase its stability. The transcription factor ELF1 binds to the MCL1 promoter to induce its expression, which can be attenuated by G3BP2 inhibition. Furthermore, C108-mediated inhibition of G3BP2, in combination with venetoclax, has anticancer effects in primary AML cells and in patient-derived xenografts (PDX). C108 increases the therapeutic efficacy of venetoclax and their combination prolongs overall survival (OS) in AML model mice. These findings identify G3BP2 as a regulator of venetoclax resistance through ELF1-mediated MCL1 transcription, suggesting new therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a malignant neoplasm characterized by the aberrant proliferation and differentiation of myeloid stem/progenitor cells with poor outcomes [1]. In recent decades, venetoclax-based combination therapy has greatly improved the outcomes of AML patients, especially those with unfit AML or relapsed/refractory (R/R) AML patients [2–5]. However, some patients fail to achieve remission or experience relapse, indicating the presence of venetoclax resistance. These patients have poorer outcomes, with a median overall survival (OS) of 2.5 months [6, 7], indicating the presence of venetoclax resistance. Studies have reported that venetoclax resistance is associated with elevated levels of MCL1 or BCL-XL, TP53 gene mutations, FLT3-ITD mutations and decreased expression of BCL2 [8–11]. Among these, MCL1 upregulation is one of the best-studied and dominant mechanisms contributing to venetoclax resistance. MCL1 is a pro-survival member of the BCL2 family of proteins that is frequently upregulated in solid tumors and neoplastic hematologic disorders [12]. Increased levels of MCL1 induce resistance to chemotherapy, radiotherapy and BH3 mimetics targeting BCL2/BCL-XL [13–15]. High MCL1 expression is common in AML, leading to resistance to venetoclax. Patients with acute monocytic leukemia (M5) often show elevated MCL1 levels and poorer responses to venetoclax [9]. Overexpressed MCL1 preferentially sequesters Bim, thereby preventing Bim-mediated activation of BAX/BAK, inhibiting apoptosis, and contributing to venetoclax resistance in AML cells [16–18]. One potential approach to overcome this resistance involves using small-molecule inhibitors that target MCL1 [19]. Nonetheless, the limited evidence from clinical trials calls for caution when using MCL1-specific inhibitors in AML patients because of safety and effectiveness concerns [20–23]. Therefore, it is essential to explore new molecular targets to conquer venetoclax resistance.

In our study, an integrated analysis identified G3BP2 as a candidate gene that mediates venetoclax resistance. We revealed that pharmacological and lentiviral inhibition of G3BP2 increased the sensitivity of primary AML cells to venetoclax and restored sensitivity in resistant cells. The results were further validated in AML xenograft mice. In addition, we showed that G3BP2 promotes venetoclax resistance by stabilizing ELF1-mediated MCL1 transcription. These results indicate G3BP2 as a promising target for combination therapies to overcome venetoclax resistance in AML.

Materials and methods

Primary AML specimens

Bone marrow cells were obtained from 86 patients newly diagnosed with AML at Fujian Medical University Union Hospital (FJMUUH). Peripheral blood cells were collected and extracted from 21 healthy donors. Seventy-nine of the 86 AML patients were included in the survival analysis, as they had complete follow-up data available. All patients provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of FJMUUH.

Drugs

ABT-199 (Abmole, USA) and C108 (Sigma‒Aldrich, USA) were used for in vitro assays and in vivo treatment of xenograft mice.

Public datasets and data availability statement

The data used in this study are available in a public, open-access repository and are available upon reasonable request. The TCGA-LAML AML cohort from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga), the Beat-AML cohort from the Vizome database (http://vizome.org/), and AML cohorts from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), including the GSE13159, GSE37642, GSE10358, GSE12417, and GSE154109 datasets, were used in this study.

Cell line culture

The human cell lines U937, THP-1, MOLM-13, OCI-AML3, HL-60, OCI-AML2, K562, NB-4, KG-1, HEL, MV4-11, and 293T were obtained from Cellcook Biotech Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). All the cell lines used in this study were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free and were authenticated using short tandem repeat (STR) profiling analysis. The AML cell lines U937, THP-1, MOLM-13, OCI-AML3, HL-60, OCI-AML2, K562, NB-4, KG-1, HEL, and MV4-11 were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Sunview, China). 293T cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Sunview, China). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Cegrogen, Germany) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sunview, China). Cells were grown in a cell incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Plasmid construction

shRNAs targeting G3BP2 (shG3BP2: GACTCTGACAACCGTAGAATA) [24] and ELF1 (shELF1: GCTTGGTGTATCAGTTTAAA) were separately cloned and inserted into the pLKO.1-EGFP-Puro plasmid to knock down G3BP2 and ELF1. The cDNA of G3BP2-ΔRRM and the full-length cDNA of G3BP2, ELF1, and MCL1 were cloned and inserted into pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-copGFP-T2A-Puro plasmids to overexpress G3BP2-ΔRRM, G3BP2, ELF1, and MCL1. The full-length and truncated fragments of the MCL1 promoter, as well as the full-length BCL2 promoter, were cloned into the pGL4.10 luciferase reporter vector. The cDNA of ELF1 was cloned and inserted into the pcDNA3.1(+) plasmid (denoted pcDNA3.1( + )-ELF1).

Lentivirus transduction

Cells were infected with lentivirus targeting G3BP2/ELF1 or overexpressing G3BP2-ΔRRM/G3BP2/ELF1/MCL1 for 12 ~ 24 h. After 72 h of infection, the cells were treated with puromycin (Beyotime, China).

Cell proliferation assays

Cell proliferation assays were performed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (APExBIO, USA). Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5×104 cells per well. Ten microliters of CCK8 solution was added after 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4 days of incubation with treatment, then incubated for 2 h. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured with a microplate reader (Bio Tek, USA) to assess proliferation.

Apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was detected via an APC Annexin V/7-AAD Apoptosis Detection Kit (BioLegend, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Stained cells were analyzed using a BD Accuri™ C6 Plus flow cytometer (BD, USA).

Cell cycle assay

Cells were fixed with 75% ethanol overnight at 4 °C, washed with PBS, and stained with propidium iodide (PI; BD, USA) before analysis on a BD Accuri™ C6 Plus flow cytometer (BD, USA).

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded in 24-well plates covered with semisolid methyl cellulose (Sigma‒Aldrich, USA) at 500 cells per well. Colonies with >50 cells were counted after 10 days.

CellTiter-Glo assay

Cells were treated with inhibitors at specific concentrations in 384-well plates. Afterward, the cells were mixed with CellTiter-Glo reagent (Promega, USA) at specific time points according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blotting (WB)

Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (cat. #P0013B; Beyotime, China) containing both protease (cat. #GRF101; Epizyme, China) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (cat. #GRF102; Epizyme, China). Protein concentrations were quantified using a Bradford protein concentration assay kit (cat. #P0006; Beyotime, China). 30 to 50 μg of protein per sample was separated by SDS‒PAGE using 10% acrylamide gels and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (cat. #ISEQ00010; Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in TBST buffer (cat. #G0004-500ML; Servicebio, China) for 2 h at room temperature. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GAPDH (cat. #10494-1-AP; 1:2000 dilution; Proteintech, China), G3BP2 (cat. #16276-1-AP; 1:1000 dilution; Proteintech, China), MCL1 (cat. #16225-1-AP; 1:1000 dilution; Proteintech, China), ELF1 (cat. #22565-1-AP; 1:1000 dilution; Proteintech, China), and BCL2 (cat. #YT0470; 1:500 dilution; Immunoway, China).

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

U937 cells were treated with either DMSO (vehicle control) or C108 for 24 h. Following treatment, the cells were harvested and washed with PBS supplemented with protease inhibitors (1 mmol/L PMSF). The cells in both groups (DMSO and C108) were subsequently separately partitioned into 6 equal parts and heated to 37, 45, 48, 53, 55, or 58 °C for 3 min. After heating, the cells were resuspended in NP40 lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) and subjected to three consecutive freeze‒thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen to achieve complete lysis. The resulting lysates were centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants containing the soluble protein fraction were collected and boiled in loading buffer for WB.

Molecular docking

The protein structure of G3BP2 was retrieved from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). The chemical structure of C108 was obtained from the PubChem database. Molecular docking of C108 to G3BP2 was then conducted using AutoDock Vina [25]. Binding energies served as docking scores to assess protein–ligand affinity; more negative values indicated higher binding stability [26]. Docking results were visualized in PyMOL 2.4 [27].

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (TransGen Biotech, China), and was reverse transcribed to generate cDNA using a reverse transcription kit according to the manufacturer’s protocols (Thermo Fisher, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China). Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method [28]. The primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

RNA stability assay

AML cells were exposed to 5 μg/ml actinomycin D (MCE, USA) in 6-well plates. qPCR was performed at the indicated time points for mRNA decay analysis.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays

RIP and ChIP assays were performed using an EZ-Magna RIP™ RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Millipore, USA) and an EZ-Magna CHIP™ Assay Kit (Millipore, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fold enrichment was determined by comparing results from the immunoprecipitation (IP) group to the IgG control group. The primers for ChIP-qPCR of MCL1 and BCL2 are listed in Table S2.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

For promoter activity assays, 293T cells were cultured in 6-well plates and cotransfected with 2 μg of pcDNA3.1( + )-ELF1 or the mock vector, 1.6 μg of the pGL4.10 firefly luciferase reporter plasmid (containing either the empty vector, full-length or truncated MCL1 promoter, or the full-length BCL2 promoter), and 0.16 μg of pRL-TK (Renilla luciferase control reporter vector). At 48 h post transfection, luciferase activity was quantified using the Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity for each sample to determine relative promoter strength.

Animal models and pharmacological treatments

Adult female NCG (NOD/ShiLtJGpt-Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22/Gpt) mice (6 weeks old) were purchased from the Shanghai Model Organisms Center (Shanghai, China).

For patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, NCG mice were preconditioned with a single intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 25 mg/kg busulfan (Sparkjade, Chnia) 48 h before being intravenously injected with primary AML cells. Following the confirmation of engraftment (monitored by percentage of human CD45 in peripheral blood), the mice were randomly assigned to four groups: vehicle, venetoclax (80 mg/kg, i.g., q.d.), C108 (30 mg/kg, i.p., b.i.d.), or the combination of venetoclax and C108 for 21 days of treatment (5 mice per group).

For cell-derived xenograft (CDX) models, mice were intravenously injected with luciferase-labeled U937 (U937-luc) cells. Treatment commenced on day 7 post-injection and continued until day 28 (5 mice per group). Disease burden was longitudinally monitored using bioluminescence imaging (BLI). In accordance with established criteria [29, 30], disease progression was defined by clinical signs, including body weight loss, reduced activity, ruffled fur, paralysis, or hunched posture. Mice were euthanized upon a significant increase in tumor burden, as confirmed by BLI. Euthanasia was also performed if mice exhibited clinical signs of morbidity.

Flow cytometry

Spleen and bone marrow samples were harvested and prepared as single-cell suspensions for flow cytometry analysis of human CD45, mouse CD45, MCL1, ELF1, and Ki-67 expression.

Histology and immunostaining

Spleen tissues from AML mice were fixed in 3.7% formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for IHC analysis. The prepared sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining or immunohistochemistry (IHC). Staining for human G3BP2, CD45, MCL1 and ELF1 was performed using established methods.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

The Seurat package in R software (version 4.2.2) was used to analyze the RNA sequencing data. Detailed quality control metrics were generated and evaluated. We mapped the UMIs to genes and subsequently removed low-quality cells. Cells were flagged as poor quality if they met any of the following criteria: (1) the number of expressed genes was lower than 200 or higher than 6000 (2) 20% or more of the UMIs were mapped to mitochondrial genes; or (3) the cells had fewer than 1000 or more than 40,000 total molecules (nCount_RNA).

Data normalization was performed using Seurat’s “NormalizeData” function with “LogNormalize” as the normalization method (scale.factor = 100,000). Variable genes were detected using the “FindVariableFeatures” function (nfeatures = 2000). The “FindIntegrationAnchors” function was used to integrate all the AML samples. The scaled gene expression data were projected onto principal components (PCs). Fifty PCs were used for nonlinear dimensionality reduction using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). The “FindClusters” function in the Seurat package was then utilized to conduct cell clustering analysis by embedding cells into a plot with a resolution parameter of 0.6. We annotated cells on the basis of the expression of curated known cell markers, as shown in Fig. 1C and Fig. S1C. The CopyKAT (copy number karyotyping of tumors) algorithm was used to analyze copy number variations (CNVs). The basic parameters of CopyKAT were as follows: ngene.chr, 5; win.size, 25; KS.cut, 0.15; and distance, “Euclidean”. RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from U937 cells with downregulated G3BP2 expression and control cells. Library construction and sequencing were performed by the Igenebook Bioinformatics Institute (Wuhan, China) using the BGISEQ-500 system. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed with the criteria of an FDR < 0.05 and a log|FC | ≥ 0.5.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 20.0 (IBM Corporation Armonk, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Student’s t-tests, Pearson correlation analyses, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, Kaplan–Meier analyses, and univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were used to analyze the data. Differences for which P was < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Integrated analysis associates elevated G3BP2 levels with resistance to venetoclax and adverse prognosis in AML patients

To identify biomarkers linked to venetoclax resistance in AML patients, we conducted an integrated analysis of three datasets. A prior CRISPR screen identified 839 genes associated with venetoclax resistance (P < 0.05; Fig. 1A, Table S3) [31]. Single-cell RNA sequencing (GSE154109) revealed that 1175 genes were upregulated in AML patients compared with healthy donors (FDR < 0.001; Fig. 1A–C, Fig. S1A–C, Table S4). Pearson correlation analysis of 542 AML samples from GSE13159 revealed 1,232 genes associated with MCL1 expression (Table S5). The intersection of these datasets revealed 26 overlapping genes (Fig. 1A, D). G3BP2 was selected for further investigation because of its strong and compelling correlation with MCL1, a key player in driving venetoclax resistance in AML patients.

Single-cell RNA sequencing showed significantly higher G3BP2 expression in AML patients compared to healthy donors (Fig. 1E, F, Fig. S1D), which was validated by qPCR in our internal FJMUUH cohort (n = 86 newly diagnosed AML patients; Fig. 1G). In the 79 patients whose complete clinical and survival data were available (Table S6), G3BP2 mRNA expression strongly correlated with MCL1 expression (Fig. 1H). Higher G3BP2 expression was associated with poor OS (Fig. 1I), which is consistent with the findings in the GSE37642 cohort (Fig. S1E). Further univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to evaluate the prognostic value of G3BP2 expression alongside established clinical variables, including age, gender, white blood cell count (WBC count), and 2022 ELN risk stratification [32]. Univariate analysis revealed that G3BP2 expression, age, and ELN 2022 risk stratification were significant predictors of survival in this cohort (n = 79). Conversely, gender, and WBC count did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 1J). Multivariate analysis confirmed that high G3BP2 expression is an independent poor prognostic factor (Fig. 1J).

Among 79 patients in the FJMUUH cohort, 28 received venetoclax-based regimens, with 14 responding to treatment and 14 showing no response. G3BP2 mRNA levels were higher in non-responding patients than in responders (Fig. 1K). Moreover, the expression of G3BP2 in AML cell lines that are resistant or sensitive to venetoclax was evaluated [19, 33]. Compared to venetoclax-sensitive (Ven-S) cells, venetoclax-resistant (Ven-R) cell lines showed elevated levels of G3BP2 (Fig. 1L). These findings support a contributory role of elevated G3BP2 in clinical venetoclax resistance.

Loss of G3BP2 mitigates AML development through the regulation of MCL1

To explore the relationship between G3BP2 and MCL1, we examined their expression across six AML datasets. Correlation analysis revealed positive associations between G3BP2 and MCL1 transcript levels in multiple cohorts (Fig. 2A, Fig. S2A). WB and qPCR confirmed that G3BP2 knockdown markedly reduced the protein and mRNA levels of MCL1 and BCL2 (Fig. 2B, C), and vice versa (Fig. S2B, C). To further confirm that G3BP2 regulates MCL1 expression in AML, we performed in vivo and in vitro experiments. Downregulation of G3BP2 suppressed cell proliferation (Fig. 2D, E, Fig. S2D), increased apoptosis (Fig. 2F–I, Fig. S2E, F), and caused G0/G1 phase arrest (Fig. 2J, K, Fig. S2G) in AML cells. Subcutaneous injection of U937-shG3BP2 into NCG mice resulted in smaller tumors with reduced volumes and weights compared to those in control mice (6 mice per group) (Fig. 2L–N, Fig. S2H, I). In vivo experiment revealed downregulation of G3BP2 prolongs the survival of CDX mice (5 mice per group) (Fig. 2O). shG3BP2-CDX mice showed lower MCL1 expression in tumor tissues than controls according to IHC analysis (Fig. 2P). These findings indicate that loss of G3BP2 mitigates AML development by decreasing MCL1 expression.

G3BP2 regulates MCL1 expression by stabilizing ELF1 mRNA to confer venetoclax resistance

To investigate how G3BP2 regulates MCL1 expression, we performed RNA sequencing on U937-shG3BP2 cells and control cells. Using a logFC ≥ |0.5| and an FDR < 0.001 as the screening criteria, we identified 2881 upregulated and 2747 downregulated genes (Table S7). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis revealed enrichment in the cell cycle, p53 signaling, apoptosis, and cellular senescence pathways (Fig. 3A). G3BP2 is an RNA-binding protein, leading us to test its capacity to directly interact with MCL1 mRNA. The online Protein-RNA prediction tool, catRAPID (http://service.tartaglialab.com/page/catrapid_group), indicates there is no significant interaction between G3BP2 protein and MCL1 mRNA (Fig. S3A), and the result was further confirmed by RIP–qPCR assay (Fig. S3B). Pearson correlations across the six AML datasets were performed to further identify 20 genes that were positively correlated with both G3BP2 and MCL1 (R ≥ 0.3, FDR < 0.001). These genes were intersected with the 2747 downregulated DEGs identified from RNA-seq data, resulting in ELF1 and GNA13 as overlapping candidates. (Fig. 3B, Table S8). Our data showed that G3BP2 knockdown reduced the expression of ELF1 and GNA13 (Fig. 3C, D). The catRAPID indicated a high likelihood of an interaction between G3BP2 and ELF1 mRNA; by contrast, no interaction was predicted between G3BP2 and GNA13 mRNA (Fig. 3E). RIP–qPCR analysis revealed reduced binding of G3BP2 to ELF1 mRNA when G3BP2 was downregulated (Fig. 3F). GNA13 was excluded in the following study because no binding was detected between G3BP2 and GNA13 mRNA (Fig. 3G). In addition, qPCR analysis of our internal AML cohort showed a positive correlation among G3BP2, MCL1, and ELF1 mRNA expression (Fig. 3H, I). To further illustrate the regulatory role of G3BP2 on ELF1, we performed WB analysis in AML cells. The results demonstrated that downregulation of G3BP2 decreased ELF1 expression (Fig. 3L), and vice versa (Fig. S3C). Previous studies have demonstrated the pivotal role of G3BP2 in tumor progression by modulating the mRNA stability of target genes [24, 34, 35]. To elucidate the effect of G3BP2 in regulating the stability of ELF1 mRNA, we performed an mRNA stability assay. Treatment with actinomycin D (5 µM) reduced ELF1 mRNA stability in G3BP2-knockdown U937, THP-1, and MOLM-13 cells (Fig. 3M, Fig. S3D). To elucidate the critical motif of the G3BP2 protein responsible for ELF1 mRNA stabilization, we generated U937 cells with a deletion of the RNA recognition motif (RRM) in G3BP2. We observed that overexpression of G3BP2 led to robust stabilization of ELF1 mRNA, whereas overexpression of G3BP2-ΔRRM showed no effect on ELF1 mRNA stability (Fig. 3N). These data suggested that G3BP2 binds to and stabilizes ELF1 mRNA, and the RRM domain of G3BP2 is needed for this interaction.

To define the role of ELF1 in AML cells, we knocked down ELF1 and observed a marked inhibition of cell viability together with increased apoptosis (Fig. S3E). These phenotypic changes were accompanied by a significant reduction in MCL1 protein levels (Fig. S3F). To establish that MCL1 acts as a direct downstream effector of ELF1, we ectopically overexpressed ELF1 in G3BP2-knockdown cells and found that MCL1 expression was restored (Fig. 3O, P).

To further confirm that G3BP2 inhibition can restore venetoclax sensitivity even in resistant cells, we used Ven-R U937 cells. Genetic downregulation of G3BP2 significantly decreased the venetoclax half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) and enhanced apoptosis (Fig. 3R, Fig. S3G). Functionally, re-expression of MCL1 in G3BP2- knockdown cells restored cell growth, conferred resistance to venetoclax, and inhibited apoptosis (Fig. 3Q–S). These data demonstrate a G3BP2–ELF1–MCL1 regulatory axis in AML cells. Integrated with clinical and functional results, elevated G3BP2/ELF1 expression drives venetoclax resistance in AML patients. Mechanistically, G3BP2 directly binds to and positively regulates ELF1 mRNA, thereby controlling MCL1 expression.

ELF1 is required for MCL1 transcription in AML cells

How ELF1 modulates MCL1 is further investigated. By using the JASPAR database (https://jaspar2022.genereg.net/) [36], we predicted potential ELF1 binding motifs in the MCL1 promoter region (Fig. 4A, Table S9), which were subsequently validated by dual-luciferase assays. The full-length MCL1 promoter (−2000 bp to +200 bp) was inserted into a luciferase reporter vector. The data showed ELF1 overexpression significantly increased MCL1 promoter activity in 293T cells compared with controls (Fig. 4B). To identify the critical binding region, we generated constructs with truncated promoter regions based on JASPAR-predicted ELF1 sites (Fig. 4C). Truncated constructs lacking the predicted binding sites exhibited reduced ELF1-induced luciferase activity. Notably, the −1000 bp to +100 bp region displayed the highest ELF1-inducible activity (Fig. 4D). The results were further confirmed by CHIP‒qPCR assays. Downregulation of ELF1 reduced ELF1 enrichment, suggesting a specific interaction between ELF1 and the MCL1 promoter (Fig. 4E–J). Moreover, ELF1 enrichment at the MCL1 promoter was attenuated in shG3BP2 cells (Fig. 4E–J). We constructed a truncated MCL1 promoter lacking the −664 to −89 bp region (MCL1-Δ576 bp). Dual-luciferase assays demonstrated that this deletion completely eliminated ELF1-mediated transcriptional activation compared to the full-length promoter (Fig. 4K), establishing MCL1-Δ576 bp as the key region for the ELF1-mediated regulation of MCL1.

G3BP2 knockdown reduced BCL2 protein levels (Fig. 2B); therefore, we extended our analysis to BCL2. JASPAR identified putative ELF1 motifs in the BCL2 promoter (Table S10). Dual-luciferase assays revealed that ELF1 overexpression increased BCL2 promoter activity, although to a lesser degree than MCL1(Fig. 4L). ChIP‒qPCR showed that downregulating ELF1 reduced ELF1’s direct binding to the BCL2 promoter, but with lower enrichment compared to MCL1 (Fig. S4A–C). Correlation analyses across cohorts revealed a weaker association between ELF1 and BCL2 than between ELF1 and MCL1 (Fig. 4M, Fig. S4D). Collectively, these data demonstrate that ELF1 enhances the transcription of MCL1 and BCL2, with a more pronounced regulatory effect on MCL1.

Molecular docking predicted a favorable binding interaction between C108 and the G3BP2 NTF2-like domain with a binding energy of −7.6 kcal/mol (Fig. 5A). The CETSA assay in U937 cells demonstrated marked thermal stabilization of G3BP2 (Fig. 5B). The IC50 values of venetoclax were 19.494 µM in U937 cells, 2.192 µM in THP-1 cells, and 10 nM in MOLM-13 cells (Fig. S5A). U937 and THP-1 cells exhibited clear resistance, unlike sensitive MOLM-13 cells, as previously reported [19, 33]. Discussion

Upregulation of MCL1 mainly causes venetoclax resistance. The clinical application of MCL1 inhibitors is limited due to safety concerns. It is imperative to identify new targets to overcome resistance. In this study, we identify G3BP2 as a target to overcome venetoclax resistance. G3BP2 confers venetoclax resistance by stabilizing ELF1-mediated MCL1 transcription. This study provides a theoretical basis for improving venetoclax resistance in AML.

Our integrated analysis showed G3BP2 elevation correlates with venetoclax resistance and poor prognosis in AML. Studies have shown that G3BP2 is aberrantly overexpressed in various cancers, where it acts as an oncogene by promoting cell proliferation, metastasis, and treatment resistance [35, 37–39]. Downregulated G3BP2 in AML cells inhibits proliferation and promotes apoptosis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target in AML.

We further examined the role of G3BP2 inhibition in venetoclax resistance in AML by treating venetoclax-sensitive and -resistant AML cell lines, as well as primary samples from newly diagnosed and refractory/relapsed patients, with C108, venetoclax, or C108 plus venetoclax. C108 is a selective small-molecule inhibitor of G3BP2. It has been reported that C108 alone or with paclitaxel significantly reduces the population of breast tumor-initiating cells that induce treatment resistance or relapse [24]. Treatment with C108 significantly extended the OS in tumor-bearing mice, with minimal safety concerns [35]. Our research revealed that the combined treatment substantially increased toxicity to AML cells, suggesting a synergistic interaction. C108, alone or in combination with venetoclax, has the potential to improve AML treatment efficacy even in the presence of venetoclax resistance. Several studies have reported the application of G3BP2 inhibitors in some preclinical anti-tumor models [24, 35]. However, no G3BP2 inhibitors are available for clinical cancer treatment. Therefore, further studies are necessary to facilitate translation to clinical practice.

Herein, we identified ELF1 as a key effector that mediates MCL1 by G3BP2. ELF1 is found to be positively correlated with both G3BP2 and MCL1 across 6 datasets, which is further verified by in vivo and in vitro studies. ELF1 is a transcription factor that is overexpressed in myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) patients. It modifies the transcriptional regulatory framework to facilitate MDS progression [40]. A previous photoactivatable ribonucleoside-enhanced cross-linking and immunoprecipitation (PAR-CLIP) analysis revealed that G3BP2 modulates the mRNA stability of more than 1,600 genes, including ELF1, CDK6, HOXC6, HMGCR, and HDGF [41]. Many of them are regulated by G3BP2 and linked to leukemia development [42–44]. G3BP2 interacts with mRNAs to regulate the expression of relevant genes and facilitate tumor progression. Herein, our data revealed no evident binding between the G3BP2 protein and MCL1 mRNA, indicating that G3BP2 may influence MCL1 expression through intermediary molecules. We believe that the role of G3BP2 in AML and other tumors is complex and context-dependent on the specific mRNA it interacts with. The role of the G3BP2-ELF1-MCL1 axis in providing venetoclax resistance is specific, as shown by several of our experiments in both venetoclax-sensitive and -resistant conditions. Therefore, we proposed that the regulatory function of the G3BP2-ELF1-MCL1 axis in AML development is more pronounced in the presence of venetoclax resistance.

The regulatory manner of RNA-binding protein G3BP2 and transcription factor ELF1 on their downstream targets is investigated. We showed that the RRM domain of G3BP2 binds to and stabilizes ELF1 mRNA. ELF1 regulates MCL1 by binding to its promoter. Transcription factors like STAT3, STAT5, c-myc, HIF-1α, NF-κB, SP1, and PU.1 promote MCL1 transcription by binding to specific promoter sites. Most of these sites are between −900 and −80 bp [45]. Although the precise binding site is not identified and has not been fully validated in vivo and in vitro, our results provide proof of concept for developing a small molecule that targets the key binding site to interfere with the G3BP2-ELF1-MCL1 signaling pathways. Further studies are warranted for understanding the regulatory pattern in the G3BP2-ELF1-MCL1 axis.

In addition to the transcriptional regulation of ELF1 on MCL1, we also revealed that ELF1 regulates BCL2 expression through direct binding to its promoter. The G3BP2-ELF1 axis transcriptionally upregulates both MCL1 and BCL2, with a stronger influence on MCL1. Our functional data indicate that the ELF1-MCL1 axis is the primary driver of venetoclax resistance in our models. The overexpression of MCL1 in G3BP2-knockdown cells restored venetoclax resistance. Thus, we proposed that MCL1 is the primary downstream target of the G3BP2-ELF1 axis. The secondary BCL2 regulation indicates that G3BP2 probably enhances antiapoptotic BCL-2 family signaling.

In summary, our study revealed an unrecognized role of the G3BP2-ELF1-MCL1 axis in mediating venetoclax resistance (Fig. 6N). Pharmacological and lentiviral G3BP2 inhibition enhances venetoclax sensitivity in primary AML cells, reversing resistance in vivo and in vitro. C108 plus venetoclax might be a promising option for AML patients resistant to venetoclax. However, future studies should evaluate its long-term safety, optimal dosing, and combination strategies in larger models.
