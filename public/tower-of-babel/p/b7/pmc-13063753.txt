Scars of birth: caesarean delivery and fertility intentions among women of reproductive age in sub-Saharan Africa
Authors: Alex Bawuah, Godness Biney, Michael Sarfo, Sanni Yaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA single multi-country study in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) conducted nearly two decades ago reported that caesarean section (C-section) delivery was associated with reduced subsequent childbearing. To address the gap created by the lack of recent regionally representative evidence that reflects changes in access to surgical delivery, maternal health systems, and fertility-related attitudes, we assessed the association between having ever had a C-section and subsequent fertility outcomes.MethodsThe study used the most recent data from the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) of 30 countries in SSA conducted between 2013 and 2024 (n = 790,139). The study used two outcome variables: (i) the number of children ever born and (ii) the desire/preference for another child. The independent variable is whether the respondent has ever had a C-section birth. The first analysis applied a negative binomial regression to examine the association between C-section births and the number of children born. The second analysis applied multivariable binary logistic regression to analyse the association between C-section births and the desire to have more children.ResultsThe mean number of children for each woman is 3.60 (95% CI:3.56–3.61). Chad had the highest mean number of children born (4.61, 95% CI:4.56–4.66) whereas Lesotho had the lowest mean number of children born (2.09, 95% CI:2.02–2.16). The results show that women who have ever had a C-section birth are 8% less likely to have more children compared to those who have not had a C-section birth (IRR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.91–0.93). Also, higher levels of education are associated with a lower likelihood of having more children, particularly for those with higher education (IRR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.50–0.52) compared to those with no education.ConclusionThese findings highlight the need for focused and practical interventions to offer counselling to mothers during perioperative, pre-operative, and post-operative periods to enhance understanding of C-section and their potential implications on future fertility. Providing women with clear and practical information during prenatal and antenatal visits can help them better understand the medical reasons for C-sections, as well as the potential risks associated with having many children.Keywords: Women, Birth, Reproductive health, Healthcare, Africa

Plain text summary

This study explores how undergoing a caesarean section (C-section) delivery influences women’s future childbearing intentions across 30 countries in Sub-Saharan Africa. Using data from Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) collected between 2010 and 2022, the study analyzes over 847,000 births and focuses on 84,000 women who had C-sections. The findings show that women who gave birth via C-section were significantly less likely to desire more children, even after accounting for factors like age, education, and household wealth. The study also reveals that access to family planning services is associated with stronger fertility-limiting intentions, especially among women who had surgical births. These results underscore the long-term reproductive and social consequences of surgical childbirth in low- and middle-income settings, where access to postnatal care and support may be limited. The research highlights the need for integrating reproductive counseling into maternal health services, especially for women who undergo C-sections.

Background

Global fertility patterns have shifted dramatically over the past several decades, with most regions experiencing sustained declines in total fertility rates due to urbanization, rising education levels, improved access to contraception, and expanded maternal health services [1]. Fertility trends influence population growth, age distribution, and long-term development, making them central to demographic planning and public health policy. While fertility rates have fallen below replacement levels in many high- and middle-income countries, sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) continues to experience persistently high fertility [2, 3]. In 2020, the region recorded an average of 4.6 children per woman, more than twice the global average of 2.3 [4–6]. Fertility in SSA remains shaped by complex socio-cultural, economic, and health system factors, contributing to maternal and child health challenges throughout the region [6, 7]. High fertility rates increase the risk of maternal morbidity and mortality, place strain on an already overburdened healthcare system, and complicate efforts to achieve sustainable economic growth and human capital development [7, 8]. In addition to socio-economic and cultural influences, clinical factors such as mode of delivery (i.e. vagina or caesarean section) may also play a role in shaping reproductive behaviour across the region [9–11].

Cesarean section (C-section) is a major obstetric surgical procedure to deliver a baby through incisions in the abdomen and uterus of the mother. C-section deliveries have become increasingly common worldwide due to a combination of factors including delayed maternal age at first birth, rising prevalence of maternal comorbidities such as hypertension and obesity, improvements in surgical techniques, and growing demand for elective procedures in both high- and low-income settings [12, 13]. Although C-sections are often lifesaving in cases of obstetric complications such as obstructed labor, placenta previa, or fetal distress, their rapid increase, especially in settings with limited resources, has raised concerns regarding their short- and long-term consequences. Among these concerns is the potential impact of C-sections on future fertility outcomes. Research suggests that women who undergo C-section deliveries may face increased risks of complications in subsequent pregnancies, such as uterine rupture, abnormal placentation, or adhesions, which can lead to reduced fecundity (ability to produce an offspring) and influence reproductive decision-making [9, 10] In addition to biological risks, psychosocial factors such as fear of repeat surgery, limited access to safe repeat C-sections, partner influence, and lack of postnatal counselling may affect fertility preferences, especially in lower-resource settings [13, 14].

While these concerns about the reproductive consequences of C-section delivery have gained attention in high-income countries [15–19], evidence from SSA remains limited. Most existing studies in SSA have primarily focused on identifying the prevalence of C- section delivery and its associated sociodemographic or clinical factors [3, 11, 12, 14, 20], both within individual countries [3, 11, 12, 14, 17] and across the region [8, 20], rather than on long-term reproductive outcomes. To our knowledge, only one study has explicitly examined the association between C-section delivery and subsequent fertility in SSA [21]. This study, conducted by Collin et al. in 2006, used Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data from 22 countries and found that women who had delivered by C- section were significantly less likely to have subsequent births or to express a desire for additional children [21]. A related study conducted in Scotland during the same year assessed whether fertility delay following C- section delivery was voluntary or involuntary, but it did not evaluate realized or intended fertility outcomes and was based in a high-income setting, limiting its applicability to SSA [22]. These studies are now nearly two decades old and may no longer reflect recent changes in access to surgical delivery, maternal health systems, or fertility-related attitudes. Furthermore, population-based research in the region rarely captures whether C-sections were medically indicated or elective, which limits the interpretation of the underlying causes of fertility differences.

To address these gaps and extend the literature further by providing a regional assessment of the association between having ever had a C-section and subsequent fertility outcomes, we used the most recent DHS data of 30 SSA countries including countries where these associations have not been explicitly explored. While we were unable to distinguish between different types of C-sections (elective vs. emergency) due to the nature of our data, our study offers updated evidence using a large sample of over 219,000 women. We examine both fertility behaviour, measured by the number of children ever born, and fertility intentions, measured by the desire for additional children while adjusting for a wide range of socio-demographic and reproductive variables. We hypothesize that women who have ever had a C-section will have fewer children and express a lower desire for additional children compared to those who delivered exclusively vaginally, independent of socioeconomic and demographic factors. These findings aim to contribute to a broader understanding of maternal health dynamics and to inform policies and programs that support women’s reproductive health and fertility planning across Sub-Saharan Africa.

Methods

Data source

The study used the most recent data from the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) of 30 countries in SSA conducted between 2013 and 2024. The countries included are Angola, Benin, Burkina Faso, Burundi, Côte d’Ivoire, Cameroon, Ethiopia, Gabon, Ghana, Gambia, Guinea, Kenya, Liberia, Lesotho, Madagascar, Mali, Mauritania, Malawi, Mozambique, Nigeria, Rwanda, Sierra Leone, Senegal, Chad, Togo, Tanzania, Uganda, South Africa, Zambia, and Zimbabwe.

The DHS is a nationwide survey that is conducted in over 85 low- and middle-income countries worldwide and follows a consistent protocol and terminology across all countries [23]. It employs a structured questionnaire to gather information on various indicators of health including maternal and child health, fertility, family planning utilisation, morbidity, and mortality [23]. The DHS uses a two-stage sampling technique to collect data, starting with selecting enumeration areas based on each country’s sampling frame, followed by selecting households from each enumeration area. Detailed information on the sampling and data collection methods can be found in the work of Aliaga and Ren [24].

The study employed the women’s dataset (IR file) from the DHS. The total pooled sample was 468,936 women. After cleaning the data and dropping all missing observations, we ended up with a sample size of 219,503 women (aged between 15 and 49 years) for the first analysis. This sample was limited to women who had ever given birth and had their birth records. The sample was further reduced to 195,032 women for the second analysis. The analysis was limited to women who responded to the question about the desire for more children.

Variables

Outcome variables

The study used two outcome variables: (i) the number of children ever born and (ii) the desire/preference for another child.

In the survey, the respondents were asked about the total number of children ever born by the respondent. This variable is thus used as the first outcome variable. Furthermore, the respondents were asked if they would like to have another child in the future “Now I have some questions about the future. Would you like to have (a/another) child, or would you prefer not to have any (more) children?”. The responses were “have another child, no more, can’t get pregnant and undecided/don’t know”. For this study, we only included those who responded, “have another” or “no more” and used it as the second outcome variable.

Independent variable

The independent variable is whether the respondent has ever had a C-section birth. This variable was constructed by going through the birth records of the respondent to find out if any of their birth was C-section birth. We coded this variable “Yes = 1” if the respondent recorded at least one C-section birth in their birth records (implying they have had a C-section birth before) and “No = 0” if they had no record of C-section birth in their birth records (implying they have never had a C-section birth).

Covariates

The study included the following covariates; age (15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49), education (no education, primary, secondary, higher), marital status (never in a union, married, living with a partner, widowed, divorced, separated), wealth (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, richest), residence (urban, rural) currently using contraceptives (no, yes), currently in employment (no, yes), and ever terminated a pregnancy (no, yes). These variables were selected based on their significant relationship with the outcome variables in the literature [25] as well as their availability in the DHS dataset.

Data analysis

The data was analysed using STATA version 18. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages and means) were used to describe the data/study sample.

We first examined the association between C-section births and the number of children born. The dependent (number of children born) variable is a count outcome, counts regression models (Poisson or Negative binomial) were considered [26]. The Poisson is preferable when the mean and variance of the outcome variable are equal (equidispersion). Otherwise, the negative binomial performs better if overdispersion is present [26]. The mean (3.60) and variance (5.68) were unequal, so we applied the negative binomial regression. Moreover, we use the overdisp command in Stata to test for overdispersion. The results (provided in Appendix Table 4) strongly suggest overdispersion in the dependent variable, further justifying our choice of the negative binomial over the Poisson regression.

The second analysis examined the influence of C-sections on the desire to have more children. The outcome variable is binary, hence we applied a binary logistic regression model [27] to achieve our objective. We accounted for the survey design (sample weight, strata and primary sampling units using the svyset command in Stata) in all regression analyses based on the DHS guidelines [28].

Ethical considerations

We used a secondary dataset that is freely available to the public from the DHS Programme, therefore no ethical approval was requested. The dataset is anonymised and more details regarding its ethical standards are available at http://goo.gl/ny8T6X.

The results show that women who have ever had a C-section birth are 8% less likely to have more children compared to those who have not had a C-section birth (IRR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.91–0.93). Also, higher levels of education are associated with a lower likelihood of having more children, particularly for those with higher education (IRR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.50–0.52) compared to those with no education. Likewise, compared to the poorest women, women in all wealth quintiles are less likely to have more children especially those within the richest wealth quintile (IRR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.92–0.95). Furthermore, rural residents are 2% more likely to have more children compared to urban residents (IRR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.03). Those using contraceptives are more likely to have more children compared to those who are not (IRR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.04). Moreover, women who are currently working are more likely to have more children compared to those who are not working (IRR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.15–1.17). Additionally, women who have ever terminated a pregnancy are more likely to have more children compared to those who have not (IRR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.11–1.13).

The association between C-section births and the desire to have more children among women (15–49 years) in SSA

Model 2 in Table 3 presents the multivariable binary logistic regression results showing the association between C-section births and the desire to have more children among women (15–49 years) in SSA. We report the adjusted odds ratios (AOR), the 95% confidence intervals (CI) and the p-values for the 0.1%, 1%, and 5% levels of statistical significance. The unadjusted odds ratios are provided in Appendix Table 5.

The results indicate that women who have ever had a C-section birth are 15% less likely to desire another child compared to those who have not had a C-section birth (AOR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.80–0.91). Furthermore, compared to women aged 15–19, the odds of desiring another child decrease significantly with increasing age, especially for those between 45 and 49 years (AOR = 0.10, 95% CI: 0.08–0.11). Higher levels of education are associated with lower odds of desiring another child compared to those with no education, particularly for those with higher education (AOR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.81–0.97). Married women are more likely to desire another child (AOR = 1.89, 95% CI: 1.75–2.03) than those who have never been in a union. Also, wealthier women are significantly less likely to desire another child, with the richest women being 28% less likely (AOR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.67–0.77) in comparison to the poorest women. Women who are using contraceptives are less likely to desire another child compared to those who are not using contraceptives (AOR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.80–0.86). Moreover, women living in rural areas are more likely to desire another child compared to those in urban areas (AOR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.19–1.32). Women who have ever terminated a pregnancy are more likely to desire another child compared to those who have not (AOR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.06–1.15). Furthermore, as the number of children increases, women are 35% less likely to desire another child (AOR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.64–0.66).

Discussion

This study investigated the association between caesarean section (C-section) and subsequent fertility outcomes among women aged 15–49 years in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) countries, using the Demographic health survey (DHS) data. The findings of this study highlight a significant negative association between C- section and subsequent parity, with women who had their first child via C- section demonstrating reduced odds of higher fertility as opposed to those who delivered exclusively vaginally. Findings from this study are in alignment with prior studies from both developed and developing countries. For example, studies conducted by Kjerulff et al. [9] and Sima et al. [19] in the United States and Norway reported a similar pattern, where women with a history of C-sections exhibited a reduced likelihood of future conceptions. To further support our findings, a systematic literature review by Gurol-Urganci et al. [10] reported that women who underwent caesarean sections had a 9% reduction in the likelihood of desiring a subsequent child, comparable to the 15% reduction identified in our analysis.

In Sub-Saharan Africa, fewer studies have explored this association comprehensively; however, emerging findings support this observed association. Particularly in Nigeria [12] and Ethiopia [11], it is evident that women who have had caesarean section exhibited reduced odds of subsequent childbearing. This negative association could be attributed to a wide range of factors, including uterine scarring, traumatic birth experiences, fear of repeated surgery, abnormal uterine implantation, lack of support from partner, and health workers advice to limit future births (subjective norms), which may influence fertility decisions and reduce the odds of future conceptions [13, 14, 29–31]. In the context of our study, these factors are likely heightened by the existing inequalities in healthcare access, lack of high-quality follow-up treatment, inadequate post-surgical counselling and structural obstacles in maternal health service provision. However, healthcare practitioners and public health officials must make concerted efforts to demystify rumours surrounding caesarean sections and to provide adequate post-operative counselling and support to mothers and their families.

Interestingly, beyond the primary relationship between C-section and fertility preferences, our study findings reveal significant associations among sociodemographic and reproductive covariates. For instance, it was noted that younger women within the range of 15–29 demonstrated a higher likelihood of desiring more children as compared to those who were within the 40–49 age range. The finding implies that adolescents seem to be more energetic and possess the strength to overcome the accompanying physiological and psychological challenges presented during pregnancy as opposed to those in the growing category of women. This trend could also be attributed to adolescents having access to the right information on pregnancy, the ability to work out and maintain a healthy body and societal norms. This observation is consistent with prior studies from SSA and elsewhere that have identified age as a major predictor of fertility preference [6, 32, 33]. Additionally, our study found relatively lower odds of desiring more children among women with higher levels of education as compared to their counterparts with no education.

Consistent with the literature [7, 34–36], education has been identified as a significant predictor of fertility behaviour, often associated with delayed marriage, better understanding of family planning, reproductive autonomy and career progressions. Conversely, women with no formal education and particularly, those residing in rural areas may value larger family sizes due to traditional socio-cultural and economic dependence on children.

Moreover, we also noted an association between economic status and fertility preference, with wealthier women significantly demonstrating lower odds of desiring more children [3, 6]. The plausible explanation to this could be due to lifestyle preferences, professional demands, access to family planning and prioritisation of child quality over quantity [5, 37].

Similarly, married women were significantly more likely to express a desire for additional children compared to those who had never been in a union. This pattern is consistent with prior studies [38–40] and may be explained by prevailing socio-cultural expectations that position childbearing as a central component of marital roles, particularly within patriarchal societies. In many contexts like SSA, women may feel a societal or familial obligation to bear children as a means of fulfilling their roles as wives and contributing to lineage continuity [8, 41]. Additionally, married women are more likely to benefit from consistent financial, emotional, and logistical support from their partners throughout the reproductive journey, including during prenatal, antenatal, and postnatal periods which may positively influence their willingness to have more children.

Moreover, our study reveals an intriguing finding where women who had ever terminated a pregnancy were more likely to desire additional children, with a 10% increase in odds compared to those who had not. One probable explanation is that such women may desire to replace lost pregnancies that occurred either due to medical complications, unplanned pregnancies, or other external factors. While there exists a paucity of literature on the association between pregnancy termination and future fertility desires, a related study by Studnicki et al. [42] found that women whose first pregnancy ended in abortion had a higher likelihood of subsequent abortions and fewer live births compared to those whose first pregnancy resulted in a live birth. Even though this study primarily focuses on outcomes rather than intentions, it reveals a distinct reproductive pattern among women with a history of pregnancy termination, which may indirectly serve as a motivation or driver of reproductive goals over time.

Strengths and limitations

The study provides empirical evidence from a nationally representative dataset of 30 SSA countries using robust analytical techniques, specifically negative binomial and binary logistic regression models, appropriately accounting for data structure and overdispersion in the count outcomes. We also controlled for a wide range of sociodemographic and reproductive covariates known to influence fertility behaviours, providing a robust and nuanced understanding of the association between C-section births and fertility outcomes. Despite these strengths, several limitations emerged from this study. These include the use of cross-sectional data, which precludes the establishment of causal relationships between C-section births and subsequent fertility preferences or outcomes. Additionally, due to convergence issues, age was excluded from the negative binomial model assessing the number of children ever born, which may have limited the explanatory power of the model. Moreover, the study relied on self-reported reproductive histories, which may lead to recall bias or social desirability bias, particularly regarding sensitive experiences such as pregnancy termination.

Conclusion

The study provides significant empirical evidence on the relationship between caesarean section birth and subsequent fertility among women aged 15–49 years in Sub-Saharan Africa. Several factors other than the negative association between C-section and subsequent fertility contributed to desire for more children. These include a mother’s level of education, marital status, current using contraceptive, economic status, age, pregnancy termination, number of children and place of residence. These findings highlight the need for focused and practical interventions to offer counselling to mothers during perioperative, pre-operative, and post-operative periods to enhance understanding of C-section and their potential implications on future fertility.

Governments and healthcare professionals across sub-Saharan African countries should adopt more holistic and inclusive approaches to maternal health education. Providing women with clear and practical information during prenatal and antenatal visits can help them better understand the medical reasons for C-sections, as well as the potential risks associated with having many children. Through such efforts, health agencies can ensure that mothers and prospective mothers are well-informed about the implications of C-section deliveries and how these may affect their future fertility decisions.
