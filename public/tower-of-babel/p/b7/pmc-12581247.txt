The effect of different micro-implant-assisted rapid palatal expanders designed digitally: a three-dimensional finite element analysis
Authors: Yixin Liu, Yusong Zhang, Peiying Lin, Jie Pan, Mengqi Xu, Xiaozhe Li, Dongxu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to use finite element analysis to compare the effects of different micro-implant-assisted rapid palatal expanders with varying support methods, different numbers and positions of micro-implants designed digitally, to provide a reference for the improved design and clinical application of expanders.MethodsThe models of the craniomaxillary complex, micro-implants, and maxillary skeletal expander were constructed by modeling softwares. The positions of micro-implants were digitally designed via the registration of CBCT and intraoral scans, and the models of the new expanders were built accordingly. The expanders were classified into tooth-bone-borne (Type-1) and bone-borne (Type-2) based on their support methods; into 4-micro-implant (Type-A), linear-6-micro-implant (Type-B), and C-6-micro-implant (Type-C) based on the number and position of micro-implants. Assemble to form six groups of models: A1, A2, B1, B2, C1, and C2. The expanders were activated in two stages: 0.4 mm along the X-axis before the separation of the two halves of the maxilla (Stage 1) and 4 mm after they were separated (Stage 2). The differences in the stresses and displacements were evaluated.ResultsType-C had the widest VMS distribution range of the craniomaxillary complex. In the same implantation position, the VMS of micro-implants and the cortical bone around them showed Type-B < Type-C < Type-A, Type-1 < Type-2. The tipping of the first molar showed Type-1 > Type-2 in Stage 1, while they were equal in Stage 2. In Stage 2, Type-A achieved parallel expansion (the parallelism of expansion in A1 and A2 were 98.3% and 96.5%, respectively), while Type-B and Type-C exhibit a front-wide, back-narrow "V"-shaped expansion. The efficiency of expansion demonstrated Type-B surpassing Type-C and Type-A, and Type-1 equaling Type-2.ConclusionsThe expansion effect of the 6-micro-implant-type was superior to those of the 4-micro-implant-type. Among the 6-micro-implant-type, the C-type had a wider stress distribution range, the linear-type had higher expansion efficiency and better stability of the micro-implants. In clinical practice, it is necessary to design the expanders individually via a digital approach to enhance the efficacy and safety of the expansion and achieve the treatment goals more precisely.

Background

Maxillary transverse deficiency (MTD) refers to the insufficient development of the maxilla compared to the mandible, resulting in an imbalance in the transverse relationship between the upper and lower jaws. It is a common form of malocclusion observed in clinical practice. Clinically, MTD typically presents as a narrow maxillary basal bone and dental arch, dental crowding, unilateral or bilateral edge-to-edge or crossbite of the posterior teeth, and a high-arched palate, among other symptoms [1–3]. MTD can affect the health of periodontal tissues, pronation, chewing, and other functions of the stomatognathic system and may even impact facial aesthetics.

Since MTD has a relatively low rate of spontaneous correction [4], timely and effective correction of MTD is highly clinically and socially valuable. Currently, maxillary expansion, which mainly includes tooth-borne rapid palatal expansion (RPE), slow palatal expansion (SPE), micro-implant-assisted rapid palatal expansion (MARPE), and surgically assisted rapid palatal expansion (SARPE), is the commonly used clinical treatment procedure employed to correct MTD. Recently, a new bone-borne expander known as the ATOZ expander has been introduced, which has an anteroposterior jackscrew design with narrow width, making it particularly suitable for patients with narrow palates [5]. With patient growth and the consequent progressive calcification and interdigitation of craniofacial sutures [6], the success rate of opening the mid-palatal suture (MPS) via the RPE is low, and skeletal expansion becomes more challenging, leading to an increase in tooth tipping due to heightened mechanical resistance [7, 8], and the MPS often expands in a “V” shape. SARPE is invasive, traumatic, expensive, necessitates superior bone quality, and poses risks of sequelae including epistaxis, fractures, infections, neurovascular injuries, and postoperative discomfort [9, 10]. Moreover, SARPE leads to lateral rotation of the maxillary half with minimal horizontal translation [11], and Jesus et al. [12] demonstrated that the buccal inclination of the molar was significant, whereas the skeletal effect was inferior to that of MARPE, and relapse was significant during the retention period. In contrast, MARPE provides a better skeletal effect, minimizes tooth tilting movements, and extends the age range for the nonsurgical treatment of maxillary expansion [13–15]. Lee et al [16] reported the clinical outcome of a 20-year-old patient with MTD with mandibular protrusion treated with MARPE in 2010. The study showed an increase in maxillary molar width of 8.3 mm and an increase in maxillary base width of 2.4 mm, which suggests that MARPE is an effective means of improving MTD in a non-surgical setting. Statistical data indicate that the treatment success rate of MARPE in young adults ranges from 81.8% to 86.9% [17, 18], significantly broadening the age range for nonsurgical maxillary expansion.

Currently, the most commonly used MARPE in clinical practice is the MSE designed by Professor Won Moon. As a tooth-bone-borne device, the MSE contains a central spiral expander close to the center of resistance of the expansion, attaches to the molars with support arms and molar bands, and includes four micro-implants positioned at the posterior palate. Some of the mechanical stresses generated by the tooth-bone-borne expander will inevitably be transmitted to the fixed teeth, and the greater the bone resistance, the greater the stress on the teeth, making it more likely to cause buccal tilting of the teeth, alveolar damage, etc. The bone-borne expander transmits the least mechanical stress to the teeth and has the least dental effect. However, the surface stress around the micro-implants of bone-borne expanders can become concentrated. When the expansion resistance increases, the micro-implants are prone to overloading and loosening [19]. Currently, there is no consensus in clinical practice on whether the tooth-bone-borne or bone-borne method is more effective.

At the same time, as the sutures gradually ossify and fuse, the resistance to expansion in major resistance areas such as the MPS increases. The commonly used MARPE device, which involves four micro-implants, has risks of failure. In Choi and Park's studies [20, 21], among the patients treated by MARPE, a percentage (13.04% and 15.8%, respectively) exhibited failure of the MPS opening. Therefore, this study designs novel expanders with 6-micro-implants to explore whether they can further enhance skeletal anchorage compared to 4-micro-implants, making the expansion stress sufficient to overcome the resistance and maintain the stability of the micro-implants. The anterior palate region is characterized by high bone quality, thin soft tissue coverage, and the absence of tooth root interference. The palatal alveolar region between the maxillary second premolar and first molar exhibits a substantial root gap, adequate cortical bone thickness, and optimal soft tissue thickness [6]. Both sites are suitable for placing the additional two micro-implants, although they have different expansion effects. However, no studies have yet compared the biomechanical differences of 6-micro-implant-type expanders with varying designs of micro-implant positions. Therefore, this study designed the linear-6-micro-implant type (adding two micro-implants in the anterior palate region) and the C-6-micro-implant type (adding two micro-implants in the palatal alveolar region between the maxillary second premolar and first molar).

With the ongoing development of digital technology and in-depth research into the treatment needs of patients with personalized malocclusion, digital design based on the registration of CBCT and intraoral scan has gradually become widely used in the design and positioning of expanders and micro-implants in MARPE. Compared with the conventional method, this approach encompasses anatomical and biomechanical considerations, which can comprehensively observe the implantation position of micro-implants in three-dimensional directions. Therefore, it can effectively avoid damage to key anatomical structures such as nerves, arteries, teeth roots, and maxillary sinuses and minimize mucosal pressure ulcers. Consequently, it enhances positioning accuracy and implantation safety, thereby facilitating the more precise achievement of treatment objectives [22, 23].

Based on the finite element method (FEM), finite element analysis (FEA) uses a series of computational procedures to find solutions, aiming to predict the stress, strain, and displacement behavior of the object under external forces, displacements, and other factors. FEA is widely used to address various complex biomechanical problems [24]. In dentistry, FEA can effectively model solid structures with accurate geometric and mechanical approximations. It has been shown to simulate real clinical situations, facilitate complex biomechanical experiments, and eliminate ineffective clinical trials involving humans. It can reduce possible risks and side effects [25]. Thus, since the 1970 s [26], the FEA has been widely used in dentistry and has become a common visualization method for evaluating the biomechanical properties of oral tissues and therapeutic treatments in vitro [27].

Therefore, the objective of this study was to utilize finite element analysis to comparatively assess the expansion effects of MARPE devices, which were designed digitally with different support methods and different numbers and positions of micro-implants, to provide theoretical support for the design of expanders for the treatment of patients with MTD.

Materials and methods

A 26-year-old male patient was selected from the Department of Orthodontics of the Stomatological Hospital of Shandong University. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Stomatological Hospital, Shandong University (Protocol NO.20240918), and the patient provided informed consent for participation.

Establishment of the craniomaxillary complex model

The patient underwent cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) and an intraoral scan. The CBCT images (NewTom 5G, Verona, Italy) were reconstructed in MIMICS 21.0 (Materialize, Leuven, Belgium) software, and the craniomaxillary complex was generated into a solid model in Geomagic Wrap 2021 (Geomagic, North Carolina, USA). Finally, in Solidworks 2023 software (Dassault Systemes, Massachusetts, USA), the suture surfaces were created and cut by Boolean operation with 1 mm to create frontonasal suture (FNS) and bilateral frontomaxillary sutures(FMS), nasomaxillary sutures(NMS), frontozygomatic sutures(FZS), zygomaticomaxillary sutures(ZMS), zygomaticotemporal sutures(ZTS), pterygopalatine sutures(PPS) [14, 28]. In the sagittal plane, cut in the middle of the maxilla to generate a 0.5 mm MPS [29, 30]. The root surfaces of teeth were uniformly enlarged 0.18 mm outward to form the periodontal ligament models, and bone blocks were offset 1.5 mm inward to form the cancellous bone models. The structures (including the skull, jaw, teeth, periodontal ligaments, MPS, and surrounding sutures) were assembled to finalize the craniomaxillary complex model.

Digital design and establishment of the expander models

Three-dimensional models of the MSE II (Biomaterial Korea, Seul, South Korea) spiral expander and micro-implants (diameter of 1.8 mm × length of 11 mm, diameter of 1.8 mm × length of 13 mm) were created based on the design specifications provided by the manufacturer.

The CBCT data of the craniomaxillary complex and the intraoral scan data of the maxilla were registered in MIMICS 21.0 software (Fig. 1). C-type 6-micro-implant group (Fig. 2d)

The angle of the micro-implant is perpendicular to the palatal bone at the implantation site, penetrating the double-layered cortical bone to achieve bicortical anchorage. The length requirement is for the tip to exceed the lateral cortical bone of the nasal cavity by 1 mm and for the bottom of the expander and the neck of the micro-implant to be 1–2 mm away from the mucosa. After measurement, micro-implants with a length of 11 mm were implanted on both sides of the MPS, and micro-implants with a length of 13 mm were implanted in the lateral region of the C-6-micro-implant group.

The assembly of the finite element models

The designed intraoral scans, MSE, and micro-implant models are combined and imported in the STL format into SolidWorks. Based on the position of the micro-implants and the morphology of the palate, new expander models of linear and C-6-micro-implant types were designed. The bands with a thickness of 0.22 mm were created according to the morphology of the bilateral maxillary first molars, and the 1-mm-diameter support arms were created between the band and the expander. In SolidWorks, three types of expanders, micro-implants, bands, and support arms are transferred to the craniomaxillary complex model according to the aforementioned design positions and assembled to form A1, A2, B1, B2, C1, and C2 models (Fig. 3). The models were classified into tooth-bone-borne (Type-1) and bone-borne (Type-2) based on their support methods; into 4-micro-implant type (Type-A), linear-6-micro-implant type (Type-B), and C-6-micro-implant type (Type-C) based on the number and position of micro-implants.Fig. 3Finite-element models: A1: traditional 4-micro-implant group with bands and support arms connected to the maxillary first molars (tooth-bone-borne); A2: traditional 4-micro-implant group without bands and support arms connected to the maxillary first molars(bone-borne); B1: tooth-bone-borne linear-6-micro-implant group; B2: bone-borne linear-6-micro-implant group; C1: tooth-bone-borne C-6-micro-implant group; C2: bone-borne C-6-micro-implant group

Before the MPS opens, a forced displacement of 0.2 mm is applied along the X-axis on both sides of the expander (Fig. 4b) to simulate the clinical effect with a daily opening of 0.4 mm/day (3 times a day × 1/6 circle/time, 1 circle = 0.8 mm) [34]. Under the condition of opening the MPS, a forced displacement of 0.2 mm is applied along the X-axis on both sides of the expander for a total of 10 steps, resulting in a total expansion of 4 mm. Under both loading conditions, the expansion is not fixed in the sagittal or coronal planes to prevent interference with the generated motion. The area around the foramen magnum is set to zero rotation and zero displacement as fixed boundary conditions [5, 14, 35] (Fig. 4c).

von Mises stress(VMS) distribution in the craniomaxillary complex

In Stage 1, the results showed that the distribution pattern of VMS in the craniomaxillary complex was roughly similar in the six groups of models (Fig. 5). From the anterior and lateral views, it was seen that the VMS were mainly concentrated in the zygomatic process of the maxilla, the pterygoid process, and the alveolar region, especially the posterior alveolar region. From the occlusal view, it was evident that the VMS was highly concentrated at the palatal cortical bone in a small area around the implantation site. The stress in the cortical bone surrounding the anterior micro-implants exhibited a wider distribution than that of the posterior micro-implants. Compared with Type-A, significant stresses were also observed in the lower edge of the pyriform foramen, nasal frontal, and anterior alveolar region in Type-B and Type-C, with a wider range of stress distributions, in which the Type-C had the broadest range of stress distributions. The expansion efficiency in Stage 2

In Stage 2, the expander is opened toward the buccal side along the X-axis by 0.2 mm on each side, for a total of 10 steps, with 2 mm opened on each side. The maximum displacement at each step is shown in Fig. 10a. The ratio of the maximum X-axis displacement of the MPS to that of the expander (2 mm) was defined as the expansion efficiency (Fig. 10b). Discussion

FEM is a commonly used mathematical method in biomechanical research. The establishment of the MPS model can directly affect the results of the biomechanical solution of the craniomaxillary complex. Therefore, when establishing the finite element model, it is necessary to simulate its shape and set appropriate material properties accurately. The actual shape of the MPS is irregular and intertwined, making it difficult to determine its exact width. In this study, based on previous research experience [37], the MPS is simplified and simulated as a thin structure with a width of 0.5 mm. The degree of ossification of the MPS can significantly affect the expansion effect [38]. The MPS maturation was defined in five stages by Angelieri [39]: Stage A: a straight, high-density sutural line with little to no inter-digitation; Stage B: a high-density sutural line that appears scalloped; Stage C: two parallel, scalloped, high-density lines that are close to one another and are occasionally separated by small low-density spaces; Stage D: palatine bone fusion that has been completed while the maxillary portion remains unfused; stage E: anterior maxilla fusion. A MPS of stage D, which is partial ossification, was selected for this study. The mechanical properties of the MPS vary significantly among individuals and at different stages of maturation. According to Provatidis et al [38], a small Young's modulus (1 MPa) was used for every unossified suture, while a large Young's modulus (1370 MPa) was used for every ossified suture. A working hypothesis was proposed for partly ossified sutures, assuming a Young's modulus equal to 500 MPa. In all cases, the Poisson’s ratio was 0.3. Thus, the first stage of this study is before the separation of the two halves of the maxilla, where the material property of the partially ossified MPS of stage D (500 MPa, 0.3) was set; the second stage is after the two halves of the maxilla were separated, where the material property of the non-ossified MPS (1 MPa, 0.3) was set.

In the registered images of the intraoral scan and 3D reconstructed image of the CBCT, the spiral expander section of MSE II and micro-implants were placed virtually, and the most suitable position, angle, and length of the micro-implants were designed. The expander's design and the micro-implant’s placement prioritize safety and comfort. This ensures that the micro-implants steer clear of critical anatomical structures and the expander doesn’t compress the palate mucosa. In clinical practice, it is crucial to plan lateral micro-implants carefully. When the interradicular space is small, or the maxillary sinus is positioned too low, making the lateral bone too thin, this position may be contraindicated. Additionally, it optimizes the cortical bone volume in the implant region, allowing the micro-implants to effectively penetrate the double cortical bone of the palatal dome and nasal base to avoid the loss of bone support stability and the bending of micro-implants.

The maxilla connects to the craniofacial skeleton through the sutures, and the primary resistance of maxillary expansion is not limited to the MPS. Thus, the VMS cloud diagram of the craniofacial complex and the maximum VMS at the landmarks were selected in this study. The results indicated that the stress distribution is primarily concentrated on the maxilla's palatine process and the palatine bone's horizontal section after loading. In particular, the VMS is most concentrated in the small area of the cortical bone around the micro-implants. The zygomatic process of the maxilla, pterygoid process, and alveolar region also showed significant stress, indicating that the palatal expansion caused by the MARPE device affects not only the maxilla but also the entire maxillofacial region. The results of the VMS of each suture and bone landmark show that the areas with the most significant resistance to expansion are the MPS and PPS. Compared with the 4-micro-implant groups, the 6-micro-implant groups had a more extensive stress distribution, with considerable stress also distributed in the nasal-frontal region, the inferior edge of the sphenoid sinus, and the zygomatic bone. This suggests that increasing the number of micro-implants can facilitate the transmission of expansion force. The C-type has a broader range of stress distribution and a higher peak value. This may be because, compared to the linear-type, two additional micro-implants on the lateral palatal slope of the C-type help disperse the force concentrated on the MPS to the base bone, facilitating better transmission and diffusion of expansion stress to the craniomaxillary complex. This is consistent with the finite element study results of Lee et al. [29] Compared with different support methods, the VMS of the tooth-bone-borne group is greater, which is consistent with the research results of Seong et al. [19] The stress of the bone-borne is highly concentrated around the micro-implants, generating a small bending moment at the force application point, while the tooth-bone-borne can more effectively transmit the expansion force to the broader resistance areas.

Since MARPE primarily relies on micro-implants to apply force directly to the basal bone for skeletal anchorage, the stability of the bone-micro-implant connection largely depends on mechanical retention [40]. Thus, the initial stabilization of the micro-implant is crucial for the success of MARPE treatment and essential for the micro-implant to fulfill its supportive function. Excessive stress on the cortical bone surrounding the micro-implant can easily disrupt the mechanical locking between the micro-implant and the bone tissue, potentially leading to a loss of initial stability and even loosening or detachment of the micro-implant [41, 42]. Also, the higher the VMS peak of the micro-implants, the greater the risk that the micro-implant will bend, deform, or fracture [43]. The results revealed that the stress distribution of the cortical bone around the micro-implants at various locations gradually decreased from anterior to posterior, with the VMS distribution in B2 being the widest. This occurs because bone stress is usually concentrated in the cortical bone area, and the thickness decreases from anterior to posterior, aligning with the findings of Hartono et al. [7]. The VMS of the MMI, PMI, and the surrounding cortical bone followed the order B < C < A. This indicated that increasing the number of micro-implants can distribute the expansion resistance, thereby reducing the risk of deformation or even fracture of the micro-implants. Furthermore, increasing the number of micro-implants could distribute resistance to expansion and enhance the initial stability of the micro-implants. The VMS of the PMI is greater than that of the MMI in M3 and M4, possibly because the anterior micro-implants share resistance to palatal expansion, resulting in a more widespread and uniform stress distribution in the micro-implants.

During expansion, the craniomaxillary complex experiences tensile and compressive stresses, in which tensile stress promotes osteogenesis, while compressive stress promotes osteoblastogenesis. The third principal stress is a valid indicator of the response to compressive stress [44]. Excessive third principal stress can lead to pathological overload and remodeling of bone after stress, resulting in fracture or bone resorption. When bone thickness and density are normal, the impact may not be significant; however, in cases of osteoporosis or reduced bone thickness, excessive compressive stress is more likely to result in fractures or bone resorption, along with overloading of the micro-implants, leading to loosening and dislodgement. Motoyoshi et al. [45] reported that when the cortical bone thickness was < 1 mm, the maximal stresses in the bone significantly increased, decreasing the success rates of micro-implants. Increasing micro-implants lowers the third principal stress in the cortical bone around posterior micro-implants, suggesting a strategy for patients with thin posterior palatal bone to decrease fracture risk and enhance micro-implant success. This aligns with Woo Moon et al.'s [46] findings.

When subjected to expansion resistance, the micro-implant is not entirely static or stable but may move laterally or axially, making it difficult to achieve true absolute bony support [47]. An axial displacement of 0.1–0.8 mm of the micro-implant was found after loading with orthodontic force [48], which may be a risk factor for failure of micro-implant support, especially with greater orthopedic force. The micro-implants on both sides of the MPS tend to displace along the long axis of the micro-implants, and the tendency for dislocation is more pronounced in the posterior areas with thinner bone thickness, probably because the micro-implants had a small area of contact between the neck and the bone, which made them more susceptible to outward dislocation. This aligns with the studies by Migliorati et al. [49], which have consistently demonstrated that a greater thickness of bone at the implantation site leads to improved stability and reduced displacement and tilt of the micro-implants. Ichinohe et al. [50] concluded that a minimum bone thickness of 4.5 mm and a cortical bone thickness of 1.5 mm are necessary for micro-implants to attain good initial stability. The micro-implants on the lateral sides of the C1 and C2 groups tend to embed along the long axis, so more attention should be given to the avoidance of tooth roots in design to prevent extrusion of the roots of the first premolar and first molar.

In the two stages, the tipping of the second premolars and second molars was basically the same when comparing the two support methods, which may be because MARPE not only causes the maxilla to expand laterally but also causes the bilateral maxilla to rotate as a whole [51]. In Stage 1, the tipping of the first molars in the tooth-bone-borne group was more significant than in the bone-borne group, indicating that before the MPS opened, some of the mechanical force was transmitted to the connected first molars, resulting in unavoidable dental effects. However, in Stage 2, it remained essentially the same, suggesting that after the MPS opened, the first molars were not functioning as an anchorage for expansion. The expansion is only supported by the micro-implants to achieve complete bony resistance. The bands and support arms on the first molars are only used to stabilize the three-dimensional position of the expander in space to prevent unnecessary stress on the micro-implants when the maxilla on one side is twisted, thus preventing the micro-implants from loosening or breaking. However, the results of Stage 1 show that if the resistance to expansion is too high or the stability of the micro-implant is not sufficient to support the expansion, causing the micro-implant to fail, the expander may become truly hybrid tooth-bone-borne, causing the first molar to bear more significant stress, resulting in buccal tipping and even bending or resorption of the alveolar bone.

The transverse displacement of the palatal suture is frequently considered an effective indicator of the expansion effect. The expansion efficiency ranked as B > C > A, while type-1 is equivalent to type-2. The results indicated that increasing the number of micro-implants significantly improved expansion efficiency, and the closer the micro-implants were to the MPS, the greater the expansion. The ratio of the lateral displacement between PNS and ANS is defined as the parallelism of palatal expansion, which was 98.3%, 96.5% in A1 and A2, respectively. The closer the applied force vector is to the resistance center of the maxilla, the more it can maximize the midface orthopedic effect. It indicated that in the sagittal plane, type A essentially achieved parallel expansion, proving that this expander was placed essentially at the center of the resistance to expansion and can effectively counteract the main expansion resistance regions of the mid-palatal suture, the pterygopalatine suture, and the zygomatic bones, to achieve an almost parallel expansion, in contrast to Cantarella et al. [52] Type B and type C exhibit a front-wide, back-narrow "V"-shaped expansion, with parallelism showing C > B, since two additional micro-implants were placed farther away from the center of resistance. This emphasizes the trade-off between decreased parallelism and increased expansion efficiency, necessitating careful clinical planning to strike a balance in the clinic.

In summary, this study offers ideas for designing an improved expansion device for treating patients with MTD. In the clinic, an individualized expander design with six micro-implants may be considered for patients exhibiting high resistance to expansion, such as those with significant suture fusion, strong perioral muscle strength, or thin bone in the posterior palate. For patients with considerable suture fusion, requiring substantial expansion in the posterior region, possessing a narrow and high palate, and having normal inter-root distances and maxillary sinus floors, adding micro-implants in the lateral palatal region could be beneficial. For patients with thin bone in the posterior palate, needing significant expansion in the anterior region, having small inter-root distances or low maxillary sinus floors, adding micro-implants in the anterior palatal region may be advisable. When selecting the support method, the tooth-bone-borne type is typically more effective in transmitting the spreading force to the craniomaxillary complex; however, when periodontal conditions are poor or retaining teeth are tilted buccally as compensation, the bone-borne type might be considered to lessen the force on the teeth before the MPS opening. When using the tooth-bone-borne type, greater caution should be exercised in monitoring the response of bones and teeth to identify issues and adjust the spreading frequency promptly. If spreading resistance becomes excessively high, preventing the MPS from opening, surgical aids may be warranted to protect the teeth from excessive stress.

However, this study, being an in vitro analysis based on a mathematical algorithm, has several limitations. First, the study assumed all structures to be isotropic homogeneous materials, yet the anatomical and physiological characteristics of the skull are incredibly complex. There are also significant differences in biological traits among individuals, as the materials of tissues are primarily anisotropic and heterogeneous. Therefore, the actual situation is much more complicated than finite element simulations suggest. Second, the research only focused on bony structures. It did not account for the impact of soft tissues, like buccolingual muscles and palatal mucosa, on the stability of the micro-implants and the expansion effect. Future studies should explore how soft tissues adapt after the force is applied and how they influence therapeutic outcomes. Additionally, FMA usually assumes static or simplified loading and has limits in simulating dynamic loading effects. This means it does not adequately capture real and complex biomechanical behaviors. In this study, two stages of expansion were simulated. However, in actual clinical practice, the cyclic nature of loading, the cumulation and consumption of stress cannot be realistically represented by finite elements. This is because the dynamic process of deformation and alteration of bones and joints is more complex. Going forward, consideration should be given to combining techniques such as visualization sensors, digital image correlation methods, and other stress–strain distribution measurement techniques to simulate the expansion process using a solid model. Besides, the finite element model in this study is based on a generic model created from the imaging data of an adult male patient. This makes the results most relevant to patients with similar craniofacial structures, including bone thickness and suture orientation. In the future, advances in digital technology will allow for the rapid creation of personalized models for specific patients. These models will use full-fidelity scanning and deep learning technologies. They will allow instant simulation of various devices' efficacy to evaluate and select the optimal treatment plan. Also, to simplify the analysis of the experimental data, this study only recorded the data of the right landmarks. However, the two sides of the skull model are asymmetrical and can affect the expansion [53], so future studies should explore how the asymmetry affects the expansion. Finally, although this study offers ideas for designing improved expansion devices for treating patients with MTD, it should acknowledge that further clinical validation is required before widespread implementation.

Conclusion

1. The number and position of micro-implants affect the force transmission and expansion effect. The stress distribution range and expansion effect of the 6-micro-implant types are superior to those of the 4-micro-implant type. Compared to the two designs with 6 micro-implants, the C-type has a more expansive force transmission range, the linear-type has higher expansion efficiency, and better stability of the micro-implants.

2. The support methods influence the transmission of the stress. Before the MPS was opened, the tooth-bone-borne method could transmit the expansion force to the craniomaxillary complex more efficiently and reduce the stress on the micro-implants and the surrounding cortical bone, but it would increase the degree of buccal inclination of the first molar. After the MPS was opened, the degree of first molar inclination and the efficiency of expansion were essentially equal between the two support methods.

3. In clinical practice, it is necessary to digitally design various numbers, positions of micro-implants, and support methods for expanders based on the patient's specific conditions and treatment goals in order to improve the success rate and safety of the expansion and achieve the treatment goals more precisely.
