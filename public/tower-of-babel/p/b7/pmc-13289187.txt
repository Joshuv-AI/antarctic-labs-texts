Beyond PVR: dynamic physiologic assessment enhances prognostic evaluation in pulmonary hypertension associated with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
Authors: Elizabeth S Tarras, Xiting Yan, Christopher Gange, Sebastian Kurz, Phillip Joseph, Inderjit Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPulmonary hypertension associated with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (PH-COPD) is associated with poor outcomes, yet resting haemodynamics incompletely capture disease severity. We evaluated whether integrating submaximal cardiopulmonary exercise testing variables with transthoracic echocardiographic and pulmonary function test parameters improves prognostic assessment in PH-COPD beyond resting haemodynamics alone.SettingSingle-tertiary pulmonary vascular disease referral centre in the USA.Participants67 patients with PH-COPD who underwent right heart catheterisation and complementary non-invasive testing between 2019 and 2024.Primary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was a composite of heart failure hospitalisation or all-cause mortality.MethodsAssociations between physiologic variables and outcomes were assessed using Cox proportional hazards models and survival tree analysis.ResultsThe composite outcome occurred in 47.1% of patients over a median follow-up of 518 (229–897) days. Several non-invasive dynamic physiologic variables were associated with adverse outcomes, including lower gas exchange-derived pulmonary vascular capacitance (GxCAP) (HR 0.997, 95% CI 0.995 to 0.999; p=0.013), lower oxygen uptake efficiency slope (HR 0.983, 95% CI 0.968 to 0.998; p=0.030), lower submaximal VO2 (HR 0.978, 95% CI 0.959 to 0.997; p=0.022) and lower ETCO2 (HR 0.886, 95% CI 0.820 to 0.957; p=0.002). Higher estimated right ventricular systolic pressure (eRVSP) on echocardiogram (HR 1.015, 95% CI 1.001 to 1.029; p=0.032) and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) on right heart catheterisation (HR 1.087, 95% CI 1.005 to 1.176; p=0.038) were also associated with adverse outcomes, although no optimal prognostic PVR threshold was identified. In the survival tree model, GxCAP<243 mL/mm Hg provided the initial and most influential split. Patients with both GxCAP<243 mL/mm Hg and eRVSP>45 mm Hg represented the highest-risk subgroup (relative HR 4.4). Including PVR did not improve prognostic performance of the analysis.ConclusionsDynamic exercise-derived physiologic markers provide prognostic information complementary to resting haemodynamic assessment in PH-COPD and may improve risk stratification across this heterogeneous population.

Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) commonly complicates chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and is associated with reduced exercise capacity and increased mortality.1 While mild elevations in mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) are frequently observed in advanced COPD, a subset of patients develops pulmonary vascular disease with pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) disproportionate to the degree of airflow obstruction. This severe PH-COPD phenotype is characterised by exertional dyspnoea, hypoxaemia, low diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (DLCO) and right heart failure despite relatively preserved spirometry and less prominent emphysema.2

Current guidelines define severe PH-COPD as PVR>5 Wood Units (WU).3 However, right heart catheterisation (RHC), the diagnostic reference-standard, provides only static haemodynamic data and is not widely accessible.4 As a result, there is growing need for non-invasive tools that can better characterise pulmonary vascular dysfunction and improve prognostic assessment in PH-COPD.

Additionally, although resting haemodynamics remain central to disease characterisation, they do not capture the abnormalities in ventilation–perfusion matching and circulatory reserve that emerge during exercise. In PH-COPD, pulmonary vascular remodelling limits the ability of distal vessels to distend appropriately in response to rising right ventricular (RV) stroke volume. This impaired RV–pulmonary vascular interaction can be captured dynamically on cardiopulmonary exercise assessment as it results in ventilation–perfusion mismatch and subsequent abnormalities in gas exchange. Dynamic, exercise-based measurements thus have the potential to detect early vascular stiffness, RV–pulmonary artery uncoupling and circulatory inefficiency that precede or outpace traditional resting markers such as PVR. Integrating exercise-derived physiologic data into the non-invasive evaluation of suspected PH-COPD may improve identification of high-risk patients across the PVR spectrum and provide complementary physiologic information beyond resting haemodynamic assessment.

We evaluated whether combining cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET)-derived variables with transthoracic echocardiographic (TTE) and pulmonary function test (PFT) parameters identifies PH-COPD patients at risk for adverse clinical outcomes more accurately than resting haemodynamics alone.

Methods

We retrospectively analysed consecutive patients with PH-COPD who underwent clinically indicated RHC between April 2019 and March 2025. COPD diagnosis was established by the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease recommendations.5 PH was defined based on current guidelines.3 Patients with CPET and TTE data within 6 months of RHC were included; those with left ventricular ejection fraction<50% or significant valvular disease were excluded. Patients and/or the public were not involved in any way in the design, conduct or reporting of this study.

Standard supine RHC was performed4 using midthoracic zero-reference and pressures averaged over several respiratory cycles6; thermodilution was used to determine cardiac output. Submaximal non-invasive CPET was performed using a standardised 6 min step protocol with continuous gas-exchange analysis.4 The protocol consisted of 2 min of rest, 3 min of progressively paced step exercises using a raised platform and 1 min of recovery while breath-by-breath gas exchange, heart rate and peripheral oxygen saturation were continuously monitored. Additional measured parameters included sub-maximum and extrapolated maximum exercise oxygen consumption (VO2) (% predicted), resting and exercise end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO2), ventilatory efficiency, oxygen uptake efficiency slope (OUES) and gas-exchange-derived pulmonary vascular capacitance (GXCAP) defined by the product of ETCO2 and the oxygen pulse. GXCAP was calculated continuously throughout exercise testing, and the end-exercise value was used for analysis.

Associations between clinical variables and a composite outcome of heart failure hospitalisation or all-cause mortality were analysed using sex and age-adjusted Cox proportional hazards models. Optimal prognostic thresholds for continuous variables were identified by iteratively testing all observed cut-points. Variables with p<0.05 were incorporated into multivariable analyses. Survival tree analysis was performed with and without inclusion of PVR. Variable importance scores were derived, and node-specific relative HRs were reported to represent risk within each terminal subgroup compared with the overall cohort baseline.

Results

67 patients met inclusion criteria (mean age 69±9 years; 52.9% female; body mass index 28.84±7.7 kg/m2; haemoglobin 13.5±2.40). 63.2% of patients had pre-capillary PH and 30.9% had combined post-capillary and pre-capillary PH. Mean FEV1 was 59.82±21.11% predicted and the mean DLCO corrected for haemoglobin (DLCOc) was 42.39±20.86% predicted. Haemodynamics demonstrated mPAP 40.85±11.55 mm Hg, PAWP 14.51±6.51 mm Hg, cardiac index 2.44±0.83 L/min/m2 and PVR 6.52±4.42 WU. 50% of patients had a PVR>5 WU. COPD pharmacotherapy was prescribed in 66.2% of patients; 23.5% were on triple therapy with a long-acting beta agonist, long-acting muscarinic antagonist and inhaled corticosteroid. 20.9% of patients were treated with at least one pulmonary vasodilator, most commonly a phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor. The composite outcome occurred in 47.1% of patients, with a median time to event of 518 (229–897) days.

Several non-invasive parameters were associated with adverse outcomes. (online supplemental table 1). These included a PFT variable: lower DLCOc (HR 0.97 (0.95 to 0.99), p=0.003); TTE indices: lower tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE)/estimated RV systolic pressure (eRVSP) ratio (HR 0.14 (0.02 to 0.99), p=0.049) and higher eRVSP (HR 1.01 (1.00 to 1.03), p=0.032); and CPET findings: lower GxCAP (HR 0.997 (0.995 to 0.999), p=0.013), lower OUES (HR 0.98 (0.97 to 1.00), p=0.03), lower sub-maximum VO2 (HR 0.98 (0.96 to 1.00), p=0.022) and lower ETCO2 (HR 0.89 (0.82 to 0.95), p=0.002). Among haemodynamic variables, higher PVR was associated with adverse outcomes (HR 1.08 (1.01 to 1.18), p=0.038); however, no optimal prognostic PVR threshold was identified.

In the survival tree model, GxCAP<243 mL/mm Hg provided the initial and most influential split. Patients with both GxCAP<243 mL/mm Hg and eRVSP>45 mm Hg represented the highest risk group (relative HR 4.4). A moderate risk subgroup was characterised by preserved GxCAP>243 mL/mm Hg combined with DLCOc<48% predicted and TAPSE/eRVSP<0.22 mm/mm Hg (relative HR 1.4) (figure 1). Including PVR neither improved prognostic performance nor altered variable importance ranking.

Discussion

In this cohort of patients with PH-COPD, several non-invasive CPET gas-exchange parameters—GxCAP, OUES, ETCO2 and sub-maximum VO2—were significantly associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Notably, GxCAP emerged as the strongest discriminator of prognosis, outperforming invasive haemodynamic markers including PVR. When combined with eRVSP, these two measures identified a subgroup with distinctly elevated risk, suggesting that integrated non-invasive physiologic assessment may provide prognostic information complementary to resting haemodynamic evaluation.

GxCAP likely reflects integrated cardiopulmonary physiology related to pulmonary vascular function, RV stroke volume and gas exchange efficiency, and prior studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between GxCAP and invasively measured pulmonary vascular capacitance during exercise,7 8 a major determinant of RV pulsatile afterload and an established PH prognostic marker.9 Whereas PVR quantifies static resistive afterload, PA compliance reflects the distensibility of the distal pulmonary vasculature,10 which is particularly relevant during exercise. In PH-COPD, pulmonary vascular abnormalities impair the ability of the small pulmonary vessels to initially recruit and later distend appropriately during exercise, contributing to dynamic worsening of GxCAP. In COPD, ventilation–perfusion mismatch may additionally influence GxCAP through alterations in ETCO2. Together, these mechanisms may explain GxCAP’s strong prognostic association, suggesting it captures vascular impairment that may not be reflected with resting PVR measurements. Although eRVSP is an imprecise surrogate of pulmonary pressures, an elevated eRVSP combined with GxCAP provided additional prognostic information, likely signalling greater RV afterload burden in patients with advanced disease.

Despite an association between higher PVR and adverse outcomes, we did not identify an optimal prognostic PVR threshold, and PVR did not emerge as a discriminating variable in our survival tree. This likely reflects the limitations of resting PVR in COPD, where hyperinflation, variable flow states and comorbid cardiac disease can obscure disease severity. Patients with similar resting PVR values may therefore have markedly different physiologic reserves, reinforcing the importance of complementary dynamic measures that capture pulmonary vascular behaviour under stress.

This study is limited by its retrospective, single-centre design and small sample size, which may affect generalisability. Additionally, as in prior studies,11 patients were included based on haemodynamic evidence of PH in the setting of COPD rather than strict WHO PH group assignment, resulting in a cohort with overlapping pre-capillary and combined post-capillary and pre-capillary phenotypes. This approach was intended to reflect the clinical heterogeneity commonly encountered in PH-COPD, where multiple mechanisms frequently coexist. A minority of patients also received PH-directed therapy, which may have influenced clinical outcomes; however, treatment timing and duration relative to physiologic testing and outcomes were variable and could not be reliably analysed retrospectively. Nonetheless, our findings suggest that non-invasive physiologic markers may provide meaningful prognostic information across a broad spectrum of PH-COPD, complementing invasive haemodynamic assessment while offering a practical approach for serial evaluation of dynamic pulmonary vascular limitation during exercise.
