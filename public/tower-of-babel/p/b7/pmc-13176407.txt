Introduction

Rupture-prone high-risk atheromatous plaques are characterized by large positively remodeled lesions with voluminous necrotic cores and thin-inflamed fibrous caps [1, 2]. The plaque volumes and remodeling can be reliably identified by noninvasive and invasive imaging [2, 3]. Although the feasibility of imaging inflammation has been demonstrated for large vessels by positron emission tomography, the detection of coronary inflammation is challenging. We propose that the development of a position-sensitive positron-counting catheter with added directionality from lead shield covers would help record quantitative uptake of specifically targeted radiotracers against inflammation.

Methods

We designed a positron-counting catheter comprising plastic scintillator (EJ-200, Eljen Technology, Texas, U.S.A.) with 0.8 mm in diameter × 3 mm in length and flexible optical fibers, with shielding (sCath) and without shielding (nsCath) scintillators. For the sCath, 0.3 mm thickness of lead was placed on the segments of circumference to obtain directionality. The outer diameter of the catheter is 1.0 mm and covered with a 0.1 mm thick black tube for light protection. The optical fibers were divided into 2 groups and connected to 2 photomultiplier tubes (PMT: H10721P-110, Hamamatsu Photonics, Hamamatsu, Japan) leading to comparators (a double system), coincidence circuit and counter on the circuit board (AMP-3033, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). To exclude noise, signals were counted when obtained simultaneously from both optical fiber systems (Fig. 1a). The assembly in a clinically compatible catheter (Hitachi corporation, and Goodman Medical Innovation Center, Tokyo, Japan) was evaluated in the in-vitro experiment and vascular ex-vivo imaging, as below.

For an in-vitro study, the catheter was placed on 18F point sources (18F-FDG diluted in different ratios, but same amounts were placed in cylindrical holes) to measure radioactivity counts for one minute for 3 times (Supplementary Fig. 1A) [4]. The ex-vivo study was performed in 9 explanted abdominal aortas from male New Zealand White rabbits; abdominal atherosclerosis was induced by 1% cholesterol diet for 3 months after balloon endothelial denudation [5, 6]. Thirty MBq of 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) was administered intravenously and rabbits were sacrificed 2 h later and aortas explanted for ex-vivo study. The positron-counting catheter was pulled back every 1 cm and counts measured for 1 min by nsCath, followed by sCath-R (right side), and sCath-L (left side) (Supplementary Fig. 1B). After 3 measurements, the autoradiography of whole aorta was performed, the artery was sectioned at 1 cm marking left side with India ink using toothpick and each segment was counted in an automatic well-type of scintillation gamma-counter for calculating percent injected radioisotope dose per gram (%ID/g) aorta. Specimens were then fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde, adjusted by vessel orientation referring India ink, paraffin embedding and serial sectioning for histopathological characterization. The area of macrophage infiltration was outlined and cell number counted automatically (QUpath software) [7] (Fig. 1b); average numbers of 3 field-views along the lumen in both right (ΦR) and left (ΦL) histological halves were obtained. All procedures followed the institutional guidelines and were approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients assessed the correlation between variables using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

In-vitro study showed linear correlation between counts and 18F point source dose until at least 100 kBq (r = 0.98, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1c). For ex-vivo study, autoradiography showed the accumulation of 18F-FDG in the abdominal aorta (Supplementary Fig. 2) and 100 sections were obtained from 9 aortas, and counts-per-minute (cpm) from each catheter were correlated with well-counts. The nsCath, sCath-R, sCath-L, and average of sCath-R and sCath-L (sCath-R + L) positron counts demonstrated high correlation coefficients with quantitative %ID/g (r = 0.49, 0.38, and 0.48, 0.48, respectively; p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Fig. 3). The positron count rate also showed excellent agreement with quantitative ipsilateral macrophage number: (sCath-R, r = 0.80; sCath-L, r = 0.73; p < 0.0001). The average macrophage numbers also correlated with sCath-R + L (r = 0.80, p < 0.0001) and nsCath (r = 0.48, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1d–g); sCath showed stronger correlation than nsCath.

Discussion

In this study, we present intravascular position-sensitive positron-counting catheter wherein the detection mode is positron counting with internal coincidence for noise cancellation, and the measurement is set as positron counting per unit time. The role and effective impact of the partial lead shielding is to establish directionality to enable detection of eccentric macrophage-rich plaques.

Several groups have reported intravascular radiation detectors in the last two decades [4, 8–13]. Although the basic structure is similar; a scintillator connected to PMT through optical fibers amplified the signals and count, and a double system between scintillator and PMT employed coincidence counting methods. While probes generally employed a single system (a scintillator with 1 optical fiber connected to 1 photomultiplier tube without comparators or coincidence circuit) [8–13], double system has the advantage of reducing noise compared with single system [4]. In the current study, we employed a double system and showed high correlations between the catheter and well counts. The target lesion uptake was expected as 27.75 kBq (“0.00075 mCi”) [10] and our radioisotope count showed high correlation with radioactivity.

In ex-vivo study, autoradiography showed the accumulation of 18F-FDG in the abdominal aorta, the positron count rates of position-sensitive shielded catheter (sCath) were correlated with ipsilateral macrophage number and %ID/g, while %ID/g was calculated based on the γ-ray activity of samples (including plaques, vessel walls), injection dose and sample weight. While γ-ray flies relatively long distances from 18F-FDG accumulated cells, emission from 18F-FDG extends maximum up to 2–3 mm and our catheter was designed to detect positron (β-ray). Theoretically, the positron-count rates indicate the number of 18F-FDG accumulated in cells and not affected by cells out of range, including extravascular structures or myocardium. This is exemplified by superior correlation of positron count rate to macrophage numbers compared with %ID/g, which include the uptake of extravascular structures (Fig. 2).

The sCath count showed excellent correlation with ipsilateral macrophage number, which was higher than that with nsCath. This superiority would be potentially useful in ecentric plaques, i.e. sCath could distinguish high or low macrophage accumulation sides, while nsCath only addressed the counts of whole circumference. This will allow detection of site with maximum accumulation of macrophages, i.e. Potentially most rupture-prone sites. While this ex-vivo study was performed without blood flow, vessel motion, or physiological deformation of the arterial and these factors may influence detector performance in-vivo, these data suggests the advantage of adding directionality with a double system for detecting accumulation of macrophages (Fig. 2).

As a future perspective, this catheter may complement diagnostic information obtained from optical coherence tomography (OCT), intra vascular ultrasound (IVUS) or near infra-red spectroscopy (NIRS) sirens regarding plaque burden or lipid cores. The possibility of lateralization and pullback could empower clinical applicability, even though further studies are needed.

The limitation of this study includes use of prototype catheter, fragility of the catheter, and lack of in-vivo study. While a double system reduces the noise, the apparent counts were generally lower than that of single system catheters in other groups. Semiconductor detectors could resolve this limitation for future studies in larger animals. The correlations between sCath counts and ipsilateral macrophage numbers were strong, however, it was not perfect. The sCath will detect macrophages in the borderline (middle of sides) area and affect the results, in addition to limited sample size.

In conclusion, while further studies are needed, the directional superiority of sCath should potentially be useful in ecentric plaques for inflammation localization, compared with circumferential inflammatory load by the use of nsCath.
