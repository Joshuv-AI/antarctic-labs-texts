Evolutionary paradigm of distinct viral coding regions of BVDV associated with different nucleotide usage patterns
Authors: Le-le An, Ze-yu Liu, Xiao-ting Ren, Yong-li Mo, Jian-hua Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Due to its high mutation rate, the nucleotide usage variation of bovine viral diarrhea virus (BVDV) is generally considered as one of evolutionary dynamics for evolving distinct coding regions and thus shaping evolutionary pathway of BVDV. Here, we analyzed 168 genetically distinct BVDV strains with verified genotypic classifications to elucidate which selective factor acting on twelve viral coding regions. We employed information entropy, dinucleotide odds ratios, relative synonymous codon usage, the effective number of codons (ENC), and context-dependent codon bias (CDCB) to characterize the genetic features underlying variations in nucleotide usage patterns. Among the twelve viral regions of the BVDV genome, the Capsid, P7, and NS4A regions exhibit stronger biases in overall nucleotide usage patterns than the other regions. Moreover, the nucleotide pair usage in these regions displays a conserved pattern rather than a genotype-specific one. Although certain dinucleotide pairs, such as UpA and CpG, are recognized for their roles in stimulating host immune responses, CpG usage is more strongly suppressed than UpA across all BVDV genomic regions. Compared with dinucleotide usage patterns, synonymous codon usage in these regions has better performance for displaying both genotype-associated evolutionary trends and function-specific coding preferences. Moreover, the overall codon usage bias reflected by ENC in each viral region reflects the interaction of nucleotide usage variability driven by mutational pressure and function-specific patterns shaped by natural selection acts on evolutionary paradigm of BVDV. Additionally, CDCB analysis provides further evidence for the pervasive influence of natural selection in shaping the codon organization of all BVDV genomic regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05035-w.

Introduction

Bovine viral diarrhea virus (BVDV), a globally distributed pathogen, infects a broad spectrum of susceptible hosts (including swine, sheep, cattle, and deer) and is responsible for a range of clinical syndromes. BVDV, a member of the Pestivirus genus within the Flaviviridae family, possesses a positive-sense single-stranded RNA genome approximately 12.3 kb in length. The genome contains a single open reading frame (ORF), which is flanked by a 5′ untranslated region (5′UTR) and a 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR). The ORF encodes a large polyprotein that is post-translationally cleaved by both viral and host proteases into four structural proteins (Capsid, E0, E1, and E2) and eight nonstructural proteins (Npro, P7, NS2, NS3, NS4A, NS4B, NS5A, and NS5B). Based on antigenic and genotypic divergences, the BVDV population is classified into three species: BVDV-1, BVDV-2, and BVDV-3 (also known as Pestivirus H) [1]. Due to its high genomic mutation rate, each species comprises multiple subgenotypes—for instance, BVDV-1 includes at least twenty-three recognized subgenotypes, while BVDV-2 and BVDV-3 each contain four subgenotypes [2–4]. Notably, a novel subgenotype, designated BVDV-2e, has recently been identified within the BVDV-2 lineage [5]. Different evolutionary rates have been reported among the three BVDV genotypes. For instance, subgenotype BVDV-1a exhibits substitution rates of 5.9 × 10⁻⁴ and 1.26 × 10⁻³ substitutions per site per year in the 5′UTR and the structural region, respectively [6]. Furthermore, the evolutionary rate of the E2 gene was found to be slightly higher than that of other genomic regions of BVDV [7]. In general, the high frequency of point mutations in the BVDV genome is primarily attributable to the error-prone nature of viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerases involved in genome replication. Continuous accumulation of mutations in the BVDV genome may result in the emergence of novel viral variants with divergent biological functions. The genetic divergence observed among the three BVDV genotypes is likely to have important implications for disease diagnostics and control strategies. Currently, many studies on the genetic features of BVDV have primarily focused on nucleotide substitutions within the viral genome and the resulting antigenic diversity of viral proteins, which are critical for understanding the evolutionary dynamics of BVDV [8–13]. Apart from nucleotide substitutions that alter amino acid composition, substitutions occurring at the third codon position - typically synonymous and traditionally considered biologically neutral - have often been overlooked. This type of evolutionary event, known as a synonymous substitution, does not result in an amino acid change but may still influence viral evolution through mechanisms such as codon usage bias, mRNA stability, and translational efficiency [14]. An increasing body of evidence suggests that variations in synonymous codon usage can significantly influence translation efficiency, protein folding, and mRNA stability [14, 15]. Previous studies have indicated that the significant synonymous codon usage bias observed in the BVDV ORF may serve as a potential marker for genotype classification [16–18]. During the viral life cycle, translation of the viral ORF into functional proteins is carried out by host translational machine associated with ribosome and multiple factors, utilizing aminoacyl-tRNAs and GTP as essential substrates. As nascent viral polypeptides emerge from the ribosome, they begin to fold co-translationally while still tethered to the ribosomal complex [19–21]. With the advancement of sequencing technologies, newly emerging BVDV isolates continue to provide valuable insight into the virus’s genetic diversity, pathogenesis, and epidemiological patterns during its ongoing evolution. Like other members of the Flaviviridae family [22, 23], analyses of synonymous codon usage variation in different viral protein genes can provide valuable insights into the genetic relationship between co-translation and protein function changes in the evolutionary pathway of BVDV. In this study, we define the evolutionary paradigm of BVDV as the composite pattern of selective pressures and mutational biases shaping the nucleotide and codon-level evolution of viral proteins. To highlight the evolutionary dynamics of BVDV, we employed information entropy and dinucleotide odds ratios to reflect the overall nucleotide usage bias and nucleotide pair bias within the given open reading frame (ORF). Shannon information entropy, a fundamental metric in information theory, mathematically quantifies the average uncertainty, surprise, or information content in a random event, based on the probabilities of its potential outcomes [24]. The nucleotide pair usage pattern is also an essential genetic feature, as it reflects the variability in dinucleotide composition across viral genomes [25]. Beyond mononucleotide and dinucleotide usage biases, the codon usage pattern—one of the key components shaping the evolutionary paradigm—can be analyzed using relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU), the effective number of codons (ENC), and context-dependent codon bias (CDCB) [26–28].

Materials and methods

The source of BVDV strains

A total of 168 BVDV strains with verified genotypic classifications were included in this study (Table S1). The ORFs of these strains were retrieved from GenBank via the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). To investigate the patterns of synonymous codon usage in different coding sequences across BVDV genome, twelve coding sequences (Npro, Capsid, E0, E1, E2, P7, and NS2 through NS5B) were extracted based on multiple sequence alignments conducted using MEGA version 6.0.

The bias of mononucleotide composition in viral coding sequence represented by information entropy

Mutations occur throughout the entire BVDV genome, leading to nucleotide composition biases within specific coding sequences that are primarily driven by mononucleotide usage. To quantify the extent of these biases, Shannon entropy (information entropy) was employed, considering the four nucleotide variations: adenine (A), guanine (G), uridine (U), and cytosine (C). In the entropy formula (H), the four variables represent the compositions of these nucleotides, while ‘N’ refers to the total number of nucleotides. The term p(x) represents the probability of a specific nucleotide at a given position in the sequence. Here, we applied this formula to assess the nucleotide composition skew across the target gene and its various codon positions.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H=-\sum\limits_{x\in N} p\left(x\right)\;\log\;p\left(x\right)$$\end{document}

The H value ranges from 0 to 1 and reflects the degree of dispersion in the contribution of the four nucleotide types: a higher value indicates more uniform nucleotide usage, whereas a lower value suggests a more biased nucleotide usage pattern.

The nucleotide pair bias in viral coding sequence represented by dinucleotide odds ratio

To quantify nucleotide pair bias, the dinucleotide odds ratio is commonly used, defined as: f(xy) = fxy/fxfy. In the formula, fxy represents the frequency of the specific dinucleotide pair (xy), while fx and fy are the frequencies of the individual nucleotides x and y, respectively. The dinucleotide odds ratio was calculated using CodonW 1.4.4 software. Notably, a dinucleotide odds ratio of less than 0.78 indicates under-representation of the corresponding dinucleotide, while a value greater than 1.23 reflects over-representation [29]. When the odds ratio falls within the range of 0.78 to 1.23, the associated nucleotide pair is considered to exhibit unbiased dinucleotide usage within the respective sequence. The dinucleotide odds ratio effectively highlights contrasts between the observed frequencies of nucleotide pairs and those expected based on the individual mononucleotide frequencies.

Quantification of synonymous codon usage in viral coding sequences

To elucidate the interaction between nucleotide compositional constraints and natural selective pressures in shaping the overall codon usage of the target coding sequence, a plot was constructed based on ENC and GC composition at the third codon position (GC3 content) [28]. The ENC value ranges from 20 to 61. An ENC value of 20 indicates that only one synonymous codon is selected for the corresponding amino acid, while a value of 61 suggests that all synonymous codons are used equally for that amino acid [28]. In this study, we define an ENC value of less than 40 as indicative of a strong over all codon usage bias in the corresponding coding sequence. Additionally, RSCU is commonly used to assess synonymous codon bias in coding sequence [27]. The RSCU values for the 12 coding sequences in the BVDV ORF were calculated to characterize the synonymous codon usage patterns. To highlight synonymous codons with significant usage bias, over-represented synonymous codons were defined as those with an RSCU value > 1.6, and under-represented codons were defined as those with an RSCU value < 0.6.

Quantification of CDCB in viral coding sequence

Not only can synonymous codons be selected non-randomly from the synonymous codon family, but neighboring nucleotides surrounding a codon can also influence the selection of that codon from the synonymous group. This genetic phenomenon is referred to as context-dependent codon bias (CDCB) [30]. Synonymous codons are not only selected non-randomly from the synonymous family, but the selection process is also influenced by neighboring nucleotides surrounding the codon. This genetic phenomenon is referred to as context-dependent codon bias (CDCB) [26, 30]. Moreover, the nucleotide that plays a crucial role in shaping CDCB is the first nucleotide immediately following the codon, known as the N1 context [31–33]. The N1 context refers to the nucleotide immediately following the codon and is denoted as XYZ_N (where X, Y, Z, and N represent any nucleotide). In this study, we examined the extent of CDCB in the 12 viral coding sequences of BVDV by computing R values, which represent the relative abundance of a codon (XYZ) in the N1 context. The R value is calculated as the ratio: R(XYZ_N) = F(XYZ_N)/F(XYZ)F(N). Where F(XYZ_N) denotes the frequency of the codon with the N1 context, F(XYZ) represents the frequency of the codon (XYZ), and F(N) refers to the frequency of nucleotide N in the N1 context. According to the R value calculation strategy, a higher R value indicates a stronger usage bias of the context N1 (XYZ_N). The calculation for the R value follows the approach outlined in a previous report [31].

Mapping the evolutionary paradigms of viral coding sequence

Principal component analysis (PCA) is commonly used to summarize the distance matrix, which records the distances between each combination of samples [34]. Here, we used the software SPSS 16.0 by means of the PCA strategy for mapping evolutionary paradigms derived from information entropy, dinucleotide odds ratio and RSCU, respectively. When PCA was employed to highlight the genetic role of nucleotide composition skew in the evolutionary paradigm of the target sequence, the data matrix consisted of three variables related to the information entropy of the three codon positions. For highlighting the genetic role of nucleotide pair bias in the evolutionary paradigm, the data matrix included 16 variables associated with the dinucleotide odds ratio. To visualize the evolutionary paradigm of the target viral coding sequence reflected by synonymous codon usage, the data matrix comprised 59 variables corresponding to the RSCU of the coding sequence. A 3D map illustrating the evolutionary paradigm of the target coding region of BVDV was constructed using the first three principal components.

Statistical analyses

A one-way ANOVA was used to compare the means of two or more groups with numerical response data, utilizing SPSS 16.0 for Windows. A significant difference was considered when the p-value was less than 0.05.

Results

Discrepancies in nucleotide usage bias among viral coding sequences

Previous studies have demonstrated that the pyrimidine (U + C) content is significantly lower than the purine (A + G) content in the BVDV ORF, with all three genotypes exhibiting a similar nucleotide usage pattern [16]. In this study, we investigated nucleotide usage bias for the 12 viral coding sequences to characterize the nucleotide composition skew in these regions across BVDV ORF. Nucleotide composition skew generally referred to the deviation observed within specific viral coding regions and their respective codon positions (Fig. 1). Although the 12 viral regions in the BVDV ORF were always following the same replication process, significant disparities in nucleotide usage bias were observed among these coding regions, particularly in the Capsid, E1, E2, and NS5B regions which exhibited significantly different usage biases for mononucleotide than other regions (p value < 0.001), respectively (Fig. 1a). Subsequently, the three codon positions of each viral region were also quantified using information entropy to assess nucleotide usage variation. Each codon position within the viral regions displayed significant differences in mononucleotide usage bias. At the first codon position, the Npro, Capsid, NS3 and NS5A regions exhibited significantly different usage biases for mononucleotide from the rest regions (p value < 0.001), respectively (Fig. 1b). At the second codon position, the Capsid, P7, and NS4A regions not only showed stronger usage biases, but also displayed greater variability (p values < 0.001) (Fig. 1c). At the third codon position, the Capsid and NS4A regions continued to show more variability in mononucleotide usage than other regions (p value < 0.001) and the Capsid region owned stronger usage bias for mononucleotide than others (Fig. 1d). Within each viral region, the nucleotide usage at the third codon position exhibited greater variability than at the first and second positions (Fig. 1b and d), suggesting that synonymous substitutions are a key evolutionary force in BVDV evolution.

Strong suppression of nucleotide pair usage (CpG) across all viral regions

Based on the mononucleotide usage bias across different viral regions of BVDV (Fig. 1), we next estimated nucleotide pair bias in these regions. Although each viral coding region exhibited similar dinucleotide usage patterns across the three BVDV genotypes, most dinucleotides showed significantly different usage biases between the genotypes, indicating a genotype-specific pattern (Table S2). Moreover, the dinucleotide usage patterns in the Npro, Capsid, E1, P7, and NS4A regions exhibited more variability compared to other regions of BVDV. In contrast, the dinucleotide usage pattern in the NS5B region showed the most convergence (Figure S1). Turning to the genetic classification based on 16 dinucleotide odds ratios for each viral region, BVDV-1 generally owned more dispersed nucleotide usage patterns compared to the other genotypes (Fig. 2). Specifically, the Npro, Capsid, and NS5B regions showed no significant genotypic differentiation associated with dinucleotide odds ratios (Fig. 2a and b, and l); the nucleotide pair usage patterns in the E0 and NS4B regions of BVDV-3 were distinct from those in other genotypes (Fig. 2c and j); BVDV-2 and BVDV-3 exhibited clear divergences in nucleotide pair usage in the E1, E2, P7, NS2, NS3, NS4A, and NS5A regions (Fig. 2d, i and k). Compared to the dinucleotide UpA usage pattern, the dinucleotide CpG suffered from a strong suppression across all viral regions (Table 1). Notably, the NS2, NS3, and NS5B regions in all three genotypes exhibited a stronger CpG usage bias with greater suppression than other regions (Table 1). Divergence in synonymous codon usage across viral regions

Based on the dinucleotide usage deviation across different viral regions of BVDV (Figure S1), PCA method was performed to illustrate the divergence of synonymous codon usages in these regions. It is observed that the Capsid, P7, and NS4A regions exhibited higher variability in synonymous codon usage compared to other regions, while the NS3 and NS5B regions showed more convergent synonymous codon usage patterns (Figure S2). We then calculated RSCU for the viral regions and identified divergences in synonymous codon usage across the different BVDV genotypes (Table S3-Table S8). Based on the evaluation criteria for the over-represented (RSCU > 1.6) and under-represented (RSCU < 0.6) codons, we identified codons that were consistently over- or under-represented across the three BVDV genotypes. In the Npro region, the over-represented codon AGA for Arg and the under-represented codons ACG for Thr, CGC, and CGA for Arg were identified (Table S3); In the Capsid region, the over-represented codons are UUG for Leu, AUA for Ile, ACU for Thr, AGA and AGG for Arg, and the under-represented codons were CUC for Leu, AUU for Ile, UCG for Ser, CAU for His, UCU for Cys, GGC for Gly, CGA, and CGG for Arg (Table S3);In the E0 region, the over-represented codons included AGU for Ser and AGG for Arg, and the under-represented codons included CUC for Leu, UCG for Ser, ACG for Thr, CGU, CGA, and CGG for Arg (Table S4); In the E1 region, the over-represented codons had AGA and AGG for Arg, and the under-represented codons had CUU for Leu, GCG for Ala, CGU, CGA and CGG for Arg (Table S4); In the E2 region, the over-represented codons contained AGA and AGG for Arg, and the under-represented codons contained UCG for Ser, ACG for Thr, GCG for Ala, CGU, CGC and CGA for Arg (Table S5); In the P7 region, the over-represented codons include AGG for Arg, and the under-represented codons had CUC for Lue, UCU for Ser, CCC for Pro, GCG for Ala, CAA for Gln, UGU, UGC for Cys, CGU, CGC, CGA and CGG for Arg (Table S5); In the NS2 region, the over-represented codons had AGU for Ser, AGA and AGG for Arg, and the under-represented codons owned UCG for Ser, CCG for Pro, ACG for Thr, GCG for Ala, CGC, CGA and CGG for Arg (Table S6); In the NS3 region, the over-represented codons included UCA for Ser, AGA and AGG for Arg, and the under-represented codons included UCG for Ser, CCC for Pro, GCG for Ala, CGC, CGA and CGG for Arg (Table S6); In the NS4A region, the over-represented codons were UCA for Ser, AUA for Ile and AGG for Arg, and the under-represented codons were AUU for Ile, UCG, AGU, AGC for Ser, CCU for Pro, GCG for Ala, UGU, UGC for Cys, CGC, CGA, CGG, CGU for Arg and GGU for Gly (Table S7); In the NS4B region, the under-represented codons include CUU for Leu, AUU for Ile, UCG for Ser, CCG for Pro, ACG for Thr, GCG for Ala, UGU for Cys, CGU, CGC, CGA, CGG for Arg (Table S7); In the NS5A region, the over-represented codons included AGU for Ser, GCA for Ala, AGA, AGG for Arg, and the under-represented codons included CUU for Leu, UCG for Ser, ACG for Thr, GCG for Ala, CGU, CGC, CGA and CGG for Arg (Table S8); In the NS5B region, the over-represented codons were AGU for Ser, AGA, AGG for Arg, and the under-represented codons were CUU for Leu, UCG for Ser, CCG for Pro, ACG for Thr, GCG for Ala, CGU, CGC, CGA and CGG for Arg (Table S8). Most synonymous codons containing the CpG dinucleotide showed strong suppression across all viral regions. Interestingly, the 12 viral regions of BVDV exhibited a consistent trend, with the number of under-represented synonymous codons exceeding that of over-represented codons. Notably, the amino acid Arg exhibited polarization in synonymous codon usage across the 12 viral regions of BVDV. These suppressing certain synonymous codons likely reflect strong natural selective forces mediate the evolutionary paradigm of BVDV with respect to codon usage.

According to the evolutionary paradigms for the 12 viral regions of BVDV associated with nucleotide pair usage (Fig. 2), PCA was also used to illustrate the evolutionary patterns based on RSCU data. For synonymous codon usage patterns across each viral region, BVDV-1 generally exhibited more divergence (Fig. 3). Compared to the evolutionary paradigms based on nucleotide pair usage (Fig. 2), those based on synonymous codon usage patterns were more effective in illustrating the genotype-specific models for most viral regions (Fig. 3). However, the ability to highlight genotype-specific models varied across different regions. The Capsid, E0, E1, P7, and NS3 regions were particularly powerful in distinguishing genotype-specific patterns (Fig. 3b, d, f and h). In addition, for BVDV-1, the Npro, E0, and NS2 regions were classified into two evolutionary groups based on synonymous codon usage patterns (Fig. 3a and c, and 3g). The NS4A, NS5A, and NS5B regions within BVDV-1 and BVDV-2 exhibited similar patterns of synonymous codon usage (Fig. 3i, k and l), while the E2, NS2, and NS4B regions within BVDV-1 and BVDV-3 showed closer synonymous codon usage patterns (Fig. 3e, g and j). These findings collectively suggest that the viral regions of BVDV exhibit heterogeneous evolutionary paradigms associated with synonymous codon usage, shaped by various selective forces and functional constraints during genetic evolution.

High variability in codon usage across different regions of BVDV

To directly assess the roles of nucleotide composition constraints and natural selective pressures in shaping overall codon usage bias, a plot of ENC values versus GC3 content was constructed for the 12 viral regions across BVDV ORF. Overall, the distinct coding regions of BVDV exhibited heterogeneous evolutionary patterns associated with their codon usage bias (Figure S3). As shown in Figure S3, the data points for the distinct viral regions of BVDV generally fell below the expected trendline rather than aligning with it, suggesting that natural selective forces play a significant role in shaping codon usage in these viral regions. Regarding the variability in overall codon usage bias across each viral region, the ENC values ranged from 45.86 to 61.00 in the Npro region, 40.53 to 61.00 in the Capsid region, 46.15 to 58.97 in the E0 region, 44.09 to 61.00 in the E1 region, 49.40 to 59.33 in the E2 region, 31.80 to 61.00 in the P7 region, 47.82 to 55.42 in the NS2 region, 48.36 to 54.12 in the NS3 region, 30.11 to 61.00 in the NS4A region, 45.54 to 55.16 in the NS4B region, 48.46 to 54.82 in the NS5A region, and 47.95 to 52.95 in the NS5B region. Notably, the NS2, NS3, NS5A, and NS5B regions exhibited stronger codon usage biases than other BVDV viral regions. Subsequently, the relationships between genotypic classification and the overall codon usage patterns of specific viral regions in BVDV were investigated. For the Npro region, the overall codon usage pattern in BVDV-1 exhibited more variability than in other genotypes (Fig. 4a). In the Capsid region, BVDV-3 displayed a generally stronger codon usage bias compared to the other genotypes (Fig. 4b). For the E0 region, BVDV-2 and BVDV-3 showed stronger codon usage biases than BVDV-1, and the GC3 content in BVDV-1 and BVDV-3 was lower than in BVDV-2 (Fig. 4c). In the E1 region, BVDV-2 and BVDV-3 exhibited stronger codon usage biases than BVDV-1, while the GC3 content in BVDV-1 was more variable than in the other genotypes (Fig. 4d). In the E2 region, although all genotypes exhibited convergent codon usage patterns, BVDV-2 and BVDV-3 showed stronger biases than BVDV-1 (Fig. 4e). For the P7 region, all genotypes displayed significant variability in overall codon usage bias (Fig. 4f). In the NS2, NS3, NS4B, NS5A, and NS5B regions, all genotypes exhibited relatively convergent patterns of overall codon usage (Fig. 4g, h, j, k and l). Finally, in the NS4A region, BVDV-2 and BVDV-3 exhibited significant codon usage bias, while BVDV-1 displayed high variability in its codon usage (Fig. 4i). Although the viral regions of BVDV are influenced by both mutation pressure from nucleotide composition and natural selection in shaping their codon usage patterns (Fig. 4), each viral region exhibits unique genetic characteristics that regulate its overall codon usage bias.

Strong discrepancy in CDCB among viral regions

Based on data associated with nucleotide usage bias, nucleotide pair bias, and synonymous codon usage bias for the viral regions of BVDV, we further quantified the R values for these regions to assess the extent of CDCB for each synonymous codon. Generally, the R values for the viral regions of BVDV exhibited a broad range of variability, with significant discrepancies in synonymous codon usage influenced by the neighboring nucleotide type in each viral region. To further emphasize this genetic characteristic, we selected synonymous codons with an RSCU ≥ 2.0 to analyze the bias discrepancies in each viral region. Based on these over-represented codons associated with favorable and unfavorable nucleotides (Table 2), the model of XYC_G (where X and Y represent nucleotides) tended to emerge in the group of codons with unfavorable nucleotides, while the model XYU_A did not exhibit this genetic feature. The evolutionary trait reflected by CDCB in these viral regions also underscores that nucleotide composition, along with natural selection, plays a key role in shaping the overall codon usage pattern for each viral region of BVDV.

Discussion

In the evolutionary paradigm of viruses, nucleotide usage patterns are nonrandom, and synonymous codon usage and codon order are also nonrandom [35, 36]. With genotypic classification of BVDV, nucleotide composition constraint in the viral genome and natural selective force play key roles in shaping the synonymous codon usage patterns within the BVDV ORF [16]. In the life cycle of BVDV, the polyprotein encoded by the BVDV ORF is cleaved into 12 distinct proteins, each with specific biological functions. As shown in Figs. 1 and Figure S1, the genetic features associated with mononucleotide and dinucleotide usage patterns in the distinct coding regions of BVDV might indicate that a given coding region with specific viral functions owns its specific nucleotide usages rather than following nucleotide usage of BVDV ORF. In viral genomes, distinct viral regions exhibit heterogeneous evolutionary patterns, shaped by differential selection pressures and functional constraints during genetic evolution [37, 38]. As for the P7 region with a high variability of mononucleotide usage pattern (Fig. 1 and Figure S1), nucleotide usage variation of this coding region might affect BVDV infection. A previous report highlighted that the P7 region, which encodes a small hydrophobic protein, plays a crucial role in BVDV replication [39]. High-level changes in the P7 region could lead to mutations that impact its biological function, thereby affecting the efficiency of BVDV replication [40–42]. Beyond the biological function limitations imposed by nucleotide composition variation, the usage of dinucleotide CpG, which is strongly suppressed in every viral region of BVDV ORF (Table 1 and Table S2), indicates that the host immune response plays a dominant role in nucleotide organization. The activity of the host’s zinc-finger antiviral protein (ZAP) is strongly correlated with the levels of CpG suppression in viral transcripts [43, 44]. CpG suppression is widespread among RNA viruses, as well as in the genomes of retroviruses and small mammalian DNA viruses. The suppression of dinucleotide CpG enables antiviral defense mechanisms targeting non-self RNA and impacts the innate immune response to viral infections [45, 46]. There are some novel issues that experimental increases of CpG content in viral genomes can impair infection. For example, Zika virus variants with elevated CpG content exhibited significantly attenuated infection phenotypes and increased protection against lethal challenges in mice [47]. While the HIV RNA genome naturally suppresses CpG dinucleotides, artificially increasing CpG abundance can disrupt multiple stages of the viral life cycle [48]. Similarly, the replication of Minute virus of mice was notably impaired when CpG content was increased in the coding sequence for the structural protein [49]. In addition to the evolutionary constraints imposed by the host immune response on nucleotide pair usage, variation in nucleotide pair usage provides novel insights into the highly heterogeneous organization of specific viral coding regions across the three genotypes (Fig. 2). This result suggests that BVDV-1, with its high variability in nucleotide pair usage across every viral coding region, likely serves as the evolutionary origin driving BVDV’s evolutionary process. Clearly, both the efficient replication of the viral genome and immune escape are strongly linked to the nucleotide pair patterns within the viral genome [50–53]. Furthermore, the deviations in dinucleotide relative abundance shown in Fig. 2 might influence base-step stacking capacities and higher-order RNA folding features to some degree. This may be due to base-step conformational preferences reflected in parameters such as slide, roll, tilt, propeller twist, and helical twist [54–57]. Additionally, dinucleotide composition-dependent RNA/DNA structural features significantly influence aspects of protein-RNA interactions [58–60].

The nucleotide and codon usage biases are often influenced by factors associated with species or protein functions [35]. Previous studies on hepatitis C virus (HCV) and classical swine fever virus (CSFV) have shown that the majority of abundant codons in the HCV ORF are C/G-ended codons, with most overrepresented codons also being C/G-ended [61, 62]. Additionally, the coding sequences for different viral proteins in CSFV, BVDV, and BDV exhibit distinct synonymous codon usage patterns, strongly suggesting that synonymous codon usage follows a virus-dependent model [17]. In viral coding regions, synonymous codon usage patterns (Table S3-S8) reveal that over-represented and under-represented synonymous codons reflect translational selection, which contributes to shaping the overall codon usage pattern of BVDV. For instance, we observed a strong bias in synonymous codon usage in the BVDV Capsid and NS5B (RNA-dependent RNA polymerase), both of which play crucial roles in viral particle formation and genome replication. This suggests that synonymous codon usage bias is closely linked to the secondary structures and biological functions of these proteins. A previous study on the BVDV NS5A structure highlighted the significant synonymous codon usage bias in helix folding regions [63]. Furthermore, synonymous codon usage bias in specific motifs of HCV NS3 and NS5A has been associated with antiviral drug resistance, indicating a strong correlation between codon usage bias and protein function [64]. Given that BVDV relies on genetic variation, persistent infection, and viral tropism for immune cells, posing a serious threat to the cattle industry, vaccination—by inducing host immunity (a natural selective force)—offers an effective strategy to prevent acute systemic infections and enhance reproductive efficiency through fetal protection [65, 66]. Generally, synonymous codon usage bias caused by translational selection is more commonly observed in unicellular organisms (including viruses) than in eukaryotes, where a favorable subset of synonymous codons is closely linked to translation efficiency and the fine-tuning of translation [67–69]. Natural selective pressure arises from various factors, including host proteinsthat recognize specific nucleotide patterns. Notably, vaccination can influence host adaptation by affecting the codon usage of CSFV [70]. Apart from synonymous codon usage bias in the viral coding regions of BVDV, neighboring nucleotides flanking a codon play a crucial role in mediating the selection of that particular codon from the synonymous family (Table 2), strongly suggesting that the bias of two neighboring synonymous codons can influence viral protein function and pathogenesis. The previous report pointed out that the usage biases of synonymous codons (UCG, CCC, GCA and AAC) had a potential link with BVDV-1c biotypes [71]. This genetic phenomenon has been observed in the genomes of various organisms, including viruses and microorganisms, and is explained as a direct consequence of dinucleotide bias [32, 72–77]. The relationship between the overall codon usage pattern and GC3 content provides new insights into the roles of distinct selective forces in viral proteins with specific biological functions (Figure S3), suggesting that fine-tuning translational selection may regulate gene expression in different regions of BVDV in distinct ways. Based on the relationships between ENC and GC3 content across different viruses, the evolutionary paradigm reflected by the overall codon usage pattern has been proposed as a new pathway for understanding gene expression through alternative regulatory mechanisms [36, 78, 79]. The evolutionary paradigms of the viral coding regions in BVDV are likely driven by a complex interplay of selective forces associated with the viral life cycle and genomic organization. A previous study indicated that the coding sequences for the three structural proteins (E0, E1, andE2) of BVDV exhibited similar overall codon usage patterns to those of CSFV and BDV, suggesting that the codon usage pattern of these three pestiviruses results from a balance between mutational pressure from the virus and translation selection by the host [17]. Hu et al. [80] investigated the effects of synonymous codon deoptimization/optimization in the nucleoprotein(NP) gene of influenza A virus on the interaction between NP and TRIM25. They found that synonymous codon usage bias significantly influenced the NP-TRIM25 interaction, strongly suggesting that synonymous codon usage can impact mRNA stability, translation speed, and the folding-function relationship of the NP gene [80]. Generally, structural protein coding regions (Capsid, E0, E1, and E2) tend to exhibit stronger purifying selection due to their essential roles in virion stability and host recognition. In contrast, non-structural protein-coding regions (P7, NS2, NS3, NS4A-4B, and NS5A-5B) may tolerate higher mutation rates while preserving conserved functional domains, thereby balancing genetic plasticity with enzymatic fidelity.

Conclusion

Based on the highly variable genomic organizations of BVDV across its three genotypes, we conducted a comprehensive analysis into the genetic characteristics of overall nucleotide usage, dinucleotide frequency, synonymous codon usage, and the neighboring nucleotides of synonymous codons in each viral coding region to better understand evolutionary paradigm of each viral coding region. Generally, both the overall nucleotide usage bias represented by information entropy and the nucleotide pair bias represented by the dinucleotide odds ratio display that natural selection strongly acts on BVDV evolution. Notably, the synonymous codon usage pattern performs better in explaining genotype-specific models than the overall nucleotide usage bias. Although the genetic variability of the viral coding regions of BVDV spans a relatively broad range, the usage patterns of nucleotide pairs, synonymous codons, and their neighboring nucleotides are still strongly controlled by natural selective pressures from the host. Overall, the synonymous codon usage pattern in each viral region of BVDV is more effective in illustrating genotype-specific evolutionary paradigms than the overall nucleotide usage and nucleotide pair usage patterns, suggesting that synonymous codon usage bias should deeply takes part in biological functions of viral proteins.
