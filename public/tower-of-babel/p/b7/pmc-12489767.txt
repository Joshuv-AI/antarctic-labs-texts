Exploring the Shared Genetic Characteristics of Peri-Implantitis and Osteoporosis: A Transcriptomic Analysis Perspective
Authors: Churen Zhang, Feixiang Wang, Jianguo Ke, Cuihong Lin, Xiaohua Zeng, Qiuyan Yang, Qiaoling Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aimsIncreasing evidence suggests an association between peri-implantitis and osteoporosis, both of which are prevalent diseases that significantly affect patient quality of life. This study aimed to investigate the shared genes and molecular mechanisms underlying the co-pathogenesis of peri-implantitis and osteoporosis.MethodsTranscriptomic data from blood samples of patients with peri-implantitis and osteoporosis. were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus database and shared differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified. Analysis of Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathways was next conducted for shared DEGs. Then, an mRNA–miRNA network of the hub genes was established based on the miRTarbase database. Potential drugs related to the hub genes were predicted using the Genecards database. Finally, the CIBERSORT core algorithm was used to calculate immune cell infiltration levels.ResultsUtilizing several gene expression datasets (GSE33774, GSE106090, GSE35956, and GSE35958), we identified a total of 10,839 DEGs. Notably, we identified 3 hub genes that exhibited differential expression in 4 datasets. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that intersected DEGs are involved in regulating oxidative stress, lipid metabolism, and osteoclast differentiation, while immune cell infiltration analyses highlighted correlations between gene expression and various immune cell types. Furthermore, our miRNA–mRNA interaction network analysis suggested complex regulatory mechanisms related to the hub genes. Finally, potential drug targets associated with these hub genes are identified.ConclusionThis study provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying peri-implantitis and osteoporosis, highlighting the potential for further investigation into targeted therapies that may improve clinical outcomes for affected patients.Clinical RelevanceThe clinical translational value ranges from biomarkers to treatment targets. Shared genes may act as diagnostic markers, informing both the risk of osteoporosis and the bone integration ability of implants. Drugs targeting common genes may offer the potential for 'one drug, two effects'.Key words: Peri-implantitis, Osteoporosis, Transcriptomic analysis

Introduction

Peri-implantitis and osteoporosis represent significant clinical challenges due to their high prevalence and associated complications. A meta-analysis in 2021 on the epidemiological trends of osteoporosis over the past 20 years showed that the global prevalence of osteoporosis was approximately 18.3%, with a prevalence of about 23.1% in women and 11.7% in men. The higher prevalence in women has been associated with the significant decline in oestrogen levels after menopause as well as endocrine and metabolic imbalances.1 In China, osteoporosis has become an important health issue for people over 50 years old, with an incidence rate of 19.2% overall, a high incidence rate of 32.1% among women, and a prevalence rate of 51.6% among women over 65 years old.2 According to a systematic review,3 the incidence of peri-implantitis at the implant level varied from 0.4% within 3 years to 43.9% within 5 years, and the frequency ranged from 1.1% to 85.0%. For patients who regularly participated in a prophylactic program, the median prevalence of peri-implantitis was 9.0%, whereas for those who did not, it was 18.8%. An analytic multicentric investigation of 1279 dental implants placed by professionals between January 2006 and October 2017 was conducted. When creating the best multivariate logistic regression model to examine the influence of each risk factor on the survival and long-term effectiveness of osseointegrated implants, osteoporosis had odds ratios that were truly deemed risk factors.4

Peri-implantitis is characterized by inflammatory processes that lead to the loss of supporting bone around dental implants,5 whereas osteoporosis is characterized by reduced bone density and increased fracture risk.6 There is thus a possibility that osteoporosis might significantly influence the prognosis of dental implants. Bone density influences the speed and severity of alveolar ridge resorption. According to research, osteoporosis causes a decrease in osteoblast numbers, which prolongs the healing cycle of tooth extraction pits, reduces the bone mass of newly produced bone, and lowers the content of growth hormones associated with bone induction.7 It also interferes with bone tissue wound healing, resulting in a reduced ability to regenerate alveolar bone.8 Furthermore, patients with missing teeth may undergo severe horizontal and vertical bone resorption, resulting in not only the formation of a serrated alveolar ridge, but also Class III jaw bone connections with relative buccal displacement of the lower jaw.9 Further investigation into the underlying biological mechanisms and possible therapeutic targets is necessary because current diagnostic and therapeutic measures, such as clinical evaluations, imaging methods, and pharmaceutical treatments, have demonstrated limitations in terms of efficacy and patient compliance.10,11

Recent studies have discovered a number of molecular fingerprints associated with peri-implantitis and osteoporosis, revealing the complex biological mechanisms that contribute to the development of these disorders. For example, research has shown that inflammation and immunological responses play an important role in the development of peri-implantitis, whereas hormonal imbalances and genetic predispositions promote osteoporosis.12,13 Furthermore, studies of the gene expression profiles linked with various disorders have revealed the presence of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), which could serve as biomarkers for early diagnosis and intervention.14,15 However, the particular connections and processes that underpin the association between these 2 diseases are poorly understood.

To address these gaps in knowledge, this study focused on analysing the expression profiles of DEGs in gingival samples from patients with peri-implantitis and in mesenchymal stem cells derived from osteoporosis patients. Utilizing bioinformatics methodologies, including differential gene expression analysis and functional enrichment analysis, this research aimed to elucidate the biological functions of identified DEGs, as well as their potential interactions within regulatory networks. The integration of large datasets allows for a comprehensive understanding of gene interactions and functional pathways, which may ultimately lead to the identification of novel therapeutic targets for both conditions.

Materials & methods

Data sources

The transcriptome datasets of gingival samples from patients with peri-implantitis (GSE33774, GSE106090) and mesenchymal stem cell samples from osteoporosis patients (GSE35956, GSE35958) were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). GSE33774 is an mRNA expression profile in GPL6244 [HuGene-1_0-st] Affymetrix Human Gene 1.0 ST Array [transcript (gene) version], including 8 gingival samples from healthy participants and 7 gingival samples from peri-implantitis patients. GSE106090 is an expression profile from array GPL21827 Agilent-079487 Arraystar Human LncRNA microarray V4 (Probe Name version), comprising 6 gingival samples from peri-implantitis patients and 6 gingival samples from healthy controls. GSE35956 and GSE35958 are expression profiles obtained by array in GPL570 [HG-U133_Plus_2] Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array. GSE35956 consists of 5 mesenchymal stem cell samples from osteoporosis patients and 5 mesenchymal stem cell samples from non-osteoporotic donors after total hip arthroplasty. GSE35958 comprises 5 mesenchymal stem cell samples from osteoporosis patients and 4 mesenchymal stem cell samples from non-osteoporotic donors after total hip arthroplasty.

Differentially expressed gene analysis

To find DEGs, GEO2R, the GEO online analysis tool (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/geo2r/), was used to identify DEGs in GSE33774, GSE106090, GSE35956, and GSE35958 between the disease groups and the control groups. DEGs were defined by the evaluation criteria of adjusted P-value <.05 and |log2(FC)| > 1.0 for GSE33774, GSE106090, GSE35956, and GSE35958. Then, a Venn diagram was established to identify intersections between the DEGs.

Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs

GeneMANIA (http://genemania.org/) contains a vast number of publicly available biological datasets for linked genes. Protein–protein, protein–DNA, and genetic interactions, pathways, reactions, gene and protein expression data, protein domains, and phenotypic screening profiles are examples. The genes related to the intersected DEGs were identified by GeneMANIA. To identify the biological function of intersected DEGs, we use Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using the Metascape database (https://metascape.org/). GO analysis shows a description of genes in different dimensions and at different levels, generally consisting of cellular component, biological process and molecular function. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis systematically analyzes biological pathways in which genes participate.

Interacting mRNA–miRNA networks and potential drugs

The miRTarbase database (https://mirtarbase.cuhk.edu.cn/) was screened to identify the miRNAs that interacted with the top three hub genes. An miRNA–mRNA network was built using Cytoscape software. Potential drugs related to the hub genes were predicted by the Genecards database (https://www.genecards.org/), which integrated the information of drugs related to genes from the Drugbank database (https://go.drugbank.com/), human metabolome database (HMDB, https://hmdb.ca/), IUPHAR database (http://www.iuphar-db.org), and Drug–Gene Interaction database (DGIdb, https://dgidb.org/).

Expression levels of the hub genes and immune cell infiltration levels

Based on the CIBERSORT core algorithm, we used the markers of 22 immune cells provided by the CIBERSORT Tx website (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/) to calculate the immune infiltration status of the transcriptome datasets (GSE33774, GSE106090, GSE35956, and GSE35958). We conducted a batch correlation analysis on the expression levels of the hub genes and immune cell infiltration levels using Spearman correlation analysis (P < .05 represented a significant correlation).

Functional enrichment analysis and immune cell infiltrations

Eight predicted drugs were related to CYBB, 2 to MGP, and 7 to ALDH1A3 (Table). The functions of intersected DEGs were enriched in regulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolic process, oxidoreductase complex, oxidoreductase activity, acting on NAD(P)H, lipid and atherosclerosis, fluid shear stress and atherosclerosis, and osteoclast differentiation (Figure 3A). The miRNAs predicted to interact with ALDH1A3, MGP, and CYBB in the miRDB database were next identified (Figure 3B). In GSE33774, the expression of CYBB was positively correlated to the infiltration levels of memory B cells, CD8+ T cells, activated memory CD4+ T cells, resting natural killer (NK) cells, activated NK cells, monocytes, M2 macrophages, resting mast cells, activated mast cells, and neutrophils. The expression of MGP was positively correlated to the infiltration levels of M2 macrophages. The expression of ALDH1A3 was positively correlated to the infiltration levels of activated memory CD4+ T cells, resting NK cells, activated mast cells, and neutrophils, and negatively correlated to the infiltration levels of CD8+ T cells, monocytes, and resting mast cells (Figure 3C). In GSE106090, the expression of CYBB was positively correlated to the infiltration levels of plasma cells, and negatively correlated to levels of resting memory CD4+ T cells and eosinophils. The expression of MGP was positively correlated to the infiltration levels of plasma cells, and negatively correlated to levels of M1 macrophages, resting dendritic cells, and resting mast cells. The expression of ALDH1A3 was positively correlated to the infiltration levels of plasma cells, and negatively correlated to levels of resting memory CD4+ T cells and eosinophils (Figure 3D). In GSE35956, the expression of MGP was negatively correlated to the infiltration levels of naïve CD4+ T cells. The expression of ALDH1A3 was positively correlated to the infiltration levels of M2 macrophages, and negatively correlated to those of activated memory CD4+ T cells and activated dendritic cells (Figure 3E). In GSE35956, the expression of CYBB was positively correlated to the infiltration levels of memory B cells and resting NK cells. The expression of MGP was negatively correlated to the infiltration levels of regulatory T cells and activated mast cells (Figure 3F).

Discussion

This study pioneers the application of integrated bioinformatics to identify shared molecular signatures between peri-implantitis, a localized inflammatory bone disease around dental implants,16 and osteoporosis, a systemic metabolic bone disorder.17 Both diseases have a significant impact on patients' health and social medical resources. Osteoporosis causes abnormalities in bone structure and metabolism, and some scholars believe that this affects bone integration and the maintenance of stable bone tissue around implants, thereby reducing the success rate of oral implant treatment.18 Osteoporosis has been shown to significantly increase the risk of peri-implantitis (odds ratio 7.74),19 although no association was found in the female cohort,20 which may be due to differences in patient management. For example,21 it has been demonstrated that in patients with osteoporosis, strict daily oral hygiene maintenance (such as brushing teeth twice a day and cleaning teeth daily) can maintain peri-implant bone loss at levels comparable to non-osteoporotic patients for up to 6 years. Additionally, there are systematic reviews indicating that although osteoporosis patients may face the risk of increased peri-implant bone resorption, the long-term retention rate of dental implants is not affected.22 Clinical practice has shown that the risk of peri-implantitis in patients with osteoporosis is highly dependent on oral hygiene interventions,21 which may explain the lack of correlation in some reports.20 Future research needs to control for such variables to clarify the mechanism.

Our analysis revealed a variety of common DEGs, most notably ALDH1A3, MGP, and CYBB, and highlighted oxidative stress pathways as a pivotal axis linking the pathogenesis of both peri-implantitis and osteoporosis. This finding goes beyond descriptive associations to propose a potential molecular mechanism underlying the clinically observed complex interplay between these conditions. ALDH1A3 is an aldehyde dehydrogenase enzyme that detoxifies aldehydes and controls cellular redox equilibrium.23 The dysregulation of ALDH1A3 points towards a shared vulnerability to oxidative stress in both conditions. In peri-implantitis, bacterial biofilms generate significant oxidative stress and potentially toxic aldehydes locally. Osteoporosis, conversely, often involves systemic alterations in redox signalling affecting bone remodelling. Its significance in cellular homeostasis and oxidative stress response could be related to the described molecular pathways.24 In a rat model of osteoporosis, dysregulation of oxidative stress-related genes such as ALDH1A3 was shown to affect peri-implant bone formation rate. In a study of bone integration of femoral bone implants, dietary supplementation with synthetic bone mineral was shown to improve bone integration by regulating bone metabolism.25 MGP is a well-known mineralization inhibitor with implications for bone metabolism.26 Its deregulation in implant periapical inflammation and osteoporosis highlights its importance in the pathophysiology of these conditions. MGP may affect bone remodelling and the balance of bone production and resorption.27 As a bone mineralization inhibitor, aberrant expression of MGP in osteoporosis may impact peri-implant bone strength.28 Osteoporosis dramatically reduces peri-implant bone volume and the bone–implant contact ratio (averaging 62.5%),29 and gradually weakens implant mechanical fixation. Meanwhile CYBB is a component of the phagocyte NADPH oxidase complex, which is involved in the production of ROS.30 High MGP expression in osteoporosis patients may limit bone mineralization, whereas ALDH1A3 modulates bone healing via oxidative stress and may be a target for peri-implantitis prevention. Exploring the downstream effects of ALDH1A3, MGP, and CYBB activation and their interactions with immune cells could provide valuable insights into the immune response and potential therapeutic interventions.

Functional enrichment analysis suggested that pathways such as regulation of ROS metabolism, the oxidoreductase complex, and oxidoreductase activity (acting on NAD(P)H) are involved. Oxidative stress, as shown by these pathways, is expected to play a critical role in the development of both diseases. We hypothesize that systemic bone metabolic anomalies caused by osteoporosis (such as MGP dysregulation impacting mineralization, decreased bone density, and microstructural damage) may contribute to the incidence and progression of peri-implantitis via the following mechanisms: (1) deterioration of the bone microenvironment: osteoporosis makes bone tissue more vulnerable to oxidative stress damage, and its ability to heal itself declines. When implants are inserted or plaque stimulation occurs later in the process, this basic fragility makes the surrounding bone tissue more vulnerable to reactive oxygen species -driven damaging inflammatory reactions (CYBB related), making it difficult to effectively resist pathogen invasion and heal damage. (2) Abnormal immune response: osteoporosis can be accompanied by minor alterations in immune system function, decreasing the local response effectiveness of immune cells such as macrophages. Excessive or persistent ROS generation (CYBB) and reduced clearance capabilities (ALDH1A3) exacerbate the inflammatory cascade reaction and impair bone homeostasis.

It is vital to acknowledge that this study has several limitations. First, wet experiments did not confirm the conclusions of the bioinformatics analysis. Secondly, the sample size was relatively small, which may have influenced the reliability of the results. Furthermore, the absence of clinical validation studies makes it difficult to demonstrate the clinical importance of the expected outcomes. Finally, the use of several datasets may result in batch differences that affect the consistency of the results. Despite these limitations, our findings offer a novel perspective and experimental foundation for a better understanding of the molecular relationship between peri-implantitis and osteoporosis. The main genes (ALDH1A3, MGP, and CYBB) and pathways (such as oxidative stress) that were identified serve to provide targeted and verifiable hypotheses for future experimental work. It will also be important to prioritize the use of available techniques (such as specialized inhibitors/agonists, public clinical dataset mining) for validation.

Conclusion

By bioinformatics analysis, ALDH1A3, MGP, and CYBB were predicted to correlate to the pathogenesis and immune infiltration of peri-implantitis and osteoporosis.
