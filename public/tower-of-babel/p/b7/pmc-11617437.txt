A Randomized Controlled Trial Evaluating the Clinical Performance of Bioactive Restorative Material and Resin-modified Glass Ionomer Cement in Carious Primary Molar Restorations
Authors: Kalava Bhavana, KS Uloopi, C Vinay, Penmatsa Chaitanya, Manumanthu Venkata Ramesh, P Ahalya
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim and objectivesTo evaluate the clinical performance (marginal discoloration, surface texture, and retention) of bioactive restorative material (ACTIVA) and resin-modified glass ionomer cement (RMGIC) in carious primary molar restorations at 6 and 12-month intervals.Materials and methodsThe split-mouth randomized controlled trial comprised 31 children aged 5–8 years having contralateral matched pair of carious primary molars with an International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS) II score 5. Sixty-two teeth were randomly allocated into two groups: group I—ACTIVA (n = 31) and group II—RMGIC (n = 31). After caries excavation, the teeth were restored with the respective materials and evaluated the marginal discoloration, surface texture, and retention after 6 and 12 months using modified USPHS (United States Public Health Service) criteria under the dental operating microscope.Statistical analysisMann–Whitney “U” test was used for intragroup comparison and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for intergroup comparison.ResultsA statistically significant difference was found in the surface texture of ACTIVA and RMGIC restorations at both 6-month (p = 0.002) and 12-month (p = 0.001) intervals, whereas the difference in the marginal discoloration and retention were not significant at both 6-month (p = 0.267 and 0.161, respectively) and 12-month intervals (p = 0.339 and 0.064, respectively).ConclusionThe surface texture of ACTIVA is found to be superior to RMGIC, whereas marginal discoloration and retention are similar to RMGIC at both 6 and 12-month intervals.Clinical significanceACTIVA bioactive restorative material and RMGIC both enhance tooth remineralization with improved adhesion and fluoride release properties, making them valuable choices in various clinical scenarios.How to cite this articleBhavana K, Uloopi K, Vinay C, et al. Introduction

The restoration of carious deciduous teeth until their normal exfoliation is a significant treatment need in pediatric dentistry. Glass ionomer cement (GIC) is the material of choice for restoring carious primary teeth. The durability of GIC restorations is found to be less due to poor mechanical properties. Hence, resin components such as hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA) are incorporated in order to improve the mechanical properties of GIC, thus resulting in resin-modified glass ionomers (RMGIs).1

Advances in material sciences have resulted in functionally beneficial “bioactive” restorative materials that exhibit bactericidal and bacteriostatic activity, contribute to the exchange of calcium and phosphate ions with the tooth substrate, and aid in the reconstruction of demineralized dentin, thereby facilitating a natural bond between the restorative material and the tooth. They also precipitate apatite on the surface and provide self-seal to protect the tooth from further damage.2

ACTIVA kids (Pulpdent Corp., Watertown, USA) is a recently introduced “bioactive” material in the market. It is a hydrophilic resin-modified glass ionomer cement (RMGIC) enriched with bio-glass and fortified with a patented rubberized polymer resin. It is the first dental restorative material with a bioactive resin matrix. This enhanced RMGIC claims to have the similar physical properties, strength, and esthetics of composites as well as greater release and recharge of calcium, phosphate, and fluoride than glass ionomers. The manufacturer labels it a “smart” material as it combines the best attributes of both composite and GIC.3

ACTIVA kids being a new bioactive restorative material, evidence regarding its clinical performance and longevity is sparse. Hence, this study was carried out to clinically evaluate the performance of ACTIVA kids in carious primary molars of 5–8-year-old children in comparison with the previous generation RMGIC.

Materials and Methods

The present study was carried out from May 2020 to September 2021. The clinical trial was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Scientific Review Board and Ethical Committee (IECVDC/19/PG01/PPD/IVV/38) in accordance with the ethical standards laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The clinical trial design was registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI REG NO: REF/2020/03/032246).

The study is a double-blinded randomized split-mouth clinical trial that followed the standards published by Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) (Fig. 1).

CONSORT flow chart outlining the protocol of the study

The sample size was calculated from previous studies.4,5 G*Power 3.1.9.2 software for power analysis indicated that a total of 62 teeth (corresponding to 31 children to achieve 90% power with 95% confidence) were required for the study. Contralateral matched pairs of primary molars having distinct occlusal cavities with visible dentin [International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS) II score 5] in 5–8-year-old cooperative children willing to participate in the study were included. Children participating in other studies, who are medically compromised and having teeth with developmental defects, were excluded.

A total of 90 children visiting the outpatient department of pediatric and preventive dentistry were screened. Among them, 31 children were chosen based on the predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The purpose and procedure of the study were explained to parents and their children. Written informed consent from parents/guardians was obtained before initiating the study.

Computer-generated block randomization technique was adopted for the recruited participants. Allocation concealment was done with sequentially numbered opaque envelopes that were sealed. Split-mouth design was followed and the teeth were allocated to either of the two groups: group I—bioactive restorative material and group II—RMGIC.

In both groups, isolation was carried out with cotton rolls and high-volume suction, and then caries excavation was done using high-speed air rotor handpiece and BR-45 round bur. Then the teeth were restored according to manufacturer's instructions. In the control group, dentin conditioner was applied to the prepared cavity for 20 seconds and then rinsed, dried, and restored with RMGIC cured with light-emitting diode (LED) light for 20 seconds. In the test group, acid etching for 15 seconds was carried out with phosphoric acid of concentration 37%, then rinsed with water for a minute and then dried, followed by application of dentine bonding agent, curing and restoration with ACTIVA followed by curing with LED light for 20 seconds. In both groups, postoperative occlusal evaluation was done using articulating paper, and final adjustments were made if required using finishing bur.

The clinical performance of the RMGIC and ACTIVA was evaluated at 6 and 12-month intervals under the dental operating microscope for marginal discoloration, retention, and surface texture by two independent examiners using the modified USPHS (United States Public Health Service) criteria. The examiners were trained in the assessment method prior to the examination. They were precalibrated at 90% reliability on 10 patients who were not included in the study. The kappa values for interexaminer reliability were found to be 0.764–1.0 for ACTIVA and 0.827–1.0 for RMGIC groups. The agreement between the examiners was found to be substantial and almost perfect.

The obtained data were tabulated and subjected to statistical analysis using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21.0. Nonparametric tests were used. Mann–Whitney “U” test was used for intergroup comparison and Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for comparison within the groups. P-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Marginal Discoloration

On intragroup comparison of marginal discoloration, in the ACTIVA group, at the 6-month interval, 77.4% (n = 24) of restorations were graded as Alpha code, a perfect restoration without marginal discoloration, and it was reduced to 58.1% at the 12-month interval. Whereas in the RMGIC group, at 6-month interval, 64.5% (n = 24) of restorations displayed Alpha code, which was reduced to 48.8% at 12-month interval. The difference in marginal discoloration was found to be statistically significant in both ACTIVA (p = 0.014) and RMGIC groups (p = 0.008), suggesting that the marginal discoloration was increased at the 12-month interval compared to the 6-month interval (Table 1).

Surface Texture

On intragroup comparison of surface texture, in the ACTIVA group, at the 6-month interval, 93.5% (n = 29) of restorations were graded as Alpha code, a perfect restoration with smooth surface, and it was reduced to 87.1% (n = 27) at the 12-month interval. Whereas, in the RMGIC group, at the 6-month interval, 61.3% (n = 19) of restorations displayed Alpha code, which was reduced to 48.4% (n = 15) at the 12-month interval and this difference was statistically significant (p = 0.046), suggesting that the surface texture of RMGIC restorations degraded at the 12-month interval compared to the 6-month interval (Table 1).

On intergroup comparison of surface texture at the 12-month interval, Alpha code representing a perfect restoration with a smooth surface was scored by 87.1% (n = 27) of restorations in the ACTIVA group and 48.4% (n = 15) of restorations in the RMGIC group and this difference was found to be statistically significant (p = 0.001), suggesting that the surface texture of ACTIVA restorations is better than RMGIC (Table 2).

Retention

On intragroup comparison of retention, in the ACTIVA group, at the 6-month interval, 96.8% (n = 30) of restorations were graded as Alpha code, a perfect restoration with intact restorative material, and it was reduced to 93.5% (n = 29) at the 12-month interval. Whereas in the RMGIC group, at the 6-month interval, 87.1% (n = 27) of restorations displayed Alpha code, which was reduced to 77.4% (n = 24) at the 12-month interval and this difference was statistically significant (p = 0.025), suggesting that the retention of RMGIC restorations reduced at 12-month interval compared to the 6-month interval (Table 1).

On intergroup comparison of retention at the 12-month interval, Alpha code representing a perfect restoration with intact restorative material was displayed by 93.5% (n = 29) of restorations in the ACTIVA group and 77.4% (n = 24) of restorations in the RMGIC group and this difference was found to be statistically not significant (p = 0.064) (Table 2).

Discussion

The restoration of carious deciduous teeth until their exfoliation is the major treatment need in pediatric dentistry. Restoring the decayed tooth with appropriate material that bears the occlusal forces, withstands bacterial and acidic attack, and counteracts changes in pH and temperature while enhancing esthetic appearance is a consistent challenge.6

Recently RMGIC with enhanced bioactivity—ACTIVA™ bioactive restorative material (Pulpdent Corporation, USA) was introduced. It is a dual cure material that mimics the physical and chemical properties of teeth and responds to changes in the oral environment, formulated with a patented, ionic resin (Embrace resin) that contains a small amount of water. The manufacturer claims higher fluoride release and recharge, intimate adaptation to the tooth structure, exceptional marginal integrity, and seal against bacterial leakage.3

Marginal discoloration of restorations is seen due to microleakage. In the present study, both ACTIVA and RMGIC showed good clinical performance in regard to marginal discoloration. The remarkable sealing of ACTIVA could be attributed to the ionization reaction, which aids in the formation of hydroxyapatite bonds to the tooth structure, and also phosphate acid groups in the ACTIVA improve the exchange between the resin and the reactive glass fillers, thereby promoting the interaction with tooth structure. Whereas, the good seal obtained in RMGIC could be attributed to the true chemical bonding of RMGIC with the tooth structure.

Similarly, Bhadra et al. found that 96% of the ACTIVA restorations are perfect without any marginal discoloration at the 1-year interval.4 Kaushik and Yadav in their in vitro study found that ACTIVA exhibited less microleakage compared to nano-hybrid composite.7 An in vitro study by Jyothi et al. revealed the absence of marginal discoloration with RMGIC indicating that the material has good bonding to the tooth structure with no microleakage.8

Restoration with a smooth surface is a prime requisite for long-lasting restoration. This is because the surface texture is critical in reducing plaque deposition, secondary caries, and discoloration.9 In the present study, ACTIVA showed a smoother surface compared to RMGIC at both 6-month and 12-month intervals. This difference can be due to the presence of high bioactive ionic resin content in the ACTIVA, which gives a smoother finish to the restoration aiding in no plaque accumulation, thereby reducing the occurrence of secondary caries. In the RMGIC used in the present study, powder has to be blended with the liquid component risking the incorporation of air bubbles that may contribute to the clinically detectable higher surface roughness.

Similarly, Bansal and John observed that ACTIVA has significantly better surface texture and wear resistance compared to RMGIC, GIC, and composites.10 Also, Bhadra et al. found that 100% of the ACTIVA restorations retained smooth surface texture at the end of 1-year follow-up.4

Retention is probably the most critical factor for the measurement of the success of restoration. In the current study, both ACTIVA and RMGIC showed good retention rates at the end of 12 months. The high retention rate of ACTIVA can be attributed to the bioactive ionomer component, which helps in bonding chemically to the tooth and sealing against bacterial leakage. Also, ACTIVA material is hydrophilic in nature which helps for better bonding with the tooth structure. Whereas the success of RMGIC is attributed to the chemical reaction between polyalkenoic acid chains and calcium ions in hydroxyapatite and the micromechanical retention achieved by infiltration of organic components into the partially demineralized dentin surface.

Congruently, Bhadra et al. also noticed that 93% of the ACTIVA restorations are completely retained at the end of 1 year.4 ACTIVA showed a 98% retention rate in a study by the dental advisor, where retention was evaluated after 24 months of follow-up.3 On the contrary, an in vitro study by Benetti et al. found that ACTIVA restorations placed in non-pretreated cavities with margins in dentine were lost due to poor adhesion combined with large volumetric contraction of ACTIVA.11

In the ACTIVA group, surface texture and retention rate are consistent over time but the marginal discoloration increased with time. Whereas in RMGIC, the clinical performance of restorations in terms of all three test parameters, that is, marginal discoloration, surface texture, and retention degraded with time. Clinically, though both ACTIVA and RMGIC showed similar marginal discoloration and retention rates, ACTIVA outperformed RMGIC with smoother surface texture. Hence, considering its bioactive nature, it can be recommended for restoration in primary molars.

Apart from the parameters evaluated in the study, the other parameters that are crucial for the longevity of restoration are the nonoccurrence of secondary caries, lack of postoperative sensitivity, and maintenance of anatomic form. Hence, there is further scope to evaluate these factors to authenticate the potential benefits of the newer bioactive material ACTIVA.

Conclusion

At the end of 12 months, ACTIVA and RMGIC showed good marginal integrity. The surface texture of ACTIVA was smoother compared to RMGIC at both 6 and 12-month intervals. Retention of both ACTIVA and RMGIC is good and comparable at both intervals. Considering the superior clinical performance and bioactive nature of ACTIVA, it can be recommended for the restoration of primary molars.

Clinical Significance

ACTIVA and RMGIC find extensive clinical usage in various restorative procedures in pediatric dentistry, and situations where moisture control is challenging. Their bioactive and adhesive properties make them versatile choices in modern dental practice.
