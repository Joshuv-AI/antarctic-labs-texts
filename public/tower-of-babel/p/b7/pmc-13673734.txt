Trunk and lower limb kinematic alterations during the forward step-down task in women with multiple sclerosis: a comparative 3D motion analysis
Authors: Hanie Farhangi, Hashem Piri, Rahman Sheikhhoseini, Zahra Ebadi, Mohamadreza Hatefi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe forward step-down (FSD) task is a fundamental daily movement requiring coordinated trunk and lower limb control. Individuals with Multiple Sclerosis (MS) often experience neuromuscular impairments that may alter movement patterns during such functional tasks. This study aimed to investigate and compare trunk and lower limb kinematics during the FSD task in women with and without MS.MethodsThirty-six women aged 20 to 40 years participated in the study, including 18 with relapsing-remitting MS (EDSS scores ranging from 1 to 4.5) and 18 healthy controls matched for age, anthropometric measures, and sex. Three-dimensional kinematic data of the trunk and lower limbs were recorded at 120 Hz using a Vicon motion capture system during the FSD task performed from a 16 cm platform. Key events were defined at 60° knee flexion and at maximum knee flexion. Joint angles in the sagittal, frontal, and transverse planes were extracted for the ankle, knee, hip, and trunk. Independent-samples t-tests were used to compare the two groups.ResultsSignificant kinematic differences were observed between groups. At 60° of knee flexion, the MS group exhibited increased ankle external rotation (P = 0.009) and greater trunk flexion (P = 0.001). At maximum knee flexion, the MS group demonstrated increased ankle external rotation (P = 0.029), reduced knee flexion (P = 0.001), decreased knee internal rotation (P = 0.01), greater hip adduction (P = 0.002), and increased trunk flexion (P = 0.001). These findings indicate altered multi-joint coordination and compensatory strategies in participants with MS.ConclusionWomen with MS exhibit distinct kinematic deviations in the trunk and lower limbs during the FSD task, reflecting neuromuscular deficits and compensatory adaptations. These alterations highlight the importance of targeted rehabilitation strategies that focus on trunk control, hip stabilization, and lower limb strength and dynamic alignment to enhance functional performance and reduce fall risk in this population.Keywords: 3D motion capture, Movement analysis, Neuromuscular disorder, Lower extremity

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by the degeneration of the myelin sheath within the central nervous system (CNS). This condition is marked by focal lymphocytic infiltration of the CNS, leading to demyelination and axonal damage [1]. According to the International Federation of Multiple Sclerosis, the estimated global prevalence of MS reached 2.8 million individuals in 2020, representing a 30% increase since 2013 [2]. MS frequently causes impairments in motor function due to the involvement of the neuromuscular, sensory, musculoskeletal, and cardiopulmonary systems. The neurological deficits associated with MS are diverse and may include visual disturbances, spasticity, muscle weakness, somatosensory deficits, intention tremor, ataxia, impaired coordination, fatigue, and postural instability [3]. Patients with MS usually demonstrate muscle weakness in the lower limbs, diminished muscle torque, asymmetry in the activation of agonist and antagonist muscles, and delayed muscle activation at the hip, knee, and ankle joints. These impairments contribute to deficits in balance and gait function, as well as increased fatigue, thereby exacerbating the risk of falls—an issue of substantial concern within this demographic [4, 5].

Furthermore, a study examining the timing and co-contraction of lower limb muscle activation during gait found that individuals with MS exhibit heightened spasticity in the quadriceps, hamstrings, and ankle plantar flexors compared to healthy controls [6]. These neuromuscular alterations further impair gait and balance control. Consequently, significant differences in gait patterns, functional performance, and balance strategies are evident between individuals with MS and healthy controls. MS patients demonstrate deviations in muscle activation timing, co-contraction patterns, and muscle force generation that align with normative motor patterns [7]. Kinematic analysis plays a crucial role in assessing movement disorders associated with MS, as it enables objective quantification of abnormal movement patterns, joint coordination, and postural control deficits. It provides valuable insights into the functional limitations encountered by this population and supports the development of individualized rehabilitation programs tailored to specific motor impairments. Additionally, kinematic analysis serves as an effective instrument for monitoring disease progression [8]. In recent years, there has been a growing interest in the application of functional screening tests for evaluating motor performance, especially within the context of injury prevention. These assessments typically involve visual observation by a clinician, who identifies any deviations from normal movement patterns. One widely used test for the lower limbs is the forward step-down (FSD) task [9]. This task involves descending stairs while bearing weight on one limb, necessitating dynamic control of the lower extremity at various angles. It challenges the strength and neuromuscular control of the lower limbs and can be easily observed within clinical settings [10]. Trunk control plays a fundamental role in the execution of functional lower limb tasks, as the trunk accounts for approximately half of total body mass and substantially influences the position of the center of mass during dynamic activities [11]. Adequate trunk stability is essential for maintaining postural control, coordinating lower limb movements, and minimizing excessive joint loading [12, 13]. In individuals with MS, impairments in trunk muscle strength, proprioception, and neuromuscular coordination have been widely reported, leading to increased postural sway and reduced balance control [14, 15]. Despite its biomechanical and functional importance, trunk kinematics have received limited attention during functional screening tasks such as the FSD, particularly in individuals with MS [15].

As a result, it is frequently employed for clinical assessments, patient evaluations, and pre-participation screenings to identify potential injury risks [16]. Prior research conducted primarily in healthy adults and individuals with musculoskeletal disorders, such as patellofemoral pain and anterior cruciate ligament injury, has demonstrated a significant correlation between clinical ratings of FSD performance and objective joint angle measurements, indicating the utility of this task as a valid functional assessment tool in clinical practice [10]. However, the applicability of these findings to neurological populations, particularly individuals with MS, remains largely unexplored. Further research is required to evaluate this task across diverse populations and pathological conditions [10]. While numerous studies have investigated gait kinematics in individuals with MS compared to healthy individuals, and while the FSD task has been examined within various musculoskeletal conditions, there remains a significant gap in the literature concerning the kinematic analysis of FSD within the MS population. Given the increasing prevalence of MS and its multifaceted impact on both individuals and society, further exploration into functional movements within this demographic is warranted. Notably, the severity of disability in individuals with MS is commonly quantified using the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS), which provides a standardized measure of neurological impairment and functional limitation. Individuals with mild to moderate disability (EDSS ≤ 4.5) are typically ambulatory without assistance but may exhibit subtle deficits in balance, coordination, and movement control that are not readily detectable during level walking. Investigating functional tasks such as the FSD in this subgroup may therefore reveal early compensatory movement strategies that are clinically relevant for rehabilitation and fall prevention.

The FSD task is a fundamental daily activity for all individuals, including those with MS. Investigating and comparing trunk and lower limb kinematics during this functional task may yield critical insights into the nature of motor impairments in MS. To date, research in this specific area is limited and inconclusive. Therefore, the present study aims to compare trunk and lower limb kinematic variables during FSD between individuals with MS and healthy controls, thereby providing novel and clinically relevant data to enhance understanding and rehabilitation strategies within this population.

Methods

Participants

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 software [17] to determine the required sample size. The primary outcome variable was sagittal-plane knee flexion angle during the FSD task. Based on effect sizes reported in a similar kinematic study of functional single-leg tasks in clinical populations (Cohen’s d = 1.0) [18], and assuming a two-tailed test with α = 0.05 and power (1–β) = 0.80, a total of 18 participants per group were required to detect significant between-group differences in lower limb kinematics. Accordingly, a total of 36 women aged 20–40 participated in this study, including 18 women diagnosed with relapsing-remitting MS and 18 healthy controls. Participants with MS were recruited from the Multiple Sclerosis Research Center of Tehran University of Medical Sciences, while healthy control participants were recruited through community advertisements and university-based volunteer sampling. Inclusion criteria for the MS group were: ([1] a confirmed diagnosis of relapsing–remitting MS by a neurologist, [2] mild to moderate disability defined as an EDSS score ≤ 4.5 [19], [3] age between 20 and 40 years, and [4] no MS relapse or symptom exacerbation within the three months preceding testing. The selected age range was chosen to minimize the confounding effects of age-related musculoskeletal degeneration and to ensure that participants were capable of safely performing the FSD task. Exclusion criteria for all participants included any history of orthopedic surgery to the lower limbs or lumbopelvic region, any other medical condition that could limit safe physical activity, a body mass index (BMI) outside the 18–25 kg/m^2 range, or the presence of significant postural abnormalities (as classified by the New York Posture Rating system). These criteria were implemented to minimize confounding factors and ensure that observed kinematic differences could be attributed to MS-related impairments rather than other health issues. These criteria minimized potential confounding factors, ensuring that observed kinematic differences primarily reflected MS-related impairments.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of Allameh Tabataba’i University (Ethics Code: IR.ATU.REC.2024.81609.1153), and all participants provided written informed consent before participation. All procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines, including the Declaration of Helsinki.

Procedures

All testing was conducted in the Motion Analysis Laboratory at Dr Javad Mowafaghian Research Center for Neuro-rehabilitation under standardized conditions. Each participant attended a single one-hour session, which included informed consent, anthropometric assessment, warm-up, marker placement, motion capture calibration, and performance of the designated motor tasks. Participants completed a brief familiarization period to ensure correct execution of movements before formal data collection. Short rest periods of 2 min were provided between trials to minimize the effects of fatigue, which is particularly relevant for individuals with MS due to fluctuations in neuromuscular control. Additionally, participants were asked to report their perceived exertion using the Borg RPE scale, and trials were only continued if participants indicated minimal fatigue (RPE ≤ 11, “light”). This procedure ensured that the kinematic measures obtained reflected stable motor performance rather than acute fatigue. Only technically valid repetitions were considered for analysis.

Prior to marker placement and data collection, all participants completed a standardized warm-up protocol lasting approximately 8–10 min. The warm-up consisted of 5 min of low-intensity aerobic activity (self-paced treadmill walking) followed by dynamic stretching and mobility exercises targeting the trunk and lower limbs, including controlled hip flexion–extension, hip abduction–adduction, knee flexion–extension, ankle plantarflexion–dorsiflexion, and trunk flexion–extension movements. The warm-up was designed to increase muscle temperature, enhance joint mobility, and reduce movement stiffness, thereby facilitating more consistent and representative movement patterns during the FSD task. All participants performed the same warm-up protocol under supervision to ensure standardization across testing sessions.

Kinematic analysis

Kinematic data were collected using a 10-camera three-dimensional motion capture system (Vicon MX, Vicon Motion Systems Ltd., UK) at a sampling frequency of 120 Hz. A total of 39 reflective markers (14 mm diameter) were placed on anatomical landmarks of the trunk, pelvis, and lower limbs according to the standardized Plug-in Gait model. Marker locations included the bilateral anterior and posterior superior iliac spines, lateral thighs, medial and lateral femoral epicondyles, tibial tuberosities, mid-shanks, medial and lateral malleoli, calcanei, metatarsal heads (1st, 2nd, and 5th), and spinal landmarks (C7, T2, L1, L3, L5). This configuration enabled accurate tracking of segmental and joint kinematics in the sagittal, frontal, and transverse planes. Although the full-body Plug-in Gait model typically includes additional markers on the head segment, head markers were not used in the present study, as head kinematics were not among the variables of interest. Trunk kinematics were calculated using a segment defined by spinal and pelvic markers (C7, T2, L1, L3, and L5) relative to the laboratory reference frame. Lower-limb joint angles were computed using standard Plug-in Gait definitions based on pelvis, thigh, shank, and foot markers. The absence of head markers does not affect the accuracy of trunk orientation or lower-limb joint kinematics as defined in this study. Following marker placement and system calibration, participants performed the FSD task on a 16 cm high step platform. When the primary objective is to examine joint kinematics at specific points within the movement cycle, employing a single step height may be sufficient, as variations in step height are unlikely to substantially influence joint angle outcomes at those defined positions [9]. Prior to data collection, participants received standardized verbal instructions and a demonstration of the task. They were instructed to maintain a controlled and relatively upright trunk posture without excessive forward or lateral trunk sway, to keep their hands placed on the iliac crests, and to lower the non-dominant limb forward until the heel lightly contacted the floor without transferring body weight. Participants began each trial standing upright on the step with feet shoulder-width apart and hands placed on the iliac crests to minimize upper limb motion. The dominant limb, defined as the preferred leg for single-leg tasks, served as the stance limb. The non-dominant limb was lowered forward until the heel lightly contacted the floor without transferring weight, after which participants returned to the initial upright stance [9]. Each repetition was performed at a controlled pace of approximately five seconds: two seconds for descent, one second for foot contact, and two seconds for ascent. Timing was guided by a metronome to ensure consistency [20]. No hand support or railings were allowed, requiring participants to rely on balance and lower limb control. Each participant completed three technically valid trials. Trials were discarded and repeated if participants lost balance or deviated from the instructed technique, including excessive trunk motion or incorrect foot placement [9].

Data processing

Kinematic computations were performed using Vicon’s Plug-in Gait model, followed by custom post-processing in MATLAB (MathWorks Inc., USA). Three-dimensional joint angle time series were computed for the trunk, hip, knee, and ankle of the stance limb at 120 Hz. The stance limb was defined as the dominant limb (i.e., the preferred leg for single-leg tasks), and all kinematic analyses were performed on this limb, as it bore the full body weight during the FSD task. Angles were defined using the conventional gait coordinate system: sagittal-plane knee flexion–extension, frontal-plane hip adduction–abduction, and transverse-plane tibial rotation and foot progression angle. In the transverse plane, “ankle rotation” refers to the rotation of the tibia relative to the foot (tibial rotation), as determined by the Plug-in Gait model, rather than the absolute orientation of the foot relative to the ground. It should be noted that transverse plane ankle measurements using this model are susceptible to greater error compared to sagittal and frontal planes, and results should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. Trunk angles were measured relative to the vertical axis. Raw marker trajectories and computed joint angles were first inspected in Nexus for completeness, then exported to CSV format for further analysis. For quantitative assessment, two key events were identified in each FSD trial: (1) the moment the dominant knee reached 60° of flexion during descent, representing mid-range flexion, and (2) the moment of maximum knee flexion at the bottom of the step-down, representing end-range movement. The 60° knee flexion angle was selected as a functionally relevant mid-range position that has been commonly used in step-down and single-leg tasks to evaluate joint control under substantial loading conditions, while minimizing the influence of end-range constraints [9, 21]. In contrast, maximum knee flexion represents the phase of greatest mechanical demand and postural challenge during the task, and is therefore sensitive to compensatory movement strategies [22, 23]. These events allowed standardized comparison of joint postures between MS and control groups. Using a custom MATLAB script, time points corresponding to 60° and peak knee flexion were automatically detected. Joint angles at these instants were extracted for the ankle, knee, and hip of the stance limb in all planes, as well as trunk angles. Values were averaged across the three trials to yield representative kinematic measures. This normalization controlled for differences in movement depth and enabled direct comparisons between groups at comparable task demands. All processing steps—including inspection, filtering, interpolation, event detection, and averaging—were double-checked for accuracy, and a second trained researcher independently verified a subset of trials to ensure reliability and reproducibility of the data.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test for all demographic and kinematic variables. Given the normality of the data distribution, the independent-samples t-tests were conducted to compare the MS and healthy control groups for each kinematic variable at two predefined knee joint angle positions: 60° and the maximum knee flexion angle for each comparison, 95% confidence intervals for the mean differences were calculated, and Cohen’s d effect sizes were reported to interpret the magnitude of observed group differences. An alpha level of 0.05 was set as the criterion for statistical significance, and all tests were two-tailed. No adjustments for multiple comparisons were made due to the exploratory nature of the analyses; however, the analyses were restricted to pre-defined variables and time points to minimize type I error. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Discussion

The present study examined lower limb and trunk kinematics during a FSD task in individuals with MS compared to healthy controls. The results revealed that participants with MS adopt a distinct integrated movement strategy, characterized by simultaneous alterations in trunk posture, hip alignment, knee flexion, and ankle rotation. These coordinated deviations likely reflect compensatory adaptations to neuromuscular impairments, including muscle weakness, spasticity, and impaired trunk control. Trunk flexion was observed in patients with MS, whereas healthy individuals exhibited trunk extension at both measured points. Specifically, healthy participants demonstrated trunk extension at 60 degrees of knee flexion and at maximum knee flexion during the FSD, while patients with MS showed trunk flexion. This finding highlights a compensatory strategy adopted by individuals with MS and underscores the complex interplay between neurological impairment and adaptive movement control in response to functional challenges. In healthy individuals, the normal pattern of trunk movement during a FSD task is characterized by a controlled and relatively stable trunk, often exhibiting slight trunk extension or maintaining a relatively upright position as the body descends. In a study by Morita et al. (2024) it was found that healthy women show trunk extension during FSD and women with patellofemoral pain show changes in trunk and lower limb sagittal movements during single-leg squat and step-down tasks [24]. To the best of our knowledge no study has been done about trunk kinematic in FSD but some studies have investigated the trunk control in people with MS. Several Studies have reported decreased trunk control in people with MS [25–27]. Lanzetta et al. examined angular displacement in the sagittal plane, and velocities of angular displacement during functional tasks accomplished in a sitting position and showed that trunk control diminished in people with MS when compared to healthy controls [28]. Using a core stability test Ozen et al. compared trunk control between people with MS with significant level of disability and healthy controls. They found trunk control, is weaker in people with MS than in healthy controls [25]. Ozkan et al. reported that trunk control as measured by trunk impairment scale is lower in people with MS compared to healthy participants [29]. In another study it was found that people with MS have more trunk sway compared to healthy controls [30]. In addition to muscle weakness in the lower extremities, trunk muscle weakness is also seen in people with MS, which causes trunk control disorders [31]. From a biomechanical perspective, trunk flexion during a FSD, considering that the trunk comprises approximately 50% of the body mass, is a powerful kinematic adjustment that redistributes loads. It tends to shift mechanical demand from the knee to the hip and the lumbar spine. Flexing the trunk causes the upper body mass to move forward. Since the upper body makes up a significant portion of total body mass, this results in the whole body’s Center of gravity (CoG) shifting anteriorly. A forward-shifted CoG may move closer to the anterior boundary of the base of support (BoS), especially during standing or walking. This can make maintaining postural stability more challenging, increasing the risk of forward falls. People with MS already have impaired postural control, so this shift can exacerbate balance issues.

In the hip joint at the moment of maximum knee flexion, individuals with MS had greater adduction in the frontal plane compared to the healthy group. One explanation for increased adduction in people with MS in comparison to healthy participants during FSD may be spasticity in hip adductors muscle group. More than 85% of people with MS are influenced by muscular spasticity [32]. Also hip adductors hyperactivity is common in these people [33]. Altered gait kinematics in frontal plane has been reported in a previous study by Pau et al. [34]. Not just kinematic but also quality of life can be affected by spasticity in people with MS [35]. Another explanation for increased adduction in people with MS could be abductor weakness. During weight-bearing tasks requiring lower extremity joints ‘flexion, the hip abductors act eccentrically for pelvis stabilization and femur mobility in the frontal plane [36]. Reduced eccentric hip abductors strength can lead to excessive hip adduction during single leg weight-bearing tasks. In this regard delayed onset of hip abductors activity in individuals with poor FSD test performance has been reported by Crossley et al. [37]. Morrison et al. believe that patients with MS have more difficulty activating the hip abductor muscles to maintain balance than healthy people [38]. Moreover, insufficient contraction or the decrease in passive tension of quadratus lumborum and oblique abdominal muscles can increase contralateral hip adduction [39]. It has been shown that strengthening exercises targeting hip lateral rotators, gluteus medius, latissimus dorsi, abdominal oblique and quadratus lumborum have the possibility to reduce hip adduction during step-down task in healthy women [40]. The authors attributed these frontal plane changes to an increase in hip abductor torque during the task, related to a possible improvement of the abductor muscles capacity to actively generate force and/or to passively resist motion [40]. At the knee joint at the moment of maximum knee flexion, individuals with MS had less flexion in the sagittal plane and less internal rotation in the horizontal plane compared to the healthy group.

No research has been done about knee kinematic comparisons between people with and without MS during FSD, but several studies have investigated the gait characteristics in these population. In a study impaired knee flexion in a non-weigh bearing situation during swing phase has been reported as the main kinematic feature of MS gait, reaching an accuracy of 83% in differentiating gait patterns of patients from controls [41]. Two other studies reported that impaired knee flexion is an important MS-associated gait characteristic [42, 43]. One explanation for decreased knee flexion in people with MS in comparison to healthy participants during FSD may be weakness of knee extensors muscle group. In a study by Wens et al. it was found that, mean muscle fiber CSA, as well as CSA of type I, II and IIa fibers of vastus lateralis were significantly smaller in MS patients compared to healthy group, independent of MS type and disease severity. Moreover, compared to healthy group, MS patients showed reduced isometric strength of the quadriceps muscle [44]. During FSD knee extensor muscles are loaded eccentrically, and people with MS may avoid to use them, because of their weakness. It has been shown that the eccentric strength of knee extensor muscles in people with MS is lower than healthy participants [45]. Increased muscle stiffness as a result of shortening and contracture of quadriceps muscle, which are the side effects of spasticity, can be another explanation [46]. In this regard Pau et al. reported abnormal activation of the rectus femoris and generalized reduced knee flexion across the whole gait in people with MS [34].

At the ankle joint at both 60 degrees and maximum knee flexion points, the MS group exhibited greater abduction in the horizontal plane than the healthy group. These findings may represent a compensatory mechanism or a direct consequence of the neurological impairments associated with MS. Understanding these biomechanical differences is crucial because altered ankle joint mechanics in MS patients can contribute to gait abnormalities, balance impairments, and an increased risk of falls. During weight-bearing movements, weakness in the hip abductors and external rotators leads to excessive hip adduction and internal rotation. This altered motion is transmitted distally through the kinematic chain, ultimately resulting in foot abduction in the horizontal plane [47]. In this regard Engkananuwat et al. showed that foot alignment is significantly affected by proximal muscle function [48]. This proximal-to-distal relationship produces a predictable pattern in which the foot assumes a compensatory position to maintain balance and stability when hip control is impaired. However, the cross-sectional design of this study limits causal inferences, and the lack of electromyography (EMG) data restricts our understanding of the precise muscle activation patterns underlying these kinematic adaptations. It should be emphasized that the proposed neuromuscular explanations for the observed kinematic alterations are based on existing literature and biomechanical reasoning, and should not be interpreted as causal relationships in the absence of direct clinical measures of muscle tone or strength. Future research should employ longitudinal designs, incorporate EMG assessments, and evaluate the effects of interventions on integrated trunk and lower limb kinematics in MS to inform more effective rehabilitation strategies.

We acknowledge that the current study has limitations that should be considered. One limitation of the present study is the relatively small number of trials per participant, which may restrict the ability to analyze within-subject variability—a factor especially relevant in individuals with MS due to fluctuations in neuromuscular control [49]. In addition, the MS group included participants with a range of disability levels (EDSS 1.0–4.5), which was defined as an inclusion criterion to ensure ambulatory ability; however, this heterogeneity may have influenced individual movement strategies and could not be examined in a severity-specific manner due to the limited sample size. Furthermore, kinematic analysis was restricted to two discrete, clinically relevant events (60° knee flexion and maximum knee flexion), which does not capture the continuous temporal characteristics of movement throughout the FSD task. Future studies should consider time-series waveform analyses or Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM) approaches to better characterize dynamic motor control strategies across the entire movement cycle. Additionally, while two key events (60° knee flexion and maximum knee flexion) were analyzed, other instants during the task, such as initial foot contact, could provide complementary insight into movement strategies and should be considered in future research. Addressing these aspects may improve the robustness of kinematic assessment and enhance understanding of functional adaptations in this population.

Conclusion

This study discovered distinct kinematic differences in trunk and lower extremity movement patterns between individuals with MS and healthy controls during a FSD task. The observed deviations likely reflect compensatory strategies in response to neuromuscular impairments and muscular changes commonly associated with MS. It should be noted, however, that as our analysis was confined to kinematic variables, interpretations regarding underlying neuromuscular mechanisms—such as spasticity or muscle weakness—remain speculative. Moreover, all trials were performed under controlled conditions with standardized rest periods to minimize the effects of acute fatigue, which is particularly relevant for the MS population. Understanding these biomechanical adaptations enhances our comprehension of functional limitations in MS and underscores the importance of targeted rehabilitation strategies aimed at improving trunk control, hip stability, and lower limb dynamic alignment, strength, and endurance, while acknowledging that individualized assessment and cautious interpretation are essential due to the heterogeneity of disease severity and motor function in this population.
