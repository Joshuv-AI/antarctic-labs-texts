“We knew it was coming: we didn’t act” perspectives from people who use drugs and substance use workforce on the impact of delays in implementation of the supervised injection facility and drug checking interventions
Authors: Margaret R Hester, Jaclyn M W Hughto, Divya Ravikumar-Grant, Saoirse Nic Gabhainn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHarm reduction techniques such as supervised injection facilities and drug checking are evidence-based strategies for reducing fatal overdoses. Despite the evidence in support of these interventions, Irish drug policy experienced delays in implementation of these strategies. This study considers the perceived impacts of delayed implementation of the supervised injection facility and drug checking in Ireland.MethodsA purposive sample of people who use drugs and people from the substance use workforce were recruited for a total of 34 semi-structured interviews. Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsOur results show perceived direct and secondary impacts of delayed implementation of the supervised injection facility and drug checking. Direct impacts of delays include the absence of evidence-based practices forcing people who use drugs to adopt make-shift safety responses and compounding the inequalities they experience. Participants expressed concern for the lives that could have been saved if the supervised injection facility and drug checking had been implemented, and the unknown scope of health consequences due to delays. Secondary impacts of delays noted the difficulties to respond to current issues or new challenges in the drug markets. Over the period of waiting for these interventions, participants reported changes in the drug market and drug use which may have implications for the existing policies. Participants described navigating frustration with bureaucracy and concern for moving forward with harm reduction.ConclusionChanges in drug use and drug markets occurred over a period of waiting for interventions to be implemented. Policymakers must consider adapting plans for a supervised injection facility to include inhalation space to meet the current needs of people who use drugs. Drug policy interventions must consider the populations most at risk, and should consider drug checking services outside of the festival setting. This study highlights that the nature of change in drug markets requires timely policy response and implementation. As drug markets become riskier, policymakers should consider framing overdose as a matter of public health urgency to facilitate timely harm reduction implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12954-025-01305-w.Keywords: Drug checking, Supervised injection facility, Harm reduction, Overdose prevention, Drug policy

Background

Rates of drug-related deaths in Ireland are among the highest in the European Union (E.U.), with a reported 97 drug related deaths per million population compared to an average of 22.5 deaths per million in E.U [1]. Ireland adopted a new national drug strategy in 2017 entitled ‘Reducing Harm, Supporting Recovery: A health-led response to drug and alcohol use in Ireland’. This national drug strategy distinguished itself from previous strategies through its integration of harm reduction approaches, including the strategic action item of implementing a pilot for a supervised or safe injection facility (SIF) and called for the establishment of a working group to examine the evidence regarding ‘back of house’ drug testing, a form of drug checking that does not offer analysis and interventions direct to the person who submitted substances for checking [2]. Drug checking and safe consumption spaces have been found to be effective strategies for reducing overdose risk and promoting safer drug use behaviours [3, 4]. This paper focuses on two interventions highlighted in Ireland’s national drug strategy for 2017–2025 [2], drug checking and the SIF, and the impact of delayed access from the perspective of people who use drugs (PWUD) and members of the substance use workforce (SUW).

Although the first legal approval for the SIF was granted in 2017, due a variety of legislative and community barriers, this facility did not open until December 2024 [5, 6]. Previous research regarding the SIF in Ireland has identified barriers to implementation, including inadequate political will and stigma, in combination with legislative challenges [7, 8]. Specifically due to objections from the public, a school, businesses, and representatives that nullified the temporary planning permission initially granted in 2019 [8].

While a working group established to explore drug checking saw the value of the intervention across a variety of settings, it was first piloted within the Irish context in 2022 at a music festival known as Electric Picnic [9]. The initial pilot in 2022 had planned on operating in three festival settings, however, it was implemented at one festival during that time due to delays in processing the proposals [9]. This pilot expanded and operated at four music festivals in 2024 [10]. In a ‘back of house’ model, festival patrons were offered the opportunity to test their personal supply by forfeiting a portion of their drugs for analysis and public health communication through the use of amnesty bins, referred to as ‘surrender’ bins at the festival. This approach differs from a “front of house” approach, which offers information regarding the contents of drugs directly to the person seeking this service and tailored interventions, such as guidance to adjust their use dependent on potency or adulterants identified [9]. Recently, Ireland has engaged with other members of the EU to implement the CrissCross EU pilot programme, which includes efforts to expand drug testing in nightlife recreational settings [10]. The review of the initial pilot stated that if the ‘back of house’ system yielded positive results, drug checking that allows for a ‘front of house’ approach that involves direct information and tailored interventions for the person seeking services should be considered [9]. As of September 2025, drug checking services have not been made available for PWUD outside the festival context.

SIFs and drug checking interventions are considered evidence-based strategies; however, research evidence is not always central to policy implementation. A study of drug policymakers in Australia revealed that academic literature was among the least utilised source of research evidence when seeking information for decision-making [11]. Within the Irish drug policy context, a 2011 study of the research-policy interface in Ireland found that research was just one aspect of the policy-making process [12]. Additional factors, such as public opinion and emotions tied to stereotypes and morality, were found to play a role in influencing the direction of policymaking [12]. Furthermore, this study found that even in cases where policy was developed in Ireland, there was often a gap in implementation due to insufficient allocation of resources [12]. It has been argued that harm reduction drug policy in Ireland operates from a place of ambiguity as a tool to manage public perceptions of the approach [13].

While the role of research in the policy process is mostly understood, little health literature considers why policy delays occur. In the field of environmental governance, the politics of delay is driven by a variety of mechanisms, such as redirecting responsibility, focusing on individualism, or pushing for solutions that do not cause disruptive change, among others, to legitimise delay and inaction in policy implementation [14, 15]. Literature around delay is sometimes described as political in nature, with research from the US found delay as a tool to stall policy from previous presidential administrations that may not align with the current administration’s agenda [16]. Conversely, research from Switzerland has found that delays in legislative mandates is not a result of gatekeeping timely implementation, but rather attributed long implementation periods to a variety of factors such as busy federal offices or challenges navigating controversial issues [17]. A number of frameworks exist to offer insight into the policy process, such as the multiple streams framework [18], or policy actors, such as the advocacy coalition framework [19], but no such framework exists to consider the impact of delayed policies.

Although barriers to harm reduction are well studied, little is understood, to our knowledge, about the impact of delayed harm reduction policy implementation. In Spain, the delayed implementation of harm reduction programmes in Madrid in the 1990s is believed to have contributed to HIV prevalence among long term injectors while other cities such as Barcelona and Seville experienced a decrease in HIV prevalence during the same period [20]. Unevenly implemented harm reduction services have also shown to impact PWUD in Sweden. While Sweden has implemented harm reduction interventions, such as Housing First models introduced in 2010 and syringe service programmes as far back as 1986, PWUD in a small city without access to these services develop their own strategies for their drug use which may increase public health risk, such as reusing injection equipment [21]. Changes in drug policy regarding the availability of harm reduction services are known to have impacts on PWUD. For example, the suspension of a syringe service programme in West Virginia, U.S. due to drug policy changes resulted in PWUD describing riskier injection practices and decreased naloxone access [22]. Drug policy changes can also have protective effects on communities. McClellan et al. (2018) reported that U.S. states with drug policies such as naloxone access and Good Samaritan laws, which offer legal protections to people who call emergency services in response to an overdose, are associated with reductions in overdose deaths [23, 24].

Changes and delays in drug policy may have impacts on PWUD, and delays are documented in Ireland. Alcohol policy in Ireland has experienced implementation challenges, with research noting little progress made in enacting policy changes advocated for by the health sector from 1990 to 2010 [25]. Change for medications for opioid use disorder also took time in Ireland. Buprenorphine-naloxone products, such as suboxone, was first licensed for use in 2006 in Ireland [26], but was not available country-wide until 2016 due to time spent on feasibility studies, and legislative delays [27]. Research has shown historically limited access of buprenorphine-naloxone to specific patient cohorts in Ireland [28]. Frontline services such as Merchants Quay played an important role in advocating for its expansion [29] and stakeholders recommended emergency protocols from the COVID-19 pandemic remain in place to allow for greater patient choice in treatment options [30]. This stepwise approach to policy implementation poses challenges for both service providers and PWUD.

Unintended consequences of public health policies have been found to be related to factors such as poor policy design or inadequate use of the evidence base [31]; however, little is known about the impact of delayed harm reduction policies. To explore this topic, in depth, semi-structured interviews were conducted with PWUD and members of the SUW to explore harm reduction delays regarding the SIF and drug checking interventions in Ireland. These interviews occurred in Autumn 2023, representing a period after drug testing was first piloted in recreational settings in Ireland and a year prior to the SIF opening. Understanding the impact of delays from the perspective of the SUW and PWUD can not only inform future public health intervention in Ireland but may prove helpful for other countries that are navigating similar hurdles. The findings of this study can be utilised to better understand the impact of delaying the implementation of harm reduction interventions and to inform future policy and practice interventions to reduce drug-related harms, including fatal and non-fatal overdoses.

Methods

Between October 2023 and January 2024, 15 PWUD and 19 from the SUW were purposively sampled through internet recruitment in Ireland. While it was not mandatory to be located in Dublin to participate in the study, most participants reported being located in or around the Dublin area. Participants completed in-depth semi-structured interviews focused on harm reduction policy and interventions, including questions on perceived impact of delays in the implementation of a SIF and drug checking interventions. The interview guide was developed in partnership with a Community Advisory Board consisting of PWUD and SUW.

Recruitment

Using purposive convenience sampling, participants were recruited through virtual and in-person means. Virtual recruitment was conducted through email circulation of the study recruitment flyer through professional networks identified by the Community Advisory Board and author networks. Social media posts on platforms such as Instagram and X were also shared. In-person recruitment was conducted solely through street outreach conducted by UISCE, the National Advocacy Service for People who use Drugs in Ireland. in Dublin.

This study recruited two distinct populations: PWUD and SUW. Prior to interviewing participants, each participant was screened to determine which population they fell within. People were eligible to participate in the study as PWUD participants if they were [1] 18 years of age or older; [2] able to read in English; and [3] used an illicit substance other than cannabis or ecstasy in the last two months. People were eligible to participate as SUW participants if they were [1] 18 years of age or older; [2] able to read and write in English; and [3] working in substance use service provision, healthcare, or advocacy in Ireland for a period of a year or longer. One participant fit the inclusion criteria of both participant groups. To reduce the possibility of identification due to the small sample sizes, this individual’s demographic information was included among PWUD. All the interviews were conducted one-to-one with one exception. One participant was a non-native English speaker and requested a friend who was aware of their drug use to serve as the translator for certain technical terms that were not commonly used in conversational English. All study procedures were reviewed and approved by the researcher’s university’s Research Ethics Committee. Verbal informed consent was obtained from the participants prior to reviewing the study plain language form and once again at the start of the recorded interview. Interviews were conducted by two researchers trained and experienced in qualitative methodologies. Interviews were conducted in a private office space provided by a substance use organisation, via Zoom or by telephone. Interviews were approximately 45 min in length. The interview guide was comprised of four sections: [1] Harm Reduction and Drug Policy; [2] Overdose Prevention Sites; [3] Drug Checking interventions; and [4] Demographic information. The Interview guide can be seen in “Additional File 1.” All PWUD were compensated 10 euros in cash for their time and expertise. Participant identification numbers from data collection were replaced with pseudonyms to present the findings to humanise the data and further protect participants’ identities.

Data analysis

A total of 34 semi-structured interviews were conducted. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed. Transcripts were coded using NVivo and analysed using reflexive thematic analysis, as this method offers a way to synthesise the voices of participants into themes while acknowledging the ways in which researchers interpret and bring meaning to data [32]. The six-step process developed by Braun and Clarke was followed: familiarisation, coding, generation of initial themes, the review and development of themes, refinement and naming of themes, and writing up [33]. The initial step of familiarisation was achieved through reviews of the transcripts and field notes prior to commencing coding. The coding process began with the inductive coding of three transcripts each by two team members, MRH and CAF, with one overlapping transcript to develop initial codes. All remaining transcripts were coded by MRH, with continuous discussion with CAF to discuss new codes generated and the application of previous codes to ensure that the codes captured the nuance and meaning from the data. Upon completion of coding all transcripts, MRH and CAF explored relationships between codes through further discussion to develop a set of initial emerging themes. The final themes were reviewed by MRH, JHW, MH, and SNG and refined through comparison and discussion. Throughout the data collection and analysis process, the research team engaged in reflexive discussions to acknowledge the role of the researcher in the collection and interpretation of data.

Direct impact of delays

Make-shift safety responses.

In the absence of evidence-based services such as SIF or drug checking, PWUD have adopted make-shift safety responses. These strategies vary from evidence-based practices to techniques that can be dangerous. While public drug use was described in terms of stigma or shame, it was also viewed as a safety technique as there is the chance of someone walking by in the event of an overdose. Several participants disclosed using with someone else present, such a friend or in a friend’s home. While these homes may not offer the healthcare services of a SIF, they were viewed as safer than using alone and safer than being outside:

I know a load of people that have their houses and they are actually, like, mainly just crack houses or gear houses…they all come in and use it because it’s a safe—it’s not a safe environment, but it’s an environment that you are not sitting out in the rain, in the lashings of rain. Or if you did OD, at least there is somebody there to help ring an ambulance, get you what you need … if you do that out in the rain on your own, that’s it, you’re gone.

Steve, PWUD, male

At times, the nature of poly-drug use was described as a makeshift safety response among PWUD. Some participants described the use of heroin in an attempt to adjust, minimise, or ‘come down’ from their crack cocaine use. The complex relationship between heroin and crack cocaine use was revealed by a participant who described his experience with overdose:

Because I OD’ed twice … twice. And I was actually glad that my best friend was with me. Only for him, I would be dead. He was the one who brought me back around … and do you know how he brought me back around? By sitting me up, putting a pipe in my mouth and putting crack in it. Boom, I was wide awake.

Damon, PWUD, male

Some participants expressed frustration regarding the lack of stop-gap measures during periods of policy delay, such as the ongoing restrictions surrounding naloxone access in Ireland. In the face of delays, one participant argued for the importance of developing additional safety measures until the SIF could be implemented:

Oh, well, it's just absolutely shocking that if you can't open it [the supervised injection facility] then really the Department should have had a Plan B to support people that were—like, you can’t come along and say we need a safe injecting facility to help all these people that are vulnerable and on the street but there’s been a delay, but we’re not going to do anything for them in the meantime. That was absolutely outrageous.

Cassandra, SUW, female

Compounding inequalities

Participants reported high levels of marginalisation faced by PWUD, particularly those who use substances such as heroin or crack cocaine or who experience homelessness. The marginalisation of PWUD was described as a factor in delays, as participants felt PWUD are rarely viewed as a policy priority. Participants argued that these delays compound the inequalities faced by marginalised groups. Participants frequently referenced specific diseases, circumstances, geographical areas, or social status in which they believed these types of delays would not occur. The differences in care across conditions and social status noted by participants caused them emotional distress:

They really, really need to push to get this [SIF] done. If you don’t get it done, just don’t let even more people die; and only homeless people and it really does get to me. It does.

Damon, PWUD, male

The delayed implementation of the SIF as viewed as contributing further to the stigmatisation and marginalisation of PWUD. Participants described the delays in implementation of the SIF as highlighting a stigma toward PWUD:

So I think by not having it you are having a countereffect; it is further stigmatising people. Another generation is stigmatised because it’s taken that long. That is seven years in the waiting [for the SIF to be implemented], seven years of people dying, of being wrapped up in addiction, not receiving the-. I mean, you wouldn’t have waited seven years to deal with Covid. You dealt with it quite-. I know it is a bit unfair because one is a global pandemic. But I think drug deaths is a public health issue.

Toby, SUW, male

The levels of marginalisation for PWUD were described as being related to the drug of choice. Participants expressed that recreational drug use, such as the occasional use of powder cocaine, is viewed through a more forgiving lens than the use of drugs such as heroin or crack cocaine.

Some participants expressed frustration regarding the pilot of drug testing facilities at a music festival as they considered recreational drug use to carry lower risk of death or harm than street-level drug use does. Social standing associated with the type of drug use was perceived to play a role in the availability of resources:

I think if you’re looking at drug testing in festivals, again it’s a certain class, it’s a certain type of person… there's a certain cohort that go to festivals and because it's recreational, it's different. Because the coke is in powder form and it's not smoking the crack cocaine, it's different. But even the powder form, if someone was to inject that powder, again they wouldn't be in that cohort … I think, number one, it's great that we can start breaking down barriers and looking at the stigma attached to drugs but I still think people are looking at certain types of drugs and certain types of drug users.

Adam, SUW, male

Grappling with health consequences

The SIF and drug checking were viewed by participants as vital tools in preserving health and life. While it was recognised that it would be unlikely that all PWUD would avail of these interventions, participants believed that a significant number of lives could have been saved, and health preserved, if these interventions had been implemented in a timely manner:

I think they should have the option to [check their drugs] because there's a lot of them being poisoned…it is getting worse really… That hostel over there, nearly every night of the week you see a fucking ambulance outside the door.

Henry, PWUD, male

Participants went on to discuss other health consequences of drug use that could have been averted with wider harm reduction implementation, such as preventing the spread of infections (e.g., HIV, hepatitis C) and serving as a point of contact for supporting mental health needs. In the face of discussing loss and other negative health outcomes, many participants grew distressed that these interventions had not been implemented:

Quite simply, people are dying. I think it's average we have an overdose fatality every day in Ireland. So, yes, it's as simple as that. Like we're losing people. People are- I think in the last maybe three months we've amputated two limbs in the hospital due to injecting drug use, DVTs, and … they're going in and hitting their femoral artery and DVTs within their legs. So, yes, it's like the proof is there that people need a safe space to inject, end of. So, yes, it just beggars’ belief why it's not open … But, again, do we care enough about these people that are losing their legs or are dying? I don't know.

Cameron, SUW, male

Secondary impact of delays

Dealing with current issues

An increase in crack cocaine use was mentioned by all interview participants. It was reported as a significant change in the landscape of drug use, particularly in comparison to the 2017 landscape when the SIF was first approved. These changes in drug trends raised the issue of how interventions may need to be altered to meet the needs of people who use crack cocaine, such as changing the SIF to include an inhalation room. Approaches to implementing this change varied, with some participants proposing a ‘step-by-step’ approach to safe or supervised consumption space implementation, while also recognising that drug trends have changed in the face of delayed implementation:

I think because it's been so long in the design that now, what's after happening is Ireland … crack cocaine has hit Ireland like snow. So therefore it should be safe consumption rooms rather than injecting rooms. Because it's taken so long to get this off the ground, time has evolved and the drug trend has evolved and now we need safe consumption rooms rather than safe injecting rooms. But you know, rather than throw the toys out of the pram, just go with safe injecting rooms for the moment to get them up and running and have them established and then, you know, we can start challenging the safe consumption.

Lauren, SUW, female

Some participants argued for a stepwise approach to policy progress, others expressed frustration and urgency. These participants argued that it was not adequate to implement what was originally planned for the SIF in 2017; rather, adapting to the current drug use patterns was required. The prevalence of smoking of crack cocaine made some participants feel it was vital to shift from an injection centre to consumptions spaces:

Like, anybody who knows anything about it, even if they opened up a safe injecting centre in the back of Merchant's Quay1 now, it is not going to solve the problem of what's happening out on the street because most of the people who are congregating out on the street and around that area are all smoking crack, and you can’t smoke in the safe injecting centre, which is just total rubbish, you know. Like, again it's to doing things piecemeal and it's thinking that we get one little piece in well then we'll be able to do the next piece and the next piece. But again that's what people [those who oppose safe injecting centres] are afraid of. They're afraid of if we let this piece in, well then, they'll look to do this and this and this.

-Grant, SUW, male

Anticipating new challenges

Both study populations perceived rapid recent changes within Irish drug markets. Changes in drug markets and patterns of drug use were viewed as a factor that impacted the relevance and urgency of harm reduction policies. Both groups described the variability of the drug market, and SUW noted the challenges associated with responding to these trends.

Concerns about synthetic opioids were discussed in 28 of 34 interviews conducted. Much of the conversation revolved around the synthetic opioid analogue fentanyl. The participants noted how both previous research and policymakers stated that there were no synthetic opioids present in the drug supply at the time of data collection. However, most SUW believed it was only a ‘matter of time’ before synthetic opioids became prominent in the drug supply in Ireland.

Among PWUD, the majority of participants believed that synthetic opioids were already present in the drug supply. Some PWUD also experienced frustration with this topic, and reported that it felt as though their expertise and lived experience were not believed by other stakeholders:

…we know people that have fentanyl. And we told them we know people. I have seen it with my own eyes, and I have touched the bag with the fentanyl in it and you're telling me it's not in Dublin. Categorically it's not. Categorically it's not? What about the two people that OD'd in my hostel? One of them is dead on fentanyl.

Simon, PWUD, male

Many participants anticipated that synthetic opioids would bring a new level of unpredictability and danger to the drug market, which would increase the benefit of access to interventions such as the SIF and drug checking. Concerns for synthetic opioids in drugs other than heroin were clear, and the potentially dangerous impact of consumption when someone is opioid naïve was also expressed. One participant described their belief that synthetic opioids could be found in the street tablet and crack cocaine supplies:

Yeah, fentanyl, it's destroying America and all, isn't it? It's very strong. Yeah [I worry about it], it's in the Tranex and all, in the street tablets … They are putting it into everything. They even tried to wash it in with the crack and it was killing people so they had to stop … I know for a fact that they tried to wash it up with crack.

-Jack, PWUD, male

Concerns for synthetic opioids often elicited a sense of urgency and participants considered this an opportunity to quickly improve Irish drug policy. In some cases, it was reported that the presence of synthetics could serve as a facilitator to implement a more robust harm reduction strategy:

Safe injecting facilities can save lives now, right now, today, not in six months' time or not while we run a pilot programme for a year. Today. They're needed today. Synthetic opioids, they're coming, they're on the streets as is and people are going to die, and lives will be lost.

Sean, SUW, male

Navigating frustration

Both PWUD and SUW participants expressed the feeling that Ireland’s drug policy struggled to shift and respond to emerging changes in the drug market. The policy systems within Ireland were perceived to be rigid in comparison to the unpredictability and variability of the drug market:

You know, it's bureaucracy. The HSE [Health Service Executive, the national body responsible for health service delivery] is very … I don't know if it's innately conservative, but it finds change very, very difficult and the process of change is incredibly slow.

Richard, SUW, male

While the change within systems was perceived to be slow, participants believed that the drug market changed quickly. Many participants reported early warning signs for certain drug trends that they perceived were ignored. In the case of crack cocaine, participants expressed frustration regarding the lack of preparation from policymakers:

I feel, as people working in the area, we allowed the crack cocaine epidemic to take place. We knew it was coming. We didn't act. I think I remember national media about three years ago saying the country is on the verge of a crack cocaine epidemic; the fact is everyone in the country working in that area knew we were three years into that before we decided to act.

Sean, SUW, male

Many interviewees mentioned how policy could shift quickly in certain circumstances. Examples of quick responses were cases such as COVID-19 or HIV response. This ability to change at a faster pace was believed to be motivated by circumstances when the impacts are on the general population, and not restricted to PWUD:

But I do think it was very much driven by that public concern that … it was really once HIV started spreading to the general population. They were like, ‘oh we better do something now’ because actually there was like a complete hierarchy where it was like drug users, they can die all they want; gay men, they can die all they want; migrants, they can die all they want. And then once the general population started getting [HIV], they were like, okay, we better do something.

Natalie, SUW, female

Participants were asked to express their views on Irish drug policy. Negative perceptions of Irish drug policy were often expressed by comparing Ireland’s policies or interventions with those of other countries. When asked about his perspective on how Ireland responds to drug use, one participant responded:

Disgraceful…Yis2 are about 20 years behind any other God given country.

Jake, PWUD, male

Participants described harm reduction policies in other countries as a reference point to where Ireland stands in the context of global harm reduction. The range of comparisons varied in complexity, ranging from easily accessible sharps containers across a city and expanding naloxone access, to more sophisticated harm reduction measures such as safe or supervised consumption spaces, drug checking interventions, low-threshold treatment, and decriminalisation. Participants expressed frustration with lack of access to some interventions available in other jurisdictions:

I just thought with Electric Picnic [music festival], I just thought that was all a gimmick just to say, oh look at what we are doing here in Ireland. Look at the rest of the world, man, is ten years ahead of all of us on all this … Do you know what I mean? If everybody, if they all just got together and just done something, like. Like, over in England there's a load of safe houses in England, America, Australia, everywhere you go in the world.

Steve, PWUD, male

The perception of being behind other countries also served as a marker of what trends could be coming to Ireland. Participants expressed beliefs that drug trends happening in the U.K. and the U.S. could serve as indicators of what Ireland can expect in the future. Participants specifically referenced levels of crack cocaine use and the contamination of crack supply with fentanyl in the U.S. as early indicators of the drug market emerging in Ireland. Participants believed that the rise in synthetic opioids occurring in the U.K. and the long-term proliferation in the U.S. would eventually make its way to the Irish drug market.

…anything that happens in England, it's only a matter of time to come over to Ireland, and then Northern Ireland, I class Northern Ireland, Ireland. You know, I know technically it's not but it's only up the road… What makes you think that if Northern Ireland and England have these problems, that we are not going to have them. What do you think, we have a magical cloud? Like, oh, Fentanyl is not allowed in here around us.

Lauren, SUW, female

Discussion

This is the first study, to our knowledge, to explore the perception of impacts of delayed harm reduction policies in Ireland. This study adds to the literature by offering a qualitative analysis from interviews with people who use drugs [PWUD] and members of the substance use workforce [SUW] in Ireland to offer insights related to the current landscape of drug policy delays, and the perceived direct and secondary impacts of these delays. The views of the two study groups are important as their combined expertise offers insights into the current harm reduction landscape. Furthermore, the involvement of people most impacted by policy and the process of policy development in harm reduction can help mitigate potential unintended or harmful consequences of an intervention [31].

Harm reduction initiatives such as SIFs and drug checking were viewed by participants as important tools for preserving life, yet these interventions faced delays within the Irish policy context, similar to those reported in Scotland [34–36]. Participants reported direct impacts of these delays, with PWUD creating makeshift safety responses when left without resources. In addition, concern for compounded inequalities, and unknown extent of negative health consequences was raised. Secondary impacts of delayed implementation were also identified, with participants describing struggles with dealing with current issues and the anticipation of new challenges. Participants expressed frustration, with concerns raised about bureaucracy and Ireland’s place in global harm reduction. While health consequences emerged as a direct impact of delay, the potential for health consequences, such as overdose risk, weaves through both direct and secondary impacts. The findings illustrate the expressed need for timely harm reduction interventions in Ireland in order to save health and lives in the context of changing drug use patterns and the availability of more lethal drugs in Ireland.

In the face of harm reduction delays, participants noted how PWUD adopted their own safety responses. While community resilience has often been studied in relation to substance use prevention, there is a growing body of work examining resilience among PWUD, particularly as it relates to resistance to negative health outcomes [37]. This research highlights some examples of community resilience to avoid adverse health outcomes through evidence-based strategies. For example, the practice of using in public spaces or with other people to avoid overdosing alone was mentioned by participants, aligning with similar findings among PWUD navigating the U.S. overdose crisis by using near or around others [38]. However, not all strategies used by PWUD are evidence-based or safe, highlighting the need to expand harm reduction services. Makeshift medicine can emerge when there are barriers to care or limited resources for marginalised groups, such as PWUD, and has the potential to carry health-risks due to the informal nature of any care provided [39].

Increased access to overdose education and naloxone distribution would be beneficial for combating misinformation and offering evidence-based strategies to PWUD in the absence of harm reduction services such as safe or supervised consumption spaces or drug checking is essential. Such an approach may be possible within the ongoing reform of Ireland’s health and social care systems under Sláintecare, which promises to ‘Strengthen preparedness for drug overdoses in light of the emergence of new synthetic drugs and increase availability and training in naloxone’ under its Social Inclusion plan for 2024 [40].

Reports of compounding inequalities were raised across participant groups. Inequitable access to drug checking technology was highlighted as a social class issue among PWUD, and participants highlighted how recreational drug use has greater access to protection than other types of drug use. Drug-checking remains limited in scope within the Irish context and is not readily available to high-risk populations. This aligns with previous findings that people who access drug checking or testing services are often well-educated young men [41–43]. Research has found people experiencing homelessness are more likely to experience a fatal overdose than the general population in Dublin [44]. The impact of substance use of choice on perceived levels of social acceptability has previously been noted in Irish substance use research. Research from Ballymun, a traditionally disadvantaged neighbourhood in Dublin, with people who use crack cocaine, found that participants expressed lower levels of societal acceptability related to their drug use compared to the use of powder cocaine or alcohol [45]. Our findings support these perceived inequities related to substance use of choice and the spectrum of stigma experienced by PWUD. Ongoing work is needed to ensure that comprehensive supports and services are available to PWUD regardless of their substance of choice or social class background. Further research should be conducted to understand these levels of stigma by specific substance types to improve harm reduction accessibility and equity.

These findings highlight how the nature of drug use has changed and how adaptations to supervised injection facilities may be necessary to deal with current issues facing PWUD. Participants reported an increase in the smoking of crack cocaine, and argued that a consumption space that offers assistance for injecting only may miss an important cohort of PWUD. These findings are in line with data released by the Health Research Board in Ireland, highlighting a 594% increase in people seeking treatment for crack cocaine from 2017 to 2023 [46]. With an increase in crack cocaine use, it is important to adapt harm reduction interventions to meet this need. A scoping review of the international literature reported readiness to utilise inhalation rooms in supervised consumption spaces among PWUD [47]. These findings add to the literature on understanding crack cocaine use within Ireland and how harm reduction interventions need to be adapted to suit the needs of this population. Further community consultation should be conducted regarding the need for and acceptability of inhalation rooms.

Participants anticipated synthetic opioids as a new challenge that would increase the urgency for timely implementation of policies such as the SIF and drug checking. Participants had concerns primarily in relation to synthetic opioids such as fentanyl. In October 2023, when data collection commenced for this research, national conversation had once again ignited regarding the synthetic opioid fentanyl due to comments made by Taoiseach Leo Varadkar and the head of the Garda National Drugs and Organised Crime Bureau indicating fentanyl would likely enter the Irish drug supply [48]. While the presence of synthetic opioids in the drug market at time of data collection was questioned, overdose spikes due to nitazenes, a type of synthetic opioid, occurred in Dublin towards the end of the data collection period, confirming the concerns expressed by participants regarding synthetic opioids [49, 50]. However, these spikes were not due to the analogue discussed in the interviews, fentanyl. Several participants reported that synthetic opioids were being found in substances other than opioids, such as street tablets or crack cocaine. This adulteration of drugs other than opioids has been reported in other countries, with the synthetic opioid fentanyl being found in numerous substances in North America [51–53]. The presence of synthetic opioids calls for more timely harm reduction policy implementation.

These results highlight the need for the expansion of drug checking technologies outside festival settings. Furthermore, the reports of synthetic opioids in substances other than opioids is concerning due to the increased risk of overdose among opioid-naïve PWUD [54]. Expanded monitoring of the drug market is required to monitor the situation. Drug checking techniques, such as test strips, have been found to be effective harm reduction measures, particularly in instances where a specific adulterant has not overtaken the supply [55–57]. While Ireland deliberates the introduction of sophisticated and accessible drug checking technologies, the use of test strips should be seriously considered as an evidence-based intervention that does not need planning permission for a fixed site to implement.

Participants expressed frustration over the pace of change in Irish drug policy. These findings support and expand upon prior alcohol and drug policy research in Ireland, which highlights a lack of political movement and the use of small incremental steps toward change [7, 58, 59]. Introducing change in drug policy was described as ‘piece-meal’ by some participants, preventing the necessary nimbleness to respond to emerging trends as they occurred in the drug market and drawing comparisons to harm reduction practices in other countries compared to Ireland. Despite these perceptions from participants of trailing behind other countries in harm reduction, these struggles are not unique to the Irish context. Similar struggles have been reported in Scotland, where scholars have described how the political climate has created barriers to translating evidence-based practices into drug policy [60]. In the U.S., the various federal, state, and local policy systems have been characterised as limiting the reach of harm reduction policy and practice [61].This incremental approach to change in policy has been previously observed, with scholars describing cautious and slow harm reduction policy mobilisation in a suburban municipality in Greater Vancouver in 2016 [62]. Similar frustrations were identified a few years later in a 2020 Canadian commentary which argued that harm reduction services could deliver more dynamic and comprehensive responses to emerging trends before they are formally recognised and delivered by policymakers or institutionalised structures [63].

For both the SIF and drug checking, process issues, such as planning permission and processing of proposals caused delays in implementation [5, 8, 9]. Legislation to implementation gaps have occurred in other areas of health in Ireland. In 2018, a popular referendum repealed the constitutional ban on abortion. Despite the legal approval for access to abortion, challenges such as logistical and infrastructure barriers were found to limit access in 2020 [64] and it took until 2025 for all 19 maternity hospitals in the country to provide abortion services [65].Recommendations from the Citizens’ Assembly on Drugs Use align with these measures, calling for greater action to improve the State’s response to substance use, noting “there is clear evidence that the State’s response continues to be hindered by delays, inaction, lack of policy innovation, under-investment, policy incoherence, and the need for more effective leadership at all levels” (p. 9) [66]. The Joint Commission on Drug Use released a response to these recommendations in October 2024, agreeing with the need for urgent action, including calls for expansion of SIFs and consumption rooms across the country and offering drug checking interventions at the community-level [67].

The framing of an issue as an urgent matter of public health may help to facilitate timely policy response and action [68, 69]. Ireland's response to COVID-19 highlights how political processes can assist in the progress of public health infrastructure. Planning permission processes were alleviated during the pandemic, allowing for rapid implementation of public health infrastructure for testing and vaccinations [70]. During this time, Ireland was able to rapidly respond to the needs of people who use drugs with increased access to methadone and the expansion of naloxone distribution [71]. COVID-19 was described as a catalyst for these changes, which had long been advocated for prior to the pandemic. Similarly, it was not until alcohol use was successfully reframed as a public health issue in combination with a significant shift in political leadership that change came in alcohol policy in Ireland [59]. Given the potential for a more lethal drug supply, the framing of drug policy as a matter of urgency may assist timely harm reduction implementation in Ireland.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations. This study intended to recruit PWUD and SUW, including policy-makers, however, no participant disclosed working on drug policy directly. This study did not capture demographic information beyond age, gender, and locality, and therefore may have missed important perspectives from groups experiencing marginalisation, such as Travellers3 or the LGBTQ + community. As this research was conducted in English, the perspectives of PWUD in Ireland who do not speak English were not captured. This study used a mixture of online and in-person recruitment for both populations. However, online recruitment was not successful for PWUD, and participants were recruited with the aid of a substance use organisation in Dublin. This may limit the transferability of our findings to other communities in Ireland.

These findings offer insight into a moment in time before the SIF was implemented and months after the initial drug checking pilot was conducted. Perspectives of participants may not align with the realities of the current drug market, policy development, and implementation. It is also important to note that qualitative research is not intended to determine if participants' perspectives are factual, but rather to capture the subjective experience of participants to offer context and understanding of a social phenomenon [72]. Participants in this study expressed frustration and concern for Ireland’s pace of harm reduction and felt as though they could not wait longer for these services. That being said, in our discussion of the results, we have taken care to describe any advances or shared challenges in the availability of harm reduction services related to our findings in order to accurately characterise the current state of harm reduction services in Ireland.

Conversations regarding synthetic opioids were focused mainly on fentanyl rather than other synthetic opioid analogues, such as nitazenes. The primary interviewer during data collection is American, which may have brought the US fentanyl contamination crisis to front of mind for interviewers and participants.

This research describes the perceived impact of delays in certain harm reduction services among PWUD and SUW, and as such, does not offer insight into the mechanisms behind those delays. Further research will be necessary to identify those challenges faced by services implementing harm reduction interventions.

Despite these limitations, this research has strengths. It addresses a live policy issue in Ireland that is also relevant to other settings. The interview guide was developed with input from key stakeholders in the substance use community with a Community Advisory Board comprised of professionals and people with lived or living experience of drug use. A robust sample of participants from both population groups were recruited. The interviewers were experienced in working with marginalised community members in interview skills and procedures. This research was conducted during a pivotal time for drug policy in Ireland, with data collection overlapping with the Citizens’ Assembly on Drugs Use and thus issues of drug policy and harm reduction were being openly and regularly discussed in the public realm.

Conclusion

While the research to policy gap is well-understood, this research highlights how delayed implementation of harm reduction strategies such as SIF and drug checking incurs both direct and secondary impacts on PWUD and SUW. Drug checking and supervised consumption spaces are evidence-based strategies to reduce the risk of health threats and death, however, policy implementation in Ireland has faced considerable delays.

Implementation delays have direct and secondary impacts which deserve further research and policy attention. There is an urgent need to adapt the existing and future supervised injection facilities to include an inhalation room and, more generally, should move toward collaboration and consultation with the PWUD and sector experts to improve the relevance and timeliness of policy implementation. Policymakers need should confirm their commitment to prioritise the needs of those at greatest risk of overdose, through the practical application of the principles of equity and inclusion.
