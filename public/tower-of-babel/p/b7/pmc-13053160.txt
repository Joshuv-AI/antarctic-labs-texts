Unraveling the Dynamics of Oxytocin in Hypothalamic Neurons
Authors: Beatriz Aznar‐Escolano, Vera Egorova, José Villanueva, Luis Miguel Gutiérrez, Virginia González‐Vélez, Amparo Gil, Sandra Jurado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOxytocin plays a critical role in social behavior and maternal physiology, yet the intracellular dynamics of oxytocin‐containing vesicles in neurons remain poorly characterized. Here, we combine experimental data from live cell imaging of oxytocin‐containing compartments with computational analysis to investigate their mobility within hypothalamic neurons. Using machine learning‐based trajectory classification, we reveal that the majority of oxytocin compartments exhibit subdiffusive motion, suggesting constraints imposed by the complex intracellular environment. This behavior likely reflects interactions with cytoskeletal structures, vesicle maturation states, or localized functional demands. Our findings provide new insights into the intracellular trafficking of neuropeptides and highlight the utility of data‐driven approaches for uncovering mechanisms of neurophysiological relevance.Keywords: anomalous diffusion, hypothalamic neurons, large dense‐core vesicles (LDCVs), machine learning, neuropeptide trafficking, oxytocin, single‐particle tracking (SPT)

Introduction

Oxytocin (OT) is a crucial neuropeptide involved in regulating essential physiological processes, such as childbirth and lactation, as well as complex behaviors, including social interaction [1, 2]. Its misregulation has been related to brain and social disorders such as autism and anxiety [3, 4].

OT is primarily synthesized in the hypothalamus, where it is stored in large dense‐core vesicles (LDCVs) within both magnocellular and parvocellular neurons. Magnocellular neurons, located in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) and the supraoptic nucleus (SON), project their axons to the posterior pituitary and extend dendrites within the brain, enabling the release of large quantities of the neuropeptide [5]. In contrast, parvocellular neurons, which are concentrated mainly in the PVN, send their dendrites into the brain, while their axons project to the median eminence, brainstem, and spinal cord, thereby mediating a wide range of central and peripheral functions [6, 7, 8, 9].

Despite the essential role of OT release in regulating fundamental behaviors, the intracellular dynamics of OT‐containing vesicle trafficking and release within hypothalamic neurons remain poorly understood. A major factor determining the probability of OT release is the number of vesicles positioned near the plasma membrane [10]. However, only a few studies have directly examined vesicle motion in hypothalamic neurons or how these dynamics influence local signaling processes. Classically, neuronal exocytosis has been described as the release of neurotransmitters at axon terminals [11]. However, accumulating evidence indicates that the somatodendritic compartment is also an active site of release, particularly for neuromodulators stored in LDCVs [12, 13]. Several studies have specifically examined the postsynaptic release of OT‐containing LDCVs [12, 14, 15], as well as other neuromodulators such as serotonin and dopamine [13, 16].

The transport and release dynamics of somatic LDCVs in hypothalamic neurons remain largely uncharacterized. Novel imaging techniques enable the labeling of LDCVs populations and allow us to track their displacement and motion speed over short time periods. However, their dynamic behavior must be inferred through statistical analysis, which often obscures particle heterogeneity and variations in diffusive properties, especially in complex regions. To ensure accurate data analysis, it is essential to use computational methods capable of estimating diffusion parameters from short trajectories.

Single‐particle tracking (SPT) techniques provide invaluable insights into the dynamics of microscopic entities in various fields, including cell biology [17]. Analyzing these trajectories to understand the underlying physical processes is crucial. While classical Brownian motion provides a baseline, many systems exhibit anomalous diffusion, characterized by a power‐law relationship between the mean squared displacement (MSD) and time, MSD τ∝τα, with α≠1 [18]. Distinguishing between these diffusion modes (normal, subdiffusive, superdiffusive) and potentially identifying the underlying generative process (e.g., fractional Brownian motion (fBM), continuous‐time random walk (CTRW)) is a key challenge.

Traditional analysis often relies heavily on MSD calculations, as thoroughly analyzed by [19] for diffusive and convective motion. However, this method has limitations, especially for short, noisy trajectories or heterogeneous motion [20, 21]. Consequently, there is growing interest in applying machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) techniques to process images of particle motion (molecules, organelles), classify trajectory types and estimate diffusion parameters [18, 22, 23, 24, 25]. These approaches often require large, labeled datasets for training and validation. In the present work, we consider the application of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Random Forest (RF) classifiers to study the individual trajectories we have observed in OT neurons under resting and stimulated conditions.

To quantify the motion, we computed the time‐averaged MSD for each trajectory and estimated its anomalous exponent, α. The distribution of these exponents revealed that the vast majority of experimental trajectories exhibited subdiffusive character, with α values predominantly less than 1. To confirm that this behavior was a general property of OT neurons rather than an artifact of specific outlier cells, we analyzed the distribution of α across the 11 individual cells that constituted the dataset (Figure 3). The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was found to be 0.0035, indicating that over 99% of the variance in vesicle mobility is attributable to local intracellular interactions rather than per‐cell effects. Furthermore, while minor statistical heterogeneity was noted between cells (Kruskal–Wallis test, p=0.0185), the median α for every neuron remained consistently within the subdiffusive regime (median α<1.0 for all cells), as shown in Figure 3. These results confirm that subdiffusion is a robust and fundamental feature of somatic oxytocin‐containing compartments and justify the treatment of trajectories as statistically independent units for motion classification. This initial finding suggests that the motion of OT compartments is largely constrained rather than free.

Machine Learning Classification

To objectively classify the diffusion mode of each trajectory, we trained two distinct machine learning models on a large synthetic dataset (100 000 trajectories) of known motion types (normal, subdiffusive, and superdiffusive; see Figure 4). The first model, a 1D Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), directly analyzed the shape of the MSD curves and achieved a high validation accuracy of 97.8% on synthetic data (Figures 5 and 6). The second, a Random Forest (RF) classifier, used features extracted from the MSD curve (α, generalized diffusion coefficient, and goodness of fit).

These results indicate that the CNN, when trained on idealized synthetic MSD curves, can effectively learn the characteristic shapes associated with different diffusion modes, with minor confusion primarily between ND and SUP.

The performance of the trained RF model on synthetic data was initially assessed using the Out‐of‐Bag (OOB) error, an internal cross‐validation method. The reported OOB error for the model was 0.1055. The OOB error is an unbiased estimate of the prediction error on unseen data. An error rate of approximately 10.5% suggests that the selected features provide considerable discriminatory power for classifying diffusion modes within the synthetic dataset, though not as high as the CNN's direct MSD curve analysis on the same synthetic data.

When applied to the 118 experimental trajectories, both models reached a strong consensus: 79% of OT compartments (93 trajectories) were classified as subdiffusive (Figure 7). A small fraction (8%, 10 trajectories) was unanimously classified as undergoing normal diffusion. Notably, no trajectories were classified as superdiffusive, and the subdiffusive population was distributed across all experimental conditions, suggesting that constrained motion is a general feature of somatic OT compartments (Figure 8).

Discussion

Dynamics of OT Compartments

Microscopic tracking of our neuronal OT compartments has revealed that they follow different behaviors under stimulation; see Figure 2. We have observed that vesicle sets are heterogeneous regarding their mobility in response to neuronal stimulation, as reported in chromaffin and PC12 cells [26, 27, 28]. We acknowledge that our data are subject to the limitations of confocal microscopy, which prevents the definitive resolution of individual LDCVs.

Trying to know more about the compartments, we measured their changes in its velocity of motion due to stimulation, and we found two groups, some mobile (about 29%) and some immobile (71%). All these microscopic observations suggested that the molecular machinery regulating LDCVs' dynamics could be different from the canonical SNARE complex. Our observations, particularly the distinct mobility patterns and activity‐dependent regulation, suggest that additional or differential regulatory mechanisms may modulate LDCV trafficking and docking prior to fusion. These could include differences in tethering factors, cytoskeletal interactions, priming proteins, or modulatory partners that act upstream of the core SNARE‐mediated membrane fusion step. For that reason, we developed a computational method to analyze the origin of these differences toward the understanding of OT regulation.

We compared the predictions of the trained CNN and RF models on the 118 experimental trajectories described in Section 2.1. Figure 7 presents a confusion matrix comparing these predictions. Key observations from this comparison are:

Both models agreed on the classification of 93 trajectories as subdiffusive (deep blue area). This is a significant portion (79%) of the experimental dataset and aligns with the observation from Figure 4 (top‐left) that the experimental data is predominantly subdiffusive.

For 10 trajectories (8%), both models agreed on a Normal Diffusion (ND) classification (light blue area). However, for 15 trajectories (13%), the RF model classified them as ND, while the CNN classified them as subdiffusive (pink area).

Neither model classified any experimental trajectory as superdiffusive (SUP) in a way that resulted in agreement, indicating slow kinetics and the implication and regulation in LDCVs mobility of different factors such as the cytoskeleton, the organization of the fusion site, the availability and isoforms of SNARE proteins or specific calcium channels [11, 29, 30].

The matrix shows no entries in the SUP/SUP cell. Given that the experimental α distribution (Figure 4) is heavily skewed toward values less than 1, most compartments exhibit a subdiffusive behavior. This agrees with our experimental records where we found a 71% of immobile compartments in line with the 79% of subdiffusive ones, as classified by the selected ML models, suggesting that different populations may be regulated by distinct anchoring mechanisms [31, 32].

Trajectories with an anomalous exponent α close to 1 (the boundary between subdiffusion/normal diffusion and normal diffusion/superdiffusion) can be inherently ambiguous. The differing sensitivities of the two models might lead them to classify these borderline cases differently. The 15 trajectories classified as ND by RF and sub by CNN might fall into this category. To further investigate these classifications, particularly the disagreements, Figure 8 visualizes the trajectories, color‐coded according to the models' predictions. The 15 disputed trajectories (magenta) are distributed across multiple experimental groups, suggesting the ambiguity is not due to one experimental condition.

Impact of Vesicle Size on Diffusion

To further investigate the physical determinants of vesicle mobility, we explored whether the diameter of the OT‐containing compartments could predict their mode of diffusion. The diameter of each vesicle, estimated during image analysis, was incorporated as an additional feature into the RF classifier alongside the previously used parameters. It is important to note that this scalar feature could not be integrated into the 1D‐CNN model, as its architecture is designed to directly process the temporal information encoded in the Mean Squared Displacement (MSD) curves.

Figure 9 illustrates the predictor importance scores generated by the RF model. The anomalous exponent is the most influential feature for the classification. In contrast, the contribution of vesicle diameter is negligible, indicating that vesicle size is not a primary factor in determining its diffusion behavior.

Predictor importance scores in the RF model. This bar chart illustrates the relative importance of each feature used by the RF classifier to predict the diffusion model.

Despite its low overall importance, including diameter did cause minor reclassifications of some borderline trajectories. The number of trajectories that the RF model classified as subdiffusive shifted from 93 to 90 (compare with results plotted in Figure 7). Correspondingly, the number of trajectories classified as Normal Diffusion by the RF model, while being classified as subdiffusive by the CNN, increased from 15 to 18. This suggests that the inclusion of the diameter feature slightly altered the decision boundary for ambiguous cases.

This lack of correlation between size and diffusion mode is visually confirmed in Figure 10. The scatter plot shows the relationship between the anomalous exponent and vesicle diameter for all trajectories, colored by their final classification. It is evident that both subdiffusive trajectories (blue asterisks) and those classified as normal diffusion (green asterisks) are distributed across the entire spectrum of measured diameters. There is no discernible trend suggesting that larger or smaller vesicles are predisposed to a specific diffusion mode.

In summary, this extended analysis indicates that vesicle size is not a determining factor for the mobility of OT‐containing compartments in our experimental context. The dominant predictors remain the parameters derived from the temporal dynamics of the trajectories themselves.

Biological Significance of Subdiffusive Behavior in Neuronal OT‐Containing Compartments

The present analysis of LDCVs' trajectories in OT neurons shows that the anomalous exponent α often falls below 1, indicating subdiffusive behavior. Subdiffusion (α<1) implies that vesicle spread grows more slowly than free diffusion, reflecting physical constraints or interactions. This behavior is indicative of complex intracellular environments, where obstacles such as the cytoskeleton, molecular crowding, and binding events constrain vesicle movement [29, 33, 34].

In the crowded cytoplasm, subdiffusion implies that vesicles do not freely traverse the dendritic compartments but instead experience varying degrees of confinement and intermittent motion, often attributed to transient trapping or tethering [35, 36]. Importantly, subdiffusion constitutes the majority of cellular transport on the mesoscale, usually exhibiting non‐Brownian anomalous behavior [27, 36, 37, 38]. The high probability of subdiffusion events implies functional utility. Recent modeling studies have shown that subdiffusive cargo movement helps maintain local concentration gradients of signaling molecules, which can be beneficial for localized signaling [39, 40, 41, 42, 43]. Moreover, slowed movement can theoretically hold vesicles near synapses or release sites, potentially facilitating a reserve pool that can be mobilized upon high‐frequency stimulation [44]. Furthermore, we hypothesize that by limiting vesicle motion, subdiffusion may reduce the energetic cost associated with maintaining constant directed transport, allowing neurons to allocate ATP to other metabolic needs [45, 46, 47, 48, 49].

Besides, the co‐existence of different vesicle pools is expected to play key roles in maintaining the competence of release and recovering secretion capacity upon repeated stimulation [26]. The functional diversity is further influenced by varying the recycling kinetics that dictate the rate and extent of release, as well as the presence of different cargos within distinct vesicle subpopulations [27]. Additionally, vesicles may have varying requirements depending on the physiological demand or the specific region where release occurs [12, 50].

Our results are consistent with previous live‐cell imaging and single‐particle tracking studies which have revealed that neuronal LDCVs undergo complex motion patterns. For example, LDCVs' transport has been visualized in hippocampal neurons and invertebrate models, showing bidirectional trafficking along axons and dendrites [37, 51]. In vasopressin neurons, two‐photon imaging demonstrated dynamic, bidirectional LDCVs' movement with distinct speeds and direction biases in axons versus dendrites, and that systemic stimuli (e.g., osmotic challenge) can upregulate LDCVs' motility [52]. In C. elegans motor neurons, time‐lapse imaging of fluorescent LDCVs showed that synapsin is required to capture DCVs at synapses, and without it vesicles roam more freely and release neuropeptide poorly [53]. These and other studies [37, 51, 52, 53] reveal that LDCVs can switch between processive runs and pauses, often spending long times in “immobile” or constrained states. As such, LDCVs are enriched at synapses or release sites, suggesting in vivo roles for both active transport and local capture [53]. Furthermore, single‐vesicle imaging has shown that LDCVs in both mammalian and invertebrate neurons do not simply diffuse freely, but move in a complex, heterogeneous manner influenced by neuronal activity and cell type [12, 54].

To date, LDCVs subclassifications are scarce in neuronal cells and, specifically, in oxytocinergic hypothalamic neurons. Our vesicle dynamics' data have allowed us to initiate a detailed characterization of the somatic OT‐containing compartments depending on their displacement, speed, and diffusion, which provides novel insights into the potential subtypes and dynamics of LDCVs in these neurons.

Implications of Subdiffusion for Neuronal Physiology and Disease

Subdiffusive motion of large dense‐core vesicles (LDCVs) has important functional consequences for neuronal signaling. Direct single‐particle tracking studies in neurons have demonstrated that LDCVs exhibit anomalous diffusion (α<1) in axons and dendrites, reflecting cytoplasmic crowding, cytoskeletal constraints, and intermittent motor engagement [55, 56]. Such constrained motion limits spatial spread and promotes local vesicle retention.

Because LDCVs carry neuropeptides and trophic factors, their spatial distribution directly shapes neuromodulatory output. Experimentally, synapsin‐dependent tethering has been shown to regulate dense‐core vesicle capture at presynaptic terminals. For example, the loss of synapsin in C. elegans prevents activity‐dependent capture of DCVs at synapses and significantly impairs cAMP‐dependent neuropeptide release [53], providing direct experimental evidence that subdiffusive immobilization via tethering is required for functional peptide secretion rather than being a passive biophysical consequence.

Similarly, vesicle over‐mobility can reduce synaptic accumulation. In Drosophila, the loss of Huntingtin (Htt) enhances capture efficiency of retrogradely transported DCVs in synaptic boutons, increasing local neuropeptide stores [57] directly linking altered vesicle trafficking dynamics to changes in peptidergic output and demonstrating that modulation of vesicle motility quantitatively reshapes synaptic peptide content.

Subdiffusion also has implications for transport energetics. Optical trapping and single‐molecule studies of kinesin motors [58] have established that kinesin advances in 8‐nm steps tightly coupled to ATP hydrolysis (one ATP per step), providing a quantifiable energetic cost for directed transport. Modeling work by Goychuk [59, 60] demonstrated that cargos in viscoelastic, subdiffusive cytoplasm can be transported efficiently by molecular motors with sublinear displacement scaling while maintaining near one‐ATP‐per‐step coupling. These models suggest that intermittent engagement between passive subdiffusion and active motor‐driven runs can minimize futile ATP consumption compared to continuous directed transport.

In neurons specifically, energetic constraints are particularly relevant due to high ATP demand for ion pumping and synaptic transmission. Hinckelmann et al. [61] showed that vesicles can carry glycolytic enzymes to locally generate ATP, directly fueling axonal transport. This experimental study underscores that vesicle trafficking is metabolically costly and that neurons have evolved mechanisms to optimize ATP allocation. Thus, limiting constant long‐range directed transport via subdiffusive retention may reduce global ATP expenditure, preserving energy for electrical signaling and plasticity.

Importantly, LDCV trafficking dynamics are plastic and can change under physiological demand. Live‐cell imaging studies show that systemic homeostatic challenge alters LDCV trafficking dynamics in hypothalamic neurons, directly linking changes in vesicle mobility to adaptive neuroendocrine responses [62]. This provides experimental support for the idea that modulating vesicle motility constitutes a homeostatic mechanism.

Disrupted LDCV transport is strongly implicated in neurological disease. Mutations in the kinesin‐3 motor KIF1A impair anterograde transport of synaptic vesicle precursors and dense‐core vesicles. Chiba et al. [63] analyzed disease‐associated KIF1A mutations and demonstrated impaired motor processivity and cargo delivery, linking altered transport dynamics to KIF1A‐associated neurological disorder (KAND), characterized by spastic paraplegia and neuropathy. These findings provide direct mechanistic evidence that altered motor‐driven transport changes vesicle distribution in disease.

Likewise, Huntington's disease models exhibit altered DCV dynamics and neuropeptide accumulation [57]. Mutations in synapsin implicated in epilepsy and autism spectrum disorders affect both synaptic vesicle clustering and dense‐core vesicle tethering [53], further supporting the idea that perturbations in vesicle trapping and mobility have pathological consequences.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Data Set

Animals

To unambiguously identify OT‐expressing neurons, we employed the mouse transgenic line OT‐Ires‐Cre:Rosa26iDTR/+OT‐Cre (Jackson Laboratories, strain ID: 024234) originally generated by Wu et al., 2012, which express Cre recombinase under the control of endogenous OT promoter (Figure 1). These animals were bred with Ai9 reporter mice, expressing tdTomato (Ai9, Jackson Laboratories, strain ID: 007909). All experiments were performed according to Spanish and European Union regulations regarding animal research (2010/63/EU). Experimental procedures were approved by the Bioethical Committee at the Institute of Neuroscience (CSIC‐UMH) and the Consejo Superior de Investigaciones Científicas. Animals were born and maintained in the Animal Facility of the Institute of Neuroscience (CSIC‐UMH), and were housed in ventilated cages in a standard pathogen‐free facility, on a 12 h light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water, controlled temperature (23°C–25°C) and humidity (40%–60%). The experimental design was optimized to minimize the number of animals.

Primary Culture of Hypothalamic Neurons

Twenty four millimeters of diameter coverslips were pretreated with poly‐D‐lysine (PDL, Sigma Aldrich, Ref: P7886) following standard procedures. P0 OTtdTom male and female mice were decapitated, brains were carefully removed and hypothalami were dissected in cold HBSS 1× (< 4°C, Fisher, Ref: 14175–053). Dissected hypothalami were incubated in digestion solution during 18 min at 37°C, to break cell unions and disintegrate the tissue. Digestion solution contained Recombinant DNase I (10 U/μL, Sigma Aldrich, Ref: 04716728001), MgCl2 20 nM (Sigma Aldrich, Ref: M2670), CaCl2 20 nM (Merk, Ref: 102378), papain (Sigma Aldrich, Ref: P3125), and EBSS 1× (Gibco, Ref: 14155‐063). After incubation, hypothalami were disaggregated mechanically pipetting through a P1000 pipette. Then, tissue was centrifuged (Eppendorf Centrifuge 5810) 5 min, 200 rpm. Supernatant was discarded and the pellet was resuspended in a solution containing EBSS 1×, trypsin inhibitor type III Ovomucoid (Sigma Aldrich, Ref: T2011) and DNAse I. Cells were then transferred to a density gradient made of trypsin inhibitor type III Ovomucoid, carefully placed with a P1000 pipette. Cells were centrifuged 5 min at 1000 rpm. Supernatant was discarded and cells were resuspended in culture medium. Culture medium was composed of Neurobasal culture medium 1× (NB, Fisher, Ref: 21103‐049) supplemented with B27 1× (Thermo Fisher, Ref: 17504‐044, Stock 50×), Penicilin/Streptomycin (10.000 U/mL, Thermo Fisher, Ref: 15140122), L‐Glutamine (2×, Thermo Fisher, Ref: 11500626, Stock 200 mM), and D‐(+)Glucose solution (45% in H2O, Sigma Aldrich, Ref: G8769). Then, 10 μL of cells were mixed with 10 μL of trypan blue solution (Sigma Aldrich, Ref: T8154) to estimate the cell number in a Neubauer chamber. Cells were plated (12.5×104 cells/well) and placed in a cell incubator at 37°C, 5% CO2. After 1 h, culture medium was added to each well. Half of the culture medium was refreshed each 2–3 days.

Oxytocinergic neurons were identified by endogenous red fluorescence signal. We performed electrophysiological recordings to assess key parameters including resting membrane potential, action potential firing, and firing rate in response to depolarizing current injection. These measurements confirmed that the neurons exhibited robust excitability and normal electrophysiological characteristics under our culture conditions (see Figure S1).

OT‐Containing Compartment Dynamics

We employed 7–14 DIV hypothalamic primary cultured neurons from OTtdTom mice, which led us to clearly identify OT‐compartments. These primary cultures were prepared by pooling the hypothalamic regions from P0 litters containing both male and female pups. Each independent culture (3 cultures), therefore derives from multiple animals (24 mice). Live imaging experiments were performed with a confocal laser system Olympus Fluoview FV300, mounted in an inverted IX71 microscope, with a 100× oil immersion objective (N.A. = 1.45). FluoView 5.0 software was used to monitor the experiments. A perfusion system was coupled to the microscope to apply different media to the cells. The experimental routine consisted of the identification of an oxytocinergic cell (recognized by the endogenous red fluorescent signal, Figure 1A) and focusing on the soma. Data was collected during the perfusion with Tyrode's basal medium (composition in mM: 125 NaCl; 2 KCl; 30 glucose; 25 HEPES; 2 CaCl2; 2 MgCl2; pH 7.4) during 10 s, followed by stimulation with Tyrode's 100 mM KCl (Stimulation, composition in mM: 27 NaCl; 100 KCl; 30 glucose; 25 HEPES; 2 CaCl2; 2 MgCl2; pH 7.4) during 60 s, and a final application of Tyrode's basal solution during 10 s. Solutions were tempered at 37°C before application. Imaging recordings consisted of a continuous time lapse of each individual cell acquired at 1 Hz during 80 s. Although endogenous OT fluorescence signal from OTtdTom neurons was clearly visible, identification of individual OT vesicles was challenging due to the inherent limitations of confocal microscopy. So, we refer to them as OT‐containing compartments.

The diameter of OT‐containing compartments was calculated from the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the fluorescence intensity profile of OT‐compartment in the confocal images. Specifically, for OT‐compartment, a ROI was defined. Semi‐automatic tracking was done using image analysis software Imaris (Imaris ×64 9.3.1, Oxford instruments). Imaris rendered parameters such as displacement length and speed, and also area and diameter of the ROIs (OT‐compartments analyzed).

The detected fluorescent puncta may represent single LDCVs, closely apposed or clustered LDCVs that cannot be optically separated, or potentially other oxytocin‐positive structures within the secretory pathway. While their size, distribution, and activity‐dependent behavior are consistent with LDCVs, we now explicitly acknowledge that our imaging approach does not allow us to exclude the possibility of vesicle clusters or other OT‐positive compartments.

Image Analysis

Time lapse experiments were analyzed semiautomatically employing the image analysis software Imaris (Imaris ×64 9.3.1, Oxford instruments). Images were filtered employing Gaussian and background subtraction filters to enhance OT‐containing compartments' detection by the software. We focused our analysis in the somatic OT‐compartments, which were segmented with the “Spots” tool. Then, we tracked OT‐compartments' movement selecting the “Brownian movement” algorithm (used for randomly moving objects) as we did not know a priori the type of movement expected for OT‐compartments in the soma. A video showing the movement of the compartments can be viewed by scanning the QR code in Figure 1B. Our analysis was targeted on somatic OT‐compartments, as somatodendritic neuropeptide release is not as widely studied and understood as axonal release [11, 12, 13].

Vesicles were tracked only while they remained within the defined imaging volume and met tracking quality criteria (signal‐to‐noise ratio, minimum trajectory length, and localization precision). When vesicles moved out of the focal plane, their trajectories were terminated and excluded from displacement quantification beyond the last reliably detected position. Therefore, displacement estimates were calculated exclusively from validated trajectory segments. Vesicles that disappeared abruptly during or shortly after KCl stimulation were not automatically classified as fusion events. Because our imaging modality does not directly report membrane fusion, we cannot definitively conclude that such events correspond to exocytosis. However, we acknowledge that activity‐induced fusion with the plasma membrane is a plausible explanation for a subset of these events following depolarization.

The reported percentages of mobile (∼29%) and immobile (∼71%) vesicles were derived from the full set of tracked OT‐compartments pooled across all analyzed cells (118 OT‐compartments analyzed are considered as 100%). Each vesicle trajectory was classified based on predefined quantitative criteria, including net displacement and mean squared displacement (MSD) within the observation window. Compartments exceeding the mobility threshold were categorized as mobile, whereas those remaining below this threshold throughout the recording were classified as immobile. The percentages reflect the proportion of trajectories falling into each category relative to the total number of analyzed vesicles.

Trajectory Analysis and Machine Learning Classification

For each experimental and synthetic trajectory, we first calculated the time‐averaged Mean Squared Displacement (MSD) over a range of lag times (τ). The MSD characterizes the average distance a particle travels over time. From the MSD curve, we extracted a set of features to describe the motion. The primary features were derived by fitting the power‐law relationship, MSD τ∝τα, to the log–log plot of the MSD curve. This analysis yielded:

To assess the robustness of our findings, we grouped trajectories by cell identity (N=11 cells) and performed a Kruskal–Wallis test [64] on the anomalous exponent α. Although the test revealed expected physiological variability between cells (p=0.0185), the median exponent for every individual cell consistently fell within the subdiffusive regime (α<1). This confirms that constrained motion is a general property of these neurons and that the pooled dataset used for machine learning represents the population behavior rather than specific outlier cells.

It is important to highlight the relationship between immobile vesicles and those displaying subdiffusive motion: these categories are related but not identical. Vesicles classified as “immobile” were defined operationally based on very low net displacement and mean squared displacement (MSD) values within the observation window, remaining below our predefined mobility threshold. In contrast, subdiffusive motion was determined from MSD analysis by fitting the anomalous diffusion exponent (α<1). While many immobile vesicles indeed exhibit strongly subdiffusive behavior (α approaching 0), not all subdiffusive vesicles are immobile. Some vesicles display constrained or corralled motion (0<α<1) yet still explore a limited spatial domain and therefore do not meet our immobility criteria.

The apparent similarity of trajectories between group 1 (10 s KCl stimulation) and groups 3‐K6 (60 s stimulation) can be explained by the fact that vesicle mobility reaches a plateau rapidly after depolarization, such that extending stimulation duration does not proportionally increase displacement under our experimental conditions. Importantly, the quantitative analyses indicate that while qualitative trajectory appearance may seem similar, there are measurable differences in mobility parameters.

Synthetic Dataset

To train and validate the machine learning classifiers, a large synthetic dataset of 100 000 two‐dimensional trajectories was computationally generated. This dataset was designed to represent three distinct modes of particle motion: normal diffusion, subdiffusion, and superdiffusion. All simulated trajectories were constructed as a sequence of 80 discrete steps, matching the length of our experimental recordings.

Trajectories exhibiting normal diffusion (corresponding to an anomalous exponent, α, of 1) were simulated to represent classic, unrestricted random walks. For these trajectories, each step was completely independent of the previous one. The size and direction of each step were randomly drawn from a Gaussian distribution, a standard method for modeling Brownian motion [19].

In contrast, anomalous diffusion trajectories (subdiffusion with α<1 and superdiffusion with α>1) were modeled to represent constrained or directed motion. For this, we used a model known as fractional Brownian Motion (fBM). Unlike classical Brownian motion, where steps are independent, fBM introduces a temporal correlation or “memory” between steps [25]. In the subdiffusive regime (α<1), this negative correlation reflects the physical constraints, which tend to return the vesicle toward its previous position. This approach allows us to differentiate between simple slow diffusion and the physiologically relevant constrained motion. While other models, such as the Continuous‐Time Random Walk [65], can also generate subdiffusive trajectories, they typically describe systems with discrete trapping events. We utilized fBM as it provides a more accurate physical representation of the continuous, elastic interactions between vesicles and the intracellular matrix in hypothalamic neurons.

To generate these correlated steps, we employed the computationally efficient Davies‐Harte algorithm [66]. This algorithm uses the autocovariance function of fractional Gaussian noise, which is determined by a parameter called the Hurst exponent (H = α/2), to precisely control the strength and nature of the temporal correlations for the desired mode of motion.

The final labeled dataset consisted of 100 000 trajectories in total: 35 000 were designated as normal diffusion (target α=1.0), 40 000 as subdiffusion (target α=0.3), and 25 000 as superdiffusion (target α=1.7). Parameters for trajectory length and the generalized diffusion coefficients were chosen to reflect the properties observed in our experimental dataset. The fidelity of this simulation process was confirmed by analyzing the resulting trajectories, which yielded distributions of α centered on their target values (see Figure 4).

A potential limitation of this approach is the assumption of a single, stationary diffusion mode. Biological vesicles may exhibit state‐switching dynamics, such as intermittent active transport (short directed runs) or transient trapping. In our dataset, the absence of superdiffusive classifications and the strong consensus between the CNN and RF models suggest that directed transport is negligible compared to the pervasive subdiffusive constraints. However, we acknowledge that trajectories containing high‐velocity directed runs that are short relative to the total acquisition time could potentially be classified as normal diffusion or subdiffusion depending on the duration of the active phase.

Machine Learning Models

Two distinct machine learning models were developed in MATLAB (R2021a, MathWorks, RRID:SCR_001622) and trained on the synthetic dataset to classify trajectory types.

The first model was a 1D Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), designed to classify trajectories based on the overall shape of their MSD curves. The raw MSD values for each trajectory were used as direct input to the network. The CNN architecture consisted of a sequence of convolutional layers, batch normalization, ReLU activation, max‐pooling layers, followed by a fully connected layer and a final softmax layer for classification. The model was trained using the Adam optimizer, and its performance during training is shown in Figure 5.

The second model was a Random Forest (RF) classifier, an ensemble learning method known for its robustness [67]. Unlike the CNN, the RF model was trained on the specific features extracted from the MSD curves. The input for the RF model consisted of three features per trajectory: the anomalous exponent (α), the logarithm of the generalized diffusion coefficient (log10Kα), and the coefficient of determination (R2). The ensemble was constructed from 300 individual decision trees.

Once trained and validated on the synthetic dataset, both the CNN and RF models were applied to the 118 experimental trajectories to predict their respective diffusion modes.
