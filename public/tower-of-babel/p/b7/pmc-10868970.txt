Dermoscopic Features of Lupus Miliaris Disseminatus Faciei (LMDF)
Authors: Siddharth Mani, Aradhana Rout, Manish Khandare
Published: 2023

Sir,

LMDF is a non-infectious granulomatous condition of unknown aetiology that is characterized by skin to red-coloured, smooth-surfaced, monomorphic papules distributed symmetrically over the central part of the face involving cheeks, chin, nose and eyelids. Rarely, the condition has been shown to involve extra-facial sites as well.[1] The condition needs to be differentiated from other facial granulomatous papules such as cutaneous sarcoidosis, granulomatous rosacea, cutaneous leishmaniasis, granuloma faciale and pseudolymphomas. The condition needs to be identified early as delay in initiation of treatment might lead to scarring of face. Dermoscopy is a tool that is now being increasingly used for early diagnosis, and the same can be used in LMDF as well. Herein, we report a case of LMDF that was initially managed as acne and later with dermoscopy found out to be a granulomatous condition.

A 24-year-old male with fitzpatrick skin type IV presented with multiple, asymptomatic, red coloured raised lesions all over the face that had gradually increased in number and size over a period of 6 months. There were no relieving or aggravating factors. Patient denied occurrence of similar lesions in the past or in the family. He had taken multiple courses of doxycycline and isotretinoin along with topical retinoids and benzoyl peroxide with no improvement. Clinical examination revealed multiple, polysized, erythematous papules, distributed symmetrically on nearly the entire face, especially the periocular areas, perioral area, malar prominence and chin. Few lesions had coalesced to form plaques in the periorbital area. The lesions were associated with mild scaling [Figure 1a and b]. Dermoscopic examination revealed the presence of scales, orange yellow areas, white streaks/scar-like areas, and follicular plugs. Few lesions showed the presence of few hairpin vessels along with some arborising vessels [Figure 2a and b]. Due to the presence of orange yellow areas in dermoscopy, a granulomatous pathology was thought of and patient was evaluated for the same. A histopathological examination was undertaken which showed the presence of epitheloid granuloma with lymphohistiocytes, few giant cells and occasional caseation necrosis well [Figure 3a and b]. Modified ZN, ZN, Giemsa and PAS stain failed to show any evidence of leprosy, cutaneous tuberculosis, subcutaneous mycosis or leishmaniasis. Serum calcium, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), IGRA (Interferon gamma release assay) and Chest X-ray were normal. Based on clinical, dermoscopic and histopathological examination, the patient was diagnosed as a case of LMDF. He was given a tapering dose of steroid starting at 40 mg, which was tapered over 5 months to which he responded well [Figure 4a and b]. At the end of 3 months, he was started on dapsone as maintenance therapy, which was continued until 3 months after stopping the steroid. Currently, the patient is off treatment for past 6 months with only residual scarring.

LMDF was initially considered to be a type of cutaneous tuberculosis due to the presence of dermal epithelioid granulomas with caseation necrosis. However, due to the persistent failure to detect mycobacterium tuberculosis and lack of response of antitubercular therapy, the disease has been classified as non-infectious granuloma.[2] The entity was also grouped with granulomatous rosacea but later was found to be phenotypically different. Few authors have proposed the term ‘FIGURE’, standing for facial idiopathic granulomas with regressive evolution, but LMDF is still the preferred nomenclature that is in use.[34] The condition can be confused with acne, papulopustular rosacea and other infectious and non-infectious facial granulomatous conditions. Exact aetiology of LMDF is still unknown; however, it is thought to be an autoimmune reaction resulting from the rupture of hair follicles, which causes granulomatous disease.[5]

There are very few case reports depicting the dermoscopic features of LMDF. Ahyan et al. described the presence of targetoid yellow white follicular plugs along with linear and hairpin vessels.[6] Chauhan et al. described the presence of follicular keratotic plugs and vessels over brownish yellow areas.[7] Similar findings were described by Li et al.[8]

We also found scales, orange yellow areas, white streaks/scar-like areas and follicular plugs along with arborising and hairpin vessels. The orange yellow area indicates the presence of granulomatous inflammation. Follicular plugs are thought to be secondary to the follicular hyperkeratosis and lateral pressure on follicles by the surrounding granulomas.[7] It is difficult to distinguish LMDF from other granulomatous disorders dermoscopically; however, salient differentiating features are mentioned in Table 1.

No large-scale study has been done for the management of LMDF and only anecdotal case reports and case series are available regarding the same. Various drugs have been used in the management of LMDF, which includes prednisolone, dapsone, isotretinoin, clofazimine, metronidazole and tetracyclines. Recently, apremilast has also been tried with some success. In addition to oral treatment, laser therapy using a 1450-nm diode laser and a 1565-nm non-ablative fractionated laser resurfacing have also been used in this condition. Our patient responded well to prednisolone and dapsone.

There are very few case reports that are published regarding dermoscopic appearance of LMDF. This report will help in adding to the existing repertoire of knowledge of dermoscopic features of this rare entity so that we can use this to confirm our diagnosis and start the management early.
