Chloroplast-genome-based insights into the systematic relationship of Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis)
Authors: Li Jin, Shaohong Qu, Wen Zhu, Qishao Li, Yunhong Tan, Zhengying Cao, Peiyao Xin, Yu Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe taxonomy of Eriobotrya and Rhaphiolepis has long been contentious due to unclear interspecific relationships, morphological ambiguities, low barcode resolution, and lack of molecular phylogenetic support. Using shallow genome sequencing, this study analyzed 29 newly assembled chloroplast genomes (159,101–159,718 bp; GC 36.6%–36.7%) comprising 113 unique genes (78 protein-coding, 31 tRNA, and 4 rRNA). Structural variation was observed in the rpl2-trnH spacer along with six small inversions. Comparative analyses revealed 13 highly variable regions in Eriobotrya, 2 in Rhaphiolepis, and 12 shared regions. Phylogenomic analysis of 42 plastomes resolved five well-supported clades, confirming the monophyly of each genus. Nuclear-plastid conflict in species such as Eriobotrya japonica suggests ancient hybridization and chloroplast capture, likely linked to overlapping flowering times and distributions. These findings clarify phylogenetic relationships and provide theoretical and practical support for taxonomy, germplasm utilization, and genetic research of Eriobotrya and Rhaphiolepis.Subject areas: plant genetics, plant bioinformatics, plant biology, plant systematics

Introduction

The genus Eriobotrya Lindl., part of the tribe Maleae in the subfamily Amygdaloideae of the Rosaceae family, includes evergreen trees and shrubs. Species within this genus are renowned for their edible fruits, which are commonly consumed fresh. Additionally, the leaves, flowers, and bark of Eriobotrya have long been utilized in traditional herbal medicine. The woods of these plants are highly valued for their quality, making them an important raw material for the production of agricultural tools and handicrafts. Moreover, Eriobotrya species are favored as ornamental trees in landscaping. Similarly, the genus Rhaphiolepis Lindl., also within the tribe Maleae and the Amygdaloideae subfamily of the Rosaceae family, consists mainly of evergreen shrubs or small trees, primarily grown for ornamental purposes. The wood of Rhaphiolepis species is known for its strength and durability, making it suitable for crafting numerous agricultural tools and implements. The Rhaphiolepis species are found in over 10 provinces in China, including Gansu, Shaanxi, Sichuan, Yunnan, Guangxi, Fujian, and Taiwan.1,2 Outside of China, Eriobotrya species are also cultivated in countries like Japan, the United States, and India.3,4

Chloroplasts, a type of plastid characterized by their green coloration, are commonly found in terrestrial plants, algae, and some protists. These organelles play a crucial role in cellular function and possess autonomous genetic information.5 The chloroplast genome (chloroplast DNA [cpDNA]) typically ranges from 110 to 170 kb in size but is highly conserved in terms of gene content and order.6 Despite its small size, the cpDNA possesses several advantages that make it particularly useful in phylogenetic research: it is usually inherited uniparentally (most often maternally, though occasionally paternally), exists in multiple copies, maintains a stable structure, lacks recombination, and is relatively easy to sequence and assemble.7 The highly conserved nature of cpDNA makes it ideal for designing primers for amplification of chloroplast fragments. Compared to the nuclear genome, the cpDNA is smaller and exists as a closed, circular, and double-stranded DNA molecule. It comprises a substantial proportion of the total DNA in plants, particularly in chloroplast-rich tissues, though its percentage varies across species, tissue types, and developmental stages.8 On a molecular level, cpDNA shows notable differences; most sequences are single-copy and highly conserved both structurally and sequentially. The homology of molecular traits can be easily assessed, with significant interspecific genetic variation and relatively low intraspecific variations.9 Furthermore, molecular evolution rates differ notably between coding and non-coding regions, which evolve independently, making cpDNA useful for phylogenetic studies at various taxonomic levels without needing additional data, ensuring comparability across taxa.10 Beyond providing abundant nucleotide and amino acid sequence information, the structural characteristics of cpDNA also offer valuable insights for phylogenetic analysis, significantly enhancing our understanding of species relationships.11

Since the early 21st century, advancements in molecular biology and high-throughput sequencing technologies have significantly propelled research on chloroplast genomes. Although the first complete cpDNA of Nicotiana tabacum was obtained in 1986, the pace of cpDNA sequencing has accelerated rapidly since then, and the database has expanded.12,13 From 2005 to 2016, the number of published cpDNA sequences increased rapidly.14,15,16 As a result, the cpDNA has become a key tool in plant evolutionary biology and is widely applied in species identification, population genetics, individual-level differentiation, and phylogenetic analyses. For instance, 17 cpDNA sequences (>42 kb) were employed to resolve evolutionary relationships among 45 angiosperm taxa.17 Similarly, 64 cpDNA sequences were analyzed to explore the major lineages of angiosperms.18 To resolve the phylogenetic relationships of Pinus, 37 cpDNA sequences from this genus and related taxa were examined, yielding support values exceeding 95% for 30 of 33 branches—surpassing those of previous studies.19 In addition, analysis of cpDNA sequences in Machilus revealed their phylogenetic relationships, showing minimal genetic divergence within Lauraceae and identifying hypervariable regions distinct from traditional molecular markers, thereby enabling the classification of cultivated Machilus species.20 In the case of Eriobotrya, random amplified polymorphic DNA (RAPD) technology was applied for the identification of cultivars. Using two RAPD primers, 19 distinct bands were generated from the genomic DNA of 16 Eriobotrya cultivars, with 16 loci detected as either polymorphic or monomorphic, and the cultivars were subsequently classified using a binary taxonomy approach.21 Later, PCR amplification and sequencing of the cpDNA trnH-psbA intergenic region were performed to analyze wild Eriobotrya species together with cultivated varieties “Zaohong No. 6” and “Xiangfei.” With the aid of bioinformatics tools, a phylogenetic tree covering 19 genera within the subfamily Maloideae was constructed.22 Their molecular phylogenetic analysis, based on the trnH-psbA DNA barcode, revealed two primary clades, with Eriobotrya and Rhaphiolepis forming the closest relationship (average genetic distance of 0.055).

In recent years, the taxonomic classification of the genera Eriobotrya and Rhaphiolepis has remained contentious due to limited early sampling of their species, variations in morphological traits (persistent sepals), and the effects of ancient hybridization and chloroplast capture.23 Several studies have reconstructed the molecular phylogenetic tree of Eriobotrya using chloroplast genome sequences and various recombined gene regions, including rbcL, trnL-F, ITS, and Adh. Through gene lineage analysis, these investigations have shed light on the origin, evolutionary history, and biogeographical distribution of the genus.24,25,26,27,28 In 2020, some scholars proposed reclassifying Eriobotrya species under Rhaphiolepis, a suggestion that triggered considerable debate in the field. Around the same time, several chloroplast genomes of Eriobotrya were published.29 However, many wild Eriobotrya species remain unexamined at the cpDNA level. The relationships between species within the Eriobotrya-Rhaphiolepis complex are still not well understood, with morphological differences often difficult to discern and conventionally used DNA barcoding markers lacking sufficient resolution. Currently, there is no universally accepted classification system based on molecular phylogenetic evidence. As more cpDNA data become available, complete chloroplast genomes have emerged as a powerful tool, offering several advantages over traditional DNA barcoding. By identifying hypervariable regions within these genomes, researchers can pinpoint highly informative DNA fragments for resolving taxonomic relationships at lower levels (such as genus, species, or subspecies). Additionally, analyzing the types of mutations in these hypervariable regions helps reveal both structural and point mutations across the genome. This study reconstructed a phylogenetic tree based on a structural comparison of chloroplast genomes to clarify the relationships among Eriobotrya species and provide both theoretical foundations and practical guidance for the development, utilization, and genetic analysis of germplasm resources.

Results

Basic characteristics of Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis) chloroplast genomes

The chloroplast genome sizes of 29 Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis) species varied between 159,101 bp (in Eriobotrya japonica) and 159,718 bp (in Emmenopterys henryi). The average base coverage ranged from 113.5× in E. ximeng_sp to 590.6× in E. gaoli_sp (Table 1). The GC content across these chloroplast genomes was consistent, ranging from 36.6% to 36.7%. Notably, the GC content in the large single-copy (LSC) region (34.3%–34.6%) and the small single-copy (SSC) region (30.0%–30.5%) was lower than that found in the inverted repeat (IR) region (43%). This elevated GC content in the IR region is likely due to the presence of four rRNA genes located there. The size of the LSC region ranged from 87,107 bp to 87,712 bp, with E. henryi having the largest region and E. japonica the smallest, while the size of the SSC region varied between 19,155 bp and 19,471 bp, with E. henryi having the largest region and E. glabrescens the smallest. The IR region size ranged from 26,317 bp to 26,362 bp, with E. bengalensis possessing the largest region and E. deflexa the smallest (Figure 1).29 CP accession numbers for Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis) (Table 2).

Species collection of Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis)

In the chloroplast genome of the Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis) species, we annotated a total of 113 genes, including 78 protein-coding genes, 31 tRNA genes, and 4 rRNA genes. Seventeen of these genes were duplicated in the IR region, comprising six protein-coding genes, seven tRNA genes, and four rRNA genes. The higher GC content in the IR region is likely due to the presence of four rRNA genes in these regions. The 18 genes composed of 6 tRNA genes contain either one or two introns. Specifically, 16 genes (including atpF, ndhA, ndhB, petB, petD, rpl2, rpl16, rpoC1, rps12, rps16, trnA-UGC, trnG-GCC, trnL-GAU, trnK-UUU, trnL-UAA, and trnV-UAC) contained one intron, while two genes (ycf3 and clpP) contained two introns. The ycf1 gene spanned the IRb/SSC boundary. The genes were categorized based on their functions, with 47 genes related to photosynthesis (such as those involved in photosystem I, photosystem II, cytochrome b/f complex, ATP synthase, NADH dehydrogenase, and RubisCO large subunit), 25 genes related to transcription and translation (ribosomal proteins and DNA-dependent RNA polymerase), 35 RNA-related genes (ribosomal RNA and tRNA genes), as well as other genes (such as maturase, envelop membrane protein, subunit of acetyl-CoA-carboxylase, and ATP-dependent protease), and 6 genes of unknown function (conserved reading frames) (Table 1).

Analysis of highly variable regions in Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis) chloroplast genomes

Using the DnaSP software, nucleotide diversity (Pi) was assessed within 600 bp windows across 42 Eriobotrya chloroplast genomes. Among 35 Eriobotrya chloroplast genomes, Pi values ranged from 0 to 0.00932, with an average of 0.0013, indicating a high degree of chloroplast genome sequence similarity and strong conservation of the plastomes within the genus. Despite the aligned sequences exhibiting minimal divergence, 14 regions displayed elevated variability (Pi > 0.004). Of these, 13 were identified as highly variable, including ycf1, ndhI-ndhG, ccsA, trnL-rpl32, ndhF, rpoA, trnV-ndhC, ndhJ-trnF, trnL-rps4, rps14-psbZ, psbD-trnE, atpA-trnG, and trnQ-rps16 (Figure S1). Eight of these regions (rpoA, trnV-ndhC, ndhJ-trnF, trnL-rps4, rps14-psbZ, psbD-trnE, atpA-trnG, and trnQ-rps16) were located in the LSC region, while the remaining five regions (ycf1, ndhI-ndhG, ccsA, trnL-rpl32, and ndhF) were in the SSC region. In contrast, a comparative analysis of seven Rhaphiolepis species revealed Pi values ranging from 0 to 0.00476, with an average of 0.00054. Only two regions (Pi > 0.004) were found to be highly variable: rpl3-psaJ and rps14-psbZ (Figure S2). A combined assessment of all 42 Eriobotrya and Rhaphiolepis species showed Pi values ranging from 0 to 0.00793, with an average of 0.00131. A total of 12 highly variable regions (Pi > 0.004) were shared between the two genera, including ycf1, ccsA, trnL-rpl32, ndhF, rbcL, trnV-ndhC, ndhJ-trnF, trnL-rps4, rps14-psbZ, trnD-psbM, atpA-trnG, and trnQ-rps16 (Figure S3). Structural mutations in the chloroplast genomes of Eriobotrya

Genome collinearity analysis revealed that the genome structure and gene order of the 29 Eriobotrya chloroplast genomes were largely consistent, with no significant differences. However, significant differences were observed in the IR/SC junction region. The IR region of the chloroplast genome in Eriobotrya ranged from 26,317 to 26,362 bp in size. At the junctions of the LSC or SSC and IR regions in the chloroplast genomes of the 29 Eriobotrya species, three genes—rps19, ndhF, and ycf1—appeared in complete or partial copies. Specifically, rps19 was located at the junction of the LSC and IRb regions, ndhF at the IRb-SSC junction, and ycf1 at the SSC-IRa junction. The full lengths of the rps19, ndhF, and ycf1 genes were 279; 2,248–2,255; and 5,628–5,688 bp, respectively. Among 10 groups of Eriobotrya species, including E. malipoense, E. fragrans, E. kachinensis, E. japonica, E. bengalensis var. angustifolia, E. obovata, E. bengalensis, E. deflexa, E. henryi, and Rhaphiolepis lanceolata, the trnH gene in the LSC region showed varying degrees of contraction from the IR-LSC boundary (5, 36, 41, 50, 52, 52, 60, 62, 102, and 118 bp, respectively), while other groups displayed a uniform 51 bp contraction. The ndhF gene crossed the IRb-SSC boundary, with the chloroplast genome of E. henryi exhibiting a 4 bp expansion into the IRb region and an 11 bp contraction in the SSC region. In contrast, E. glabrescens showed a 6 bp contraction in the SSC region. The ycf1 gene crossed the SSC-IRa boundary, with its segment in the IRb region extending between 6 and 60 bp, while its segment in the IRa region consistently extended by 6 bp (Figure 3).

Formation of stable hairpin structures by small inversions in the chloroplast genomes of Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis)

Small inversions can form stable single-stranded hairpin structures, typically associated with gene replication and genomic rearrangements, which may lead to genetic variations and increased adaptability. In the chloroplast genome of Eriobotrya, we utilized RNAstructure 5.6 to map and identify six such small inversion events located in the regions ndhD-psaC, psbC-trnS-UGA, psbE-petL, petG-trnW-CCA, trnR-ACG-trnN-GUU, and psbK-psbI (Figure 4). These inversion rings ranged in size from 4 to 9 bp, with stem lengths between 5 and 17 bp. Further analysis of the stem sequences revealed that they consisted of short repeat sequences with small inversions (Table 3).

Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) consisting of 1–6 repeat units were identified in the chloroplast genomes of 42 Eriobotrya species (Figure 5). The number of mononucleotide repeats ranged from 67 to 82, with an average of 73. Dinucleotide repeats varied between 14 and 17 (average of 15), while trinucleotide repeats ranged from 1 to 3, averaging 2. Tetranucleotide repeats were found in numbers ranging from 4 to 8 (average of 6), pentanucleotide repeats occurred at a frequency of 1–2 (average of 1.5), and hexanucleotide repeats were rare, appearing in 0–1 instance (average of 1). Overall, mononucleotide repeats were the most abundant, followed by dinucleotide and tetranucleotide repeats, while hexanucleotide repeats were the least common. Additionally, E. deflexa, E. henryi, and E. umbellata each contained a rare seven-unit sequence repeat.

The chloroplast genomes of 42 Eriobotrya species were also analyzed for complement repeats, forward repeats, palindromic repeats, reverse repeats, and tandem repeats (Figure 6). Complement repeats ranged from 1,014 to 1,178, with an average of 1,078 and a length of 13–25 bp. Forward repeats varied between 1,215 and 1,402, averaging 1,326 and ranging in length from 13 to 94 bp. The palindromic repeats ranged from 1,369 to 1,380, with an average of 1,327 and a length of 13–62 bp. The number of reverse repeats was 1,220 to 1,322, with an average of 1,265, and with lengths between 13 and 87 bp. Tandem repeats ranged from 63 to 90, with an average of 73 and a length range of 1–78 bp. Among these, palindromic repeats were the most abundant, followed by forward, reverse, and complement repeats, while tandem repeats were the least abundant.

Construction of phylogenetic tree based on chloroplast genome

Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the complete chloroplast genome based on both ML (Figure 7) and BI analyses (Figure 8). Subsequently, phylogenetic trees were also generated from four individual datasets: the LSC, SSC, IR, and CDS regions (Figures S4 and S5). A comparison of the trees derived from these different datasets showed that the trees generated from the complete chloroplast genome provided a clearer understanding of the phylogenetic relationships within the Eriobotrya genus (including the formerly distinct Rhaphiolepis genus). The results from both ML and BI analyses, based on the chloroplast genome, indicate that Eriobotrya and Rhaphiolepis are closely related, clustering together but each forming a distinct monophyletic clade. The ML tree exhibited support values ranging from 78% to 100% and revealed a six-clade topology: the first clade, a basal lineage, included E. bengalensis and E. henryi; the second clade consisted of E. glabrescens and E. platyphylla, both from Myanmar and not yet recorded in China; the third clade included E. laoshanensis, E. malipoensis, E. cavaleriei, E. deflexa, E. dadunensis, E. prinoides, and E. japonica; the fourth clade was composed solely of E. fragrans; the fifth clade encompassed R. parvifolia, R. indica, R. salicifolia, R. ferruginea, R. pachyphylla, R. macrophylla, and R. reticulata; and the sixth clade included E. japonica, E. malipoensis, E. denticulata, E. obovatifolia, E. bengalensis var. henryi, E. nujiangensis, E. prinoides, E. henryi, E. microphylla, as well as three putative new species, E. ximengensis_sp, E. lancangensis_sp, and E. gaoligongensis_sp.

Phylogenetic tree reconstructions of Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis) using maximum likelihood (ML) based on chloroplast genome data

Phylogenetic tree reconstructions of Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis) using Bayesian inference (BI) based on chloroplast genome data

In contrast, the BI tree (with posterior probabilities [pp] = 1) revealed five distinct clades. The only divergence from the ML tree occurred in the fourth clade, where E. fragrans clustered with Rhaphiolepis species. The remaining clades displayed a topology consistent with that of the ML tree. Analyses based on the LSC and SSC regions of the chloroplast genome yielded Ml and BI trees that aligned well with those based on the complete chloroplast genome. Specifically, the ML tree from the LSC region revealed six clades, while the BI tree showed five clades. The SSC region-based analyses revealed five clades in the ML tree and four in the BI tree. Further analyses based on the IR region and protein-coding genes (CDS) also yielded ML and BI trees. The IR-region-based ML tree resulted in five clades but with relatively low support values. The BI tree for the IR region exhibited a comb-like structure, likely due to the region’s lower mutation rate, which limits its ability to resolve phylogenetic relationships. Conversely, both ML and BI trees based on CDS data consistently supported the formation of five well-defined clades. These clades encompass various Eriobotrya species and newly identified taxa, reflecting both their geographic distribution and phylogenetic relationships.

Phylogenetic analysis based on ribosomal gene sequences

The ribosomal gene sequences from the 42 samples ranged in length from 6,775 to 6,803 bp. Following manual alignment, an ML phylogenetic tree was constructed (Figure 9), incorporating members from the genera Phippsiomeles, Stranvaesia, Pyrus Lindl., Dichotomanthus Kurz, Docynia Decne., Malus Mill., Photinia Lindl., Heteromeles, Chaenomeles Lindl., Cydonia Mill., Pyracantha M. Roem., Pourthiaea Decne., and Gillenia Moench as outgroups. The analysis strongly supported the monophyly of Eriobotrya (with ML bootstrap support of 100%), and the sister relationship between Eriobotrya and Rhaphiolepis was also strongly endorsed (ML bootstrap support = 100%).

Conflict analysis between ribosomal gene and chloroplast genome sequences

Phylogenetic trees constructed independently from ribosomal and chloroplast genome data exhibited significant topological inconsistencies. Under the premise of overlapping taxa, the ribosomal-genome-based phylogeny clustered E. prinoides, E. henryi, E. japonica, E. malipoensis, E. fragrans, E. bengalensis var. henryi, and E. obovatifolia, along with various Rhaphiolepis species, into a single clade. In contrast, the chloroplast-genome-based phylogeny revealed notable shifts in phylogenetic positions. For example, E. henryi specimens from Xinping, Yunnan (LAU10006) and Ximeng, Yunnan (LAU10005), clustered separately; E. prinoides from Xinping, Yunnan (LAU10022) was placed differently than its counterpart from Shimian, Sichuan (LAU10023); and E. japonica from Kunming, Yunnan (LAU10019) showed a divergent position compared to accessions from Shimian, Sichuan, and Zayü, Tibet (LAU10013, LAU10017). Furthermore, discrepancies were observed in the branching patterns of E. deflexa, E. obovatifolia, and E. bengalensis var. henryi. Notably, E. fragrans was nested within a major clade containing Rhaphiolepis species such as R. salicifolia, R. parvifolia, R. ferruginea, R. glabra, R. macrophylla, and R. pachyphylla (Figure 10). These results underscore considerable phylogenetic discordance between trees generated from ribosomal versus chloroplast genome datasets.

Discussion

Types of mutation in chloroplast genomes of Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis)

The complete chloroplast genome serves as a valuable source of genetic markers for phylogenetic studies, owing to its highly conserved structure.30,31 Although gene order in chloroplast genomes is generally near-collinear, evolutionary changes can lead to structural modifications such as gene insertions, deletions, inversions, and duplications. Additionally, evolutionary processes may involve events like sequence inversions and the expansion or contraction of genes at the junctions between SC and IR regions.32,33 Research on mutation dynamics in chloroplast genomes has highlighted the critical role of repeat sequences in driving insertions/deletions (indels) and nucleotide substitutions.34 In comparative analyses at the family, subfamily, and genus levels within Malvaceae, a high co-occurrence rate (86%–96%) was observed among repeat sequences, single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), and indels.35 This finding strongly supports the hypothesis that repeat sequences act as predictive indicators of mutation hotspots and suggests their evolutionary conservation in angiosperm chloroplast genomes. In the study of the Colocasia esculenta chloroplast genome, 30 high-resolution chloroplast molecular markers were developed based on the distribution of repeat sequences and were successfully applied to phylogeographic studies of this species and its close relatives.36 These markers demonstrated high variability at SNP and SSR sites, underscoring the value of repeat sequences in predicting mutation hotspots. In addition, indel mutations serve as valuable biomarkers for resolving phylogenetic relationships.37 In the chloroplast genomes of Pinaceae, as few as nine indels were sufficient to resolve phylogenetic relationships among subfamilies. Chloroplast genomes typically exhibit a GC content of approximately 37%, with AT content generally exceeding GC content. The IR regions tend to have a higher GC content than the LSC and SSC regions, likely due to the presence of four rRNA genes within the IRs.38 Structural changes in the chloroplast genomes (plastomes) of plants within the Mimosoideae subfamily—especially the expansion of the IR regions—along with the presence of repetitive sequences, are key factors influencing genome size variation. Phylogenetic analyses based on plastome data have successfully clarified deep evolutionary relationships within this group, uncovering a novel evolutionary lineage characterized by IR expansion, referred to as the IR Expansion Clade (IREC).39 In the subfamily Papilionoideae, analysis of chloroplast DNA (cpDNA) from 18 species revealed significant differences in genome size, with 13 species lacking IR regions. These genomes contain a large number of SSRs and long terminal repeats (LTRs). The core genes are primarily subject to purifying selection. Additionally, these genomes exhibit SNPs, indels, dynamic changes at genome boundaries, and gene rearrangements. Six mutation hotspots were identified within the single-copy (SC) regions. Phylogenetic analysis delineated seven primary clades. These findings provide an important molecular basis for species identification, evolutionary research, and taxonomic work within the group.40 In this study, a comparative analysis of 29 newly sequenced chloroplast genomes was carried out. Each genome comprised 113 genes, including 78 protein-coding genes, 31 tRNA genes, and 4 rRNA genes. The genome size, gene order, and composition of these 29 Eriobotrya genomes closely resemble those of previously published Eriobotrya genomes, with genome sizes ranging from 159,101 to 159,718 bp.41,42,43 When compared to Magnolia liliifera of the Magnoliaceae family (>158,177 bp), Eriobotrya genomes are larger. However, they are smaller than those of the sister groups Cotoneaster wilsonii (>159,999 bp) and Photinia villosa (>160,401 bp).44 Across all newly sequenced Eriobotrya chloroplast genomes, the GC content ranged from 36.6% to 36.7%, consistently lower than AT content, with IR regions displaying relatively higher GC content than the LSC and SSC regions.

The structure and evolution of chloroplast genomes, including factors such as size, GC content, and gene content, showed considerable similarities across different species. Nonetheless, some species-specific variations were evident, including differences at the SSC/IR and LSC/IR junctions, as well as in the rps19 and ycf1 genes. Furthermore, a substantial number of repeat sequences and SSRs were identified in the chloroplast genomes of various species. Minor expansions in the IR boundary genes were also noted, with the recombination events at the LSC boundary likely contributing to the stability of the IR regions.45 A comparative examination of the IR and LSC boundary regions across 29 complete Eriobotrya genomes revealed minor variations in the junction sites. The rps19 gene consistently spans the LSC/IRb boundary without variation in sequence length within Eriobotrya. The trnH gene in the LSC region contracted from the IR-LSC junction by varying lengths (5, 36, 41, 50, 52, 52, 60, 62, 102, and 118 bp) in 10 species, while the remaining species showed a contraction of 51 bp. Additionally, the ndhF gene in the IRb region extended between 12 and 1,074 bp, while the ycf1 gene in the IRa region extended from 1,068 to 1,074 bp. The variation in the length of the non-coding rpl2-trnH region directly drove the contraction of the IR region in Eriobotrya chloroplast genomes.

Highly variable regions in the chloroplast genomes of Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis)

Early studies revealed that cultivar Eriobotrya japonica populations exhibit no detectable variation, whereas wild populations show multiple substitution and insertion/deletion (InDel) variants.46 Surveys of wild loquat resources across 14 provinces in China and several Southeast Asian countries have documented 26 species. The genetic diversity of E. japonica is significantly lower than that of wild species, having undergone a severe genetic bottleneck during the domestication process.47 Although there are differences in the length of the non-coding rpl2-trnH region and significant variation in the trnH gene among Eriobotrya species, the overall nucleotide diversity across the entire chloroplast genome for these taxa was only 0.13%. This value is comparable to the 0.3% reported for species such as Phoebe sheareri, P. omeiensis, and P. americana, as well as the 0.36% observed in Populus species. However, it is significantly lower than the nucleotide diversity of the Epimedium chloroplast genome (3.97%) and the Cymbidium genome (3.70%).48,49,50,51,52 These findings indicate that Eriobotrya chloroplast genomes exhibit relatively low nucleotide variability.

Previous research has demonstrated that commonly used molecular markers, such as matK, rbcL, psbA-trnH, rpl16, Adh, and ITS, have not been fully effective in resolving the phylogenetic relationships within Eriobotrya at the molecular level.53,54 The mutation rates within chloroplast genomes can vary across regions, potentially influenced by factors such as DNA repair mechanisms, replication timing, and selection pressures.55,56,57 A comparative analysis of chloroplast genomes from 42 Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis) species revealed 12 hypervariable regions: ycf1, ccsA, trnL-rpl32, ndhF, rbcL, trnV-ndhC, ndhJ-trnF, trnL-rps4, rps14-psbZ, trnD-psbM, atpA-trnG, and trnQ-rps16. These regions are proposed as candidate marker genes for future phylogenetic studies of Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis). Interestingly, trnV-ndhC and ndhF have also been recognized as hypervariable regions in Horsfieldia (Myristicaceae),58 and ycf1, ccsA, and trnD-psbM have been identified as variable regions in Populus species.59

Phylogenetic analysis of chloroplast genomes in Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis)

Phylogenetic trees constructed using different datasets revealed that chloroplast genome sequences offered greater resolution in elucidating the phylogenetic relationships among Eriobotrya species, including Rhaphiolepis. Phylogenetic analyses based on the chloroplast genome, using both ML and BI methods, showed that Eriobotrya and Rhaphiolepis clustered together, but each formed distinct monophyletic clades within their respective genera. The ML tree exhibited strong bootstrap support values ranging from 78% to 100%, forming six primary clades: the first clade, a basal lineage, included E. bengalensis and E. henryi; the second clade comprised E. glabrescens and E. platyphylla, both from Myanmar and not yet recorded in China; the third clade included E. laoshanica, E. malipoensis, E. cavaleriei, E. deflexa, E. dadunensis, E. prinoides, and E. japonica; the fourth clade consisted solely of E. fragrans; the fifth clade comprised R. lanceolata, R. indica, R. salicifolia, R. ferruginea, R. umbellata, R. major, and R. impressivena; and the sixth clade comprised E. japonica, E. malipoensis, E. serrata, E. obovatifolia, E. bengalensis var. henryi, E. nujiangensis, E. prinoides, E. henryi, E. microphylla, and three putative new species identified in this study, E. ximengensis, E. lancangensis, and E. gaoligongensis. The BI tree (pp = 1) yielded five clades, with the only difference being that E. fragrans clustered with Rhaphiolepis, while the overall topology remained consistent with the ML tree.

With the growing body of molecular data, it has become evident that single-gene sequences provide limited resolution for phylogenetic studies, and different molecular markers can produce inconsistent results. In recent years, single genes or concatenated gene fragments have been widely used to reconstruct the phylogeny of Eriobotrya. However, due to frequent interspecific hybridization and substantial intraspecific morphological variation, the relationships among Eriobotrya species remain unclear. Previous studies using rbcL, trnL-F, ITS, and Adh sequences to reconstruct phylogenies, along with gene lineage analyses, have explored the origin, evolution, and biogeography of the genus.24 For example, nrDNA Adh sequences from 14 Eriobotrya species were utilized to reconstruct a phylogeny that divided the genus into two main clades, though some species remained indistinct. Since single or concatenated gene fragment analyses often include a limited number of variable sites, phylogenies constructed from these fragments typically offer low support and are inadequate for resolving species-level relationships. In contrast, the chloroplast-genome-based phylogenies presented in this study, which exhibit stronger support in both ML and BI analyses, were more effective in elucidating the relationships within Eriobotrya and Rhaphiolepis.60

The conflict between the ribosomal genome and chloroplast genome in Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis)

Since nuclear and cytoplasmic genomes follow fundamentally different evolutionary paths, cytonuclear discordance is almost inevitable. Gene tree incongruence often reflects the independent inheritance pattern of these genomes—specifically, the nuclear and cytoplasmic genomes—which may possess distinct or even conflicting evolutionary histories (nucleo-cytoplasmic conflict), a phenomenon commonly observed in plants.56 At both the genus and species levels, discrepancies were observed between phylogenetic trees constructed from ribosomal and plastid genomes, especially when comparing nuclear and chloroplast data. In a study of the Asteraceae genus Diplostephium, conflicting phylogenetic signals indicated a pattern of reticulate evolution within the contemporary Andean radiation.61 They compared nuclear, plastid, and mitochondrial genomes and provided evidence of introgressive hybridization among genera. Several researchers have proposed that gene flow via introgression can result in phylogenetic discrepancies between organellar genomes and species-level trees.62,63 Since organellar DNA is typically maternally inherited and does not undergo recombination after fertilization,48 even a single hybridization event can lead to the complete replacement of an ancestral plastid or mitochondrial genome. This replacement can obscure its phylogenetic position relative to the species tree.64 Hybridization is widely regarded as a common feature in rapid evolutionary radiations,65 often coinciding with periods of intensified speciation. Hybridization events, both historical and more recent, often lead to cytonuclear discordance in various biological systems. For instance, in genera such as Helianthus66 and Pedicularis,67 cytoplasmic introgression has been documented among species that coexist extensively and have ample opportunities for hybridization.

Hybridization is also evident within the Eriobotrya genus. For instance, overlapping flowering periods have been documented: E. prinoides blooms from September to January, with peak flowering from late November to mid-December, while E. japonica typically flowers between October and December. These overlapping periods provide opportunities for interspecific hybridization. Historical hybridization events within the genus may have produced first-generation hybrids that subsequently backcrossed with sympatric Eriobotrya species over extended periods. Such processes can result in the retention of nuclear DNA from one species and chloroplast DNA from another, a phenomenon known as chloroplast capture.68 In the transitional tropical-subtropical zones of the Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau, numerous Eriobotrya species display evidence of nuclear-plastid conflicts and chloroplast capture, likely driven by hybridization and introgression. The genus is believed to have originated in tropical Asia during the Eocene, and environmental changes such as the intensification of the East Asian monsoon and global cooling in the Miocene may have shifted these transitional zones. This, in turn, promoted secondary contact and interspecific hybridization.69 The study further suggests that overlapping and shifting geographic distributions may contribute to cytonuclear discordance, reflecting asynchronous genetic exchange between nuclear and plastid genomes across species.

Conclusion

This study performed chloroplast genome resequencing on 29 species of Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis), systematically analyzing their sequence features and structural characteristics. A total of 113 unique genes were annotated, among which 17 genes were found to be duplicated in the inverted repeat (IR) regions, and several genes contained introns. Functional categorization revealed that these genes are primarily involved in photosynthesis, transcription and translation, RNA genes, and other essential biological processes. Additionally, several highly variable regions were identified within the chloroplast genomes of both Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis), which may hold significant value for studies of interspecific genetic variation and phylogenetic analysis. Phylogenetic trees based on 42 chloroplast genome sequences were constructed using both maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) methods. The results indicated that Eriobotrya and Rhaphiolepis clustered together, but each formed distinct clades within their respective genera. The ML tree resolved six major clades, while the BI tree yielded five, both providing new insights into the phylogenetic relationships among the species. Notably, instances of nucleo-cytoplasmic conflict were observed, wherein incongruences existed between phylogenetic trees inferred from chloroplast and nuclear ribosomal genomes. Preliminary analyses suggest that these conflicts may result from hybridization and gene introgression caused by overlapping flowering periods and geographic distributions. Overall, these findings deepen our understanding of the evolutionary relationships within Eriobotrya, offering new perspectives for the phylogenetic study of loquat and its related species. The results provide valuable references for parental selection and germplasm utilization in hybrid breeding and offer theoretical support for taxonomic revision and the strategic conservation of Eriobotrya lineages.

Limitations of the study

The study included 42 samples covering 27 species, but compared to the diversity within the genus Eriobotrya and Rhaphiolepis, the sample size is still limited and may not fully represent the genetic diversity of the entire genus. Sampling was mainly concentrated in regions such as Yunnan, Sichuan, and Hainan, with insufficient representation of species from other regions, which may not accurately reflect the geographic distribution and evolutionary history of the entire genus. It is necessary to expand the sampling range to further clarify the temporal and spatial evolutionary history of the two genera. Although the chloroplast genome has advantages in phylogenetic studies, there are widespread hybridizations and chloroplast capture events within and between the genera Eriobotrya and Rhaphiolepis. Relying solely on chloroplast genome data may not fully resolve the complex phylogenetic relationships within the genus Eriobotrya. The study identified 12 hypervariable regions based on the chloroplast genome, and further analysis and screening of these regions are needed to find suitable DNA barcodes for species identification.

Experimental model and study participant details

Plant materials

A total of 42 chloroplast genomes from 27 Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis) were used in this study. Among these, 29 chloroplast genomes were newly sequenced, while the remaining 13 were obtained from the NCBI database (Schedule 1). In total, 37 samples representing 18 Eriobotrya species were collected (Table 3 and Figures S6–S9). Most sampling sites were located in western China and parts of Southeast Asia. Voucher specimens were deposited at Southwest Forestry University (SWFU) and the Biodiversity Research Group at the Xishuangbanna Tropical Botanical Garden, Chinese Academy of Sciences (XTBG-BRG).

Method details

Plant growth conditions

All leaf samples were collected from natural populations. The sampling sites across western China and Southeast Asia are characterized by a subtropical to tropical monsoon climate. Specific habitat information for each accession (e.g., altitude, canopy coverage) is available upon request.

DNA extraction, chloroplast genome sequencing, assembly, and annotation

Fresh leaf material from various Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis) species was sent to Annoroad Gene Technology Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China) for total genomic DNA extraction using a modified CTAB protocol.70 Indexed libraries with an average insert size of 500 bp were constructed and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform, generating 150 bp or 250 bp paired-end reads. Approximately 5 Gb of raw sequencing data were obtained per species. Raw reads were quality-filtered using the NGS QC Toolkit71 (https://github.com/mjain-lab/NGSQCToolkit). Low-quality bases (Q < 20) were trimmed from the 5' and 3' ends, and reads containing adapter sequences or ambiguous nucleotides were removed to ensure high data quality.

Using the E. japonica chloroplast genome (GenBank accession KT633951) as a reference, high-quality reads were assembled de novo using GetOrganelle72 (https://github.com/Kinggerm/GetOrganelle). After filtering 37 initial assemblies for redundancy and quality (Table 3), 29 finalized chloroplast genomes were retained. These genomes were annotated using GeSeq73 (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/gs_documentation.html) and subsequently inspected and manually adjusted in Geneious74 (https://www.geneious.com/). Chloroplast genome maps were generated using OGDRAW75 (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/OGDraw.html). Ribosomal DNA sequences were retrieved from the Rosaceae chloroplast genome database23 (https://lcgdb.wordpress.com/category/rosaceae/).

Chloroplast genome characterization and mutation detection in the genus Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis)

Analysis of 29 Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis) chloroplast genomes was performed using Geneious74 (https://www.geneious.com/), focusing on genome size, GC content, and gene count. Simple sequence repeats (SSRs), consisting of one to ten nucleotide units, were identified across 42 Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis) chloroplast genomes using MISA v2.176 (https://webblast.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/). These SSRs included mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotide repeats. The search parameters were set as follows: repeat units sized 1-5 bp, with minimum repeats of 10 for mononucleotides, 5 for dinucleotides, and 4 for tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotides. A minimum distance of 100 bp was set between two SSRs. Non-SSR repeat types, including complementary, forward, palindromic, and reverse, were detected using the REPuter program77 (https://bibiserv.cebitec.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer/manual.html), while tandem repeats were identified using DNAMAN software (https://www.dnaman.net/).

Expansion and contraction of inverted repeat (IR) regions

Based on the annotated chloroplast genomes, the IRscope online tool (https://irscope.shinyapps.io/irapp/) was used to compare the four main regions (LSC, IRb, SSC, IRa) of the 29 Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis) chloroplast genomes and to examine structural variations at the junctions of these regions based on gene distribution.

Comparison of variable regions in chloroplast genomes of the genus Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis)

MAFFT v7.31078 (https://mafft.cbrc.jp/alignment/software/) was used to independently align three datasets: 35 Eriobotrya chloroplast genomes, 7 Rhaphiolepis chloroplast genomes, and a combined dataset of all 42 Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis) chloroplast genomes. Alignments were manually refined. Subsequently, the aligned sequences were subjected to sliding window analysis using DnaSP v5.1079 (http://www.ub.edu/dnasp/) to calculate nucleotide diversity (Pi) for each dataset. The analysis used a sliding window length of 600 bp with a step size of 200 bp. Pi curves were plotted using R software, and DNA fragments with Pi values exceeding 0.004 were identified as highly variable regions.

Phylogenetic reconstruction

To investigate the phylogenetic relationships within the genus Eriobotrya and its allied genera, 47 complete chloroplast genome sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.31078 (https://mafft.cbrc.jp/alignment/software/). This dataset included sequences from 42 individuals of Eriobotrya (Rhaphiolepis) (29 newly sequenced and 13 from NCBI) and five outgroup species: Heteromeles arbutifolia (GenBank: MK920281), Photinia prunifolia (GenBank: MK920279), Pyrus hopeiensis (GenBank: MF521826), Cotoneaster submultiflorus (GenBank: MK920286), and Sorbus commixta (GenBank: MK920288). Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using Maximum Likelihood (ML) and Bayesian Inference (BI) methods. ML analysis was performed with IQ-TREE v1.6.780 (https://iqtree.github.io/release/), running 1000 bootstrap replicates to assess branch support, and the K3Pu+F+R2 substitution model was identified as the best-fit model by the nested ModelFinder. For BI analysis, the TVM+I+G substitution model, selected by jModelTest v2.1.1081 (https://github.com/ddarriba/jmodeltest2), was applied. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analyses were run in MrBayes v3.2.782 (https://nbisweden.github.io/MrBayes/download.html) for ten million generations, sampling every 1000 generations, with the first 20% of trees discarded as burn-in. Due to varying evolutionary rates across different chloroplast genome regions, separate ML and BI phylogenetic trees were also constructed based on the LSC, SSC, IR, and protein-coding sequence (CDS) regions. All resulting trees were visualized and refined using FigTree v1.4.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses in this study were performed using the built-in functions or algorithms of the respective software tools. Specific statistical parameters, including the settings for the sliding window analysis used to calculate nucleotide diversity (Pi) (window length 600 bp, step size 200 bp), the number of bootstrap replicates (1000) in phylogenetic analysis, and the generations of Bayesian MCMC runs (10 million) with burn-in proportion (20%), are explicitly stated in the corresponding subsections of the method details. The specific parameters of the software used for identifying SSRs and non-SSR repeats have also been detailed above. All software used for analysis is listed in the method details and will be fully presented in the key resources table.
