Production, use and nutritive value of fruit wastes as feed in and around the city of West Arsi Zone and Sidama Regional State
Authors: Bekana Selgan Duguma, Abule Guye, Ajebu Nurfeta, Adugna Tolera, Sintayehu Yigrem
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The study was carried out to evaluate the availability, use as livestock feed and nutritional value of fruit waste in a few chosen urban (within) and peri-urban (around) areas of West Arsi and Sidama Regional State, Ethiopia. The study areas were chosen using a muti-stage purposive sampling technique and 306 respondents in total-102 from each of Shashemene, Hawassa and Yirgalem-were randomly chosen and interviewed. We used established methodology to examine the nutritional values of six (avocado seed, avocado peel with pulp, papaya pomace, mango, pineapple and banana peels) commonly used fruit waste (FBPs) samples for chemical composition and digestibility analysis. Chi-square was used to examine qualitative results while two-way analyses of variance were used for quantitative factors. Avocado, banana, mango, papaya and pineapple waste were the major useable FBPs for feeding livestock. These wastes were fed to livestock after chopping and wilting without mixing to other feeds. Perishability, health concern, shortage of storage facilities and knowledge were the major limitations whereas low price, high demands, good quality, palatability, use as a source of income and reduction in environmental pollution were the major opportunities for feeding livestock. The range of crude protein (CP) contents were from 5.49 to 19.51 % where papaya pomace contained the highest (p < 0.05) CP content while avocado seed were the lowest (p < 0.05) in CP content. The content of neutral detergent fiber was <45 %, except pineapple peels. The range of acid detergent fiber and acid detergent lignin contents were from 11.23 to 24.29 % and 1.69–13.54 %, respectively. The mean in-vitro dry matter digestibility was within the range of 70.67–88.17 %. In conclusion, the year round production and good nutritive value of fruit wastes make them a choice feed as a source of protein and energy in the study areas. We recommend animal performance evaluation and quantification of these wastes for further use by livestock.

Introduction

Livestock production in (urban) and around (peri-urban) the cities is an important sub-sector of the livestock production systems in Ethiopia. Agro-industrial and fruit wastes (banana, mango, avocado, pineapple and papaya) are used as a feed resource by smallholder farmers in and around the cities. Agro-industrial processing plants (such as Yirgalem Integrated Agro-industrial Park) are being operational in Ethiopia, which produces large quantities of fruit wastes. At Yirgalem Integrated Agro-industrial Park, they started production of oil from avocado which is only about 5 % of the total weight. Large quantities of wastes and culled avocado (35–40 tons on fresh bases) are being lost per day (personal communication). It is important to gather information about these wastes in terms of species of fruit and how much is produced per day, the status of utilization by farmers and industries. The use of these wastes could help in reducing feed cost and scarcity [1].

Increased utilization of the fruit wastes was observed due to due to the contents of bioactive compounds needed by livestock and the need for utilizing the different parts of these wastes (peels, pomace, seeds, etc.) [2] by farmers. Previous authors [3,4] suggested that environmental, economic and social issues motivated the use of fruit-wastes as livestock feed. Large quantities of peel wastes from industries and households results in dumping and environmental problems where 25–30 % of the total products were generated from fruit processing alone which can be used as a source of feed rather than damping [5].

Though fruit wastes are used farmers, little information is available concerning the amount produced, problems that may encounter and the way it is used for livestock feeding. Additionally, there is little study, which assessed the chemical composition of fruit wastes in Ethiopia. Nevertheless, utilization of fruit wastes can help in minimizing environmental pollution and feed shortage [6]. Thus, feeding fruit waste to animals could be an option in order to increase the profitability of livestock while reducing pollution to the environment. Such utilization is important in reducing the shortage of feed which is prevalent in the country. Therefore, evaluation of the use and nutritive value of fruit wastes in Ethiopia is important. The purpose of this investigation was to determine the amount, utilization, nutritive value of fruit wastes in and around selected cities in Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Description of the study areas, sampling techniques, sample size determination and data collection, sample collection and preparation, chemical analysis and in-vitro dry matter digestibility and data analysis were made following previous procedures [7]. Chemical and in vitro digestibility were made on selected fruit wastes namely avocado seed, avocado peel with pulp, papaya pomace, mango, pineapple and banana peels following the procedure used for vegetables [7].

Production and use of fruit wastes in selected production systems and locations

Production of fruit wastes

The fruit wastes mostly used by households are given in Fig. 1(a and b). Avocado, banana, mango, papaya and pineapple wastes were the most widely available and utilized fruit wastes in urban production system in descending order. Around the cities avocado, banana, mango, pineapple and papaya wastes were the most widely available and used fruit wastes in their descending order.Fig. 1The major fruit wastes available in urban (a) and peri-urban (b) production systems.Fig. 1

The major fruit wastes available in urban (a) and peri-urban (b) production systems.

The availability of fruit wastes varied among locations. This finding indicates that avocado, banana, mango, papaya and pineapple wastes were the most prevalent fruit wastes in and around Shashemene and Yirgalem. On the other hand avocado, papaya and mango wastes were available fruit wastes followed by banana and pineapple wastes in Hawassa in their descending order whereas avocado, mango and banana wastes were available fruit wastes around Hawassa followed by papaya and pineapple wastes.

The current finding showed that fruit wastes were available during most months of the year and utilized as feed in and around the cities, which is in line with other study [2] who reported that fruit wastes generated from industrial process were the most abundant food waste used as livestock feed. The wide use of avocado and mango wastes in the current study areas is consistent with other study [12] who indicated the rich non-structural carbohydrates contents of mango and the high fat content of avocado. The same author suggested that addition of avocado and mango wastes to ruminants feed is a practical remedy to minimize animal feed costs and alleviate nutritional deficiencies. Additionally, it has been shown that avocado seed contains a substantial amount of carbohydrates, lipids and CP that could warrant its utilization as animal feed [13]. Mango peels and seed kernels are high in digestibility and CP content indicating the usefulness of these wastes as a supplement for animals [14]. The production and use of banana wastes in the current study is in line with previous study [15] which reported that the use of banana wastes as ruminants feed could help in reducing problems associated with feed shortage and poor quality faced by livestock keepers in the tropics and subtropics. For example, it has been shown that banana wastes are good alternative feed to hay in lambs fed banana psudostem and leaves supplemented with concentrate under subtropical conditions [16]. With emerging fruit and vegetable processing industries in Ethiopia huge amount of wastes could contribute in mitigating shortage of feed which is prevalent during the long dry season. The impact of such wastes on animal performance warrant further study. Banana peel wastes could be good sources of nutrients and minerals for animals [17]. Also, the production and use of papaya wastes in the present assessment is in agreement with other study [18] which reported that dried papaya pomace is a valuable wastes for laying hen where they recommended the inclusion level of 5 %. Moreover, pineapple wastes are reported to be useful as livestock feed in the current study which is consistent other findings [19,20] which reported the usefulness of silage made from pineapple wastes as an alternative to conventional green fodder to improve productivity.

The availability of the widely utilized fruit wastes varied among months for each fruit by-product. Avocado and mango wastes were abundant from December to March. Banana wastes were relatively abundant during December to May in Shashemene and Hawassa towns, but from November to June in Yirgalem. Papaya and pineapple wastes were in short supply throughout the year in Shashemene and Hawassa towns while they are in short supply from November to June and not available in the remaining months of the year in Yirgalem town.

Around the cities, almost all fruit wastes were available during late November to May except for avocado and banana wastes, which are available year round. However, the amount available is affected by the months of the year and types of fruit wastes. Avocado wastes were abundant from December to March around the cities. Banana wastes were ample from December to May around Hawassa and Yirgalem. Mango, papaya and pineapple wastes were less available from November to May and in short supply in the remaining months of the year around in cities.

Almost all the fruit waste utilizers in the study areas revealed that they utilized fruit wastes as feed mostly during feed scarcity (December to May). Corroborating to the current finding, it has been shown that [21] the use of non-conventional feed resources were year round but very often during the dry season (December to May). It was also indicated [22] that fruit wastes are used throughout the year.

Practices and constraints in using fruit waste as a feed for livestock

The practices of fruit waste feeding for livestock are shown in Table 4. Most (78.09 and 89.85 %) of the households feed fruit wastes to farm animals alone, followed by combining with salt, respectively. According to the information obtained from households, they get these wastes for free and also purchase from juice houses and cafes. Most (89.32 and 86.72 %) of livestock owners in the study areas processed the fruit wastes prior to feeding, respectively, mainly through reduction in particle size (chopping) and moisture (wilting) content. A few numbers of fruit waste utilizers in Shashemene and Hawassa feed without processing. Consistent with the current results, it was indicated [3] that fruit wastes could be utilized as animal feed as either main feed or supplements to meet the production objective of the farm. It was reported that fruit wastes (banana, mango and pineapple) were fed with or without processing and fed either fresh, dried or in the form of silage [6]. Most households in the study areas reported the difficulties of storing fruit wastes since it is liable to spoilage and lack of appropriate storage facilities which is consistent with previous report [22] which reported that high water content of most fruit wastes could lead to spoilage and creates handling, transport and storage problems.

Most respondents reported no any negative impact of feeding fruit wastes on livestock health, except for avocado and mango seed which cause esophagus obstruction and bloating when fed without chopping and in excess amounts (Fig. 2). It was reported that avocado and mango seeds were one of the indigestible foreign bodies encountered next to plastic bags in the rumen of cattle which they suggested can result in low production and mortality [24]. The perishable nature of fruit wastes was the top constraints affecting its utilization in the current study which is in line with other findings [25] which indicated that fruit wastes are highly perishable. This might be due to the high moisture contents, which make it susceptible to spoilage, a peculiar feature of fruit wastes. High moisture content (>80 %) of most fruit wastes is the main reason for perishability, handling, transport and storage problem [22]. According to the current study, safety issues are also the major problem in feeding fruit wastes which is consistent with other findings [26,27] which reported that contamination of fruit wastes with pesticides, fungal toxins and heavy metals as some of the major factors affecting the use of fruit wastes as livestock feed. This problem might be due to poor handling and hygienic practices during and after processing of fruit wastes. According to the respondents absence of storage facilities and lack of knowledge were the most common problems of fruit waste utilization.

Major opportunities of fruit wastes utilization

The opportunities of fruit waste use are given in Table 6. Low price, good quality, high demand for fruit wastes, good palatability, use as income source and reduction in environmental pollution were the major opportunities of fruit waste utilization as feed in descending order. The majority of the households ranked low price, good feed quality and high demand as first, second and third major opportunities of fruit waste utilization. Similarly, Wadhwa and Bakshi [6] reported that using fruit wastes could reduce feed cost, increase profits, help with waste management and reduce environmental pollution. Also, other authors [12, 1) suggested that the use of fruit wastes as livestock feed reduces feed cost and environmental pollution. Moreover, it was reported [28] that fruit wastes were available in large quantity and is cheap leading to reduction in the costs of feed for the farmers. According to Sahoo et al. [29] fruit wastes are viable feedstuff for ruminants, and its use as a feed could be effective in minimizing environmental impacts associated with disposal of such wastes. Nutritive value of commonly utilized fruit wastes

The nutritive value of the fruit wastes is shown in Table 7. There was significant (p < 0.01) interaction effect of location and type of fruit wastes in chemical constituents. In comparison with other fruit wastes, papaya pomace and banana peel had greater (p < 0.05) total ash content while avocado seed sampled from Hawassa had lower (p < 0.05) total ash content than other fruit wastes in all locations. The CP content of papaya pomace was greater (p < 0.05), whereas that of avocado seed from Shashemene and Hawassa was less than (p < 0.05) the other fruit wastes in all locations. The NDF content of pineapple peel was greater (p < 0.05, while mango peel from Shashemene and Hawassa and papaya pomace from Yirgalem were lower (p < 0.05) than other fruit wastes in all locations. Banana peels from all locations and pineapple peel from Shashemene contained greater (p < 0.05) ADF, but mango peels from Hawassa and avocado seed from Yirgalem was lower (p < 0.05) in ADF contents than the others. The ADL content of avocado peel with pulps and banana peel from Shashemene and Hawassa and Yirgalem were higher (p < 0.05) than the others. The greatest (p < 0.05) ether extract content was for avocado peel with pulps. The IVDMD of papaya pomace from all locations and avocado seed from Yirgalem was higher (p < 0.05) while the IVDMD of pineapple peels was less (p < 0.05) followed by banana peel as compared with other The highest mean total ash content of banana peel in the present study is similar with the result (18.7 %) recorded by Ref. [30], but more than the results (12.71 and 16.3 %) observed by other researchers [23,31], respectively. The lowest mean total ash content of avocado seed observed in the current study is consistent with the result (2.7 %) observed by Okoruwa et al. [32], but less than result (3.59 %) reported by Ibhaze [33]. The contents of CP for most fruit wastes are greater than 7 %, the level which is greater than the minimum CP requirement for ruminants. The highest mean CP content of papaya pomace in the present study is consistent with the result (19.2 %) observed by Bogale et al. [18]. The mean CP content of avocado peel with pulps from this study was greater than the result (7.19, 10.3 %) reported by others [12,34], respectively. The lowest mean CP content of avocado seed in this study is consistent with the result (5 %) observed by Okoruwa et al. [32], but greater than the results (1.6, 1.61 %) observed by Ejiofor et al. [13] and Ibhaze [33], respectively. The difference in nutritive value of fruit wastes might be due to variation in variety, environmental factors and post-processing handling practices, corroborating with other study [35] which indicated that species and agro-ecology results in variation in the nutritive value of forage crops.

The highest mean NDF content of pineapple peel in the current study is comparable with other result (45 %) [23]. The mean NDF content of mango peels in the current study is greater than the findings (11.8 %) by Ref. [12], but lower than the result (23.7 %) observed by Tegene et al. ([23]. The average ADF and ADL composition of banana peel was the highest, which is consistent with the result (24.4 and 13 %) observed by Ref. [30], respectively. The lowest mean ADF and ADL content of avocado seed in the current study is higher than the values (27.57 and 24.43 %) reported by Ibhaze [33], respectively. This variation might be due to variety and environmental factors. The highest in-vitro dry matter digestibility was observed for papaya pomace followed by avocado seed in the current study regardless of location. The highest IVDMD could be due to the lowest NDF and ADF contents of papaya pomace and avocado seed compared with other fruit wastes. Greater digestibility value indicates that it is appropriate to be used as feed for livestock. However, pineapple peels had the lowest IVDMD followed by banana peel as compared with others fruit waste, which could be due to the cell wall composition. The result for IVDMD of banana peel in the current study is greater compared with the result reported by Ref. [36]. In general the variation in chemical composition of agro-industrial by-products could be due to morphological fraction or place of origin, handling during collection and processing, or the nature of climate [37] and accessions, geographical origins, and conservation techniques [38].

Conclusion

Fruit wastes are available and used as livestock feed resources in and around cities. From fruit wastes, avocado, banana, mango, papaya and pineapple are commonly available and utilized for feeding livestock to supplement poor quality basal feed such as hay and crop residues. The fruit wastes are available and utilized during most part of the year to overcome seasonal feed scarcity. However, perishability, safety, unavailability of proper storage facilities and lack of knowledge affects the proper utilization of fruit wastes. The CP contents of most fruit waste are optimum (>7 %) to fulfill the requirement for the normal functioning of the microbes in the rumen. Except for the pineapple peel, the in-vitro dry matter digestibilities of fruit wastes were greater than 72.41 %. In summary fruit wastes have promising nutritive values to be used as a supplement to poor quality roughage. Further research, which evaluates the performance of animals fed fruit wastes, are recommended.
