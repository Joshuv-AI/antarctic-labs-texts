Theoretical and Modeling Study about the Low-Temperature Reaction Mechanism Between Oxymethylene Ether-2 (OME2) Radicals and O2
Authors: Wei He, Kaixuan Chen, Yusen Nie, Quanyi Luo, Liucun Zhu, Kang Shen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractOxymethylene ether-2 (CH3–O–CH2–O–CH2–O–CH3, OME2), a carbon-neutral fuel, was hydrogenated from CO2 captured in air or exhaust gases and reused for synthesis with renewable electricity. In the current work, two different potential energy surfaces (PESs) for the reaction of OME2 radicals with O2 were constructed at the CCSD(T)/CBS//M062X/6-311++G(d,p) level. Based on the Rice–Ramsperger–Kassel–Marcus (RRKM) theory and transition state theory, the temperature- and pressure-dependent rate constants for the relevant reactions on the PES were calculated by solving the master equation. The Arrhenius equation has been used to fit the temperature- and pressure-dependent reaction rate constants. The main reaction channels on the PES are discussed, showing that initial adduct generation and intramolecular H-transfer reactions are the key reaction channels for low-temperature combustion. Among them, the HO2 concerted elimination reaction channel needs to overcome higher energy barriers leading to uncompetitive HO2 concerted elimination reactions. The calculated rate constants were updated to the OME2 combustion model, and the updated model is in considerable agreement with experimentally measured data on the ignition delay time in the shock tube. The present work provides support for further studies on the oxidation reaction of long-chain OME..

Introduction

Polyoxymethylene dimethyl ether {[CH3O(CH2O)nCH3], POMEn}, with the molecular formula CH3O(CH2O)nCH3, has a high cetane number and is an ideal class of alternative fuel for compression–ignition engines. (POMEn), a carbon-neutral fuel, may be produced from renewable electricity and obtained by hydrogenation of carbon dioxide captured from air or exhaust gases.1–3 The absence of C–C bonds in the POMEn molecule and its high oxygen content have attracted a large number of researchers to study its combustion reaction kinetics due to the significant reduction in carbon soot emissions from engines.4–8

Sun et al.(9) investigated the combustion kinetics of POME3 [CH3O(CH2O)3CH3] by experimental and numerical simulations and identified and quantified species in low-pressure laminar premixed flames using synchrotron vacuum ultraviolet photoionization mass spectrometry, showing that H-abstraction reactions dominate fuel consumption and that hydrocarbons with an absence of C–C bonds larger than C2 in the fuel molecular structure are difficult to form. He et al.(10) developed a detailed POME3 kinetic model including 225 species and 1082 reactions based on theoretical calculations, which was validated in extensive experimental studies carried out in rapid compression machines and homogeneous charge compression ignition engines. Kopp et al.(11) studied the kinetics and thermochemistry of dimethoxymethane using high-level ab initio [CCSD(T)/aug-cc-pV(D+T)Z/B2PLYPD3BJ/6-311++g(d,p)] and statistical mechanics methods. The results show that H-abstraction from methylenedioxy is more favorable than that from the terminal methyl group. Sun et al.(12) investigated the oxidation properties of 1,2-dimethoxyethane in a jet stirred reactor and showed that the large rate of H-abstraction reactions in 1,2-dimethoxyethane and the inhibition of RO2 elimination reactions resulted in a strong low-temperature activity of 1,2-dimethoxyethane. Jacobs et al.(13) developed a detailed chemical reaction model for dimethoxymethane by analogy and theoretical calculations and performed new ignition delay time experiments in a shock tube, a rapid compression machine, and a laminar flow reactor, showing that the reactivity of dimethoxymethane at intermediate temperatures is controlled by branching between pathways initiated on primary or secondary fuel radicals. Cai et al.(14) experimentally investigated the autoignition of POME2–4 in an impact tube and proposed a highly automated model development process consisting of automatic mechanism generation and optimization, which was shown to reproduce the experimental results of autoignition of POME2–4 very well. Ferraro et al.(15) investigated the effect of the PODE3 addition on soot particle formation in a burner-stabilized premixed vinyl flame through experiments and numerical simulations, which indicated a reduction in the total number and size of soot particles in the POME3-mixed flame. Li et al.(16) developed a detailed kinetic model for dimethoxymethane based on AramcoMech 2.0, including 121 species and 646 reactions and updating the submechanism for methyl formate. The model was also validated against literature experimental data including ignition delay times, species concentrations in jet stirred reactors, laminar premixed flames, and laminar burning velocity, and the results showed that the model reproduced these experimental results well.

Low-temperature combustion is an advanced engine technology that not only maintains high thermal efficiency but also reduces pollutant emissions.17 Low-temperature combustion is sensitive to the chemical composition of the fuel and this particular chemical reaction triggers spontaneous combustion.18 Understanding the process of low-temperature combustion of fuels is important for designing new engines and revealing ignition phenomena and the formation of pollutants.4 The fuel initially undergoes an H-abstraction reaction, producing fuel radicals that undergo an addition reaction with O2 at low temperatures. Previous research work has focused on alkane and linear ether fuel radical addition reactions with O2.19–21 It is therefore necessary to investigate the low-temperature oxidation of oxymethylene ether-2 (OME2) in order to provide a predictive model for its future practical application and engine design.

In the current work, the detailed low-temperature oxidation reaction mechanism of OME2 has been investigated by theoretical calculations. The PES for the reaction of OME2 radicals with O2 is constructed at the CCSD(T)/CBS//M062X/6-311++G(d, p) level and the temperature- and pressure-dependent rate constants for the relevant reactions on the PES are calculated by solving the master equation. The results of this work provide theoretical support for the subsequent study of the low-temperature combustion properties of OME2, and they also provide a theoretical reference for the study of the combustion kinetics of long-chain POMEn.

Theoretical Calculations

Electronic Structure Methods

Structural optimization, zero-point energy calculations, frequency calculations, and transition state calculations for all species of the O2 addition to the PES of the two OME2 radicals are performed at the level of M062X/6-311++G(d, p)22 in the Gaussian 09 package.23 The transition state structure was determined to have only one imaginary frequency, which was verified by vibrational mode animation to ensure that the configuration was connected to the corresponding reaction coordinates. For cases of uncertainty, the transition state structure was analyzed by intrinsic reaction coordinates (IRCs) to ensure that the transition state structure was connected to the specified reactants and products. The reaction of O2 addition to the OME2 radical has no obvious energy barrier, and the corresponding conformations were obtained by relaxation scans in 0.1 Å steps from 1.5 to 4.0 Å along the minimum energy pathway at the M062X/6-311++G(d,p) level, and the conformations were optimized, frequency-calculated, and energy-calculated.

At the M062X/6-311++G(d,p) level, all stationary points on the PESs are scanned for one-dimensional relaxation in 10° steps along the torsional coordinates of the corresponding configuration in order to determine the lowest energy configuration. The CCSD(T) method was used to calculate single-point energies on the cc-pVDZ and cc-pVTZ basis sets respectively based on the previously reported formulas,24 extrapolated to the complete basis set limit to obtain more accurate single-point energies.

Rate Constant Calculations

Temperature- and pressure-dependent rate constants for the relevant reactions in the PES of OME2 radicals with O2 were calculated on the MESS package based on transition state theory and the RRKM theory and the Eckart tunneling effect correction to solve the master eq.25,26 The asymmetric Eckart approximation is used to estimate the tunnel correction in the calculation of the rate constant.27 The probability of energy transfer during rate constant calculations is described by the single exponential drop model ⟨ΔE⟩down = 300*(T/300)0.85 cm–1.28 In the current work, argon was chosen as the dilution gas, and the collision frequencies of the dilution gas with the reactants were calculated using the Lennard-Jones (L-J) model. The L-J parameters for the initial reactants were obtained by applying empirical formulas from earlier studies29,30 and were calculated as follows for O2 + OME2-1, σ = 5.7 Å and ε = 350.9 cm–1, and O2 + OME2-2, σ = 5.82 Å and ε = 352.4 cm–1. For the dilute gas (argon), the L-J parameters were obtained from the study by Hippler et al.(31)

The one-dimensional hindered rotor was found by relaxation scanning at the M062x/6-311++G(d,p) level in increments of 10°. For stationary points with multiple low-frequency vibration modes, the low-frequency vibration modes are all treated with a one-dimensional hindered rotor model. The reaction of O2 addition to OME2 radicals has no obvious energy barrier, leading to the inability of conventional transition state theory to calculate the rate constant for this step of the reaction. For this type of addition reaction, the rate constants are instead calculated using microvariational transition state theory (μVTST).32

The rate constants for the corresponding reactions of OME2 radicals with O2 on the PES were calculated by solving the master equation. The rate constants were calculated for a temperature range of 200–2000 K and a pressure range from 0.01 to 100 atm.

Results and Discussion

Potential Energy Surfaces

Previous studies have shown that peroxyl radical stabilization, QOOH stabilization, and concerted elimination reactions of HO2 and OH are the channels for the main reactions in the low-temperature oxidation of fuels.18,31,33,34 In the current research work, HO2 concerted elimination reactions via C–O breaking and QOOHs radicals via β-breaking of C–C were not considered due to the high energy barrier. The relative energies of the species and transition states involved in the PES studied in the current work are given in Tables S1, S2 of the Supporting Information.

Potential Energy Surfaces for OME2-1 Radical + O2

The PES for the reaction of O2 addition to the OME1 radical is shown in Figure 1a. The addition of O2 to OME2-1 to produce RO2–S1 is a barrier-free reaction with a potential well of −34.96 kcal/mol. RO2–S1 undergoes intramolecular H-transfer reactions through the 6-membered ring transition state, 8-membered ring transition state, and 10-membered ring transition state to yield QOOH–S11, QOOH–S12, and QOOH–S13 with energy barriers of −12.82, −5.11, and −9.59 kcal/mol, respectively. In addition, RO2–S1 reacts via the 4-membered ring transition state in a concerted elimination reaction to produce OH and (methoxymethoxy)methyl formate (PS11) with a potential barrier of 10.85 kcal/mol (TS11). Energetically, this reaction is not dominant in the consumption of RO2–S1.

PES of the OME2 radical + O2 reaction system calculated using the CCSD(T)/CBS//M062X/6-311++G(d,p) method. (a) OME2-1 + O2 PES; (b) OME2-2 + O2 PES. The channel shown by the dashed line is excluded from the RRKM/ME calculation.

QOOH–S11 produced by the isomerization reaction of RO2–S1 has the lowest energy barrier to overcome among the three isomerization reactions. QOOH–S11 may undergo a 4-membered ring closure to form 2-(methoxymethoxy)-1,3-dioxetane (PS12) and split an OH radical which has an energy barrier of −2.08 kcal/mol (TS13). In addition, QOOH–S11 may also possibly undergo a 3-membered ring closure to be formed as 2-(methoxymethoxy)oxirane (PS13) and split a HO2 radical at an energy barrier of 25.84 kcal/mol (TS14). In QOOH–S11 consumption, compared to OH elimination reactions, HO2 elimination reactions need to cross a very high energy barrier and are also heat-absorbing processes, which is not favorable. It is obvious that the 4-membered cyclization reaction is the main channel for QOOH–S11 depletion.

RO2–S1 may also be isomerized to form QOOH–S12, and this isomerization reaction requires the highest energy barrier to be crossed and, therefore, is not the main consumption pathway for RO2–S1 from an energetic point of view. QOOH–S12 may subsequently form 2-methoxy-1,3,5-trioxane (PS15) by cyclization with the simultaneous loss of an OH radical and an energy barrier of −13.05 kcal/mol (TS17). In addition, QOOH–S12 may dissociate to form HO2 and 4-methoxy-1,3-dioxolane (PS14) by overcoming a higher energy barrier of 18.76 kcal/mol (TS16). Although the isomerization of RO2–S1 s to generate QOOH–S12 requires higher energy, the subsequent reaction of QOOH–S12 by concerted elimination of OH has a low energy barrier, so the generated QOOH–S12 may be decomposed quickly. QOOH–S12 is consumed similarly to QOOH–S11; concerted elimination of OH occurs more readily than concerted elimination of HO2.

QOOH–S13 generates 1,3,5,7-tetraoxocane (PS17) + OH and 1,3,5-trioxepane (PS16) + HO2via cyclization reactions at an energy barrier of 15.2 and 44.0 kcal/mol via transition state TS110 and via transition state TS19, respectively. There is no advantage from an energy-related point of view for the reaction of QOOH–S13 to coordinately eliminate HO2.

Potential Energy Surfaces for OME2-2 Radical + O2

Figure 1b illustrates the PES for the reaction of O2 addition to the OME2 radical. The addition of O2 to the OME2-2 radical to generate RO2–S2 is an energetic barrier-free reaction, which has a potential well of −35.05 kcal/mol. QOOH–S21, QOOH–S21, and QOOH–S21 are formed by the intramolecular H-transfer reactions of RO2–S2 via the 6-membered ring transition state, 6-membered ring transition state, and 10-membered ring transition state, which have energy barriers of −12.37, −14.40, and −15.59 kcal/mol, respectively. In addition, a concerted elimination reaction of RO2–S2 may occur through the 4-membered ring transition state to produce OH and methoxymethyl methyl carbonate (PS21) with a potential barrier of 1.27 kcal/mol, and energetically, this reaction is not favored in the consumption of RO2–S2.

RO2–S2 may be isomerized to generate QOOH–S21, and this isomerization reaction requires the highest energy barrier to be crossed and, therefore, is not the main consumption pathway for RO2–S2 from an energy-related point of view. QOOH–S21 produces PS12 + OH and PS13 + HO2 by a cyclization reaction via TS24 and TS23 for the energy barriers of −2.34 and 29.23 kcal/mol, respectively.

QOOH–S22 may undergo a 4-membered ring closure to produce 2,4-dimethoxy-1,3-dioxetane (PS24) and split an OH radical which has an energy barrier of −13.15 kcal/mol (TS26). In addition, QOOH–S22 may undergo a 3-membered ring closure to be formed as 2,3-dimethoxyoxirane (PS25) and split a HO2 radical at an energy barrier of 28.06 kcal/mol (TS14).

QOOH–S23 formed by the isomerization reaction of RO2–S2 has the lowest energy barrier to overcome among the three isomerization reactions. QOOH–S23 may undergo a 6-membered ring closure to produce 2-methoxy-1,3,5-trioxane (PS27) and split an OH radical with a potential barrier of −5.31 kcal/mol (TS210). In addition, QOOH–S23 may also undergo a 5-membered ring closure to form 4-methoxy-1,3-dioxolane (PS26) and split an HO2 radical which has an energy barrier of 27.10 kcal/mol (TS29). Although the isomerization of RO2–S2 to QOOH–S23 has the lowest energy, QOOH–S23 is followed by consumption mainly through OH elimination reactions, requiring higher energy than the OH concerted elimination reaction of QOOH–S22. Thus, energetically, QOOH–S23 is not as energetically demanding as QOOH–S22 for RO2–S2.

As can be seen from the above discussion, RO2–S1 and RO2–S2 generate QOOH through isomerization reactions, with QOOH taking over mainly through the concerted elimination of OH depletion, while the concerted elimination of HO2 depletion requires overcoming a very high energy barrier and is therefore not advantageous.

Temperature- and Pressure-Dependent Rate Coefficients

The combination of fuel radicals with O2 is the initiating stage of low-temperature fuel combustion and is directly related to the chain reaction leading to autoignition.20 In addition, it was shown that the potential well of peroxy radicals is sensitive to temperature and pressure.21,35

To further improve the understanding of the contribution of the different reaction channels, rate coefficients were calculated for the main reactions at different sites of O2 addition to the OME2 radical. The rate constants are calculated for the temperature range 200–2000 K and the pressure range 0.01–100 atm based on the transition state theory solving the master equation. The calculated rate coefficients are provided in the format of Arrhenius expressions in Tables S3, S4 in the Supporting Information. Figure 2 shows the rate constants of the reactions of the two OME2 radicals with the radicals with O2 at different pressures plotted as a function of the rate constants. The rate constants for the oxidation of the two radicals may be seen in Figure 2, which show a positive correlation with pressure, increasing with pressure, and a negative correlation with temperature, decreasing with increasing temperature. The lack of high-temperature rate constants at some pressure conditions can be seen from the figure and may be due to the OME2 peroxyl radicals equilibrating with the subsequent products faster than their collisional energy transfer. This is because at low temperatures, the reactants pass through low-energy barriers to produce intermediate species of products, but as the temperature increases, they are susceptible to “potential well merging”, and the rate constants are calculated directly in the form of reactants to products.

Rate constants for O2+ OME2 radicals at different temperatures and pressures: (a) OME2-1+O2 → RO2–S1; (b) OME2-2+O2 → RO2–S2.

The calculated results for the rate coefficients of the two OME2 radicals show a rather weak pressure dependence at 200–400 K, as shown in Figure 2. From 400 K, the rate constants for the combination of the two radicals of OME2 with O2 demonstrate a clear pressure dependence, becoming larger with increasing pressure and temperature and deviating increasingly from the high pressure limit.

It is worth noting that the rate constant for OME2-1 radical oxygen addition is larger than that for OME2-2, which is similar to the primary addition of O2 to diethoxymethane.21 A comparison by longer chains of OME may also be required in future studies to see if this is a property of OME.

OME2-1 Radical + O2

The HO2 elimination reaction is generally considered to be a chain termination channel because of the low reactivity of HO2.20 In the current system, the production of HO2 radicals requires overcoming a higher energy barrier and the reaction channel is not competitive, so the rate constants for the channel of the HO2 elimination reaction are not given in the figures.

Figure 3 compares the rate constants for the favorable reaction channels of OME2-1 radicals with O2 over a temperature range of 200–2000 K and pressures of 0.01 and 100 atm. At the two pressures present, the RO2–S1 stabilization shows a negative temperature dependence, with the temperature range of the rate constants increasing with pressure and the reaction dominating, whereas the other bimolecular products and the QOOH stabilization are difficult to compete with.

Rate constant of the OME2-1 + O2 favorable reaction channel. (a) p = 0.01 atm; (b) p = 100 atm.

The rate constants for the combination reaction of the OME2-1 radical with O2 to form RO2–S1, QOOH–S12, and QOOH–S13 show a negative temperature dependence at a pressure of 0.01 atm, as can be seen in Figure 3. The temperature range of the rate constants for these reactions widens as the pressure increases. The absence of a rate constant at higher temperatures is due to the fact that QOOH and RO2–S1 equilibrate rapidly in a shorter time than the internal energy relaxation. Notably, the combination reaction of the OME2-1 radical with O2 to form QOOH–S11 did not solve for a rate constant at a pressure of 0.01 atm. Furthermore, the figure shows that the main source of OH is the reaction of the radical OME2-1 with O2 at high temperatures to form PS12 + OH.

OME2-2 Radical + O2

The rate constants for the favorable reaction channels of OME2-2 radicals with O2 are compared in Figure 4 for a temperature range of 200–2000 K and pressures of 0.01 and 100 atm. From Figure 4, it is known that the main product channel at low and high pressures is RO2–S2. As the pressure decreases, the bimolecular product channel competes more fiercely with stable RO2–S2 and eventually overtakes it in the high-temperature regime.

Rate constant of the OME2-2 + O2 favorable reaction channel. (a) p = 0.01 atm; (b) p = 100 atm.

At p = 0.01 atm RO2–S2 + O2, the predominant product channel is RO2-S. As the temperature increases, the bimolecular product (PS24 + OH) channel begins to dominate. This reaction channel is, therefore, also the main source of OH production. The reaction of OME2-2 with O2 to form QOOH–S21, QOOH–S22, and QOOH–S23 occurs at temperatures above 500 K when the potential wells merge, resulting in no rate constant value above 500 K.

It is noteworthy that the reaction rate constant for the reaction of OME2-2 combined with O2 to form RO2–S2 at p = 100 atm is always the largest in the range of 200–2000 K. Furthermore, unlike at p = 0.01 atm, the rate constants for the reaction channels of OME2-2 binding to O2 to form QOOH–S21,QOOH–S22, and QOOH–S23 show a pressure dependence, with larger rate constants than for the bimolecular product channels. The rate constants of the PS23 + OH and PS24 + OH channels increase with increasing temperature above 1200 K, competing with those of the OME2-2 and O2 combination channels to produce QOOH–S21, QOOH–S22, and QOOH–S23.

Validations of the Calculated Rate Coefficients

Cai and colleagues14 measured the ignition delay of three OMEn (n = 2–4) on a shock tube experimental setup and developed a detailed kinetic model to predict the ignition delay. To further understand the low-temperature oxidation kinetics of OME2–4, a reaction path analysis of the ignition delay of OME2–4 was carried out at 20 bar, 825 K, ϕ = 1.0. The results show that at current working conditions, the OME2, OME3, and OME4 radicals account for 84, 86, and 86% of the fuel consumption via primary O2 addition, respectively. Therefore, it can be concluded that fuel radical addition with O2 is important in the low-temperature oxidation of the three OMEs. It is worth noting that the current model simulation results are faster than the experimental data. A modification of the model of Cai et al. was made in the current work to use the calculated rate constants to simulate experiments with the ignition delay. The ignition delay was simulated using closed homogeneous in Chemkin-Pro software.36

Figure 5 shows the experimental data for an OME2 equivalent ratio of 1 and a pressure of 20 bar, as well as the simulation results of the model optimized by Cai et al.(14) and the simulation results of the model updated for the current work. For the ignition delay at higher temperatures, the simulation results of the updated model and Cai’s model14 are very close and both reproduce the experimentally measured data within a reasonable range. The prediction of ignition delay time is slow at low temperatures, and comparison of the rate constants reveals that the rate constants of the Cai et al.(14) model for the OME2-2 combined with O2 reaction are larger than those for the OME2-1 combination reaction. Calculations in the current work show that the rate constants for the combination of OME2-2 and O2 are smaller than those for the combination of the two compounds, OME2-1 and O2. Calculations by Ruan et al.(21) for the combination of dimethoxymethane radicals with O2 also show that the rate constants for the CH2 attached to the oxygen atoms at both ends are smaller than those for the CH3. In addition, the rate constants of the combination reactions of alkane radicals with O2 were also compared and found to be smaller for the addition of O2 on CH3 than for those on CH2. Since the CH2 in the OME2 is connected to the O on both sides, it may lead to a smaller rate of O2 addition on the CH2 than that on the CH3. Therefore, it is necessary to further verify this property of the OME by more experiments in the future.

Ignition delay time of the OME2/air mixture at 20 bar. The symbols indicate the experiments, and the dashed and solid lines indicate the simulation results with the updated model and the Cai model, respectively.

Conclusions

In this work, the CCSD(T)/CBS//M062X/6-311++G(d,p) method was used to investigate the mechanism of the low-temperature oxidation reaction of the carbon-neutral synthetic fuel OME2. First, two different PESs for the reaction of OME2 radicals with O2 were constructed; then the RRKM/Master equation and microscopic transition state theory were used to calculate the temperature- and pressure-dependent rate coefficients. In addition, the relationship between molecular structure and reactivity is discussed, and the results indicate that the intramolecular H-transfer channels in the 6-membered ring transition state play an important role in the chain branching. HO2 concerted elimination reaction channels need to overcome higher energy barriers leading to a less competitive HO2 concerted elimination reaction. Finally, the calculated rate constants were updated to the OME2 combustion model, and the updated model is in considerable agreement with the experimentally measured data for the ignition delay time in the shock tube. In conclusion, this study not only provides a comprehensive understanding of the low-temperature oxidation mechanism of OME2 but also serves as a reliable reference for long-chain OME reaction systems.
