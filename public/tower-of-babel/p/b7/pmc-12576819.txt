Recovery of High-Voltage Oxygen Redox Activity by Eliminating Residual Oxygen Dimers
Authors: Youngsin Kim, Hyuk-Joon Kim, Dae Soo Jung, Okkyun Seo, Akhil Tayal, Hojoon Lim, Saki Imada, Jooha Park, Sung-Pyo Cho, Youngjun Jeon, Jihyeon Kim, Donggun Eum, Seongmin Kang, Byungwook Kang, Kyoungoh Kim, Hongjin Ahn, Jonghun Park, Ho-Young Jang, Sung-O Park, Joohyeon Noh, Chaelin Lee, Jong Deok Lee, Kun-hee Ko, Hyunsoung Jin, Kisuk Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Oxygen-redox layered oxides are promising electrode materials with the potential for superior energy density; however, their real-world employment is hindered by severe electrochemical irreversibility. The fundamental origin of this irreversibility has been difficult to pinpoint due to the complex evolution of both the structure and redox centers during cycling. Herein, we reveal that oxygen redox irreversibility is governed by the formation and persistence of oxygen dimer states and, for the first time, demonstrate the reversible recovery of the intrinsic high-voltage oxygen redox plateau (∼4.5 V vs Li/Li+), which is typically lost after initial charge. Our in-depth tracking of oxygen dimer behavior establishes a direct correlation between the recoverability of the high-voltage plateau and the residual concentration of oxygen dimers in the electrode material. Furthermore, we identify sluggish dimer dissociation kinetics as the root cause of the irreversible loss of high-voltage oxygen redox activity. More importantly, we demonstrate that these kinetically trapped oxygen dimers can be eliminated by facilitating electron transfer from transition metal to oxygen dimers via redox reshuffling at a moderately elevated temperature, thereby fully restoring the high-voltage oxygen redox activity. These findings clarify the role of oxygen dimer kinetics in redox irreversibility and provide valuable insights toward achieving voltage-hysteresis-free anionic redox in oxygen redox electrodes.

Introduction

Lithium-rich layered oxides have emerged as promising cathode materials to overcome the limitations of conventional cathodes, primarily due to their potentially high energy density. , The capability to leverage both transition metal and oxygen as redox-active centers enables cumulative capacity contributions from cationic and anionic redox reactions, boosting the overall energy density. However, their practical implementation has been hampered by the gradual degradation of electrochemical performance during cycles, which has often been attributed to the irreversibility associated with oxygen redox. − Over the past decades, extensive efforts have been made to address this irreversibility and to elucidate its structural and electrochemical origins. − Despite these efforts, a clear understanding remains elusive due to the complexity of coupled structural dynamics and redox center evolution. The interplay among transition metal disordering, , metal–oxygen decoordination, , the formation of intermediate oxygen species, − and subsequent shifts in active redox centers , creates negative feedback loops that obscure both mechanistic insights and potential strategies to mitigate electrochemical irreversibility in lithium-rich layered oxide cathodes.

According to previous studies, , oxygen oxidation during the first charge generates localized oxygen holes (O2–→O–+e–) within the layered oxide lattice, displaying a high-voltage plateau at around 4.5 V (vs Li/Li+). However, this process typically induces significant structural changes involving severe transition metal disordering, leading to alterations in electrochemical properties. ,, In particular, localized oxygen holes tend to be stabilized either through transition metal–oxygen coupling or oxygen–oxygen pairing (i.e., O–O dimerization, O2 n–, 0 ≤ n ≤ 3), with the latter being often accompanied by significant transition metal migration. − ,, These structural and chemical changes during the first charge contribute to pronounced electrochemical irreversibility upon discharge, manifesting as an unusually large voltage hysteresis in the initial cycle, as schematically illustrated in Figure a. Furthermore, continued transition metal disordering over cycling drives a progressive structural transformation from a typical O3-type layered oxide to a spinel- or rocksalt-like phase, resulting in a gradual decline in discharge voltage, commonly referred to as voltage decay , (Figure a). Recently, it was reported that the structural degradation caused by transition metal disordering could be substantially mitigated in O2-type lithium-rich layered oxides, where a modified oxygen stacking sequence improves structural stability, effectively suppressing the voltage decay phenomenon over prolonged cycling. ,− This marks a step toward achieving a more reversible oxygen redox reaction. However, even in structurally robust, O2-type lithium-rich layered oxides, considerable voltage hysteresis persists during the first cycle. Moreover, the characteristic high-voltage plateau associated with oxygen oxidation disappears entirely after the initial charge, leading to distinctly different electrochemical profiles in subsequent cycles. This is particularly puzzling, as no significant structural changes are observed during the first cycle, ,, yet the electrochemical behavior undergoes a clear transformation. It suggests that the fundamental origins of voltage hysteresis and voltage decay stem from different underlying mechanisms, highlighting a gap in our understanding of oxygen redox reversibility beyond a simple structure–property relationship.

In this work, we show that hysteresis-based irreversibility does not originate from structural deterioration but rather from the sluggish kinetics of oxygen dimer dissociation in the layered structure, making it reversible with sufficient kinetic enhancement. Using an O2-type layered oxide with minimal structural change as a model system, we isolate and probe the electrochemical irreversibility dictated by the evolution of the redox centers without compounding structural effects. Our findings reveal that O–O dimers form during the first charge; however, they are not fully reduced to their pristine O2– states upon subsequent discharge, even when enough electrons are available for complete reduction. We confirm that this incomplete reduction arises from a high kinetic barrier, resulting in an alternative reduction of neighboring transition metals (i.e., Mn). More importantly, we establish a strong correlation between residual O–O dimers in the discharge state and the disappearance of the high-voltage oxygen redox plateau during the ensuing charge process. Inspired by this insight, we experimentally demonstrate that removing this kinetically trapped oxygen successfully restores the high-voltage plateau without compromising the layered structure in the subsequent charge process. Furthermore, the recovery is shown to be enabled by the reduction of O–O dimers to O2–, accompanied by electron transfer from transition metals at moderately elevated temperaturea process we term redox reshuffling. These findings provide solid evidence that kinetic shifts in oxygen-related redox centers are the primary cause of hysteresis-based irreversibility, suggesting that accelerating the oxygen dimer dissociation kinetics would be key to achieving a highly reversible electrochemical performance.

Results

Irreversible Shift in Charge Profile Even with Minimal Structural Changes

We first investigated the structure–property relationship of an O2-type Li x [Li0.15Ni0.15Mn0.70]­O2 electrode obtained through a conventional synthetic route via ion-exchange process from P2-type Na0.75[Li0.15Ni0.15Mn0.70]­O2, , as a function of cycling. For comparison, we also synthesized a compositionally similar O3-type counterpart and conducted the same analysis (see experimental details in Supporting Note 1 and Figure 1). Figure b presents the initial three charge–discharge cycles of the O2–Li x [Li0.15Ni0.15Mn0.70]­O2 electrode, signifying changes in the charge profile, particularly the disappearance of the characteristic oxygen redox plateau around 4.5 V (vs Li/Li+) during the first cycle. To provide a clearer picture, we overlaid the charge (Figure c) and discharge (Figure d) profiles for the first 40 cycles in the next figure set. Normalized profiles of the O2–Li x [Li0.15Ni0.15Mn0.70]­O2 electrode are shown for the first cycle and every subsequent tenth cycle up to 40 cycles, within a voltage range of 2.0–4.8 V at a current density of 20 mA g–1. Figure d clearly illustrates that the overall electrochemical profile remains largely unchanged over extended cycling, exhibiting minimal voltage decay, which is consistent with previous findings, ,, in contrast to the O3 counterpart (Supporting Note 2 and Figure 2). However, a noticeable shift in the profile occurs only after the first charge process in Figure c, after which the profile stabilizes, causing substantial first-cycle voltage hysteresis. To further verify this observation, we comparatively measured quasi-open-circuit voltage (quasi-OCV) for the first two cycles in Figure e and Supporting Figure 2e. It confirms the distinct change in the charge profile and the disappearance of the characteristic 4.5 V redox plateau after the first cycle, implying that the pristine and discharged states of the electrode material are not equivalent.

Despite the evident changes in the electrochemical profile, no direct correlation with structural evolution was identified, as detailed in Supporting Note 2. X-ray diffraction and Raman spectroscopy analysis of the pristine, charged (4.80 V vs Li/Li+), and discharged (2.00 V vs Li/Li+) states of O2–Li x [Li0.15Ni0.15Mn0.70]­O2 confirm that the original layered structure is well preserved without significant transition metal disordering, in agreement with previous reports ,, (Supporting Figure 3). High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images further validate the structural reversibility of the pristine layered oxides during the first cycle, where a notable shift in the electrochemical behavior was observed (see Supporting Figure 4 and Supporting Information 2 for more details on the structural analysis). On the other hand, probing the oxidation states of redox centers revealed anomalies in the first cycle, particularly for oxygen. Ex situ hard X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) analysis indicated that Ni2+/Ni4+ redox reaction reversibly occurs at 3.3–4.3 V during charge and at 3.6–4.2 V during discharge, accompanied by slight Mn reduction due to lithium deficiencies in O2–Li x [Li0.15Ni0.15Mn0.70]­O2 , (see Supporting Note 3 and Supporting Figure 5 for more details). However, O K-edge spectra from soft XAS provided evidence of redox asymmetry, displaying oxygen oxidation at ∼4.5 V in the charge, followed by reduction in substantially distinct voltage ranges of 2.9–3.5 and 4.2–4.7 V during subsequent discharge ,, (Supporting Note 4 and Figure 6). This behavior is similar to what has been reported in O3-type lithium-rich layered oxides, , where the reduction of O–O dimers, formed due to the stabilization of oxidized lattice oxygen, takes place at low voltage around 3 V. It suggests that O–O dimers also dominantly form in the O2-structure, despite the absence of significant structural disorders. While this change in oxygen redox sequence appears to be linked to the shift in charge profile, quantitative analysis of the O–O dimers could not be further resolved using soft XAS. Additionally, although the formation of the O–O dimers through stabilization is reasonable, it is still unclear why their reduction does not restore the original lattice state and the high-voltage oxygen redox activity despite negligible structural changes in the O2-structure.

Correlation between the Disappearance of the High-Voltage Plateau and the Presence of Residual O–O Dimers

Taking advantage of the O2-type layered structure with minimal transition metal disordering, we could quantitatively probe changes in oxygen-related bonding, such as O–O dimers, from ex situ Raman spectroscopy. It is noteworthy that transition metal migration that typically occurs in conventional O3-type lithium-rich layered oxides , introduces diverse oxygen-transition metal bonding configurations, complicating the precise identification of O–O dimers. Figure a,b presents Raman spectra as a function of state of charge (SOC) during charge and discharge, respectively, in the first cycle. The peaks at 606.4 and 486.5 cm–1 correspond to the symmetrical stretching (A1g) and symmetrical deformation (Eg) modes of TM (transition metal)–O in the layered structure, while the peaks at 636.1 and 569.7 cm–1 are associated with symmetrical stretching (A1g spinel‑like phase) and symmetrical deformation (F2g) modes of TM–O bonding in a spinel-like phase induced by transition metal migration. ,, To our surprise, we observed an additional new sharp peak at 477.7 cm–1 (highlighted in black), which emerges progressively during charge and is particularly pronounced in the oxygen oxidation region of the charge profile. In the field of biochemistry, it is well-known that the oxyhemoglobin, a key component in oxygen transport in hemoglobin, involves an interaction between Fe2+ in the heme and the O2 molecule, producing a resonance structure of Fe3+–(O–O)− that exhibits a similar sharp peak in Raman spectroscopy. ,− While the peak position differs slightly from that of oxyhemoglobin, its appearance during oxygen oxidation strongly suggests that it stems from newly formed oxygen species with dimeric character, likely coordinated with neighboring transition metal, i.e., TM–(O–O) bond (see more discussion in Supporting Note 5). Upon discharge, the intensity of the 477.7 cm–1 peak gradually decreased, reaffirming its relevance with oxygen redox activity. We could confirm this correlation from additional experiments where we systematically widened the voltage window during the first charge, as provided in Supporting Figure 7. A stepwise increase in charging voltage, which gradually extends the oxygen redox plateau, results in a corresponding increase in the intensity of the 477.7 cm–1 peak, reinforcing its connection to the high-voltage oxygen redox plateau. Simultaneously, we observed that a reduction peak at around 3.32 V in the differential discharge capacity curve, previously attributed to the electrochemical reduction of O–O dimers in O3-type lithium-rich layered oxides, , steadily increases in intensity (Supporting Figure 8). This trend further validates the link between the presence of O–O dimers and the peak at 477.7 cm–1.

Tracking the evolution of the 477.7 cm–1 peak as an O–O dimer signature provides intriguing insights into electrode behavior across charge–discharge cycles. First, when the O–O dimers are only partially formed in the initial cycle, an additional high-voltage plateau continues to appear in later cycles; otherwise, it does not. As depicted in Supporting Figure 7, partial oxygen redox in early cycles allows an additional voltage plateau to appear in subsequent cycles when operated over an extended voltage range. Moreover, the extent of the redox plateau in a given cycle is dictated by the quantity of newly formed O–O dimers in the prior cycle. Another key observation is that once oxygen oxidation occurs during the first charge, the signature of the O–O dimer does not completely disappear, even at a fully discharged state. As Figure b and Supporting Figure 7 illustrate, whenever O–O dimers emerge during charge, a significant fraction remains in the discharged electrode. More importantly, these residual O–O dimers correlate with the loss of plateau capacity in subsequent charging cycles. Figure c demonstrates that the residual O–O dimer content, as estimated from the peak intensity, inversely corresponds to the fraction of the high-voltage redox plateau retained in the following cycle. This suggests an intricate relationship not only between the high-voltage redox plateau and the formation of an O–O dimer but also between residual O–O dimers and the shifting redox mechanism, a topic discussed in detail later. Meanwhile, the incomplete reduction of O–O dimers during discharge implies involvement of an alternative reductive process to sustain the observed discharge capacity of 287.5 mAh g–1 in Figure b. This hypothesis is supported by our XAS results in Supporting Figure 5, which reveal the concurrent reduction of Mn4+ to Mn3+. These findings indicate competition between the reduction processes of O–O dimers and Mn4+, contributing to the observed redox shift.

High Kinetic Barrier for the Reduction of the O–O Dimer

To uncover the origin of the incomplete reduction of the asymmetrically complexed O–O dimers even at fully discharged states, we performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations to examine the energetics of O–O dimers reverting back to pristine O2– states, as illustrated in Figure d (see Supporting Note 6 for details). It reveals that the oxygen dimer (O2 n–, Reaction Coordinate 0 in the figure) is indeed less stable than the lattice O2– state (Reaction Coordinate 1) by approximately 0.5 eV, while the electron deficiency of the dimer is partially compensated by the presence of Mn3+, as illustrated in the figure below. This suggests a thermodynamic driving force for the O–O dimer to revert to O2– ions in a discharged state. ,, However, despite being thermodynamically favorable, this reversion is kinetically hindered by a substantial kinetic energy barrier of approximately 1.2 eV, indicating that once formed, the dissociation of the O–O dimer is sluggish. Furthermore, this process requires electronic reshuffling, involving electron transfer from Mn3+ to the oxygen dimer, i.e., from Mn3+–(O–O) n− to Mn4+–O2– (Figure d and Supporting Figure 9). To further validate the kinetic hindrance, we investigated the dynamics of the metastable O–O dimer at various temperatures using ab initio molecular dynamics calculations in Figure e. The results show that O–O dimers remain intact without bond dissociation over our computational time scales exceeding 160 ps at 300 and 400 K. Only at temperatures above 500 K does bond dissociation occur, as evidenced by an abrupt increase in the interatomic distance between oxygen atoms. While the temperature and time scales in our ab initio simulations do not directly replicate experimental conditions, these findings suggest that thermal activation may facilitate the transition of O–O dimer back to O2– ions.

Inspired by the computational implications, we explored whether a simple heat treatment could aid in reverting O–O dimers to O2– ions in the discharged state. To test this, fully discharged electrodes were retrieved from one-cycled cells and heated in 25 °C increments from 125 to 200 °C (∼475 K) (see Experimental and Supporting Figure 10 for more details). Figure a presents the XRD patterns of the first discharged state and its heat-treated counterparts. No peak shifts or new peaks were observed, confirming that heat treatment below 200 °C does not significantly alter the pristine layered structure. On the other hand, we found that, as shown in Figure b, the 477.7 cm–1 peak in Raman spectra declines in discharged electrodes with increasing treatment temperatures, indicating a reduction in residual O–O dimers. More surprisingly, this decrease in residual O–O dimers leads to the recovery of the high-voltage oxygen redox plateau in the subsequent charging process. Figure c shows the second charge curves of electrodes heat-treated at temperatures between 125 and 200 °C after an initial charge and discharge cycle. In contrast to the untreated electrode (bottom figure), heat-treated electrodes (colored yellow to red) exhibit a distinct high-voltage oxygen redox plateau, which becomes more pronounced as treatment temperature increases, consistent with the progressive removal of residual O–O dimers. The differential capacity curves on the right further highlight the gradual reemergence of the characteristic high-voltage oxygen redox plateau. Additionally, we observed that the Mn redox peak at approximately 2.76 V (indicated by an arrow) diminishes as the high-voltage oxygen redox plateau reappears, suggesting that heat treatment induces the concurrent oxidation of Mn and reduction of O–O dimers, as suggested in Figure d. The restoration of the oxygen redox plateau was consistently observed regardless of the heat treatment atmosphere, validating that these changes are not caused by external oxidation or reduction conditions (Supporting Figure 11).

This coupled phenomenonwhere the recovery of the oxygen redox plateau is linked to the elimination of O–O dimersis further observed across various discharge states. To examine this, we heat-treated electrodes discharged respectively at 2.76, 2.91, 3.07, and 3.32 V (vs Li/Li+) at 200 °C (see in Supporting Note 7 and Supporting Figure 12 for structural analysis of samples). The Raman spectra in Figure d (left, before heating) show that electrodes discharged at higher voltages possess more residual O–O dimers due to incomplete electrochemical reduction, which could only partly eliminate them. After heat treatment, the quantity of residual dimers is substantially reduced in all samples, as depicted in Figure d (right after heating). Nevertheless, electrodes discharged at higher voltages, particularly the one at 3.32 V, could not fully remove the dimers. We suppose that the driving force for dimer dissociation decreases with premature discharge cutoff, making the process insufficiently fast even at 200 °C. Notably, this incomplete removal of the O–O dimer results in a limited recovery of the high-voltage oxygen redox plateau, as presented in Figure e. Consistent with the findings in Figure c, the high-voltage oxygen redox plateau during the second charge re-emerged when the residual O–O dimers were effectively removed by heat treatment. Conversely, when a substantial amount of residual O–O dimers remained, the recovery of the oxygen redox plateau was inhibited.

Facilitating the Reversion of O–O Dimers to O2–

The conversion of the O–O dimers, from (O2) n− to 2 O2–, should necessarily accompany electron transfer from neighboring transition metals to the O–O dimer. To investigate this mechanism in detail, the oxidation states of transition metals during heat treatment were probed by using in situ heating XAS. Figure a,b shows changes in the oxidation states of Ni and Mn as the electrode was ramped to 200 °C over 20 min and then held at 200 °C for 5 h, with data collected every 10 min over the 320 min heat treatment. The enlarged absorbance, highlighted by green and red square boxes for Ni and Mn, respectively, indicates that while the oxidation state of Ni remains nearly constant, Mn undergoes noticeable oxidation throughout the heating process. − Although the Mn oxidation state in the as-discharged electrode is initially lower than that of Mn4+ due to lithium deficiency in the pristine sample (Supporting Note 3), it is noteworthy that Mn oxidation occurs exclusively during heat treatment. To quantitatively assess these oxidation state changes, the energy position at the half-height of the normalized XAS spectrum was tracked over time. Unlike Ni, whose energy state remained nearly unchanged, Mn exhibited a significant increase in absorption energy during the temperature ramping, indicating apparent oxidation, which was then maintained during the holding period at 200 °C. This distinct oxidation behavior confirms that electron transfer occurred from Mn during the conversion of the O–O dimers to the corresponding compound, O2–.

Further evidence for the reversion of O–O dimers to O2– comes from extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) data at the transition metal K-edge as a function of heating time. Figure c,d shows the EXAFS spectra of Ni and Mn, respectively, during heat treatment. The first and second shells in the EXAFS spectra correspond to TM–O and TM–TM coordination, respectively. Quantitative analysis of TM–O scattering intensities reveals that, unlike Ni, the intensity associated with Mn–O increases during heating and then stabilizes, indicating increased ordering in Mn–O bonding. This observation is in good agreement with the expected reversion process illustrated in Figure d, where the distorted local structure of Mn3+–(O–O) n−, caused by the presence of the dimer and mixed Mn3+/Mn4+ states, is relieved through electronic reshuffling involving electron transfer from Mn3+ to the oxygen dimer, forming Mn4+–O2–. Together with the oxidation state changes observed in hard XAS analysis, these results confirm that electron transfer from Mn to the O–O dimers drives the reduction of O–O dimers to O2– via redox reshuffling. These findings imply that the kinetics of oxygen dimer dissociation would be critically governed by the stability of the metastable Mn3+–(O–O) n− intermediate and its conversion to Mn4+–O2–. Therefore, facilitating the redox reshuffling process should be a key focus of future research to enable hysteresis-free oxygen redox.

Conclusion

We successfully isolated and identified electrochemical irreversibility induced by oxygen dimer states using O2-type lithium-rich layered oxides as a platform. Combined insights from Raman spectra, electrochemical tests, and theoretical calculations revealed that the presence of residual O–O dimers in the electrode is closely linked to the disappearance of the characteristic oxygen redox plateau, driving the hysteresis-based irreversibility in oxygen redox electrodes. Furthermore, we uncovered that the residual O–O dimers (or incomplete reduction) originate from a high kinetic barrier associated with their reversion process to O2–, where a metastable Mn3+–(O–O) n− intermediate traps the dimer state. Our follow-up experiment, designed to liberate the dimer state, successfully facilitated its reversion to O2–, leading to the full recovery of the high-voltage oxygen redox plateau, marking a critical step toward achieving voltage-hysteresis-free oxygen redox. This work underscores the necessity of ensuring reversibility not only in structural transformations but also in oxygen-related redox centers by promoting the reversion of trapped oxygen states through redox reshuffling. Future research on anionic redox electrodes should prioritize strategies to enhance this reversibility. More broadly, the thermodynamic and kinetic properties of O–O dimers presented here offer new insights in other fields where metal ions and O–O dimers are closely coupled, including lithium–air batteries, , catalytic chemistry, and biochemistry.
