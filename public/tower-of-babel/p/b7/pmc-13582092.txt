Hand-side matching in TOETVA: assessing feasibility and surgical outcomes through learning curve and propensity score-matched cohorts
Authors: Xiaoyong Wen, Wu Li, Shiwei Zhou, Yu Mao, Zeyang Liu, Hui Li, Yulong Tang, Xiaohua Song, Xiaowei Peng, Peng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo address the lack of guidance for operating the non-dominant side in TOETVA by assessing whether non-dominant-hand TOETVA on the non-dominant side (left hand–left lobe) is a feasible strategy with similar surgical outcomes compared with the conventional dominant-hand approach (right hand–right lobe).MethodsThis is a single-center, single-surgeon retrospective cohort. Consecutive unilateral TOETVA cases (August 2016–August 2020) were included. Patients were grouped as NDH-TOETVA (left hand on the left lobe) or DH-TOETVA (right hand on the right lobe). Learning curves were segmented using cumulative sum (CUSUM) of operative time into Phase I (initial), Phase II (adaptation), and Phase III (mastery). Phase III outcomes were compared after 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) on key covariates.ResultsCUSUM identified distinct phase boundaries: DH-TOETVA Phase I cases 1–13, Phase II 14–54, Phase III ≥ 55; NDH-TOETVA Phase I 1–17, Phase II 18–37, and Phase III ≥ 38. After PSM in Phase III, 176 patients were analyzed (NDH 88 cases, DH 88 cases). Post-match baseline characteristics were balanced. There were no significant differences between NDH and DH in operative time, estimated blood loss, central neck lymph nodes retrieved/positive, length of stay, postoperative drainage, and complications (P > 0.05).ConclusionsHand-side matching is a feasible strategy with compared surgical outcomes in TOETVA. Findings support incorporating this strategy and bilateral-hand training into standardized practice with phase-based, objective assessment.Trial registration numberChinese Clinical Trial Registry (UIN: ChiCTR2500097005, https://www.chictr.org.cn), 2025.2.11.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00464-026-13104-w.

Transoral endoscopic thyroidectomy via the vestibular approach (TOETVA) is widely regarded as the only endoscopic thyroidectomy that follows a natural-orifice concept and achieves a truly scarless neck [1]. By creating a working space through the oral vestibule, TOETVA avoids a cervical incision, meets the cosmetic expectations of many—particularly younger—patients, and provides satisfactory exposure for central neck dissection (CND) with favorable safety and efficacy [2, 3]. With accumulating experience and progressive standardization, TOETVA has moved from early exploration toward routine clinical adoption [4, 5].

Despite these advantages, TOETVA differs fundamentally from open surgery [6]. The surgeon operates bimanually within a confined corridor, and the two thyroid lobes lie on opposite sides of the trachea, requiring careful planning of instrument trajectories and procedural sequencing [7]. Some right-hand-dominant surgeons habitually perform key maneuvers with the right hand on both sides [8]. While intuitive on the right, using the right hand for the left lobe often necessitates crossing the trachea into the contralateral space, which can lead to suboptimal viewing angles, instrument crowding or a “chopstick effect” restricted tip motion, and a less favorable line of traction–dissection–energy application (Fig. 1A). These factors may increase the difficulty of fine dissection and the protection of critical structures such as the recurrent laryngeal nerve and parathyroid glands [9].Fig. 1Schematic diagram of different surgical strategies. To address these ergonomic and technical challenges, we advocate a hand-side matching strategy whereby right-hand-dominant surgeons perform key steps with the right hand on the right lobe (Fig. 1B) and with the left hand on the left lobe (Fig. 1C). Aligning the operating hand with the operative side should better match anatomic corridors and instrument force vectors, reduce midline crowding and “chopstick effect.”

This study aimed to determine whether a hand-side matching is a feasible strategy with compared surgical outcomes in TOETVA, particularly on the non-dominant side where practical guidance is lacking.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study conducted at a tertiary hospital. We consecutively accrued 298 patients who underwent unilateral TOETVA between August 2016 and August 2020. Patients were grouped according to the matching of surgeon hand dominance with the operative side:

NDH-TOETVA (non-dominant-hand TOETVA): right-hand-dominant surgeon performed left-lobe key steps with the left hand (non-dominant hand operating the non-dominant side).

DH-TOETVA (dominant-hand TOETVA): right-hand-dominant surgeon performed right-lobe key steps with the right hand (dominant hand operating the dominant side).

All patients received detailed preoperative counseling and consented to TOETVA. All procedures were performed by the same lead surgeon. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No.ChiCTR2500097005). Reporting followed the STROBE guidelines.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) underwent TOETVA with postoperative pathology confirming papillary thyroid carcinoma; (3) maximal tumor diameter ≤ 4 cm; (4) no pre-existing parathyroid disease; (5) normal preoperative vocal cord mobility; (6) no previous neck surgery or radiotherapy; and (7) provided informed consent.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) clinically/radiologically evident lateral neck lymph node metastasis (cN1b); (2) uncontrolled severe hyperthyroidism requiring priority management; (3) tumor invasion of adjacent critical structures; (4) distant metastasis; and (5) intraoperative conversion to open surgery.

Surgical technique and perioperative care

Preoperative preparation, vestibular access creation, key steps (thyroidal mobilization, recurrent laryngeal nerve identification/protection, and CND), and postoperative management followed our previously published protocol [10, 11]. In the NDH-TOETVA group, the left hand was consistently used for left-side key maneuvers; in the DH-TOETVA group, the right hand was used for right-side key maneuvers. Perioperative care followed a standardized pathway across the study period.

Outcomes and definitions

Baseline variables: sex, age, BMI, Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, maximal tumor diameter.

Perioperative variables: operative time (skin incision to closure), estimated blood loss, number of central neck lymph nodes retrieved and involved, length of postoperative stay, and drainage volume on postoperative day (POD) 1 and 2.

Complications: transient and permanent recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) palsy; transient and permanent hypoparathyroidism; chyle leak; postoperative hemorrhage; and mental nerve symptoms, recorded per clinical documentation.

Transient RLN palsy: postoperative impaired vocal cord motion recovering within 6 months; permanent: persisting ≥ 6 months.

Transient hypoparathyroidism: symptomatic hypocalcemia and/or low PTH requiring supplements resolved within 6 months; permanent: supplementation and/or low PTH ≥ 6 months.

All patients received guideline-based TSH suppression.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as n (%). Normality was assessed prior to parametric testing. Between-group comparisons used the t test for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables. All tests were two-sided with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Learning-curve and phase definition

Operative time was used as the process metric to construct cumulative sum (CUSUM) learning curves in chronological order. Upward trends indicate cases taking longer than the overall target, whereas downward trends indicate performance surpassing the target. Peaks and slope reversals on the curve were identified as key nodes. Integrating CUSUM trends, the downward trajectory of operative time, and observed stabilization of technique, the learning process was divided into three phases: Phase I (initial learning), Phase II (adaptation), and Phase III (mastery/plateau). Phase sample sizes were determined post hoc based on these curve nodes and were used for stratified comparisons.

Propensity score matching (PSM)

To compare Phase III outcomes between groups while minimizing confounding, propensity scores were estimated using a multivariable logistic regression model including the following preoperative covariates: sex, age, BMI, Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, and maximal tumor diameter. These variables were selected because they were clinically relevant, consistently available across the cohort, suitable for standardized preoperative assessment, and have been reported to be associated with thyroidectomy difficulty [12, 13]. 1:1 greedy nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was applied with a caliper of 0.02. Before matching, continuous and categorical variables were compared using independent-samples t tests and χ2 tests, respectively; after matching, paired t tests and McNemar tests were used.

To evaluate whether the CUSUM-defined phases were accompanied by changes in quality-related surgical outcomes, we compared perioperative variables across phases. In the DH-TOETVA group, compared with Phase I, Phase III showed shorter operative time (148.4 ± 31.8 vs 204.2 ± 48.1 min, p < 0.001), lower blood loss (40.9 ± 35.0 vs 70.0 ± 26.8 mL, p = 0.005), shorter postoperative stay (2.1 ± 1.0 vs 3.6 ± 1.0 days, p < 0.001), a lower rate of transient RLN injury (1.0% vs 15.4%, p = 0.003), and a higher number of retrieved central lymph nodes (8.7 ± 5.3 vs 4.1 ± 2.4, p < 0.001).

Similar trends were observed in the NDH-TOETVA group. Compared with Phase I, Phase III was associated with shorter operative time (148.0 ± 36.9 vs 199.6 ± 47.8 min, p < 0.001), lower blood loss (34.4 ± 23.8 vs 56.5 ± 28.9 mL, p = 0.001), shorter postoperative stay (2.2 ± 1.3 vs 4.1 ± 1.7 days, p < 0.001), a lower rate of chyle leakage (1.1% vs 11.8%, p = 0.015), and a higher number of retrieved central lymph nodes (8.8 ± 5.5 vs 4.9 ± 4.1, p = 0.010). Discussion

This single-center study analyzed consecutive cases using a CUSUM approach based on operative time to characterize the learning curve and, in Phase III, employed PSM to compare perioperative outcomes between non-dominant-hand TOETVA (NDH-TOETVA; left hand for the left side) and dominant-hand TOETVA (DH-TOETVA; right hand for the right side). Two principal findings emerged. First, NDH-TOETVA did not require more cases than DH-TOETVA to reach the plateau (Phase III). Second, once Phase III was reached and baseline differences were balanced by PSM, the two approaches showed no significant differences in operative efficiency, safety, or central neck dissection quality. In addition, comparisons across learning phases showed that, with increasing experience, operative time, blood loss, postoperative length of stay, and certain complications decreased, whereas the number of retrieved central lymph nodes increased. Taken together, these findings suggest that the CUSUM-defined learning phases reflected not only improved efficiency, but also progressive gains in procedural safety and surgical quality. Under standardized training and a fixed operative pathway, NDH-TOETVA appears feasible and achieves surgical outcomes comparable to those of the conventional approach. These findings provide preliminary support for hand-side matching as a practical operative strategy in TOETVA.

Previous study has indicated that surgeons tend to prefer using dominant hand during cut and resection; however, the use of non-dominant hand does not negatively impact overall surgical performance, suggesting its feasibility in specific scenarios [8]. Our findings prompt a reassessment of the longstanding clinical preference among right-hand-dominant surgeons to execute left-sided key maneuvers with the right hand (i.e., crossing the midline). That preference has been shaped by the intuitive dexterity and strength of the dominant hand, path dependency from early endoscopic training, and concerns that non-dominant-hand maneuvers may be slower, more difficult, and carry higher complication risks [14, 15]. Yet, our phase segmentation and matched comparisons suggest this paradigm merits reconsideration, as NDH-TOETVA did not reach Phase III later than DH-TOETVA.

Several mechanisms may account for the accelerated speed of skill acquisition in NDH-TOETVA. First, path-anatomy alignment means that performing left-sided key steps with the left hand avoids traversing the trachea, reduces instrument crossover and the “chopsticks effect,” increases tip freedom, and creates a more favorable spatial relationship among the camera, the energy device, and traction. Second, skill transfer may shorten the adaptation period, as procedural schemas, spatial orientation, and recognition of critical structures developed through prior dominant-hand experience can be partially transferred to the non-dominant hand [16, 17]. Third, workflow and cognitive-load optimization may contribute, because same-side maneuvers reduce repeated midline crossing and unnecessary motion, thereby lowering instantaneous cognitive load and facilitating steadier team coordination.

Consistent with these mechanistic advantages, using the left hand for the left side also improves exposure of key structures and facilitates precise work in the deeper central neck region (levels VI). Visualization of the RLN corridor, Berry’s ligament, and the paratracheal space becomes more reliable, blind-zone traction and awkward angle reversals are mitigated, and access to posterior paratracheal and retrotracheal planes near the tracheal wall is enhanced. Building on these considerations, the Phase III propensity-matched comparison showed no between-group differences in operative time, blood loss, total and positive central nodes, length of stay, postoperative drainage, or complication rates. These findings indicate that, once proficiency is achieved, NDH-TOETVA is as safe and efficient as DH-TOETVA. It may be particularly useful in selected scenarios, such as large left lobes, Hashimoto-related fibrosis and adhesions [18], a dense Berry’s ligament [19] or variant superior pole vessels [20], and central neck dissections requiring deep elevation along the posterior tracheal and paratracheal planes [21], where a same-side approach helps maintain a stable expose–confirm–resect cycle and reduces midline crowding.

Methodologically, our learning-curve analysis differs from many previous studies that treat learning as a single-peak phenomenon modeled by a global polynomial or logistic fit [22, 23]. Instead, we used operative-time CUSUM to detect peaks and slope reversals, thereby defining Phase I (initial learning), Phase II (adaptation), and Phase III (mastery) within a phase-based framework. This approach better reflects the nonlinear and staged nature of surgical skill acquisition [24]. Importantly, phase progression in our cohort was accompanied not only by improved time efficiency, but also by favorable changes in several quality-related outcomes, including blood loss, postoperative stay, selected complications, and nodal yield. At the same time, our CUSUM curves identified not only the key positive-to-negative slope transition, but also early and mid-course local peaks. The early peak likely reflected initial stabilization of pathway construction and instrument coordination, whereas the later improvement corresponded more closely to optimized team coordination, improved handling of complex cases, and refinement of fine motor skills [25]. Minor fluctuations in the curve are therefore better interpreted as natural noise arising from case complexity, team learning, and equipment or process variation, rather than as evidence of skill regression. Compared with a single global fit, this phase-wise interpretation offers a more faithful representation of actual skill progression and aligns well with the Fitts–Posner model of cognitive, associative, and autonomous stages of learning [26].

From a training and standardization perspective, these findings support incorporating hand-side matching (right hand for the right side, left hand for the left side) into TOETVA procedural standards [27]. This strategy does not appear to delay the attainment of proficiency during training and yields outcomes comparable to the conventional dominant hand-dominant side approach in mature practice. We therefore suggest introducing bilateral hand competence early in residency and fellowship training, including endoscopic spatial orientation, instrument handling and exchange, same-side triangulation, and the establishment of consistent traction–dissection–energy lines [28]. Standardization should also extend to team and equipment setup, including camera angle, traction direction, and energy platform placement, in order to reduce procedural variability during training.

For assessment of trainee progression, CUSUM nodes may serve as phase thresholds and can be complemented by sliding mean operative time to monitor stabilization. In addition, task-specific quality metrics—such as RLN identification time, Berry’s ligament division time, and completeness of central neck dissection—may provide useful process-level indicators of technical development. On this basis, a stepwise credentialing pathway with in-theater supervision and graded access to increasingly complex cases may be justified. Standardizing the operative pathway while adopting objective process metrics may help shorten the learning curve without compromising safety, thereby improving both reproducibility and dissemination.

Limitations

This study was a single-center, single-surgeon, retrospective analysis, reflecting the experience of a single right-hand-dominant surgeon at a tertiary referral hospital using a conventional endoscopic platform. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings to other institutions, surgeons with different training backgrounds, left-hand-dominant surgeons, or robotic platforms remains uncertain and requires validation in multi-center, multi-surgeon, prospective studies.

Although propensity score matching balanced the measured preoperative covariates, some complexity-related factors—such as gland morphology, inflammatory adhesion, anatomical variation, and surgeon-perceived case difficulty—were not included because they were difficult to standardize retrospectively. These unmeasured factors may still have contributed to residual confounding.

During the earliest phase, the surgeon briefly used the right hand for left-sided maneuvers before standardizing to a left hand–left side strategy. Exclusion of these crossover cases reduced recall bias and technique heterogeneity, but may also have introduced selection bias and led to a somewhat more favorable estimate of the NDH-TOETVA learning trajectory by omitting part of the initial exploratory cost.

Finally, the cohort included unilateral TOETVA cases only, and operative time was used as the primary variable for CUSUM-based learning-curve analysis. Although several quality-related outcomes were also examined, these were not directly incorporated into the learning-curve model itself. In addition, loss to follow-up in a subset of patients limited assessment of late oncologic outcomes, including recurrence and long-term survival. Future studies should incorporate complexity-adjusted and quality-based metrics together with standardized long-term follow-up.

Conclusion

Hand-side matching (right hand–right lobe, left hand–left lobe) is a feasible strategy with compared surgical outcomes in TOETVA. When implemented under standardized training, it provides a rational and ergonomically aligned pathway that can be incorporated into procedural standards and credentialing protocols. Further validation in multi-center, multi-surgeon cohorts, along with complexity-adjusted endpoints, is necessary to fully establish its generalizability and optimize clinical adoption.
