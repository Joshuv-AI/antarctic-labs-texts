Successful intravascular lithotripsy after all other options failed with stent under-expansion: a case report and review of alternative options
Authors: Philipp Breitbart, Hannah Billig, Christoph Schöfthaler, Grigorios Korosoglou, Dimitrios A Vrachatis, Dzan Horozic, Singhat Tul Llah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

Learning points

Even in high-grade stenoses which do not appear calcified on angiography, it may be necessary to perform predilatation and/or intravascular imaging, not only for stent sizing but also to avoid the risk of stent under-expansion.

Introduction

Stent under-expansion, often caused by severe circumferential coronary calcification, must be prevented as it increases the risk of neoatherosclerosis and stent thrombosis.1 Intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) is an effective lesion preparation strategy to enable sufficient expansion of a stent that was initially not sufficiently expanded.2 Here, we report the case of under-expansion of a stent in the middle right coronary artery (RCA) due to previously undetectable bilateral calcifications in the angiographic images, which was ultimately successfully expanded using IVL.

Case presentation

We present the case of a 62-year-old male patient, who presented in our emergency department due to unstable angina. Cardiovascular risk factors included hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, and positive family history for coronary artery disease. On physical examination, blood pressure was elevated (150/85 mmHg) and heart rate was normal (85 b.p.m.). Oxygen saturation was 98%. Heart and lung auscultation revealed normal findings, whereas the electrocardiogram exhibited no abnormalities. Laboratory markers, including renal parameters, C-reactive protein, and high-sensitivity troponin T (hs-TnT, 7.0 ng/L) were within the normal range.

Echocardiography revealed global normal systolic left ventricular ejection fraction of 62%. Due to typical symptoms and high-risk profile of the patient, coronary angiography was performed on the following day. Coronary angiography revealed a mild stenosis in the distal circumflex artery (orange arrow in Figure 1B), and no further significant lesions in the left coronary artery system (Figure 1A–C). In the RCA, a high-grade 90% stenosis was detected in the right ventricular (RV)-branch of the RCA (blue arrow in C and F), while multiple 75% lesions were noted in the proximal and proximal to mid RCA (green and orange arrows in D–F, respectively).

Coronary angiography showed a mild stenosis in the distal circumflex artery (arrow in B) without further significant lesions in the left coronary artery system (A–C). In the right RCA, a high-grade 90% stenosis was observed in the right ventricular (RV)-branch (solid arrows in D and F), whereas multiple 75% lesions were noted in the proximal and proximal to mid RCA (dotted arrows in D–F, respectively).

Although recent data on whether predilatation is necessary for all coronary interventions are unavailable, older studies do not entirely dismiss the concept of direct stent placement in non-CTO (chronic total occlusion) lesions that are neither tortuous nor heavily calcified.3 Intracoronary imaging is currently a class IIa recommendation for selected patients (e.g. left main intervention) with chronic coronary syndrome, according to the latest guidelines on myocardial revascularization from the European Society of Cardiology.4 In this context, direct stent placement without intracoronary imaging was successfully performed in the RV-branch using a 2.5 × 12 mm drug-eluting stent (DES). Afterwards, a fractional flow reserve measure was performed in the mid RCA during intravenous adenosine infusion (210 µg/min/kg), measuring a pathologic value of 0.75, indicative of inducible ischaemia. Since the stent to the RV-branch was advanced through the proximal RCA lesions without any resistance, and the lesion was judged as only mild-moderately calcified by angiographic criteria, the operating physician decided to proceed with direct stenting of the remaining RCA lesions. Subsequently, a 3.0 ∗ 20 mm DES could be advanced again without problems in the mid RCA (see Supplementary material online, Video S1A, care was taken not to cover the RV-branch with the 3.0 ∗ 20 mm DES, as indicated by the big yellow dot in the online video). During inflation of the stent, however, a waste was observed in the mid- to distal part of the balloon which remained during inflation up to 16 bar (Figure 2A, Supplementary material online, Video S1B and magnification in Supplementary material online, Video S1C). Under-expansion of the stent was verified using stent clear imaging (Figure 2B). In addition, circumscribed, bilateral calcifications were now noted adjacent stent under-expansion in the stent clear image, which were not previously detectable by conventional angiographic images (orange arrows in Figure 2B). Despite post-dilation with a semi-compliant 3.0 ∗ 12 mm balloon up to 24 bar, the waste remained (orange arrow in Figure 2C and visualized by stent clear in Figure 2D). Subsequently, an ultra-high-pressure 3.0 ∗ 15 mm OPN non-compliant (NC) balloon was advanced through the area of stent under-expansion, with the help of a guide-liner extension catheter, but also failed to open. The waste remained during ultra-high-pressure inflation (orange arrow in Figure 2E), while the balloon ruptured at 50 bar (see Supplementary material online, Video S2A). Stent under-expansion improved but remained in the final angiographic image (Figure 2F and Supplementary material online, Video S2B). After consideration of other options to facilitate stent expansion, IVL was considered as the only remaining option since rotational atherectomy may be associated with deformation or dislodgement in case of fresh implanted stents. However, with a trans-radial approach, the IVL balloon could not be advanced through the area of stent under-expansion despite the use of an extra back-up and a guide-liner extension catheter, so that additional puncture of the right femoral artery was necessary. Using the trans-femoral approach, an Amplatz AL-1 extra buck-up catheter, and a guide-liner extension catheter, a 3.0 ∗ 12 mm ‘Shockwave’ IVL balloon was advanced through the area of the under-expansion (Figure 3A). Subsequently, complete expansion of the stent was achieved at 4 bar (orange arrows in Figure 3A; Figure 3B and C). After post-dilation with a 3.5 ∗ 15 mm semi-compliant balloon (see Supplementary material online, Video S3A) and an additional DES (4.0 ∗ 16 mm) placement in the proximal RCA, a good final angiographic result could be achieved (Figure 3D–F) and Supplementary material online, Videos S3B and C).

An ultra-high-pressure 3.0 ∗ 15 mm OPN non-compliant (NC) balloon also failed to open, and the waste improved but also remained (E and F).

Follow-up

The patient was continued on dual platelet treatment with 100 mg aspirin and 10 mg prasugrel per day, and his further clinical course was uneventful during the next 6 months.

Discussion

Moderate and severe coronary calcification, affecting up to 25% of patients undergoing coronary intervention,5,6 is associated with stent under-expansion, which is a strong predictor for higher long-term rates of in-stent restenosis, stent thrombosis, myocardial infarction, or death.1,7,8 Although predilatation is commonly practiced in clinical settings, previous studies comparing direct stent implantation with predilatation have generally shown no significant differences between the two approaches. In the specific context of acute myocardial infarction, direct stenting may even offer certain advantages.9–11 However, while primary stenting can yield outcomes comparable to stenting after predilatation, careful lesion preparation is recommended, particularly when treating complex or heavily calcified lesions.11 In this context, adequate preparation of the target lesion is mandatory to reduce the risk of stent under-expansion or stent loss. Therefore, intervention guiding using intracoronary imaging is recommended, especially for heavily calcified lesions.1,4,12,13

Due to the increased risk of in-stent restenosis or/and stent thrombosis, sufficient treatment of stent under-expansion is necessary. In the event of under-expansion, special attention is required to facilitate safe detachment of the stent balloon, avoiding stent dislodgement or balloon fracture. As in the presented case, the recommended escalation strategy in case of stent under-expansion is (i) repeated inflation of the stent balloon, (ii) application of a semi-compliant followed by a non-complaint balloon at high pressures, and (iii) the utilization of an OPN NC high-pressure balloon. Although our subsequent use of IVL as a bail-out strategy for stent under-expansion is currently ‘off-label’, this approach has been shown to be safe and effective in cases of immediate and late under-expansion.2

In addition, the only alternative option of rotational atherectomy is an excellent option in chronically under-expanded stents14 but cannot generally be recommended in immediate cases due to the risk for deformation or dislodgement of fresh implanted stents and for burr entrapment.15 In contrast, the applicability and safety of IVL even in immediately under-expanded stents were recently demonstrated in a small case series.16

In the presented case, angiographic imaging did not reveal significant calcification within RCA, leading to the exceptional decision to proceed with direct stenting. However, retrospective analysis of this case underscores the intrinsic value of preparatory steps, even for lesions that appear less complex by initial visual assessment. In this regard, lesion preparation does not only help verifying the extent of expansion of the balloon within the lesion but also supports determining the optimal stent diameter. In addition, the value of intravascular imaging using intravascular ultrasound or optical coherence tomography (OCT) needs to be underlined in this context since these modalities aid the detection of calcium arc > 270°, calcium length > 5 mm, and/or increased calcium thickness; the latter detectable only by OCT, determining the need for lesion preparation prior to stent implantation.17

The present case report highlights the role of IVL as a safe and effective treatment option in case of immediate stent under-expansion. This has significant implications on daily practice as under-expansion may lead to high rates of in-stent restenosis and stent thrombosis. However, this case report highlights the crucial role of lesion preparation and predilatation, even in lesions that appear less complex based on angiographic criteria. In addition, intravascular imaging may have helped to detect severe calcification within the lesion, triggering predilatation and possibly obviating the need for IVL due to stent under-expansion.

Direct stent implantation, on the other hand, should only be considered selectively in specific cases—such as in the presence of a large thrombus burden—and even then, only after intravascular imaging for adequate stent sizing.
