Clinical, biographical and healthcare-related factors associated with accelerated health decline in persons with multimorbidity: an evidence mapping review protocol
Authors: Philip Schmidt, Amaia Calderón-Larrañaga, Jose M Valderas, Marjan van den Akker, Christiane Muth, Svetlana Puzhko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionMultimorbidity contributes significantly to poor population health outcomes while straining healthcare systems. Although some multimorbid patients experience an accelerated health decline (a decline in well-being or functional status that cannot be attributed to the natural ageing-related health deterioration), others can remain stable for years. Identifying risk factors for accelerated health decline in persons with multimorbidity could help prevent complications and reduce unnecessary interventions. Our review, therefore, aims to map the evidence on the clinical, biographical and healthcare-related factors associated with an accelerated health decline in multimorbid individuals.Methods and analysisWe will use the evidence-mapping review methodology. We will perform a systematic comprehensive literature search in Medline via Pubmed, Cochrane Library, EMBASE, Web of Science and Google Scholar using two broad concepts: ‘multimorbidity’ and ‘longitudinal studies’. We will search with MeSH terms (eg, ‘Multimorbidity’ (Majr), ‘Longitudinal Studies’ (Majr)) and free text words (eg, multimorbidity, multiple chronic condition*, longitudinal), from inception to date of the final search. All original quantitative studies involving participants in primary care and related healthcare settings will be included. Abstract/titles and full-text screening and data extraction will be performed independently by two or more researchers to minimise selection and reporting bias, with conflicts resolved by consensus. The data will be analysed qualitatively, and topics will be extracted to create evidence clusters. Risk factors will be classified in groups and cross-referenced against the outcomes from respective studies into combinations of exposure-outcome clusters. The resulting evidence clusters will be described narratively and presented as bubble plots. The search, initiated in January 2023, will be updated following this protocol review to reflect the most current evidence; exact dates will be reported in the results manuscript.Ethics and disseminationDue to the nature of the proposed evidence map, ethics approval will not be required. Results from our research will be disseminated through publications in peer-reviewed journals and presentations at local, national and international conferences.OSF registration DOI https://osf.io/q72xa/ Keywords: Risk Factors, Review, Multimorbidity, GENERAL MEDICINE (see Internal Medicine), Primary Care

Introduction

Multimorbidity, commonly defined as the co-occurrence of at least two chronic conditions in the same individual,1 presents a significant health and economic burden. A systematic review published in 20232 reported that the prevalence of multimorbidity in Europe is approximately 39.2% (95% CI: 33.2% to 45.2%), with a higher prevalence observed in North America (43.1%, 95% CI: 32.3% to 53.8%). The overall global prevalence of multimorbidity in adult community settings is 37.2% (95% CI=34.9% to 39.4%).2 The prevalence of multimorbidity rises with increasing age: 20% of individuals experience multimorbidity before the age of 40,3 two-thirds of those over 65 have it,4 5 and by 70, 75% of the population is affected.6

The prevalence of multimorbidity is rapidly increasing. While this trend is especially pronounced in low-income and middle-income countries, where populations are ageing at a faster rate,7 high-income countries are also experiencing a significant surge.8 This growth in prevalence is primarily driven by an ageing population and advancements in medical care, which have improved survival rates for individuals with chronic illnesses. Additionally, important changes in lifestyle (eg, smoking cessation, reduced alcohol consumption, improved diet and sleep quality, sufficient physical activity) may contribute to prolonging life expectancy in this population, further increasing the prevalence of multimorbidity.9,11 A 2017 report by the Academy of Medical Sciences12 indicates that, within primary healthcare services of many high-income countries, multimorbidity is regarded as a norm rather than an exception. In the next 20 years, multimorbidity is anticipated to rise even more, especially involving complex multimorbidity (four or more diseases affecting different organ systems13). Patients over the age of 65 are expected to spend nearly two-thirds of their additional years of life expectancy with four or more chronic conditions.13

Multimorbidity can be associated with worse clinical outcomes, such as poor quality of life,14 increased disability and functional decline,15 and premature death.16 Due to the synergistic effects between multiple chronic conditions and their consequences, one of which is polypharmacy (intake of five or more drugs), the negative impact of multimorbidity on health can surpass the impact of individual conditions.17 Patients with multimorbidity, therefore, require more complex clinical management which can also lead to increased care costs.

Attempts to study multimorbidity have been hindered by a lack of consensus on how multimorbidity is defined and measured, the number of chronic conditions to be included in the count of morbidities, or the value of simple conditions counts vs weighted indexes,18 19 among other challenges. Another challenge is that the development and progression of multimorbidity involves numerous interrelated factors, including ageing-related biological factors, socioeconomic, psychosocial and behavioural determinants, as well as medical interventions and treatments.18 20 While the cross-sectional approach may offer insights into multimorbidity clusters, the development of multimorbidity over time and the sequencing of diseases, which are crucial for prevention and clinical management, remain undiscovered. Longitudinal study designs are warranted to better understand the development of multimorbidity and disease sequencing over time, which is crucial for designing preventive strategies.

While there are many cross-sectional studies evaluating risk factors for negative health outcomes in patients with multimorbidity,21,23 this topic lacks extensive investigation in longitudinal studies. A systematic review by Cezard et al24 identified 35 studies evaluating multimorbidity longitudinally. These studies focused on identifying patterns of chronic conditions and their trajectories over time. Factors associated with the onset and progression of multimorbidity in these studies included older age, increased socioeconomic and area-level deprivation, excess body weight and suboptimal health behaviours.24 Of importance, different studies suggest that the progression of multimorbidity and incremental decline in health may not follow a linear pattern but instead represent a multistage process with diverse trajectories in disease development.24,27

Individual-level multimorbidity phenotypes may evolve or change over time, leading to different trajectories.27 While some patients maintain a stable health status for a long time, others experience health declines surpassing the natural age-related deterioration.28 This ‘accelerated health decline’ may be triggered by specific factors or events that cause irreversible damage to patients’ health, preventing them from returning to their previous state.28 Despite the relevance of such triggers, little research has been devoted to their systematic identification and further classification. Timely detection of such risk factors could help identify key targets and facilitate the development of interventions aimed at minimising the progression and negative impacts of multimorbidity. Conversely, in cases where further medical intervention may offer limited benefit, a structured de-escalation of care may be appropriate. At the patient level, this approach can enable clearer communication about prognosis, improve care planning and ensure that treatment decisions are more closely aligned with individual health goals. At the population level, the ability to recognise such patterns may inform the development of risk-based care models and guide resource allocation towards patients most likely to benefit from proactive or tailored interventions.

In our study, we aim to assess the scope of existing evidence on the association between clinical, biographical and healthcare-related factors with accelerated health decline in multimorbid patients, as evaluated in longitudinal studies, and to identify clusters of evidence related to specific exposure-outcome combinations. This will help us understand future research priorities and directions for both original and knowledge synthesis studies. Based on our findings, we will issue recommendations to address gaps in primary research where evidence is lacking and to encourage synthesis of knowledge in areas with a substantial body of evidence.

Methods and analysis

We will conduct an evidence mapping review (EMR). This approach was developed “to systematically identify and report the range of research activity in broad topic areas or policy domains”.29 A systematic review on this methodology conducted by Miake-Lye et al30 found that the evidence mapping review is defined diversely by different studies. Most studies, however, include a systematic search of a broad field to identify either gaps in knowledge or future research needs, or both, and present results in a user-friendly format, often a visual figure or graph. Evidence maps are especially useful for addressing broad research questions, as their focus is on exploring evidence rather than summarising it. The EMR approach follows a scoping review methodology30 31 but does not include a comprehensive synthesis of the results from the included original studies. Instead, it aims to organise the data in a cross-tabular format, categorising the literature according to the resulting clusters of evidence.

This protocol will adhere, where applicable, to the ‘Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews’ (PRISMA-ScR) checklist (online supplemental file 1).

Review question and objectives

In our study, we will address the following review question: “What is the evidence on the clinical, biographical, and healthcare-related factors associated with an accelerated health decline in persons with multimorbidity?”. This broad research question was defined after several meetings with known experts on multimorbidity research in Germany, Spain, Sweden, UK, Australia and Singapore. Our specific objectives will be (1) to synthesise the scope of evidence on the clinical, biographical and healthcare-related factors associated with accelerated health decline in patients with multimorbidity; (2) to identify clusters of evidence on risk factors associated with specific outcomes reflecting an accelerated health decline; and (3) to show the main knowledge gaps.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined according to the PICO (Population, Intervention/Exposure, Comparison, and Outcome) structure. Preliminary criteria were piloted and further refined after several discussions with collaborating partners. Population, settings: adult (ie, 18 years old and older) patients with multimorbidity (ie, the simultaneous presence of two or more chronic diseases)1 in primary care-related settings (eg, community, clinical). We will not apply a strict definition of multimorbidity as part of the inclusion or exclusion criteria. Studies will be eligible if they explicitly mention multimorbidity or examine populations with two or more coexisting health conditions, regardless of the specific terminology used or the operationalisation of chronicity. This inclusive approach is intended to capture the full range of conceptualisations of multimorbidity across the literature. We focused on the primary care setting because, in our clinical and research experience, it is particularly well suited for identifying and monitoring patients with multimorbidity—especially those at risk of accelerated decline—given its longitudinal approach and generalist scope. That said, we do not exclude patients seen in hospital settings, provided their care remains within the realm of generalist medicine. Specialist ambulatory services will be included for those systems that consider them part of the primary care arrangements. Exposure: clinical (eg, specific disease patterns, biomarkers, nutritional status), biographical (eg, specific life events, changes in life circumstances) and healthcare-related factors (eg, treatment regimens, drug-drug interactions, drug-disease interactions), as well as other literature-informed relevant factors for patients with multimorbidity that could have an impact on accelerating their health decline. Outcome: the term ‘accelerated health decline’ has not been well defined in the literature. As shown by our pilot search, the following endpoints have been previously studied to describe the negative impact of multimorbidity on health: functional decline32 and cognitive impairment,26 unplanned healthcare utilisation (eg, hospital readmissions, acute care use),33 decreasing quality of life,7 falls34 and mortality,17 among others. Our theoretical definition of ‘accelerated health decline’ is a decline in health that progresses more rapidly than the median rate of decline for a reference population of the same age. Nevertheless, given that we do not expect the majority of included studies to report the speed of health decline across percentiles or provide an explicit measure of the rate of decline (eg, change in health status per unit time), the operational definition of ‘accelerated health decline’ will be a decline in well-being and/or functional status that is measured through clinical parameters and/or profiles of healthcare use (eg, unplanned hospitalisations, emergency department visits, fall-related fractures, disability, dementia, institutionalisation, mortality). This pragmatic approach allows inclusion of relevant evidence even when precise rates of decline are unavailable. When available, studies reporting rate-based measures will be considered and highlighted accordingly. The classification of outcomes will be reviewed and adjusted iteratively based on the findings from the literature. This process will facilitate content analysis and help identify research clusters and knowledge gaps. Study designs: all original quantitative studies examining the longitudinal association of clinical, biographical or healthcare-related factors with negative outcomes reflecting an accelerated health decline in adult patients with multimorbidity will be eligible. We will exclude studies focusing on pediatric populations, defined as patients younger than 18 years old, as multimorbidity in this population may differ substantially from that in adults due to variations in physiology, patterns, the nature of the most burdensome conditions and the dynamic developmental variations affecting health status. Of note, in PubMed, it is not possible to specifically include populations aged 18 years and older, as the closest available option is the MeSH-defined ‘19+years’ category. We will use this filter to reduce the number of irrelevant pediatric studies, based on insights from our pilot search. In other databases, we will apply the most appropriate filters to reflect the 18+ population. COVID-19/SARS-CoV-2-related studies will be excluded, given that, even if the pandemic had a great impact on the health of patients with multimorbidity as well as on many of the above-mentioned risk factors (eg, loneliness, lack of social support, lower access to healthcare services), this topic deserves dedicated research efforts. Since we are focusing on the health problems that can be addressed by primary care services, studies focusing on highly specialised settings, such as transplant patients, postoperative in-hospital settings, intensive care settings or studies conducted in nursing homes, will also be excluded. We will moreover exclude qualitative studies, case reports, opinion papers, commentaries and editorials. Studies on multimorbidity focusing exclusively on methodological aspects (eg, improving the performance of predictive models) will not be included. Reviews with a structured literature search (eg, systematic, scoping, evidence mapping) will be included for the identification of relevant original studies. Unstructured reviews (eg, narrative reviews), studies lacking a detailed description of the methodology and conference abstracts without available full-text papers will be excluded. We will not apply any restrictions regarding language, geographical location or publication date. The search, initiated in January 2023, will be updated following this protocol review to reflect the most current evidence; exact dates will be reported in the results manuscript.

Databases and search strategy

We will perform a systematic comprehensive literature search in Medline (via Pubmed), Cochrane Library, EMBASE, Web of Science and Google Scholar. In order to cover all possible risk factors for an accelerated health decline in people with multimorbidity evaluated through longitudinal studies, we will use two broad concepts: ‘multimorbidity’ and ‘longitudinal studies’. We will search with MeSH terms (eg, ‘Multimorbidity’ (Majr), ‘Longitudinal Studies’ (Majr)) and free text words (eg, multimorbidity, multiple chronic condition*, longitudinal). The search strategies will be designed for PubMed (online supplemental file 2) and will be adjusted to other databases. We will conduct a thorough search of references in relevant articles and will perform cited reference research, focusing on forward citation tracking of the most pertinent papers. A specialised medical sciences librarian will be involved in fine-tuning the search strategy. All screening and data extraction steps will be conducted independently by at least two reviewers to minimise selection and reporting bias.

Study selection

The studies identified by the literature search across all databases will be exported to the Systematic Reviews platform RAYYAN (https://www.rayyan.ai/). After duplicate removal, titles/abstracts will be screened by two or more reviewers from the research team. Before the screening, a stepwise calibration test will be performed using a sample of 30 studies, aiming to obtain 80% agreement between reviewers. In case 80% agreement is not achieved, the inclusion and exclusion criteria will be refined. This calibration step will be repeated until the 80% agreement threshold is reached. The reasons for inclusion/exclusion will be documented, and conflicts will be resolved by discussion and consensus. The papers included after titles/abstracts screening will be exported to the reference manager Zotero 7 for Windows (https://www.zotero.org/download/), and full-text articles will be retrieved. The full texts will be screened for inclusion by two or more independent reviewers from the team, with conflicts resolved by consensus. The included studies will be subjected to discussion by the research group. Full-text screening and documentation of exclusion reasons will be conducted in Rayyan. Zotero 7 will be used in parallel to manage full-text documents and facilitate team access.

Data extraction

An Excel file will be prepared to organise the extracted data, which will include information on the research study design, sample size, settings (healthcare context, country of origin, study period), patient characteristics (age, sex/gender, definition of multimorbidity), the aim of the study, characteristics of the exposure (clinical, biographical or healthcare-related factors), clinical and healthcare utilisation outcomes (hospitalisations, physician visits, emergency department visits, disability, dementia, institutionalisation, mortality), time of follow-up (months) and methods of analysis. We will also extract detailed information on how multimorbidity is defined or operationalised (the number and types of conditions considered, whether a predefined list or index was used, the timeframe for chronicity (if reported), and whether conditions were weighted or counted equally). The data extraction sheet will undergo a calibration test on five full-text papers for reliability assurance. Data extraction will be performed by two or more reviewers independently.

Reporting the results

The identified studies will initially be described based on the characteristics mentioned in the previous section. Content analysis will then be performed to cluster and summarise the types of risk factors and associated outcomes reported in the studies. Initially, two researchers will independently code the extracted data using an inductive approach to identify all risk factors and outcomes. Codes will then be grouped into categories and themes through iterative discussion within the research team and refined as needed to identify and aggregate overarching themes and ensure consistency and comprehensiveness. These results will be recorded in the data extraction file, which will subsequently be reviewed by other researchers. All categories of risk factors and outcomes for data analysis will be adjusted as needed throughout the development of the evidence map and finalised in consultation with the research team. As we will include all the risk factors that can be linked to the accelerated health decline, we anticipate identifying a relatively large amount of risk factors and a certain heterogeneity in exposure definitions between different studies. Therefore, the identified risk factors will be aggregated into broader groups, under the three domains: clinical, biographical and healthcare-related factors. Given the potential for some risk factors to overlap across domains, the research team will review these cases collectively. Decisions on classification will be made by consensus, considering the primary context and relevance as presented in the source studies. When appropriate, overlapping factors will be acknowledged in multiple domains to accurately reflect their multidimensional nature. The groups of risk factors will then be cross-referenced against the outcomes from the respective studies. A risk factors group belonging to one of the three main domains, along with a corresponding outcome, will be defined as a cluster of evidence. The number of studies with similar combinations of clusters will be counted. The resulting evidence clusters will be described narratively and presented as bubble plots. Based on the size of the clusters, knowledge gaps will be identified, and clusters with sufficient information will be targeted for subsequent knowledge synthesis studies.

Patient and public involvement

The protocol was discussed with a group of international experts in multimorbidity research at regular meetings until the agreement was achieved for all stages of the process. Patients were not involved in this study.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics

As an evidence mapping review, the study did not require ethics approval.

Dissemination

Study results will be presented at pertinent national and international conferences targeting healthcare providers and researchers and submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals. Recommendations for further original research will be formulated based on identified knowledge gaps, while performing knowledge synthesis will be recommended for clusters with sufficient evidence.
