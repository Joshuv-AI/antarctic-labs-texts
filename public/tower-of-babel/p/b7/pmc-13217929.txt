ERK1/2 hijack STAT3 via Thr714 phosphorylation to suppress gene expression
Authors: Jingjing Liu, Yimei Hao, Huijun Yu, Tianyuan Peng, Xiaomei Lu, Y Eugene Chinn, Jicheng Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHyperactivation of STAT3 has been observed in various tumor types. However, we observed that STAT3 transcriptional activity was undetectable in cells exhibiting high levels of Tyr705 phosphorylation when Thr714 was hyperphosphorylated. The hydroxyl group of Thr714 was indispensable for STAT3 transcriptional activity, and Thr714 mutations were epistatic to other STAT3 modifications. While STAT3-Y705F functioned as a loss-of-function mutant, STAT3-T714E acted as a gain-of-function mutant that repressed transcription. Consistent with these findings, Stat3+/T714E mice exhibited severe primary immunodeficiency, reduced fertility, and increased mortality. Because Thr714 contains an S/T-P motif, it shares upstream kinases with Ser727. Notably, Thr714 phosphorylation exhibited marked regional heterogeneity in lung tumor tissues. Transcriptomic analyses revealed that, in KRAS-driven cancer cells, STAT3 predominantly functions as a transcriptional repressor. Treatment with MAPK inhibitors induced Thr714 dephosphorylation, thereby enhancing feedback STAT3 activation. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the transcriptional activator STAT3 can be converted into a transcriptional repressor through ERK1/2-mediated phosphorylation of Thr714.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02818-y.Keywords: STAT3, Thr714 phosphorylation, ERK1/2, Suppressor

Introduction

Signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) is a latent transcription factor regulated by phosphorylation at multiple residues. In response to IL-6 family cytokines, phosphorylation of Tyr705 by JAK1 and JAK2 is essential for STAT3 activation [1] and is widely used as a marker of STAT3 activity. Accordingly, mutation of Tyr705 to phenylalanine (Y705F) is commonly employed as a dominant-negative STAT3 mutant [2]. In contrast, the role of Ser727 phosphorylation remains incompletely understood despite extensive investigation. Initially identified as a positive regulatory modification that facilitates the recruitment of transcriptional coactivators [3], Ser727 phosphorylation was thought to be essential for maximal STAT3 transcriptional activity [4–6]. However, subsequent studies have demonstrated that Ser727 can be phosphorylated by multiple kinases independently of Tyr705 phosphorylation, leading to repression of STAT3 transcriptional activity through an as-yet-unknown mechanism [7–11]. Notably, Ser727-phosphorylated STAT3 has been reported to enhance RNA polymerase II-mediated transcriptional elongation rather than transcriptional initiation [12]. Additionally, accumulating evidence suggests that Ser727 phosphorylation contributes to STAT3-driven tumorigenesis independently of Tyr705 phosphorylation [13]. Mitochondrial Ser727-phosphorylated STAT3 has been implicated in the regulation of cellular respiration [14], metabolic reprogramming [15], and mitochondrial transcription [16], thereby promoting oncogene-driven tumorigenic transformation [16–18]. Paradoxically, several oncogene-targeted therapies, such as MEK inhibitors, induce adverse effects through feedback STAT3 activation [19, 20], potentially as a consequence of Ser727 dephosphorylation.

In addition to its well-characterized transcriptional activation function, STAT3 can also suppress gene transcription. For instance, STAT3 regulates glucose homeostasis by directly binding to promoters and suppressing gluconeogenic gene expression in mouse livers [21–23]. Similarly, Tyr705-phosphorylated STAT3 represses excessive production of interferons (IFNs) during influenza A virus infection [24]. However, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. It has been reported that inhibition of TGF-β1 expression by activated STAT3 is reversed by high-glucose-activated ERK1/2 [25]. Moreover, repression of the pro-apoptotic gene and stress-induced mTOR inhibitor REDD1 by STAT3 requires both the N-terminal domain and Tyr705 phosphorylation [26, 27]. Interestingly, binding of STAT3 to canonical STAT-binding sites within the REDD1 promoter is not necessary for STAT3-mediated reduction of REDD1 expression [27], suggesting a non-canonical mechanism. Consistent with this notion, STAT3 has been proposed to function as an adaptor protein rather than a classical transcription factor in certain contexts. For example, STAT3 suppresses SNAI expression by facilitating glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β)-mediated degradation of SNAI through modulation of its phosphorylation [28]. In KRAS-mutant lung adenocarcinoma, STAT3 inhibits NF-κB-induced IL-8 expression by sequestering NF-κB in the cytoplasm [29]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the mechanisms underlying STAT3-mediated transcriptional repression remain poorly defined.

In this study, we identify hyperphosphorylation of STAT3 at the Thr714 residue and demonstrate that its hydroxyl group is indispensable for STAT3 transcriptional activity. All Thr714 mutations function as epistatic and dominant-negative variants. In contrast to the Tyr705 mutant, Thr714 mutants retain the ability to bind the STAT3 consensus DNA sequence, thereby blocking transcription driven by wild-type STAT3 or other transcription factors. These properties define Thr714 mutants as gain-of-function variants. Both Thr714 and Ser727 are located in an S/T-P motif (serine or threonine followed by proline), a conserved sequence recognized by cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), GSK3, and MAPKs [30, 31]. Thus, Thr714 and Ser727 share common upstream kinases and can be considered functionally related residues. In cells with active ERK1/2, STAT3 phosphorylated at Tyr705 and Thr714 suppresses transcription of STAT3 target genes. Consequently, ERK1/2-hijacked STAT3 represses the expression of IFNγ- and TNFα-responsive genes in KRAS-driven lung cancer. Treatment with MAPK inhibitors in these cancer cells induces Tyr705 phosphorylation due to Thr714 dephosphorylation, ultimately leading to STAT3 transcription-dependent drug resistance.

Results

STAT3 activation does not guarantee STAT3 transcriptional activity

A common limitation of STAT3 dual-luciferase assays arises from the absence of serum starvation, a procedure frequently used in molecular biology and generally assumed to reduce basal protein phosphorylation. To assess the effect of basal STAT3 phosphorylation on its transcriptional activity, we compared the transcriptional activity of STAT3 induced by the IL-6 family cytokines interleukin-6 (IL-6) and oncostatin M (OSM) under serum-starved and non-starved conditions. We found that 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) significantly inhibited STAT3 transcriptional activity (Fig. 1A and 1B). However, IL-6 and OSM induced comparable levels of STAT3 Tyr705 phosphorylation under both conditions (Fig. 1C). Thus, STAT3 transcriptional activity cannot be assessed solely based on Tyr705 phosphorylation. Additionally, cells maintained in 10% FBS exhibited elevated basal ERK1/2 activation (Fig. 1C), suggesting that elevated Ser727 phosphorylation may contribute to the reduced STAT3 transcriptional activity [8]. However, substituting of Ser727 with glutamic acid reduced IL-6-induced STAT3 transcriptional activity by only 29%, which was substantially weaker than the 65% reduction observed in 10% FBS (Fig. 1A and 1D). These findings suggest the existence of an additional phosphorylation-dependent regulatory mechanism that can render activated STAT3 transcriptionally inactive.

Thr714 is indispensable for STAT3 transcriptional activity

To identify additional serine or threonine residues crucial for STAT3 transcriptional regulation, we first analyzed extensive STAT3 mass spectrometry data generated in our laboratory using MaxQuant software to quantify phosphorylation levels of serine and threonine (Fig. 2A, upper panel). We then reviewed reported STAT3 serine and threonine mutations associated with autosomal dominant hyper-IgE syndrome (AD-HIES) [32, 33] (Fig. 2A, lower panel). While Ser/Thr mutations within the STAT3 Src homology 2 (SH2) domain frequently exhibited dominant-negative effects, a greater number of phosphorylated Ser/Thr residues were detected in the STAT3 transactivation domain (Fig. 2A), suggesting dynamic regulatory modification in this region. Notably, all point mutations (e.g., Y705H/C, L706M/P, K707E, T708N/S, K709E, F710C, I711C/T, C712G, V713L/M, T714A/I/K, P715L, and T716M) within the 704–716 amino acid region (PYLKTKFICVTPT) are pathogenic [34], indicating that this region constitutes the core of the STAT3 transactivation domain (Fig. 2A). Among these residues, Thr714 was particularly intriguing. First, although Tyr705 phosphorylation is considered the canonical marker of STAT3 activation, heterozygous Tyr705 mutations are associated with relatively mild pathogenicity, whereas heterozygous mutations at Thr714, Pro715, or Thr716 result in more severe disease. Second, the “T714PTT” sequence closely resembles the “S727PRT” (Fig. S1A) in the context of the S/T-P motif. Third, Thr714 exhibited a notably high level of phosphorylation (Fig. 2A, upper panel, Fig. S1B), consistent with previous mass spectrometry studies [10, 35].

STAT3 dual-luciferase assays revealed that while Thr716 or Thr717 mutations produced divergent effects depending on the specific substitution, both STAT3 -T714E and -T714A mutants exhibited markedly reduced transcriptional activity (Fig. 2B). Further analysis demonstrated that all Thr714 mutations severely impaired STAT3 transcriptional activity (Fig. 2C), indicating that the hydroxyl group of Thr714 is indispensable for STAT3 transcriptional activity. Additionally, Thr714 mutations were epistatic, overriding the effects of other mutations (Fig. 2D-F).

Since preserving the hydroxyl group of Thr714 while preventing its phosphorylation was not feasible, we disrupted the S/T-P motif by mutating Pro715 to alanine, which prevented Thr714 phosphorylation by GSK3, CDKs, and MAPKs. This mutation increased basal STAT3 transcriptional activity in the absence of cytokine stimulation, while attenuating cytokine responsiveness (Fig. 2G). Tyr705 phosphorylation remained intact (Fig. S2A), further supporting the conclusion that STAT3 transcriptional activity cannot be inferred from Tyr705 phosphorylation alone. Structural analysis revealed that in DNA-bound STAT3 dimers, both Thr714 residues are positioned symmetrically outside the quaternary structure (Fig. S2B), suggesting a potential role as an interface for recruiting transcriptional coactivators and the transcription initiation complex. Consistent with this hypothesis, the transcriptional coactivator CBP [36] and RNA polymerase II were pulled down by DNA-bound STAT3-WT but not by STAT3-T714E (Fig. 2H and I). A previous study reported that Ser727 phosphorylation primarily enhances RNA polymerase II-mediated transcriptional elongation rather than initiation [12], whether Thr714 phosphorylation serves a similar function remains unclear.

Thr714 mutations impair STAT3 transcriptional activity at multiple levels

To facilitate analysis of Thr714 phosphorylation, an anti-pSTAT3 (T714) serum was generated (Fig. S3). Surprisingly, IL-6 stimulation induced Thr714 phosphorylation with kinetics closely paralleling those of Tyr705 phosphorylation (Fig. 3A), consistent with previous LC–MS analyses in EGF- and TRAP-treated human endothelial cells [10]. Correspondingly, IL-6-induced Thr714 phosphorylation was inhibited by the STAT3-Y705F mutation, whereas Tyr705 phosphorylation was markedly suppressed by STAT3-T714E or STAT3-T714A mutations (Fig. 3B and 3 C). These findings suggest that IL-6-induced Thr714 phosphorylation is interdependent with Tyr705 phosphorylation and that the STAT3-T714E mutation does not accurately mimic the phosphorylated state.

Due to decreased Tyr705 phosphorylation, nuclear translocation of the STAT3-T714E and STAT3-T714A mutants was reduced (Fig. 3D and Fig. S4A). Nevertheless, reduced Tyr705 phosphorylation alone did not explain why STAT3-T714E exhibited lower transcriptional activity than STAT3-Y705F, particularly since the Y705F mutation partially rescued STAT3-T714E activity (Fig. 3E). In addition, STAT3 was able to form disulfide-linked dimers independently of Tyr705 phosphorylation (Fig. S4B). Despite the reduced Tyr705 phosphorylation, STAT3-T714E retained DNA-binding capacity (Fig. 3F). These findings suggest that a subset of STAT3-T714E proteins can enter the nucleus and occupy STAT3-binding sites, thereby functioning as transcriptional repressors.

We hypothesize that when wild-type and mutant STAT3 proteins coexist, STAT3-Y705F reduces total STAT3 transcriptional activity by losing intrinsic transcriptional activity and forming dominant-negative heterodimers with STAT3-WT. In contrast, STAT3-T714E additionally occupies STAT3-binding sites in a Tyr705-phosphorylated state, thereby blocking transcription driven by STAT3-WT homodimers or other transcription factors. Thus, STAT3-Y705F represents a loss-of-function mutant, whereas STAT3-T714E behaves as a gain-of-function transcriptional repressor.

Thr714 and Ser727 share common upstream kinases

Given that IL-6 family cytokines induce phosphorylation of both Tyr705 and Thr714, we sought to identify kinases responsible for OSM-induced Thr714 phosphorylation. Cells were treated with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (JAK inhibitor I or PP2), PKC inhibitors (sotrastaurin or rottlerin), or MAPK inhibitors (U0126 or FR180204). MAPK inhibitors suppressed OSM-induced Thr714 phosphorylation, whereas JAK inhibition paradoxically increased it (Fig. 4A). Overexpression of ERK2 (GOF), a constitutively active mutant, inhibited Tyr705 phosphorylation (Fig. 3F), supporting competitive regulation between Tyr705 and Thr714 phosphorylation. We further investigated whether constitutively active KRAS mutant could induce Thr714 phosphorylation via the MAPK pathway in the absence of cytokine stimulation. KRAS-G12D robustly induced Thr714 phosphorylation, an effect largely abolished by the MEK1/2 inhibitor U0126 and partially reduced by the ERK1/2 inhibitor FR180204. The GSK3β inhibitor CHIR99021 also inhibited Thr714 phosphorylation, consistent with the preference of the S/T-P motif for GSK3β. Several PKC isoforms also appeared to be involved in the KRAS-ERK1/2-GSK3β cascade (Fig. 4B). Overall, ERK1/2 was the major contributor to KRAS-G12D-induced Thr714 phosphorylation, although other pathways may also participate. ERK1/2-catalyzed Thr714 and Ser727 phosphorylation was further confirmed by in vitro kinase assays (Fig. 4C).

As ERK1/2 can translocate to the nucleus [37], we wondered whether ERK1/2 catalyzes Thr714 phosphorylation in the nucleus. Subcellular fractionation revealed that unphosphorylated STAT3 preferentially entered the nucleus following OSM stimulation (Fig. 4D), whereas relative levels of Thr714-phosphorylated STAT3 increased by approximately 1.5-fold in both cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions (Fig. 4E), suggesting that a substantial fraction of STAT3 is phosphorylated at Thr714 after nuclear entry.

pSTAT3 (Tyr705, Thr714) suppresses PCK1 expression

To investigate the physiological relevance of Thr714 phosphorylation, we generated Stat3+/T714E mice [38]. Heterozygous mice exhibited extremely low survival rates, consistent with the embryonic lethality of Stat3 deficiency [39]. In addition, most female Stat3+/T714E mice died after parturition for unknown reasons (Table 1). Heterozygous dominant-negative STAT3 mutations cause STAT3 hyper-IgE syndrome (STAT3-HIES), a type of primary immunodeficiency disorder [40, 41], and three Thr714 mutations have been reported: two (T714A/I) in STAT3-HIES patients [33, 42, 43] and one (T714K) in a patient with an uncharacterized primary immunodeficiency [44]. Elevated serum IgE concentrations were detected in Stat3+/T714E mice [38], indicating markedly reduced STAT3 transcriptional activity, and confirming the essential role of Thr714.

STAT3 regulates glucose homeostasis by suppressing expression of gluconeogenic genes in the liver, through a mechanism dependent on Tyr705 phosphorylation and DNA binding [21–23]. We first examined whether expression of PCK1 was sensitive to serum starvation. In medium containing 10% FBS, PCK1 expression was not induced, regardless of cytokine stimulation. In contrast, serum starvation robustly induced PCK1 expression, and repression was observed only in OSM-treated cells (Fig. 5A), correlating with stronger ERK1/2 activation (Fig. 1C). These results suggest that STAT3-dependent repression of PCK1 is mediated by pSTAT3 (Tyr705, Thr714) rather than pSTAT3 (Tyr705). Consistently, STAT3-Y705F failed to repress PCK1 expression, whereas STAT3-T714E retained a mild inhibitory effect (Fig. 5B). It was conjectured that Tyr705 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of STAT3-T714E were impaired by the Thr714 mutation (Fig. 3C-D). In vivo, fasting-induced Pck1 expression was significantly reduced in Stat3+/T714E mice (Fig. 5D) despite minimal changes in blood glucose (Fig. 5C), indicating Thr714 phosphorylation-dependent repression of Pck1. Correspondingly, hepatic Thr714 phosphorylation was significantly reduced by fasting (Fig. 5E), and increased fat accumulation was observed in the liver of STAT3+/T714E mice (Fig. 5F). These results suggest a cooperative repression of gluconeogenic genes by STAT3 and ERK1/2, with long-term effects on lipid metabolism through as-yet-unknown mechanisms.

STAT3 functions as a transcriptional repressor in KRAS-driven cancer cells

Constitutively active KRAS mutants significantly increased STAT3 Thr714 phosphorylation via the MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 4B). In STAT3 dual-luciferase assays, STAT3 transcriptional activity was nearly completely inhibited by KRAS-G12D (Fig. S5C). Although the MAPK inhibitor U0126 reduced STAT3 transcriptional activity (Fig. S5C) by decreasing Tyr705 phosphorylation (Fig. S5D), it partially restored STAT3 transcriptional activity (Fig. S5C). Both Thr714 and Ser727 were phosphorylated by ERK1/2 (Fig. 4C); however, Thr714 phosphorylation was the primary contributor to KRAS-G12D-induced inhibition of STAT3 transcriptional activity (Fig. S5E).

Immunofluorescence analysis of human lung adenocarcinoma tissues revealed relatively uniform Thr714 phosphorylation with moderate intensity in tumor-adjacent tissues, but marked regional heterogeneity in tumors, where high-intensity regions often contained a large number of enlarged cells (Fig. 6A). Although the percentage of cells positive for Thr714 phosphorylation was significantly higher in tumor-adjacent tissues (Fig. 6B), the mean fluorescence intensity was lower than that in tumor tissues (Fig. 6C). Subsequently, STAT3-knockout A549 cells stably overexpressing STAT3-WT, -T714E, or -T714A were subjected to transcriptome analysis. Although some statistical dispersion was observed in the STAT3-T714E group, variability between samples was still significantly smaller than that between groups (Fig. 6D). The two-dimensional score plot showed that the STAT3-T714E and STAT3-T714A groups overlapped (Fig. 6E), suggesting minimal differences between these two groups, consistent with the findings described above. Based on data from the ChIP-Atlas database, we selected the top 100 genes whose expression levels changed significantly upon STAT3 knockout (information for 84 genes was ultimately obtained). Following differential gene expression analysis, we proposed dividing STAT3-dependent gene expression into three groups in KRAS-driven cancer cells (Fig. S6A).

To explore the reasons for the differential expression patterns of STAT3 target genes across these groups, we validated the protein levels of the canonical STAT3 target genes c-MYC [45] and BCL2 [46] (Fig. 6F) and examined STAT3 DNA binding (Fig. 6G). STAT3 has a confirmed binding site near the first intron of c-MYC (Fig. S7). After STAT3 knockout, c-MYC protein levels increased slightly, indicating that STAT3 exerts a modest inhibitory effect on c-MYC expression. STAT3 also has a confirmed binding site approximately 1,000 bp upstream of the transcription start site of BCL2 (Fig. S7), but the repression of BCL2 expression by STAT3 was particularly pronounced (Fig. 6F). Based on the overall distribution of STAT3 binding sites in the genome, we proposed a preliminary model for STAT3-mediated gene repression (Fig. 6H). In group one (G1), STAT3 primarily functions as an adaptor protein, assisting other transcription factors in regulating gene expression. Expression of these genes depends mainly on whether STAT3 entered the nucleus efficiently. In group two (G2), genes are activated by multiple transcription factors, and the binding position of STAT3 determines the extent to which it hinders transcription. Gene expression was conditionally dependent on STAT3 and regulated by both Tyr705 and Thr714 phosphorylation; most STAT3 target genes fell into this group. In group three (G3), gene expression was primarily regulated by other transcription factors. As STAT3 binds within the gene body, high levels of gene transcription can be inhibited by pSTAT3 (Tyr705, Thr714). BCL2 likely belongs to this category (Fig. S7).

Next, we analyzed the biological functions associated with STAT3-repressed gene transcription. Expression of approximately 917 genes was significantly altered following STAT3 knockout in A549 cells (Fig. 6I). Gene set enrichment analysis revealed that the top signaling pathways inhibited by STAT3 included interferon and TNF response pathways, as well as apoptosis-associated processes (Fig. 6J), which was confirmed by IFNγ- and TNFα-induced cell apoptosis assays (Fig. 6J). In addition, previous studies have reported that disruption of STAT3 signaling promotes angiogenesis, and thus KRAS-induced lung tumorigenesis [29]. Our transcriptome data revealed that STAT3 knockout promoted the expression of PDGFA, a growth factor subunit crucial for angiogenesis (Fig. S6B).

In KRAS-driven cancers STAT3 is a double-edged sword

As constitutively active KRAS mutants suppress IFNγ- and TNFα-induced apoptosis through MAPK-catalyzed Thr714 phosphorylation of STAT3, MAPK inhibition was predicted to sensitize cancer cells to cytokine-induced apoptosis. Indeed, trametinib treatment enhanced the IFN signaling pathway (Fig. 7A) and increased IFNγ- and TNFα-induced cell apoptosis (Fig. 7B). However, MAPK inhibitors also induced feedback STAT3 activation (Fig. 7C), leading to increased expression of canonical STAT3 target genes (Fig. 7D, right panel). We discovered that Thr714 mutations (both T714E and T714A) promoted MAPK-inhibitor-induced STAT3 activation (Fig. 7E), indicating mechanistic differences from cytokine-driven activation. In summary, although MAPK inhibitors may enhance cancer immunotherapy, the net effect of STAT3 activation remains uncertain. KRAS inhibitors, which do not induce STAT3 activation (Fig. 7D, left panel), may offer an alternative strategy. Optimizing combinations of KRAS/MAPK and STAT3 inhibitors warrants further investigation.

Discussion

Phosphorylation of STAT3 at Thr714 has been frequently observed by mass spectrometry due to its high stoichiometry; however, over the past three decades, this residue has received little attention. One major reason is that Thr714 and Ser727 share an S/T-P motif and were therefore considered functionally redundant. Additionally, Ser727 phosphorylation plays a pivotal role in metabolic reprogramming, which is essential for MAPK-dependent oncogenic transformation [14, 17]. Consequently, research efforts have largely focused on Ser727, leaving the functional significance of Thr714 largely unexplored.

Neither Tyr705 nor Thr714 phosphorylation can be effectively mimicked by amino acid substitution. STAT3-Y705F represents a dominant-negative loss-of-activation mutant, as substitution of Tyr705 with phenylalanine fundamentally alters the biochemical consequences of Tyr705 phosphorylation and abolishes the DNA-binding capacity required for transcriptional activation. In STAT3+/Y705F cells, a fraction of STAT3-WT proteins is sequestered in the cytosol through heterodimerization with STAT3-Y705F. However, STAT3-WT homodimers can still initiate transcription of target genes, resulting in only partial impairment of STAT3 transcriptional activity. In contrast, the STAT3-T714E mutant retains certain biochemical features associated with Thr714 phosphorylation and remains competent for DNA binding. In STAT3+/T714E cells, STAT3-WT proteins are not only sequestered in the cytosol by STAT3-T714E proteins but also form heterodimers that translocate to the nucleus and occupy STAT3-binding sites, thereby preventing STAT3-WT homodimers from regulating transcription of target genes (Fig. S8). Consequently, STAT3+/T714E mice were difficult to maintain under specific-pathogen-free conditions (Table 1), whereas STAT3+/Y705F mice remained viable and developed normally [24]. Conceptually, if the transcriptional activity of STAT3-Y705F is defined as zero, STAT3-T714E exhibits negative activity rather than complete inactivity. Thus, STAT3-Y705F represents a loss-of-function mutant, whereas STAT3-T714E functions as a gain-of-function transcriptional repressor. Importantly, this interpretation is based on the conventional view of STAT3 as a transcriptional activator. If pSTAT3 (Tyr705, Thr714) was broadly accepted as a transcriptional repressor, STAT3-T714E might instead be regarded as a constitutively active mutant.

The relationship between Tyr705 and Thr714/Ser727 phosphorylation remains elusive. We propose two regulatory modes. In the first mode, Thr714/Ser727 phosphorylation occurs independently of Tyr705 phosphorylation. Following STAT3 activation and DNA binding, Thr714/Ser727 phosphorylation may be imposed by additional kinases. Conversely, prior Thr714/Ser727 phosphorylation could inhibit subsequent Tyr705 phosphorylation. In the second mode, Thr714/Ser727 phosphorylation is required for maximal Tyr705 phosphorylation and depends on distinct receptor complexes. These two models are not mutually exclusive and may operate in a context-dependent manner.

Thr714/Ser727 dephosphorylation-induced STAT3 activation represents a key mechanism of drug resistance in many cancers. Accordingly, combined inhibition of the MAPK and STAT3 pathways has demonstrated therapeutic benefit in melanoma [19], lung adenocarcinoma [47], esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [48], myeloma [49], and pancreatic cancer [50]. Feedback activation of STAT3 has been reported in multiple oncogene-addicted cancers through IL-6/JAK1 and FGFR/PI3K signaling pathways [20]. However, inhibition of the PI3K/mTOR signaling pathway fails to suppress STAT3 activation and instead increases macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) expression and secretion, which ultimately activates JAK1/STAT3 signaling [51]. The regulatory network is further complicated by extensive pathway crosstalk. In BRAF-mutant melanoma, inhibition of JAK2 activates MAPK signaling [52]. Mechanistically, in KRAS-mutant pancreatic cancer, inhibition of STAT3 promotes STAT1- and STAT4-mediated TGF-α expression, leading to activation of the EGFR pathway [53], suggesting that STAT3-T714E may indirectly promote STAT1- and STAT4-dependent TGF-α expression. Notably, combined inhibition of BRAF, MEK, and JAK2 or STAT3 is insufficient to simultaneously suppress both MAPK and JAK2/STAT3 pathways. Further studies have shown that effective dual inhibition requires the addition of an ERK inhibitor [52]. These observations underscore a unique role for ERK1/2 and ERK1/2-mediated STAT3 phosphorylation in therapeutic resistance. Consequently, managing the dual roles of STAT3 may necessitate increasingly complex combination therapies, posing substantial clinical challenges.

Finally, a critical unresolved question is how Thr714 phosphorylation is physiologically regulated. While cells may dynamically modulate Thr714 phosphorylation through a finely tuned enzymatic activity between kinases and phosphatases, the broad roles of these enzymes limit the physiological specificity achievable. Instead, context-specific interactions between STAT3 and its modifying enzymes likely dictate Thr714 phosphorylation and the resulting transcriptional output, however, the precise mechanisms underlying Thr714 phosphorylation remain poorly understood, representing a major barrier to deciphering its physiological functions and underscoring the need for detailed mechanistic studies.

Materials and methods

Plasmids and site-directed mutagenesis

Human STAT3 cDNA was cloned into the pcDNA3.0 plasmid and the pCDH-CMV-PGK-Puro plasmid (RRID:Addgene_72265). STAT3 mutants were generated using the QuikChange II site-directed mutagenesis kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Escherichia coli competent cells (DH5α), DNA markers, and 2 × Phanta Max master mix were purchased from Vazyme Biotech Ltd. (Nanjing, China). All mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

STAT3 dual-luciferase assay

HEK293T cells (RRID:CVCL_0063) were plated in 48-well plates in DMEM containing 10% FBS. The plasmid containing STAT3-responsive elements (SIE) linked to a luciferase reporter gene and plasmid pRL-TK Renilla luciferase reporter (RRID:Addgene_11313) were transfected with wild-type STAT3 or STAT3 mutants. At 24 h posttransfection, cells were treated with 10 ng/mL IL-6 or OSM for 6 h. Luciferase activity was measured with a luminometer by using a luciferase assay kit (Shanghai BioScience Co., Ltd.).

Western blot and antibodies

Total cell lysate was prepared by using the RIPA buffer and analyzed by standard western blot. A total of 40–80 μg protein lysate was loaded and separated using 8–12% SDS-PAGE. Then the proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membrane (0.45 μM) and blocked with 5% defat milk in PBST. Generally, the primary antibody dilution was 1:1000. Fluorescent labeled secondary antibodies (Dylight 800, goat anti mouse IgG and Dylight 680, goat anti rabbit IgG, ImmunoWay Biotechnology Company) were used for the detection. Signals were detected and images were visualized using Odyssey infrared imaging system.

Antibodies for STAT3 (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 9139, RRID:AB_331757), phospho-STAT3 (Tyr705) (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 9145, RRID:AB_2491009), phospho-STAT3 (Ser727) (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 9134, RRID:AB_331589), p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 4695, RRID:AB_390779) and phospho-p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) (Thr202/Tyr204) (Cell Signaling Technology Cat# 4370, RRID:AB_2315112) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. Anti-β-actin (YT0099), anti-BCL2, and anti-MYC was purchased from ImmunoWay Biotechnology Company. Polyclonal antiserum for STAT3 Thr714 phosphorylation was generated by immunizing a rabbit with a phosphorylated peptide (KFICVpTPTTC) corresponding to STAT3 Thr714 phosphorylation site.

Immunoprecipitation

HEK293T cells were washed once with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail and phosphatase inhibitors. The lysates were cleared by high-speed centrifugation and incubated overnight with Anti-FLAG magnetic beads (Beyotime Biotech Inc). The beads were washed three times with ice-cold RIPA buffer and then boiled in 1 × Laemmli buffer for SDS-PAGE.

Mice

Genetically engineered mice carrying the Stat3-T714E mutation were generated by Jiangsu Aniphe Biolaboratory Inc., China, on a C57BL/6 background using the CRISPR-Cas9 system, as described previously [38]. All care and handling of mice were performed in accordance with the Guide to the Care and Use of Experimental Animals, and all experiments were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Binzhou Medical University. The one-week survival rate was calculated without regard to sex.

Transcriptomic analysis

A549 cells (RRID:CVCL_0023) were starved in FBS-free DMEM overnight and treated with IL-6 for 4 h. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent. RNA purity was assessed using a NanoPhotometer® spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, CA, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. Clustering of the indexed samples was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System using the TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

After cluster generation, libraries were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw reads in FASTQ format were first processed through in-house Perl scripts. Clean reads were obtained by removing reads containing adapters, reads containing ploy-N and low quality reads. The clean reads were then mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2. Only reads with a perfect match or one mismatch were further analyzed and annotated based on the reference genome. Differential expression analysis between two conditions/groups was performed using the DESeq2 R package, and heatmap visualizations were generated using the pheatmap R package.

Quantitative RT- PCR

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent. Reverse transcription was performed with the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) kit (Vazyme Biotech Ltd.). Gene expression was quantified using a LightCycler® 96 Instrument (Roche). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the ΔΔCT method.

ChIP-qPCR and -western blot

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) followed by qPCR or Western blotting was performed as previously described [54]. Briefly, A549 cells cultured in a 10-cm Petri dish were treated with 25 ng/ml IL-6 for 30 min prior to collection. Both anti-STAT3 and anti-Flag antibodies were used effectively for STAT3 enrichment.

Immunofluorescence

Fifteen pairs of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections from patients with non-small cell lung adenocarcinoma (10 males and 5 females, aged 39–77 years) were obtained from Shanghai Outdo Biotech. Sections were air-dried at 65 °C for 60 min, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating sections in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a microwave oven for 10 min, followed by cooling at room temperature for 30 min.

Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer, washed with PBS, and then incubated with Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL). After washing, sections were mounted and sealed, and images were acquired using a Vectra Polaris imaging system.

Mass spectrometry

Following immunoprecipitation with an anti-STAT3 antibody (CST 9139), an 86-kDa band was excised from the Coomassie-stained gel, digested with trypsin, and analyzed by capillary column liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MSMS) to identify phosphopeptides. The digest was analyzed in both survey and targeted modes. Survey experiments were performed using the data-dependent multitask capability of the instrument, acquiring full-scan mass spectra to determine peptide molecular weights and product ion spectra to determine amino acid sequences in successive instrument scans. All collision-induced dissociation (CID) spectra collected in the experiment were used to search the human reference sequence database with the Mascot search program.

Targeted experiments focused on specific STAT3 peptides, including the phosphorylated and unmodified forms of the Tyr705, Ser727, and Thr714 tryptic peptides. Chromatograms for these peptides were plotted based on known fragmentation patterns, and the peak areas were used to determine the extent of phosphorylation.

Apoptosis assay

Cells were treated with IFNγ (100 ng/mL) and TNFα (25 ng/mL) (TargetMol, USA), with or without U0126 (TargetMol, USA), for 48 h and then subjected to the Annexin V/PI assay. Apoptotic cells were subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least three times. For animal experiments, each group contained a minimum of five mice. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (RRID:SCR_002798). Significant differences between two groups were evaluated using an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test. Multiple comparisons involving two factors were analyzed by ordinary two-way ANOVA, with multiplicity-adjusted p-values reported for each comparison. Statistical significance is indicated in the figures as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, **** P < 0.0001.
