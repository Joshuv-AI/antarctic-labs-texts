Application of saphenous nerve infrapatellar branch protection technique in total knee arthroplasty
Authors: Zhi-Qiang Mao, Kai Li, Zhou Zhang, Ting Li, Jian-hua Lu, Meng-Qiang Fan, Xiao-bing Chu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis randomized controlled trial conducted a comparative analysis of postoperative outcomes between a modified anteromedial incision with saphenous nerve protection and the traditional medial parapatellar approach in total knee arthroplasty (TKA). We aimed to test the hypothesis that the nerve-sparing modified technique would significantly reduce postoperative anterior knee numbness and pain while maintaining surgical feasibility.MethodsSixty-eight TKA candidates (January 2022-June 2023) were prospectively randomized into two groups: Modified (n = 34) and Traditional (n = 34) approaches. The modified technique featured an anteromedial incision with infrapatellar branch visualization and preservation, whereas the control group received the traditional medial parapatellar exposure. Primary outcomes included numbness area and the Visual Analog Scale score; secondary outcomes encompassed surgical parameters (duration, blood loss) and functional recovery (Hospital for Special Surgery Knee score).ResultsThirty one patients were randomized to the M group and 29 patients allocated to the T group. Compared to the T group, the M group demonstrated significantly lower VAS scores at 3 days, 7 days, and 2 weeks postoperatively, with statistical significance (P < 0.05). The M group showed a statistically significant reduction in the area of numbness compared to the T group at all postoperative follow-up intervals: 2 weeks, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year (P < 0.05 for all time points). However, postoperative HSS scores did not differ significantly between the M group and the T group at any follow-up interval (P > 0.05).ConclusionThe nerve-preserving modified approach achieved superior patient-reported outcomes (PROs) in reducing anterior knee numbness and early postoperative pain without compromising prosthesis implantation accuracy or functional recovery trajectories, suggesting its potential as a standardized refinement in TKA surgical protocols.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2500107220, 6 August 2025, retrospectively registered.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09099-5.Keywords: Knee joint, Saphenous nerve, Protection technology, Total knee arthroplasty, Modified surgery

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a whole-joint disease [1] involving all joint tissues, with key risk factors including aging, obesity, joint injury, and genetic predisposition [2]. These factors synergistically drive cartilage degradation and synovial inflammation, ultimately leading to progressive functional impairment [3]. Clinically, KOA manifests as pain and restricted mobility, potentially progressing to disability in advanced stages [4]. Total Knee Arthroplasty (TKA) is currently the treatment of choice for end-stage KOA. Studies have found that the 10-year survival rate of patients after TKA has exceeded 90% [5]. With the increasing elderly population in China, the number of KOA patients with receiving TKA is also rising. As people’s cognitive concepts and living standards improve, patients’ expectations for postoperative outcomes are also increasing. Despite recent advancements in TKA surgery, studies have shown that while over 80% of patients are satisfied with their new joints, approximately 20% remain dissatisfied [6]. Postoperative pain is a potential contributing factor to this dissatisfaction. Beswick [7] reported that 10–34% of post-TKA patients experience moderate to severe pain, leading to dissatisfaction. Postoperative lateral knee cutaneous dysesthesia may also be a contributing factor, with Macdonald [8] reporting an incidence of lateral knee numbness ranging from 35 to 100% after TKA. As a result, up to 100% of patients experience lateral knee skin numbness after knee arthroplasty as an unavoidable complication [9]. This numbness lasts longer and is more painful in patients after TKA, and some patients do not achieve the expected therapeutic effect. Addressing these refractory numbness and pain symptoms is essential to improve patient satisfaction.

Tanavalee et al. [10] reported a postoperative area of skin numbness (AON) reaching 51.7 cm² at two weeks following TKA, which progressively diminished to 2.1 cm² by one year postoperatively. This persistent sensory deficit has been attributed to the conventional TKA approach, which frequently causes iatrogenic injury to the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve distal to the surgical incision. In a comparative study, Tsukada et al. [11] demonstrated that utilizing an anterolateral approach, as opposed to the traditional medial parapatellar approach, significantly reduced lateral knee skin numbness, ultimately leading to improved patient satisfaction metrics. Current TKA techniques lack standardized methods for saphenous nerve branch preservation. Based on anatomical preservation principles, we hypothesize that our modified surgical approach will demonstrate superior outcomes in reducing both the incidence and severity of postoperative sensory deficits and pain intensity compared to conventional approaches, while maintaining equivalent joint stability and functional outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design

A double-blind randomized controlled trial (RCT) was conducted in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 guidelines [12]. The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University of Chinese Medicine (Approval ID: 2025-KLS-514-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Study population

In this study, 68 patients who underwent TKA for the first time at Zhejiang Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine between January 2022 and June 2023 were selected and randomly divided into Modified group (M group) and Traditional group (T group), with 34 patients in each group. Randomization

The participants were randomly assigned to either the experimental group or the control group using a computer-generated randomization sequence. To ensure allocation concealment, the randomization list was prepared by an independent statistician who was not involved in the recruitment or treatment processes. The allocation sequence was stored in sealed, opaque envelopes, which were opened only after the participants had completed the baseline assessments and provided informed consent. This method ensured that both the researchers and participants were blinded to group assignment until the intervention began. The randomization ratio was 1:1, with an equal number of participants allocated to each group. This approach minimized selection bias and ensured the comparability of the two groups at baseline.

Surgical procedures

Preoperative preparation

The patients were assessed for the degree of internal and external knee deformity, knee mobility, (Visual Analog Scale) VAS score [13], (Hospital for Special Surgery Knee) HSS score [14], and underwent improved preoperative related examinations, such as full-length standing radiographs of both lower limbs, to assess the risk of surgery.

Surgical steps

After successful general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the supine position. The level of the knee joint line was determined using the lower edges of the medial and lateral knee eyes as markers, and this was marked with a marker pen. After driving the blood out with a tourniquet, the affected limb was routinely disinfected and draped. A longitudinal incision was made in the middle of the anterior knee, extending from 3 cm proximal to the upper edge of the patella to the medial end of the tibial tuberosity distally. The incision was revealed in two steps: first, above the joint line level, a conventional medial parapatellar incision was used to expose the joint cavity. Then, the incision was pulled apart on both sides to tighten the skin below the joint line by using retractors, while retaining the superficial layer of the soft tissues above the patellar tendon(which contains the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve Fig. 1). To increase the laxity of the knee-extension device, the quadriceps tendon could be incised proximally to the extent necessary to turn over the patella. Residual meniscus, and the anterior cruciate ligament were removed. The knee was positioned in flexion, and the medial and lateral collateral ligaments were protected. Distal femoral osteotomy was performed after intramedullary positioning of the femur. The prosthesis was selected with a 5–7 degree valgus alignment. A “four-in-one” osteotomy guide was used for the femoral condyles, and for the PS prosthesis, additional intercondylar osteotomy was performed for the upper and lower beveled cuts. The femoral prosthesis specimen was tested and mounted. The tibial osteotomy mold was tested to determine the size of the tibial prosthesis (Stryker Triathlon® System, Kalamazoo, MI, USA). Tibial osteotomy was performed, and the rotational alignment of the tibial prosthesis and the appropriate type were determined. The proximal tibial bone bed was prepared, the trial component was removed, and the knee was tested for equal flexion or extension clearance and correct lower extremity alignment. The bone surface was repeatedly rinsed with a high-pressure pulse lavage gun, dried, and bone cement was mixed. All prostheses were installed and fixed in the dough stage. The spacer was measured and installed. The patellar articular surface was trimmed and shaped with bone biting forceps. The patellar tracking was checked to ensure good knee flexion or extension mobility and joint stability. The tourniquet was released, and bleeding points were treated with electrocautery. The corresponding tissues were sutured layer by layer, and the wound was wrapped with a cotton-padded bandage under pressure. The operation was then completed.

(a-c). Intraoperative anatomical views demonstrating preservation of the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve (IPBSN) and surrounding soft tissues during the modified surgical approach

In the M group

In the M group, the surgical procedure commenced with the knee in flexion, employing a two-stage approach for cutaneous incision and deep exposure.

Part 1 (Fig. 2a): A layered incision was performed along the premarked surgical trajectory from proximal to distal, terminating at the joint line level. Through sequential dissection following medial parapatellar principles, the articular cavity was systematically exposed while preserving neurovascular structures.

Yellow line : Approximate course of the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve and its branches across the knee surface; a Red line demarcate the Modified surgical incision (Part 1), designed to minimize injury to the IPBSN; b Red lines demarcate the traditional median surgical approach, which commonly causes direct damage to the IPBSN

Part 2: The knee flexion angle was incrementally increased to facilitate lateral retraction of the proximal wound margins, creating tension-controlled exposure of distal tissues. Using a surgical scalpel, the incision was extended distally to the medial tibial tuberosity region, maintaining strict epidermal-level division in the distal segment. Critical anatomical landmarks including branches of the IPBSN were meticulously identified and preserved at the joint line interface.

Subperiosteal dissection was then performed distal to the joint line using curved hemostatic forceps, achieving complete soft tissue elevation from the osseous surface. This technique ensured preservation of the IPBSN-containing soft tissue layer while creating adequate working space for subsequent tibial preparation. Proximal extension of the medial parapatellar arthrotomy enabled controlled relaxation of the extensor mechanism, permitting safe patellar eversion and unobstructed visualization for precision osteotomy and prosthetic implantation.

In the T group

In the T group, the articular cavity was exposed through the traditional medial parapatellar approach.(Fig. 2b).

Perioperative management

During the preoperative anesthesia induction period, both groups of patients received 2 g of cefazolin sodium. Intraoperatively, 1000 mg of vancomycin was mixed into 40 g of bone cement to prevent infection, and 1 g of tranexamic acid was administered for hemostasis. All patients in both groups received special nursing care postoperatively, including nasal catheter oxygen inhalation and monitoring of vital signs. Both groups were administered the same multimodal analgesia regimen, as well as symptomatic treatments such as gastric protection and antiemesis, and neurotrophic therapy.

Clinical outcome assessment

A member of the surgical team recorded operative duration, incision length, and intraoperative blood loss for each patient. Two physician assistants, blinded to surgical details and not involved in the study, conducted follow-up evaluations including preoperative and postoperative VAS scores, HSS scores, and area of numbness (AON). Follow-up data were averaged to enhance accuracy.

Skin sensory conditions

The skin sensation around the incision of the affected knee was observed in both groups before surgery, and at 14 days, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year postoperative. This included whether the skin on the lateral side of the surgical incision felt numb after surgery, as well as the size and change of the numbness area (the AON was measured in cm2) [10]. Specific measurements were taken as follows: First, a sterile cotton swab was lightly touched on the outer side of the incision to determine if sensation was present and if numbness was felt, thereby identifying the approximate range of sensory abnormality. Then, tests were conducted in an outward-to-inward and top-to-bottom order, with a double-knee comparison to identify specific areas of abnormal sensation. The extent of the numbness was drawn with a marker pen, and the size of the area was measured using a ruler (see Fig. 3 and Fig. 4). Regular postoperative follow-ups were performed to observe whether the numbness improved and whether the area of numbness decreased.

Efficacy evaluation

1)The pain scores of the patients in both groups were observed and evaluated preoperatively, and on postoperative days 1, 3, 7, and 14 using the VAS scores [13]. The VAS was administered as follows: A horizontal line was drawn on a piece of paper, divided into 11 equal intervals labeled “0” to “10”. “0” indicates no pain, while “10” indicates extremely painful and intolerable pain. Patients were asked to mark their pain level on the line according to their subjective feelings.

2)The HSS scores [14] of patients in both groups were observed preoperatively, and on postoperative day 1, 7, and 14, as well as at month 1 postoperatively. The scores were evaluated according to the Hospital for Special Surgery scoring system, which includes seven main aspects: pain, function, range of motion, muscle strength, flexion contracture, extension lag, and stability. Patients were able to assess their affected knee joints based on the criteria for each item.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using G * Power (http://www.gpower.hhu.de/) power analysis. We used a two-tailed matched-pairs t test with the following settings, and our primary outcome measure had an effect size of 0.74, a significance level (alpha) of 0.05, and a power of 0.80. Based on these criteria, a sample size of 30 patients was required for both groups. Finally, to compensate for the potential loss of follow-up, we enrolled a total of 68 patients.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0(IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Given the moderate sample size in this study, normality assumptions were rigorously evaluated using Shapiro-Wilk tests. Continuous variables with normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent samples t-tests. Non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR) and analyzed with Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate, with frequency counts reported as percentages. The selection of parametric versus nonparametric methods was determined based on the distribution characteristics verified by preliminary diagnostic checks.

Result

In this study, a total of 68 patients were consecutively enrolled between January 2022 and June 2023. Six patients were excluded based on predefined exclusion criteria, leaving 62 patients for randomization. These patients were allocated equally to either the Modified group (M group, n = 31) or the Traditional group (T group, n = 31). Subsequently, two patients in the T group were excluded from the final analysis due to postoperative loss to follow-up and noncompliance with measurement protocols. Discussion

TKA is a widely accepted orthopedic procedure for the treatment of end-stage KOA, which can effectively reduce and eliminate pain, improve limb function and enhance quality of life, but most patients will develop postoperative skin sensory deficits on the lateral side of the incision, and there is no unanimity in the understanding of why this phenomenon occurs in the postoperative period, and there is no consensus on its prevention and treatment [15–17]. During surgery, most clinicians focus only on how the knee prosthesis is fitted, that the line of force should be good, and how well the soft tissue balance should be done, and assume that the surgery will be successful. However, for patients, unintentionally they find that the TKA produces numbness or paresthesia in front of the knee, and they feel very uncomfortable and may even think that the surgery has failed. Comprehensive literature reported that the lowest subjective sensation of numbness in patients after medial parapatellar approach were 37% [18]– [19], and among these patients, about 2/3 of them did not complain of discomfort for anterior knee numbness, but still 1/3 of them complained of discomfort, and even when they were asked whether they agreed to do the other side of the knee again, the answer of this part of the patients was negative [20].

Peri-incisional sensory loss in TKA is frequently reported in patients and in the literature, however, the impact on clinical outcomes is unknown. It has been hypothesized that the lack of sensation around the total knee incision may lead to poor patient prognosis, difficulty with daily activities such as kneeling, and worse overall patient satisfaction [21, 22]. That’s why it makes sense to re-conceptualize anterior knee numbness after knee surgery. It is generally accepted that anterior knee sensation is innervated by the IPBSN, and that postoperative numbness and pain in the anterior skin of the knee is due to damage to the IPBSN [23]. Persistent postoperative pain originates from either intra- or extra-articular sources, and the cause of extra-articular pain is primarily a neuroma that forms after injury to the IPBSN, a lesser-known condition.

Anatomical studies of the saphenous nerve reveal that it originates from the posterior division of the femoral nerve and bifurcates into superior and inferior branches at the medial femoral condyle. The superior branch, identified as the medial femoral cutaneous nerve, is present in 60% of cases, while the inferior branch, the IPBSN [24], is consistently observed (100% prevalence). Notably, 80% of IPBSN fascicles traverse laterally through the patellar tendon tissue[25]. According to the cadaveric study by Kalthur et al. [26] on 32 male knees, the IPBSN exhibited 1–3 terminal branches. A single-branch configuration was most prevalent (40.6%), followed by two-branch (34.3%) and three-branch variants (25%). Notably, 26 branches (81%) coursed below the joint line. This anatomical evidence confirms that avoiding iatrogenic injury to this nerve during surgical procedures effectively reduces postoperative sensory complications such as pain and numbness. In our cohort, potential anatomical variations may explain residual postoperative numbness in some patients undergoing the modified technique, though the overall incidence of hypoesthesia was significantly reduced compared to conventional approaches (P < 0.05).

The necessity of (Infrapatellar Fat Pad) IFP resection during TKA remains debated. A double-blind randomized [27] controlled trial demonstrated that IFP excision does not adversely affect postoperative knee function or gait kinematics. Consistent with findings by Michalak et al. [28], our study found no significant differences in functional outcomes, pain scores, or patellar tendon morphology (length/thickness) between patients with or without IFP resection. Consequently, routine IFP excision was performed in our protocol, with no observed impact on postoperative recovery metrics in the modified surgery group.

In our study, the majority of patients did not actively report numbness during the initial postoperative days. However, the area of numbness progressively increased within the first two postoperative weeks, followed by a reduction in the affected region by one month post-surgery. To evaluate the clinical significance of treatment outcomes, we employed the Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) [29], which represents the smallest change in treatment outcomes perceived as meaningful by individual patients. Two primary methodologies were utilized to calculate the MCID: the anchor-based method and the distribution-based method [30, 31]. The anchor-based approach relies on external anchors, such as patient-reported improvement, serving as a subjective assessment. In contrast, the distribution-based method employs statistical computations, typically defining the MCID as half the standard deviation of change scores between pre- and post-treatment assessments, representing an objective analytical framework [30, 32]. The calculated results demonstrated that a significant proportion of patients undergoing the modified surgical technique achieved clinically meaningful improvements, particularly in the reduction of numbness extent and pain alleviation. This finding indicates that attainment of the MCID threshold reflects substantive clinical benefits, validating the therapeutic efficacy of the procedural modifications.

We also considered the sensation of numbness after TKA surgery, which should appear immediately after nerve damage. However, in the days following surgery, patients typically did not report numbness, and no specific dermatomal distribution of numbness was detected during skin stimulation. The hypothesis that early postoperative pain may mask numbness is speculative and requires validation in studies using simultaneous pain and sensation assessments. With the advancement of surgical science and technology, the concepts of minimally invasive, rapid recovery, and increased patient comfort have been increasingly applied to orthopedics. We are constantly exploring new surgical approaches and challenging new techniques to meet our patients’ needs. Developing new techniques that preserve more anatomical structures is the key to addressing patients’ real suffering and enhancing their perioperative comfort. In this context, it is crucial to complete the surgery without compromising the outcome and to preserve as many nerve fibers of the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve as possible, thereby reducing postoperative skin numbness and pain. In this study, by improving the surgical incision and saphenous nerve protection technique, we aim to help more patients reduce surgical complications and improve their postoperative satisfaction.

Several limitations exist in our study. Firstly, this was a single-center randomized controlled trial with a limited sample size, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Secondly, postoperative functional assessment relied exclusively on the HSS score, without incorporating validated patient-reported outcome measures such as the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) or Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC). Thirdly, the evaluation of postoperative numbness area and pain scores depended on manual measurements rather than computer-assisted quantification, introducing potential subjective bias. Fourthly, the study design did not account for anatomical variations of the saphenous nerve, which may influence clinical outcomes. Finally, the disparity in final enrollment numbers (31 patients in Group M vs. 29 in Group T) resulted from post-randomization exclusions or loss to follow-up rather than inherent flaws in the randomization protocol. Despite these limitations, rigorous adherence to randomization and blinding procedures was maintained throughout the trial to minimize potential biases. Future investigations should entail multicenter collaborations, expanded sample sizes, prolonged follow-up durations, and more comprehensive outcome assessments to thoroughly evaluate the long-term impacts of this modified surgical methodology.

Conclusion

This double-blind randomized controlled trial demonstrates that the modified incision-exposure technique used in this study effectively protects the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve without causing operational difficulties in osteotomy, ligament balancing, gap balancing, or prosthesis implantation during total knee replacements. This technique results in a smaller area of postoperative skin numbness compared to the traditional surgical method, with less postoperative pain and faster recovery.
