Retinal nerve fiber layer thickness in epilepsy: a meta-analysis comparing affected patients with healthy controls
Authors: Liu Jiangyi, Lv Shuping, Sun Yulei, Nathan Congdon, Chen Lei, Liang Yuanbo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEmerging evidence suggests that neurophysiological alterations in epilepsy may contribute to retinal changes. This meta-analysis aimed to evaluate retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness in patients with epilepsy compared to healthy controls, as measured by optical coherence tomography (OCT).MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus for studies published from inception to December 1, 2024, assessing RNFL thickness using OCT in epilepsy patients versus healthy individuals. Clinical studies and systematic reviews were included. The subjects were patients with epilepsy diagnosed by clinicians. Patients with suspected epilepsy were not included in this study analysis. There were no language or geographical restrictions.Two authors independently evaluated the quality of the literature, and a third party arbitrated the different evaluation results. Only literature judged to be of medium or high quality was included. This paper conducted a meta-analysis of the RNFL values collected from epilepsy patients and healthy controls, and conducted subgroup analysis and heterogeneity analysis on epilepsy type, epilepsy duration, OCT type, and study type.Data were analyzed using R studio (version 4.4.1).ResultsA total of 20 studies involving 1307 epilepsy patients and 880 controls were included. The pooled analysis revealed a significantly reduced mean RNFL thickness in epilepsy patients compared to controls (random-effects model, Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)= -0.62, 95% CI -0.82 to -0.42, I² = 73%, p < 0.01). Subgroup analysis indicated that country accounted for a part of heterogeneity (Q = 9.98, df = 2, p = 0.0068). Further analysis based on national income levels (middle or high-income) demonstrated a consistent effect (SMD = -0.62, 95% CI -0.82 to -0.42, I² = 73.5%, p < 0.0001). No significant differences were observed in subgroup analyses based on OCT type (p = 0.36) or study design (p = 0.54).ConclusionOCT can be used as an examination instrument to detect changes in brain nerves.This Meta-review suggests that there is an association between epilepsy and changes in the optic nerve, Mean RNFL thickness is significantly decreased in epilepsy patients relative to healthy controls.It provides a theoretical basis for utilizing RNFL alterations as potential structural indicators associated with disease progression in patients with epilepsy. Furthermore, it offers a conceptual framework for future investigations exploring the relationship between observable ocular structural changes and epilepsy-related neurological processes.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-026-04789-7.Keywords: Epilepsy, RNFL, OCT, Meta-analysis

Introduction

The eyes and brain originate from a common embryological structure, the neural tube, and share notable similarities in vascular architecture, neural composition, and immune environment [1]. This shared developmental origin makes the retina a uniquely accessible extension of the central nervous system, offering a non-invasive window for in vivo assessment of neural and microvascular tissues [2].

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder affecting approximately 50 million people worldwide. Increasing evidence suggests that epilepsy involves not only cortical dysfunction but also systemic and peripheral manifestations [3]. Early identification of epileptic seizures is one of the important bases for judging the progression of epilepsy. In clinical practice and drug treatment trials, self-reports of epileptic seizures are often used as a reference basis for treatment decisions [4]. However, this approach is largely unreliable. Research shows that through video electroencephalogram (EEG) monitoring, approximately 47% to 63% of epileptic seizures are not promptly identified by patients [5]. Especially at night, the rate of missed diagnosis of epileptic seizures is even higher, reaching up to 86% [6]. According to the expert consensus [7], among the secondary prevention measures for epilepsy, the most crucial one is to identify brain damage and the early signs of epileptic seizures. Visual disturbances occur in up to 30% of epilepsy patients, and accumulating data indicate that recurrent seizures and associated pathophysiological changes may lead to retinal neurodegeneration, which means retina may serve as a sensitive indicator of epilepsy-related neurophysiological damage [8].

This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to synthesize current evidence on RNFL thickness in epilepsy patients compared to healthy controls as assessed by Optical coherence tomograph (OCT). OCT is a non-invasive, high-resolution imaging technology widely used to assess ocular structures [9–11]. In epilepsy, it offers a promising method for detecting subclinical neurodegenerative changes and may serve as a surrogate marker for disease burden and progression. By identifying structural retinal changes associated with epilepsy, this study may offer a conceptual foundation for future research investigating the relationship between observable ocular structural changes and epilepsy-related neurological processes. If a reliable association between visible retinal changes and seizure-related pathology can be established, this approach could reduce healthcare burden, improve patient safety, and provide a convenient supplementary method for disease assessment and longitudinal monitoring.

Methods

Search strategy

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [12] and was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, ID: CRD42024608855). A comprehensive literature search was performed using PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science to identify relevant studies which is no language restrictions published from database inception to December 1, 2024.

Search terms included combinations of keywords related to epilepsy, retinal structure, and OCT imaging: (“Epilepsies” OR “Seizure Disorders” OR “Cryptogenic Epilepsies” OR “Aura” OR “Awakening Epilepsy”) AND (“retinal thickness” OR “RNFL” OR “retinal nerve fiber layer”) AND (“optical coherence tomography” OR “OCT” OR “optical coherence tomography angiography”). Two independent researchers (JL and SL) conducted the searches and cross-verified the results. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (YS).

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data were extracted based on pre-specified research objectives, covering basic study details (title, authors, year, country, study design) and key parameters (sample size, age, gender, OCT/OCTA device type, and peripapillary RNFL thickness).

Given the diverse study designs—including case-control, cross-sectional, and cohort studies—two validated quality assessment tools were used. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) (available at http://www.ohri.ca/programs/clinical_epidemiology/oxford.asp). was applied for case-control and cohort studies, evaluating selection, comparability, and exposure, with a maximum score of 9; studies scoring > 4 were deemed eligible. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) (available at http://jbi.global/critical-appraisal-tools) tool was applied for cross-sectional studies, with a maximum score of 11, and studies scoring > 4 considered medium to high quality.

RNFL thickness was reported using either a six-sector (temporal, temporal-inferior, nasal-inferior, nasal, nasal-superior, temporal-superior) or four-quadrant (superior, nasal, inferior, temporal) segmentation approach. For consistency, data from six-sector measurements were merged to align with the four-quadrant format for statistical analysis.

Data extraction and quality assessment were conducted independently by two researchers (JL and SL), with discrepancies resolved through consensus. Additionally, reference lists of included studies were screened to identify any eligible studies missed during the initial search.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio (version 4.4.1), applying the meta and metadat packages. A random-effects model with a two-sided p-value threshold of < 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance. Mean retinal thickness values were calculated for each study to enable inter-study comparisons, and to facilitate subsequent data analysis, the included data were standardized (quantitative data: mean ± standard deviation, binary data: X/Y).

A meta-analysis of means was performed using Hedges’ g to adjust for variance between study designs and small sample sizes [1, 2]. Cohen’s d was initially calculated for each study pair to assess the strength and direction of differences, with Hedges’ g subsequently applied for corrected effect size estimates. Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic, with values of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding studies with fewer than 50 participants to minimize potential variability.

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on factors including study region/country, epilepsy type (Generalized epilepsy, Drug-resistant epilepsy) and duration, OCT device type (Heidelberg, Carl Zeiss. Topcon), and study design(cross-sectional study, case control study, study cohort), to investigate their influence on outcomes. Additional analyses explored RNFL thickness in patients with different epilepsy types and durations to assess disease impact from multiple perspectives. Forest plots were generated to visualize effect sizes and variability, with funnel plots used to evaluate potential publication bias. Standardized mean differences (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated using random-effects modeling (REML) to account for heterogeneity. When ≥ 10 studies were available, Egger’s test was applied to detect publication bias and assess robustness of results.

Ethics statement

This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. As it involved no patient-identifiable information, institutional ethics committee approval was not required.

Results

Literature identification and screening

A total of 241 articles were identified through database searches: 97 from PubMed, 27 from Scopus, and 117 from Web of Science. Before preliminary screening, 31 records (12.9%) were excluded based on document type (dissertations, reviews, conference abstracts, and letters). An additional 133 studies (55.2%) were excluded as irrelevant, and 41 (17.0%) were duplicates. (Fig. 1)

The remaining 36 articles were reviewed in full by two independent researchers. Studies were further excluded for lacking a healthy control group (n = 7, 2.9%) or not reporting RNFL measurements assessed by OCT (n = 6, 2.5%). As a result, 23 articles (9.5%) were retained for quality assessment using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) and the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) tools.

Among case-control studies, two were rated as low-quality, five as moderate-quality, and two as high-quality. Two cohort studies were rated as moderate-quality. Among cross-sectional studies, one was classified as low-quality, nine as moderate-quality, and two as high-quality. Three low-quality studies were excluded, leaving 20 eligible studies for final analysis [13–32].

Data extracted from these 20 studies are summarized in Table 1 summary of included studies. The sample included 11 cross-sectional, seven case-control, and two cohort studies, conducted in Turkey [14–20, 22–25] (n = 11), China [27, 30] (n = 2), the United Kingdom [29, 31] (n = 2), Spain [26] (n = 1), Germany [13, 21, 32](n = 3), and Italy [28] (n = 1). Four studies focused on pediatric and adolescent epilepsy, while the remaining 16 addressed adult populations. Of the 20 studies, five reported only mean RNFL data, five reported only quadrant-specific RNFL data, and ten provided both. Consequently, 15 studies were included in the meta-analysis for mean RNFL and 15 for quadrant-specific RNFL.

The final pooled sample comprised 1,307 epilepsy patients (1,732 eyes) and 880 healthy controls (1,241 eyes).

Comparison of mean and quadrant-specific RNFL thickness between epilepsy patients and healthy controls

Fifteen studies provided data on mean RNFL thickness [13–18, 20–22, 25–27, 29, 30, 32], with two applying masked OCT image interpretation [27, 30]. The pooled analysis included 1,030 epilepsy patients (1,353 eyes) and 689 healthy controls (830 eyes) (Table 1; Fig. 2).

Heterogeneity test result

Substantial heterogeneity was observed in the overall analysis (I²=73%, τ²=0.0988, p < 0.01), warranting the use of a random-effects model. Meta-analysis results: RNFL values in epilepsy patients were significantly lower than those in healthy controls (SMD = − 0.62, 95% CI − 0.82 to − 0.42, p < 0.001). The funnel plot appeared symmetrical, indicating no substantial publication bias. Sensitivity analysis confirmed that exclusion of any single study did not materially alter the results. Egger’s test (t = 0.44, df = 13, p = 0.6668) further supported the absence of publication bias.

Analysis of quadrant-specific data revealed significant RNFL thinning in epilepsy patients compared to controls in the inferior (SMD = − 0.53, 95% CI [–0.75, − 0.30], p = 0.002), nasal (SMD = − 0.47, 95% CI [–0.70, − 0.25], p = 0.0005) and superior quadrants (SMD = − 0.53, 95% CI [–0.80, − 0.27], p = 0.0007) under the random-effects model. Under the fixed-effect model, the temporal quadrant also demonstrated a significantly lower NRFL among epilepsy patients (SMD = − 0.28, 95% CI [–0.38, − 0.18], p < 0.0001). (Table 2)

In patients with generalized epilepsy, significant RNFL thinning was found in mean RNFL (Hedges’s g = − 0.54, 95% CI − 0.84 to − 0.34, p = 0.0004), and the inferior (–0.42, − 0.67 to − 0.17, p = 0.0010) and temporal quadrants (–0.31, − 0.60 to − 0.01, p = 0.0394), with minimal heterogeneity (I² = 0–14%). No significant differences with health control were observed in the nasal or superior quadrants.

For refractory epilepsy, significant thinning was detected only in the temporal quadrant (Hedges’s g = − 0.33, 95% CI − 0.54 to − 0.11, p = 0.0031, I²=0%). No significant differences were found in mean RNFL or the other quadrants, with high heterogeneity noted in several comparisons.

RNFL thickness and epilepsy duration

As shown in Table 3 Meta-analysis of retinal structural alterations in epilepsy type &disease durationand supplement, studies were grouped by epilepsy duration. Only one study involved patients with a disease duration under five years [23], precluding meta-analysis for this group. The 5–10 year subgroup included two studies [25, 27], while the 10–20 year group comprised nine studies [13, 15–19, 22, 25, 27]. Two studies involved patients with epilepsy for over 20 years [26, 29].

In the 5–10 year subgroup, significant RNFL thinning was observed in the inferior (Hedges’s g = − 0.80, 95% CI − 1.05 to − 0.55, p < 0.0001) and superior quadrants (–0.36, − 0.60 to − 0.12, p = 0.0036), both with negligible heterogeneity. No significant differences were detected for mean, nasal, or temporal RNFL thickness, though high heterogeneity was present in these comparisons.

In the 10–20 year group, RNFL thickness was significantly reduced across all parameters, including mean RNFL (–0.60, 95% CI − 0.87 to − 0.33, p = 0.0006) and each quadrant, with low-to-moderate heterogeneity.

Among patients with epilepsy exceeding 20 years, significant thinning was observed in all quadrants except the nasal. Minimal heterogeneity was noted in most analyses, supporting the consistency of these findings.

RNFL and geographic location

Fifteen studies provided both mean RNFL data and regional classifications, allowing for subgroup analysis based on study location: Germany, Turkey, and other countries (including China, Spain, and the UK) showed in supplement. Heterogeneity within these subgroups was as follows: I² = 0% for Germany, 25% for Turkey, and 78% for other countries. The German and Turkish subgroups demonstrated relatively low heterogeneity, while the “other countries” subgroup exhibited considerable variability. Overall heterogeneity across all included studies remained substantial (I² = 73.5% [55.8%; 84.1%]).

A test for subgroup differences revealed a statistically significant effect of study country on the results (Q = 9.98, df = 2, p = 0.0068), indicating that geographic region Or ethnicity contributed to the observed heterogeneity. To further explore this, studies were regrouped based on national income levels (middle- and high-income countries). The analysis showed no significant difference between these economic groups (Q = 0.02, df = 1, p = 0.8864), suggesting that economic status alone did not account for the heterogeneity.

Importantly, the finding of significantly reduced mean RNFL thickness in epilepsy patients compared to healthy controls was consistent across all geographic regions.

Subgroup analysis by OCT device type divided studies into three categories. Under the random-effects model, subgroup heterogeneity remained substantial (I² = 73.5% [55.8%; 84.1%]), and no significant difference was found between OCT device types (Q = 2.06, df = 2, p = 0.3573). However, when applying a fixed-effects model, a significant difference emerged between the three OCT types (Q = 11.68, df = 2, p = 0.0029), suggesting that OCT device type may influence effect estimates in fixed-effects meta-analyses.

Discussion

This meta-analysis, synthesizing data from 20 studies, confirms that patients with epilepsy exhibit significantly reduced mean RNFL thickness compared to healthy controls, across all four retinal quadrants but the nasal quadrant often showed less consistent or non-significant thinning compared to the inferior or superior quadrants. The relatively less consistent thinning observed in the nasal quadrant may be explained by the topographic organization of the visual pathway and the phenomenon of trans-synaptic retrograde degeneration (TSRD), whereby epileptogenic propagation, particularly involving post-chiasmal pathways such as Meyer’s loop, may preferentially affect the superior and inferior arcuate fiber bundles [33–35]. In addition, according to the ISNT rule, the inferior and superior RNFL sectors are physiologically thicker and therefore may exhibit more detectable structural loss on OCT, whereas the thinner nasal quadrant may be more susceptible to a relative “floor effect,” limiting the statistical detectability of further thinning [36, 37]. Regional vascular and metabolic differences may also contribute to this asymmetric vulnerability pattern [23, 38].

These consistent findings highlight the retina as a window into central nervous system integrity in epilepsy. Bayat’s study [39] showed that there was no significant difference in retinal structure between patients with epilepsy treated with multiple drugs and those treated with a single drug, suggesting that the effect of antiepileptic drugs on the retina was relatively limited. However, this study did not consider the potential impact of epilepsy type and duration on the optic nerve. This meta-analysis makes up for this deficiency. The results indicated that patients with an epilepsy duration of 5–10 years exhibited significantly reduced RNFL thickness compared with healthy controls. However, substantial heterogeneity was observed across the included studies, and the sources of this variability could not be fully determined. One possible explanation for the observed heterogeneity may lie in differences among studies regarding seizure frequency and the dosage or type of antiepileptic medications. These factors were not consistently reported or controlled across the included literature, and several studies did not provide detailed information on these variables. As a result, the potential influence of these clinical characteristics could not be adequately accounted for, limiting our ability to further reduce heterogeneity and draw definitive conclusions. In patients with epilepsy for more than 20 years, although the results still showed thinning of the retinal fiber layer, the heterogeneity was smaller, indicating that the results in this group were more consistent. There are two possible explanations for this phenomenon: On the one hand, epileptic patients have frequent attacks, which may continuously damage the optic nerve during the repeated attacks. As the disease lasts for a long time, the optic nerve damage becomes more obvious [13–41]. On the other hand, age is an important factor affecting retinal fiber layer thickness, which decreases with age. Therefore, the thinning of RNFL thickness in patients with epilepsy duration > 20 years cannot be attributed solely to the effects of epilepsy. Due to the failure to obtain original data, this study was unable to rule out the interference of age as a confounding factor on RNFL values. In the future, this phenomenon can be further explained by conducting subgroup analysis on epilepsy patients with the same number of years of illness and adjusting by age.

As for the relationship between epilepsy-related neurodegeneration and national income level, although the subgroup analysis stratified by national income level did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence that socioeconomic factors are unrelated to RNFL impairment in epilepsy. Socioeconomic disparities may exert indirect effects on retinal neurodegeneration through mechanisms such as delayed diagnosis, suboptimal disease control, inadequate treatment adherence, or limited access to specialized neurological care. Therefore, the absence of a detectable moderating effect at the country-income level likely reflects the limitations of using a macroeconomic proxy rather than conclusively excluding a potential influence of socioeconomic determinants.

In addition, subgroup analyses based on epilepsy subtype revealed differential patterns of RNFL involvement. Patients with generalized epilepsy exhibited significant thinning in three quadrants, whereas those with refractory epilepsy showed a significant reduction confined to a single quadrant. This variation may suggest heterogeneity in the extent or distribution of retinal neuroaxonal involvement across epilepsy subtypes, potentially reflecting differences in seizure burden, disease chronicity, or cumulative neurobiological stress. Nevertheless, given the relatively limited number of studies within certain subgroups and the inherent heterogeneity of the included populations, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further large-scale, well-controlled studies are warranted to elucidate whether distinct epilepsy phenotypes are associated with specific patterns of retinal structural alteration. The heterogeneity between the two was small, and the results were more convincing. Due to the limited number of included literature, this study was unable to conduct subgroup analysis of more epilepsy types. Based on the above results, we speculate that different types of epilepsy may have different degrees of damage to the optic nerve.

Pathophysiological Insights

The close anatomical and physiological link between the eye and brain, through shared neural pathways and vascular ecosystems, provides a plausible basis for retinal alterations in epilepsy. Seizure-related sympathetic activation may disrupt ocular perfusion, leading to ischemia, neural stress, and eventual ganglion cell degeneration and axonal loss [42–44]. OCT offers a non-invasive tool to visualize these neurovascular changes in vivo [45], presenting opportunities for improved diagnosis, monitoring, and prognostication in epilepsy care [46].

Clinical implications

Retinal thinning may reflect systemic neurological involvement and serve as an early biomarker of disease severity and cumulative burden. This pooled analysis confirms that patients with epilepsy exhibit significant RNFL thinning both in mean values and in individual quadrants when compared with healthy controls, with this effect largely consistent across studies despite substantial heterogeneity. The absence of significant modifying effects by age and AED exposure suggests that retinal alterations in epilepsy may reflect disease-related neurodegenerative mechanisms rather than treatment-related or demographic factors. Incorporating OCT into the clinical management of epilepsy may offer a practical adjunct for longitudinal monitoring of retinal neuroaxonal integrity. Instead of predicting seizure events, OCT measurements may help track structural changes associated with chronic disease burden and treatment exposure, thereby supporting comprehensive disease surveillance over time.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small pooled sample size and the predominance of single-center, cross-sectional studies reduce the generalizability and statistical power of our findings. Another important limitation lies in the scope of retinal structural data. The majority of included studies focused exclusively on peripapillary RNFL measurements, with limited reporting on macular RNFL or other retinal layers such as the GCC and choroid. This narrow focus restricts the comprehensiveness of structural assessment and impedes a holistic understanding of retinal alterations in epilepsy.

Furthermore, sources of heterogeneity identified in subgroup analyses, such as epilepsy subtype, disease duration, and OCT device type, were only partially accounted for, potentially affecting the precision of certain pooled effect estimates. Although significant differences were observed across different epilepsy duration groups, with longer disease duration being associated with greater RNFL thinning, patients with longer disease duration also tended to have a higher mean age. Therefore, the potential confounding effect of age on RNFL measurements cannot be excluded. Given that physiological aging is independently associated with progressive RNFL thinning, the observed relationship between epilepsy duration and retinal structural changes should be interpreted with caution.

In addition, inconsistent reporting of seizure frequency, antiepileptic drug exposure, and imaging protocols across studies limited the ability to conduct more detailed exploratory or stratified analyses. These methodological constraints may have contributed to residual heterogeneity and reduced the overall interpretability of certain subgroup findings. Collectively, these limitations underscore the need for large-scale, standardized, and methodologically rigorous retinal imaging studies incorporating comprehensive clinical data across diverse epilepsy populations.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis provides evidence that epilepsy is associated with significant thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer, supporting the concept that retinal changes may serve as a peripheral reflection of central neurodegenerative processes in epilepsy. These findings underscore the potential utility of retinal imaging, particularly OCT, as a non-invasive adjunctive biomarker for neurological disease monitoring.

However, given the limitations in available evidence, particularly regarding epilepsy subtype, seizure burden, and long-term AED effects, caution is warranted in interpreting these results. Future prospective, longitudinal, and subtype-specific investigations are essential to clarify causal relationships, delineate the temporal evolution of retinal alterations, and determine the clinical value of OCT-based retinal biomarkers in epilepsy management.

Incorporating standardized, multi-layer retinal assessments alongside detailed clinical and neuroimaging data will be critical for advancing our understanding of the complex interplay between epilepsy, antiepileptic therapy, and neuro-ophthalmic health.
