Intrathecal morphine for postoperative analgesia in major laparoscopic abdominal surgery (IMPACT-Scope): a protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Wudi Ma, Ciro Morgese, Wei Xiong, Yuxiao Liu, Jinyu Wu, Qiulan He, Ming Wei, Jiangtao Zhou, Ruiming Liang, Natalie Ives, Fangzhou Xu, Louise Jackson, Matthew Soden, Kar Keung Cheng, Fang Gao Smith, Xia Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEffective perioperative pain management is a cornerstone of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery, yet standard opioid-based intravenous patient-controlled analgesia often leads to insufficient pain control, excessive adverse reactions and delayed recovery following laparoscopic abdominal surgery. Intrathecal morphine (ITM) has shown promising analgesic efficacy in abdominal and obstetric procedures but high-quality evidence supporting its use in major laparoscopic surgery remains limited. The ITM for postoperative analgesia in major laparoscopic abdominal surgery (IMPACT-Scope) trial aims to determine whether ITM, when added to usual care, improves postoperative recovery quality compared with usual care alone.Method and analysisThis is a multicentre, parallel-group, two-arm, randomised, sham-controlled, double-blind, superiority trial with an integrated full health economic evaluation. A total of 700 adult patients undergoing elective major laparoscopic or robotic abdominal surgery at 15 tertiary teaching hospitals in South and Central China will be enrolled. Participants are randomised (1:1) to assign either ITM (300 µg of preservative-free morphine) or sham ITM plus usual care. The primary outcome is the quality of recovery at postoperative day (POD) 1, measured by the 15-item Quality of Recovery questionnaire (QoR-15). Secondary outcomes include QoR-15 at POD 2 and POD 3, opioid consumption, postoperative mobilisation, adverse events and length of stay. Health economic and safety evaluations are embedded. Analysis will follow the modified intention-to-treat principle using regression models adjusted for minimisation variables.Ethics and disseminationThe protocol (V.2.0, approved on 11 October 2024) has been reviewed and approved by the IEC for Clinical Research and Animal Trials of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Ref number: 20240222) and the local ethics committees of all participating sites. Introduction

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) is a multidisciplinary perioperative care framework that integrates evidence-based interventions to minimise surgical stress, accelerate recovery and improve outcomes for patients undergoing major surgery.1 2 Implementation of ERAS pathways has consistently been shown to reduce postoperative pain, complications and hospital stay, while expediting return to usual daily activities and functional status.2 3 Moreover, ERAS pathways are recognised as cost-effective across a range of abdominal surgical procedures.4 5 Effective perioperative pain management remains a cornerstone of ERAS, with current strategies focusing on multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia to promote early mobilisation and recovery.6 7

Intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (IV-PCA) with opioids remains the current reference standard postoperative pain management technique for patients undergoing major laparoscopic abdominal surgery.8 However, IV-PCA is frequently associated with suboptimal analgesia and opioid-related adverse reactions (ARs), including sedation, nausea, ileus and respiratory depression and opioid addiction, which may delay postoperative recovery and compromise patient safety.9 10 Epidural analgesia, once considered as the first-line analgesic treatment for major abdominal procedures, offers limited benefit in laparoscopic surgery and is associated with technical challenges and side effects.11–15

Intrathecal opioid administration for postoperative pain control was first introduced in 1979.16 Morphine is the opioid drug approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for intrathecal use and is recommended as the first-line intrathecal drug by the Poly Analgesic Consensus Conference.17 compared with systemic opioid administration, intrathecal morphine (ITM) achieves potent and long-lasting analgesia with lower doses and fewer systemic ARs.18–20 While ITM is routinely used in obstetric anaesthesia, its role in laparoscopic abdominal surgery remains uncertain. Theoretical advantages of ITM include enhanced pain control and reduced systemic opioid consumption and side-effects,21 but the existing evidence base is inconsistent.22–24 Some studies have shown improvements in pain scores, opioid use and patient satisfaction following major abdominal surgery,22 23 whereas others have not demonstrated measurable benefits in recovery.24

Importantly, previous randomised trials have been limited by small sample sizes, single-centre designs and a predominant focus on clinician-centred endpoints, such as pain scores or opioid consumption, rather than on patient-centred measures of recovery. Recent calls from the perioperative research community have emphasised the need for large, pragmatic trials using outcomes that reflect patients’ lived experiences and priorities following surgery, recognising that traditional clinician-reported metrics may not adequately capture the quality of recovery after surgery.25 26 Whether the analgesic benefits of ITM translate into meaningful, patient-centred improvements in postoperative recovery remains unknown.

To address these gaps, we designed a large, multicentre, sham-controlled randomised trial to evaluate the impact of ITM as an adjunct to standard IV-PCA within an ERAS framework for major laparoscopic abdominal surgery. The primary objective of this multicentre randomised controlled trial is to determine whether the addition of ITM to standard opioid-based IV-PCA enhances patient-centred quality of recovery within an ERAS pathway for major laparoscopic abdominal surgery.

By enrolling a broad population across multiple centres and integrating cost-effectiveness analysis, this study aims to provide definitive evidence on the role of ITM in modern ERAS practice.

Methods and analysis

Study design

ITM for postoperative analgesia in major laparoscopic abdominal surgery (IMPACT-Scope) is a multicentre, parallel group, two-arm, randomised sham-controlled, blinded, superiority trial with health economic evaluation. This protocol is based on the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines 24 and the SPIRIT checklist (online supplemental appendix 1). Study setting

Recruitment will take place at 15 tertiary teaching hospitals in South and Central of China.

Study timeline

Recruitment began on 15 December 2024 and is expected to be completed by 31 December 2026. Participant follow-up will continue until 30 days after surgery, and final analyses will be undertaken after completion of follow-up of the last participant and database lock.

Recruitment and consent

Patients requiring elective major laparoscopic abdominal surgery and who meet the eligibility criteria can be recruited into the trial. Eligibility will be confirmed following discussions with the patient and a review of their medical notes by the research team. The study protocol, including treatment allocations, randomisation procedures, specific trial procedures, potential risks and benefits, will be detailed for eligible individuals during screening in the patient information sheet (online supplemental appendix 5).

Randomisation, grouping and blinding

After eligibility has been confirmed, and informed consent has been given, the patient will be randomised at the level of the individual in a 1:1 ratio to receive either ITM plus usual care (Intervention) or Sham ITM plus usual care (Control) through the Central Randomisation System of the First Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University. A minimisation algorithm will ensure balance in the intervention allocation over the following variables: age group (<60 years or ≥60 years), gender, surgical specialty and centre. Information on randomisation and group allocation remains blinded to the clinical research staff responsible for the data collection, outcomes assessors and participants. However, due to the nature of the study, the anaesthetists delivering the intervention are aware of the allocations to ensure the safe care.

Interventions and implementation

In the Intervention group, participants will receive ITM, which consists of an intrathecal injection of 300 µg of preservative-free morphine. To maintain the pragmatic nature of the trial, the spinal injection will be performed according to local protocols available at the participating centres regarding neuraxial anaesthesia/analgesia procedures.

In the Control group, participants will receive sham ITM, which mimics the standard procedure, but the dura is not breached. In the sham procedure, the skin is sterilised as for a neuraxial injection and the L3/L4 intervertebral space is identified by palpation. The skin is then infiltrated with a subcutaneous injection of 1% lidocaine (3–10 mL) at the level of L3/L4. Pressure is then applied to the sterilised skin with a capped syringe for approximately 1 min, followed by another subcutaneous injection of 3 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride at the same level.

In both groups, the procedures must be performed by an anaesthetist before induction of anaesthesia. After the intervention, opaque occlusive dressings will be applied.

Perioperative analgesic management and usual care

Perioperative analgesic management, other than the allocated study intervention, will follow local ERAS pathways and institutional standard operating procedures at participating centres. Variation in practice between centres is expected and is consistent with the pragmatic design of the trial. All perioperative analgesic interventions will be prospectively recorded.

Intraoperative analgesic management

Intraoperative analgesic management will be at the discretion of the attending anaesthetist in accordance with local practice and institutional protocols. Opioid and non-opioid analgesics may be administered as clinically indicated. Examples of agents that may be used include opioid analgesics (eg, fentanyl, morphine, remifentanil) and non-opioid analgesics (eg, paracetamol, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, ketamine and intravenous lidocaine). These agents are not mandated or restricted by the protocol. Continuous intraoperative infusions of analgesic agents are permitted but must be discontinued at the end of surgery. In addition, local anaesthetic infiltration of surgical wounds is permitted. However, regional analgesic techniques, including epidural analgesia, peripheral nerve blocks and fascial plane blocks, are not permitted to minimise confounding. All intraoperative analgesic interventions, including agents, doses, routes and duration of administration, will be recorded prospectively.

1. Multimodal analgesia protocols.

All participants will receive multimodal analgesia in accordance with local ERAS pathways and institutional protocols. A predetermined, protocolised multimodal analgesic regimen is not mandated by the study. Instead, multimodal analgesic strategies will reflect local standard practice at each participating centre, consistent with the pragmatic design of the trial. This will normally include regular non-opioid analgesics, such as paracetamol and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, unless contraindicated. Additional opioid and non-opioid analgesics may be used according to local practice.

Regional analgesic techniques, including epidural analgesia, peripheral nerve blocks and fascial plane blocks, are not permitted to avoid confounding the assessment of the study intervention. This is the only restriction placed on multimodal analgesic management; the choice, dosing, route, timing and duration of administration of other analgesic agents remain at the discretion of local institutional protocols, and will be recorded prospectively.

2. IV-PCA.

All participants will receive opioid-based IV-PCA. To preserve the pragmatic nature of the trial, the choice of opioid and PCA settings will follow local institutional protocols. Participating centres may use locally available opioid agents (eg, morphine, sufentanil, fentanyl, hydromorphone), with PCA parameters (including bolus dose, lockout interval and maximum dose limits) determined according to their standard practice and recorded prospectively. Background infusions of opioids will not be permitted.

3. Rescue analgesia thresholds.

Rescue analgesia will be administered when postoperative pain is considered inadequately controlled according to local clinical assessment and institutional protocols. While no rigid universal threshold is imposed in this trial, rescue analgesia is expected to follow routine clinical practice across participating centres in China, where it is commonly administered when the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS 0–10) pain score is ≥4. Rescue medications could include additional opioid or non-opioid analgesics according to local protocols. All rescue analgesic administration, including indication, drug, dose, route and timing, is recorded prospectively.

4. Opioid consumption.

All postoperative opioid consumption, including IV-PCA and rescue opioid administration, will be converted to oral morphine equivalent daily dose (MEDD) using standardised conversion factors (online supplemental appendix 2) to provide a standardised measure of total postoperative opioid consumption across participants.

Cost-effectiveness outcomes

Resource use data will be collected prospectively to estimate the costs associated with both trial arms during surgery, immediately after surgery, and in the follow-up period. The resource use to be monitored will include: (1) the cost of preoperative care; (2) the cost of the analgesic and other treatment costs during surgery; (3) costs experienced immediately after surgery; (4) costs associated with the treatment of postoperative pain. Information on unit costs or prices will be sourced to attach to each resource use item, to enable an overall cost per patient to be calculated. Resource use data will be captured via trial reporting mechanisms. The health outcomes considered will reflect the primary and secondary outcomes for the trial, at baseline, POD 1, POD 2, POD 3 and at POD 30.

Sample size

QoR-15 is a well-established patient-reported outcome measure for assessing the quality of recovery after surgery and anaesthesia and is validated in the Chinese language.27 It employs a scale ranging from 0 to 150, where higher scores indicate better recovery. The minimum clinically important difference for QoR-15 is established at 6 points.28

Considering studies that investigated QoR-15 scores at POD 1 in populations similar to this study, we adopted a SD of 23.1 to calculate the sample size.29–31 To detect a 6-point difference between groups with 90% power at a 5% significance level, a total of 626 participants would be required. Factoring in a 10% attrition rate, we estimated the sample size up to 700 participants in total (350 in each group).

Statistical analysis

Data will be analysed using R software and SAS V.9.4. Analysis will follow a modified intention-to-treat approach, including all randomised participants who underwent surgery. Continuous data will be summarised as mean with SD or median with IQR, and categorical data as numbers and percentages. Between-group comparisons will adjust for minimisation variables and baseline scores where applicable. The primary outcome, the QoR-15 score on POD 1, will be analysed using a linear regression model adjusted for the baseline QoR-15 score and minimisation variables. The mean difference with its 95% CI and a p value for the intervention effect will be reported. Secondary continuous outcomes will be analysed similarly. Binary outcomes will be analysed using logistic regression with a logit link function, followed by marginal standardisation to estimate adjusted risk ratios and risk differences with 95% CIs. No p values will be reported for secondary outcomes. Exploratory subgroup analyses will be conducted for the primary outcome according to surgical specialty (colorectal, upper gastrointestinal, hepatobiliary/pancreatic, gynaecological and urological surgery), age group and sex. Treatment-by-subgroup interaction tests will be performed and interpreted cautiously because the trial is not powered for subgroup comparisons. A sensitivity analysis will be undertaken for the primary outcome to explore the effect of missing data on the results.

Data monitoring and management

In this study, data will be managed and analysed in accordance with the guidelines of Good Clinical Practice, ensuring strict confidentiality. All research data will be recorded in paper form of case report form (CRF) and subsequently entered into the electronic CRF on OpenClinica system by the authorised investigators. Data will be stored on secure servers at The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, to which only authorised users will have access. The Data Protection Policy and the Conditions of Use of Computing and Network Facilities in the First Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University set out the security arrangements under which sensitive data should be processed and stored. Data management and statistical analysis will be conducted by the clinical trial unit of The First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University.

All records created by following trial procedures, and all documents listed in guidance relating to the conduct of the trial, must be retained and archived. All relative documents will be securely retained for a minimum of 10 years but with an expectation for storage up to 25 years. Data will be available on reasonable request thereafter.

Discussion

There is currently no conclusive evidence to support the use of ITM analgesia in the postoperative setting for patients who have undergone abdominal laparoscopic surgery. This study is the first nationwide, large multicentre, randomised controlled clinical trial in China designed to evaluate the clinical and the cost effectiveness of ITM in patients undergoing all types of laparoscopic surgery. Effective analgesia is a key component of ERAS. By optimising conventional postoperative analgesic regimens in anaesthetic management, we aim to provide reliable evidence to the optimal analgesia strategies for laparoscopic surgery.

Currently, postoperative analgesia for major laparoscopic surgery both in China and globally primarily relies on IV-PCA or regional nerve blocks. These approaches are often associated with excessive opioid consumption, frequent adverse effects and limited postoperative mobility. ITM, as a neuraxial analgesic technique, provides potent and long-lasting pain relief with significantly lower opioid requirements. Although it has been extensively used in postoperative analgesia after caesarean section, high-level evidence supporting its applicability in major laparoscopic surgery is lacking. This study is the first to systematically investigate ITM across a full spectrum of laparoscopic procedures—including gastrointestinal, hepatobiliary, pancreatic, urological and gynaecological surgeries.

This trial adopted a pragmatic multicentre design in which perioperative analgesic management, apart from the allocated intervention, follows local ERAS pathways and institutional standard operating procedures. As a result, variability in analgesic practice between centres is expected and reflects routine clinical care. This variability is an intentional feature of the study design and enhances the generalisability of the findings to real-world perioperative settings. To account for between-centre differences, centre is included as a minimisation variable in the randomisation process.

Postoperative analgesia follows a common framework across participants, consisting of opioid-based IV-PCA, multimodal analgesia and rescue analgesia as required. However, specific drug choices, dosing regimens and prescribing practices remain at the discretion of local institutional protocols, and no uniform prescribing protocol is imposed beyond key protocol restrictions. This approach preserves real-world variability while ensuring that key elements of postoperative pain management are consistently applied across trial arms.

Variations in background analgesic practice should therefore be interpreted within the context of a pragmatic trial design that prioritises external validity while incorporating design features to minimise imbalance between groups.

This flexibility, however, does not extend to regional analgesic techniques, including epidural analgesia, peripheral nerve blocks and fascial plane blocks, which are not permitted under the protocol. Unlike variation in pharmacological analgesic choice, the use of such techniques could not be reliably distinguished from the effects of the ITM intervention itself and their use was therefore restricted to protect the internal validity of the primary comparison.

Potential role in ERAS process

The core of ERAS emphasises multidisciplinary collaboration to reduce surgical stress and accelerate functional recovery. This study advances analgesic intervention—a single intrathecal injection of morphine prior to anaesthesia induction to provide sustained analgesia for 24–48 hours postoperatively. This approach is expected to overcome the limitations of conventional postoperative analgesia that often delay early mobilisation. By markedly reducing intraoperative and postoperative opioid exposure, ITM is anticipated to lower the incidence of ERAS-related complications such as postoperative nausea and vomiting and paralytic ileus. This pre-emptive analgesia strategy represents a potential enhancement to the existing ERAS framework.

Comparing to the previous ITM study design

Previous studies investigating ITM have been limited by single-centre settings, small sample sizes and restricted surgical scope. This trial enrols over 700 patients across 15 tertiary teaching hospitals in South and Central China, employing a centralised randomisation system based on the minimisation principle. To minimise bias, a sham injection control group and double-blind design (with both participants and outcome assessors blinded to group allocation) are implemented, ensuring the methodological rigour and reliability of the results.
