A retrospective case series of amebic colitis clinical features at a tertiary hospital in Beijing from 2021 to 2024
Authors: Yan Hong Gao, Miao Yu, Li Ning Wang, Li Feng Dong, Jun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmebic colitis, an intestinal infection caused by the parasite Entamoeba histolytica, is increasingly common in nonendemic regions, particularly among young men and men who have sex with men.PurposeThis study aims to analyze the clinical manifestations, endoscopic features, pathological characteristics, and treatment outcomes of amebic colitis in male patients diagnosed at a tertiary hospital in Beijing.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted involving 12 male patients aged 28–44 years who were diagnosed with amebic colitis between 2021 and 2024.ResultsClinical evaluation revealed that 83.3% of the patients presented with abdominal pain and/or diarrhea. Endoscopic examination revealed segmental erosions or ulcerations in the ileocecal region, rectum, or entire colon, characterized by irregular ulcers covered with white coating. Histopathological analysis revealed large trophozoites on the inflamed colonic mucosa, with prominent nuclei and evidence of red blood cell phagocytosis in most specimens. Follicular inflammation was identified in 75% of the patients. Coinfections, including human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), latent syphilis and hepatitis C, ulcerative colitis, and a history of anal fistula surgery, were also noted.ConclusionsOur observed case series may suggest a potentially increasing recognition of amebic colitis in Beijing, though larger population-based studies are needed to confirm true changes in disease prevalence. Colonoscopy plays a critical role in diagnosis by revealing characteristic segmental ulcers in the ileocecal region or rectum. Accurate diagnosis and optimal clinical management require effective collaboration between clinicians and pathologists, supported by specific immunohistochemical staining.Clinical trialThis trial was not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11920-4.Keywords: Amebic colitis, Colonoscopy, Nonendemic regions, Men who have sex with men, Amebiasis

Introduction

Amebic colitis, caused by the protozoan Entamoeba histolytica, is a significant intestinal infection that is particularly prevalent in developing countries with inadequate sanitation. The pathogen is transmitted via the fecal‒oral route, and its trophozoites colonize the large intestine after reaching the distal ileum. Beyond the colon, E. histolytica can disseminate to extraintestinal sites, leading to severe complications such as liver and lung abscesses [1]. Amebic bowel disease accounts for an estimated 40,000–100,000 deaths annually, making it the second leading cause of mortality from parasitic infections [2, 3]. The clinical manifestations of amebic colitis are diverse and nonspecific and typically include abdominal pain, diarrhea with characteristic “jam-like” stools (hematochezia), and dysentery. These symptoms often overlap with those of other gastrointestinal disorders, such as ulcerative colitis or Crohn’s disease, making accurate diagnosis challenging.

Emerging evidence from case reports has established a strong association between amebic colitis and male homosexuality. In developed regions, including East Asia and Europe, this condition is increasingly recognized as a sexually transmitted intestinal disease (STID), which is transmitted predominantly through oral‒anal sexual contact [4–6]. In Japan, for example, risk factors include MSM, age under 50 years, prior syphilis infection, and HIV status [7]. Among HIV-negative individuals, contact with sex workers is a significant risk factor, whereas for HIV-positive individuals, male-to-male sexual contact is more strongly associated with the condition than immunosuppression. Consequently, amebic colitis is gaining recognition as a STID in these populations [8].

Despite its increasing prevalence in specific demographics, comprehensive reports on amebic colitis associated with MSM remain limited, with most literature consisting of sporadic case reports. Additionally, many clinicians and pathologists lack familiarity with this condition, which can further delay diagnosis. This study analyzed data from 12 patients diagnosed with amebic colitis at a tertiary hospital in Beijing over the past three years, aiming to provide a theoretical basis for enhancing the understanding and diagnostic accuracy of amebic colitis, revealing gender-related disease characteristics, optimizing treatment strategies and prognosis evaluation, guiding precise endoscopic and pathological discrimination, and strengthening public health prevention and control awareness.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This retrospective observational study consecutively included all patients diagnosed with amebic colitis confirmed by pathology at Chui Yang Liu Hospital Affiliated with Tsinghua University during 2021–2024. All 12 patients who met the inclusion criteria during the study period were male. All 12 patients included in this study were male, reflecting a characteristic of the patient population diagnosed at this center during the study period, rather than a result of intentional selection.

Results

Chief complaints

As shown in Table 1, all 12 patients were male, and no female patients were included in the study, with a mean age of 35.08 years (SD ± 5.76). The predominant clinical symptoms included diarrhea (75%), bloody stools (58.3%), and mucoid bloody stools with a foul odor, with distension reported by 16.7% and 16.7% of patients, respectively. These patients exhibited neither systemic symptoms (including fever or malaise) nor clinical manifestations suggestive of liver abscess. The duration of symptoms ranged from 2 weeks to 6 months, with a median duration of 3 weeks.

Comorbidities

In this cohort, all patients presented with mild to moderate disease activity, and none developed known severe complications such as fulminant colitis, toxic megacolon, or intestinal perforation. In addition to HIV status, we actively investigated other potential immunosuppressive conditions, including diabetes, active tuberculosis infection, and the use of corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants. The results showed that besides one patient with HIV infection, one patient with Hepatitis C Virus infection, one patient with latent syphilis, no patients had type 2 diabetes or active tuberculosis or were undergoing systemic immunosuppressive therapy.

History of past illness

50% (6/12) of the patients had a history of good physical health, and the other patients presented with comorbid conditions such as anal fistula, ulcerative colitis, sexual dysfunction, insomnia, and anxiety.

Personal and family history

A total of 58% (7/12) of the patients identified as men had sex with men (MSM), and 25% (3/12) had concurrent sexually transmitted infections, including syphilis and HIV. The family history was unremarkable.

Physical examination

In all 12 patients, vital signs were normal, and 3 patients had abdominal tenderness and no rebound pain.

Laboratory examination

The hematological and biochemical profiles of these patients were within normal limits. Among the eight individuals who underwent routine stool analysis and fecal occult blood testing, six demonstrated positive occult blood results. No ova or parasites were detected in the fecal samples. Microscopic examination revealed 3–30 red blood cells per high-power field (RBCs/HPF) and 0–3 white blood cells per high-power field (WBCs/HPF).

Endoscopic examination

Table 2 and Table S2 reveals that the ileocecal region and rectum were the most frequently affected sites, with involvement in 75% (9/12) and 50% (5/12) of the patients, respectively. The entire colon was affected in 25% (3/12) of patients. Lesions were also observed in the ascending, transverse, descending, and sigmoid colon, albeit less frequently, with the distal ileum involved in one case. Endoscopic examination typically reveals multiple ulcerative lesions covered with a pseudomembrane resembling a coagulated egg white, varying in size and shape (oval or irregular), with normal mucosa between the ulcers (Fig. 1A-L). Biopsies were taken from the ulcer edge or the junction between necrotic and normal mucosa. Typically, 3–5 pieces were collected per patient for pathological examination.

Pathological examination

Pathological examination revealed acute and chronic inflammation with ulcer formation, occasionally accompanied by eosinophilic infiltration, cryptitis, and occasional granulation tissue hyperplasia. A definitive diagnosis was confirmed by the identification of amebic trophozoites (Fig. 1M). Special staining techniques, such as periodic acid–Schiff (PAS), were used to confirm the presence of amebic organisms (Fig. 1N). Typical histological presentations included erosions or ulcers with “dirty necrosis” (i.e., liquefactive necrosis with abundant cellular debris and neutrophil infiltration). Differentiation between amebic trophozoites and macrophages was primarily based on morphological characteristics: trophozoites are generally smaller, with amphophilic cytoplasm (clear peripherally and granular centrally), and a characteristic concentric nucleolus (“ring-shaped” nucleus). When necessary, special histological stains (such as PAS staining or iron hematoxylin staining) were used to highlight trophozoite morphology for assisted differentiation. We defined the grading criteria for amoebic trophozoites as follows: Occasional refers to the observation of 1–2 trophozoites per 6 slides; Numerous indicates that trophozoites are present in every field of view; Moderate (visible) describes cases falling between these two ranges. We attempted to analyze the correlation between ulcer size and the number of amoeba trophozoites, but no significant relationship was found. This may be related to the small sample size, and further multicenter studies with larger samples are needed. For example, Case 4 presented with an ulcer approximately 6 mm in diameter in the ileocecal region, and pathological examination of the biopsy tissue revealed numerous amoebic trophozoites. In contrast, Case 1 exhibited a larger ulcer (about 15 mm in diameter) in the same region, yet only occasional amoebic trophozoites were observed in the pathological biopsy.

Final diagnosis

On the basis of the chief complaint, personal history, endoscopic examination, and pathological findings, all the patients were diagnosed with amebic colitis.

Treatment

Among the 12 patients, 9 received metronidazole or dichloride orally, along with oral intestinal probiotics, and 3 patients were lost to follow-up due to being unreachable by phone. Metronidazole 750 mg three times daily orally for 10 days or Metronidazole 750 mg three times daily orally for 10 days.

Outcome and follow-up

As of May 2025, the follow-up period has been as long as 3 years. The digestive tract symptoms of these 9 patients were relieved or disappeared during a month, except for 3 patients who were lost to follow-up. Among the 9 patients, 3 patients underwent colonoscopy reexamination, which revealed smooth mucosa and disappearance of previous colonic ulcers or erosions, The remaining six patients showed clinical improvement based on resolution of symptoms (abdominal pain, diarrhea), normalization of inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein), and negative repeat stool testing, which served as objective evidence of healing. Among the 9 treated patients, no relapses or long-term complications were reported during the follow-up period (median: 60 months; range: 29–136 months).

Discussion

Amebic colitis, caused by invasion of the colon wall by Entamoeba histolytica, has an incubation period ranging from one week to several months, with most cases presenting within 1–2 weeks. Interestingly, 4–10% of asymptomatic infected individuals may develop symptoms more than a year after the initial infection [9]. Among the 12 patients in our study cohort, the symptom duration ranged from one week to six months, with a median symptom duration of three weeks. The majority reported abdominal pain and diarrhea of varying severity, with 75% exhibiting classic symptoms of amebic colitis, including mucoid bloody stools with a foul odor. Notably, one patient presented atypically, experiencing upper abdominal discomfort and postprandial stuffiness without significant abdominal pain or diarrhea. This observation aligns with findings by Carrero et al. [10], highlighting that not all carriers of E. histolytica manifest acute symptoms. Our study likely suffers from selection bias as we only included patients who underwent colonoscopy with pathological confirmation. Consequently, patients with mild or atypical presentations who did not require endoscopic evaluation may have been excluded, potentially skewing our cohort toward more severe cases.

Epidemiologically, amebic colitis is strongly associated with poor socioeconomic conditions and inadequate sanitation in endemic regions, including specific provinces in China, such as Guangxi, Henan, Guangdong, Heilongjiang, and Jiangxi [11]. Low levels of education, low income, consuming unwashed fruits and vegetables, drinking well water, frequently dining out, not using anti-diarrheal medication, and living in crowded households are all risk factors associated with high levels of Entamoeba infection [12, 13]. However, in developed countries, the incidence of amebic colitis has recently increased among men who have sex with men (MSM), likely due to oral‒anal sexual contact, positioning amebic colitis as a sexually transmitted intestinal diseases (STID) (Table S1). Most of these reports are based on retrospective data analyses. Studies from multiple countries, including Australia, Spain, Japan, and China, indicate that case numbers range from 3 to over 1,800, with infection rates among MSM reaching as high as 65.7% to 100%. In our study, all patients diagnosed with amebic colitis via colonoscopy were male, with 58% identified as MSM which aligns with reports from James et al. [14]. Additionally, three patients were found to have coinfections, including HIV, syphilis, and hepatitis C. These findings are consistent with the literature, which identifies MSM as a high-risk group for intestinal protozoan infections due to fecal‒oral transmission through oral‒anal contact. This underscores the urgent need for targeted public health initiatives and heightened clinical awareness of this demographic. Admittedly, as a single-center study with a small sample size, our research warrants further investigation through multi-center studies with larger sample sizes.

Amebic colitis predominantly affects the ileocecal region but can also involve other segments of the colon. Endoscopic examination typically reveals irregular ulcers covered by necrotic changes on the ulcer surface, along with hyperemic and edematous mucosa prone to bleeding, while sparing the intervening mucosa. These findings often mimic those of inflammatory bowel disease [15], intestinal tuberculosis [16], and Behçet’s disease [17], complicating the differential diagnosis. Consequently, a definitive diagnosis requires identifying E. histolytica trophozoites in the stool or colonic mucosa via diagnostic techniques such as stool culture, immune-enzymatic assays, PCR, or endoscopic biopsy. Although stool cultures can detect motile trophozoites, they are time-consuming and less sensitive due to potential contamination. Hasan HK et al. [13] reported that the infection rate of amebic trophozoites in stool was only 21.68% (562/2592). In our study, the fecal occult blood positivity rate was 75% (6/8), with no ova or parasites detected in any of the patients. Serological tests can indicate past or ongoing infections but cannot differentiate between acute and chronic states. PCR and real-time PCR offer high sensitivity and specificity across sample types but require advanced laboratory resources, which may not be accessible in resource-limited settings. Thus, colonoscopy has become a vital diagnostic tool for amebic colitis, allowing precise lesion localization and facilitating biopsy collection for pathological examination. While colonoscopy provides valuable diagnostic information, molecular and antigen-based tests represent important alternative diagnostic modalities, particularly in resource-limited settings or when endoscopy is contraindicated. Sometimes, amebic trophozoites are not easily recognized on endoscopic mucosal biopsies. In 12 cases, there were 2 cases where the pathologist initially failed to observe the amebic trophozoites. When the endoscopist advised the pathologist to pay particular attention to the presence of amebic trophozoites, the pathologist subsequently identified them. Possible reasons for the analysis are as follows: (1) Sampling limitations: Biopsies may not have been taken from the ulcer edge, where trophozoites are most densely distributed. (2) Number of sections examined: Initial examination may have involved limited sectioning, while re-examination included additional levels, increasing detection sensitivity. (3) Pathologist awareness: In non-endemic regions, pathologists may have lower vigilance for amoebic trophozoites morphologically. Re-examination after clinical suggestion may improve recognition. Consequently, when an endoscopist strongly suspects amebic infection, they should remind or communicate with the pathologist. Furthermore, analogous to that of peptic ulcers, biopsy of ulcer edges improves pathogen detection rates, as the ulcer center primarily contains necrotic tissue where amebic trophozoites are rarely identifiable. The primary value of endoscopy lies in detecting mucosal lesions suggestive of amebic infection (such as aphthous ulcers or serpiginous ulcers) and guiding biopsy sampling. Although trophozoites may occasionally be directly observed on ulcer surfaces, diagnosis typically relies on microscopic examination of biopsy specimens (to identify trophozoites) or molecular/antigen testing of fresh samples. Therefore, a high index of suspicion during endoscopy combined with targeted biopsies is key to improving diagnostic yield. Among the twelve patients, none presented with concurrent amebic liver abscess. Among currently available anti-amebic medications, metronidazole (Mtz) represents the first-line therapeutic agent for the treatment of amebiasis, demonstrating superior clinical efficacy. However, in vitro tests have shown that trophozoites are resistant to metronidazole. In light of these challenges, scientific investigators have strategically developed novel therapeutic targets, encompassing both metabolic pathways and cellular components, to address the emerging limitations in current treatment modalities [18]. These research endeavors are expected to facilitate the development of innovative antiamebic therapeutics characterized by enhanced resistance profiles and improved safety parameters. The administration of oral metronidazole to these 9 patients resulted in favorable clinical outcomes, suggesting the absence of metronidazole resistance in the current treatment context. Possible reasons for the analysis are as follows: (1) In vivo vs. in vitro differences: The in vitro testing environment cannot fully replicate the complex intestinal microenvironment (e.g., hypoxia, commensal flora). (2) Drug metabolism: Metronidazole is metabolized into active compounds in vivo, and its mechanisms may include immunomodulatory effects beyond direct amoebicidal activity. (3) Definition of resistance: The breakpoints for in vitro resistance may not fully correlate with clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

This study documented an increase in the number of diagnosed cases of amoebic colitis at this institution, highlighting the need for enhanced clinical vigilance and targeted testing among high-risk groups, such as MSM. However, in the absence of population-based incidence data, it cannot be determined whether amoebic colitis is generally prevalent in Beijing. Diagnosis requires a high index of clinical suspicion, especially in young men and MSM residing in nonendemic areas of Beijing. Accurate identification of Entamoeba histolytica trophozoites via an integrated approach—incorporating clinical assessment, endoscopic examination, and histopathological evaluation—is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and to facilitate prompt and appropriate treatment. The generalizability of these findings is substantially limited by the small sample size and the single-center, retrospective nature of this study and incomplete follow-up. Further multi-center studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these observations.
