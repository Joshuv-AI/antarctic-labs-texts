Accuracy of human papillomavirus testing in detecting persistent or recurrent disease at follow‐up after conization
Authors: Ghina Alaref, Lone Kjeld Petersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCurrently, the Test of Cure after conization consists of two elements, namely human papillomavirus (HPV) testing and cytology. Given the high sensitivity of HPV testing and its modest resource requirements, we will explore its potential as a stand‐alone test for identifying persistent/recurrent cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) during follow‐up after conization, including women at increased risk, such as those with involved resection margins.Material and MethodsThis retrospective study examined 473 women who underwent conization in Funen, Denmark, between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2024. During the post‐conization follow‐up period, data were manually extracted from patients' medical records and PatoBank.ResultsAt the follow‐up, cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 1 or worse (CIN1+) persistent/recurrence was experienced by 55 women (11.6%) (median age of 42 years (range 22–87 years)). Among those with involved margins, 30.3% (40/132) experienced persistent/recurrent CIN1+ compared to 3.6% (7/193) with free margins; and 66.4% (314/473) tested negative for high‐risk HPV after conization, and only four of them developed persistent/recurrent CIN1+. HPV testing alone predicted the risk of CIN1+ persistence/recurrence with 93% sensitivity and 98.7% negative predictive value (NPV). Combining negative HPV test results with free resection margins yielded a sensitivity and NPV of 100%. Co‐testing combined with free margins also provided a sensitivity and NPV of 100%.ConclusionsUsing HPV testing as a stand‐alone test after conization demonstrates an NPV nearly equal to that of the Test of Cure. Adding free margins to HPV testing further enhanced sensitivity and NPV for detecting CIN1+ persistence or relapse during follow‐up. Our results indicate that HPV testing alone can be used as post‐conization follow‐up for women with negative margins.

INTRODUCTION

Human papillomavirus (HPV) causes dysplasia, and patients with persistent dysplasia due to HPV undergo conization as treatment. 1 Women who have been treated for cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) remain at a higher risk for the persistence or recurrence of CIN. 2 In Denmark, HPV testing is part of the gold standard for determining the risk of persistent or recurrent CIN after conization. Present‐day follow‐up protocols after conization include the Test of Cure, which combines cytology and HPV testing. 1 This co‐testing remains the recommended standard test in the follow‐up period after conization because it improves the detection of CIN recurrence or persistence. 1 Furthermore, surgical margin status is acknowledged as a risk factor for predicting CIN recurrence. 3 However, despite its prognostic value, margin status has not been directly integrated into the Test‐of‐Cure framework, which instead emphasizes the number of follow‐up tests required before returning women to the routine screening program.

Previous studies have evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of post‐treatment HPV testing. A systematic review and meta‐analysis by Arbyn et al. 3 revealed that positive surgical margins were associated with an increased risk of residual or recurrent cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 2 (CIN2+). However, post‐treatment high‐risk HPV testing was a more accurate predictor of treatment failure than margin status alone. These findings strongly support using HPV testing as the primary follow‐up tool after excisional treatment.

Several studies have evaluated the diagnostic performance of HPV testing as a stand‐alone test during the follow‐up period after conization. These studies demonstrated that HPV testing alone is adequate to identify women at risk of developing CIN. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Nevertheless, more population‐based studies are needed to confirm the performance of HPV testing as a stand‐alone follow‐up test in different clinical settings within different screening programs, as well as its potential integration into national follow‐up strategies.

Additionally, the potential benefits of reducing the use of current tests, such as enhanced efficiency in healthcare and decreased discomfort for patients in the future, have not been fully explored.

The overall objective of this historical cohort study is to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of HPV testing alone for the detection of recurrent or persistent CIN during the follow‐up period after conization, compared with the standard co‐testing approach.

Specifically, the study aims to: Determine the negative predictive value (NPV) of HPV testing alone compared to co‐testing with both HPV and cytology.Assess the impact of margin status, free versus involved, on the risk of CIN recurrence/persistence during follow‐up post‐conization.Determine the risk of CIN development in HPV‐positive and cytology‐negative patients after conization.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Setting

In Denmark, cervical cancer screenings, diagnostic evaluations, and treatments are provided free of charge through the public healthcare system. After conization, women have one or two follow‐up exams, depending on the resection margin status, as well as the results of the histological assessment and cytology/HPV test results. All relevant data are recorded in the National Pathology Database (PatoBanken). Women are routinely monitored for one year after conization, with either one or two follow‐up visits, depending on the results of the initial visit and the margin status of the conization procedure. During the follow‐up period, HPV and cytology tests are performed six months after conization. If either test results in an abnormal finding, a second follow‐up visit is scheduled six months later, at which time both tests are repeated. This second visit also applies to women with involved margins at baseline conization. Women are returned to the routine screening program if their cervical cytology results are normal, and no high‐risk HPV is detected. 1

Study design and participants

For this historical study, we extracted data on patients who have undergone cervical conization, using the KLDC03 code for conization according to the NOMESCO classification of Surgical Procedures. 8 Data were collected for the period from January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2024. This included a total of 632 women. Patients were identified by their unique personal identification numbers (CPR numbers), which are used in all health registries. The study population was identified as having undergone conization at Odense University Hospital‐Svendborg Hospital (OUH). Data from their medical records and information from PatoBanken were extracted. This included treatments and diagnoses received outside the hospital, such as from private clinics or other regional hospitals.

We excluded the women who did not complete the first follow‐up visit. This includes women who went directly to either another conization or hysterectomy as part of their treatment plan. We also excluded women with cancer at baseline conization histology because they are subject to different treatment and follow‐up guidelines. Therefore, a total of 473 patients were included in the analysis (Figure 1).

Flow chart illustrating the patient inclusion process in the follow‐up analysis and the exclusion criteria.

Relevant data included demographics and age at the time of conization as well as baseline histology results from the conization, one‐year follow‐up results of HPV testing and cytology, and duration of follow‐up. In addition, information on whether women have a persistent HPV infection or CIN, as well as any relapse and the related histological type, was collected. A persistent HPV infection after treatment was defined as a positive HPV result at the first follow‐up appointment, which occurs six months post‐treatment. Moreover, the medical record data included information on the margin status of the baseline conization. Specifying which, if any, margins were involved. The data also contained histological findings from any subsequent conization or hysterectomy procedures.

HPV results were reported as either negative or positive for high‐risk HPV (HPV 16, HPV 18, or another type of high‐risk HPV). Data on HPV subtype ahead of conization were not consistently available in the patients. Cytological results from the smear test were reported according to the 2001 Bethesda System. 9 Concerning the patient group in this study, cervical cytologic changes were defined as follows: atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASCUS), atypical squamous cells (ASCH), low‐grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL), high‐grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL), and atypical glandular cells (AGC). At the time of baseline conization, some women presented with both CIN and adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS) lesions. In cases involving multiple concurrent lesion types, classification was based on the most severe histological finding. Women were categorized as having AIS unless invasive cancer was present. For data analysis purposes, the worst diagnosis was always used. If AIS was involved, lesions were classified as such.

Persistence/recurrent disease was confirmed by the histological results, whether from cervical biopsies, from the result of another conization procedure, or a hysterectomy, if applicable. For the purposes of this study, CIN grade 1 or worse (CIN1+) includes CIN1, CIN2, CIN3, CIN, and AIS. Although CIN2+ is the established clinical threshold for treatment, recurrent CIN1 lesions were included due to their relevance in follow‐up care. The presence of a persistent or recurrent CIN1 lesion indicates persistent HPV‐related dysplasia, meaning patients cannot be discharged from surveillance. Since CIN1 may be an early precursor to CIN2 lesions, evaluating CIN1+ as the primary outcome provides a more comprehensive evaluation of recurrent dysplasia. To ensure comparability with standard clinical endpoints, persistent/recurrent CIN2+ was also included.

During the data collection process, double entry was used to ensure correct patient information and to avoid typographical errors. The data were handled pseudonymized.

Data analysis

All women in this study were observed from the day of their first conization until the final recorded HPV test or cytology result in their medical records. The accuracy of HPV testing as a stand‐alone test for identifying persistence/recurrence of CIN1+ at the first follow‐up visit was assessed by calculating the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and NPV. Additionally, HPV status was analyzed together with free margin status. At the time of conization, cases with normal histopathology were considered to have negative margins. For women with no dysplasia present, the margins were considered free of disease. A positive margin was defined as the presence of CIN of any grade at the resection margin. The results were then compared to the calculated sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of the co‐test, which includes both HPV testing and cytology. Given that margins have previously played a significant role in the recurrence of the disease, we examined data on margin status in combination with the co‐test as well. The risk of persistency/recurrence in women who were HPV positive but cytology negative was analyzed separately.

Chi‐squared and Fisher's exact tests were employed to illustrate the differences in proportions. Furthermore, to demonstrate the association between age and relapse, a t‐test was conducted on these two variables. Statistical analysis was performed using StataNow 18.

RESULTS

A total of 473 women were available for final analysis, which constituted 74.8% of the total sample. The median age was 42 years (range 22–87 years), and participants were followed for an average of 14.6 months after conization. A total of 55 women had relapse or persistent disease (11.63%) during follow‐up. When CIN2+ was used as a secondary measure, 10.15% (48/473) of women experienced persistent/relapse disease. Reporting both CIN1+ and CIN2+ persistent/recurrence allows us to identify all types of recurrent dysplasia during the follow‐up period. The distribution of women who underwent cervical conization and their results of HPV testing and cytology results during the follow‐up period is shown in Figure 2. Persistent or recurrent disease was correlated with HPV positivity and margin status. 13 of the 159 women (8.2%) with a positive high‐risk HPV result who presented with a negative cytology result at their first follow‐up visit, as well as involved surgical resection margins, experienced CIN1+ persistence/recurrence. Conversely, three women (0.6%) with positive HPV test results and negative cytology, as well as free surgical margins, experienced CIN1+ persistence/relapse. A subgroup analysis was performed using CIN2+ persistence/recurrence as a secondary outcome. Of the 159 women who were HPV‐positive at follow‐up (33.6%), 60 had positive cytology (12.7%), while 99 had normal cytology (20.9%). Among the HPV‐positive women with normal cytology, CIN2+ was identified in one case with free margins and 12 cases with involved margins. Among the HPV‐negative group (66.4%), only two cases of CIN2+ persistence/recurrence were observed, both of which occurred in women with positive cytology and involved margins. HPV‐negative status was strongly associated with an absence of CIN2+ recurrence, even when margins were involved. These findings highlight the strong predictive value of follow‐up HPV testing in identifying women at risk for persistent/recurrent disease.

Surgical margins were free in 193 cases (40.8%). Of these, 186 cases (96.4%) showed no persistence/relapses, while seven cases (3.6%) showed CIN1+ persistence/relapses. Among women with involved margins, 132 women (27.9%) had one or more involved margins, and 40 women (30.3%) experienced a relapse. In women, 69 (52.3%) with involvement of the vaginal margins, we found that 19 (27.5%) had a relapse. Furthermore, 53 women (40.2%) had involved endocervical margins, of whom 23 (43.4%) had a relapse. Additionally, 32 women had margins that could not be evaluated, and five of them (15.6%) experienced a relapse.

Predicting the risk of CIN1+ persistence/recurrence using HPV testing alone resulted in a sensitivity of 93%, PPV of 33.3%, and NPV of 98.7%. Adding free margins to HPV testing substantially improved diagnostic accuracy, providing a sensitivity of 100%, a PPV of 17.1%, and an NPV of 100% as a predictor of recurrent dysplasia. The combined cytology and HPV test with free margins status had given a sensitivity of 100%, PPV of 15.2%, and NPV of 100% (Table 2). Although there was no significant difference in persistence or recurrence risk across baseline histological categories, women with CIN3 or AIS at the time of conization had substantially higher risk estimates, with risk ratios of 3.95 and 4.7, respectively. This was compared to the risk ratios of 0.0, 3.95, and 2.7 for CIN1, CIN2, and CIN, respectively, when women with normal results were used as the reference group. The risk ratio for women with involved margins was significantly higher at 4.3 compared to those with free margins (p = 0.000). Twenty‐five percent of the women in the study underwent conization based on a medical assessment despite having CIN1 or normal histology. The procedure was performed for diagnostic or therapeutic reasons, including persistent bleeding, an unidentifiable transformation zone, and inconsistent biopsy results (Table 1).

A total of persistence/recurrences and their histopathological diagnoses were summarized according to baseline histology. The majority of CIN1+ persistence/recurrences occurred among women with CIN3 at baseline, including CIN1 (five cases), CIN2 (three cases), CIN3 (28 cases), and CIN (two cases). Women with CIN2 at baseline showed only a small number of persistence/recurrences (CIN1: 4, CIN2: 1, CIN3: 2). Among those with normal or CIN1 histology at baseline, only a few cases of low‐grade persistence or recurrence cases were observed (CIN1: 2; CIN2: 2; CIN3: 1; CIN: 1; AIS: 1). Women with AIS at baseline experienced one CIN3 and two AIS persistence/recurrences. Overall, the distribution of recurrence types varied substantially by baseline histology. High‐grade baseline lesions (CIN3 and AIS) contributed to most clinically significant recurrences.

As a part of the treatment plan for some women, 73 women (11.6%) underwent a second conization. Of those women, 39 had CIN1+ at the second conization histology. A total of 25 of the 73 women did not complete the first follow‐up test and were excluded from the main analysis. Forty‐six women completed the follow‐up test after the second conization and did not have cancer at the time of the second conization. We examined these 46 women, accounting for 63% of the total group. Among them, four women (8.7%) developed persistence/relapse with CIN1+. All of these had a positive HPV test after reconization, while three had positive cytology.

Of the total population, 100 women (21.1%) tested positive for high‐risk HPV combined with normal cytology results at the first follow‐up visit. These women were evaluated separately based on their risk of developing cytological abnormalities over time. Final cytology results were available for 98 of the 100 women. Twelve of these women (12.2%) developed ASCUS or worse (ASCUS+) at their most recent follow‐up. Nevertheless, the majority (N = 86, 87.8%) remained cytologically normal, indicating that cytological progression, though possible, is relatively uncommon in this subgroup over a median follow‐up period of 16.4 months.

Furthermore, 100 women (21.1%) with normal histological findings at baseline conization were examined to assess the risk of developing recurrence. Histological examination in those without dysplasia in their conization revealed normal findings, infection, papilloma, and leiomyoma cases. Our data showed that 95 of the women (95%) did not have persistent/recurrent CIN1+ after their conization. Four women (4%) had developed persistent/recurrent CIN1+, and one woman (1%) had cancer. Three of the women with persistent/recurrent CIN1+ had both positive high‐risk HPV and positive cytology in their follow‐up tests. The fourth woman had positive high‐risk HPV and negative cytology. To investigate an association between women's age and persistence/recurrence of CIN1+, we performed a t‐test on these two variables. We found no statistically significant association between age and risk of persistence or relapse (p = 0.53) as a mean age of 44.8 years (95% confidence interval (CI) = 43.41–46.26) was found among 415 women without persistence/relapse, compared to a mean age of 43.51 years (95% CI = 39.7–47.3) among the 55 women with persistent/recurrent CIN1+.

DISCUSSION

This historical study demonstrates that the negative predictive value (NPV) of HPV testing as a stand‐alone test after conization is nearly equal to the NPV of the Test of Cure, which includes both HPV and cytology tests: 98.7% and 99.3%, respectively. Adding free margin to HPV testing and co‐testing may further enhance the sensitivity and negative predictive value for detecting CIN1+ persistence/relapses over an average follow‐up period of 14.6 months. In this scenario, both the sensitivity and NPV reach 100%. This highlights that HPV testing combined with free margins is as effective as the currently employed co‐test when combined with free margins for identifying women at low or no risk of persistence/recurrence after conization.

The findings of our study align with the prevailing understanding that the presence of HPV after treatment effectively predicts disease recurrence, as demonstrated in previous studies, including a meta‐analysis. 5 , 10 , 11 , 12 Additionally, evidence from several studies 6 , 7 indicates that HPV testing combined with free resection margins has a similar sensitivity and NPV to those of combined tests. These results support our findings, which show that HPV testing and co‐testing, combined with free resection margins, yield 100% sensitivity and an NPV. When we compared HPV testing combined with free resection margins to co‐testing alone for identifying women at risk of CIN1+ recurrence, we found that HPV testing had higher sensitivity and NPV than the Test of Cure. However, one study showed that combining the HPV test and margin status did not improve relapse prediction. 13

The current literature, including a systematic review and meta‐analysis, 3 , 12 , 14 , 15 , 16 indicates that resection margins play a crucial role in predicting persistence/recurrence after conization. The involvement of resection margins is a significant risk factor for persistence/recurrence of CIN1+. Our data show that women with involved resection margins have a statistically significant increased risk ratio of CIN1+ persistence/recurrence within 14.6 months (p < 0.000). Additionally, the association between a specific resection margin and a heightened risk of persistence or recurrence could be explored. Our results showed that 43% of women with involved endocervical margins (n = 53) experienced CIN1+ persistence/recurrence during the follow‐up period. Results from a 16‐year follow‐up study in Sweden 15 demonstrated that an involved endocervical resection margin has a hazard ratio of 2.72 for CIN recurrence. These results indicate that involvement of the endocervical margin poses the greatest risk, underscoring the importance of closely monitoring patients with this condition.

In our study, age was not a statistically significant factor in developing recurrence after conization (p > 0.05). This finding has also been demonstrated in other studies. 17 One long‐term study conducted in Spain has demonstrated that individuals over the age of 35 exhibit an elevated risk of disease recurrence. 18 However, we acknowledge that this finding from our study could be because the analyses were not stratified by age and margin status due to the small size of the subgroups. This could be considered a limitation.

Women who are positive for high‐risk HPV and have normal cytology at the first follow‐up visit after conization have a 12% risk of developing ASCUS+ within an average follow‐up time of 16.4 months. This suggests that women with positive high‐risk HPV results are more likely to experience recurrent cervical dysplasia. In line with our results, a historical Korean study reported that a positive HPV test result between three and six months after conization was a significant risk factor for recurrent cervical cytology dysplasia. 19

Our retrospective data revealed that 95% of patients with normal histology (N = 100) at the initial follow‐up did not experience persistence or recurrence of CIN1+ within the subsequent follow‐up period. This suggests that this patient group has a very low risk of recurrence. However, despite this low risk in these patients, current national guidelines (published in 2019) recommend the Test‐of‐Cure as the post‐treatment follow‐up plan for these women as well. 1

One of the study's strengths is that data for all 473 participants were extracted directly from their medical records. This ensured high data accuracy and enhanced the validity of the findings. Additionally, the passive follow‐up through patients' medical records and the nationwide pathology database (PatoBank) is regarded as a strength of the study. Utilizing PatoBank data on histological, cytological, and HPV testing, which has been systematically collected and reported with nearly 100% coverage, 20 can reduce information bias in this study. The risk of recurrence in patients undergoing treatment for CIN is elevated for at least ten years, and the short follow‐up period in our study may contribute to the low recurrence/persistence rate observed among patients. The mean follow‐up duration of approximately 14 months was determined by the availability of data from the hospital's administrative system over the past five years. This limited follow‐up period may reduce the ability to detect late persistence/recurrences and should therefore be acknowledged as a limitation of the study.

Although a longer follow‐up period could be obtained through national registries, the present period reflects real‐world clinical practice and aligns with Denmark's standard Test‐of‐Cure protocol. This period was considered sufficient for evaluating early recurrence or persistence, which is defined as occurring within the first year after conization. However, longer follow‐up studies would provide additional insight into late recurrence and confirm the long‐term accuracy of using post‐treatment HPV testing to predict outcomes.

This study has some notable limitations that should be acknowledged. First, 147 women (23.3%) were excluded from the analyses due to their absence at the first follow‐up visit or missing HPV and cytology results. These cases were classified as missing data, which, in some instances, may be explained by women proceeding directly to subsequent treatment, such as repeat conization or hysterectomy. These exclusions may introduce selection bias, which could lead to an over‐ or underestimation of sensitivity and NPV. However, missing data also reflect the realities of clinical practice. Additionally, seven of the 473 women in the total study population had positive HPV test results despite having free resection margins and later experienced relapse. Including these cases could introduce bias, affect the robustness of the findings, and lower the NPV of HPV testing when combined with margin status.

Previous studies, 21 , 22 including one on the Danish population, demonstrated good concordance between self‐collected and practitioner‐collected HPV samples for HPV detection (Cohen's kappa = 0.70). A study of the Danish population 21 revealed that when using the practitioner‐collected samples as the reference standard, the sensitivity and specificity of HPV detection were 80.9% and 91.6%, respectively, for self‐collected samples. Similar findings were observed in an observational study in the Netherlands, 23 which revealed that HPV self‐tests are nearly as precise as tests performed by doctors, with relative sensitivities and specificities of 94% and 102%, respectively. These results support the use of HPV self‐sampling as an alternative in screening programs. Testing the diagnostic accuracy of HPV self‐sampling compared to healthcare‐professional provided Test‐of‐Cure in low‐risk groups of women with free margins could potentially bring us closer to an HPV self‐test for women with low risk of persistent/recurrent disease after conization.

Patients with a very low risk of recurrence, such as those with normal baseline histology, may undergo follow‐up tests at reduced intensity and could be safely returned directly to the normal screening program. This could both relieve the use of healthcare resources and reduce patient burden without compromising safety. Although our data are promising, a larger dataset is needed to determine whether other risk factors can be accounted for in these cases. The present study was designed as a retrospective, registry‐based cohort study. Due to the fact that this is a quality assurance study, we were permitted to collect data from the system over the past five years. Since all eligible cases available in the registry were included, no prior sample size or power calculation was conducted. Based on the Danish register data, it will be possible to test in a larger cohort prior to a prospective study.

CONCLUSION

Our results suggest that follow‐up with HPV testing alone is sufficient for women with negative margins following conization. Data potentially support a shift to HPV self‐testing, meaning women would not need to schedule a follow‐up appointment after conization and could instead perform an at‐home HPV test. This approach could be implemented in areas where HPV self‐testing is already introduced in screening programs. To expand on these findings and test this approach, a national data material with prolonged post‐cone observation time must be analyzed before testing the approach in a prospective design, which must include women's perspectives on self‐testing after conization.
