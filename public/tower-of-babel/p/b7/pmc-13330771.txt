Comprehensive characterization and recovery of nickel, cobalt, cerium, and lanthanum from nickel–metal hydride batteries through bioleaching of spent medium by Acidithiobacillus thiooxidans
Authors: Zoha Heidarinejad, Mahdi Farzadkia, Seyyed Mohammad Mousavi, Mitra Gholami, Roshanak Rezaei Kalantary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although lithium-ion batteries receive widespread focus, managing and reducing the environmental risks posed by spent nickel–metal hydride (Ni–MH) batteries is still a significant global issue. Choosing a sustainable and eco-friendly method for these batteries is challenging due to their complexity. This study aimed to characterize Ni–MH batteries in terms of their physicochemical, structural, morphological, surface, and thermal properties, followed by the recovery of metals from these batteries through spent medium bioleaching by Acidithiobacillus thiooxidans. The results of measuring the metal contents of spent Ni–MH batteries showed that these batteries contain 464 000 mg kg−1 Ni, 30 800 mg kg−1 Co, 149 000 mg kg−1 Ce, and 45 000 mg kg−1 La, making them valuable metal reservoirs. Structural analysis of the powder revealed a crystalline structure composed of CoLaNi4, Mn2Y, Ni, and Ni(OH)2 phases, along with various functional groups. The structure and functional groups of the powder changed after the bioleaching process. The ANC test showed that the amount of 15.6 mmol H+/gr battery powder is required for the leaching of metals from the powder of these batteries. The highest recovery of Co and Ni was achieved within 2 h with values of 100% and 72%, respectively, and the highest concentrations of Ce and La in the bioleached solution were 72 ppm and 325 ppm, respectively. In fact, achieving complete and comprehensive information about Ni–MH batteries can help select the most efficient recycling method with the least environmental impact for these wastes, and bioleaching is an effective method for recovering metals from these batteries.

Introduction

Nickel–metal hydride (Ni–MH) rechargeable batteries are used due to their relatively low cost, high energy density, lower environmental impact, and resistance to overcharge.1 These batteries are widely used in hybrid electric vehicles (HEVs)2 and various consumer electronic devices such as cell phones, notebook computers, and video recorders.3–5 It is estimated that around 500 000 hybrid electric vehicles will be in use worldwide in 2021, consuming around 3.146 × 106 kW h Ni–MH batteries.6,7 With a growth rate of 50% for hybrid electric vehicles in Europe between 2018 and 2019, the annual Ni–MH battery waste volume will reach 33 600 tons in 2030.5,8

Whereas the disposal of these batteries in landfills leads to environmental pollution, including groundwater9 and soil and the disposal of these batteries in incinerators leads to air pollution. On the other hand, these batteries contain many valuable and vital metals, so recycling these metals can have a high economic value.1,2,10 However, approximately less than 6% of these batteries are recycled. The rest ends up in landfill sites or is processed informally by the local population in developing countries.11

A Ni–MH battery is composed of four main components, including a positive nickel electrode, a negative metal hydride electrode, an alkaline electrolyte (potassium hydroxide or sodium hydroxide), and a paper separator.11–13 So these batteries are a source of different metals such as cobalt (Co), nickel (Ni), zinc (Zn), iron (Fe), manganese (Mn), and various rare earth elements (REEs) such as cerium (Ce), neodymium (Nd), lanthanum (La), samarium (Sm), and praseodymium (Pr). The concentration of metals in these spent batteries varies depending on their application and cell type, for example, smaller portable spent batteries contain 36–42 wt% Ni, 3–4 wt% Co, and 8–10 wt% REEs.8 Given that these spent batteries are an important source of heavy metal and REEs reserves.3 Therefore, as one of the urban resources, REEs and precious metals are considered.3,14 Considering that these batteries contain a significant amount of Ni and REEs, their economic impact is significant in many chemical industries such as metal catalysts and permanent magnets. Even more of these metals can be used to make new Ni–MH batteries.2

On the other hand, the toxic effects of certain elements and metals found in spent Ni–MH batteries include the following: REEs can lead to respiratory sensitivities, dermatitis, allergic reactions, and lymphocyte proliferation, and are also carcinogenic.15 Nickel is carcinogenic and can lead to hematogenous contact eczema, allergy, and systemic allergy syndrome.16 The effect of Co on human health is characterized by a complex clinical syndrome with a set of neurological, endocrine, and cardiovascular deficits.17

Therefore, it is necessary to develop an efficient recycling plan to recover the valuable elements and safely dispose of the harmful parts of these batteries.18,19

Furthermore, no proper pretreatment has been performed on spent Ni–MH batteries under different environmental conditions, which is primarily due to the insufficient characteristics of these batteries. As a result, a comprehensive roadmap for the recycling and management of these batteries has yet to be established. Therefore, further research in this area is highly recommended.

Given that, these batteries contain a large amount of valuable and vital metals, which recovery and recycling are very important and effective.20 Various techniques of metal recycling in these spent batteries include pyrometallurgy, hydrometallurgy, and biohydrometallurgy (bioleaching).5 The most significant advantages and disadvantages of various metal recovery methods are listed in Table 1. Although pyrometallurgical and hydrometallurgical methods are widely used for the recovery of valuable metals from spent Ni–MH batteries, these methods are often associated with numerous environmental and economic limitations.21 Pyrometallurgical techniques typically require high operating temperatures and significant energy consumption, which can lead to greenhouse gas emissions and the loss of aluminum (Al) and lithium (Li) metals.22 Hydrometallurgical processes, despite offering relatively high metal recovery efficiencies, typically rely on the use of concentrated acids, oxidizing agents, and complex chemical treatments, which lead to the generation of toxic secondary effluents and increased operational costs.23 In contrast, bioleaching has emerged as a sustainable and environmentally friendly alternative for the recovery of metals from secondary sources.24,25 This process utilizes microorganisms, such as Acidithiobacillus species—which are particularly effective due to their ability to thrive in acidic environments—to produce acidic metabolites and leaching agents under mild operating conditions.26,27 Furthermore, bioleaching has a lower environmental impact and shows better compatibility for processing complex and low-grade solid wastes.28 Despite the slower kinetic performance of bioleaching relative to standard chemical processes, modern optimization strategies (e.g., thermal pretreatment, ultrasonication, and microbial adaptation) have been shown to markedly improve metal dissolution efficiency.29,30 Consequently, bioleaching represents a promising approach for the extraction of Ni, Co, and REEs (Ce and La) from waste Ni–MH batteries.

Despite the growing focus on lithium-ion batteries, Ni–MH batteries remain a critical but often overlooked source of valuable metals. Establishing an efficient recovery process necessitates a comprehensive characterization of these spent materials to understand their chemical stability and the complexities of their waste matrix. Considering that the background of the studies shows that no study has been done on the comprehensive characterization of these batteries and then the selection of the bioleaching method using the Acidithiobacillus thiooxidans bacteria to recover metals from these batteries simultaneously, there is a research gap in this field.

To address this gap, the present study was conducted to conduct a comprehensive characterization of spent Ni–MH batteries along with the recovery of valuable metals from these batteries by bioleaching method according to the following: (i) comprehensive characterization of spent Ni–MH batteries by performing CHNS/O tests, initial pH determination, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), XRD, XRF, FTIR, Fe-SEM, EDX-mapping, particle size distribution (PSA), acid neutralization capacity (ANC) test. (ii) Using the bioleaching method to recover Ni, Co, Ce, and La from spent Ni–MH batteries using Acidithiobacillus thiooxidans bacteria at different times. (iii) Evaluation of the overall economic value of metals in spent Ni–MH batteries.

Materials and methods

Preparation of spent Ni–MH batteries powder

Nickel–metal hydride batteries have wide applications in various electrical and electronic devices and hybrid electric vehicles (Fig. S1).

The spent Ni–MH cylindrical batteries of various brands in the AA and AAA sizes were gathered from electronic repair shops situated within Tehran province, Iran (Fig. S2). The preparation steps of the batteries were as follows: (1) to avoid self-ignition and short-circuiting during disassembly, the batteries were completely discharged first.31 (2) The spent Ni–MH batteries were manually disassembled under the laboratory hood into different parts of the anode, cathode, paper separators, and cylindrical cases. Then, the separated parts were weighed and dried at 60 °C for 12 h to remove moisture and electrolyte from the battery powder. The overall flowsheet of the spent Ni–MH battery powder preparation procedure is shown in Fig. 1. (3) The cathode and anode powder were crushed by a home blender (Moulinex, AR1044, 180 W) to obtain a fine powder. (4) Subsequently, the final powder was subjected to a #200 mesh sieving process, with a particle size of less than 75 microns, in order to obtain a homogeneous mixture. At the end, the obtained powder was used for characterization experiments.25

Flowsheet of the spent Ni–MH battery powder preparation procedure.

Elemental analysis of spent Ni–MH battery powder

Chemical digestion and elemental content analysis

The concentration of metals in the spent Ni–MH battery powder was determined following chemical digestion, utilizing an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES). In chemical digestion, 0.25 gr of the spent Ni–MH battery powder was placed in a platinum crucible containing a mixture of sodium potassium carbonate and boric acid, then placed in a furnace at a temperature of 950 °C. In the next step, the crucible content was cooled and then dissolved in HCL at a ratio of 1 : 1. In the next step, 5 ml of a mixture of nitric acid and hydrochloric acid (in an optimal ratio of 1 : 3) was added to the contents of the crucible and heated for 30 minutes.32 Finally, after cooling the solution, its contents were filtered and the amount of metals was measured using ICP-OES (Spectro Arcos, Spectro Analytical Instruments, Kleve, Germany). Additionally, X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy (XRF; PW1410, Philips, Netherlands) was employed to ascertain the quantity of metals present in the battery powder sample and to substantiate the precision of the metal concentration reported by ICP-OES analysis.

CHNS/O analysis

The weight fractions of the elements carbon (C), hydrogen (H), nitrogen (N), sulfur (S), and oxygen (O) elements in spent Ni–MH battery powder were measured using a CHNS/O Elemental Analyzer (ECS 4010, NC technologies (Costech), Italy) in several steps as mentioned in the supplementary material.

Characterization of spent Ni–MH batteries using various analyses

Determination of initial pH

In order to ascertain the acidic, neutral, or alkaline nature of the spent Ni–MH battery powder, 1 gr of the battery powder sample was added to 50 ml of distilled water. The solution was then placed in a shaker incubator at 140 rpm and a temperature of 30 °C for 24 h.33 The initial pH of the sample was measured using a digital multimeter (Consort C1010, Belgium) with an Ag/AgCl electrode.

Thermogravimetric analysis

The alterations in the mass of the spent Ni–MH battery powder in response to temperature fluctuations over time were examined using thermal gravimetric analysis and differential scanning calorimetry (TGA-DSC; Q600, TA, America). In this experiment, 5.4100 mg of Ni–MH used batteries powder sample was heated in the temperature range of 25 °C to 1000 °C in an air atmosphere with a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 and an airflow of 100 ml min−1.

X-ray diffraction spectrometry

To identify the phase constitutions of the spent Ni–MH battery powder before and after the bioleaching process and analyze its crystal structure from X-ray diffraction spectroscopy (XRD, PW1730, Philips, Netherland) equipped with a source of Cu-Kα radiation with the current of 30 mA, and a voltage of 40 kV, with a step size of 0.05 from 10° to 80°, was used on the sample.

FTIR analysis

To determine the functional groups, and bonds in the battery powder before and after the bioleaching process, Fourier transform spectroscopic analysis (FTIR: AVATAR, Thermo, USA) was used in the wavelength range of 400 to 4000 cm−1 with a precision of 2 cm−1 at room temperature.

Surface morphology and EDX analysis

A field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; MIRA3, TESCAN-XMU, Czech Republic) equipped with a back-scatter detector was used to analyze the surface morphology of the spent Ni–MH battery powder before and after the bioleaching process. This microscope uses secondary electrons and back-scattered electrons to show the texture and morphology of the spent Ni–MH battery powder sample.33 For this purpose, the sample of spent Ni–MH battery powder was placed on an aluminum piece and then covered with a thin layer of gold. Additionally, a mapping analysis was conducted to ascertain the elemental distribution of the sample via an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy system (EDX: XFlash 6/10, BRUKER, Germany).

Particle size distribution

To better understand the physicochemical properties and analyze the particle size distribution of the spent Ni–MH battery powder, a particle size analyzer (PSA: Malvern Mastersizer 2000 laser particle size analysis, Malvern, UK) was used. In this test, a list of particle size fractions is defined with a mathematical function, and the volume percentage of particles in each interval is determined.34

Acid neutralization capacity test

This test is a leaching method for solid waste that shows information about the buffering capacity and the hydration progression of the spent Ni–MH battery powder against acid or chemical attack. Given that a decrease in pH typically results in increased solubility of most metals, a higher ANC value suggests that the waste exhibits enhanced resistance to leaching under acidic conditions. Furthermore, it provides confirmation of the chemical stabilization of heavy metals.33,35

The basis of this test is to measure the amount of nitric acid needed to change the pH value of the spent Ni–MH battery powder sample to the required level. In this test, various amounts of 1 M HNO3 were added to flasks containing 1 gr of the spent Ni–MH battery powder and 100 ml of distilled water. Subsequently, the solution was agitated for a period of 24 h until the pH value was fixed. Finally, the pH value of the samples was measured and the titration curve was drawn based on the final pH and the amount of millimoles of H+ per gram of the spent Ni–MH battery powder.36

Bioleaching of spent Ni–MH batteries

Bacterial strains used and growth conditions

Acidithiobacillus thiooxidans was selected as a chemolithotrophic, sulfur-oxidizing, and acidophilic bacterium to evaluate its ability to leach base metals and REEs from spent Ni–MH batteries.37 This bacterium uses elemental sulfur as an energy source and produces sulfuric acid, which leads to the dissolution of metals from the waste. This bacterium has the ability to grow in strongly acidic environments of pH 1 or less.27 Fig. S3 illustrates the small and motile cells of A. thiooxidans, with sizes ranging from approximately 0.5 to 1 µm.

The mesophilic bacterial strain Acidithiobacillus thiooxidans (PTCC 1717) was purchased from the Iranian Research Organization for Scientific and Technology in Tehran. The bacteria culture medium consists of MgSO4·7H2O (0.5 g L−1), (NH4)2SO4 (2 g L−1), KCl (0.5 g L−1), sulfur (5 g L−1), and K2HPO4·3H2O (0.25 g L−1) in 100 ml of distilled water. The initial pH of the bacterial culture medium was adjusted to about 3.5 using 98% H2SO4. For bacterial growth, the culture medium was placed at 30 °C and 140 rpm in a shaker incubator (Pars Azma, England). Given that the low pH was suitable for the growth of A. thiooxidants, there was no need to sterilize the culture medium.

Spent medium bioleaching experiment

Bioleaching experiments were conducted using the spent medium approach. In the bioleaching of the spent medium, first, bacteria were inoculated with 10% inoculation containing 107 cells in 100 ml of culture medium and placed in a shaker incubator at a temperature of 30 °C and 140 rpm. After the bacteria reached the logarithmic growth phase in which the cell density reached its maximum, the bacterial cells were separated from the medium and the inoculation liquid was used to perform experiments. Experiments were performed at a pulp density of 30 gr L−1 of Ni–MH battery powder in 100 ml of inoculation liquid and at times of 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 24, 48, and 72 h. After the bioleaching experiments, the obtained liquid was filtered and the concentration of recovered metals was determined at different times by ICP-OES. Chemical leaching experiment

Chemical leaching of Ni, Co, Ce, and La from Ni–MH battery powder using commercial H2SO4 at the same concentration as bioleaching experiments was performed to determine the leaching efficiency by bio-acids produced by A. thiooxidans bacteria. Chemical leaching experiments were performed at pulp densities of 10, 20, 30, and 50 g L−1 and 100 ml of culture medium without bacterial inoculation. The pH of the culture medium was adjusted to 1 with concentrated H2SO4, similar to that of the spent medium. The spent medium was also performed at pulp densities of 10, 20, 30, and 50 g L−1 and in 100 ml flasks. All chemical leaching experiments and bioleaching of the spent medium were performed in a shaker incubator at 30 °C and 140 rpm. At the end of the experiments, the samples were passed through a 0.22 syringe filter and the concentration of leached liquor metals was determined by ICP-OES.

Results and discussion

Structure spent Ni–MH batteries

A Ni–MH battery comprises three principal components: a positive electrode (cathode), a negative electrode (anode), and a KOH electrolyte contained in a gas-permeable polymer separator. These components are housed within a steel-based case.13 These batteries are divided into three categories, cylindrical, button, and prismatic/rectangular based on their geometrical configuration.38 Common alloys in the electrode designs of these batteries include the following: A2B7 (including elements LaCePrNdNiCoMnAl plus Mg), AB5 (containing elements LaCePrNd-NiCoMnAl), AB2 (including metals VTiZrNiCrCoMnAlSn), where “AxBy” refers to A type metals (LaCePrNd or TiZr) to that of the B type metals (VNiCrCoMnAlSn).39 The separators in these batteries are composed of porous polymer films or non-woven fabrics.40

The material/mass balance flow diagram of the battery powder production process is shown in Fig. 2, which provides a comprehensive overview of the spent Ni–MH battery powder preparation process. The average weight of size AA batteries was 21.86 gr and size AAA batteries were 10.23 gr.

Overall flowsheet and mass balance of the battery powder production process.

As seen in Fig. 2, spent Ni–MH batteries are composed of various components including anode, cathode, paper separator, specification label, cylindrical case, electrolytes, and other residues. The mass balance results of the cathode and anode metal powder production process of spent Ni–MH batteries showed that the spent Ni–MH batteries contained 24.14% cylindrical case, 17.10% anode, 2.72% anode metal powder, 34.07% cathode metal powder, 9.10% paper separator, 1.09% specification label, 12.75% electrolyte, and other residues. According to the studies, the electrodes comprise about 50 to 70 wt% of the batteries.18,41 According to Lin et al.'s study, different parts of batteries, including anode, cathode, paper separator, and cylindrical case of Ni–MH batteries, were included in 29.3%, 33.2%, 10%, and 20.7% weight of the batteries, respectively.42 Also, mass balance calculations for powder production showed that from 2200 gr of spent Ni–MH batteries, about 750 gr of final battery powder is produced.

The metal part of Ni–MH batteries consisting of Ni, Cu, and Zn metals can be directed to smelting factories as a secondary raw material and the paper separator part of these batteries can be used as a substrate for alternative fuel production in cement factories, steel making, and thermal power plants.43

Quantification of elemental content

The concentration of metals in the powder of spent Ni–MH batteries, as determined by ICP-OES analysis following chemical digestion, is illustrated in Table 2. According to the results, 78.47wt% of the spent Ni–MH battery powder consists of 40 elements. With 464 000 mg kg−1, Ni was the most abundant element in the spent Ni–MH battery powder. The second most abundant element in the powder is Co, with a concentration of 30 800 mg kg−1. Perämäki et al.'s study on Ni–MH batteries showed that Ni (40 wt%) and Co (7.6 wt%) are the main components of these batteries.8 Also, since the cathode of these batteries is composed of a hydrogen-absorbing alloy La0.8Nd0.2-Ni2.5Co2.4Si0.1, so Ni and Co are the dominant metals in the cathode of Ni–MH batteries.44

As can be seen, the concentration of Cd in the current study was 10 400 mg kg−1. In many studies, the reason for the presence of Cd in the content of Ni–MH batteries has been stated due to the wrong labeling of Ni–Cd batteries, which are labeled as Ni–MH batteries.10,42,45

Also, the amount of potassium (K) in the battery powder sample was 48 900 mg kg−1. The high potassium content is due to the presence of residual electrolyte in the battery powder, which has been reported in numerous studies and it indicates that the alkaline electrolyte of batteries is KOH.4,46,47

The total REEs content in the spent Ni–MH battery powder was approximately 19.67 wt%. Among REEs, Ce (at 149 000 mg kg−1) had the most abundant concentration in the spent Ni–MH battery powder, followed by La, Nd, and Pr. Spent Ni–MH battery powder is a suitable source of REEs that can be used to meet future needs.

Zielinski et al.'s research on Ni–MH cylindrical batteries showed that rare earth elements Ce, La, Pr, and Nd are the main elements of black mass powder and the total content of REEs in cylindrical batteries is 14.8–15.9 wt%.4

The battery powder contains Yttrium, which is added as an oxide to the electrolyte or electrode, and the addition of Y2O3 reduces the storage corrosion rate.48

The results of the ICP-OES analysis showed that the spent Ni–MH battery powder consists of valuable and diverse elements. Thus, it is estimated that recycling 1 ton of nickel batteries is worth about 10 933 US$.7 Also, if these metals enter the environment, irreparable damage will be caused to human health and the environment. It is therefore recommended that the appropriate method for the recovery of these metals from spent Ni–MH battery powder be selected.

As the results showed, Ni–MH batteries contain more than 70% different metals and REEs, so proper and effective recycling of these metals is not only environmentally beneficial, but also economically profitable.49

The XRF results of the powder are shown in Table 2. Although XRF cannot detect low-concentration elements, XRF analysis confirmed the results of ICP-OES analysis.33 The data obtained from this analysis shows a relative mass fraction of the elements.50 The results of this analysis also show that alkali metals such as Na, and K are present in the powder sample. Also, Ni, Co, La, Ce, Zn, and Cd elements with a higher weight percentage are the dominant metals in battery powder.

CHNS/O analysis of spent Ni–MH powder

To accurately estimate the concentration of carbon (C), hydrogen (H), nitrogen (N), oxygen (O), and sulfur (S) elements in the spent Ni–MH battery powder sample, CHNS/O analysis was performed. The data obtained from this analysis are absolute data.50 The results of the analysis demonstrated that 52.36 wt% of the spent Ni–MH battery powder is composed of carbon.

From the point of view of the recycling process of spent Ni–MH batteries, it is important to estimate the amount of carbon, especially in hydrometallurgical recycling processes that require washing the powder to recover metals. Because carbon remains undissolved and causes problems for the filtration process. Therefore, carbon separation by methods such as flotation, before the leaching process, leads to the improvement of the leaching process.4

The elemental composition of the spent Ni–MH battery powder was determined to be 0.786 wt% for H, 8.572 wt% for N, 0.085 wt% for S, and 3.961 wt% for O, respectively. Also, the results of Rasoulnia et al.'s study showed that spent Ni–MH batteries contain 29.3 wt% carbon, 4.99 wt% nitrogen, and 0.65 wt% hydrogen.51

Initial pH of spent Ni–MH batteries powder

The initial pH of the spent Ni–MH batteries powder in deionized water was 9.1 after 24 h. A pH value exceeding 7 indicates the alkaline nature of the powder, which is attributable to the presence of alkaline metals, including Li, Na, and K, in the battery powder.

Thermal analysis of the spent Ni–MH battery powder

Thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) of spent Ni–MH battery powder was performed to investigate the relationship between temperature and battery powder weight, and the result of TGA/DTG analysis is shown in Fig. S4. The weight of the battery powder gradually decreased as the temperature increased from 25 to 1000 °C. So about 22.86% of the weight of the battery powder sample was reduced, which is attributed to the burning of organic materials or the removal of moisture in the battery powder.52 The first stage of weight loss occurred up to 250 °C, which is probably related to the removal of moisture and volatile substances in the sample.33,53 Then, from 250 to 400 °C, weight loss continued at a faster rate, which resulted in approximately 13% weight loss in the sample. The reason for this weight loss is probably due to the decomposition of Ni(OH)2 into NiO.54 Between 400 and 800 °C, very little change in powder weight was observed, likely because most volatile components had already been removed, and the remaining material consisted primarily of thermally stable oxides.55 From 800 to 1000 °C, the sample weight decreased gradually (less than 3%).

Analyses of spent Ni–MH battery powder structural phases and functional groups evaluation

XRD spectrum

X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD) is a technique that provides complete information about the crystal structure and phase structure of a sample.36 The XRD pattern of spent Ni–MH batteries before and after the bioleaching process can be seen in Fig. 3.1(a and b). As illustrated in Fig. 3.1(a and b), the XRD spectrum reveals that the peaks observed in the 20° to 70° range correspond to crystalline phases with exceptionally strong, distinct peaks, accompanied by several weaker, more pronounced peaks. The presence of CoLaNi4, LaNi5, Mn2Y, Ni, and Ni (OH)2 are the main phases shown in the XRD analysis of the spent Ni–MH battery powder before the bioleaching process (Fig. 3.1(a)). After the bioleaching process, LaNi5, NiO2, and Ni were observed in the XRD analysis. The peaks of 44.6° and 51.7° 2θ degrees correspond to Ni in the sample before the bioleaching process, and the peaks of 44°, 52°, and 77° 2θ degrees correspond to the presence of Ni in the sample after the bioleaching process (Fig. 3.1(b)). In the results of Bertuol et al.'s study, a peak of Ni was observed in the cathode powder in XRD analysis.45 Sharp and specific peaks 19°, 33°, 38° and 52° 2θ indicate the presence of Ni(OH)2 in the structure of the battery powder before the bioleaching process and also indicate the dominant structure of Ni in the battery cathode, which is consistent with the study of Farzana et al. (2019), Sobianowska-Turek (2018) and Meshram et al. (2016).43,46,56 Also, in the study of Landa-Castro et al. (2020), it has been pointed out that the highest intensity of the peak is related to Ni(OH)2, which is consistent with our study.44 After the bioleaching process, the presence of NiO2 was also observed in peaks 33°, 38°, 52°, 58°, and 63° 2θ degrees (Fig. 3.1(b)).

(1) XRD pattern of spent Ni–MH battery powder (a) before and (b) after bioleaching, and (2) FTIR spectra data of spent Ni–MH battery powder (c) before and (d) after bioleaching.

Also, the peaks of 30.2°, 42.5° and 69° 2θ degrees indicate the presence of LaNi5 in the powder structure before the bioleaching process. Given that LaNi5 is a common active material used to form the negative electrode in Ni–MH batteries, the LaNi5 identified in this study is therefore attributed to the anode powder of spent Ni–MH batteries.57

This analysis partially confirms the results of the ICP-OES analysis because Ni, Co, and La are the metals observed in high concentrations in the spent Ni–MH battery powder.

FTIR studies

FTIR is an excellent analysis for identifying functional groups in a sample. The FTIR spectrum of spent Ni–MH battery powder in the range of 400–4000 cm−1 before and after the bioleaching process is shown in Fig. 3.2(c and d). The comparison between the different spectra of Fig. 3.2(c) and (d) showed that some peaks are less intense after the bioleaching process and some have been removed, which indicates the leaching of metals and their transfer to the solution.25 Before the bioleaching process, the peaks observed in the FTIR spectrum were as follows: the stretching vibration at the wavelength of 436.87 cm−1 is related to the vibrations of Ni–OH inorganic chemical bonds.58 Peak 866.04 cm−1 is related to C–H functional groups in the structure of the battery powder. Further, the peak observed at the wavelength of 1400.92 cm−1 indicates the O–H bond of carboxylic acid groups. After the bioleaching process, the peaks observed in the FTIR spectrum were as follows: the presence of a new peak at wavelength 1385.75 cm−1 after the bioleaching process is related to sulfate vibrations, which may be due to the components of the bacterial culture medium.25,60 The peak identified at the wavelength of 1632.49 cm−1 can be attributed to the nitrogen in the medium that is deposited as N–H or the amide bands of bacterial cells.61 The stretching vibrations at the wavelengths of 3435.09 cm−1 and 3637.08 cm−1 are related to the O–H functional groups in the bioleached residue.

Particle size distribution of spent Ni–MH battery powder

Particle size is one of the important parameters that affect the leaching efficiency of metals within the waste matrix.64 Further crushing of the waste results in finer particles that create a larger contact surface that increases metal dissolution in the leaching process.65 At the same time, further size reduction will lead to the accumulation of very fine particles, which will reduce the metal leaching efficiency.66 Therefore, identifying the particle size distribution of spent Ni–MH battery powder is very important in determining the efficiency of the metal leaching process in the powder.

The particle size distribution of spent Ni–MH battery powder after crushing and grinding steps is shown in Fig. S6. The results of the analysis indicated that 97% of the sample particles were smaller than 79 µm. Also, the values of the parameters X10 (particle size below 10% of the cumulative distribution), X50 (average particle size containing 50% of smaller particles and 50% of larger particles), and X90 (particle size below 90% of the cumulative distribution)67 are equal to 4.62, 11.34 and 29.03 µm, respectively. Additionally, the findings indicated that 43% of the particles were within the 0.01 to 10 µm range, while 47% of the particles were within the 10 to 30 µm range. The results of this analysis showed that the grinding and sieving process of used Ni–MH battery powder was well done.

Acid neutralization capacity test

Acid neutralization capacity (ANC) is one of the important parameters in the disposal of stabilized waste.35 This parameter is calculated from the acidity of the waste, which is considered an index of changes in the initial pH of the waste to the reference pH value. This value is expressed as ANCx. X is the selected reference pH below the ANC index. Also, the final pH is important to predict the behavior of waste after landfilling.33,36 The ANC test results of spent Ni–MH battery powder are shown in Fig. S7. As shown in Fig. S7, the final pH of the titration reached 6 using 7 mmol H+ gr−1 of battery powder and 4 using 15.6 mmol H+ gr−1 of battery powder. It can thus be concluded that the pH of battery powder does not decrease readily during acidification, as battery powder with an elevated initial pH tends to exhibit a high ANC. Also, the acid neutralization capacity of battery powder is high. The data of this test were useful because of providing correct insight into the use of acids to leaching metals from battery powder and also emphasizing the environmental issues of landfills.36

Spent Ni–MH batteries can be considered as secondary metal sources for metals with industrial applications such as Mn, Ni, Al, Co, Zn, and REEs such as La, Ce, Pr, Y, Nd, Yb. Therefore, these batteries can help to meet the needs caused by the supply of metals.

On the other hand, by recovering these metals from nickel metal hydride batteries, it is possible to prevent the adverse effects that the metals in these batteries have on the environment, including water, soil, air, and also on humans and animals. Spent medium bioleaching of spent Ni–MH battery powder

The recovery of metals (Ni and Co) and REEs (Ce and La) from battery powder at different times is shown in Fig. 5.

Recovery of metals at different times from Ni–MH battery powder.

The highest recovery of Ni and Co metals from battery powder waste was observed to be 72% and 100%, respectively, within 2 h. Then, in the period of 4 h, the recovery of Ni and Co metals was 70% and 98%, respectively. As seen in Fig. 5, over time, the recovery of Ni and Co metals decreases, which is due to the reduction of sulfuric acid in the bioleaching process. So that within 72 h, the recovery of Ni and Co was 45% and 69%, respectively. A study by Naseri et al. (2019) on the recovery of metals from spent coin cells by the bacteria Acidithiobacillus thiooxidans also showed that over time, the recovery of metals in the bioleaching process decreased due to the decrease in sulfuric acid production.25 In a study conducted on spent Ni–MH batteries, the use of yeast extract in a culture medium of Gluconobacter oxydans bacteria resulted in the recovery of 12% Ni, 22.5% Co, 28.8% Mn, and 19.5% total REEs within one day.68

As can be seen in Fig. 5, the highest concentrations of rare earth elements Ce and La were 72 ppm and 325 ppm, respectively, within 2 h. The lowest concentration of Ce and La in the solution after the bioleaching process was also observed at 72 h (1 ppm and 38 ppm, respectively). The lower REE leaching yield in the study could be due to the high amount of base metals in the Ni–MH battery powder that are acid consuming and compete with REEs for reaction with the leaching agents.68 In the bioleaching process by Acidithiobacillus thiooxidans, the bacterium catalyzes the oxidation of elemental sulfur into intermediate compounds, ultimately through the formation of sulfate ions and the release of protons (H+) causes a decrease in the solution pH and an increase in metal dissolution (eqn (2)).692

The predominant mechanism for the dissolution of base metals (Ni and Co) and REEs (La and Ce) from spent Ni–MH batteries is based on the acidolysis mechanism. In the bioleaching of these batteries, Ni, Co, Ce, and La are transferred into the aqueous phase through the following chemical reactions:69–713NiO + H2SO4 → NiSO4 + H2O4CoO + H2SO4 → CoSO4 + H2O5CeO2 + 2H2SO4 → Ce(SO4)2 + 2H2O6La2O3 + 3H2SO4 → La2(SO4)3 + 3H2O

Chemical leaching of spent Ni–MH battery powder

To determine the success rate of the bioleaching process, chemical leaching tests were conducted. Fig. 6 shows the results of the percentage metal recovery by chemical leaching and spent medium bioleaching at different pulp densities (10, 20, 30, and 50 g L−1). At a pulp density of 10 g L−1, the recovery percentage of Co and Ni in the spent medium bioleaching was 100% and 25%, respectively, while in chemical leaching, 100% and 53% were observed. Also, the extraction values of Ce and La were observed to be higher in chemical leaching than in the spent medium bioleaching.

Comparison of metals recovery at different pulp densities between chemical leaching and bioleaching.

The extraction percentages of Co, Ni, Ce, and La at a pulp density of 20 g L−1 in the spent medium bioleaching were 100%, 59%, 3%, and 67%, respectively, while in chemical leaching, the values were 100%, 47%, 0.7%, and 48%, respectively. As can be seen in Fig. 6, the extraction percentage of Ni, Ce, and La at a pulp density of 30 g L−1 in the spent medium bioleaching was higher than in chemical leaching. The higher recovery of metals in the bioleaching may be due to the fact that in addition to the sulfuric acid produced by A. thiooxidans in the bioleaching process, the bacteria can produce other metabolites such as polypeptides, amino acids, phosphatidylinositol, organic compounds, etc., which leads to higher extraction of metals in the bioleaching process compared to chemical leaching.25,72

In the pulp density of 50 g L−1, the extraction percentage of Co and Ce (equal to 42% and 0.5%, respectively) in the spent medium bioleaching was observed, while in chemical leaching it was 25% and 0.1%. As can be seen in Fig. 6, the extraction percentage of metals at pulp density of 50 g L−1 was lower than that at pulp densities of 10, 20, and 30 g L−1. Given that at pulp density of 50 g L−1, the amount of dissolving agent is lower compared to the amount of pulp used, it may result in lower extraction percentages of metals and REEs.73,74

Comparison of metal recovery efficiency from spent Ni–MH batteries with previous studies

A comparative analysis of the bioleaching performance between the present study and previously reported literature is summarized in Table 4. The results demonstrate that the application of Acidithiobacillus thiooxidans in the spent-medium bioleaching process offers a significant kinetic advantage over other microbial systems. The most notable feature of this research is its rapid leaching kinetics. While most studies utilizing Gluconobacter oxydans or Pseudomonas sp. reported leaching durations ranging from 1 to 14 days,68,75,76 and investigations with Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans extended up to 20 days,77 the current study achieved substantial metal recovery in a significantly shorter timeframe of only 2 h. Furthermore, the process efficiency in the present study was maintained at a higher pulp density (3% w/v), whereas most previous studies68,75–78 were restricted to a lower solid loading of 1% (w/v). Increasing the pulp density is a critical factor for industrial scalability, and achieving superior recovery in a fraction of the time used in other studies (e.g., 20 days for A. ferrooxidans) highlights the effectiveness of the biogenic leaching agents produced by A. thiooxidans. In relation to REEs, while G. oxydans demonstrated promising total REE recovery over extended durations, the current work managed to solubilize substantial amounts of Ce (72 ppm) and La (325 ppm) within only 2 h. These findings demonstrate that the indirect bioleaching approach employed in this study enhances metal dissolution kinetics, thereby establishing this process as an efficient method for recycling spent batteries.

Future prospects

To enhance the recovery efficiency of valuable metals from spent Ni–MH batteries and facilitate the scalability of the bioleaching process, future research must prioritize overcoming the inherent kinetic limitations and mass transfer barriers. A highly promising avenue in this field involves the integration of the biogenic lixiviant derived from Acidithiobacillus thiooxidans with process intensifiers and kinetic enhancers. For instance, future studies could investigate the synergistic use of surfactants to reduce the surface tension between the battery powder and the leaching agent, or the application of ionic catalysts (such as Ag or Cu ions) which can enhance the dissolution rate of base metals and REEs. Furthermore, although the spent medium approach protects microorganisms from the direct toxicity of battery powder, conducting adaptation tests to evaluate the tolerance threshold of A. thiooxidans against spent Ni–MH battery powder is recommended for future research.

Conclusion

In the present study, first, a comprehensive analysis of used Ni–MH cylindrical batteries from various aspects was performed, which provided useful and important information for future studies to overcome the challenges of recycling these batteries and select the correct method for recycling metals from these batteries. Then, as a solution for recycling metals from these batteries, the bioleaching process using the spent medium method using bacteria A. thiooxidans was used. These wastes contained a significant amount of Ni at the rate of 464 000 mg kg−1, Co at 30 800 mg kg−1, REEs such as Ce at the rate of 149 000, and La at 45 000 mg kg−1. In addition, the presence of other metals such as Zn, Mn, and Li in battery powder makes them a strong reservoir of metals. Therefore, it is concluded that the recycling of spent Ni–MH batteries is both economically and environmentally beneficial. Furthermore, the PSA analysis demonstrated that 97% of the powder particles were less than 79 µm in diameter.

The results of the bioleaching process showed that the highest recovery of Ni and Co from battery powder was obtained in 2 h and at a pulp density of 30 g L−1, respectively, in amounts of 100% and 72%. Also, the highest concentration of rare earth elements, Ce and La in the pulp density of 30 g L−1 in 2 h was 72 ppm and 325 ppm, respectively. The Fe-SEM, EDX, XRD, and FTIR analysis of Ni–MH powder before and after bioleaching confirmed the effectiveness of A. thiooxidans activity on metals mobilization.

In general, the results of this study help researchers and policymakers fully understand Ni–MH batteries and select efficient methods for recovering metals from these batteries. Also, the results of the bioleaching process on the powder of these batteries showed that bioleaching can act as an environmentally friendly and efficient method for recovering metals and REEs from Ni–MH batteries. Given that the bioleaching process is a time-consuming process, this study also showed that the highest metal recovery occurs in the initial short times in the bioleaching process, which is a significant result. As a result, it is suggested that researchers pay more attention to these batteries in future work, because in addition to their environmental impacts, these batteries are a reservoir of precious metals and REEs with high economic value.
