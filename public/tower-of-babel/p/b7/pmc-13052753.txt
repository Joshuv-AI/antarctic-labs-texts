Synthetic data-augmented machine learning for 30-day readmission prediction in patients with chronic conditions: a retrospective real-world study
Authors: Todd Brothers, Keyuri Adhikari, Maryam Ramazani, Ahmed Imtiaz, Mohammad Al-Mamun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo develop and evaluate an explainable machine learning framework enhanced with synthetic data generation to predict unplanned 30-day hospital readmissions among patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), heart failure (HF) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and to identify key clinical and social predictors of readmission.DesignA retrospective cohort study using electronic health record data incorporating both structured variables and information extracted from unstructured clinical notes. Synthetic data were generated using advanced resampling and deep learning-based techniques to address outcome imbalance and improve model training.SettingIntensive care unit and general ward admissions at a single tertiary academic medical centre included in the MIMIC-IV (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV) database.ParticipantsAdult patients (≥18 years) were admitted with a primary diagnosis of COPD (n=14 050), HF (n=7097) or T2DM (n=12 735) between 2008 and 2019, with complete 30-day follow-up and no in-hospital mortality during the index admission.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was unplanned all-cause hospital readmission within 30-days of discharge. Predictors were drawn from six domains, including demographics, comorbidities, clinical acuity, therapies, behavioural factors and care continuity. Predictive performance was evaluated using multiple machine learning methods and fivefold cross-validation, with model interpretability assessed using established goal and local explanation approaches.ResultsEnsemble-based machine learning models demonstrated the strongest predictive performance across all three disease cohorts. Key predictors of readmission included higher illness severity, greater comorbidity burden, medication non-adherence, gaps in preventive care and limited social support. Models incorporating synthetic data augmentation showed improved discrimination compared with models trained on original data alone.ConclusionsAn explainable synthetic-data driven framework incorporating clinical, behavioural and social data can support prediction of 30-day readmissions among patients with common chronic conditions using routinely available electronic health record data.

Introduction

Nearly one in five hospitalised patients in the USA is readmitted within 30 days, contributing to over US$26 billion in annual healthcare expenditures across all payers, of which more than US$17 billion primarily among Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries may be preventable.1 2 These readmissions are frequently driven by suboptimal transitions of care, clinical instability at discharge, limited outpatient follow-up and patient-level social and behavioural risk factors. In response, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) implemented the Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program (HRRP) in 2012 to penalise hospitals with excessive readmissions for select conditions. Despite these efforts, readmission rates remain elevated nationally, particularly among patients with chronic diseases that require complex, coordinated management.3 Since the implementation of HRRP, 30-day readmission rates for targeted conditions among Medicare beneficiaries have declined from 21.5% to 17.8%, reflecting the programme’s impact on improving care transitions and reducing avoidable hospitalisations.4

Although much of the policy focus on hospital readmissions in the USA has been driven by payer-based incentives, readmissions represent a persistent challenge across diverse healthcare systems, including those with predominantly publicly funded models. National analyses from England have demonstrated sustained rates of emergency readmissions over time, underscoring that avoidable rehospitalisation is a systemic issue not limited to market-based or insurance-driven systems but one that reflects broader challenges in care transitions, chronic disease management and post-discharge support.5

Among these chronic conditions, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), heart failure (HF) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are particularly consequential due to their high prevalence and frequent hospital utilisation.610 COPD is the fifth leading cause of hospitalisation among older adults and the third leading cause of readmissions, impacting over 16 million Americans and associated with 30-day readmission rates of 17–20%.2 7 8 HF affects approximately 6.2 million US adults and accounts for over 1 million hospitalisations annually, with 30-day readmission rates ranging from 21% to 25%.9 10 T2DM, affecting more than 37 million Americans, is often a key driver of medical complexity, with readmission rates between 14% and 22%, largely due to inadequate glycaemic control, comorbid cardiovascular and renal disease and adverse social determinants of health.1113 Importantly, these conditions frequently coexist. More than 40% of individuals with HF also have T2DM, and up to 30% of patients with COPD are affected by diabetes.3 7 This clustering contributes to synergistic increases in disease burden, healthcare utilisation and vulnerability to negative post-discharge outcomes.1416

Existing predictive models such as the LACE index (Length of stay, Acuity of admission, Commorbidities, and Emergency department visits), the HOSPITAL score (Hemoglobin at discharge, discharge from an Oncology service, Sodium level at discharge, Procedure during index admission, Index admission type, number of hospital Admissions in the past year, and Length of stay) and CMS risk-adjusted algorithms have been developed to estimate 30-day readmission risk but largely rely on administrative data and often focus on single disease states.17 18 These models frequently lack granularity in capturing dynamic clinical parameters, behavioural risk factors and care coordination metrics and are further constrained by data imbalance between readmitted and non-readmitted patients, limiting their real-world applicability.19 In contrast, recent advances in machine learning have demonstrated improved capacity to model high-dimensional electronic health record (EHR) data, capturing non-linear relationships among clinical variables and incorporating longitudinal measures of illness severity and healthcare utilisation. Multiple studies have shown that machine learning approaches can outperform traditional regression-based models for readmission prediction, particularly when social and behavioural factors derived from both structured data and unstructured clinical documentation are included.2024 However, challenges related to outcome imbalance, multimorbidity and interpretability persist, especially in heterogeneous populations with coexisting chronic conditions, underscoring the need for robust and transparent predictive frameworks. Accurate prediction of 30-day readmission risk in patients with COPD, HF and T2DM is therefore critical for enabling transition-of-care teams to prioritise patients for early intervention, medication reconciliation and coordinated care strategies aimed at reducing preventable re-hospitalisations.

The objective of this study was to predict 30-day all-cause hospital readmission among patients with COPD, HF and T2DM, and to identify key predictors contributing to readmission risk using a machine learning-driven novel synthetic data framework. We hypothesised that synthetic data-fed predictive models incorporating multidimensional clinical, demographic and utilisation-based factors would outperform in predicting the 30 days readmission risk. The findings from this study may guide the design of targeted, multidisciplinary interventions aimed at improving care transitions and reducing preventable readmissions among patients with complex chronic conditions.

Materials and methods

Data source and extraction

This retrospective cohort study is reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for observational studies and used data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, V.2.2) database.25 26 MIMIC-IV is a publicly available, de-identified EHR dataset developed by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in collaboration with Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. It contains granular clinical notes, laboratory, pharmacological and demographic data from over 60 000 intensive care unit (ICU) admissions between 2008 and 2019. All investigators completed the required training in human subject research through the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative and obtained credentialing through the PhysioNet platform. Because the dataset is fully de-identified in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act Safe Harbour provisions, institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research because the study involved retrospective analysis of de-identified data.

Study population

We identified adult patients (aged ≥18 years) with a documented diagnosis of COPD, HF or T2DM using validated International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes (J44.x - COPD, I50.x - HF and E11.x - T2DM).27 To ensure diagnostic specificity, only hospitalisations with a primary admission diagnosis attributable to the index condition (ie, COPD exacerbation, acute decompensated HF or diabetes-related metabolic events) were retained. For individuals with multiple qualifying admissions, the earliest eligible encounter was selected as the index hospitalisation. Patients were excluded if they were younger than 18 years of age, expired during index hospitalisation, had a length of stay of less than 1 day, or did not complete 30-day follow-up data (online supplemental figure 1). Deaths during the index hospitalisation were identified using discharge disposition records in the MIMIC-IV database and excluded because these patients were not at risk for post-discharge readmission. From the initial EHR patient pool (n=250 000), a total of 14 050 patients with COPD, 7097 with HF and 12 735 with T2DM met inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was unplanned all-cause hospital readmission within 30 days following discharge from the index hospitalisation. Readmissions were identified by matching patient identifiers and comparing admission and discharge timestamps across encounters. Planned readmissions such as elective procedures and scheduled therapeutic admissions were excluded based on admission type and provider documentation. Only the first unplanned readmission within the 30-day post-discharge period was considered.3 28 29

Variables and covariates

Candidate predictors were selected based on clinical relevance, prior literature and availability within the MIMIC-IV database. Variables were grouped into six domains: (1) demographics and socioeconomic status, (2) comorbidities, (3) clinical history and acute illness severity, (4) disease-specific markers and therapies, (5) behavioural and social factors and (6) care continuity indicators. Variables included age, sex, race/ethnicity, insurance type, residential setting, ICD-coded comorbidities, prior hospitalisations, ICU admission and mechanical ventilation. Disease-specific features reflected condition-relevant therapies, laboratory studies and specialty consultations.

Behavioural and care continuity variables were extracted from both structured EHR data and unstructured clinical notes using natural language processing (NLP) techniques (online supplemental table 1). To improve clarity and support future implementation, variables identified as important by model interpretability analyses were derived from both structured and unstructured EHR data. Structured variables included demographics, comorbidities, laboratory values, vital signs, illness severity scores (eg, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and Charlson Comorbidity Index), medication classes and prior healthcare utilisation. Unstructured variables were obtained from free-text clinical notes and primarily captured behavioural and care continuity factors, including medication non-adherence, substance use, documented social support, discharge education and outpatient follow-up planning. The study population was identified through a combination of structured data queries and NLP-based analysis of clinical documentation. Multiple NLP pipelines were employed to harmonise and verify key admission and discharge information. Additionally, behavioural and social determinants of health, including substance use, non-adherence and social support, were derived from both structured fields and unstructured notes using these NLP methods.

Care continuity indicators included discharge education, outpatient follow-up and referrals to post-acute services. Information for all domains was derived from both structured data (eg, diagnosis codes, laboratory measurements, medications) and unstructured clinical documentation. Free-text clinical notes including discharge summaries, progress notes and consultation reports were processed using rule-based algorithms and NLP techniques to extract clinical entities and contextual information. NLP processing consisted of simple, rule-based text analysis focused on clearly documented clinical concepts. Notes were preprocessed using standard text cleaning procedures, and predefined clinical keywords and phrases were used to identify behavioural and care continuity indicators. Extracted information was transformed into structured variables and cross-checked against structured EHR fields when available. Only unambiguous and clinically interpretable information was retained. All NLP-derived variables were cross-validated against structured EHR fields where available to ensure internal consistency. Quantitative measurements from both structured fields and clinical narratives were incorporated into the final analytical cohort(s) (figure 1).

Data augmentation and synthetic data generation

To address substantial class imbalance in 30-day readmission outcomes across the COPD (readmitted: 35%, not readmitted: 65%), HF (readmitted: 35%, not readmitted: 65%) and T2DM (readmitted: 35%, not readmitted: 65%) cohorts. We employed three data augmentation techniques: synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE), conditional tabular generative adversarial network (CTGAN) and tabular variational autoencoder (TVAE). These methods were chosen to represent both classical resampling strategies and advanced generative approaches tailored for structured clinical data.2832 SMOTE is a widely used resampling technique that increases the number of readmission cases by generating additional data points similar to existing patients with the outcome of interest through interpolation of observed samples. In contrast, CTGAN and TVAE are data-driven synthetic data generation methods that learn patterns from the original dataset to produce realistic patient records while preserving relationships between clinical variables. Together, these approaches were used to address outcome imbalance and improve model training while maintaining the overall structure of the clinical data.2833 CTGAN is a synthetic data generation method designed to create realistic tabular datasets by learning patterns in real-world clinical data, particularly when variables differ in type and distribution.30 Recently, CTGAN has been adapted for structured EHR data synthesis to support privacy-preserving and utility-preserving modelling. When applied to the MIMIC-III database,34 differentially private CTGAN variants achieved high fidelity (≥98%) under privacy budgets of ε<1.0. In other EHR-based applications35 involving over 28 000 inpatient encounters, CTGAN-generated data preserved marginal and joint feature distributions Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) <0.05 and enabled downstream models for length-of-stay prediction with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) performance within 2–4% of real-data benchmarks. TVAE is a deep learning–based synthetic data generation method that creates realistic patient records by learning patterns and relationships present in the original EHR data. It has been applied in prior EHR-based studies, including length-of-stay prediction and adverse drug event detection, where synthetic datasets demonstrated fidelity and downstream model performance comparable to analyses using real-world data.35 36 Synthetic data generation was performed only within training datasets to avoid incorporation of information from validation samples. Further details of these models are provided in supplementary text.

Machine learning models

To predict 30-day unplanned hospital readmission among patients with COPD, HF and T2DM, we trained four supervised machine learning classifiers on four versions of each dataset: the original (baseline) dataset and datasets augmented using SMOTE, CTGAN and TVAE. The classifiers included logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), gradient boosting and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), selected to represent a mix of linear and ensemble-based modelling approaches. Each data augmentation technique was applied within the training folds following data partitioning to prevent information leakage. These augmented datasets were subsequently used to train the machine learning classifiers.

LR served as the baseline linear model due to its high interpretability and computational efficiency.37 RF, an ensemble of decision trees trained via bootstrap aggregation, captures non-linear relationships and reduces overfitting through majority voting.38 Gradient boosting constructs sequential decision trees, with each iteration correcting errors from the previous model, enabling flexible and accurate function approximation.39 XGBoost builds on this by incorporating regularisation, tree pruning and parallel processing and has shown strong performance in clinical prediction tasks.40 All models were developed using stratified fivefold cross-validation to ensure robustness and reduce overfitting. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using grid search within each training fold, and optimal configurations for each classifier-dataset pair were conducted (online supplemental table 2).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarised patient characteristics for three cohorts: COPD, HF and T2DM. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were expressed as means with SD or medians with IQRs. Missing data were addressed using multiple imputations with chained equations under the assumption of missing at random using MICE package in R.41 First, a multivariable LR was used to identify independent predictors of 30-day readmission. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical significance and included in the models

Model evaluation and selection

Model performance was evaluated using stratified fivefold cross-validation, with all metrics computed on the held-out validation folds. Evaluation metrics included accuracy, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), precision, recall (sensitivity) and F1 score. Accuracy was defined as the proportion of correctly classified observations among the total number of predictions. AUC measured the model’s ability to discriminate between positive and negative classes across all probability thresholds. Precision reflected the proportion of true positives among all predicted positives, while recall quantified the proportion of actual positives correctly identified by the model. F1 score, the harmonic mean of precision and recall, was used to balance sensitivity and positive predictive value, particularly in the presence of class imbalance. The optimal model for each dataset (ie, baseline, SMOTE, CTGAN and TVAE-augmented) was selected based on the highest combined performance of recall and F1 score, as these metrics are most relevant to clinical contexts where false negatives (ie, missed high-risk patients) are more consequential than false positives. A sensitivity analysis included stratified models for each primary condition (COPD, HF or T2DM), comparing patients with and without the other two comorbidities within each cohort. All analyses were conducted using Python (V.3.9; Python Software Foundation) and SQL.

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

The final analytical sample consisted of 33 882 adult ICU patients, comprising 14 050 with COPD, 7097 with HF and 12 735 with T2DM (table 1). Age distributions varied significantly across cohorts: patients with COPD and HF were predominantly older, with 64.02% and 65.90%, respectively, aged ≥65 years, whereas the T2DM cohort included a larger proportion of younger individuals, with 69.20% under the age of 65. The HF cohort had a higher proportion of male patients (55.53%), whereas gender distribution was approximately balanced in the COPD and T2DM cohorts. Racial composition was more homogeneous in the HF and COPD groups, with white patients comprising 57.85% and 59.82%, respectively, compared with a more racially diverse distribution observed in the T2DM cohort. Insurance status also varied across cohorts, with Medicare coverage being most prevalent among patients with HF (59.48%) and COPD (45.05%). Comorbidity patterns demonstrated a high burden of multimorbidity across all cohorts. Hypertension was the most prevalent condition, present in 61.01% of patients with COPD and 70.44% of those with HF. Comorbid HF was present in 31.19% of patients with COPD, while 29.21% of patients with T2DM had comorbid COPD. Current smoking was most common in COPD (50.23%), and medication non-adherence was reported in 70.06%, 50.15% and 25.00% of patients with COPD, T2DM and HF, respectively. The documented social support was observed in 79.85% patients in the COPD cohort, 69.56% of T2DM and 68.52% of HF.

In multivariable models adjusted for demographic, clinical and behavioural covariates, age ≥65 years was significantly associated with increased 30-day readmission risk in COPD (adjusted OR (aOR) 1.42; 95% CI 1.30 to 1.55; p<0.001) and HF (aOR 1.89; 95% CI 1.66 to 2.14; p<0.001), but not in T2DM. Medicaid and uninsured status were consistently associated with increased readmission risk across cohorts. Notably, non-adherence to prescribed medications was strongly associated with higher readmission risk across all three cohorts (COPD: aOR 1.51; HF: aOR 1.37; T2DM: aOR 1.29; all p<0.001)(online supplemental table 3).

Model performance across augmentation strategies

Performance evaluation of machine learning classifiers trained on TVAE-augmented data demonstrated consistent superiority of ensemble-based models across all three chronic disease cohorts (ie, COPD, HF and T2DM) (table 2). Gradient boosting consistently emerged as the best-performing model in the COPD and T2DM cohorts, whereas XGBoost performed best in the HF cohort, as determined by F1 score. In the COPD cohort, gradient boosting achieved an F1 score of 0.84±0.01, with accuracy and precision of 0.89±0.001 and 0.89±0.01, respectively. LR, although demonstrating relatively high recall (0.80±0.01), lagged in overall performance (F1 score, 0.78±0.001). Similar trends were observed in T2DM, where gradient boosting produced the highest F1 score (0.83±0.01), precision (0.87±0.01) and recall (0.80±0.01), outperforming all other classifiers.

In the HF cohort, XGBoost achieved the best F1 score (0.80±0.01), demonstrating balanced performance across accuracy, precision and recall. While gradient boosting and RF performed comparably (F1 scores: 0.80±0.02 and 0.81±0.02, respectively), LR again showed inferior performance, with an F1 score of 0.77±0.01. The performance superiority of ensemble models over linear classifiers underscores the ability of tree-based algorithms to capture non-linear relationships and interaction effects among clinical variables in high-dimensional EHR data (table 2).

Discriminative ability, assessed using the AUC, further supported these findings. The best-performing models exhibited strong separation, with AUCs ranging from 0.91 to 0.95 across cohorts (figure 2). In the COPD cohort, gradient boosting and XGBoost both reached AUCs of 0.93, with near-identical ROC curves. In HF, all ensemble models surpassed an AUC of 0.92, with XGBoost performing marginally better. In T2DM, gradient boosting and XGBoost tied at an AUC of 0.94–0.95, with the former selected due to higher recall and model interpretability.

Model performance for test sets

Model generalisability and robustness were further validated on held-out test sets (online supplemental table 4). In the COPD cohort, gradient boosting achieved an F1 score of 0.92, recall of 0.96 and AUC of 0.93. In the HF group, both XGBoost and gradient boosting yielded F1 scores of 0.91 and AUCs of 0.93. In the T2DM cohort, gradient boosting was selected as the top model with an F1 score of 0.91 and AUC of 0.94. Across cohorts, these models also demonstrated high precision (range 0.88–0.91) and recall (range 0.90–0.96), further supporting their utility in high-risk patient identification for unplanned 30-day readmissions.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of machine learning models trained on synthetic datasets augmented using TVAE, stratified by the presence or absence across the three disease cohorts: COPD, HF and T2DM. Models trained on subgroups without comorbid conditions consistently demonstrated superior predictive performance across all evaluated metrics (online supplemental table 5). In the COPD cohort, exclusion of patients with HF and T2DM yielded higher accuracy (0.90±0.005 vs 0.89±0.004), recall (0.86±0.011 vs 0.77±0.011) and F1 score (0.89±0.006 vs 0.83±0.007). In the HF cohort, models excluding COPD and T2DM comorbidities similarly outperformed inclusive models, with gains in accuracy (0.90±0.007 vs 0.88±0.007), recall (0.84±0.010 vs 0.77±0.014) and F1 score (0.88±0.008 vs 0.82±0.011). In the T2DM cohort, removal of COPD and HF comorbidities led to the highest observed performance improvements, with accuracy increasing from 0.91±0.004 to 0.93±0.004, recall from 0.80±0.014 to 0.88±0.008, and F1 score from 0.87±0.008 to 0.92±0.004.

Multivariable LR analyses further evaluated the independent predictors of 30-day readmission across disease-specific cohorts (online supplemental table 3). In the COPD cohort, patients aged ≥65 years had significantly increased odds of readmission compared with those aged 18–44 (aOR 1.42; 95% CI 1.30 to 1.55; p<0.001), as did those aged 45–64 (aOR 1.21; 95% CI 1.10 to 1.33; p=0.002). Similar age-related risk gradients were observed in the HF cohort, with the ≥65 group exhibiting the highest risk (aOR 1.89; 95% CI 1.66 to 2.14; p<0.001). Male sex was associated with higher readmission odds in HF (aOR 1.18; 95% CI 1.07 to 1.30; p=0.001), but not in COPD or T2DM. Across all three cohorts, non-adherence to prescribed medications consistently emerged as a robust risk factor (COPD: aOR 1.51, p<0.001; HF: aOR 1.37, p<0.001; T2DM: aOR 1.29, p<0.001).

Discussion

Key findings

Our study demonstrated that prediction models trained on TVAE-generated synthetic data outperformed baseline models in estimating 30-day unplanned readmissions among patients with COPD, HF and T2DM. Ensemble methods, particularly gradient boosting, exhibited the highest predictive performance, especially when paired with synthetic data augmentation. These findings suggest that the inclusion of multiple comorbidities introduces heterogeneity that may impair model discrimination and calibration. Improvements in recall and F1 scores, particularly in subgroups with fewer comorbidities, underscore the importance of stratified model evaluation to accommodate clinical complexity and promote fairness across diverse patient populations.

Key drivers of readmission risk included clinical acuity markers such as SOFA and CCI scores, medication non-adherence, multimorbidity and prior healthcare utilisation patterns. Notably, social and behavioural factors often extracted from unstructured clinical notes emerged as significant predictors, highlighting the importance of multidimensional modelling in high-risk populations. Further, model interpretability analysis revealed that in the COPD cohort, older age and incomplete pneumococcal immunisation were key predictors, while in the T2DM cohort, insulin use, poor glycaemic control and gaps in immunisation coverage dominated.

SHAP and LIME interpretability techniques provided complementary insights, with SHAP offering population-level explanations and LIME supporting patient-level understanding. Together, these tools confirmed the relevance of clinical severity indices and preventive care measures in guiding model predictions, thereby enhancing transparency and enabling personalised risk stratification for targeted interventions. Lastly, sensitivity analyses reinforced the importance of both clinical severity and social-behavioural determinants in predicting 30-day readmissions. These results further highlight the need for comprehensive discharge planning strategies that address medication adherence and preventive care utilisation.

Relation to prior work

Recent studies have shown that feedforward neural networks outperform traditional LR models using severity-of-illness scores when predicting in-hospital mortality.4244 However, existing readmission prediction models often lack granularity and fail to incorporate patient-specific clinical and behavioural risk factors, limiting their accuracy particularly in modelling 30-day unplanned readmissions.4247 The importance of social and behavioural determinants in predicting 30-day readmissions is supported by systematic evidence showing that factors such as insurance status, self-management behaviours and other social risks contribute to readmission risk among patients with chronic conditions such as diabetes.48 Additionally, studies examining predictive models have found that incorporating individual and community-level social determinants, including socioeconomic status and other social risk factors, can improve risk discrimination for readmission or death, particularly among high-risk subgroups.49 Our study has clearly demonstrated the importance of incorporating these additional vital elements to improve the identification of patients at risk for 30-day readmission.

High-acuity populations, such as those discharged from ICUs, remain understudied despite their heightened risk of early readmission.50 ICU patients often experience greater physiological instability, more severe acute illness and a higher incidence of complications such as nosocomial infections, delirium or organ dysfunction. Additionally, these patients frequently face fragmented transitions of care, with limited continuity between inpatient teams and outpatient providers, making postdischarge care coordination more challenging. Access to high-fidelity critical care data to address these concerns also remains sparse. The availability of the MIMIC-IV database offers a unique opportunity to address these gaps through detailed, longitudinal patient data that capture both inpatient and postdischarge variables. Yet, prior studies using the MIMIC database have primarily focused on general ICU populations or single-disease cohorts without incorporating a multidimensional framework that combines provider notes, laboratory values, comorbidity burden and care coordination indicators to inform actionable interventions at discharge.5153

Beyond descriptive readmission trends, several studies have demonstrated that readmission risk prediction models and machine learning approaches have been evaluated and validated in non-US health systems, including Europe and Canada, with similar challenges related to multimorbidity and care transitions. Notably, the HOSPITAL score and other prediction tools have shown consistent performance across international cohorts, suggesting that key drivers of readmission risk are shared across diverse healthcare systems despite differences in financing and organisation.54 55

Our results expand on previous work by demonstrating that explainable machine learning models incorporating both structured and unstructured EHR data can outperform traditional models that rely solely on demographic or diagnostic inputs. Compared with previously established tools, our integrated approach demonstrated superior predictive discrimination, particularly among patients with multimorbidity and complex social needs. This enhanced performance supports the potential of our model to inform more personalised and effective interventions during transitions of care, ultimately improving patient outcomes and reducing preventable readmissions.

Clinical contextual prediction models

Consistent with prior literature, our models confirmed that disease-specific clinical factors including inhaled therapy utilisation and ED visits in COPD, renal dysfunction and electrolyte disturbances in HF and glycaemic variability in T2DM are significant drivers of 30-day readmission risk.5658 However, our study advances existing evidence by integrating SHAP and LIME analyses, which surfaced several non-traditional and modifiable predictors linked to both clinical and social risk domains. In the COPD cohort, SHAP values identified high SOFA and CCI scores and incomplete pneumococcal vaccination as top predictors aligning with known associations between comorbid burden and immunisation gaps in readmission risk. Notably, older age and Medicare coverage further emphasised the role of chronic disease burden and fragmented post-acute care access in this group. These findings are supported by studies showing that elderly patients with COPD often lack continuity in primary and pulmonary specialty care, particularly following ICU stays.

Similarly, in our HF cohort, SHAP and LIME highlighted poor immunisation rates and high SOFA and CCI scores, which were consistent with national trends indicating low vaccination rates and higher severity of illness scores increases the incidence of rehospitalisation.5961 In the T2DM cohort, SHAP analysis ranked insulin use, hyperglycaemia and lack of immunisation coverage among the most influential features. These variables capture both physiologic instability (eg, poor glycaemic control) and preventive care gaps. Prior studies have demonstrated that patients with T2DM with poor vaccine adherence have higher hospitalisation and infection-related complication rates, supporting the model’s attribution.62 63

Crucially, social and behavioural determinants, often under-represented in traditional models, emerged as equally important as physiological markers. Features such as missed appointments, medication non-adherence, absence of caregiver documentation and substance use were consistently ranked among the top SHAP features across all cohorts. These social determinants of health (SDoH)-driven predictors reflect broader issues in transitions of care, including poor health literacy, limited transportation access and social isolation. Their quantification in our models adds a novel layer of interpretability and clinical actionability, reinforcing that postdischarge planning must address not only clinical stability but also social infrastructure to reduce preventable readmissions. Together, these insights validate the clinical logic of our model outputs and underscore the value of integrating both structured and unstructured EHR data, particularly when identifying patients who may appear physiologically stable yet remain at elevated risk due to unmet social needs.

Strengths and methodological rigor

A major strength of this study lies in its comprehensive data integration and transparent model interpretability. By leveraging MIMIC-IV’s rich clinical documentation and augmenting minority outcome classes with synthetic data (TVAE, CTGAN and SMOTE), we mitigated class imbalance while preserving performance across diverse subgroups. The application of TVAE-based data augmentation was critical in enhancing model sensitivity to rare outcomes. Unlike traditional oversampling techniques such as SMOTE or GAN-based CTGAN, TVAE preserved clinical feature distributions while generating realistic synthetic patient profiles. This led to improved model calibration and classification metrics, particularly F1 score and recall, which are essential for reducing false negatives in readmission prediction. Given its superior performance across all model architectures and disease cohorts, TVAE was selected as the preferred augmentation method; thus, only TVAE-based results are reported in this study. The use of SHAP and LIME interpretability tools ensured both global and local model transparency, increasing the trustworthiness and potential clinical utility of our predictions. Additionally, rigorous internal validation and subgroup analyses further bolstered model robustness.

A key methodological contribution of this study lies in the empirical comparison between CTGAN and TVAE, both of which are increasingly used for synthetic data generation in healthcare. While CTGAN has shown promise in prior studies, its performance in our setting was consistently lower in terms of recall and F1 score.31 35 36 This is likely due to CTGAN’s reliance on adversarial training, which, although powerful, is prone to mode collapse and difficulty in maintaining distributional fidelity across mixed-type variables which are known challenges in EHR-related data. In contrast, TVAE’s variational autoencoding approach allowed it to model the underlying feature distributions more faithfully by capturing latent structure and probabilistically decoding synthetic observations. This resulted in greater stability in training, stronger preservation of inter-variable dependencies and improved model generalisability, particularly in clinically heterogeneous cohorts such as those with COPD or HF. These advantages, combined with its regularisation properties, contributed to the superior performance of TVAE-augmented models across all evaluation metrics and subgroups. As such, TVAE emerged as the most robust method for generating minority class instances in our study context, offering both predictive performance gains and methodological transparency.

Practical considerations

These findings support the clinical application of explainable artificial intelligence tools to enhance transitional care planning when thoughtfully integrated into existing EHR workflows. Risk stratification models that surface actionable drivers such as social risk factors, medication non-adherence or gaps in care coordination can guide targeted clinician-led interventions, social work referrals or telehealth outreach, particularly in health systems with limited resources. Importantly, many high-importance predictors identified in this study (eg, age, comorbidity burden, illness severity scores, medication history, prior utilisation and discharge disposition) are routinely captured in structured EHR fields, enabling automated feature extraction without additional documentation burden.

In practice, model results and inference could be triggered at predefined clinical time points, such as initiation of discharge planning or completion of a discharge summary, using real-time structured data feeds. Information derived from unstructured clinical notes, including indicators of medication non-adherence or social support, could be extracted using lightweight natural language processing pipelines similar to those applied in this study and executed asynchronously to avoid disruption of clinical workflows. Predicted readmission risk could then be presented within existing EHR interfaces, such as discharge planning dashboards or transitions-of-care worklists, rather than disruptive alerts, allowing care teams to prioritise patients for intervention.

The use of synthetic data methods, such as TVAE, not only improves model performance in small or imbalanced cohorts but also facilitates scalable model development while preserving patient privacy, supporting broader implementation across institutions with heterogeneous patient populations. Although local data mapping, validation and calibration would be required before deployment, the modelling framework relies on commonly available EHR data elements and does not depend on proprietary variables or institution-specific workflows, suggesting potential portability. By embedding such models into routine discharge processes, health systems may improve risk identification, support more efficient allocation of transitional care resources and align with national policy initiatives focused on health equity, care quality and value-based reimbursement.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the use of data from a single tertiary care centre may limit generalisability, though the MIMIC-IV database remains one of the most detailed and validated critical care datasets available. Although we evaluated model robustness using disease-specific sensitivity analyses, the models were developed and internally validated within a single dataset and were not externally validated; therefore, caution is warranted when applying these models to populations or care settings that differ from the study cohort. Second, despite using unstructured data to capture social determinants, some variables may remain underdocumented or inconsistently recorded. Third, while synthetic data improved recall and sensitivity, real-world validation is needed before clinical deployment. Fourth, we did not evaluate calibration, cost-effectiveness or workflow integration feasibility, all of which represent essential next steps. Fifth, caution is warranted when interpreting black-box models in under-represented populations to avoid perpetuating bias. Sixth, like prior MIMIC-based readmission studies, these findings should be interpreted in the context of a single data source. Seventh, the quality and consistency of clinical notes affected by missing timestamps, typographical errors and variable documentation may degrade the reliability of NLP-derived features. Eighth, potential temporal shifts in care practices over the multiyear span of MIMIC-IV may introduce drift, particularly if models are not explicitly adjusted for year or protocol changes. Additionally, excluding patients who died during the index hospitalisation may introduce selection bias by limiting the analysis to patients who survived to discharge; however, this approach is standard in readmission studies, as death represents a competing outcome. Lastly, despite class balancing efforts, residual imbalance in outcomes may still bias model performance, a concern noted in prior synthetic augmentation studies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the successful integration of structured and unstructured EHR data, augmented with synthetic sampling, into machine learning models that robustly predict 30-day unplanned readmissions among patients with COPD, HF and T2DM. Incorporating social and behavioural risk factors coupled with clinical severity markers supports the adoption of multidimensional risk prediction frameworks to capture real-world patient complexity and identify actionable drivers of readmission. Future research should focus on external validation using multicentre prospective data, real-time clinical integration and prospective evaluation to determine their impact on reducing readmissions and improving patient-centred, equitable transitional care.
