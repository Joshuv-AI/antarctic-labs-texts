Concise synthesis of bufogargarizin B by a conformation-controlled skeletal reorganization approach
Authors: Peicheng Yang, Yi Shen, Jinghan Gui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bufogargarizins are a class of unique 19-norbufadienolides isolated from the venom of Bufo bufo gargarizans. Here we report a concise synthesis of bufogargarizin B from inexpensive, commercially available dehydroepiandrosterone by means of a conformation-controlled skeletal reorganization approach. The synthesis features the rapid construction of the 5/7/6/5 tetracyclic core framework by means of a SmI2-mediated ketone–allylic acetate transannular cyclization, and the installation of stereochemically diverse 5,7- and 7,5-fused ring systems through a biomimetic retro-aldol/transannular aldol cascade reaction. In addition, an efficient, scalable synthesis of 2-pyrone-5-boronate, a key building block for the introduction of the side chain of bufadienolides, has been developed, and the highly functionalized all-cis D ring of bufogargarizin B is installed by means of a series of highly chemo- and regioselective redox transformations. This work vividly demonstrates that conformational control plays a critical role in the precise installation of desired stereocenters in transannular cyclization products.

Introduction

Bufadienolides are an important family of cardiotonic steroids that are found in both plants and animals1–3. For example, they are major components of toad venoms, which have been used as herbal medicines—such as ChanSu—for centuries in China, owing to their various biological activities, such as cardiotonic, anticancer, and anti-inflammation activities4,5. Structurally, bufadienolides possess a characteristic α-pyrone motif at the C17β position and a cis-fused C/D ring with either a 14β hydroxyl group or a 14β,15β epoxide. Owing to the intriguing structures and important biological activities of bufadienolides, they have attracted significant attention from synthetic chemists6,7, which has culminated in various creative approaches for the installation of the functionalized D ring8–13. Notably, the preceding studies have focused mainly on targets with the classical 6/6/6/5 tetracyclic steroidal skeleton. In contrast, bufadienolides with rearranged core frameworks—that is, abeo-steroids—have been far less thoroughly investigated, and their potential pharmaceutical value has not been fully elucidated. Therefore, the development of efficient strategies for concisely synthesizing these structurally unique natural products to facilitate their biological evaluation is highly appealing.

Two representative members of rearranged bufadienolides are bufogargarizins A (1) and B (2) (Fig. 1a), which were isolated by Ye et al. in 2010 from the venom of Bufo bufo gargarizans14. Since then, several other bufogargarizins with various oxidation states have been identified from the same source15–18. Structurally, bufogargarizins are 19-norbufadienolides with a unique 7/5/6/5 or 5/7/6/5 tetracyclic core framework and a highly functionalized all-cis D ring with a characteristic α-pyrone motif. Although 1 and 2 are proposed to be biosynthetically related to each other by means of a retro-aldol and transannular aldol cascade via 314, chemical synthesis of their complex and highly oxidized frameworks poses considerable challenges. In 2019, the Li group reported the construction of the 7/5/6/5 tetracyclic ring system of bufogargarizin C by means of an intramolecular [5 + 2] cycloaddition19. Later, in 2023, they completed the first syntheses of 1 and 2 by means of an elegant Ru-catalyzed [5 + 2] cycloaddition reaction of a vinyl ether cyclopropane-yne20. Very recently, Xu and co-workers reported concise installation of the 5/7/6/5 tetracyclic core of 2 via a Birch reduction and an oxidative cleavage/transannular aldol cascade21. As a continuation of our work on the efficient synthesis of abeo-steroids24, we herein report a completely distinct, skeletal reorganization approach to bufogargarizins that utilizes a conformation-controlled, SmI2-mediated ketone–allylic acetate transannular cyclization to precisely install the pivotal 5/7/6/5 tetracyclic core framework and 1α,5α-stereocenters of bufogargarizin B. Our work suggests that conformational control plays a critical role in determining the stereochemistry of transannular cyclization products25. Importantly, we are able to divergently access stereochemically diverse 5,7-fused ring systems with 1α,5α-, 1β,5α- or 1β,5β-stereocenters through finely tuning the olefin geometry and flexible conformations of cyclodecenones. Accordingly, this conformation-controlled transannular cyclization is not limited to the synthesis of bufogargarizin B, which is the topic of the present study, but could be potentially applicable to the synthesis of a series of other bioactive natural products with similar 5,7-fused ring systems, such as bufospirostenin A, grayanotoxin II, auriculatol A, principinols B and E (Fig. 1b).

Results

Retrosynthetic design

As described above, we surmised that 1 could be accessed from 2 by means of a biomimetic retro-aldol/transannular aldol cascade (BRAC) reaction. We hypothesized that the highly oxidized D ring of 2, including the 16β acetate and the 14β,15β epoxide, might be installed from the C16–C17 olefin by means of highly chemo- and regioselective redox transformations of olefin 4 (Fig. 2a). The characteristic α-pyrone motif at C17 could be established by means of a Suzuki–Miyaura coupling26,27 or a Stille coupling28,29 of an enol triflate derived from 7 with 2-pyrone-5-boronate (5) or 5-(trimethylstannyl)−2H-pyran-2-one (6), respectively. We envisioned that the 5/7 cis-fused A/B ring system of 7 could be constructed through a SmI2-mediated, diastereoselective transannular cyclization of cyclodecenone 8, which might in turn be accessed by β-fragmentation of a C5 alkoxy radical derived from classical steroid dehydroepiandrosterone (9) or 19-hydroxyandrostenedione (10)30.Fig. 2Retrosynthetic analysis and synthetic challenges.a Retrosynthetic analysis of bufogargarizin B. b Representative flexible conformations of cis- and trans-cyclodecene. Previously, our group had developed a BBr3-mediated, cis-selective transannular Prins cyclization to build the 5/7-fused hydroazulenol motif, which enabled us to complete a concise synthesis of the 5(10 → 1)abeo-steroid bufospirostenin A31. However, this key transformation suffered from low yield (43%) and incomplete conversion (70%). Therefore, a more robust synthetic protocol for the transannular cyclization of 8 to generate 7 needs to be established, which constitutes the most challenging part of this synthetic plan. It is well established that cis- and trans-cyclodecene can exist in several different conformations (those with the least strain energies demonstrated in Fig. 2b)32,33, which are expected to have a significant impact on the stereochemical outcome of the key transannular cyclization (8 → 7), i.e., the stereochemistry of two contiguous stereocenters at C1 and C5. Accordingly, the goals of this synthetic endeavor lie in the following two aspects: 1) control of the olefin geometry during the alkoxy radical fragmentation to divergently access (Z)- or (E)−8; and 2) control of the flexible conformations of (Z)- or (E)−8 to divergently synthesize three or four possible diastereomers of 7. To our knowledge, systematic investigation of the influences of the olefin geometry and flexible conformations of cyclodecenones (such as 8) on the divergent construction of stereochemically diverse 5,7-fused ring systems—a characteristic structural motif found in many bioactive natural products as exemplified in Fig. 1b—has remained to be disclosed.

Conformation-controlled skeletal reorganization

We began by investigating the stereocontrolled synthesis of (Z)- or (E)−8 and their skeletal reorganization to access 7, which has the 5/7/6/5 tetracyclic framework of 2 (Fig. 3). To this end, acetyl protection of 10 and subsequent diastereoselective reduction of the C3 ketone with K-selectride delivered 11 in 86% yield with 10:1 diastereomeric ratio (Fig. 3a)34. Mukaiyama hydration of 1135, followed by protection of the C3 hydroxyl group as the tert-butyldiphenylsilyl ether, furnished an intermediate with the desired α-tertiary alcohol at C5 (not shown) in 71% yield. In sharp contrast to the known radical fragmentation of the steroidal C5α alcohol with a C19 methyl group that typically yields trans-cyclodecenone exclusively30,31, treatment of the analogous C19-acetoxy C5α alcohol under identical conditions (PhI(OAc)₂, I₂, hv) delivered cis-cyclodecenone (E)−8 exclusively in 41% yield (for selected optimization and mechanistic rationale, see Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2 in the Supplementary Information). Pleasingly, after extensive experimentation, we were able to develop an alternative synthetic route to access (Z)−8 and (Z)-12 stereoselectively. Mukaiyama hydration of 935, followed by protection of the C3 hydroxyl group, produced the desired 5α-configured alcohol (not shown) in 77% yield. In contrast to the alkoxy radical fragmentation described above, subjection of this C5 alcohol intermediate to phenyliodine(III) diacetate/I2 smoothly furnished trans-cyclodecenone 13 exclusively in 96% yield30,31. This interesting result clearly suggested that the C19 acetyl group had a significant effect on the C5 alkoxy radical fragmentation (for mechanistic rationale, see Supplementary Fig. 2 in the Supplementary Information). We hypothesized that, in the absence of the C19 acetyl group, the resulting C10 carbon radical existed in the chair-chair-chair conformation, which underwent fast oxidative elimination to give trans-cyclodecenone 13. In the presence of the C19 acetyl group, the C10 radical was stabilized by a hyperconjugative interaction with the adjacent σ*-orbital of the C19–O bond, which would lead to an unfavorable oxidative elimination to give the trans-cyclodecenone. Instead, strain-release induced conformational isomerization would take place to generate the most stable boat-chair-boat conformation, which then underwent oxidative elimination to afford cis-cyclodecenone (E)−8. To continue the synthesis of (Z)-8, Riley oxidation of 13 with SeO2 in dioxane and pyridine, followed by chemoselective reductive of the resulting C19 aldehyde with LiAlH(OtBu)3, furnished allylic alcohol (Z)−12, which could be smoothly converted to acetate (Z)−8.

With allylic acetates (Z)- and (E)-8 in hand, we moved on to explore the pivotal SmI2-mediated ketone–allylic acetate transannular cyclization to construct the 5/7/6/5 tetracyclic core framework (Fig. 3b)36–41. Treatment of allylic acetate (E)-8 with SmI2 and hexamethylphosphoramide at 0 °C furnished cis-fused hydroazulenol 14, which has the undesired stereochemistry at both C1 and C5. The structure of 14 was determined by X-ray crystallographic analysis of p-nitrobenzoate derivative 15. The stereochemistry of this reaction could be rationalized by assuming that (E)-8 adopted a chair-boat-chair conformation, which underwent ketone–allylic acetate reductive coupling with SmI2 to produce 14 via radical I42. In comparison, we were pleased to find that treating (Z)-8 with SmI2 and hexamethylphosphoramide at room temperature delivered cis-fused hydroazulenol 16 with the desired stereochemistry at both C1 and C5 in 60% yield, along with undesired diastereomer 14 in 27% yield. Notably, (E)-8 exists in a single conformational isomer, as clearly indicated by its 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 3c). In sharp contrast, (Z)-8 exists in a mixture of two conformational isomers, as confirmed by variable-temperature NMR spectroscopy. These NMR studies suggested that the major chair-chair-chair conformation of (Z)-8 might lead to major product 16 via radical intermediate II and that the minor boat-chair-boat conformation of (Z)-8 might give rise to minor product 14 via radical intermediate III. Moreover, we hypothesized that the 16/14 ratio should be related to the ratio of conformational isomers of (Z)-8 at the reaction temperature. Pleasingly, we were able to verify this hypothesis by performing the reaction at different temperatures. At –78 °C, 16 and 14 were obtained in 72% and 17% yields, respectively, indicating that the proportion of the major chair-chair-chair conformation of (Z)-8 was higher at –78 °C than at room temperature or at −20 °C. Collectively, these results vividly illustrate the importance of conformational control in the transannular cyclization of medium-sized rings. In addition, these results clearly demonstrate that SmI₂-mediated cyclization enables the divergent construction of stereochemically diverse 5,7-fused ring systems through control of olefin geometry and conformational flexibility. This stands in contrast to conventional intramolecular aldol reactions, which typically afford a mixture of diastereomers21,23.

Completion of the synthesis of bufogargarizin B

Having established a reliable method for constructing the core framework, we moved on to complete the synthesis of bufogargarizin B (2) by introducing the high oxidation state on ring D (Fig. 4), which is by no means a simple task in natural product synthesis43. To this end, we decided to introduce the α-pyrone motif at C17 via a Suzuki–Miyaura coupling26,27 or a Stille coupling28,29, which in turn required the development of an efficient and scalable route to α-pyrone-containing building block 5 or 6. Although preparation of these two compounds was known, the reported methods either suffered from the use of an expensive starting material, such as 1726,27, or required a long reaction time and gave a low isolated yield (Fig. 4a)29. To circumvent these issues, we synthesized 5 on a gram scale from inexpensive, commercially available furfuryl alcohol (20) (Fig. 4b). Specifically, an Achmatowicz reaction44 of 20 with N-bromosuccinimide in THF/H2O delivered pyranone 21 in 73% yield on a decagram scale. Ozonolysis of olefin 16 led to diketone 24 in 90% yield (Fig. 4c). Protection of 24 as the trimethylsilyl ether and subsequent treatment with Tf2O uneventfully delivered the C17 enol triflate (not shown), which underwent Suzuki–Miyaura coupling with 5 to furnish 25 in 63% yield over three steps46. Chemo- and regioselective hydroboration of the C16–C17 olefin of 25 with BH3·Me2S, followed by sequential oxidation with NaBO3 and Jones reagent47, delivered diketone 26 in 53% yield. Dehydrogenation of 26 to introduce the C14–C15 olefin proved to be nontrivial because its deprotonation under basic conditions occurred preferentially at C17 rather than at C15. Eventually, we found that treatment of 26 with 4 equiv of tBuONa, followed by addition of PhSeBr, gave a C15 selenide, which was oxidized with 30% H2O2 and pyridine to yield enone 27 as a 3:2 mixture of diastereomers. Subjecting this mixture to epimerization with LHMDS and subsequent quenching with PivOH increased the diastereomeric ratio to 4.6:1. Corey–Bakshi–Shibata reduction of 27 and subsequent acetylation delivered C16β acetate 2820, which was smoothly elaborated to the target natural product 2 through epoxidation (N-bromosuccinimide, H2O) and deprotection (with tetra-n-butylammonium fluoride).

Biomimetic retro-aldol/transannular aldol cascade

As alluded to earlier, 1 might be derived from 2 through a BRAC reaction48–53 via the putative diketone intermediate 314. Previous work from the Li group suggested that the 7/5/6/5 core framework of 1 could be efficiently converted to the 5/7/6/5 core framework of 2 using DBU in refluxing THF20. Very recently, Nagorny and co-workers explored the reversibility of the transannular aldol reaction with DBU23. However, a systematic investigation into the BRAC reaction of 5,7-fused β-hydroxyl ketones with different stereocenters at C1 and C5 (1α,5α-, 1β,5α-,1β,5β-, etc.), which would provide more information on the interconversion and thermodynamic stability of these diastereomers, has not been reported. In addition, isolation of the hypothetical biosynthetic diketone intermediate similar to 314 and investigation of its BRAC reaction to access the 5/7/6/5 and 7/5/6/5 tetracyclic frameworks has remained to be disclosed.

We began our investigation by preparing the substrates for the BRAC reaction, i.e. the β-hydroxyl ketones 30 and 31, as well as 10-membered triketone 32 (Fig. 5). Thus, treating 13 with Me2AlCl triggered a trans-selective carbonyl–ene cyclization31, which proceeded via the conformational isomerization from “parallel” conformation IV to “crossed” conformation V to give 1β,5α-tertiary alcohol 29 exclusively in 87% yield (Fig. 5a). Ozonolysis of olefins 29 and 14 uneventfully afforded ketones 30 and 31 in excellent yields. We envisioned that regioselective oxidative cleavage of the C10–C19 bond of 12 would provide a facile access to the biosynthetic aldol precursor 32. Inspired by the seminal work of Baran and co-workers54, we subjected allylic alcohol (E)−12 to Mukaiyama’s conditions (Fe(acac)3, PhSiH3, and O2 in EtOH)55–58, which delivered triketone 32 in 56% yield. Notably, our work enabled a stereodivergent access to β-hydroxyl ketones 24, 30 and 31 with 1α,5α-, 1β,5α- and 1β,5β-stereocenters at C1 and C5, as well as facile synthesis of 10-membered triketone 32, thus setting the stage for exploring the crucial BRAC reaction (Fig. 5b). We were particularly interested in identifying a different base for this reaction other than DBU that had been previously employed20,23. Interestingly, we found that treatment of 24 with tetra-n-butylammonium fluoride in THF at 50 oC for 4 h furnished alcohols 33 and 34 bearing the 5/7/6/5 tetracyclic framework in 23% and 26% yields, respectively, along with alcohol 35 possessing the 7/5/6/5 tetracyclic framework in 20% yield (entry 1). The structures of both 34 and 35 were unambiguously confirmed by means of X-ray crystallographic analysis. We surmised that β-hydroxyl ketone 33, 34 or 35 might have formed via the chair-boat-chair conformation VI, chair-chair-chair conformation VII or chair-chair-chair conformation VIII, respectively. Importantly, the generation of 33 and 35 during the deprotection of 24 provided strong experimental evidence for the proposed biosynthetic relationship between 1 and 2 via a BRAC reaction14. Intriguingly, subjection of diastereomers 30 or 31 to the same conditions delivered 33 in good to excellent yields (entries 2, 3). In contrast to Nargony’s work that the 1α,5α-configured product (such as 34) was the thermodynamically most stable diastereomer, our results might arise from the stabilizing hydrogen bonding interaction between C3 and C5 hydroxyl groups of 33. Notably, triketone 32 could undergo the intramolecular aldol reaction to furnish 34 and 35 as main products, indicating that 32 exists mainly in the chair-chair-chair conformation (VII or VIII after deprotonation). Conceivably, a synthesis of bufogargarizin A (1) would be accomplished from 35 through the synthetic sequence outlined in Fig. 4.

Discussion

In summary, we have accomplished the synthesis of bufogargarizin B in 18 steps from dehydroepiandrosterone and constructed the core skeleton of bufogargarizin A via the BRAC reaction. This synthesis hinges on a conformation-controlled skeletal reorganization that is enabled by a stereocontrolled radical cyclization and a SmI2-mediated ketone-allylic acetate transannular cyclization, providing rapid access to the 5/7/6/5 tetracyclic core framework from the 6/6/6/5 ring system of classical steroids. Our results demonstrate that controlling the conformation of flexible medium-sized rings is crucial for the precise installation of desired stereocenters. In addition, our scalable synthesis of 2-pyrone-5-boronate (5), as well as the highly chemo- and regioselective redox transformations required for the installation of the highly functionalized D ring, can be expected to find application in the synthesis of other structurally complex bufadienolides. More importantly, our stereodivergent access to 5,7-fused ring systems with different stereocenters should also be applicable to the synthesis of other natural products via conformation-controlled transannular cyclization.

Methods

SmI2-mediated ketone–allylic acetate transannular cyclization

To a solution of (Z)−8 (30.0 mg, 0.05 mmol, 1.0 equiv) in dry THF (1.0 mL) was added HMPA (0.13 mL, 0.75 mmol, 15.0 equiv) at room temperature. After stirring for 10 min at –78 °C, SmI2 (2.3 mL, 0.1 M in THF, 2.3 mmol, 4.5 equiv) was added, and the resulting mixture was stirred at the same temperature for 0.5 h. The mixture was quenched with sat. aq. NaHCO3 (10 mL), and extracted with EtOAc (3 × 10 mL). The combined organic layers were washed with brine, dried over Na2SO4 and concentrated in vacuo. The residue was purified by flash chromatography (SiO2, 4:1 → 2:1 petroleum ether:EtOAc) to furnish compound 16 (19.4 mg, 72%) and compound 14 (4.6 mg, 17%) as white foams.
