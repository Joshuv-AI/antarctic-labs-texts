Red blood cell distribution width-to-albumin ratio as a novel predictor for mortality in breast cancer patients admitted to ICU: a retrospective analysis using MIMIC-IV 3.1
Authors: Chenyan Hong, Ke Yin, Shenchao Guo, Jin Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe red blood cell distribution width-to-albumin ratio (RAR), an emerging biomarker integrating inflammation and nutritional status, has not been systematically evaluated for its prognostic value in breast cancer patients admitted to intensive care units (ICU).MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from the MIMIC-IV 3.1 database, including 881 adult breast cancer patients admitted to the ICU. Patients were stratified into high- and low-RAR groups based on maximally selected rank statistics. Kaplan–Meier analysis, multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, restricted cubic splines, subgroup analysis, time-dependent concordance index (C-index) curves and Boruta feature selection (iterative random forest with shadow feature comparison) were applied to assess the association between RAR and 1-year all-cause mortality. Robustness was examined using E-value and propensity score weighting methods.ResultsPatients with high RAR (> 4.96) had significantly higher 1-year mortality compared to those with low RAR (log-rank P < 0.001). In adjusted models, high RAR independently predicted mortality (HR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.33–2.06). Across the four models, E-values for the point estimates ranged from 2.18 (fully adjusted model, HR 1.65) to 2.82 (unadjusted model, HR 2.19); the E-value for the lower confidence limit of the primary model was 1.73. Each standard deviation increase in RAR was associated with a 17% higher mortality risk (HR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.09–1.25). Restricted cubic spline analysis demonstrated a linear dose–response relationship (P for nonlinearity > 0.05). Incorporating RAR into SOFA and APS III scores improved prognostic performance (P < 0.001). Feature importance ranking further highlighted RAR as a major predictor. Sensitivity analyses using overlap-weighting (HR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.25–1.96) and matching-weighting (HR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.22–1.93) confirmed robustness.ConclusionRAR is an easily obtainable biomarker derived from routine laboratory testing that may enhance risk stratification for ICU-admitted breast cancer patients. Its integration into prognostic models may facilitate early decision support.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-026-03711-x.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignancies among women worldwide, representing a significant global health concern. According to GLOBOCAN 2020, breast cancer accounted for approximately 2.3 million new cases and 685,000 deaths globally, making it the most frequently diagnosed cancer and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality in women [1]. The increasing incidence and high treatment costs have placed a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where access to timely diagnosis and treatment is limited [2]. Despite advances in therapeutic strategies, predicting prognosis in breast cancer patients remains challenging. Traditional prognostic tools often rely on histopathological features and advanced molecular profiling, which may not be readily available or cost-effective in routine clinical practice. Therefore, there is a growing need for simple, accessible, and inexpensive biomarkers to aid in prognostication and guide personalized treatment.

The red cell distribution width-to-albumin ratio (RAR) has garnered growing attention as a prognostic biomarker reflecting the interplay between inflammation and nutritional status. While red cell distribution width (RDW) [3–5] and serum albumin [6–8] have been individually associated with survival outcomes, their combined ratio may offer improved prognostic utility due to its integration of two distinct but complementary physiological domains. In population-based studies, elevated RAR has been linked to increased risks of mortality and morbidity, particularly in older adults and patients with chronic conditions, where systemic inflammation and hypoalbuminemia frequently coexist [9]. Lu et al., utilizing data from the MIMIC-III database, demonstrated that an elevated RAR is independently associated with increased all-cause mortality among cancer patients [10]. RAR captures subtle shifts in erythropoiesis and protein metabolism that may not be apparent when RDW or albumin are assessed alone. In the context of oncology, and breast cancer specifically, this ratio may serve as a surrogate for tumor-induced systemic changes. Breast cancer progression is known to be influenced by a pro-inflammatory tumor microenvironment and host nutritional status, both of which are reflected in this composite index. Unlike molecular techniques or pathological features, RAR is inexpensive, routinely available, and does not require specialized equipment, making it particularly valuable in resource-limited settings.

Machine learning (ML), as a major field within artificial intelligence, demonstrates the capacity to extract key features from complex, multidimensional datasets. Consequently, it has found widespread application in creating diagnostic and predictive models for the accurate identification of numerous diseases [11]. The RandomForest algorithm is an ensemble learning technique that improves predictive accuracy and robustness through the aggregation of outputs from multiple decision trees, each trained on a distinct subset of samples. This decision tree-based method enabled the identification of the most influential variables [12]. Compared to conventional single-model approaches, Random Forest can accommodate high-dimensional covariate spaces, capture non-linear relationships and complex variable interactions without prior specification, and provide data-driven rankings of predictor importance, thereby facilitating the identification of clinically informative biomarkers [13].

To date, no studies have investigated the prognostic significance of RAR in breast cancer. Given that both elevated RDW and hypoalbuminemia have been independently associated with systemic inflammation and poor outcomes in various malignancies, exploring the potential correlation between RAR and breast cancer prognosis is warranted. Establishing such a relationship could offer a novel, cost-effective biomarker for risk stratification and treatment decision-making in clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective analysis was conducted using health-related data extracted from version 3.1 of the MIMIC-IV database from 2008 to 2022. The MIMIC- IV database is a large, publicly available critical care database that contains health data from intensive care unit (ICU) admissions, including patient demographics, vital signs, laboratory results, and clinical outcomes. The data is fully de-identified and its use does not impact the clinical treatment of the patients. Accordingly, this study complied with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was granted a waiver of institutional ethical approval and informed consent.

Breast cancer patients were identified from the MIMIC-IV database according to diagnostic codes based on the 9th and 10th revisions of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9 and ICD-10). Only a minimum ICU stay threshold of ≥ 24 h was applied; no upper limit on length of stay was imposed, ensuring that patients with prolonged ICU courses were not excluded. For individuals with multiple ICU admissions, only data from the first admission were included in the analysis. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) age under 18 years at the time of initial ICU admission; (b) missing RDW or albumin values on the first day of admission; and (c) ICU stay of less than 24 h. A total of 881 eligible patients were ultimately included and stratified into two groups according to RAR (Fig. 1).

Data collection

The following variables were primarily included in this study: demographic characteristics (age, gender), vital signs [respiratory rate, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, saturation of peripheral oxygen (SpO2)], comorbidities [hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, acute kidney injury (AKI) stage, heart failure, cirrhosis, sepsis, COVID-19], laboratory tests [white blood cell (WBC), hemoglobin, red cell distribution width (RDW), platelets, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin, albumin, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), glucose, anion gap, lactate dehydrogenase], disease severity scores [Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, Acute Physiology Score III (APS III), Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS), Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI)], continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), invasive ventilation, one-year mortality. The above variables obtained during the first 24 h following ICU admission were extracted for analysis. RAR was calculated using the following formula: RAR = red cell distribution width (RDW, %) divided by serum albumin (g/dL). The primary endpoint of the study was all-cause mortality within one year following hospital discharge.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1), with a significance level of p < 0.05. Continuous variables are expressed as mean±standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentage (%).

The optimal cut-off value of RAR was obtained for early warning of mortality risk, under the maximally selected rank statistics using the survminer package. To assess potential optimism arising from the data-derived threshold, the cut-off selection via maximally selected rank statistics and the subsequent Cox model were subjected to 1000 bootstrap resamples. We report the range (or interquartile range) of selected cut-off values and the optimism-corrected hazard ratio with 95% confidence interval, following similar bootstrap-validation approaches in ratio-based biomarker studies [14, 15]. Based on this cutoff, patients were categorized into high- and low-RAR groups. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Log rank test were subsequently constructed for each group. Baseline characteristics between groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, the independent t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables.

Regarding RAR as a continuous (increase per unit) or categorical variable, cox regression models with varying degrees of adjustment for potential confounders were employed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all-cause mortality. The selection of covariates was based on prior literature and clinical expertise, rather than data-driven approaches [16]. In Model 0, only RAR was included without any adjustment for covariates. Model 1 adjusted for age, gender, diabetes, hypertension, AKI stage, CKD, heart failure, sepsis3, cirrhosis and COVID-19 in patients. Model 2 adjusted for the covariates in Model 1 and WBC, hemoglobin, AST, total bilirubin, creatinine, BUN, anion gap, LDH, CRRT and ventilation. Model 3 adjusted for the covariates in Model 2, as well as SOFA, APS Ⅲ, OASIS and CHARLSON. Considering the MIMIC database lacks certain prognostic factors (e.g. tumor pathological staging), E-Value was calculated using the EValue package to assess potential unmeasured confounding [14]. The E-value for a given risk or rate ratio (RR) was calculated as: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:E\:value=RR+\sqrt{RR\times\:\left(RR-1\right)}\:\:$$\end{document}). For the confidence interval, the same formula was applied to the limit closest to the null (set to 1 if the interval crossed it). Because 1-year mortality is a common outcome, hazard ratios were first converted to approximate risk ratios before computing the E-value, following the approach implemented in the E-value R package. To avoid model overfitting, the variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to assess multicollinearity among all included covariates (Supplementary Fig. 1). Based on Model 3, restricted cubic spline analysis was employed to characterize the dose–response relationship between RAR and all-cause mortality, with threshold effect analysis conducted when appropriate [15]. Moreover, we conducted additional subgroup analyses and mediation analysis by adjusted Cox models.

Furthermore, a time-concordance index curve was generated to assess the incremental prognostic value of RAR for mortality. The Boruta feature selection algorithm (a wrapper around the random-forest classifier) was applied to assess the relative importance of RAR and other baseline variables in predicting 1-year all-cause mortality. At each iteration, it creates ‘shadow’ features by randomly permuting copies of all real predictors, fits a random forest on the combined set, and retains only those real predictors whose importance significantly exceeds the maximum importance among the shadow features across all iterations. Because random forests can distribute importance among strongly correlated predictors, the resulting ranking should be interpreted qualitatively rather than as a strict unique ordering. Finally, to enhance the robustness of the analysis results, sensitivity analyses were conducted using two alternative propensity score (PS) weighting approaches: overlap-weighting and matching-weighting.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 881 patients with breast cancer were ultimately enrolled in this study. Based on an optimal cutoff value of 4.96 for the RAR (Fig. 2A), patients were stratified into the high RAR group (n = 472) and the low RAR group (n = 409) (Fig. 1). The clinical characteristics differed partially between the two groups. Notably, although patients in the high RAR group were younger than those in the low RAR group, they exhibited a higher 1-year all-cause mortality rate (Table 1).

Survival analysis

According to the Kaplan–Meier curves, patients with a high RAR demonstrated a significantly higher all-cause mortality rate (log-rank P < 0.001, Fig. 2B).

Four Cox regression models were constructed to evaluate the prognostic impact of RAR in this population (Table 2). The variance inflation factors (VIFs) for all variables included in the models were less than 5 (Supplementary Figure. 1), indicating no significant multicollinearity. In Model 3, after full adjustment for covariates, the high RAR group (compared with the low RAR group) had a hazard ratio of 1.65 (95% CI: 1.33–2.06) for 1-year all-cause mortality. Similar results were observed in Model 0 (HR:2.19, 95% CI: 1.81–2.65), Model 1 (HR:1.95, 95% CI: 1.59–2.38), and Model 2 (HR:1.83, 95% CI: 1.47–2.27). When RAR was analyzed as a continuous variable, each standard deviation increase in RAR was associated with a 17% higher risk of all-cause mortality after multivariable adjustment (HR:1.17, 95% CI: 1.09–1.25) (Table 2). Across the four models, all E-values were greater than 2, indicating that only unmeasured confounders with risk ratios of at least the corresponding E-values could fully account for the observed associations, thereby reinforcing the robustness of our results. Meanwhile, Bootstrap validation (1000 resamples) yielded a median cut-off of 4.96 (IQR: 4.79 to 5.02), with an optimism-corrected HR of 1.52 (95% CI: 1.29–1.922), consistent with the primary estimate.

Restricted cubic splines were fitted with three knots placed at the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles of the RAR distribution, following Harrell’s recommendation. The reference value was set at the median RAR. Thus, a positive and linear association was observed between RAR and HR for 1-year all-cause mortality (p for nonlinear > 0.05) (Fig. 3A). The solid line represents the estimated log hazard ratio and the shaded area denotes the 95% confidence interval.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis with stratification was performed to investigate the association between RAR and 1-year all-cause mortality, along with an evaluation of potential interactions. The observed positive relationship remained consistent across subgroups, including age, hypertension, diabetes, CKD, heart failure, sepsis, and ventilation (all p for interaction > 0.05) (Fig. 3B). The aforementioned factors did not have a significant impact on the relationship between RAR and 1-year all-cause mortality.

The value of predicting prognosis

Time-dependent analysis indicated that the inclusion of RAR significantly enhanced the prognostic value derived from SOFA and APS Ⅲ (Fig. 4A), and the concordance index increased from 0.610 to 0.640 for SOFA, from 0.632 to 0.653 for APS Ⅲ (p < 0.001 for both) (Supplementary Table 1). Bootstrap analysis (1000 resamples) confirmed that the C-index improvement was statistically significant: ΔC-index for SOFA + RAR vs. SOFA alone = 0.030 (95% CI 0.014–0.052, P < 0.001); ΔC-index for APS III + RAR vs. APS III alone = 0.021 (95% CI 0.008–0.041, P < 0.001).

Model performance was further evaluated by (i) the time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (time-dependent AUC) for discrimination; (ii) calibration plots comparing predicted versus observed 1-year survival probabilities for calibration; and (iii) decision curve analysis (DCA) comparing net clinical benefit across threshold probabilities for models with and without RAR (Supplementary Figure. 3). We note that the Hosmer–Lemeshow test is designed for logistic regression and was therefore not applied to our Cox survival models; instead, calibration was assessed graphically using the standard calibration-curve approach for time-to-event data.

To assess the importance of predictors for 1-year all-cause mortality, the Boruta algorithm, based on random forest methodology, was utilized. For the Boruta analysis, the 1-year mortality event rate was 53.1%, and no additional class-balancing technique was applied. Random forests underlying the Boruta algorithm are relatively tolerant of moderate class imbalance, and feature importance was judged against shadow features rather than absolute classification accuracy, mitigating the impact of outcome imbalance on the ranking. The feature importance plot demonstrated that RAR emerged as a key contributor to outcome prediction, second only to the CCI (Fig. 4B).

To complement the Boruta importance ranking and clarify the direction and magnitude of each predictor’s contribution, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were computed for the fitted random-forest model. A SHAP summary (beeswarm) plot and a SHAP dependence plot illustrating all predictors were generated (Supplementary Figure. 2).

Exploratory mediation analysis: Because RAR is the ratio of RDW to albumin, decomposing its effect into pathways mediated by its own numerator and denominator does not fully satisfy standard mediation assumptions. The following analysis is therefore exploratory and hypothesis-generating only. Mediation analysis revealed that RDW exhibited a significant mediating effect (P = 0.010), while albumin demonstrated a masking effect (P = 0.044) between RAR and mortality risk. Nevertheless, RAR made a significant contribution to the total effect (P < 0.01 for both) (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Figure. 4).

Sensitivity analysis

To further assess the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted using two distinct propensity score weighting approaches. The use of overlap-weighting successfully balanced 34 baseline covariates between the two groups (absolute standardized mean difference < 0.1) (Fig. 5A), ensuring comparability for subsequent analysis. Survival analysis with overlap-weighting revealed that, compared to the low RAR group, the high RAR group had a 1-year all-cause mortality hazard ratio of 1.56 (95% CI:1.25–1.96, p < 0.001) (Fig. 5B). Similar conclusions were obtained when the match-weighting method was applied, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.53 (95% CI:1.22–1.93, p < 0.001) for high RAR compared to low RAR (Fig. 5C and D).

Discussion

Our study suggests that an elevated RAR may serve as a potential adverse prognostic factor in breast cancer patients admitted to the ICU, particularly in resource-limited settings. The significant correlation between higher RAR levels and 1-year all-cause mortality persists even after controlling for confounding variables. Given its accessibility and low cost as part of routine hematological testing, RAR may represent a novel and reliable biomarker for identifying breast cancer patients in ICU at elevated risk of poor prognosis in clinical practice.

The RDW has recently been recognized as a potentially significant predictive and prognostic factor in breast cancer. An association was observed between increased RDW and a heightened risk of breast cancer, and RDW was proposed as a useful marker for differentiating malignant from benign breast lesions [17]. Elevated after-treatment RDW was associated with poor outcomes including overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) in patients with breast cancer [18]. A study involving 1,783 patients demonstrated that relatively high preoperative RDW (> 13.5) and CA 15 − 3 levels (> 11.4) exhibited the strongest predictive value for mortality and recurrence, respectively, among hematological markers [19]. Yao et al. found that higher RDW levels were associated with larger tumor size and an increased incidence of lymph node metastasis, suggesting that RDW might serve as a surrogate indicator of tumor aggressiveness [20]. Moreover, elevated RDW was related not only with more advanced tumor stages but also with increased levels of inflammatory markers, highlighting the contribution of systemic inflammation to cancer prognosis [21]. As a simple and cost-effective prognostic marker for adverse outcomes in breast cancer patients, RDW facilitates early risk stratification and timely clinical intervention. In addition to RDW, serum albumin has been extensively investigated in relation to cancer prognosis. Hypoalbuminemia, defined as a reduced serum albumin concentration, often reflects poor nutritional status and systemic inflammation, both of which may contribute to increased morbidity, more frequent complications, and prolonged hospitalization [22]. Lower albumin levels have been correlated with more advanced disease stages and increased mortality in patients with colon and breast cancer [23–25]. These findings suggest that monitoring of albumin may offer valuable prognostic information, serving as a simple indicator of a patient’s overall health, nutritional condition, and risk of adverse clinical outcomes. Meanwhile, although we employed multivariable adjustment, propensity-score weighting, E-value quantification, and dose-response analysis–all of which support a robust association–the observational design precludes causal conclusions. Reverse causation (e.g., greater illness severity driving higher RAR rather than RAR causally increasing mortality) cannot be excluded. We therefore frame RAR as an independent prognostic marker rather than a proven causal driver of mortality. Future Mendelian randomization or prospective interventional studies would be required to establish causality.

The combination of RDW and albumin into a single metric, known as RAR, has attracted increasing interest as a potential prognostic indicator. Population-based studies demonstrated that a higher baseline RAR was associated with an increased risk of all-cause and cause-specific mortality [9, 10, 26], suggesting its universal role in inflammation-driven pathologies. However, studies specifically investigating the association between RAR and prognosis in breast cancer patients are currently lacking. Our study fills this gap in the literature. In this retrospective cohort study, breast cancer patients in ICU with a high RAR showed a significantly increased risk of 1-year all-cause mortality, with restricted cubic spline analysis confirming a dose-response relationship. The linear association between RAR and mortality may stem from its dual reflection of chronic inflammation (elevated RDW indicating oxidative stress [27] and immune dysfunction [28]) and malnutrition (hypoalbuminemia impairing immune response and tissue repair [29]). In breast cancer, systemic inflammation promotes tumor progression via NF-κB-mediated cytokine release [30], while hypoalbuminemia exacerbates chemotherapy toxicity and reduces treatment tolerance [31]. In critically ill breast cancer patients, several overlapping pathological processes may amplify the prognostic value of RAR. First, tumor-driven systemic inflammation elevates RDW through impaired erythropoiesis, increased oxidative stress, and chronic cytokine exposure. Second, cytotoxic chemotherapy and radiotherapy induce additional bone-marrow suppression and erythrocyte dysregulation, further increasing RDW beyond levels observed in cancer patients not receiving treatment. Third, the catabolic state of critical illness, compounded by frequent severe infections and evolving multi-organ dysfunction, accelerates hypoalbuminemia through increased capillary permeability, reduced hepatic synthesis, and heightened catabolism. By capturing these inflammatory, nutritional, and organ-dysfunction signals within a single ratio, RAR may reflect the cumulative physiological burden unique to this population–potentially explaining its strong prognostic performance relative to either component alone. In our study, we performed Cox regression analysis with adjustment for covariates, conducted subgroup analyses and calculated the E-value to minimize the potential impact of unmeasured confounding. We found that the adverse effect of RAR on prognosis was consistent. Moreover, our findings extend prior work in two directions. Population-based and ICU-based studies have linked elevated RAR to higher all-cause mortality across mixed malignancies and in general critically ill populations. A study reported that RAR was independently associated with mortality in a heterogeneous cancer ICU cohort; our study refines this observation by demonstrating a similar association specifically in breast cancer patients, with a fully adjusted HR of 1.65 that is broadly consistent with the magnitudes reported in other cancer types. Separately, studies in general (non-ICU) breast cancer cohorts have individually linked elevated RDW and hypoalbuminemia to poorer survival. Our work extends these observations by demonstrating the added value of combining these two markers into a single ratio and evaluating it within the specific clinical context of ICU admission, where acute physiological derangement may amplify the prognostic information carried by each component. Our results suggest that RAR may enhance the prognostic predictive value of the SOFA or APSⅢ score. To validate the robustness of the prognostic value of RAR, we further employed a ridge plot of feature importance and propensity score methods (overlap-weighting and matching-weighting) to confirm our findings. Based on these observations, we consider RAR to be a reliable prognostic factor in clinical practice.

This study has several notable strengths. First, to the best of our knowledge, no prior studies have specifically investigated the association between RAR and prognosis in breast cancer patients admitted to ICU, addressing an important and previously underexplored research gap. Second, we applied the feature importance ridge plot to quantify the prognostic contribution of RAR, a relatively underused yet informative approach in clinical prognostic modeling. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. A key limitation of this study is that the MIMIC-IV database does not record tumor-specific prognostic information, including TNM stage, molecular subtype (ER/PR/HER-2 status), and oncologic treatment regimens. These variables are likely associated with both RAR and mortality and may act as residual confounders. Although we mitigated this through multivariable adjustment, propensity-score weighting, and E-value quantification–demonstrating that an unmeasured confounder would need a risk ratio exceeding 2.18 to fully explain the observed association–bias from unmeasured oncologic factors cannot be excluded. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Additionally, the patient population was derived from the MIMIC database and limited to breast cancer patients admitted to the ICU, which may limit the generalizability of our results to the broader breast cancer population. Furthermore, the absence of RDW and albumin means that only a portion of breast cancer patients are included in the analysis, which introduces potential selection bias. Although we attempted external validation using the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD), the limited number of eligible patients reduced the statistical power of the analysis. In that analysis in the eICU-CRD (n = 242), the hazard ratio for high versus low RAR was 1.11 (95% CI: 0.89 to 1.37); this analysis was underpowered and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Validation in larger, prospective, multicenter cohorts–ideally including offline real-world data–remains essential to establish broader applicability. Future studies could further investigate longitudinal trajectories of biomarkers [32].

Conclusion

In ICU-admitted breast cancer patients, a higher RAR was independently associated with increased 1-year all-cause mortality. As a routine laboratory parameter, RAR may aid risk stratification; however, prospective studies incorporating tumor stage, molecular subtype, and treatment data are needed to confirm and extend these associations.
