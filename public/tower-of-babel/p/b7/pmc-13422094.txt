Things We Do for No Reason™: Withholding opioids among patients with acute pain and opioid use disorder
Authors: Anna‐Maria South, Susan L. Calcaterra, Marlene Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHospitalists care for patients with acute pain who have an opioid use disorder and require pain management. Hospitalists may withhold opioids for acute pain due to a variety of reasons. However, adequate pain management in patients with opioid use disorder, including with opioids, is the standard of care. This piece explains how to best manage acute pain in hospitalized patients with opioid use disorder with supportive evidence and discharge planning considerations.

CLINICAL SCENARIO

After undergoing a fasciotomy for compartment syndrome, a 37‐year‐old man is admitted to hospital medicine for a skin and soft tissue infection. On hospital day 2, the patient is writhing in pain with dilated pupils, yawning, and has gooseflesh skin. He endorses daily intravenous fentanyl use. After obtaining additional history, the hospitalist diagnoses the patient with opioid use disorder (OUD). The patient requests opioids for pain, but the hospitalist worries about exacerbating the patient's OUD and instead schedules acetaminophen and ibuprofen. Later, the nurse finds the patient using fentanyl in his room, and the patient requests to self‐direct his discharge.

WHY YOU MIGHT THINK WITHHOLDING OPIOIDS AMONG PATIENTS WITH ACUTE PAIN AND OUD IS NECESSARY

Hospitalists may withhold opioids among patients with acute pain and OUD due to fears of worsening the OUD, knowledge gaps, regulatory concerns, stigma, and challenges with coordinating discharge plans. Some hospitalists believe that withholding opioids, even for severe pain, reduces the risk of exacerbating OUD. 1 This perception has been shaped by the tightening of opioid prescribing practices after prescription opioids were recognized as drivers of the “first wave“ of the US opioid epidemic. 2 US healthcare quality measures promoting opioid stewardship have further encouraged hospitalists to reduce opioid prescribing broadly. 3

Given national recommendations to offer and initiate methadone and buprenorphine, gold‐standard medications for OUD (MOUD), hospitalists may withhold non‐MOUD opioids (e.g., oxycodone, hydromorphone) in favor of MOUD initiation or continuation. 4 Hospitalists may worry that prescribing opioids for acute pain in patients on MOUD could destabilize recovery and lead to return to opioid use, cause oversedation, 5 or, in the case of buprenorphine, precipitate withdrawal. 6

Concerns about the legality of prescribing opioids for patients with OUD could impact hospitalists' prescribing decisions as federal regulations prohibit outpatient prescribing of full‐agonist opioids for opioid withdrawal and OUD. 7 In contrast, federal regulation permits opioid administration for opioid withdrawal and OUD management among hospitalized patients with medical or surgical conditions other than addiction. 7

Stigma towards patients who are labeled as “drug seeking” also influences a hospitalist's decision to avoid opioids if hospitalists perceive that patients exaggerate their pain to coerce hospitalists to prescribe opioids. 8

Finally, hospitalists may avoid prescribing opioids so that they do not have to de‐escalate them before discharge.

WHY WITHHOLDING OPIOIDS AMONG PATIENTS WITH ACUTE PAIN AND OUD IS UNNECESSARY AND POTENTIALLY HARMFUL

Prescribing opioids for acute pain in hospitalized patients with OUD is safe, improves patient experience, and aligns with regulatory standards. 9 Because patients with OUD have a higher opioid tolerance than those without OUD, they often require higher doses of opioids to manage acute pain. 5 For patients not on MOUD with acute pain, baseline opioid requirements must be met before acute pain can be relieved. One study demonstrated that opioid doses of 200–320 mg morphine equivalents over 24 h did not increase oversedation or other harms when used to manage opioid withdrawal among hospitalized patients with OUD. 10 Regardless of MOUD status, patients with OUD experiencing acute pain refractory to acetaminophen or nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) will benefit from opioids, including oxycodone and hydromorphone. 5 , 11 , 12 When therapeutically dosed, MOUD provides 24‐h withdrawal suppression but only 4–8 h of analgesia; dosing methadone or buprenorphine two or three times daily during hospitalization can enhance pain control. 5

Treating acute pain with opioids does not worsen OUD. 5 , 13 Kantor et al. found that hospitalized patients with OUD on methadone who received opioids for acute pain had similar post‐discharge methadone retention rates compared to community‐matched patients with OUD on methadone. 14 Another study among outpatients on MOUD who received opioids for cancer‐related pain had similar findings. 5 In contrast, inadequately treated pain or opioid withdrawal in patients with OUD contributes to higher rates of self‐directed discharge and in‐hospital unprescribed opioid use, and strains the patient‐healthcare worker relationship. 3 , 8 Withholding opioids can also reduce opioid tolerance in patients already facing high morbidity and mortality; one study found that 7.8% of patients with OUD died in the year following hospital discharge. 15 Another study found an acute increase in overdose risk within 2 weeks after hospital discharge. 16

WHAT YOU SHOULD DO INSTEAD

Hospitalists should use a multimodal approach to treat acute pain in patients with OUD. 5 Non‐opioid analgesics (e.g., acetaminophen, NSAIDs, lidocaine patches) should be offered first, followed by opioids such as oxycodone or hydromorphone at doses adjusted for opioid tolerance when non‐opioid analgesics provide insufficient analgesia. 1 , 4 For patients on buprenorphine, hydromorphone, or fentanyl are preferred due to their high opioid receptor affinity. 11 When available, hospitalists should engage an acute pain service and/or anesthesia service to support advanced strategies such as patient‐controlled analgesia pumps, nerve blocks, or ketamine infusions. Hospitalists should also validate patients' pain and create collaborative plans, recognizing that patients with OUD often require higher doses of opioids. Monitoring and treating both opioid withdrawal and acute pain are essential, and concerns about oversedation can be addressed with appropriate hold parameters and continuous monitoring when indicated.

Alongside treating acute pain, hospitalists should continue or initiate MOUD. 11 Continuing MOUD prevents opioid withdrawal, which can worsen acute pain and destabilize patients in their recovery. 6 , 11 Patients on therapeutic buprenorphine doses for OUD treatment (16–32 mg) will not experience withdrawal when short‐acting opioids like oxycodone or hydromorphone are added for acute pain. 6 Hospitalists should continue methadone in patients enrolled in an opioid treatment program unless there are medical contraindications (e.g., coma, impaired consciousness, known or suspected gastrointestinal obstruction). In contrast to buprenorphine, methadone will never cause precipitated withdrawal. 17 Methadone and buprenorphine both provide analgesia and can be administered in split doses to leverage their analgesic effects during hospitalization. However, maintenance doses of buprenorphine and methadone are insufficient to treat acute pain.

Given the potency of synthetic opioids that dominate the drug supply, hospitalists should routinely offer and initiate methadone or buprenorphine for patients not engaged in OUD treatment. Hospitalists may have more success if they offer “as needed” short‐acting opioids (e.g., oxycodone, hydromorphone) when initiating MOUD. 10 To avoid precipitated withdrawal when initiating buprenorphine, hospitalists can use low‐dose buprenorphine initiation protocols or emerging injection protocols using extended‐release buprenorphine. 11 , 18 In the hospital, low‐dose buprenorphine initiation protocols often involve the use of oxycodone or hydromorphone to ensure acute pain and opioid withdrawal symptoms are treated while buprenorphine doses are titrated. Similarly, co‐use of short‐acting opioids with methadone initiation provides patients with analgesia for breakthrough pain or withdrawal symptoms. 19 In a small study of 23 patients at one hospital, MOUD at discharge increased from 33% to 65% in the same cohort of patients following implementation of a short‐acting opioid agonist treatment withdrawal protocol among patients with OUD. 10

When opioids are used for acute pain during hospitalization, hospitalists should set clear expectations for tapering non‐MOUD opioids given the patient's anticipated course of pain based on their medical or surgical diagnoses. For patients needing short‐term opioids after discharge and planning to continue MOUD, hospitalists should arrange a warm handoff to the outpatient clinician and coordinate an opioid taper plan that includes MOUD continuation. 20 If no outpatient clinician is available, hospitalists can prescribe a short course of opioids for acute pain alongside methadone or buprenorphine linkage. 21

If a patient plans to return to illicit opioid use after discharge, non‐MOUD opioids and/or MOUD should be used in the hospital to manage withdrawal and pain, but hospitalists should neither taper opioids nor prescribe them at discharge. 11 Figure 1 illustrates what should be done to manage acute pain for patients with OUD during hospitalization and at discharge.

CONCLUSION

Returning to the clinical scenario, the hospitalist discusses the patient's in‐hospital substance use. The patient explains that uncontrolled pain and withdrawal led him to use fentanyl and expresses a desire to continue medical treatment in the hospital. The hospitalist prescribes multimodal analgesia with scheduled acetaminophen, ibuprofen, and as‐needed oxycodone and intravenous hydromorphone. After further discussion about MOUD, the patient proceeds with a low‐dose buprenorphine initiation. The patient is discharged on buprenorphine–naloxone 8–2 mg three times daily and a 3‐day oxycodone taper coordinated with his outpatient clinician, whom he sees the following week to continue buprenorphine treatment.
