Discovery of biomarkers for primary Sj ögren’s syndrome based on multi-omics data, construction of diagnostic models, and clinical correlation analysis
Authors: Le Qiang, Nan Wang, Yuhan Jia, Jiahui Xue, Yue Jin, Lei Sun, Xiaohan Ni, Yanlin Wang, Min Feng, Chong Gao, Jing Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePrimary Sjogren's syndrome(pSS) exhibits significant clinical heterogeneity, which poses challenges for accurately assessing disease activity, predicting organ involvement, and diagnosing seronegative patients(SNSS). This study aimed to delineate the metabolic landscape of pSS by integrating metabolomic, lipidomic, and multi-dimensional clinical data to identify novel biomarkers for these purposes and to uncover the intrinsic links between metabolic dysregulation and immune dysfunction.MethodsUntargeted metabolomic and lipidomic analyses were performed on plasma samples from a discovery cohort and an independent validation cohort. A machine learning-based metabolic model was developed using selected features, and its diagnostic performance was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis.ResultsCompared with healthy controls(HC), pSS patients exhibited significant alterations in 65 metabolites from the metabolomic analysis and 63 lipids from the lipidomic analysis, indicating systemic metabolic pathway disruptions.Network analysis revealed extensive correlations of metabolomic/lipidomic profiles with clinical parameters and immune cell subsets. A panel of four biomarkers was identified and validated, demonstrating high efficacy in distinguishing SNSS. Furthermore, the levels of redox-related metabolites were significantly associated with age, sex, and anti-SSA antibody status. Seven biomarkers showed significant correlations with the EULAR Sjögren’s Syndrome Disease Activity Index(ESSDAI). Specific metabolic signatures were also identified for different organ involvement phenotypes, achieving AUC values of 0.763 and 0.871 for predicting pulmonary and hematological involvement, respectively.ConclusionThis study systematically defines specific metabolic features of pSS, establishes a validated diagnostic model for SNSS, and confirms the close association between metabolic disturbances and clinical heterogeneity. The findings provide novel metabolic biomarkers and insights for the precise diagnosis and management of pSS.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13075-026-03855-1.

Introduction

Primary Sjögren’s syndrome(pSS) is a chronic systemic inflammatory disease that predominantly affects middle-aged women, with an estimated prevalence ranging from 0.5% to 1% of the total population [1, 2]. pSS is generally classified as a disease of the exocrine glands, characterized by lymphocyte infiltration and autoimmune damage to the salivary and lacrimal glands, which can results in progressive glandular dysfunction, xerostomia, and dry eyes [3]. Currently, diagnostic testing methods for pSS include the non-stimulated saliva secretion test and the Schirmer test to assess glandular function, salivary gland biopsy (LSGB) to evaluate autoimmune activity, and serological testing to detect specific antibodies, such as anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB antibodies [4]. However, these autoantibodies are only present in 50% to 75% of pSS patients and can also be found in other systemic autoimmune diseases, which limits their specificity and positive predictive values [5]. Early identification and monitoring of pSS progression are crucial to prevent irreversible damage. Therefore, it is essential to develop reliable diagnostic biomarkers for evaluating seronegative pSS [5].

The chronic inflammatory state of pSS can drive significant metabolic alterations with important clinical implications [6]. As emerging systems biology approaches, metabolomics and lipidomics are increasingly employed to evaluate metabolic and lipid disturbances in human diseases. Utilizing untargeted or targeted strategies, these methodologies enable the efficient discovery of potential candidate biomarkers associated with the disease. Following rigorous validation in independent cohorts, such candidate biomarkers hold promise for application in disease diagnosis, activity assessment, and treatment efficacy monitoring [7, 8]. A study utilizing untargeted metabolomics methods found that pSS patients can be distinguished from healthy controls (HC) by 21 significant metabolites, including alanine, tryptophan, and glycolic acid [9]. Additionally, a study based on untargeted lipidomics identified three metabolites [phosphatidylcholine (18:0/22:5), triglycerides (16:0/18:00/18:1), and acylcarnitine (12:0)] were identified as specific biomarkers for pSS [10]. These findings suggest that metabolomics and lipidomics methods, particularly those employing ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS), are essential tools for discovering potential biomarkers and understanding metabolic remodeling in chronic diseases, including pSS [11, 12]. Although these biomarkers can facilitate more accurate disease diagnosis and dynamic monitoring, their full clinical utility has not yet been realized.

In this study, we employed untargeted metabolomics and lipidomics to systematically analyze the plasma metabolomic and lipidomic profiles (MLP) of patients with pSS.We performed correlation analyses among MLP, clinical parameters, and immune function indicators. By integrating univariate and multivariate statistical analyses with machine learning models, we identified a panel of key metabolites and successfully pinpointed specific potential biomarkers capable of seronegative patients (SNSS), patients with xerostomia, and HC. Furthermore, to elucidate disease heterogeneity, we stratified pSS patients based on the EULAR Sjögren’s Syndrome Disease Activity Index (ESSDAI) [13]and organ involvement to explore the key metabolic pathways associated with disease progression. This study enhances our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying the onset and progression of pSS, establishing a crucial foundation for optimizing the diagnosis, classification, prognosis evaluation, and personalized treatment of the disease.

Methods

Clinical sample collection

This study employed a discovery cohort and an independent validation cohort design. Between May 2016 and October 2021, 204 participants were enrolled in the discovery cohort, comprising 154 patients with pSS and 50 HC, for initial biomarker screening. An independent validation cohort consisted of 133 participants, including 48 pSS patients, 60 patients with xerostomia, and 25 HC, for subsequent validation of candidate biomarkers. All pSS patients were diagnosed according to the 2016 ACR–EULAR classification criteria [14], and disease activity was assessed using the ESSDAI [13]. Patients with xerostomia were defined as individuals presenting with unexplained dry mouth symptoms not fulfilling the pSS classification criteria and without systemic autoimmune diseases [15, 16]. Exclusion criteria included comorbid autoimmune or metabolic diseases, pregnancy or lactation, antibiotic use within the past four weeks, or a history of surgical trauma. Age- and sex-matched HC were recruited from the health screening center of the same hospital, confirmed to be in good health without relevant subjective symptoms. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Shanxi Medical University (Approval No. 2022YX063), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Following an overnight fast, venous blood samples were collected from all participants in the morning. Within 2 h of collection, the samples were centrifuged promptly to isolate plasma, which was then stored at − 80 ℃. To preserve sample integrity, all plasma aliquots underwent only a single freeze-thaw cycle prior to metabolomic and lipidomic analyses.

Plasma sample pretreatment and quality control

Plasma samples were thawed at 4 °C before the extraction of polar metabolites and lipids, following the workflow detailed in Supplementary Fig. 1. For polar metabolite extraction, proteins were precipitated using cold methanol. Briefly, plasma was mixed with pre-chilled methanol, vortexed, sonicated, and incubated at − 20 °C to facilitate complete protein precipitation. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, vacuum-dried, reconstituted in a methanol-water solution, recentrifuged, and the final supernatant was used for analysis. Lipid extraction was performed using a methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) liquid-liquid extraction method. Plasma was mixed with extraction solvent, vortexed, sonicated, incubated at − 20 °C, and centrifuged to collect the upper organic phase. The organic phase was dried, reconstituted in a resuspension solution, centrifuged, and the supernatant was used for subsequent analysis. Specific reagent volumes, incubation times, temperatures, and centrifugation parameters are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

To systematically monitor the quality and stability throughout the analytical process, quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal aliquots from each individual sample. These QC samples underwent the identical pretreatment and instrumental analysis procedures as the experimental samples.

UHPLC-MS/MS analysis

Untargeted metabolomic and lipidomic analyses were performed using a SCIEX ExionLCTM AD ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system coupled with a TripleTOF 5600 + high-resolution mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation for both metabolites and lipids was achieved on a single ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) installed within the ExionLC AD system, operating under reversed-phase (RP) gradient conditions.

Metabolomics analysis conditions are as follows: the column temperature was maintained at 35 °C with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Mobile phase A was water-acetonitrile (9:1, v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B was acetonitrile-water (9:1, v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid. A 33-minute gradient elution program was used. For lipidomics: conditions were optimized separately for positive and negative ionization modes. The column temperature was set to 45 °C for positive and negative mode, with a constant flow rate of 0.25 mL/min for both. Mobile phase A was acetonitrile-water (6:4, v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B was isopropanol-acetonitrile (9:1, v/v) containing 10mM ammonium acetate. A 32-minute gradient elution was applied. Detailed gradient programs are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Mass spectrometric data were acquired in both positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes. Key parameters were set as follows. For metabolomics: the spray voltage was 5500 V for positive mode and − 4500 V for negative mode; the ion source temperature was 550 °C for both; the curtain gas, ion source gas 1, and ion source gas 2 pressures were set to 40, 55, and 55 psi, respectively. For lipidomics: the spray voltages were 4500 V (positive) and − 4500 V (negative); the ion source temperatures were 450 °C (positive) and 550 °C (negative); gas pressure settings were identical to metabolomics. Complete parameters are listed in Supplementary Table 3. The mass scan ranges were set to m/z 50–1200 (full scan MS) and m/z 25–1000 (MS/MS) for metabolomics, and m/z 100–1500 (full scan MS) and m/z 50–1500 (MS/MS) for lipidomics.

A systematic quality control strategy was implemented throughout the data acquisition sequence. This included injecting blank samples prior to plasma sample analysis to assess and subtract system background noise, using a randomized sample order for analysis, and inserting a QC sample after every 10 experimental samples to continuously monitor and correct for potential signal drift, thereby ensuring data reliability and reproducibility.

Data preprocessing and compound identification

Raw mass spectrometry data were acquired in profile mode. All raw data files (.wiff format) were converted to the open mzML format using the MSConvert [17, 18] utility within the ProteoWizard software suite (v3.0.21295) [19] to ensure data compatibility and reusability. Subsequent preprocessing and feature extraction were performed using the XCMS Rpackage (v3.20.0) [20, 21]. The preprocessing workflow encompassed chromatographic peak detection, integration, and retention time correction. The detailed parameters for these steps are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

A pooled QC sample was analyzed intermittently throughout the analytical sequence. Metabolite features were retained for subsequent statistical analysis only if they exhibited a relative standard deviation (RSD) of less than 30% in peak area across all QC injections. All experimental samples in this study were prepared and analyzed in a single, continuous UHPLC-MS batch under identical instrumental conditions. To assess potential intra-batch signal drift, the retention time and peak intensity stability of the QC samples were monitored throughout the sequence. Prior to multivariate analysis, data were log-transformed and mean-centered.As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, the tight clustering of QC samples in the two-dimensional PCA score plot indicates excellent system stability and reproducibility. Minor retention time shifts within the analytical run were corrected using the retcor function in XCMS.

Compound identification was performed by matching experimentally acquired MS/MS spectra against reference spectra in the MS-DIAL [22], Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) [23], PubChem [24], and the commercial database ChemDataSolution [25]. The confidence of annotation was rigorously assigned according to the hierarchical system proposed by Schymanski et al. [26]. The majority of metabolites and a subset of lipids (e.g., phospholipids, sphingolipids) were annotated with Level 2b (putative structure) confidence. This assignment was based on the following evidence: (1) accurate precursor ion mass (mass error < 15 ppm) obtained from high-resolution mass spectrometry; (2) MS/MS spectra acquired in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode; and (3) spectral matching against databases with a reverse dot product threshold of > 0.8 [27, 28]. Representative mirror plots (e.g., butterfly diagrams) showing the match between experimental and database spectra for key metabolites, along with their similarity scores, are provided in Supplementary Fig. 3. For a subset of lipids (e.g., fatty acids and glycerolipids) where high-quality MS/MS spectra were not triggered or were insufficient, annotations were based solely on accurate precursor ion mass and are reported with Level 4 (confident molecular formula) confidence.Pathway enrichment analysis of the identified metabolites was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway database [29]and the clusterProfiler R package [30].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in the R environment (version 4.4.0) [31]. During data preprocessing, metabolites with a missing rate exceeding 20% across all samples were first removed to ensure data reliability. Subsequently, missing values in the remaining data were imputed using the random forest [32] algorithm implemented in the mice package [33].Differentially expressed metabolites were identified by combining univariate and multivariate statistical analyses. First, a two-sample t-test was performed, and the resulting p-values were adjusted for the false discovery rate (FDR) using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Multivariate analysis involved Orthogonal Projections to Latent Structures-Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) performed using the ropls package [34]. Metabolites meeting all the following criteria were considered potential biomarkers: variable importance in projection (VIP) score from OPLS-DA > 1, fold change (FC) > 1.5 or < 0.67, and FDR-adjusted p-value < 0.05.

Associations between metabolite levels and clinical parameters were assessed using Spearman’s and Pearson’s correlation analyses. Significant correlations (adjusted p < 0.05 and |r| > 0.2) were used to construct co-occurrence networks, which were visualized using Gephi software [35].For diagnostic model construction, machine learning models were built. First, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, implemented with the glmnet package [36], was used for feature selection. The optimal regularization parameter (λ) was determined via 10-fold cross-validation, selecting the λ value that minimized the mean squared error (λ.min). Subsequently, a Random Forest(RF) model was built using the selected features via the randomForest package [37]. Hyperparameters (such as mtry, with a search range from 1 to the square root of the total number of features) were optimized using 10-fold cross-validation with a grid search implemented in the caret package. Model diagnostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curves(ROC) and the area under the curve (AUC), calculated with the pROC package [38]. For other group comparisons involving non-parametric data, the Wilcoxon test was used. Potential non-linear relationships between age and metabolite levels were explored using generalized additive models (GAMs) fitted with the mgcv package [39, 40]. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data visualization was performed using ggplot2 [41]. All R packages, software and database used and their functions are listed in Supplementary Table 5.

This separation clearly demonstrated the differences in plasma metabolite and lipid levels between pSS patients and HC. Constructing a multivariate classification model for pSS

Initially, we utilized various machine learning algorithms, including LASSO regression and RF algorithm, to identify significant variables in MLP (Supplementary Fig. 5A-D). Further screen for significant variables with an AUC greater than 0.9 through the ROC analysis. Subsequently, the diagnostic performance of the model was evaluated using ROC analysis. Finally, external validation using an independent cohort confirmed the clinical applicability of the diagnostic model.

Through machine learning based feature selection, we identified 12 metabolites (Fig. 4B). Based on the screening criteria of AUC > 0.9, we further identified four significant variables (Supplementary Table 8). Associations of age, sex, and SSA status with the metabolome in pSS

However, the systematic relationships between these clinical variables and the metabolic profile in pSS remain insufficiently explored. To address this, we conducted an analysis based on metabolomic and lipidomic data from 154 pSS patients. We first assessed the impact of age on the metabolic profile. To accurately capture potential non-linear relationships, we employed GAMs. For each metabolite, both linear and non-linear models were fitted. Results from the GAMs were reported if the non-linear term was significant, the estimated degrees of freedom were ≥ 1.5; otherwise, the linear model result was adopted (Supplementary table 9A). Analysis of age-related associations revealed distinct linear and non-linear patterns among metabolites and lipids. eighty-nine metabolites demonstrated linear age-related changes, of which six exhibited statistically significant positive correlations with age (p-value < 0.05; Fig. 5B). The age-associated metabolites included specific sphingolipids, amino acid derivatives, and glycerophospholipids. Beyond linear associations, 39 metabolites exhibited significant non-linear relationships with age and were subsequently classified into two distinct trajectory-based clusters (Fig. 5 C). The majority of metabolites (n = 36) constituted Cluster 2. This cluster encompassed a diverse array of compounds, including amino acids, fatty acid oxidation products and oxylipins, as well as various glycerophospholipids and sphingolipids. Metabolites within this cluster typically reached peak levels in the young goup, followed by a steady decline with advancing age. In contrast, Cluster 1 comprised only three specific lipids: PC O-38:4, TG 50:2, and TG 50:1, which demonstrated a near-linear increasing trend. To further elucidate the dynamic evolution of the metabolic profile with age, we compared metabolite differences between age groups (Young group < 35 years, Middle-aged group, Old group ≥ 65 years) [45]. The number of differentially abundant metabolites increased with the widening age gap (Fig. 5D). In the old and young age groups comparison, the vast majority of differential metabolites (e.g., sphingolipids and phospholipids like HexCer 34:1;O2 and SM 42:2;O2) were up-regulated, while only 13-HPOT was down-regulated. This suggests that dysregulated lipid metabolism, particularly the accumulation of specific complex lipids, may be a prominent feature of aging in pSS.

Furthermore, significant sex-specific metabolic differences were identified (Fig. 5E, Supplementary table 9B). Compared to males, female patients exhibited significantly lower levels of metabolites such as biocytin, 9,10,13-TriHOME, and NAE 20:0, while levels of PC 32:1, arachidonic acid, and several phospholipids were significantly higher, revealing distinct sexual dimorphism in the metabolic regulation of pSS.

Finally, in seropositive patients(SPSS), we identified six differentially abundant metabolites. Arginine, tyrosine, 11-HpODE, LPC 18:0, and N6-succinyladenosine were significantly up-regulated, whereas 17-hydroxylinolenic acid was down-regulated (Fig. 5 F, Supplementary table 9 C). This indicates that SSA antibody positivity may be closely associated with specific disturbances in amino acid metabolism, fatty acid oxidation, and vitamin metabolism.

Stratification of disease activity and organ involvement in pSS by MLP

To investigate the association between the metabolic landscape and disease manifestations in pSS, we first assessed its correlation with the overall ESSDAI score, drawing edges between nodes for significant correlations (p < 0.05, |r| > 0.2). This identified a panel of twelve metabolites significantly associated with ESSDAI (Fig. 6 A, Supplementary table 10A). The panel demonstrated excellent discriminatory performance, achieving perfect accuracy in distinguishing patients with low disease activity from healthy controls in both the discovery and validation cohorts (AUC = 1.0 each). Furthermore, it effectively stratified disease activity levels, differentiating between low and moderate (AUC: 0.797 discovery, 0.88 validation), moderate and high (AUC: 0.731 discovery, 1.0 validation), and low and high activity (AUC: 0.831 discovery, 1.0 validation) (Fig. 6B, C). Given that the ESSDAI integrates specific organ involvement, we next delineated metabolic signatures underlying distinct organ manifestations. Spearman correlation analysis revealed distinct plasma metabolic patterns associated with organ involvement (Fig. 6D). Through ROC analysis, we screened for significant variables (AUC > 0.65 or 0.75). For pulmonary involvement, a three-metabolite signature (characterized by upregulated pyroglutamic acid and downregulated lysophosphatids) achieved a combined AUC of 0.723 (Fig. 6E). For hematological involvement, a five-molecule signature yielded a combined AUC of 0.79, with significant expression differences in metabolites such as 13(S)-HODE, 17-hydroxylinolenic acid, and leukotriene A4 (Fig. 6 F). Furthermore, we identified specific metabolic signatures for constitutional, articular, and cutaneous involvement, with combined AUCs of 0.783, 0.752, and 0.695, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 6B-D, Supplementary table 10B). Our findings indicate that overall pSS disease activity, as assessed by the ESSDAI, correlates with a distinct plasma metabolic profile. Moreover, this global profile can be further resolved into characteristic metabolic signatures that correspond to involvement of specific organ systems.

Discussion

This study employed an integrated untargeted metabolomics and lipidomics approach to systematically characterize the metabolic profile of pSS and investigate the correlations between the plasma lipidomic profile of pSS patients and inflammatory markers (e.g., CRP, ESR), liver function parameters, various autoantibodies, and immune function indicators, thereby constructing an integrated metabolic-immune network. Furthermore, leveraging a machine learning algorithm, we developed a diagnostic model for seronegative pSS, which demonstrated exceptional discriminative performance in an independent validation cohort. Further analysis revealed the presence of metabolic disturbances in pSS patients that were closely associated with age, sex, autoantibody status, disease activity, and organ involvement. These disturbances were particularly characterized by significant dysregulation in the metabolism of polyunsaturated fatty acids, amino acid homeostasis, and phospholipid pathways.

Consistent with previous studies, patients with pSS exhibit a coordinated dysregulation in polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) metabolism [9, 46]. This imbalance is characterized by a concomitant increase in pro-inflammatory mediators, such as arachidonic acid and hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid (HPODE), alongside a decrease in anti-inflammatory/antioxidant metabolites like traumatic acid, suggesting a shift toward a pro-oxidative state in the disease microenvironment [47, 48]. Simultaneously, a significant shift in tryptophan metabolism toward the kynurenine pathway, indicated by an elevated kynurenine/tryptophan (Kyn/Trp) ratio, was closely associated with the immune-activated status [49]. Furthermore, profound alterations in phospholipid metabolism were observed, including elevated levels of lysophosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylcholine [50], as well as a reduction in sphingosine-1-phosphate accompanied by an accumulation of dihydrosphingosine within the sphingolipid signaling pathway [10, 51]. Collectively, these changes indicate enhanced membrane remodeling, oxidative stress, and dysregulated immune signaling in pSS. The aforementioned metabolic alterations demonstrated significant correlations with clinical immunological parameters, constituting the core of the metabolic-immune interplay network.

Our study delved into the associations between key clinical factors (sex, age, and anti-SSA antibody status) and patterns of metabolic disturbances in pSS, revealing the significant value of metabolomics in characterizing disease heterogeneity. A large-scale clinical study in recent years demonstrated that the age at diagnosis in pSS patients is significantly associated with clinical manifestations, immune marker profiles, and patterns of systemic involvement [45]. In our investigation, several age-associated metabolites exhibiting positive linear correlations were identified. Pyroglutamic acid levels increased progressively with age, suggesting a potential link to cumulative oxidative stress damage over the course of pSS. This finding contrasts sharply with observations from Huang et al. in age-related atrial fibrillation (AF), where Pyroglutamic acid levels in the myocardium of aged mice with AF were reported to be decreased compared to adult AF mice [52]. This divergent pattern suggests that Pyroglutamic acid metabolism may play distinct roles in different age-related pathologies. Notably, the sphingolipid profile in pSS patients was also altered with advancing age. Authoritative recent reviews indicate that acid sphingomyelinase (ASM) activity increases significantly with age in various tissues, including the brain, plasma, and heart, and represents a core driver of aging-related pathology [53, 54]. Our analysis found that the number of differentially expressed metabolites between different age groups (early-onset, middle-aged, late-onset) increased with widening age gaps. Specifically, various sphingolipids and phospholipids (such as HexCer 34:1;O2, SM 42:2;O2) were significantly upregulated in late-onset patients. These findings indicate that dysregulated lipid metabolism—particularly the abnormal accumulation of complex lipids—may be a prominent feature of the aging process in pSS.

Secondly, this study revealed significant sex-specific metabolic differences in pSS. Female patients exhibited elevated levels of arachidonic acid and various phospholipids, alongside significantly reduced levels of metabolites such as biocytin, 9,10,13-TriHOME, 13(S)-HOPT, and NAE 20:0 compared to males. This finding suggests that sex hormones may influence the immune response in pSS by modulating lipid metabolism and inflammatory mediators. Recent studies provide potential molecular mechanisms for this phenomenon. Sex hormones regulate phospholipid metabolism by promoting the expression of the remodeling enzymes MBOAT1 (via estrogen receptor) and MBOAT2 (via androgen receptor). These enzymes incorporate MUFAs into phospholipids, shifting membrane composition away from inflammation- and ferroptosis-susceptible PUFAs [55]. This aligns well with our finding of significantly elevated levels of arachidonic acid (a PUFA) and related phospholipids in female pSS patients. These results provide a mechanistic basis for the aberrantly activated immune response in pSS, offering a potential metabolic explanation for the marked sex disparity in pSS prevalence observed clinically.

Current diagnosis of pSS primarily relies on salivary gland biopsy and serological tests [4, 56]. However, both methods have significant limitations. Salivary gland biopsy is invasive; it may yield inconclusive results due to insufficient sampling and can cause patient discomfort [57]. Serological tests, on the other hand, are susceptible to misdiagnosis, as a substantial proportion of pSS patients are seronegative and present with symptoms overlapping with other conditions, such as sicca syndrome [1, 5]. These limitations underscore the need for more reliable and non-invasive diagnostic approaches. Metabolite reprogramming represents a promising source of biomarkers for disease diagnosis [8, 58]. In our comparative study, seronegative pSS patients exhibited a distinct metabolic profile characterized by significant downregulation of arginine, tyrosine, 11-HpODE, biocytin, and N6-succinyladenosine, along with a marked upregulation of 17-hydroxylinolenic acid. This characteristic metabolic signature suggests a shift in unsaturated fatty acid metabolism toward an antioxidant state, accompanied by suppressed metabolism of specific amino acids and vitamin derivatives.

Based on a machine learning algorithm, we developed a diagnostic model for pSS patients. This model effectively distinguished seronegative pSS patients, patients with xerostomia, and healthy individuals in an independent validation cohort, demonstrating strong generalizability and robustness. Furthermore, several metabolites correlated with clinical indicators—including inflammatory markers, liver function, and immune parameters—as well as with disease activity assessed by the ESSDAI score. This suggests that these metabolites reflect certain pathological features of the disease. Integrating such metabolic information with conventional clinical measures enables multidimensional patient profiling, thereby contributing not only to a more accurate diagnostic framework for pSS but also to refined patient stratification.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the observed metabolic alterations. For example, it remains unclear whether lipid dysregulation drives or results from autoimmune activity, necessitating longitudinal or experimental studies. Second, while the untargeted metabolomics approach provided broad coverage, its limited dynamic range and sensitivity may have failed to capture certain low-abundance yet biologically important metabolites, such as key lipid mediators. Third, metabolite identification and pathway analysis rely on public database completeness and accuracy, which may introduce annotation bias. Although stringent quality control and Level 2 MS/MS annotations were applied, the findings remain exploratory and require targeted validation. Finally, as a single-center study, the cohort is relatively homogeneous; external validation in larger, multi-center prospective cohorts is needed to confirm generalizability and clinical applicability.

Despite the promising diagnostic performance of the 4-MLPs model, its translation into clinical practice faces several challenges. These include the costs associated with assay development and implementation, the need to establish standardized protocols to ensure reproducibility, and critically, the requirement for validation and integration into real-world primary care workflows to demonstrate its utility in routine practice.

Conclusions

In summary, our study not only provides deeper insights into the metabolic dysregulation of pSS but, more importantly, paves the way for novel metabolomics-based precision diagnostics. Future research, involving larger longitudinal cohorts and functional experiments to validate the mechanisms of key metabolites, holds promise for identifying new therapeutic targets and ultimately advancing pSS management into an era of personalized medicine.
