Beyond cognition in post-stroke cognitive impairment: neuropsychiatric symptoms and stroke-specific cognitive screening
Authors: Polona Rus Prelog, Matija Zupan, Senta Frol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Depression and apathy can confound post-stroke cognitive test performance.•Validated post-stroke depression and apathy scales should be used routinely.•Relying only on MoCA may miss focal and domain-specific post-stroke deficits.•Stroke-specific screens (e.g. OCS, ACE-III, CCAS-S) complement global measures.•Tailored screening plus neuropsychiatric assessment improves PSCI evaluation.

Pantoni et al. [1] provide an excellent synthesis of post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI), highlighting its heterogeneous mechanisms, trajectories, and the frequent prominence of executive and attentional deficits over classical amnestic patterns. Their article also emphasizes the high prevalence of depression, anxiety, apathy, fatigue, and sleep disturbance after stroke, and the limited but evolving evidence base for structured cognitive assessment and rehabilitation. Beyond the mechanisms and trajectories summarized by the authors, a key challenge in practice is how we interpret cognitive test results at the bedside. In everyday stroke care, performance on cognitive screening is shaped not only by brain injury itself but also by co‑occurring emotional and motivational changes, as well as by the characteristics of the instruments we rely on. Therefore, we wish to expand on two aspects that are highly relevant for everyday practice and research design: the role of neuropsychiatric symptoms—especially depression and apathy—as potential confounders of cognitive performance, and the choice of cognitive screening tools beyond a sole focus on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA).

First, the review rightly notes that neuropsychiatric symptoms are common after stroke and can exacerbate functional impairment and reduce engagement in rehabilitation [1]. Stroke-specific literature shows that depression, apathy, and related symptoms are not only frequent but closely intertwined with cognitive and functional outcomes [2,3]. Recent population‑based work illustrates this clearly. In a cohort of >24,000 stroke survivors, patients with post‑stroke aphasia had a ∼70% higher one‑year risk of depression than those without aphasia (incidence roughly 44 vs. 28 per 1000 person‑years), with even greater risk after haemorrhagic stroke [2]. In the same and related cohorts, aphasia has been associated with higher rates of fatigue, agitation, emotional distress, anxiety, and insomnia, and with greater likelihood of co‑occurring non‑verbal cognitive difficulties [[2], [3], [4]]. Taken together, these findings support the view that post‑stroke mood and behavioural changes are strongly shaped by both lesion characteristics and communication barriers, and that neuropsychiatric symptoms cannot be treated as a marginal add‑on when interpreting cognitive test performance.

Apathy, in particular, has been identified as one of the most common post-stroke neuropsychiatric syndromes and is consistently associated with worse disability, poorer quality of life, and cognitive decline, often being misdiagnosed as depression if not explicitly assessed [[5], [6], [7]]. Systematic reviews further suggest that post-stroke apathy is more strongly related to cognitive impairment and depressive symptoms than to lesion location alone, highlighting its close interaction with the broader PSCI phenotype [4,7].

These data support the authors’ view that neuropsychiatric symptoms should be actively assessed, but they also underscore the risk that unrecognized depression and apathy may be interpreted as “pure” cognitive decline or attenuated cognitive recovery [[6], [7], [8]]. We therefore suggest that future PSCI studies and clinical pathways routinely include brief, stroke‑appropriate measures of depression and apathy alongside cognitive testing, particularly when cognitive screens are used to define impairment or to select patients for trials. In practice, this could involve depression scales with established use and validation in post‑stroke cohorts, such as the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [9] and the Patient Health Questionnaire‑9 (PHQ‑9) [10]. HADS has shown a stable two‑factor structure, good responsiveness to change, and overall suitability for assessing anxiety and depression in working‑age stroke survivors during rehabilitation, while PHQ‑9 demonstrates acceptable validity and reliability for screening post‑stroke depression, with recommended cut‑offs around 6–10 depending on population and phase of stroke [9,10]. For apathy, instruments such as the Apathy Evaluation Scale (AES) and the Starkstein Apathy Scale have been shown to be reliable and valid in stroke survivors, with recent work reporting good discriminative validity of AES (AUC ≈0.80; sensitivity 87.5%, specificity 72.2%) and robust psychometric properties of revised Starkstein scales in chronic stroke [11,12]. Incorporating such validated measures would allow adjustment for, or stratification by, these symptoms and help distinguish true cognitive impairment from motivational and affective phenomena that substantially influence test performance and day‑to‑day functioning.

Second, Pantoni et al. [1] recommend a stepwise diagnostic strategy with MoCA as a useful initial screen, followed by comprehensive neuropsychological assessment when indicated, and they appropriately highlight MoCA’s sensitivity to milder deficits relative to the Mini–Mental State Examination. While MoCA remains a pragmatic and widely validated choice in stroke populations, it is important to acknowledge that it provides a largely global score and may underestimate or incompletely characterize focal, domain-specific post-stroke deficits—such as neglect, apraxia, and language disturbances—that are particularly relevant for stroke rehabilitation [[13], [14], [15]].

In this context, stroke‑specific and domain‑structured tools such as the Oxford Cognitive Screen (OCS) offer a complementary perspective [16,17]. The OCS was explicitly designed as a brief, stroke‑specific screen to profile focal post‑stroke deficits across multiple domains—language, praxis, number processing, memory, and spatial/controlled attention—with subtests adapted to be inclusive for patients with aphasia and neglect. Initial validation demonstrated feasibility at the bedside, good sensitivity to post‑stroke impairments, and the ability to differentiate profiles associated with left versus right hemisphere damage. Subsequent work in acute and subacute stroke cohorts has confirmed its validity and reliability, including independent psychometric evaluations and adaptations into different languages, as well as tablet‑based extensions (OCS‑Plus) that can detect subtle impairments in patients classified as unimpaired on more traditional global screens [[18], [19], [20], [21]].

Recent work on cognitive assessment after stroke also points to other brief instruments—such as the Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination‑III (ACE‑III) and more targeted scales like the Cerebellar Cognitive Affective Syndrome scale (CCAS‑S) in cerebellar stroke—as sensitive options for detecting subtle or topography‑specific deficits [22,23]. ACE‑III has shown good discriminative capacity for vascular cognitive impairment in stroke cohorts, while CCAS‑S can be completed at the bedside in acute cerebellar vascular lesions and detects cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome in the majority of patients [[23], [24], [25]]. Taken together, this literature aligns with the authors’ emphasis on domain specificity and suggests that, rather than privileging a single instrument, stroke services and studies might explicitly consider when a domain-general screen like MoCA is sufficient and when a stroke-specific or extended battery (such as OCS, ACE-III, or topography-focused tools) may add clinically meaningful information.

In summary, we believe that integrating cognitive health into stroke care and implementing structured PSCI assessment pathway is an imperative; we suggest that this framework could be further strengthened by systematically measuring depression and apathy as potential confounders and modifiers of cognitive trajectories, and by explicitly acknowledging the role of stroke-specific, domain-structured screening tools as complements to MoCA and comprehensive neuropsychological testing. These refinements would improve the interpretability of cognitive measures, reduce the risk of misattributing neuropsychiatric symptoms to cognitive decline, and better align assessment strategies with the heterogeneous clinical reality of PSCI.
