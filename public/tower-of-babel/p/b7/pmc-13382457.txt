Clinical outcomes of invasive versus conservative strategies for very high-risk patients with non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndrome: an observational cohort study in a real-world population of China
Authors: Yuanhui Liu, Lin Zeng, ShiJie Yu, Xuhui Lin, Kailun Qiu, Peng Yin, Chongyang Duan, Ling Xue, Maigeng Zhou, Feng Liu, Ning Tan, Pengcheng He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAlthough an invasive strategy (IS) within 2 h is recommended for very high-risk patients with non-ST-segment elevation acute coronary syndrome (NSTE-ACS), the optimal strategies remained debated due to a lack of evidence, because these patients were excluded from clinical studies. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate whether IS was associated with lower in-hospital and long-term all-cause death compared with conservative therapy among such patients, and whether the timing of IS differentially affected patient prognosis.MethodsThis retrospective observational study recruited very high-risk patients with NSTE-ACS registered in the Chinese Cardiovascular Association Database-Chest Pain Centre between January 2015 and April 2021. Patients were grouped as receiving conservative therapy or IS within 2 h, 2–24 h, or >24 h after admission. The primary outcome was in-hospital all-cause death. Multivariable regression and inverse probability weighting analyses were conducted to account for measured confounding and assess the robustness of the findings.FindingsA total of 35,122 very high-risk patients with NSTE-ACS were finally included (mean age 64.2 years). Compared to the conservative strategy, IS at any time point (within 2 h, 2–24 h, >24 h) was associated with the reduced risk of in-hospital (adjusted odds ratio, 0.25–0.42, P < 0.001–0.009) and long-term all-cause death (median follow-up 551 days, adjusted hazard ratio, 0.33–0.45, P < 0.001). The incidence of in-hospital and long-term all-cause death did not differ significantly by IS timing. The inverse probability weighting analysis yielded consistent findings.InterpretationFor very high-risk patients with NSTE-ACS, IS was associated with a lower risk of in-hospital and long-term all-cause death compared to conservative strategy. However, among patients who underwent invasive management, mortality outcomes were broadly similar across different timings of IS. Further randomised controlled trials are warranted to verify this finding.FundingThis work was supported by High-level Hospital Construction Project and Basic and Applied Basic Research Fund of Guangdong Province.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed for systematic review, retrospective cohort study and randomised controlled trials published before Oct 1, 2025 using the search terms “non-ST-segment elevation acute coronary syndrome (NSTE-ACS)”, “non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infraction”, “unstable angina”, “very high-risk”, “invasive strategy”, “percutaneous coronary intervention”, “coronary angiography”, and “timing”. Furthermore, despite the guideline recommendations, most of such patients did not receive invasive strategy within 2 h in real-world clinical practice. Therefore, the optimal strategies for these patients remained debated.

Added value of this study

A total of 35,122 very high-risk patients with NSTE-ACS registered in the Chinese Cardiovascular Association Database-Chest Pain Centre between January 2015 and April 2021 were finally included, and the results demonstrated that invasive strategy was associated with a lower risk of in-hospital and long-term all-cause death compared to conservative strategy, with similar outcomes across different timings of invasive strategy.

Implications of all the available evidence

The finding suggests that invasive strategy was associated with a lower risk of in-hospital and long-term all-cause death compared to conservative therapy in very high-risk patients with NSTE-ACS. However, patients treated with invasive strategy at any time point had similar risk for in-hospital and long-term all-cause death. These findings raise important hypothesis-generating insights and highlight the urgent need for randomised controlled trials on the strategy for very high-risk patients with NSTE-ACS.

Introduction

Compared with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), recent research indicated a rising incidence of non-ST-segment elevation acute coronary syndrome (NSTE-ACS), accompanied by longer hospitalisation length and higher in-hospital mortality.1, 2, 3 Since outcomes are still falling short of expectations, optimising treatment is necessary for the benefit of such patients. Between 1995 and 2015, the use of early angiography (<72 h) and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) increased from 9% to 60% and from 12.5%–67%, respectively, which brought between those years a significant reduction of 6-month mortality for these patients.4 Previous guidelines suggest an invasive strategy (IS) within 2 h in very high-risk patients with NSTE-ACS, owing to evidence indicating that untreated individuals in this risk category experience poor short-term and long-term clinical outcomes.5,6 Moreover, the latest 2023 ESC Guidelines recommend immediate invasive treatment for this patient group without specifying a definite time limit for treatment initiation.7 However, these recommendations are based on the consensus opinion of the experts, because very high-risk patients have generally been excluded from clinical studies, including randomised controlled trials (RCTs). In addition, available evidence indicates that compared to selective IS, routine IS does not reduce all-cause death in the overall population of patients with NSTE-ACS.8, 9, 10

Furthermore, inconsistent with guideline recommendations, approximately 58.3% of very high-risk patients did not receive IS in real-world clinical practice, and of those who did, only 11.1% underwent PCI within 2 h of admission.11 Similar results were reported from MINAP (Myocardial Infarction National Audit Project),12 which reported only 16.4% of high-risk patients, including very high-risk patients, received IS within 24 h. The gap between the guidelines and clinical practice drives the urgent need to demonstrate the optimal treatment and timing of intervention in very high-risk patients with NSTE-ACS. Therefore, the present study had two objectives. First, we evaluated whether IS was associated with lower in-hospital and long-term all-cause death compared with conservative therapy among very high-risk patients with NSTE-ACS. Second, among patients treated with IS, we evaluated whether the timing of IS differentially affected patient prognosis. We also clarified the invasive-versus-conservative comparison and the timing comparison address related but distinct clinical questions.

Methods

Study design and study population

This retrospective observational cohort study utilised the data of patients with NSTE-ACS derived from the Chinese Cardiovascular Association (CCA) Database-Chest Pain Centre—a national cardiovascular registry founded by the CCA in 2015. The number of Chest Pain Centre in China is unevenly distributed across provinces. Guangdong Province has the highest count of 424, and Henan has 353. Detailed distribution data are presented in Supplementary Appendix S1. All certified Chest Pain Centres across China are required to contribute patient data to this registry.13,14 For the present analysis, we extracted records of patients with NSTE-ACS. This patient cohort consisted of those with non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) and unstable angina (UA), who were admitted to hospitals from January 1, 2015, to April 1, 2021. Only patients admitted for NSTEMI and UA and stratified as very high-risk were included. The diagnoses and classification were made by cardiologists according to standard guidelines.15, 16, 17 NSTEMI is defined as patients with (1) typical symptoms of angina; (2) elevated cardiac biomarker values; and (3) without new ST-elevation on ECG. Patients were classified as UA if they presented with typical symptoms of angina, no positive cardiac biomarker findings, and without new ST-elevation on ECG. The classification of patients with NSTEMI and UA as very high-risk within the database is determined by experienced clinical cardiologists following international guidelines,5 with at least one of the following criteria: (1) cardiovascular instability manifested as haemodynamic unstable or cardiogenic shock; (2) recurrent or refractory chest pain despite medical treatment; (3) arrhythmias posing an immediate threat to the patient's life; (4) mechanical complications of MI; (5) heart failure directly attributed to NSTE-ACS; (6) ST-segment depression exceeding 1 mm in >6 leads, accompanied by ST-segment elevation in aVR and/or V1. Exclusion criteria were: (1) patients with missing admission data, defined as lacking discharge diagnosis; (2) patients with documented refusal of follow-up treatment and discharged; (3) patients diagnosed with malignant tumours that affect 1-year survival rate; (4) patients who received coronary artery bypass graft treatment and (5) patients without IS or medical treatment data.

Ethics

Ethics approvals of the National Chest Pain Centres Program were obtained from the Institutional Review Boards of the Peking University First Hospital Ethics Committee (2020–242, approved December 14, 2020)18 The requirement for written informed consent was waived, as per the ethics review, owing to the retrospective design. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection

Patients were divided into four groups according to the treatment they received. Patients who received medication therapy without IS were included in the conservative therapy group. Patients who received invasive treatment (coronary angiography or PCI treatment) within 2 h, within 2–24 h, and more than 24 h after admission were classified as the <2 h IS group, the 2–24 h IS group, and the >24 h IS group, respectively. The specific time point of IS in patients was defined according to the time interval between the completion of preliminary diagnosis by the attending physician (based on the patient's clinical condition) and the performance of percutaneous needle puncture.

Primary discharge diagnosis was made by cardiologists of each Chest Pain Centre. And all data (including sex, age, heart rate, blood pressure, biochemical testing, risk stratification, medical history, treatments, medication, complementary procedure tests, and primary discharge diagnosis) were retrospectively collected from medical records by trained, designated data manager at each registered hospital. These data managers completed unified training organised by the China Chest Pain Centre Headquarters. The training covered definitions of data elements, data collection, data abstraction and data reporting procedures. Upon completion of the training and passing the assessment, a training certificate will be issued. After data collection, a national-provincial-prefectural three-level quality control mechanism was implemented to ensure data accuracy and reliability.18

Sex refers to biologically determined sex (male/female) based on physiological characteristics. Given that laboratory reference ranges and units of measurement are different across medical centres, we established uniform cut-off values for all included laboratory parameters to ensure data consistency and comparability. The normal value of troponin T/I is 0.0–14.0 pg/ml, of D-dimer is 0.0–500 ng/ml, of myoglobin is 25.00–58.00 ng/ml, and of creatine kinase isoenzymes is 0.00–3.61 ng/ml or 0–25 U/L. These variables are converted into binary variables “negative”, or “positive” according to the corresponding normal cut-off values. For patients who had more than one record during this period, only the first record that met the inclusion criteria was included.

In addition, this study cross-referenced patients’ records with the China National Death Registration System (Chinese Centre for Disease Control and Prevention, CDC). Using unique national identification numbers, we verified the survival status of all Chest Pain Centre-registered patients to obtain accurate vital status for all patients registered in the Chest Pain Centre. Therefore, the post-discharge follow-up data of patients were collected from the Chinese CDC databases.

Clinical outcomes

The primary outcome was in-hospital all-cause death. The secondary outcome was all-cause death during follow-up. In-hospital all-cause death was determined according to the corresponding records within the Chest Pain Centre database, while all-cause death during follow-up was identified using the data contained in the CDC database.

Statistical analysis

The main clinical comparisons were IS at each timing category versus conservative therapy. Comparisons among the three invasive-strategy timing groups were considered secondary. We compared baseline characteristics among the patient groups defined above. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages (%), while continuous variables were described using the mean ± standard deviation if data were normally distributed or median (interquartile range) if non-normally distributed. For statistical comparisons, analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal-Wallis test were used for continuous variables based on their distribution. Categorical variables were compared using, as necessary, Chi-squared or Fisher's exact test. Ordinal variables were analysed using the Kruskal-Wallis test.

For long-term all-cause death, unadjusted Kaplan–Meier curves were generated and compared using the global log-rank test. Treatment strategy was included in the outcome models as a four-level categorical variable: conservative therapy, IS within 2 h, IS within 2–24 h, and IS > 24 h after admission. For in-hospital all-cause death, a multivariable logistic regression model was used; for long-term all-cause death, a Cox proportional hazards regression model was used. An overall test for the treatment-strategy effect was performed using a likelihood ratio test comparing models with and without the four-level treatment-strategy variable. Pairwise comparisons between treatment groups were performed as post-hoc analyses and were derived as model-based contrasts from the same fitted model. To address multiplicity arising from multiple pairwise comparisons, pairwise P values were adjusted using the Holm method. The global treatment-group test was reported before pairwise comparisons.

Variables were included if they were clinically relevant, prognostically important, supported by prior literature, or showed associations in univariable analyses. Then these variables were used for adjustment in the multivariable analysis. Subgroup analyses were performed by sex. To assess whether this factor modified the association between treatment strategy and all-cause death outcomes, we conducted stratified analyses and tested for interaction via likelihood ratio tests comparing models with and without the interaction term.

To assess the robustness of the findings and address measured confounding, inverse probability weighting (IPW) analysis was employed to improve covariate balance across treatment groups.19,20 Briefly, the treatment variable had four categories: conservative therapy, IS within 2 h, IS within 2–24 h, and IS > 24 h after admission. We estimated the generalised propensity score for the four treatment categories using multinomial logistic regression. Because the database had two versions with different covariate availability [version 1.0 n = 15,706 (44.7%), version 2.0 n = 19,416 (55.3%)], propensity scores were estimated separately within Version 1.0 and Version 2.0 to make maximal use of the covariates available in each version.

For Version 1.0, the multinomial logistic regression model for predicting treatment strategy included the following factors: age, sex, heart rate, type of NSTE-ACS, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, aspirin, Killip class, clopidogrel, statin, and beta-blockers. For Version 2.0, the model included a broader set of covariates: age, sex, type of NSTE-ACS, smoking status, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidaemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, anaemia, chronic kidney disease, prior atrial fibrillation, heart rate, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, Killip class, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein-C, troponin, brain natriuretic peptide (BNP), serum creatinine, aspirin, clopidogrel, anticoagulant therapy, unfractionated heparin, low molecular weight heparin, bivalirudin, fondaparinux, statin, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor/angiotensin II receptor blocker, and beta-blockers.

Inverse probability weights were calculated as W_i = 1/P (A = a_i|X_i), where Ai denotes the observed treatment category for patient i, ai denotes the treatment actually received, and Xi denotes the covariates included in the propensity score model. The resulting weights from the two version-specific propensity score models were then combined for the IPW analyses. We assessed covariate balance using absolute standardised mean differences, with values < 0.10 considered indicative of acceptable balance. The distributions of the weights were examined. Because no materially influential extreme weights were observed, the primary IPW analyses used untruncated weights. Weighted logistic regression and weighted Cox proportional hazards models with robust standard errors were then used for in-hospital and long-term all-cause death, respectively. IPW-adjusted Kaplan–Meier curves were constructed and compared using a weighted global log-rank test.

The group definitions based on timing of IS could introduce immortal time bias, as patients in the delayed treatment groups had to survive until the time of treatment. To assess the robustness of the findings to potential immortal time bias, we performed landmark analyses by excluding patients who died within 24 h and, separately, those who died within 72 h. These analyses were used to assess whether early deaths materially influenced the associations between IS timing and outcomes.

All analyses were performed in R (Version 4.2.1, R Project for Statistical Computing) using the packages ‘tableone’, ‘survival’, ‘nnet’, ‘lmtest’, and ‘survey’. All statistical tests were two-sided and a P value < 0.05 was considered significant. For pairwise comparisons, multiplicity-adjusted P values were interpreted.

Role of the funding source

The funders had no role in the study design, data collection and analysis, the decision to publish, or the preparation of the manuscript.

Results

Patients characteristics

Of the 1,703,958 NSTE-ACS cases recorded in the CCA Database-Chest Pain Centre from January 1, 2015 to April 1, 2021, a total of 35,122 patients were finally included (Fig. 1), with 23,417 (66.7%) being male. There are 15,706 patients (44.7%) in version 1.0 and 19,416 (55.3%) in version 2.0. The distribution of treatment strategies by database version is shown in Supplementary Table S1. Among 35,122 included patients, 28,101 (80.0%) were patients with NSTEMI and 7021 (20.0%) were patients with UA. The majority of patients received IS therapy (60.4%, 21,221/35,122), and the remainder underwent conservative treatment (39.6%, 13,901/35,122). Among the patients who received IS therapy, 17,410 patients (82.1%) had it performed within 2 h of admission, while a minority of patients received it between 2 and 24 h (10.2%, 2172/21,221) or more than 24 h (7.7%, 1639/21,221) after admission. The detail of PCI was required to be recorded only for patients receiving IS within 2 h (in version 2). Before the procedural.

Patients treated with conservative strategy had lower levels of triglycerides, total cholesterol, low density lipoprotein-C level and non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (P < 0.001). Likewise, the percentage of BNP and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide positive cases was higher in the conservative therapy group (P < 0.001).

After applying IPW analysis (Supplementary Table S2), the baseline characteristics of patients were well balanced. Patients receiving conservative therapy were younger, showed lower prevalence of prior revascularisation history, had higher BNP and creatinine levels, lower LVEF, and shorter hospital stay duration.

In-hospital all-cause death

The incidence of in-hospital all-cause death was significantly higher in the conservative than in the IS group (P < 0.001) (Table 2). In the multivariable logistic regression model including treatment strategy as a four-level categorical variable, the overall association between treatment strategy and in-hospital all-cause death was statistically significant (global P < 0.001). After adjusting for potential confounders, pairwise model-based contrasts showed that IS at each timing category was associated with lower odds of in-hospital all-cause death compared with conservative therapy (adjusted OR: 0.25–0.42). A total of 18,167 patients who received IS were excluded. To evaluate the impact of these patients on the results, we performed the same multivariate analysis comparing these 18,167 IS patients with those who received conservative treatment. The result showed that the excluded IS patients were also associated with a lower risk of in-hospital all-cause death compared to those in the conservative treatment group (adjusted OR: 0.31, 95% CI: 0.25–0.39, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S4).

After IPW analysis, patients treated with IS at any time point remained to be associated with a lower risk of in-hospital all-cause death compared to those treated with conservative therapy. Likewise, the effect of IS timing remained non-significant regarding this outcome (Supplementary Table S5).

The landmark analysis for in-hospital all-cause death was consistent with the multivariable logistic regression and IPW analysis (Table 3, Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Table S5).

Long-term all-cause death

The median follow-up time was 551 days for the entire study population, among whom 8076 participants (23.0%) were lost to follow-up (median follow-up: 710 days for those with complete follow-up), and patients lost to follow-up were treated as censored at discharge in the analysis. In the Cox proportional hazards model, the overall association between treatment strategy and long-term all-cause death was statistically significant (global P < 0.001). Multivariable Cox regression analysis revealed that treatment with IS at any time category was associated with lower hazards of long-term all-cause death compared with conservative therapy (adjusted HR: 0.33–0.45) (Table 2, Table 4, and Fig. 2). This result remained the same in the other multivariable models (Supplementary Table S6). However, patients in the >24 h IS group had a significantly higher risk of long-term all-cause death than those in the <2 h IS group. Compared to patients in the 2–24 h IS group, those in the >24 h IS group had a trend for increased risk of long-term all-cause death, which was however not significant. After IPW analysis, similar results were obtained. Compared to conservative therapy, IS at any time point was related to lower risk for all-cause death during follow-up. In turn, pairwise comparisons between groups indicated that the timing of IS did not significantly impact all-cause death risk during follow-up (Supplementary Table S7, Fig. 3).

Consistent results were observed for long-term all-cause death in the landmark analysis, multivariable Cox regression, and the IPW analysis (Table 4, Supplementary Table S6, and Supplementary Table S7).

Subgroup analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared between male and female patients receiving IS and conservative treatment. Females who received conservative treatment were older and generally in a frailer clinical condition. The female in the conservative group also had a higher proportion of patients with heart failure and Killip class ≥ II (Supplementary Table S8). However, the association of treatment strategy with in-hospital and long-term all-cause death was not significantly different among the subgroup analysis of sex (P for Interaction = 0.818 and 0.133) (Supplementary Table S9 and Supplementary Table S10).

Discussion

The present study with very large contemporary dataset demonstrated that IS at any time point was associated with lower risk for in-hospital and long-term all-cause death in very high-risk patients with NSTE-ACS, compared to those receiving conservative treatment. In addition, the timing of IS did not significantly impact either in-hospital or long-term all-cause death.

Several studies revealed that a routine IS does not significantly reduce the risk of all-cause death during follow-up compared to conservative strategy in patients with NSTE-ACS, but it performs better in terms of reducing the risk of myocardial infarction, refractory angina, and rehospitalisation.9,10 Large RCTs and studies reporting on their long-term outcome results23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28 have compared the impact of routine IS and conservative treatment on clinical outcomes for patients with NSTE-ACS. Consistently, the findings showed that routine IS does not reduce the overall risk of long-term all-cause death for these patients, and in fact might increase risk of perioperative complications such as perioperative myocardial infarction and bleeding.

However, these trials carried out during a period before the regular application of radial vascular approach for coronary angiography, drug-eluting stents, and antiplatelet therapies, alongside the intensified lipid-lowering therapy, thereby constraining the broader generalisability of these results. In recent years, various research efforts have focused on the elderly population. Some RCTs29,30 failed to demonstrate that routine IS improves all-cause death in patients with NSTE-ACS within this population segment. However, Berg et al. recently proposed that invasive treatment combined with optimal medical therapy leads to better long-term outcomes compared to a conservative treatment approach in patients with NSTE-ACS with aged ≥80 years.31 It is worth noting, however, that very high-risk populations are usually excluded from afore mentioned studies. Thus, whether IS can improve clinical outcome for very high-risk patients with NSTE-ACS remains controversial.

The association hereby reported between IS and reduced risk of in-hospital and long-term all-cause death in very high-risk patients with NSTE-ACS adds further evidence to current guideline's recommending early invasive therapies for such patients and contrasts with those based on the expert consensus.5,6 To our knowledge, this is the first study specifically focussing on the very high-risk patients with NSTE-ACS with a large sample size to compare invasive versus conservative management in a real-world setting. Furthermore, the present study also investigated whether the timing of IS (<2 h, 2–24 h, or >24 h after admission) was associated with different risks of these outcomes. While RCTs have established the benefit of an IS in low risk patients with NSTE-ACS, these trials systematically excluded the very high-risk patients-precisely the group where clinical practice has existed and guideline evidence is weakest. The present findings may thus help filling the gap in contemporary medical treatment for the selection of treatment strategies in these patients. Previous meta-analyses have concluded that although IS may reduce the risk of short term all-cause death (2 years), there is no difference in long-term all-cause death between IS and conservative treatments for all risk categories of patients with NSTE-ACS.10 Still, it was proposed that the short-term beneficial effect of IS, due to aggressive antiplatelet therapy and stent placement,32 might be offset by the progression of non-culprit coronary artery disease in long-term survivors.10

The latest meta-analysis suggested that early IS compared to delayed IS does not reduce all-cause death, myocardial infarction, heart failure-related hospitalisation, revascularisation, or increase major bleeding or stroke in patients with NSTE-ACS at all risk categories.33 However, early IS (median 3.43 h, range, 1.47–5.40 h) significantly reduces the risk of recurrent ischaemia and shortens hospital stay compared to delayed IS (median 41.3 h, range, 29.3–53.2 h). In high-risk patients, early IS might be associated with a lower risk of mortality.33 Current guidelines on early IS for patients with NSTE-ACS at high-risk are mainly based on subgroup analyses of patients with GRACE scores ≥140 in the TIMACS34 and VERDICT35 trials, which suggested that early IS can improve outcomes in high-risk patients. A real-world study including data from 2,191,772 patients treated between 2006 and 201236 found that IS was effective in reducing in-hospital death in patients with NSTEMI and cardiac shock. However, it was noted that the patients with cardiac shock did not fully represent the very high-risk group defined by later updated guidelines. In addition, determining whether cardiac shock occurred on admission or during hospitalisation was not possible, so it was unclear whether cardiac shock was an accompanying symptom of NSTEMI or a complication of IS. More importantly, the timing of IS in such study is only accurate to days which is not consistent with current guidelines recommending early IS within 2 h for very high-risk patients. Moreover, studies have shown that patients with NSTEMI at highest risk are least likely to receive early invasive management strategies.37 Therefore, whether patients with NSTE-ACS at very high-risk benefited from IS within 2 h remained unclear.

Our study observed that males received less conservative treatment than female patients. Compared with males, females are more likely to attribute chest pain to anxiety, stress or non-cardiac causes when it occurs.38 This may lead to delayed presentation of medical care among female patients, thus delaying invasive treatment. Recently, a large national cohort study showed that females with AMI exhibited a higher prevalence of frailty, more comorbidities, and were older than males,39 findings similar to those of our study. In addition, females are proved to be about twice as likely as males to experience bleeding.40,41 The above reasons might lead physicians to use less interventional treatment and favour more conservative approaches for female patients. However, the association between IS and lower in-hospital all-cause death was observed in both male and female patients.

We noticed that patients in the conservative group tended to be older, presented with worse clinical conditions and had a higher prevalence of Killip class ≥ II, but had a shorter hospital stay duration than those in the IS group. Therefore, we investigated the interval from discharge to death in different groups. The result showed that more patients died on the day of discharge in the conservative group (Supplementary Table S11). In addition, we also evaluated the duration between the time of death and hospital discharge across two subgroups: elderly versus non-elderly patients and patients with Killip class ≥ II versus those with Killip class < II. The result showed that patients with a higher Killip class and older age tended to have a shorter discharge to death interval in both IS and conservative groups (Supplementary Table S11). Moreover, the discharge to death interval in the conservative group was shorter than that in the IS group at any time point. In China, most critically ill patients choose discharge, because patients and their families prefer to receive end-of-life care at home rather than in the hospital. These factors may explain the shorter discharge-to-death interval in the conservative group. We also performed a sensitivity analysis to evaluate whether 18,167 excluded patients receiving IS influenced the primary findings, and the result showed that the main finding was not materially changed after considering these excluded patients receiving IS, although selection bias due to missing treatment data cannot be completely ruled out.

Our results showed the effect of IS applied at any time point on short- and long-term survival outcomes was broadly similar. Patients with NSTE-ACS at very high-risk often exhibited an unstable clinical condition, characterised by symptoms such as hypotension, haemodynamic instability during admission and refractory chest pain, as well as more complex comorbidities.5 In these patients, there is still uncertainty regarding the optimal timing for invasive management to restore coronary artery flow.33 The decision hinges on balancing the acute risks associated with the timing of IS, such as exacerbated haemodynamic disturbances and contrast-induced nephropathy, against the potential detrimental effects of delayed revascularisation. Resolving this issue relies on careful consideration of the patient's clinical condition and haemodynamic status, and on the expertise of the medical team. In the present study, patients undergoing IS within 2 h were younger, had a higher LVEF, and fewer comorbidities. This may suggest that in the very high-risk population, IS within 2 h is more appropriate for cases with a relatively good baseline condition. However, for patients at very high-risk with complex coronary lesions, or poor clinical condition, IS within 2 h may not be feasible if the medical team has limited expertise or resources or lacks experience in using left heart assist devices for complex coronary intervention. In such cases, the patients should ideally be transferred to a nearby general hospital to be adequately treated, or patients should delay receiving interventional treatment.

This study has some limitations. First, this was a retrospective observational study with non-random treatment allocation. The substantially different numbers of patients across treatment groups reflect real-world treatment selection in clinical practice, but also raise concerns about baseline imbalance, limited overlap, and confounding by indication. Conservative treatment may have been selected for patients with more comorbidities, frailty, contraindications to invasive procedures, or poorer clinical status, which are themselves associated with worse outcomes. Although multivariable adjustment and IPW analyses were used to improve balance in measured covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as frailty, coronary anatomy, treatment contraindications, physician decision-making, patient preference, and hospital-level resources cannot be ruled out. Therefore, the observed association between IS and lower mortality should not be interpreted as definitive causal evidence. Further prospective multicenter studies are needed to determine the optimal treatment strategy and timing of IS in very high-risk patients with NSTE-ACS. Second, our study lacked catheterisation laboratory data such as number of coronary artery lesions, number of stents, application of drug-eluting stents, and TIMI flow grading from coronary angiography. The absence of these data limits our ability to fully assess the extent and severity of coronary artery disease and evaluate the effectiveness of the different treatment strategies. Third, 23% lost to follow-up may bias the results of long-term outcomes. However, this might not influence the result of invasive strategy on in hospital all-cause death. Finally, the results of the present study should be considered as hypothesis-generating at best; and this remains a gap in evidence, and calls for future research to explore this issue further.

In this retrospective observational cohort study of very high-risk patients with NSTE-ACS, IS was associated with lower risk of in-hospital and long-term all-cause death compared with conservative therapy. Among patients who underwent invasive management, mortality outcomes were broadly similar across different timings of IS.
