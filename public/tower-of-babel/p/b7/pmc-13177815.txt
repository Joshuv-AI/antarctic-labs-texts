The intensive care unit (ICU) has long relied on physiological parameters, biochemical markers, and imaging modalities to monitor evolving organ dysfunction. Yet the liver, one of the most metabolically active and hemodynamically sensitive organs, often declares distress only when injury is already established. With the advent of two-dimensional shear wave elastography (2D-SWE), clinicians may finally have a real-time, noninvasive tool capable of assessing hepatic congestion, perfusion, and early structural change at the bedside. The question is whether this technology is ready for routine integration into critical care practice.

Why the Liver Matters in Critical Illness?

The liver represents a dynamic interface of venous return, inflammatory signaling, metabolic homeostasis, and drug clearance. Even modest elevations in central venous pressure (CVP) can cause marked hepatic congestion and rising stiffness on elastography; experimental and clinical work have demonstrated a clear relationship between venous pressure and liver stiffness.1 Sepsis-induced liver injury, often under-recognized in its early stages, contributes significantly to morbidity and mortality through microcirculatory collapse, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction.2

2D-SWE: A Technology with Growing Promise

Two-dimensional-SWE quantifies liver stiffness by analyzing the velocity of shear waves within hepatic tissue. In stable outpatient populations, elastography is established for staging fibrosis; ICU applications are more complex but increasingly studied.

Detecting Hepatic Congestion

As an extension of bedside ultrasound, SWE offers dynamic, repeatable evaluation of hepatic congestion. Studies report that rising liver stiffness in critically ill patients correlates with echocardiographic signs of right-sided dysfunction and features of fluid overload, suggesting SWE may add objective data where CVP and clinical exam are limited.3

Identifying Early Sepsis-related Liver Dysfunction

Conventional liver enzymes lack sensitivity for early hepatic dysfunction in sepsis. Pilot ICU studies indicate that serial increases in liver stiffness may precede biochemical derangement and correlate with clinical deterioration, supporting a role for trend-based monitoring.4

Assessing Vasopressor-related Hypoperfusion

Vasopressors, while lifesaving, can impair hepatosplanchnic blood flow; animal and human data suggest catecholamines can reduce hepatic microcirculation even when systemic hemodynamics are acceptable.5 Elastography may help detect such perfusion–congestion mismatches earlier than laboratory tests.

Are We Ready for Routine Use in the ICU?

Despite physiologic plausibility and promising data, several limitations prevent immediate widespread adoption.

The ICU is a Noisy Physiological Environment

Intra-abdominal pressure, positive-pressure ventilation (PEEP), patient positioning, and generalized edema all influence stiffness readings; mechanical ventilation alone can change values substantially, so standardization of timing and technique is essential.6

Limited High-quality Outcome Data

Most studies are observational and underpowered. No randomized controlled trials have tested SWE-guided interventions to show outcome benefit. The technology remains hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing.

Multiple Overlapping Pathologies

Congestion, inflammation, cholestasis, and fibrosis can all raise stiffness—often simultaneously—so elastography requires interpretation within the full clinical context rather than used in isolation.

Operational Challenges

Probe availability, acoustic windows in obese or edematous patients, operator training, and workflow integration can impede routine use.

Used judiciously alongside echocardiography, POCUS, and labs, SWE can broaden the clinician's understanding of shock physiology.

Why India can Lead the Research?

India's ICUs care for a high burden of sepsis, tropical infections, and cardiac complications—conditions that uniquely affect hepatic physiology. Many tertiary centers already have elastography-capable machines but lack ICU-focused studies; multicenter Indian research could answer feasibility, prognostic, and management questions while ensuring results are generalizable to our context.

Conclusion

2D-SWE provides a promising, noninvasive window into hepatic physiology in critical illness. Its ability to quantify congestion and detect evolving liver injury is compelling, but current evidence is preliminary and influenced by many confounders. For now, SWE should inform clinical thinking rather than dictate management. With standardized protocols and robust multicentre trials, especially in high-burden settings like India, elastography could become a valuable addition to multimodal ICU assessment.
