Treatment Preferences of Patients With Myasthenia Gravis: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Meg Mendoza, Elena Wolff, Michael Chou, Vera Bril, Hans Katzberg, Ahmed Bayoumi, Carolina Barnett‐Tapia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsThe burden of myasthenia gravis (MG) is often underestimated, and studies usually focus on the symptom burden. However, treatment‐related adverse events also contribute to patients' burdens and affect their treatment decisions. Understanding the treatment preferences of people with MG will help inform patients, clinicians, and policy makers. We conducted a qualitative study to understand the most relevant factors that people with MG consider when making treatment decisions.MethodsFifteen patients with a wide range of MG severity participated in semi‐structured interviews. Interviews were recorded and transcripts were analyzed using line‐by‐line open coding to generate themes. We used these themes to identify the most relevant treatment characteristics for people living with MG.ResultsFour themes were identified: MG symptoms, treatment side effects, treatment delivery preferences, and treatment goals. Double vision was the most bothersome symptom for 40% of patients, and 33% reported weight gain as the most bothersome side effect of treatment. Most patients preferred treatments in the form of pills (67%). The most frequently reported treatment goals were returning to normal or “zero symptoms” (40%), discontinuation of prednisone (40%), and a preference to take fewer pills (33%).DiscussionOur results underscore the importance of tailoring treatments to meet patient needs and preferences, emphasizing key treatment goals such as prednisone reduction, a preference for oral medications, and symptom resolution. We will incorporate these themes into a discrete choice experiment to gain a deeper understanding of how people with MG make treatment decisions when considering potential benefits and adverse events.

Introduction

There is currently no cure for myasthenia gravis (MG), and patients typically require chronic treatment to control their symptoms; complete remission (i.e., no MG symptoms while off all MG treatments) is rare [1, 2, 3]. Traditional treatments cause broad immunosuppression and are associated with many adverse events [4]. Current MG treatment guidelines recommend a treatment goal of achieving remission or minimal manifestation status, with minimal adverse events from treatment [5].

Previous work has shown that many patients with active symptoms considered their MG status as acceptable [6]. It is conceivable that these patients may be overtreated and thus exposed to unnecessary risks and side effects associated with immunosuppressive medications [7]. In contrast, studies have also shown that many individuals with MG have high symptom burden despite treatment, and qualitative work has shown that many patients feel “undertreated” [8, 9, 10]. Together, these concerns illustrate the importance of incorporating patients' perspectives into treatment decisions [11]. However, there has been limited research into treatment preferences and decision‐making in people with MG. We conducted a qualitative study of people with MG, with the aim of understanding their experience and preferences regarding symptoms and treatments. Our goal is to develop a discrete choice experiment (DCE) to study preferences and decision making in MG [12].

Methods

The University Health Network Research Ethics Board (Study number: 18–5514) approved this study, conforming to the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants provided their informed consent.

We recruited adult patients (≥ 18 years) from the Ellen and Martin Prosserman Centre for Neuromuscular Disease, Toronto, Canada. All participants had a confirmed diagnosis of MG, based on clinical presentation and either positive serology (acetylcholine receptor (AChR), muscle specific kinase (MuSK), or low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 4 (LRP4) antibodies) or abnormal repetitive nerve stimulation or single fiber electromyography. Purposive sampling was used to ensure broad representation of MG patients. Participants were excluded if they were unable to communicate in English.

Each participant was interviewed by one investigator (MM) for approximately one hour over an audio‐only call conducted through an institutionally approved platform (Teams, Microsoft, Redmond, WA). A 1‐on‐1 approach was selected to avoid potential discomfort of discussing sensitive topics in a focus group. Interviews were semi‐structured with questions regarding participants' experiences with MG, including symptom burden, treatment modalities, and side effects. We did not provide a specific timeframe and encouraged participants to describe their overall experiences. Participants were also asked to rank elicited therapeutic benefits and side effects from most to least important.

Interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. All transcripts were analyzed independently by three reviewers (MM, EW and MC) using a line‐by‐line open coding approach to generate themes inductively; we used the Dedoose platform for transcript coding [13]. An iterative coding manual was developed that evolved throughout content analysis using the constant comparative method. Data saturation, where no new codes are being generated, was used to determine sample size [14]. Coding consistency was re‐checked once the coding manual was static.

Statistical Analysis

We describe continuous data through means and standard deviations (SD), and categorical data through counts and percentages. Statistical analyses were conducted with R statistical software v3.60 (R Statistical Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

Four themes were identified across the patient interviews: MG symptoms, treatment side effects, treatment delivery preferences, and treatment goals.

Theme 1: MG Symptoms

Ptosis

One of the most frequently reported symptoms was eyelid drooping (11 of 15; 73%). “And I noticed my right eye, I was having a really hard time keeping it open. It was really droopy” (Patient 005, Female). Or:“[…I could not open my eyes. And my family did not understand. I was in the car …and I was like screaming, like, I can't open my eyes, I cannot see that. I had to put my head back, I couldn't lift my eyelids. I just had like a little slit at the bottom and if I tilted my head I could kind of see… That was really scary. I could not lift my eyelids].”(Patient 011, Female).

Diplopia

Most patients also reported double vision (11 of 15; 73%): “I used to experience double vision, especially on the highway. And I was doing lots of highway driving at the time so that distance of a few car lengths, like four or five car lengths I would get double vision” (Patient 010, Female). Diplopia was also the symptom most commonly reportedly as “most bothersome” by patients (33%).

Fatigue

Most patients also reported fatigue (10 of 15; 67%). “The worst is when general myasthenia gravis hits me because I'm absolutely useless to myself. I find that I need more rest.” (Patient 001, Male). One patient further described how fatigue affected his work schedule. “[…is just a general I start work about 7:30 or 8:00. I'm in the office at 7:30. We don't take a lunch, and by about 2:00, I'm done. I'm just generally tired, and I feel any more than that would not be a good thing so I pack everybody in, in the morning, try and do shorter appointments.]” (Patient 013, Male).

Difficulty Speaking

Approximately half of patients (8 of 15; 53%) reported problems speaking. “Well, I remember, as I said, having a conversation, I was at tea with friends, and I couldn't say the words properly and I couldn't figure out why” (Patient 004—Female). Or: “And I would start sounding drunk, like my tongue was swollen and I couldn't close my lips properly.” (Patient 011—Female).

Difficulty Chewing and Swallowing

Many patients (6 of 15; 40%) reported difficulties chewing and swallowing: “I had never had any problem with choking until later. That's the weirdest thing. Yeah, I had some issues where when I drink or eat, but more drinking, would cause me to feel I'm choking.” (Patient 001—Male). Another patient described having to modify her eating habits to accommodate swallowing difficulties. “I found that I could eat my breakfast alright and swallow it and then as the day went on it just became very difficult to swallow anything and so, I tried to load up as much as I could first thing in the morning.” (Patient 005—Female).

Hand Weakness

Some patients (6 of 15; 40%) reported difficulty with their hands. “…then things kept falling from my hands when I grasped it.”(Patient 003—Female). “The same when I was trying to carry two‐pint glasses in my hands, once or twice they would just slip right through, like I couldn't get grasp of it.”(Patient 011—Female).

Leg Weakness

Some patients (6 of 15; 40%) reported difficulties with their legs: “When I'd stand up, it was hard to stand up properly. I have to hold on. Before I had this problem, I would just stand up without holding on to anything. But now I'm feeling like I'm getting weaker.”(Patient 003—Female). “I noticed it because it was in my legs. I walked out the door, and my legs gave. It wasn't like I was fatigued or in pain. I just couldn't make my legs move normally so that's why I was just able to make it back in using walls as support, which is very out of my ordinary because two weeks before that I was biking 20K.” (Patient 012—Female).

Arm Weakness

Some patients (5 of 15; 33%) reported arm weakness. “Then, when my son was born, I went back to work when he was maybe three weeks and things similar, I'd be carrying him and I was like, whoa, I've got to put this kid down. Again, not all the time and sometimes I'd just rest him on the counter, because I felt like I was going to drop him.” (Patient 011—Female). “But then I also have muscle weakness because sometimes I do have weakness on my arms, not so much on my legs, but on my arms, my shoulders, my neck, my tongue as well.” (Patient 014—Male).

Theme 2: Treatment Side Effects

Weight Gain

This was both the most reported side effect (7 of 15; 47%) and the side effect most frequently cited as worst (5 of 15; 33%). One patient expressed her dislike of her “Well, unfortunately with Prednisone of course I gained weight, which I hated. I gained probably 30 pounds. I lost it of course afterwards after I came off the Prednisone.” (Patient 005, Female). Another patient expressed her frustration with difficulty losing the weight gained while on prednisone. “Yeah. The regular kind of weight gain with Prednisone, not outrageous but for me noticeable and kind of frustration there because I think it's tough to lose that weight. And I don't know what it is, but even when you're really active so that's been a little bit frustrating.”(Patient 010, Female).

Gastrointestinal Symptoms

Diarrhea was the second most frequently reported side effect (6 of 15; 40%). “The only downside that I've had with the Mestinon is the cholinergic side effects so instead of taking a 60 milligram tab, I have to take half because otherwise, I pretty much have urgent bowel movements.” (Patient 012, Female). Patients also described other gastrointestinal issues that accompanied diarrhea. “I had a lot of diarrhea with the 60 milligrams and I had to actually go on ranitidine, common name Zantac. I had to take that, prescribed, daily because the 60 milligrams of Mestinon was very hard on my stomach.” (Patient 007, Male). “So, there's Imuran and whatever the other one is and they basically both, I was vomiting, diarrhea, I was so sick for ages.” (Patient 011, Female).

Abdominal Pain

3 of 15 (20%) patients reported abdominal pain as a side effect of their MG related medications. One patient explained how his abdominal pain would influence his daily life. “It was more upset stomach in the terms of pain, abdominal pain, just cramps, and it's really, really tough. Sorry, I don't know how to express it, but it's really tough to get through the day without me having pain for mostly six to eight hours in my stomach, even though I still want to eat. That's the range, from six to eight hours a day, I feel pain.” (Patient 001, Male).

Diabetes/Hyperglycemia

Increase in blood sugar was reported by 3 of 15 (20%) participants, who attributed this to prednisone use: “Another problem I'm having with prednisone is the diabetes. Right now, basically, my blood sugar level is high. In general, prednisone is causing those problems to me…” (Patient 008, Male). “Prednisone induced hyperglycemia so because of my cystic fibrosis and my family history, I'm at risk for diabetes in both senses. So, because of the Prednisone, I've had elevated blood sugars since.” (Patient 012, Female).

Mood Swings

3 of 15 (20%) participants also reported mood swings as a side effect of their medications. “I saw the adverse effect of the Prednisone, which was the mood swings, weight gain.”(Patient 007, Male), and “…I'm a little aggressive when I'm on the medication.” (Patient 015, Female).

Theme 3: Treatment Delivery Preferences

Preference for Pills

Most participants (10 of 15; 67%) expressed that pills were their preferred mode of treatment. “I think I would like to have one pill once a day or twice a day and it works right away.” (Patient 014, Male), and: “Taking the pills is actually really a convenience to me in this case.”(Patient 008, Male). Another patient explained that she prefers pills over needles. “I don't like needles, no needles. I prefer to take pills, no needles.”(Patient 009, Female).

At‐Home Treatment

A large proportion of patients reported a preference for at‐home treatments (7 of 15; 47%). “IVIg was okay in the beginning, but later on the whole day I was there in the hospital and I was getting scared of those people coughing all over me. And I'm so scared I get all this bacteria from them.” (Patient 003, Female). Some patients specifically highlighted their dislike of hospitals: “I would prefer at‐home treatment, based on my experience going through this illness. My trips to the hospital was not, I'm going to say, the best experience and I hate hospitals. I don't like hospitals. I would prefer at‐home treatment.” (Patient 007, Male).

Fewer Side Effects

Almost half of participants preferred treatments with fewer side effects (7 of 15; 47%). “Anything with less foreseeable side effects, even though I'm not sure whether that type of medication exists. It probably doesn't exist.” (Patient 009, Female). Another patient identified specific side effects that were intolerable or tolerable. “side effect‐wise, I think maybe I just don't tolerate that many side effects because really to me, it's the GI side effects that are tough. Yeah, maybe anti‐cholinergic side effects I would tolerate, like, dry mouth and all that kind of stuff. I think I can handle those.” (Patient 012, Female).

Theme 4: Treatment Goals

Discontinuation of Prednisone

A significant number of patients (6 of 15; 40%) expressed their desire to discontinue prednisone. One patient specifically expressed concerns over its safety: “… I know that one of the things I've asked in the past is, the intention for me was to get off prednisone. Prednisone is a very dangerous drug to me, in my opinion. Likewise, Imuran. I don't think Mestinon is as dangerous as the prednisone or the Imuran. Imuran solely negates your immune system. Prednisone does all sorts of harm to your body. And the intention for me was to get off prednisone” (Patient 007, Male). Another patient reported preferring her MG symptoms over prednisone. “Like, I am willing to deal with MG and not deal with prednisone.” (Patient 011, Female).

Returning to Normal

Many patients (6 of 15; 40%) expressed a desire of achieving zero symptoms; however, many patients felt that was not possible: “… I would really like to get back to normal. Getting back to normal would be about the best thing that could happen, but it's not going to happen as far as I know. As far as I know, right now is probably the best I will be, and it might get worse. That would be about as good as it's going to get.” (Patient 006, Male). Another patient expressed the importance of going back to normal regarding his ability to work and support his family: “I don't know about the other patients, but for myself, I definitely want normal, no symptoms, mainly because, basically, as I have been told, I'm still young. I don't believe that. Basically, I still have a family to support, and I need to work, unfortunately. I prefer no symptoms, being able to walk, when I present people can look at me on zoom. I'm not turning on a video, that kind of stuff.” (Patient 008, Male).

Taking Fewer Pills

Some patients (5 of 15; 33%) reported the goal of reducing the number of pills: “… I don't know what amounts it comes in, but I do have to take three pills morning and evening. It would be nicer if there was access to two pills or even a one…Yeah, and that way you don't feel like you're popping a whole bunch of pills” (Patient 010, Female). And:“If you have to take a pill three times a day, I wish they would come up with time‐released so in that way you have it throughout your system for the whole day.” (Patient 001, Male).

Discussion

In this qualitative study of individuals with MG, we have identified four domains relating to how people with MG approach treatment decisions: MG symptoms, medication side effects, treatment delivery options, and overall treatment goals.

Improvement of symptoms often drives therapeutic decisions in MG, and clinical trials focus on symptoms as the primary outcome. We found that ptosis, diplopia and general fatigue were the most frequently reported symptoms, and one‐third of our participants reported diplopia as their worst symptom, despite having generalized symptoms. This is in keeping with previous studies, where diplopia is often ranked as a bothersome symptom [15, 16].

This is not surprising as diplopia affects many daily activities, such as reading, working on a computer, and driving. However, diplopia is often considered a “mild” symptom, and outcome measures that weight items such as the MG Composite give severe diplopia a low score compared to other MG manifestations [17]. This likely reflects clinicians' concerns with life‐threatening manifestations, such as swallowing or respiratory difficulties, that are typically weighted as more severe. Additionally, patients with pure ocular symptoms are usually excluded from clinical trials. Our study suggests that diplopia has a marked detrimental effect on patients' quality of life and treatment decisions should reflect this. Generalized fatigue is also a common patient‐reported symptom linked to disease severity and reduced quality of life [16]. However, many commonly used measures in MG trials do not include measures of fatigue; likewise, many patients reported hand weakness that is not measured by most MG outcome measures.

Weight gain, diarrhea, abdominal pain, hyperglycemia and mood swings were the most frequently reported side effects, and one‐third of patients reported weight gain as the worst side effect. In agreement with our results, a recent qualitative study of 14 US patients with MG reported weight gain and diarrhea amongst the most prevalent side effects caused by MG treatments [11]. Bacci et al. showed that, along with severe side effects such as blood clots and pneumonia, weight gain was reported as being poorly tolerated by patients [11]. Many of these side effects are secondary to chronic steroid use, which is in keeping with reducing or stopping prednisone emerging as a treatment goal for patients. Surprisingly, infections were not a major treatment‐related concern for the patients interviewed, despite the potential of being a severe complication of immunosuppressive therapy. Interestingly, some patients reported avoiding hospitals and physicians' offices given frequent infections, so while it seems there is a concern regarding infections, it was not directly tied to the treatments in this study. Future work is needed to understand how people with MG internalize the risk of infections in relation to treatments.

Most patients preferred pills to infusions and at‐home treatments rather than hospital‐based. This is concurrent with a study on treatment preferences of patients with chronic immune disorders, who preferred subcutaneous injections instead of intravenous infusions at a hospital, due to convenience [18]. Many of the emergent interventions approved for MG are intravenous, with increasing uptake of at‐home infusions, rather than in hospitals or infusion centers. The availability of at‐home infusions may shift patient preferences; this was not captured in our study since at the time of the interviews, novel biologics were not widely available in Canada and i.v. immunoglobulin home infusion is not available. Additionally, subcutaneous route and formulations with extended time in between infusions are becoming available, which may also change how patients make treatment decisions [19]. Our findings suggest that if available, patients would prefer oral formulations, and ideally formulations minimizing the number of pills taken per day; administration route preferences will be formally studied in the upcoming DCE.

While many patients felt that complete remission was unrealistic, patients expressed a desire for not only symptom improvement but for full resolution, indicating the need for efficacious therapies. With the expansive growth of novel therapeutics for MG, the aim should shift from “improvement” to reaching a satisfactory level of disease control, such as Minimal Symptom Expression (MSE), although this is concept‐limited given the known floor effects of the Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily living scale, from which it is derived [6]. Other potential goals include reaching Patient Acceptable Symptom State (PASS) or remission [6, 20, 21]. In keeping with the multiple side effects of prednisone, an important goal is to reduce the dose and ideally stop completely. Therefore, steroid‐sparing efficacy is an important factor for new treatment considerations in people with MG.

Some limitations of this study included limited generalizability inherent to qualitative studies. Additionally, recruitment from a single tertiary academic center may reflect more severe patients than in other settings. Some patients had long disease duration, which may result in recall bias regarding remote symptoms and/or side effects. Many participants received more than one treatment simultaneously or successively, and this may affect their recollection of specific side effects attributed to an intervention or a given dose. However, our goal was to understand the most concerning adverse events for patients, regardless of the causative medication.

Clinical decision‐making involves balancing potential benefits and adverse events of treatments. We have identified important factors regarding outcomes, efficacy, and adverse events for people living with MG. Further work is needed to understand how these individuals make trade‐offs when making these decisions, and we have incorporated the findings from this qualitative study into an ongoing DCE. For a hypothetical new intervention for MG, our DCE survey includes the following attributes: 3 efficacy attributes defined as improvement in ocular, bulbar, and generalized symptoms; one efficacy attribute defined as ability to reduce prednisone dose, and one attribute for administration route. While in the present study infections did not come up as a main concern, we added one attribute on infection risk to better understand how patients incorporate this into their decision‐making process. The upcoming DCE will allow better understanding of which treatment characteristics are more important for patients, assessment of whether some patient groups have different preferences, and whether patients' preferences differ from those of clinicians.
