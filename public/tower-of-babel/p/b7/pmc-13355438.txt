Renal vein thrombosis with inferior vena cava extension in PLA2R-positive membranous nephropathy: A radiological mimic of acute pyelonephritis
Authors: Srikanth Nalla, Pravin Sudhakar Patil, Sumavally Maydari, Pawar Swathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Renal vein thrombosis (RVT) is a recognized but often underdiagnosed complication of nephrotic syndrome, particularly in membranous nephropathy. We report the case of a 37-year-old male with PLA2R-positive membranous nephropathy who developed complete thrombosis of the left renal vein with extension into the inferior vena cava (IVC) over a length of approximately 5 cm. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) demonstrated poor enhancement of the left kidney with extensive perinephric and periureteric fat stranding, closely mimicking acute pyelonephritis. Laboratory evaluation revealed massive proteinuria (∼12 g/d) with no clinical or laboratory evidence of infection. The patient was treated with anticoagulation and immunosuppressive therapy (modified Ponticelli regimen), with subsequent clinical improvement. This case highlights an important diagnostic pitfall and underscores the importance of clinico-radiological correlation.

Introduction

Renal vein thrombosis (RVT) is a well-recognized but often underdiagnosed complication of nephrotic syndrome, particularly in membranous nephropathy, which carries the highest risk of thromboembolic events among glomerular diseases. The hypercoagulable state in nephrotic syndrome results from urinary loss of natural anticoagulants such as antithrombin III, increased hepatic synthesis of procoagulant factors, and enhanced platelet aggregation [1].

Imaging plays a crucial role in diagnosis; however, RVT may present with nonspecific radiological findings such as renal enlargement, poor nephrogram, and perinephric fat stranding. These features overlap significantly with acute pyelonephritis, creating a potential diagnostic pitfall [2]. Awareness of this overlap is essential to avoid misdiagnosis. We present a case of PLA2R-positive membranous nephropathy complicated by RVT with IVC extension, highlighting this important radiological pitfall.

Case presentation

A 37-year-old male presented with a 10-day history of dull, aching left flank pain. There was no history of fever, dysuria, hematuria, or vomiting. He was a known hypertensive with no other significant comorbidities.

On examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable. Abdominal examination was unremarkable.

A 24-hour urinary protein estimation during the same admission revealed massive proteinuria of 12,075 mg/d, confirming nephrotic syndrome.

Collectively, these findings did not demonstrate evidence of urinary tract infection and were consistent with nephrotic syndrome.

Further evaluation confirmed PLA2R-positive membranous nephropathy. ANA profile was negative.

CT protocol

Contrast-enhanced CT was performed using a multidetector CT scanner following intravenous administration of nonionic contrast (approximately 80-100 mL at 3-4 mL/s). Images were acquired in corticomedullary and nephrographic phases with thin-section reconstructions and multiplanar reformations.

Imaging findings

Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated complete thrombosis of the left renal vein with extension into the suprarenal inferior vena cava over a length of approximately 5 cm, representing the dominant imaging abnormality.

Associated findings included:•Enlargement of the left kidney.•Reduced and heterogeneous enhancement of the left renal parenchyma with a poor nephrogram, best appreciated during the nephrographic phase.•Extensive perinephric and periureteric fat stranding extending into the left lumbar region.•Mild prominence of the proximal left ureter.

No focal renal mass, urinary tract calculus, or significant hydronephrosis was identified.

These associated renal parenchymal and perinephric changes closely resembled acute pyelonephritis on imaging; however, the presence of complete renal vein thrombosis with IVC extension established the diagnosis of venous outflow obstruction.

Management and follow-up

The patient was treated with anticoagulation (Apixaban), modified Ponticelli regimen (intravenous methylprednisolone followed by oral corticosteroids), and supportive care.

Apixaban was chosen due to patient preference for oral anticoagulation and institutional experience; however, evidence for direct oral anticoagulants in nephrotic syndrome remains limited, and vitamin K antagonists remain an alternative.

At 3-month follow-up, the patient was asymptomatic with stable renal function.

Follow-up renal Doppler ultrasound demonstrated no obvious thrombus within the inferior vena cava; however, assessment of recanalization of the left renal vein was limited due to suboptimal sonographic window.

Proteinuria showed a declining trend on follow-up.

Discussion

The principal radiological challenge in this case was differentiating renal vein thrombosis from acute pyelonephritis. The CT findings of renal enlargement, poor nephrogram, and extensive perinephric fat stranding are commonly associated with inflammatory renal disease and may strongly suggest acute pyelonephritis. However, these findings are not specific and may also occur in the setting of venous outflow obstruction.

In our patient, the presence of complete thrombosis of the left renal vein with extension into the suprarenal inferior vena cava was the key imaging finding that explained the associated renal abnormalities. Venous obstruction results in increased renal venous pressure, impaired renal perfusion, and interstitial edema. These changes can manifest as delayed renal enhancement, renal enlargement, and perinephric inflammatory-appearing stranding, thereby closely mimicking infection.

The differential diagnosis for a poorly enhancing enlarged kidney with perinephric fat stranding includes acute pyelonephritis, renal infarction, obstructive uropathy, and renal vein thrombosis. Acute pyelonephritis is usually supported by fever, elevated inflammatory markers, pyuria, and positive urine cultures. Renal infarction typically demonstrates wedge-shaped cortical perfusion defects and may show the cortical rim sign. In contrast, our patient lacked clinical evidence of infection and demonstrated direct visualization of renal vein thrombosis extending into the IVC, making RVT the most likely diagnosis.

Membranous nephropathy is the glomerular disease most strongly associated with thromboembolic complications. Hypercoagulability results from urinary loss of anticoagulant proteins, increased hepatic production of clotting factors, and platelet activation. The risk increases substantially in patients with heavy proteinuria and hypoalbuminemia, as demonstrated in our patient with proteinuria exceeding 12 g/d.

Previous reports by Mittal et al. and Majid et al. described renal vein thrombosis in membranous nephropathy but primarily focused on clinical presentation and management [3,4]. In contrast, the present case emphasizes the radiological appearance and diagnostic challenge posed by RVT when it mimics acute pyelonephritis.

Previous studies have described the relationship between pyelonephritis and RVT in the opposite direction. Wirth et al. [5] and Bencherifi et al. [6] reported cases in which acute pyelonephritis led to RVT and IVC thrombosis through thromboinflammatory mechanisms [5,6]. In those cases, patients presented with clear clinical and laboratory evidence of infection.

Our case represents a reverse diagnostic scenario, wherein RVT secondary to nephrotic syndrome produces imaging findings that mimic acute pyelonephritis in the absence of infection.

Importantly, our patient had no fever or urinary symptoms, normal inflammatory markers, and serial urinalyses that did not demonstrate evidence of urinary tract infection. These clinicoradiological findings were critical in distinguishing RVT from acute pyelonephritis and avoiding diagnostic delay.

Failure to recognize this entity may lead to unnecessary antibiotic therapy and delayed anticoagulation, increasing the risk of thrombus propagation and serious complications such as pulmonary embolism. Early recognition allows appropriate management and improved outcomes.

From a radiological perspective, careful evaluation of the renal veins and IVC is essential when imaging findings and clinical presentation are discordant. Identification of a venous filling defect and absence of infective features should prompt consideration of RVT.

Conclusion

Renal vein thrombosis should be considered in the differential diagnosis of an enlarged poorly enhancing kidney with perinephric fat stranding, particularly in patients with nephrotic syndrome. Although these findings may closely resemble acute pyelonephritis, identification of renal vein thrombosis and associated IVC extension on contrast-enhanced CT is critical for establishing the correct diagnosis and initiating appropriate anticoagulant therapy. Careful clinico-radiological correlation remains essential to avoid diagnostic error.
