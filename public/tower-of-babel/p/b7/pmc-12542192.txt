Operational and clinical procedures of heroin-assisted treatment in Switzerland: a nation-wide survey study
Authors: Maximilian Meyer, Adrian Quinto, Adrian Guessoum, Johannes Strasser, Kenneth M. Dürsteler, Undine E. Lang, Marc Vogel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeroin-assisted treatment (HAT) was introduced in Switzerland in 1994 and comprises the prescription of diacetylmorphine (DAM, heroin) for patients with severe opioid use disorder. Provision of Swiss HAT is limited to specialised treatment centres, 22 of which operate today. The aim of this study was to assess the characteristics and clinical procedures of these centres.MethodsA questionnaire was designed and sent out to all operating Swiss HAT centres.ResultsThe response rate was 91% and all questionnaires were filled in by the medical heads of the corresponding centres. All centres employed psychiatrists and prescribed psychiatric medication. Additionally, 85% reported to offer in-house psychotherapeutic treatment and 95% reported to employ social workers. Few treatment barriers to HAT were found, with the mean time from referral to treatment initiation being 10 days. Only one centre reported to employ a waiting list. Off-label prescriptions, including intramuscular and intranasal administrations, were common. All centres offered the concurrent prescription of methadone and slow-release oral morphine. Furthermore, all centres prescribed take-home DAM. However, vast differences among centres regarding DAM dosing and titration were found. No uniformly employed factors for converting DAM to other opioids exist across centres. Overdoses and seizures were reported to be very rare.ConclusionsSwiss HAT is overall easily accessible, patient-centred and safe for patients and staff. Some procedures are based on local tradition rather than on scientific evidence. Evidence-based treatment recommendations are needed to further improve quality of care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12954-025-01325-6.

Introduction

Heroin-assisted treatment (HAT) was introduced in Switzerland in 1994 for patients with opioid use disorder (OUD) who do not stabilise with conventional oral opioid agonist treatment (OAT) [1]. It comprises the prescription and usually medically supervised administration of diacetylmorphine (DAM, pharmaceutical heroin). Following introduction in Switzerland, HAT has been implemented successfully in many European countries and Canada [2–4]. In addition, methadone, slow-release oral morphine (SROM), buprenorphine, and levomethadone are used for conventional OAT [5]. Compared to OAT with methadone, HAT is superior regarding treatment retention and reduction of high-risk substance use, and it was found to be cost-effective [6]. This is mostly explained by significant reductions of criminal offending [7, 8].

In 2022, 1801 patients received HAT in Switzerland, corresponding to 10% of all patients in Switzerland receiving OAT [9, 10]. This number has remained largely stable for the past two decades. This is likely explained by the rapid treatment uptake by eligible patients following its implementation, an ageing patient population, and fewer young people developing OUD when compared to the 1980s [11]. Notably, all treatment centres providing HAT must be licensed through the Federal Office of Public Health (FOPH). However, no regulation exists regarding the operating body, which can either be a hospital, a tax-funded organisation, or a privately funded institution (e.g., a foundation).

Unlike the prescription of traditional oral opioid agonists, access to HAT is strictly regulated. Unchanged since 1994, juridical entry criteria comprise a minimum age of 18, history of OUD for at least 2 years, at least two unsuccessful treatment attempts for OUD, and physical, mental, or social consequences due to their opioid use [12]. Furthermore, take-home prescriptions for traditional OAT are available for up to 30 days, depending on cantonal regulation. In HAT, take-home prescriptions are limited to a maximum of seven days and only available for patients with uninterrupted HAT for at least six months, deemed socially and medically stable, and a risk of misuse estimated to be low by the prescribing physician [12]. Importantly, prescription of DAM is limited to 22 specialised treatment centres. However, HAT centres are allowed to delegate DAM dispensing to other institutions, e.g., to ensure treatment continuation during hospital stays.

In Switzerland, DAM has been approved for intravenous (IV) and oral use. Three products are available for HAT: Diaphin® IR (instant-release tablets), Diaphin® SR (slow-release tablets), and Diaphin® solution (100 mg/ml). Notably, reports of off-label therapeutic use have become more common in recent years [13, 14]. As the Swiss HAT patient population is ageing, health concerns associated with IV injections have paved the way for novel routes of administration [15]. These off-label routes of administration include intramuscular injections due to the deterioration of peripheral access veins, and intranasal DAM administrations which are currently being investigated in an observational cohort study [16, 17].

To a great deal, procedures in Swiss HAT are not standardised, and scientific literature providing comprehensive guidance regarding every-day clinical decision-making is scarce. Although international protocols exist, their guidelines are often chosen for pragmatic reasons and are not a result of systematic assessments of different treatment procedures [18–20]. Equally, a handbook intended for clinicians working in Swiss HAT was released by the FOPH in the year 2000 but has since not been updated [21]. In Switzerland and internationally, HAT centres likely adopted standards and entry criteria that are based on previous study procedures or traditions but have only rarely been scientifically scrutinised. The present study aims to compare existing treatment structures, regulations, and clinical procedures in Swiss HAT. This will be instrumental for future establishment of national HAT standards and identification of substantial differences which should be investigated in further research. In the short term, it may allow for an update of the Swiss handbook to better reflect the clinical reality of how HAT is practised. It may also provide guidance for international treatment providers.

Methods

Questionnaire

A questionnaire in German language was developed by the study team. Topics were selected based on the expertise of the study team (two residents, two psychiatrists, and one psychologist), who have longstanding experience in substance use disorder research and HAT. The study team discussed and reached consensus on inclusion of specific items to ensure their relevance in covering the study aim. The questionnaire was piloted by another physician with several years of experience in HAT who was not involved in its design. Feedback from the piloting process was used to improve language clarity and reduce ambiguity. The questionnaire was then translated to English. The questionnaire contained questions on several different areas and characteristics of centres and applied treatment procedures, such as centre size, available routes of administration, injection sites, or take-home dosing. Furthermore, it asked for several dosing scenarios and conversion factors, such as transfer from 100 mg IV, initial doses of IV or oral DAM as well as dose limits. Items consisted of multiple-choice questions, yes/no-questions, free-text answers. The questionnaire consisted of 77 items and three case vignettes (see Additional file 1).

Participants and study procedures

A complete list of all authorised centres for HAT in Switzerland was retrieved through the FOPH [22]. We generated a personalised participant code for each centre and sent it out to the respective responsible medical directors. All data were collected through the online survey tool EFS Survey (Tivian XI GmbH, Germany).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise results. The provided case vignettes were answered by centres in free-text form. The responses were then summarised and categorised by MM and AQ independently from each other to ensure quality. No inferential statistical methods were employed.

Results

The response rate was 91% (i.e., 20 of 22 Swiss HAT centres). All questionnaires were answered by the head physician of the respective centre.

Operational and treatment-related centre characteristics

Eleven centres (55%) were operating for 25 years or longer, 8 centres (40%) were operating 15 years or longer and 1 centre (5%) started operating within the past 5 years. Eleven centres (55%) were affiliated with psychiatric clinics, whereas nine centres were run by public or private organisations (e.g., tax-funded or a foundation, as opposed to hospital-departments). Fourteen (74%) centres continued the prescription of DAM for patients during short hospital stays (i.e., when being treated for a physical illness in another hospital). Eighteen centres (90%) opened every day of the year, whereas 2 centres (10%) did not operate on weekends. The median number of employees was 12 (M = 18; SD = 16), with the lowest number being five and the maximum number being 80 employees. Being in treatment for mental or physical disorders to receive HAT was not required in 19 (95%) centres, whereas one (5%) centre required patients to be in treatment in case of acute psychosis.

Centres were asked whether they employed a fixed or flexible schedule for the number of daily visits, i.e., one visit in the morning and one in the afternoon, or a timeframe in which the patient can choose their number of visits. In total, 18 (90%) centres stated to have a fixed schedule, and two (10%) centres stated to have a flexible schedule. Furthermore, 11 (55%) centres stated to have a time limit for the self-administration (injection procedure), ranging from five to 20 min. Seven (35%) centres limited the number of injection attempts to three. When asked about the procedure in cases where the time- or attempt-limit was reached, seven (35%) centres stated that the patient had to inject IM or SC, one (5%) centre stated that staff handled the injection, and six (30%) centres stated, that the patient would be required to switch their route of administration to oral or intranasal. In addition, five (25%) centres stated to seek individual solutions with patients having trouble injecting. An overview of operational centre characteristics is provided in Table 1.

IV DAM titration schedules for HAT initiation in patients with illicit intravenous heroin use

The maximum available total daily IV DAM dose at day three ranged between 70 mg (Example centre 5) and 1620 mg (Example centre 15).

Oral DAM titration at HAT initiation in patients with illicit intravenous heroin use

Regarding titration of the oral DAM dose in a patient with illicit intravenous heroin use at initiation of treatment, most centres (n = 11) stated to prescribe an initial dose of 100 mg in the morning with one or two additional doses within the same day. Daily dose increases mostly consisted of 100 mg to 200 mg increments. One centre stated to be more careful, only increasing the dose every other day. Again, there were large variations between centres, resulting in one centre providing a lower initial oral DAM dose (100 mg) than other centres provided intravenously (up to 195 mg). Centres were asked, how many times patients were found to be attempting to divert DAM prescribed for supervised intake (i.e., remove DAM from the clinic rather than using it onsite). Diversion attempts ranged from 0 to 4.5 attempts per patient per month (M = 0.27, SD = 1.0). Within the past month, six centres (30%) reported zero total diversion attempts, eight centres (40%) reported between one and three total diversion attempts, three centres (15%) reported five total diversion attempts, one centre (5%) reported 10 diversion attempts, and one centre (5%) reported 20 diversion attempts. One centre (5%) specified that three of their patients try to divert medication almost daily, culminating to a total of 90 diversion attempts in those three patients.

Treatment goals

Treatment goals were overall similar across centres with the exception of abstinence, as four (20%) centres stating that abstinence from all opioids was a typical treatment goal, and 8 (40%) of centres stating that even abstinence from illicit opioids was not a typical treatment goal (Table 8).

Discussion

This is the first study to systematically explore the clinical procedures applied in the different Swiss HAT centres. The response rate for the survey was 91% with 20 out of 22 centres participating. Our results are therefore representative of the current state of HAT in Switzerland. Importantly, we found substantial differences between local procedures and structural organisation between the centres, that have almost all been operating for 15 years or more. Some of these variations, such as number of staff and different professions employed, are well explained by the different size of the treatment centres, which varies between a low double digit number to several hundred, or different modi operandi such as opening times or number of available dispensings. However, our results also demonstrate an apparent lack of standardisation in areas which would be well amenable to a more unified approach, such as dosing schedules or treatment goals. Following, we discuss the most important points in more detail.

Our results suggest relatively few barriers to initiate HAT, as referral is possible by various institutions as well as the patients themselves. This includes legal authorities, as 60% of centres stated to conduct mandated treatment, underlining that the efficacy of HAT is recognised not only in the medical field but also on a political level. Although the average time to treatment initiation of ten days is likely short from an international perspective, a range of three days to a maximum of three weeks was reported. While some delay can be explained by administrative processes (e.g., applying for approval at the FOPH), the extent of this difference between centres is still surprising. As the state of health of patients applying for HAT is often critical and untreated OUD can be life-threatening, a delay of up to three weeks could be detrimental. However, as most centres stated to also offer conventional OAT, for which cantonal approval can often be obtained within a few working days, it may be the case that some centres start patients on oral agonists while approval is pending. Notably, this is speculative, as we did not assess this in our questionnaire. Furthermore, only one centre reported to use waiting lists for new treatment referrals, suggesting that there is generally sufficient treatment capacity for new patients.

Equally, most centres reported the availability of waiting rooms, a range of possible injection sites, routes of administration (including off-label), and several other opioid agonists available for combination with DAM. Two centres reported that slow-release DAM tablets were unavailable. Slow-release DAM is likely redundant due to its pharmacological similarity to slow-release oral morphine, commonly used in conventional OAT in Switzerland. Interestingly, only one centre reported the availability of a drug checking service. This is likely explained by centres not tolerating non-prescribed substances at their treatment sites, as it might interfere with the treatment goal of other patients aiming to cease illicit substance use. Although only 60% of centres stated abstinence from illicit substances to be a treatment goal, the presence of street drugs directly at treatment centres might threaten the stability of patients who managed to stop using them. On the other hand, instances of unregulated synthetic opioids emerging in Switzerland have sparked the discussion of expanding preventive measures to mitigate a potential future overdose crisis [23, 24]. Currently, drug checking in Switzerland is mostly limited to clubbing or harm-reduction facilities (e.g., safe injection rooms) [25]. Expanding its access to treatment centres might be instrumental in the future and seems feasible, considering that 70% of centres already offer a needle exchange service. Equally, 30% of centres not offering a needle exchange service although having injection paraphernalia available, cannot be explained by cantonal legislation, as harm-reduction is part of the federal four-pillar drug policy and federal law explicitly defines harm-reduction goals [12]. Rather, it may point towards an area of tension between medical treatment and the harm-reduction paradigm.

Three centres reported allowing groin injection. These are considered dangerous due to the proximity of the iliac vein and artery but reports of the practice within and outside treatment settings are common [26, 27]. Injection assistance through trained nurses, as practiced in 50% of centres, could mitigate the risks of groin injections and provide a chance of helping patients to a less harmful route of administration. One centre reported that injections are done by staff which seems to be an isolated and local tradition. For patients seeking the effect of fast-acting opioids but unable to use peripheral access veins, other routes of administration such as the intranasal route may be a promising option to reduce injection events [16]. Surprisingly, employed routes of administration among centres varied greatly, from centres not prescribing oral DAM alone to various combinations of injectable, oral or nasal DAM. It is unclear why these differences exist among treatment centres, as route of administration of DAM prescriptions should ideally follow patients’ preferences. They might be explained by some smaller centres lacking know-how and clinical experience, leading providers to opt for the most straightforward prescription approach, rather than individualised prescribing.

Take-home prescriptions for DAM were available in all participating centres, likely due to the positive experiences made during the COVID-19 pandemic and the resulting relaxation of the legal regulations [28, 29]. However, 60% of centres limited carry doses to DAM tablets only. Even though legislation does not consider galenics in its take-home criteria, the injectable solution is not available as a pre-packaged dose but must be prepared individually from vials for each patient and are typically handed out in generic syringes. This may deter centres from liquid take-home prescription, as additional resources are needed to label syringes which otherwise could be mistaken for a less dangerous substance outside of HAT settings. Compared to tablets, liquid take-home doses are also more challenging for logistical reasons, as patients need to keep the solution cooled to prevent degradation of DAM to morphine. Interestingly, six centres reported to never fully exhaust the permitted seven-day take-home duration, whereas one centre reported prescribing take-home doses for a duration longer than legislation allows. Similarly, vast differences were found in the proportion of patients receiving take-home DAM, ranging from 10% to 90%. This points towards centres handling and applying take-home criteria differently and that no uniform standard exists. Some providers, particularly in smaller centres, may be more cautious than others due to concerns of overdose or diversion. Furthermore, all centres have individual contracts with health insurances, which may limit the availability to provide take-home doses due to financial constraints. This might particularly hold true for privately run centres, for which a healthy economic performance is paramount to be able to operate.

Swiss HAT is led by psychiatrists, as underlined by their employment in all participating centres. Likely, this will guarantee some form of treatment for most concurrent psychiatric disorders, reflected by all centres also dispensing psychiatric medication. From an international perspective, this might also be a key success factor of Swiss HAT. In 95% of centres patients are not required to receive psychiatric treatment for concurrent disorders from third parties but are able to receive additional psychiatric care simultaneous to HAT. Less than half the centres employed a general physician or internal medicine specialist, suggesting shortcomings in treatment of physical illnesses, albeit most centres dispensed somatic medication. Furthermore, only two centres employed peer workers, even though their positive impact in addiction treatment settings is well established [30]. However, the work of peers is not reimbursed by health insurance in Switzerland. This illustrates that there is a clear need for establishing reimbursement when aiming to introduce new treatment forms. On the other hand, all but one centre employed social workers, and more than half employed psychologists, pointing towards Swiss HAT comprising broad psychosocial support rather than limiting itself to the mere dispensing of medication.

We found large variances for opioid conversion factors between DAM and other opioid agonists as well as for dosing schedules. For example, one centre reported to convert 100 mg IV DAM to 15 mg oral methadone, whereas another centre stated 50 mg oral methadone as equivalent dose. This indicates that some centres are operating with their own clinical experience and traditions. There is a lack of evidence-based conversion guidelines. The FOPH handbook on HAT provides recommendations based on clinical experiences and suggests converting 100 mg IV DAM to 30 mg oral methadone, which aligns with the mean of provided doses (31.4 mg) by centres [21]. The vast difference of opioid conversion standards among centres was reflected in almost all provided examples: the initial oral DAM doses provided for patients switching from 100 mg methadone ranged from 100 mg to 1000 mg, again underlining that local practices are likely not evidence based. Equally, DAM titration formulas did in most instances deviate from the suggested guidelines outlined in the HAT handbook, in many cases significantly exceeding the suggested doses. Clinicians have likely gathered more experience since the release of the handbook 25 years ago, leading to less precautious DAM prescribing. Using overly cautious procedures could result in lower treatment retention and potential underdosing of patients. Studies evaluating conversion factors and dosing procedures are necessary to find the best patient-centred dosing schedules. The very low number of life-threatening intoxications reported by the participating centres suggests that higher doses and conversion factors applied are safe.

The need for evidence-based treatment standards is also exemplified by 16 centres stating that they used a defined maximum IV DAM dose. This limiting is likely explained by safety concerns (such as overdose events or fear of diversion) but comes at the risk of insufficiently addressing individual opioid needs of patients, potentially putting them at risk, as they might revert to unregulated opioid-use. Safety concerns are also reflected in 70% of centres reporting to conduct either a mandatory pre- or post-assessment (or both). Approximately 1800 patients receive HAT in Switzerland, whereas only one intoxication requiring the presence of emergency responders was reported within the past month in this study. As 40% of centres stated to not conduct any post-assessment - even though most centres had a post-administration waiting room available - it is therefore questionable whether post-assessments are required at all. They might present a treatment barrier, as patients need to tolerate waiting for a certain amount of time instead of pursuing other private or professional activities. On the other hand, many centres in Switzerland follow the FOPH handbook recommendation and use the vigilance score (V-Score), a clinician-rated tool which can be conducted in a short timeframe. Further research is needed to investigate whether post-assessments in HAT improve patient safety. Likewise, only very few centres reported seizures following DAM administration within the last year. This aligns with reports in available literature, stating that HAT is overall safe regarding intoxications although seizures occasionally happen [6, 31]. Centres were also asked to estimate the number of medication diversion attempts, as diversion of DAM is a safety concern among treatment providers [32]. Centres noted that diversion attempts exist, although overall they appear to be a rare phenomenon. The increasing availability of take-home in recent years may have reduced the occurrence of this phenomenon. As for patient and staff security, only two centres reported the employment of security personnel. As one participating centre operates within a prison, this result was expected [33]. Overall, security personnel are likely unnecessary in HAT settings but increase the operating costs and might discourage patients from treatment participation. From an international perspective, employing security staff may however still be warranted, depending on centre location and patient population. Furthermore, urinalyses are practised by all but one centre at treatment initiation, whereas only one centre stated to use the results to inform staff about whether take-home criteria were fulfilled. This indicates that urinalyses are rarely utilised to assess non-opioid substance use in Swiss HAT.

Finally, it is important to note that variability in treatment procedures may also reflect an approach to patient-centred care, rather than negligence. As exemplified by the case vignettes, centres commonly stated to seek individual solutions with patients instead of rigidly following predefined rules and consequences. Whereas the standardisation of dosing regimens and conversion factors would be helpful in the future, patients and providers likely benefit from being able to handle delicate clinical situations more flexible and individualised. This is also particularly important to account for differences in patient populations between centres. As our case vignettes do not cover psychosocial treatment aspects of HAT, such as managing unstable housing, age-related needs, or support in attending official appointments, future research should explore how HAT centres employing social workers handle these challenges.

Conclusions

Treatment procedures differ substantially among Swiss HAT centres, most of which offer a broad range of medical, psychiatric, and social services. Some centres seem to follow local rules and traditions not sufficiently grounded in scientific evidence. These traditions mostly seem to affect dose conversion and titration procedures. However, Swiss HAT is overall easily accessible, individualised, and safe for staff and patients alike. Take-home dosing is practised commonly, although not all centres exhaust the full take-home duration permitted by law. Diversion is a rare phenomenon, likely explained by frequent take-home prescriptions. Although the time to treatment initiation is short from an international perspective, administrative processes seem to delay initiation in some centres. Evidence-based treatment recommendations, e.g., through scientific studies addressing specific topics such as dosing, and an update of the Swiss HAT handbook, could contribute to establishing the most effective and patient-centred practice. Likewise, clinical flexibility in handling delicate situations relating to diversion, use of additional substances, and intoxication allow for patient-centred care by finding individual solutions.
