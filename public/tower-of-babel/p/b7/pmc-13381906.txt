Study on the dynamic failure performance of bamboo fiber/high–density polyethylene composites with different fiber content using digital image correlation
Authors: Jianxiu Hao, Jinjiang Zhou, Yanqiu Ma, Mengyuan Dun, Haitao Fu, Guanggong Zong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study employed a low–speed drop–hammer–impact test combined with the digital image correlation technique to investigate the dynamic failure performance of bamboo fiber/high–density polyethylene (BPE) composites and to explore the mechanism of fiber content’s influence on it. Results indicated that with the increasing of BF content, the density, acoustic velocity, and Poisson’s ratio of BPE gradually decreased, while the elastic modulus initially decreased and then increased. Although the introduction of Bamboo Fiber (BF) enhanced the rigidity of BPE, allowing the materials to withstand greater impact forces during initial fracture stages, it reduced the toughness of BPE. BPE with 80% BF content showed a higher crack propagation rate and a shorter fracture process compared to composites with lower BF content. However, according to the stress intensity factor (SIF) history, the introduction of BF could enhance the resistance of crack propagation, which depends on the dispersibility of BF and the interfacial bonding properties between BF and high–density polyethylene (HDPE). Overall, BF could increase the rigidity of BPE and restrict the crack propagation at the initial fracture stages. As an eco-friendly and sustainable material, the dynamic fracture toughness of BPE can contribute to the application and promotion of biomass fiber-filled composites.Keywords: Natural fiber reinforced composites, Bamboo fiber, Dynamic failure, Digital image correlationSubject terms: Materials science, Physics

Introduction

Natural fiber reinforced composites (NFRCs) are eco-friendly and sustainable materials prepared from biomass fibers, thermoplastic polymers, and other additives1,2. Due to their excellent water and corrosion resistance, NFRCs have a long service life and the potential to utilize recycled plastics and waste fibers as raw materials during processing3,4. Moreover, NFRCs are easily moldable, require no paint for finishing, and are insect–resistant5,6. These characteristics have led to extensive application of NFRCs in several fields such as construction, landscaping, and furniture manufacturing7,8. High–density polyethylene (HDPE), characterized by a stable chemical structure, resistance to ultraviolet aging, and recyclability, frequently serves as the primary raw material of NFRC production9,10. Bamboo, a fast–growing forest resource with a short growth cycle and widespread distribution, is important for developing a circular economy. Processing bamboo or bamboo product residues into bamboo fiber (BF) and compounding them with HDPE produces could make bamboo fiber/high–density polyethylene (BPE) composites, where the physical properties of BF can be effectively harnessed to produce BPE composites with excellent mechanical properties.

Owing to its high flexural modulus, renewability, and biodegradability, BF is considered an ideal reinforcing material11,12. However, to achieve optimal performance of BF in HDPE, physical and chemical modifications of BF are crucial to enhance compatibility between them13. Regarding physical modifications, adjusting the fiber content and dimensions, especially the diameter, considerably impacts the mechanical properties of the composite8,11,14,15. Additionally, common BF modification methods include stretching, corona discharge, thermal treatment, and plasma treatment16. However, these physical treatments often have drawbacks such as high energy consumption, poor cost–effectiveness, and low continuous production capability17. Chemically, appropriate surface treatments of BF can substantially enhance its mechanical properties and the interfacial adhesion between BF and polyolefin materials18–20. Using maleic anhydride–grafted polyolefins as compatibilizers can improve interfacial bonding performance by undergoing esterification reactions with the hydroxyl groups of BF and molecular entanglement with the polymer matrix21. Additionally, silane, acetylation, and alkali treatments are effective surface modification methods for BF11,22. Chemical modifications can enhance the hydrophobicity of the BF surface, ensuring better interface bonding strength with HDPE and consequently improving the mechanical properties of the composite23,24. As BPE is commonly employed for outdoor products, its lifespan and safety become concerns, especially when the material experiences localized damage and fractures under instantaneous impact forces. This is due to the non-uniform distribution of strain and stress fields in the composites, and failure behaviors of the composites subjected to impact loading illustrate complicated mechanisms. Some studies have investigated the amount of energy absorption, ballistic limit speed, and failure mechanism of elastomeric nanocomposites through the results of high-speed impact tests. The penetration resistance and failure mechanism of flexible biocomposites during impact were validated using a constitutive material model for hemp fabric and a user-defined material model (VUMAT) for the nonlinear behavior of rubber materials with a damage criterion in ABAQUS/Explicit25,26, which is of certain significance for the promotion of elastomeric and flexible composite materials. Therefore, studying the dynamic fracture toughness of BPE materials with varying BF content is crucial for advancing the development of high biomass fiber–filled composites27–29.

Digital Image Correlation (DIC) is a non-contact optical measurement technology that tracks changes in a series of deforming images captured by a DIC camera system. It is widely used to identify the full-field strain distribution on a specimen during loading, including the mechanical testing of single fibers or composite materials30,31. For example, DIC could be used to analyze the fiber-matrix interactions in composite laminates, which revealed the location of damaged regions in the form of transverse cracks and indicated that the damage originated in the transverse ply below the surface32. In addition, research indicated that the fiber content has a significant impact on the interfacial bonding and mechanical properties of ultra-highly filled wood fiber/polyethylene composites33. Herein, BPE materials were prepared by controlling the content of BF (0%, 20%, 50%, and 80%). Dynamic fracture toughness tests were conducted on the samples using three low–speed impact energies (10, 20, and 30 J). During the testing process, the digital–image correlation (DIC) technique was employed to capture image data and analyze the stress region of the dynamic fracture process for BPE materials. In this study, we aim to investigate the mechanism by which BF content affects the dynamic fracture toughness of BPE. Then, we explore the application of natural fiber reinforced composites in high-strength and dynamic load fields such as building formwork and plank roads by integrating methods involving analyses of crack propagation rate and history, stress intensity factor (SIF) history, and surface micromorphology.

Materials and methods

Materials

High density polyethylene (HDPE, 5000 S) was purchased from PetroChina Daqing Petrochemical Company (Daqing, China) with a density of 0.949–0.953 g/cm3 and a melt flow index of 0.8–1.1 g/10 min according to ASTM1238. Bamboo Fiber (BF) was supplied by Zhejiang Lin’an Mingzhu Bamboo & Wood Powder Co., Ltd. (Linan, China) with a size of 150–400 μm and a bulk density of 0.45–0.53 g/cm3. Maleic anhydride-grafted polyethylene (MAPE) with a grafting ratio 1wt.% was purchased from Shanghai Rizhisheng New Technology Development Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) with a melt flow index of 0.7–0.9 g/10 min at 190 °C. Polyethylene wax (PW) was used as the lubricant during compounding and purchased from Shandong Qilu Petrochemical Company (Zibo, China).

Specimen preparation

The BF was dried in an oven at 65 °C for 48 h to remove moisture. Following the formulation listed in Table 1, the respective weights of the raw materials were measured. After a 15-min blending process, the materials were added to a pelletizer for granulation. Depending on the ratio of BF to the total amount of HDPE and MAPE in each formulation (0%, 20%, 50%, and 80%), the samples were named BPE0, BPE2, BPE5, and BPE8, respectively. The BPE granules were further ground using a grinder. An equal weight of BPE granules from each group was placed in a hot–press mold with dimensions of 150 × 200 × 14 mm. The mold was then placed in a hot–press machine set at 180 °C and subjected to a pressure of 1.2 MPa for 8 min. Subsequently, the mold was placed in a cold–press machine and water–cooled until fully set. After demolding, BPE materials with uniform thickness and smooth surfaces were obtained. Tests

Density measurement

Samples from each BPE material group were cut into blocks measuring 20 × 20 × 14 mm3, with three to five blocks per group. Each block was weighed using an electronic balance with an accuracy of 0.001 g. Subsequently, the blocks were submerged in a graduated cylinder filled with distilled water. The volume of each block was determined by measuring the increase in water level as marked by the cylinder’s graduations. Finally, the density of each BPE material group was calculated based on the volume and weight of each block, and an average value was determined.

Dynamic elastic modulus and Poisson’s ratio measurement

Dynamic fracture toughness test

Specimen preparation

BPE materials measuring 64 × 28 × 14 mm were prepared for dynamic fracture toughness testing. A notch with a depth of 13–14 mm was cut into the center of the specimen using a 1-mm cutting saw blade. Subsequently, a 1-mm depth precrack was introduced at the top of the notch using a blade. The specimen surface was spray–coated with white paint, and speckles were drawn on the surface using an oil–based black marker. Figure 2 illustrates the specimen design, including the coordinate system at the crack tip used to determine dynamic parameters and a dashed box indicating the 25 × 25 mm region of interest for focused sampling. Five specimens of each sample were tested for average values.

Samples style for dynamic fracture toughness testing.

Experimental setup and test procedure

Following the ASTMD7136-2005 standard, low–speed drop–hammer–impact tests were performed on samples from each group using an Instron CEAST 9350 testing machine. The impact device had an 8-mm diameter loading roller impact head and support rollers with a 4 mm diameter. Three energy gradients (10, 20, and 30 J) were set for the impact energy. High–speed imaging technology was employed to analyze the surface deformation of the BPE specimens during the fracture process, with the specific testing method illustrated in Fig. 3. The Photron Fastcam SA-Z high–speed camera and flash were synchronized by a computer, recording the entire process from the initial contact of the impact head with the specimen surface to the final damage of the specimen at a rate of 100,000 fps. Furthermore, speckle images for each deformation state were sequentially paired and correlated, enabling the assessment of the two displacement components (u, v) of the specimen surface.

The starting frame of the crack was determined by comparing stress field images, and stress images of the entire damage process of the specimen were exported at intervals of 10 frames. DIC analysis and evaluation

To achieve high measurement accuracy, it is necessary to manually spray black and white speckles on the sample surface. According to Huygens’ principle, each point on the speckle surface can be regarded as a point light source. When the light reflected from the object surface is superimposed in space, interference occurs, forming randomly distributed bright and dark spots. DIC determines the displacement vector of a pixel by tracking or matching the position information of the same pixel point in speckle images before and after deformation on an object’s surface. Subsequently, the full–field displacement of the entire specimen surface is obtained from point to surface. Initially, the imaging surface of the test specimen should have a random speckle pattern that reflects deformation information. During the test, the speckle distribution after loading deformation is recorded and stored in a computer. Finally, the Vic-2D 6 image analysis software (Correlated Solutions, Inc., SC, USA) uses relevant mathematical algorithms to obtain the displacement field information of the specimen surface.

By analyzing the orthogonal displacement data near the crack tip using multipoint least squares, the real–time Mode-I SIF of the composite material can be determined35. Ultimately, Mode-I and -II dynamic SIFs of the specimen throughout the dynamic fracture process are obtained. Surface micromorphology

Following liquid nitrogen treatment, the fracture surfaces of the dynamic fracture specimens were quickly brittle–fractured for sampling, followed by gold sputtering. Subsequently, the micromorphology of the specimen fracture surfaces was observed by adjusting the magnification of the FEI Quanta200 scanning electron microscope under an accelerating voltage of 30 kV.

Results and discussion

Density, elastic modulus, and Poisson’s ratio

The density, transverse wave number, longitudinal wave number, elastic modulus, and Poisson’s ratio values for each BPE group were obtained using the drainage and ultrasonic echo methods (Table 2). Based on the data and Eqs. (4) and (5), a custom program was employed to calculate the Mode-I and -II dynamic SIFs for each BPE before and after the propagation of prefabricated cracks under the impact loading conditions for simply supported beams.

According to the variations in the corresponding performance parameters in Table 2, it is evident that the material density gradually decreases from 0.98 to 0.75 kg/m³ as the BF content increases from BPE0 to BPE8. This is likely attributed to the lower density of BF compared to HDPE, leading to an overall reduction in density. The longitudinal and transverse wave numbers decrease with increasing BF content, dropping from 2193 to 908 m/s for BPE0 to 1347 and 503 m/s for BPE8, respectively. This indicates that the porous structure of BF and its dispersion affect the acoustic wave propagation in BPE materials, which is also related to the decrease in material density to some extent. However, the elastic modulus exhibits fluctuations with the addition of BF, decreasing from 0.75 (BPE0) to 0.57 GPa (BPE2) and increasing to 0.62 GPa (BPE8). This suggests that the addition of BF alters the material’s microstructure, thereby affecting its elastic properties. Additionally, the Poisson’s ratio decreases as the BF content increases, reflecting the changing relation between the material’s shear properties and volumetric deformation as the BF content varies.

Dynamic impact performance analysis

Figure 5a1–a3 present the force–time curves for BPE materials with varying BF contents as the impact energy increases. Observing the characteristics of these curves, it is evident that all test samples exhibit two peak force stages under 10-, 20-, and 30-J impact energies. Taking Fig. 5a1 as an example, the time intervals for the two peak forces for all samples ranged from 0.321 to 0.551 ms and from 4.034 to 5.282 ms, respectively. However, the BPE0 sample consistently demonstrates a higher peak force under all three impact energies than samples with added BF. The BPE8 sample reaches its initial peak force earliest under all three impact energies, specifically at 10 (0.321 ms; 2168 N), 20 (0.195 ms; 2423 N), and 30 J (0.22 ms; 2996 N). However, with increasing impact energy, the second-stage peak force tends to decrease with higher BF content (BPE2 > BPE5 > BPE8). This could be attributed to the greater rigidity of the BPE samples with higher BF content during the initial crack propagation phase, thereby suppressing crack formation. However, as the impact energy rises, the increased BF content weakens the bond strength between BF and HDPE, inducing a declining trend in impact force during the mid to late stages of sample fracture.

Force–time, energy–time, and displacement–time curves for BPE materials with different BF contents. (a1), (b1), and (c1) 10-J impact energy; (a2), (b2), and (c2) 20-J impact energy; and (a3), (b3), and (c3) 30-J impact energy.

In conjunction with Fig. 5b1–b3, it is evident that under all three impact energies, the BPE8 sample exhibits the earliest energy absorption phenomenon, corresponding to the time of the initial peak force. However, the BPE8 sample consistently showed the lowest energy absorption values under all three impact energies, with the maximum energy absorption being only 32.7%, 23.2%, and 14.3% of BPE0, respectively. As the impact energy increases, the ratio of the maximum energy absorption values between BPE8 and BPE0 gradually decreases. BPE0, as a thermoplastic polymer, has high ductility and can absorb more energy due to its inherent toughness when subjected to localized impact loads38. The decline in energy values for samples with different BF contents might be attributed to the increased brittleness of the material due to higher BF content, leading to reaching the maximum damage energy in a shorter time. Similarly, Fig. 5c1–c3 clearly show that the BPE8 sample has the smallest relative impact displacement, specifically at 10 (9.79 mm), 20 (10.28 mm), and 30 J (9.18 mm), primarily caused by the increased brittleness of material.

Analysis of crack propagation rate and propagation history

Figure 6 presents the crack propagation rate curves for BPE materials with varying BF contents, and the dynamic crack propagation lengths (mm) are presented in Table 3. At an impact energy of 10 J (Fig. 6a), all four samples display comparable crack propagation rates during the initial phase of sample fracture (0–10 frames). However, as the impact duration extends to 20 frames, BPE8 achieves a crack propagation rate of 6.56 mm/frame. A clear trend emerges—as BF content increases, the crack propagation rate also increases (BPE8 > BPE5 > BPE2 > BPE0). Compared to the pure HDPE BPE0 sample, the introduction of BF into the BPE samples disrupts the continuous structure of the HDPE resin. As BF content increases, the spacing between fiber particles decreases. When BPE samples with BF are subjected to impact tensile forces, stress concentrations easily occur at the interface between BF and HDPE and at BF agglomeration sites, inducing higher crack propagation rates during the initial fracture phase. As the impact energy increases to 20 J (Fig. 6b) and 30 J (Fig. 6c), the impact fracture times for all samples converge. However, the BPE5 sample exhibits a higher crack propagation rate than BPE8, reaching a maximum of 7.82 mm/frame under 30-J impact energy. This phenomenon might be related to the interfacial bonding performance between BF and HDPE within the BPE5 material.

SIF analysis

Figure 8 presents the Mode-I and -II SIFs for BPE materials with varying BF contents. As shown in Fig. 8a1–c1, before the initiation of crack propagation (t − ti < 0) and after the initiation of crack propagation (t − ti > 0), all samples display an increasing trend in the Mode-I SIF. Defining the moment of crack initiation (t − ti = 0) as the initial frame, frame 0 represents the initial phase. Under 10-J impact energy, the BPE8 sample reaches an initial SIF of 0.987 MPa·m1/2 at frame 0, which is 2.7 times that of the BPE0 sample, and reaches a peak value of 4.215 MPa·m1/2 at frame 50, the highest in the group. As BF content increases, BPE samples with BF show a trend of decreasing and then increasing SIFs (BPE8 > BPE2 > BPE5), with local fluctuations in the data, possibly due to the random distribution of BF particles along the crack propagation path causing minor oscillations in the Mode-I SIF39. A higher Mode-I SIF indicates increased resistance to crack propagation; however, there is a correlation between a higher Mode-I SIF and a longer crack propagation process. The introduction of 80% BF, despite increasing the material’s brittleness, provides some resistance to crack tip propagation. With the increase in impact energy, BPE samples with BF exhibit higher Mode-I SIFs than BPE0, further suggesting that BF can offer resistance at the crack tip as the impact strength increases.

(a1) Mode-I SIF at 10-J impact energy, (b1) Mode-I SIF at 20-J impact energy, (c1) Mode-I SIF at 30-J impact energy, (a2) Mode-II SIF at 10-J impact energy, (b2) Mode-II SIF at 20-J impact energy, and (c2) Mode-II SIF at 30-J impact energy.

As shown in Fig. 8a2–c2, compared to the Mode-I SIF, the Mode-II SIF values exhibit minor fluctuations, but the overall curve is relatively smooth. This indicates that the primary mode of failure for all BPE samples is still a crack opening perpendicular to the stress direction. As BF content increases, all samples under different impact energies exhibit similar characteristics, suggesting that crack propagation in the shear mode parallel to the stress direction is negligible.

Fracture surface micromorphology analysis

The micromorphology of the impact fracture surface of BPE materials with different BF contents is shown in Fig. 9. HDPE is a widely used thermoplastic polymer. In the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images, the fracture surface of HDPE exhibits typical ductile fracture characteristics, featuring an irregular and rough surface morphology, indicating the plastic deformation the material underwent before fracturing (Fig. 9a). Additionally, the fibrillar structures in some areas further confirm the plastic deformation caused by the elongation of molecular chains.

Micromorphology of the impact fracture surface of BPE materials with different BF contents. (a) BPE0, (b) BPE2, (c) BPE5, and (d) BPE8.

The fracture surface of BPE2 was observed using SEM (Fig. 9b). It can be observed that the fibers do not aggregate and the fracture roughness is high, which is due to the small amount of fibers added, the fracture surfaces of the composites presented ductile fracture features. In addition, fiber extraction holes are rarely seen, because the addition of MAPE can effectively improve the interface bonding strength of BF and the resin matrix. Therefore, when the sample is subjected to an impact force, it retains strong toughness to absorb energy and displacement, thus inhibiting crack propagation and reducing the possibility of instantaneous fracture.

As shown in Fig. 9c, compared to BPE2, BPE5 has a more evident distribution of BF particles. Although the size of the BF particles in both materials is similar, the higher BF content in BPE5 makes the distance between BF particles smaller, leading to an increase in the interfacial bonding area, but there is still no phenomenon of fiber aggregation. In addition, the fracture surface of the BPE5 sample showed the pores where fibers were pulled out. This may be because the higher BF content results in greater randomness at the effective bonding sites between BF and HDPE.

Compared to BPE5, the fracture surface of BPE8 material is denser and smoother (Fig. 9d). This is because with the increase of BF content, the stiffness of the composites can be significantly improved, the ductile fracture characteristics decrease and the brittle fracture increases during the impact. Moreover, under such a high bamboo fiber filling, the contact rate between fibers increases, and the bond strength between fibers is significantly lower than that between fibers and resin. Therefore, this may be the main reason affecting the crack propagation path and propagation rate.

Conclusions

In this study, we employed a low–speed drop–hammer–impact test combined with the digital image correlation (DIC) technique to conduct a comparative study on the dynamic fracture toughness of bamboo fiber/high–density polyethylene (BPE) composites with different bamboo fiber (BF) contents were conducted. The following conclusions can be drawn:

The introduction of BF alters the microstructure of the BPE composites. As the content of BF increases, the stiffness of the BPE composite increases. When subjected to dynamic loads, the stress transmission efficiency increases, the crack propagation rate is faster, and the crack propagation process is shorter. However, an excessively high BF content can lead to uneven fiber dispersion and stress concentration, which has an adverse effect on the dynamic fracture resistance of the composites. From another perspective, we find that the introduction of BF effectively enhanced the Mode-I stress intensity factor (SIF) of BPE and provided some resistance to crack tip propagation during the impact fracture process. Therefore, natural fiber content is only one factor affecting the dynamic fracture of BPE composites. In future research, a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanism of BF influence on the dynamic fracture toughness of BPE materials can be revealed by enhancing and toughening BF as well as regulating the aspect ratio of BF and its interface with HDPE, effectively improving the dynamic fracture performance of BPE materials, and expanding the application scope of high-fiber content BPE materials in the field of construction engineering.
