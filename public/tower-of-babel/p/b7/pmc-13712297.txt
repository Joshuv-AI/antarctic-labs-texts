Unnatural amino acid-based miniPROTACs enable efficient oncoprotein degradation
Authors: Jianchao Zhang, Xiao Chen, Congli Chen, Fengming Li, Kefan Liao, Xiaoxiao Song, Ya Zeng, Jie Wang, Ming-Yuan Su, Chris Soon Heng Tan, Lijing Fang, Hai Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) harness the cell's proteolytic systems to eliminate harmful proteins for therapeutic purposes. Unnatural amino acids (UAAs) offer unique structural features for drug design but have not yet been applied to targeted protein degradation. Here, we develop a set of linker-free PROTACs in which Lys-derived UAAs—ornithine (Orn) and 2,4-diaminobutyric acid (Dab)—serve as degrons that are directly conjugated to target ligands, yielding compact and effective degraders. Both Orn-BA and Dab-BA promote the efficient degradation of EML4-ALK, with Dab-BA exhibiting superior potency, attributed to its enhanced ability to promote ternary complex formation with the target and E3 ligase. Dab-BA also significantly suppresses tumor growth in a xenograft mouse model. The general applicability of this UAA-based linker-free PROTAC approach is further demonstrated by the development of BCR-ABL degraders. Our study represents the first application of UAAs in targeted protein degradation and offers a complementary strategy for E3 ligand discovery.Key words: Unnatural amino acids, Degron, PROTAC, Degradation, Linker-free, N-end rule, Proteolysis, Ubiquitin

Introduction

ALK-positive non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is a subtype of lung adenocarcinoma driven by chromosomal rearrangements that fuse the ALK gene with partner genes, most commonly EML4. The EML4-ALK fusion constitutively activates ALK kinase signaling, driving tumor progression. This alteration is predominantly found in younger, non-smoking patients and accounts for approximately 3%‒7% of all NSCLC cases1. Several ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), such as brigatinib, have been developed to lock the kinase domain in an inactive conformation, leading to marked clinical responses1,2. However, acquired resistance arising from secondary mutations or bypass pathway activation typically develops within a year3. This clinical challenge underscores the need for alternative strategies that can eliminate the oncoprotein rather than merely suppress its activity.

Targeted protein degradation (TPD) offers a unique advantage in overcoming drug resistance by harnessing the cellular proteolytic machinery to eliminate the disease-driving proteins4, 5, 6. Among TPD modalities, proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) and molecular glues (MGs) represent the most advanced strategies, with several molecules advancing into clinical development4, 5, 6. While PROTACs are modular and programmable, consisting of a warhead for the protein of interest connected to a ubiquitin E3 ligase ligand typically via a flexible linker, their molecular weights tend to be over 700 Da, which often compromise drug-like properties5,7. In contrast, MGs act by inducing de novo interactions between E3 ligases and target proteins, typically by binding one component and reshaping its interface to enable complex formation4. MGs are generally smaller (<500 Da) and comply with Lipinski's rule of five8. However, the discovery of MGs remains largely empirical, with limited generalizable design principles.

A key challenge in the TPD field is the development of rational, modular strategies for creating compact degraders with favorable properties. Whereas the majority of PROTACs employ either CRBN or VHL ligands for these E3s, we have developed AATacs—a new class of single amino acid-based degraders that recruit E3 ligases via the N-end rule pathway, the first genetically defined ubiquitin-dependent degradation route9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15. This proteolytic pathway selects proteins based on their exposed N-terminal residue, known as the N-degron, which is recognized by distinct E3 ligases targeting different residue types16,17. UBR family ligases recognize basic residues (i.e., Lys, Arg, His) or bulky hydrophobic residues (i.e., Leu, Ile, Trp, Phe, Tyr)16,18. ZYG11B and ZER1, as adaptors of the CRL2 complex, bind N-terminal residues such as Gly, Cys, Ala, Ser, and Thr19,20. GID4, a substrate receptor of the GID/CTLH complex, recognizes N-terminal Pro, Val, and Phe21,22. By directly conjugating N-degron residues to target ligands, we previously developed “minimal” AATacs—linker-free PROTACs with molecular weights near 500 Da that retained potent degradation efficiency with improved drug-like properties9.

Certain UAAs exhibit high structural similarity to natural amino acid residues. For instance, ornithine (Orn) and 2,4-diaminobutyric acid (Dab) closely resemble Lys, differing only by one or two methylene units in their side chains, respectively. UAAs have been broadly applied in medicinal chemistry to improve pharmacological properties, including molecular recognition, aqueous solubility, and cellular permeability23, 24, 25. Given their structural mimicry, we hypothesized that UAAs could also serve as E3 ligase-binding elements in the design of PROTACs. This approach may expand the repertoire of degrons beyond canonical residues, allow delicate modulation of the E3-substrate interactions, and offer new avenues for developing small degraders with improved selectivity and drug-like properties.

Herein, we have developed UAA-based linker-free PROTACs by directly conjugating Lys-derived Orn and Dab to warheads targeting oncoproteins such as EML4-ALK and BCR-ABL. Compared to Orn-BA and Lys-BA, the Dab-BA molecule, being the smallest in molecular size, showed superior performance in ternary complex formation, target ubiquitylation and degradation, and inhibition of cell proliferation. This strategy facilitates the development of small, efficient degraders with broad applicability. Importantly, the use of smaller UAAs as degrons not only reduces molecular weight and improves drug-like properties but also expands the repertoire of E3 ligase ligands, providing new opportunities for fine-tuning the E3-substrate interactions and designing optimized PROTACs.

Results

Design and evaluation of linker-free PROTACs incorporating unnatural amino acids

Given the close structural resemblance of Lys to Orn and Dab—which differ from Lys by one and two fewer methylene units in their side chains, respectively—we sought to determine whether these UAAs could serve as functional degrons for PROTAC development. To this end, we directly conjugated Lys, Orn, and Dab to the ALK ligand Brigatinib analog (BA), generating Lys-BA, Orn-BA, and Dab-BA (Fig. 1A). We next treated H3122 cells with varying concentrations of Lys-BA, Orn-BA, and Dab-BA for 24 h. All three compounds induced dose-dependent reduction of EML4-ALK in H3122 cells (Fig. 1B). The half-maximal degradation concentrations (DC50) were 162.9 nmol/L for Lys-BA, 139.7 nmol/L for Orn-BA, and 38.9 nmol/L for Dab-BA. At 500 nmol/L, the maximal degradation (Dmax) achieved by each compound was 77% for Lys-BA, 80% for Orn-BA, and 92% for Dab-BA (Fig. 1B and C). When H3122 cells were treated with higher concentrations of Lys-BA, Orn-BA, and Dab-BA (1, 10, and 20 μmol/L), a slight decrease in degradation efficiency was observed at 20 μmol/L, indicative of a mild hook effect (Supporting Information Fig. S1A). Interestingly, when we tested 2,3-diaminopropionic acid (Dap), a UAA with a side chain one methylene unit shorter than that of Dab, conjugated to BA to form Dap-BA, no significant EML4-ALK degradation was observed following 24-h treatment of H3122 cells with varying concentrations of Dap-BA (Fig. S1B and S1C). Therefore, we focused our subsequent investigation on Lys-BA, Orn-BA, and Dab-BA.

We subsequently examined the temporal kinetics of EML4-ALK turnover following treatment with 500 nmol/L of each compound in H3122 cells. All three compounds initiated detectable EML4-ALK degradation within 1 h. The degradation half-lives (t1/2) were 9.3 h for Lys-BA, 8.3 h for Orn-BA, and 3.5 h for Dab-BA (Fig. 1C and D). To further assess the degradation potency and selectivity of these compounds, we performed quantitative proteomic analysis following 12-h treatment of H3122 cells with Lys-BA, Orn-BA, or Dab-BA. The results revealed that EML4-ALK was the primary degradation target for all three compounds, and each exhibited relatively high selectivity across the proteome. The reduction in EML4-ALK protein levels was approximately 23-fold for Lys-BA, 30-fold for Orn-BA, and 44-fold for Dab-BA (Fig. 1E). Taken together, these results demonstrate that the UAA-based degraders Orn-BA and Dab-BA effectively induce EML4-ALK reduction, with Dab-BA—featuring the smallest degron moiety—promoting more efficient and faster clearance of EML4-ALK compared to Orn-BA and Lys-BA.

Dab-BA displays superior performance compared to Lys-BA and Orn-BA in vitro

To investigate whether the observed differences in EML4-ALK reduction translated into functional outcomes, we next compared the antiproliferative effects of Lys-BA, Orn-BA, and Dab-BA in H3122 cells using a CCK-8 assay. Dab-BA exhibited the most potent growth inhibition, with an IC50 of 39.2 nmol/L, followed by Orn-BA (IC50 = 72.8 nmol/L) and Lys-BA (IC50 = 113.9 nmol/L) (Figs. 1C and 2A). Under the same experimental conditions, the ALK inhibitor Brigatinib inhibited H3122 cell proliferation with an IC50 of 56.2 nmol/L (Fig. S1D). We further compared the effects of Lys-BA, Orn-BA, and Dab-BA on cell cycle progression and apoptosis in H3122 cells. Following 24-h treatment at 500 nmol/L, flow cytometry analysis revealed that Dab-BA induced the highest proportion of cells arrested in the G1 phase, accompanied by a corresponding decrease in the proportion of cells in S and G2 phases (Fig. 2B and C). Orn-BA elicited a moderate effect, while Lys-BA had the least impact on cell cycle distribution (Fig. 2B and C). In addition, under the same condition (500 nmol/L, 24 h), Dab-BA also resulted in the highest proportion of early and late apoptotic cells, with Orn-BA exhibiting an intermediate effect and Lys-BA showing the weakest induction of apoptosis (Fig. 2D and E).

UAA-based PROTACs induce EML4-ALK degradation through the proteasome system

While natural amino acid-based PROTACs have previously been shown to promote target degradation via the ubiquitin proteasome system9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, whether linker-free degraders incorporating UAAs function through the same mechanism remains unknown. To this end, we treated H3122 cells with Orn-BA or Dab-BA in the presence of the inhibitor against the proteasome (i.e., MG132) or the lysosome (i.e., chloroquine (CQ)). We found that MG132, but not CQ, blocked the degradation of EML4-ALK induced by either Orn-BA or Dab-BA, indicating that these UAA-based PROTACs also act through the proteasome system (Fig. 3A). We also observed that the natural amino acid-based Lys-BA promotes EML4-ALK degradation via the proteasome (Supporting Information Fig. S2A).

We further examined the degradation kinetics of EML4-ALK in response to Lys-BA, Orn-BA or Dab-BA treatment. H3122 cells were treated with cycloheximide (CHX) to block new protein synthesis, and residual EML4-ALK levels were measured at various time points. EML4-ALK protein levels declined rapidly in cells treated with Lys-BA, Orn-BA, or Dab-BA, whereas they remained largely unchanged in the DMSO-treated control cells (Fig. 3B and Fig. S2B). The turnover of EML4-ALK induced by Lys-BA, Orn-BA, or Dab-BA was impeded by MG132 (Fig. 3C and Fig. S2C), supporting the requirement of the proteasome for effective substrate clearance. The degradation of EML4-ALK mediated by Lys-BA, Orn-BA, and Dab-BA was effectively attenuated by excess BA, indicating that binding of these degraders to EML4-ALK is required for effective degradation (Fig. 3D and Fig. S2D).

An E3 ubiquitin ligase is typically required to recognize protein substrates and catalyze their polyubiquitylation, a prerequisite for proteasome-mediated degradation. Members of the UBR family, including UBR1, UBR2, UBR4, and UBR5, have been reported to recognize N-terminal basic residues such as Lys and promote substrate turnover via the N-end rule pathway18. Given the close structural similarity between Lys and its derivatives Orn and Dab, we wonder whether a shared set of E3 ligases mediates the EML4-ALK degradation induced by Lys-BA, Orn-BA, and Dab-BA. To determine the E3 ligase(s) specifically responsible for PROTACs-mediated EML4-ALK reduction, we knocked out UBR1, UBR2, UBR4, and UBR5 individually in H3122 cells using CRISPR-Cas9. Notably, UBR2 knockout effectively abrogated EML4-ALK degradation induced by all three compounds, whereas knockout of UBR1, UBR4, or UBR5 had no significant impact (Fig. 3E and Fig. S2E). These findings suggest that UBR2 is the primary E3 ligase responsible for recognizing Lys, Orn, and Dab-based degrons in this linker-free PROTAC system.

We next investigated the molecular basis underlying the functional differences among Lys-BA, Orn-BA, and Dab-BA. Given that these three linker-free PROTACs share an identical ALK-binding warhead but differ solely in their amino acid moiety, we hypothesized that the differences in the degron–E3 ligase interactions may be key to their activity. To assess this, we examined their binding affinities for UBR2 using isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC). All three compounds exhibited comparable affinities for UBR2, with dissociation constants (Kd) of 1.94 μmol/L for Lys-BA, 1.59 μmol/L for Orn-BA, and 1.48 μmol/L for Dab-BA (Supporting Information Fig. S3). Notably, Dap-BA, a structurally similar compound that lacks degradation activity, also exhibited a comparable binding affinity (Kd = 2.01 μmol/L) (Fig. S3). These results suggest that binary binding to UBR2 alone may not fully explain the observed functional differences among these compounds.

The efficiency of substrate degradation by PROTACs appears to positively correlate with the extent and stability of ternary complex formation involving the target, PROTAC, and E3 ligase26, 27, 28. To further investigate this possibility, we assessed the extent of ternary complex formation involving UBR2, ALK, and each of the four compounds in living cells using a NanoBRET assay. All three degraders—Lys-BA, Orn-BA, and Dab-BA—increased the BRET signal in a dose-dependent manner. Among them, Dab-BA induced the strongest BRET signal, followed by Orn-BA, while Lys-BA produced the weakest response (Fig. 3F). In contrast, Dap-BA, a non-functional compound, failed to induce any BRET signal even at higher concentrations (Fig. 3F). Moreover, we carried out co-immunoprecipitation experiments to assess the interaction between UBR2 and EML4-ALK after treatment with DMSO, Lys-BA, Orn-BA, Dab-BA or Dap-BA. The data showed that Dab-BA treatment resulted in the most robust co-precipitation of HA-UBR2 with Flag-EML4-ALK (Fig. 3G), consistent with the NanoBRET results. In contrast, no interaction was detected in the Dap-BA-treated cells (Fig. 3G). We also assessed the ability of these compounds to promote EML4-ALK ubiquitylation. Dab-BA induced the highest level of ubiquitylation, followed by Orn-BA, while Lys-BA showed the weakest effect (Fig. 3H). No increase in ubiquitylation was observed with Dap-BA treatment (Fig. 3H). Together, these results suggest that Dab-BA's superior efficiency stems from its enhanced ability to promote ternary complex formation and subsequent target ubiquitylation and degradation.

Dab-BA effectively suppresses in vivo tumor growth through oral administration

Because PROTAC-mediated degradation can be triggered by transient target engagement6,7, we next examined the duration of EML4-ALK degradation following transient exposure to Dab-BA. H3122 cells were treated with Dab-BA for 24 h, followed by compound removal and incubation in drug-free medium for an additional 12‒48 h. The data showed that EML4-ALK protein levels remained markedly suppressed after Dab-BA washout, indicating sustained degradation beyond compound exposure (Supporting Information Fig. S4). This prolonged degradation effect following transient exposure provided a rationale for evaluating Dab-BA using an intermittent dosing regimen in vivo.

To assess the physicochemical and in vitro ADME properties of Dab-BA, we next evaluated its aqueous solubility, metabolic stability, plasma stability, plasma protein binding, and cytochrome P450 (CYP) inhibition (Table 1). Dab-BA displayed exceptional aqueous solubility, with a measured solubility of 867 mmol/L in ddH2O, suggesting suitability for oral dosing without the need for additional solubilizing agents. Moreover, Dab-BA exhibited excellent liver microsomal stability in human (t1/2 > 184 min) and mouse (t1/2 = 102 min), as well as excellent plasma stability in both human and mouse plasma (t1/2 > 372 min for both species). Dab-BA showed moderate plasma protein binding (76.1% in human plasma and 51.8% in mouse plasma) and no detectable inhibition of the evaluated CYP isoforms (1A2, 2C9, 2C19, 2D6, and 3A4) at concentrations up to 50 μmol/L (Table 1).

The pharmacokinetics of Dab-BA were then examined in mice. Following intravenous administration (iv) at 2 mg/kg, Dab-BA exhibited a low clearance (CL = 0.7 L/h/kg), a large volume of distribution (Vz = 8.4 L/kg). After oral administration (po) at 10 mg/kg, Dab-BA displayed a long half-life (t1/2 = 33.3 h), a high oral exposure (Cmax = 339 ng/mL and AUC = 5121 h⋅Table 1). Collectively, these results indicate that Dab-BA possesses favorable PK properties in mice and supports its oral dosing potential.

We next evaluated the antitumor efficacy of Dab-BA through oral dosing in an H3122 xenograft mouse model. H3122 cells were inoculated subcutaneously into the right flanks of female nude mice. When the average tumor volume reached approximately 36 mm3, animals were randomly divided into three groups and administered either vehicle, Brigatinib or Dab-BA via oral gavage every other day for a total of seven doses. Tumors were excised and imaged two days following the final treatment (Fig. 4A). Treatment with either Brigatinib or Dab-BA significantly inhibited tumor progression, with Dab-BA showing a greater reduction in tumor volume and a slower tumor growth compared with Brigatinib (Fig. 4B and C).

Both immunohistochemical staining and Western blotting analysis demonstrated a substantial reduction in EML4-ALK protein levels in tumor tissues from the Dab-BA group, but not in the vehicle or Brigatinib groups (Fig. 4D and E). No significant changes in body weight were observed in mice treated with Dab-BA or Brigatinib (Fig. 4F), and H&E staining revealed no observable morphology abnormalities in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney across the vehicle, Brigatinib, and Dab-BA groups, supporting the favorable tolerability and lack of overt toxicity of Dab-BA treatment in mice (Fig. 4G). Collectively, these findings indicate that Dab-BA is an efficacious and well-tolerated degrader with oral activity in vivo, showing improved antitumor efficacy compared with the small-molecule inhibitor Brigatinib under the tested conditions.

Exploring the potential applicability UAA-based linker-free degraders to other targets

To explore whether this UAA-based linker-free strategy could be extended beyond EML4-ALK, we applied this approach to BCR-ABL, an oncogenic fusion protein that drives chronic myeloid leukemia (CML). We constructed three compounds by directly conjugating Lys, Orn, or Dab to dasatinib analog (DA), a binder of BCR-ABL, yielding Lys-DA, Orn-DA, and Dab-DA, respectively (Fig. 5A). K562 cells were treated with increasing concentrations of Lys-DA, Orn-DA, and Dab-DA for 48 h. All three compounds effectively induced BCR-ABL reduction at nanomolar concentrations (Fig. 5B). The DC50 values were 7.7 nmol/L for Lys-DA, 3.4 nmol/L for Orn-DA, and 3.1 nmol/L for Dab-DA, with maximal degradation (Dmax) of 97%, 99%, and 98%, respectively, at 20 nmol/L (Fig. 5B and C). In contrast, treatment with Dap-DA did not result in detectable BCR-ABL reduction across a range of concentrations (Supporting Information Fig. S5A and S5B). All three degraders also exhibited strong antiproliferative activity, with IC50 values of 1.1 nmol/L for Lys-DA, 0.29 nmol/L for Orn-DA, and 0.28 nmol/L for Dab-DA (Fig. 5C and D).

We next assessed the degradation kinetics of BCR-ABL by treating K562 cells with 20 nmol/L of each degrader for different durations. Lys-DA initiated detectable reduction at 6 h, with a calculated degradation half-life (t1/2) of 20 h (Fig. 5C and E). Orn-DA triggered degradation after 24 h, with a slightly longer t1/2 of 25 h. In contrast, Dab-DA began reducing BCR-ABL levels as early as 6 h post-treatment and showed the fastest degradation rate, with a t1/2 of 9 h (Fig. 5C and E). These results indicate that UAA-based linker-free PROTACs can be effectively extended to other targets such as BCR-ABL, and among the three degraders, Dab-DA not only shows the most potent degradation activity but also induces the most rapid BCR-ABL clearance.

We further evaluated the turnover of BCR-ABL in response to Lys-DA, Orn-DA or Dab-DA treatment. K562 cells were treated with CHX to block new protein synthesis, and residual BCR-ABL levels were monitored at distinct time points. A marked reduction in BCR-ABL levels was observed in cells exposed to any of the three degraders, while protein levels remained relatively stable in DMSO-treated cells (Fig. 6A). The degradation of BCR-ABL triggered by these compounds was effectively blocked by MG132 (Fig. 6B). Moreover, the Lys-DA, Orn-DA, and Dab-DA-induced decrease in BCR-ABL protein levels was substantially attenuated in UBR2 knockout K562 cells, but not in cells lacking UBR1, UBR4, or UBR5 (Fig. 6C).

Discussion

Despite the remarkable progress in the development of the PROTAC technology over the years, it still faces notable challenges hampering its clinical adaptation, including large molecular size and a limited repertoire of E3 ligase ligands5,29,30. In this study, we present a minimalist PROTAC strategy that bypass the need for linkers and further utilizes UAAs as degradation signals to recruit N-end rule E3 ligases. By directly conjugating these compact degrons to target warheads, we successfully achieved potent and selective degradation of oncoproteins such as EML4-ALK and BCR-ABL. This approach not only expands the scope of recruitable E3 ligases through the incorporation of noncanonical degrons, but also offers a modular and streamlined chemical design that significantly reduces the molecular weight of degraders.

Although Lys, Orn, and Dab differ by only one or two methylene groups in the side chain, the resulting linker-free degraders lead to clear differences in degradation efficiency. These functional differences correlate with their ability to form a productive ternary complex and promote substrate ubiquitylation, rather than the differences in the binary binding affinity for UBR2, which were found to be comparable among all three compounds. The ability to form a productive ternary complex remains hard to predict, but is widely recognized as a primary driver of effective PROTAC-induced target degradation26, 27, 28. Our study suggests that subtle changes in degron structure can significantly affect the binding affinity of the E3–PROTAC–target ternary complex, ultimately impacting degradation efficiency. This is reminiscent of CRBN-based degraders, where minor modifications to the E3 ligand—such as altering substituents on thalidomide analogs—can significantly impact the degradation of the same target protein, such as BRD4, by modulating ternary complex formation and cooperativity31. Similar observations have been made with DCAF16-based degraders, which employ electrophilic warheads attached to a BRD4 binder (JQ1) to enable covalent engagement of DCAF16 via a template-assisted mechanism32,33. In this system, subtle modifications to the electrophilic moiety on the JQ1 scaffold led to substantial differences in BRD4 degradation potency32,33. These variations were linked to differences in the efficiency of ternary complex formation with DCAF16. Subtle changes to the E3-binding moiety often influence degrader performance by modulating ternary complex formation, as demonstrated across diverse E3 ligase systems, highlighting the importance of modulating E3 ligand structure for more effective PROTAC development.

Interestingly, all three degraders selectively recruited UBR2, rather than other N-recognin E3s such as UBR1, UBR4, or UBR5. Although UBR family members are generally thought to recognize overlapping sets of N-degrons, there is emerging evidence that individual UBRs may exhibit distinct substrate preferences in certain biological contexts. For example, cleavage of NLRP1B by anthrax lethal toxin exposes an N-terminal leucine, resulting in its UBR2-dependent, but not UBR1-dependent, degradation via the N-end rule pathway and subsequent activation of caspase-134. Moreover, UBR1 and UBR2 mediate proteasomal degradation of RGS4 and RGS5 under normoxia, while under prolonged hypoxia, UBR4, but not UBR1, UBR2, or UBR5, cooperates with KCMF1 to redirect the same substrates toward autophagic degradation via K63/K27-linked ubiquitin chains, suggesting a context-dependent functional specialization among UBRs35,36. In our previous work, the Arg-based PROTAC primarily engaged UBR4, with UBR1 and UBR2 contributing to a lesser extent11. More recently, we demonstrated that an Arg-based PROTAC designed to degrade α-synuclein selectively recruited UBR113. These observations imply that degron recognition by UBR family members could involve a degree of specificity under certain conditions, rather than being strictly redundant. One possibility is that the same N-degron may preferentially recruit different UBRs depending on the cellular context or physiological state. Alternatively, structurally distinct N-degrons may exhibit intrinsic preferences for specific UBR ligases, even under similar conditions. Such preferences may be influenced by factors including the relative expression levels of UBR proteins, their subcellular localization, or post-translational modifications that modulate their substrate selectivity. Although the precise determinants remain to be fully elucidated, these findings point to a more nuanced and context-sensitive recognition mechanism within the N-end rule pathway.

UAAs have garnered increasing interest in drug development due to their unique structural and chemical properties, which enable the introduction of novel molecular interactions and functions not easily achieved with natural residues23,37. UAAs have been widely employed in drug design, spanning small molecules, peptides, and antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs), underscoring their broad applicability in medicinal chemistry23, 24, 25,37,38. For example, Bortezomib, a proteasome inhibitor approved for multiple myeloma, incorporates a leucine boronic acid to facilitate binding to the proteasome active site39. Moreover, Semaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist used for treating type 2 diabetes and obesity, replaces the eighth-position alanine with UAA 2-aminoisobutyric acid (Aib), which enhances peptide stability by conferring resistance to DPP-4-mediated proteolysis40,41. ARX788, a Phase III antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) targeting HER2-positive breast cancer, incorporates the UAA p-acetylphenylalanine (pAcF) to enable site-specific payload conjugation, improving drug–antibody homogeneity and therapeutic efficacy42. Additionally, genetically encoded epoxide-containing tyrosine (EPOY), a type of UAA, can covalently target proteins such as PD-L1 and KRAS, reacting with multiple nucleophilic residues and showing potential in the development of covalent protein drugs43. Here, we expand the scope of UAA utility by demonstrating, for the first time, that Orn and Dab, two UAAs structurally related to Lys, can serve as functional degron moieties to recruit E3 ligases and induce potent target degradation using a linker-free PROTAC strategy.

Conclusions

UAA-based miniPROTACs show superior efficacy against EML4-ALK and BCR-ABL, exemplifying the dual advantage of UAAs in conferring bioactivity while maintaining molecular compactness. This strategy expands the repertoire of ligandable E3 ligases and offers a modular chemical design for next-generation therapeutics. With their structural diversity and tunability, UAAs represent a versatile scaffold for targeted degradation, covalent inhibitors, peptide-based drugs, and ADCs, providing a complementary platform for E3 ligand discovery and AI-driven drug design.

Reagents

Primary antibodies included anti-ALK (3633, 1:2000), anti-HA (2999, 1:2000), anti-Myc-tag (2040, 1:2000), anti-c-Abl (2862, 1:1000), and anti-UBR5 (65344, 1:1000) from Cell Signaling Technology. Anti-UBR2 (ab217069, 1:1000) and anti-UBR4 (ab86738, 1:1000) were sourced from Abcam. Anti-GAPDH (HRP-60004) was supplied by Proteintech. Anti-UBR1 (sc-515753) was provided by Santa Cruz Biotechnology. Anti-Flag (A8592) and anti-FLAG® M2 magnetic beads (M8823) were purchased from Sigma. The compounds MG132, cycloheximide, and brigatinib were obtained from MedChemExpress. Dasatinib was acquired from Energy Chemical.

PROTAC compounds

The detailed synthesis procedures for all PROTACs used are provided in the Supporting Information

Cell culture

H3122 cell line was sourced from Meisen Chinese Tissue Culture Collection (MeisenCTCC, China). K562 and HEK293T cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). H3122 and K562 cells were cultured in RPMI1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS (Transgen) and antibiotics (penicillin and streptomycin). HEK293T cells were maintained in DMEM containing 10% FBS and penicillin, streptomycin. Routine screening for mycoplasma contamination was conducted using a MycoBlue Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Plasmid constructs

The full-length UBR2 coding sequence was subcloned into the pCDH-GFP-puro vector via the NheI and NotI restriction sites, generating a construct with a C-terminal HA tag. The UBR2 fragment (residues 98‒167) was inserted into the EcoRI site of the pHTN vector to produce an N-terminal HaloTag fusion protein. The ALK fragment (residues 1094‒1400) was ligated into the EcoRI site of the pNLF1-N vector, resulting in an N-terminal NanoLuc-tagged construct. The plasmid pCMV-myc-Ub (Cat#: P25115) was obtained from MiaoLing Biology. The lentiviral construct pCDH-CMV-Flag-EML4-ALK V1-EF1-puro was kindly provided by Professor Hongbin Ji. All sgRNA oligonucleotides were inserted into the BsmBI cloning site of the lentiCRISPR v2 vector (#52961, Addgene) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Immunoblot analysis

Cells were collected and lysed on ice for 30 min using RIPA buffer containing 50 mmol/L Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1% NP-40, 150 mmol/L NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and protease inhibitor cocktail. The lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 21,130×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were retained for further analysis. Protein concentrations were determined with a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime). Equivalent amounts of protein from each sample were combined with loading buffer, heat-denatured, and resolved by SDS-PAGE, followed by transfer onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature before incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (Millipore) and captured with a Tanon-5200 Automatic Chemiluminescence Imaging System (Tanon, China).

The co-immunoprecipitation assay

HEK293T cells expressing HA-UBR2 and/or Flag-EML4-ALK were exposed to DMSO, Lys-BA, Orn-BA, Dab-BA, or Dap-BA for 24 h prior to analysis. Cells were subsequently lysed in buffer containing 50 mmol/L Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 0.2% NP-40, 150 mmol/L NaCl, 2 mmol/L EDTA, and protease inhibitor cocktail at 4 °C for 20 min. The lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 14,000×g, and a portion (5%) of the total extract was reserved as input control. The remaining supernatant was incubated with 10 μL anti-FLAG® M2 magnetic beads at 4 °C for 4 h with gentle rotation. After five washes with ice-cold lysis buffer, bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 2 × SDS loading buffer. Samples were then resolved by SDS-PAGE and subjected to immunoblot analysis using the indicated antibodies.

The ubiquitylation assay

HEK293T cells co-expressing HA-UBR2, Flag-EML4-ALK and/or myc-Ub were treated with DMSO, Lys-BA, Orn-BA, Dab-BA, or Dap-BA for 24 h and subsequently incubated with MG132 for an additional 4 h. Ubiquitylation of EML4-ALK was analyzed by denaturing immunoprecipitation. Briefly, cells were disrupted in SDS-containing denaturing buffer composed of 50 mmol/L Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mmol/L NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1% SDS, and 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, followed by boiling for 10 min to ensure complete protein denaturation. The lysates were then diluted tenfold with a corresponding non-denaturing buffer lacking SDS. After clarification by centrifugation at 21,130×g, the cleared supernatants were incubated with anti-FLAG magnetic beads at 4 °C for 4 h. Beads were washed extensively with native lysis buffer, and the bound proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE, followed by immunoblot detection using the specified antibodies.

Lentiviral production and infection

Lentiviral particles were generated by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with the sgRNA-expressing transfer vector together with the packaging plasmid psPAX2 and the envelope plasmid pMD2.G using polyethylenimine (PEI). Culture supernatants containing viral particles were harvested after 48 h transfection, and passed through 0.45 μm filters to remove debris. The viruses were then used to infect target cells grown in medium containing 8 μg/mL polybrene. Infected cells were obtained in the presence of 1 μg/mL puromycin. The sgRNA targeting sequences used were: sgUBR1-1#, TGAGAGGATGGAAATCAGCG; sgUBR1-2#, GGACCCAGACTTGGAAAAGC; sgUBR2-1#, ATGTTCGGCCGAGGAGATTG; sgUBR2-2#, GTACCCAAAATCTACTGCAG; sgUBR4-1#, CCGGAACCAACTTCAGTCAG; sgUBR4-2#, GCACCATGAGAAGCAGTACG; sgUBR5-1#, TCCATTTCGTGGTTCACCCG; sgUBR5-2#, GTAGGACAAGCGACTCTCCA.

Cell viability assay

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well in 100 μL culture medium. After exposure to the indicated compounds at various concentrations for 48 h, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was subsequently recorded according to the manufacturer's protocol (YEASEN).

Cell cycle and apoptosis assays

H3122 cells were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells per well. Next day, cells were treated with Lys-BA, Orn-BA, or Dab-BA (500 nmol/L) for 24 h. For cell cycle determination, treated cells were rinsed three times with PBS and fixed in pre-chilled 70% ethanol at 4 °C for 2 h. Following fixation, cells were washed with cold PBS and stained with propidium iodide (PI) using the Cell Cycle and Apoptosis Analysis Kit (Beyotime, C1052) for 30 min at 37 °C. DNA content was then measured by flow cytometry on a BD FACSCanto SORP system. Apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime, C1062). Both floating and adherent cells were collected, washed with PBS, resuspended in binding buffer, and incubated with FITC-conjugated Annexin V and PI for 15 min in the dark. Stained samples were analyzed by flow cytometry using the BD FACSCanto SORP.

Protein expression and purification

A DNA fragment bearing the UBR box domain of human UBR2 (residues 98‒167), fused to an N-terminal His6 tag, was inserted into the pETDuet-1 vector via EcoRI restriction site. The plasmid is transformed into E. coli BL21 DE3 cells and induced with 0.5 mmol/L IPTG at 16 °C overnight. The cells were lysed in 20 mmol/L HEPES pH 7.4, 300 mmol/L NaCl, 2 mmol/L MgCl2, 20 mmol/L imidazole, 0.5 mmol/L TCEP and 1 mmol/L PMSF by ultrasonication. The extract was centrifuged at 16,000×g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was incubated with Ni-NTA resin (30210, QIAGEN) for 1 h at 4 °C. The nickel column was washed with a gradient of imidazole (20, 50, and 70 mmol/L) in wash buffer (200 mmol/L NaCl, 2 mmol/L MgCl2, 20 mmol/L Hepes pH 7.4, 0.5 mmol/L TCEP). The bound protein was eluted with 300 mmol/L imidazole, further purified on a Superdex200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated in 20 mmol/L HEPES, pH 7.4, 200 mmol/L NaCl, 2 mmol/L MgCl2.

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) analysis

ITC assays were performed using a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC instrument (Malvern Panalytical) at 25 °C. Purified UBR2 protein fragment (residues 98‒167) and each compound (Lys-BA, Orn-BA, Dab-BA, or Dap-BA) were dissolved in the same buffer (20 mmol/L HEPES, 200 mmol/L NaCl, 2 mmol/L MgCl2). For each measurement, the UBR2 protein (50 μmol/L, in the syringe) was titrated into a solution of a single compound (5 μmol/L, in the cell) with 19 injections of 2 μL each, except for the first injection of 0.4 μL, at an interval of 150 s and a reference power setting of 10 μcal/s. Analysis of the ITC data was performed with MicroCal PEAQ-ITC Analysis Software.

Quantitative proteomics

Cell culturing: H3122 cells were treated with DMSO, Lys-BA, Orn-BA, or Dab-BA (500 nmol/L) for 12 h. Cells were harvested and disrupted in 8 mol/L urea lysis buffer supplemented with EDTA-free protease inhibitors using mechanical scraping followed by sonication. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 21,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA assay.

Sample preparation and MS analysis: Samples were processed using the SISPROT workflow as previously described. LC–MS/MS analysis was carried out on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) interfaced with an UltiMate 3000 HPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a 20 cm × 100 μm i.d. analytical column in-house packed with 1.9 μm/120 Å C18 particles (Dr. Maisch GmbH). Loading and separation were performed at flow rates of 3 μL/min and 300 nL/min, respectively. Peptide separation employed a binary solvent system consisting of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water (buffer A) and 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in acetonitrile (buffer B). A 120-min gradient was implemented as follows: 2%–6% buffer B over 1 min, 6%–22% over 106 min, 22–36% over 8 min, 36–80% over 2 min, followed by equilibration at 98% buffer B for 3 min. Full MS spectra were recorded across an m/z range of 350–1250 at a resolving power of 60,000. Data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode was employed. Each duty cycle comprised one full MS scan and up to 40 MS/MS scans, acquired at resolutions of 60,000 for MS1 and 15,000 for MS/MS, respectively. Higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) was performed with a normalized collision energy of 28.

Peptide identification: Raw MS files were analyzed using MaxQuant (version 2.4.7.0) in conjunction with the human UniProt FASTA database (release of July 23, 2023). Label-free quantification (LFQ) was performed under default settings. Carbamidomethylation was defined as a fixed modification, while Met-loss (M), Met-loss plus acetylation (M), and protein N-terminal acetylation were treated as variable modifications. Peptide and protein identifications were filtered at a 1% false discovery rate (FDR).

Data processing: To ensure the reliability of the mass spectrometry data, only proteins supported by at least two peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs) were considered, and potential contaminants were removed. For global normalization, we assumed that samples subjected to the same experimental workflow would exhibit comparable overall proteomic abundance, despite treatment-induced changes in individual proteins. Median intensity values were determined independently for each dataset in the control and treatment groups. To harmonize medians within each group, datasets were adjusted by scaling with a constant factor when required.

To identify proteins showing differential degradation between control and treatment groups, statistical analysis was conducted using the LIMMA package in R (version 4.3.0). This approach allowed robust assessment of proteomic differences. For each protein, P values and log fold change (logFC) were computed, and proteins with P < 0.05 were considered significantly altered.

NanoBRET cellular ternary complex formation assay

HEK293T cells (8 × 105) were transfected with HaloTag-UBR2 plasmid (pHTN-UBR2, 2 μg) and NanoLuc-ALK plasmid (pNLF1-N-ALK, 0.2 μg). Next day, 2 × 105 transfected cells were replated and cultured for 24 h in the presence or absence of 100 nmol/L HaloTag® NanoBRET® 618 Ligand (Promega). Cells were subsequently treated with different concentrations of Lys-BA, Orn-BA, Dab-BA, or Dap-BA for 6 h. NanoBRET signals were measured 10 min after addition of the NanoBRET® Nano-Glo® Substrate. Background signals from cells lacking the HaloTag® NanoBRET® 618 Ligand were subtracted to calculate the NanoBRET ratios, which were expressed in milliBRET units (mBU) as the ratio of acceptor (618 nm) to donor (460 nm) signals multiplied by 1000. To evaluate intracellular ternary complex formation, fold changes in BRET signals were determined by dividing the mBU values from compound-treated cells by the mean mBU of DMSO-treated controls.

Solubility determination of Dab-BA

A surplus of Dab-BA was added to a centrifuge tube containing 100 μL water, and incubated with shake for 30 min. The supernatant was then collected after centrifuge. The concentration of the resulting saturated solution was determined from its peak area on the Dab-BA calibration curve.

Liver microsomal stability assay

Liver microsomal stability was evaluated using pooled human and mouse liver microsomes. Liver microsomes were diluted to a final protein concentration of 0.5587 mg/mL in PBS (100 mmol/L, pH 7.4). Microsomal incubations were performed in 96-well plates at 37 °C. For each incubation, 358 μL of the microsomal suspension was mixed with 40 μL of NADPH solution (10 mmol/L in PBS) to initiate metabolic reactions or with 40 μL of PBS as a negative control. The mixtures were pre-incubated at 37 °C for 10 min with shaking (800 rpm). Reactions were initiated by the addition of 2 μL of test compound solution. At designated time points (0.5, 15, 30, 45, and 60 min), 50 μL aliquots were transferred into 200 μL of ice-cold methanol or acetonitrile containing internal standards (ketoprofen, 35 ng/mL; carbamazepine, 7.5 ng/mL; diphenhydramine, 5 ng/mL; and tolbutamide, 10 ng/mL) to terminate the reactions. Samples were centrifuged at 3220×g for 40 min, and 100 μL of the supernatant was transferred to an analysis plate for LC–MS/MS analysis after dilution with water. The natural logarithm of the remaining parent compound (%) was plotted against incubation time, and the slope (k) was determined by linear regression. In vitro plasma stability assay

Test compounds (10 mmol/L stock solutions in water or DMSO) or the positive control propantheline (10 mmol/L in acetonitrile) were diluted tenfold to prepare working solutions. Human and mouse plasma samples were thawed at 37 °C and centrifuged at 3220×g for 10 min at room temperature to remove clots, and the supernatant was transferred to fresh tubes. For the assay, 2.5 μL of 1 mmol/L working solution was spiked into 497.5 μL of pre-warmed plasma in duplicate. 50 μL of spiked plasma was immediately mixed with 300 μL of ice-cold methanol or acetonitrile containing internal standards (35 ng/mL ketoprofen, 7.5 ng/mL carbamazepine, 5 ng/mL diphenhydramine, and 10 ng/mL tolbutamide), vortexed for 5 min, and centrifuged at 3220×g for 40 min to precipitate proteins. Spiked plasma samples were incubated at 37 °C with gentle shaking (∼60 rpm), and reactions were terminated at designated time points (15, 30, 60, and 120 min) by adding 300 μL of ice-cold methanol or acetonitrile containing internal standards, followed by vortexing and centrifugation at 3220×g for 40 min. The supernatant from each time point was transferred to an analysis plate containing an appropriate volume of water prior to LC–MS/MS analysis. The natural logarithm of the percentage of remaining parent compound was plotted against incubation time, and the slope (k) was determined by linear regression. The in vitro half-life (t1/2) was calculated using Eq. (1).

Plasma protein binding

Plasma protein binding of Dab-BA was determined using a high-throughput dialysis (HTD) device. Dialysis membranes were pre-soaked sequentially in water (60 min), 20% ethanol (20 min), and dialysis buffer (20 min). Dab-BA was prepared as a 200 μmol/L working solution in DMSO. Human or mouse plasma samples were thawed at 37 °C, centrifuged at 3220×g for 10 min to remove clots, transferred to fresh tubes, and pre-warmed at 37 °C for 10 min. For spiking, 3 μL of the working solution was added to 597 μL plasma and vortexed at 1000 rpm for 2 min. 50 μL of spiked plasma was mixed with 50 μL dialysis buffer and 200 μL methanol containing internal standards (35 ng/mL ketoprofen, 7.5 ng/mL carbamazepine, 5 ng/mL diphenhydramine, 10 ng/mL tolbutamide) to generate T0 samples. For equilibrium dialysis, 120 μL of spiked plasma and 120 μL of dialysis buffer were loaded into duplicate chambers of the HTD device, which was incubated at 37 °C with shaking (100 rpm, 5% CO2) for 6 h. Post-dialysis, 50 μL samples from the plasma and buffer chambers were mixed with 50 μL blank plasma or dialysis buffer and 200 μL methanol with internal standards to generate P and B samples, while 50 μL of remaining spiked plasma was processed similarly to generate T6 samples. All samples were centrifuged at 3220×g for 40 min, and 100 μL of the supernatant was transferred to an analysis plate containing water for LC–MS/MS analysis.

Cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzyme inhibition assay

The inhibitory effects of test compounds on major human cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes were evaluated using pooled human liver microsomes according to a standard cocktail inhibition assay. Incubations were conducted in PBS (100 mmol/L, pH 7.4) containing human liver microsomes (final protein concentration 0.2 mg/mL) and a five-substrate cocktail comprising phenacetin (CYP1A2, 40 μmol/L), diclofenac (CYP2C9, 6 μmol/L), S-mephenytoin (CYP2C19, 50 μmol/L), dextromethorphan (CYP2D6, 10 μmol/L), and midazolam (CYP3A4, 5 μmol/L). Furafylline, sulfaphenazole, N-3-benzylnirvanol, quinidine, and ketoconazole were used as positive control inhibitors for CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4, respectively. Test compound or positive controls (1 μL) at various concentrations were added to 96-well incubation plates in duplicate. Liver microsomes were pre-warmed at 37 °C for 5 min and then added to each well (179 μL). Reactions were initiated by the addition of NADPH (20 μL of 10 mmol/L solution; final concentration 1 mmol/L) and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. Reactions were terminated by the addition of 300 μL ice-cold methanol containing internal standards (35 ng/mL ketoprofen, 7.5 ng/mL carbamazepine, 5 ng/mL diphenhydramine, and 10 ng/mL tolbutamide). Samples were centrifuged at 3220×g for 40 min, and 100 μL of the supernatant was transferred to an analysis plate containing an appropriate volume of water for LC–MS/MS analysis. Metabolite formation was quantified by LC–MS/MS by monitoring acetaminophen (CYP1A2), 4-hydroxydiclofenac (CYP2C9), 4′-hydroxymephenytoin (CYP2C19), dextrorphan (CYP2D6), and 1′-hydroxymidazolam (CYP3A4). Enzyme activity was expressed as the percentage of metabolite formation relative to vehicle controls. IC50 values were determined by nonlinear regression using a four-parameter logistic model with XLfit software.

Pharmacokinetic (PK) studies of Dab-BA

Dab-BA was formulated in ddH2O and administered to BALB/c mice via intravenous (iv, 2 mg/kg, n = 3) or oral (po, 10 mg/kg, n = 3) routes. Blood samples were collected from the retro-orbital venous plexus into EDTA-K2 tubes at 0.083, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24 h post-dose. Plasma was obtained by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Dab-BA concentrations in plasma were quantified by HPLC, and pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated using non-compartmental analysis with Phoenix WinNonlin (version 8.1).

BALB/c mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (China) and housed in a pathogen-free facility with controlled temperature and humidity (23 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 10% humidity). All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care Committee of the Southern University of Science and Technology.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

IHC was performed to examine EML4-ALK expression in H3122 xenograft tumor sections. Tumor slices were first deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) at 95 °C. Endogenous peroxidase activity was inhibited using an endogenous peroxidase blocker (ZSGB-BIO) for 10 min. After application of the corresponding secondary antibody for 1 h, the staining was developed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB), followed by nuclear counterstaining with Hematoxylin.

H&E staining

Tissue sections from the heart, liver, lung, spleen, and kidney of H3122 tumor-bearing mice treated orally with vehicle, Brigatinib, or Dab-BA were processed and stained using the Hematoxylin and Eosin Kit (Beyotime, C0105S) following the kit protocol.
