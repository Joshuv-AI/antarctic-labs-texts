RNA‐Guided Engineering of the Chloroplast Genome Enabled by Plastid‐Expressed Guide RNAs
Authors: Malihe Mirzaee, Corinne Best, Evelyn V Wachowski, Pal Maliga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOur goal is to develop RNA‐guided engineering of the chloroplast genome using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. We designed chloroplast minigenes to obtain properly sized single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) in tobacco chloroplasts. The sgRNA 5′ end is defined by transcription from an rRNA operon promoter, and its 3′ end by processing a downstream tRNA (trnG) or a hepatitis delta virus (HDV) ribozyme. Cas9 is expressed from a nuclear gene and is targeted to chloroplasts by fusion to a transit peptide. Cas9 incorporated the sgRNA and introduced double‐strand breaks in the plastid DNA (ptDNA). We report here that the double‐strand DNA break in the ndhA and rpoC1 genes was repaired by microhomology‐mediated end joining (MMEJ), resulting in deletions in the ptDNA. We further showed that nuclear‐expressed sgRNA can be delivered into chloroplasts by fusion with a viroid RNA, as one possible approach for RNA‐guided engineering of the ptDNA without direct chloroplast genome transformation. These results are the first step of RNA‐guided editing of the chloroplast genome in any crop.Keywords: chloroplast DNA, CRISPR/Cas9, microhomology mediated end joining, single guide RNA, tobacco

Introduction

Chloroplast (plastid) genome engineering in tobacco ( Nicotiana tabacum ) requires selective enrichment of transformed plastid genomes in tissue culture cells, which carry thousands of genome copies per cell. The DNA to be inserted, which can be a mutant version of a native gene or a sequence entirely foreign to the plastid DNA, is typically linked to a selective spectinomycin resistance marker gene and is flanked by sequences homologous to the native plastid genome. Homologous recombination then enables insertion or deletion of large DNA fragments. This methodology is applicable to crops in which spectinomycin selection is effective, such as potato, tomato, soybean, and sugar beet (Maliga 2021; Bock 2015).

RNA‐guided engineering using CRISPR/Cas9 brought a revolution to engineer the genomes of prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms (Jinek et al. 2012; Anzalone et al. 2019), including the nuclear genome of plants (Gao 2021; Tuncel et al. 2025; Peterson et al. 2021). Challenges facing CRISPR/Cas9‐based genome editing have been assessed for editing plant organellar genomes. The perceived bottleneck of the application is the absence of a mechanism to import single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) into chloroplasts (Son and Park 2022; Nakazato and Arimura 2024; Kim and Chen 2024). To overcome this limitation, we decided to generate well‐defined sgRNAs directly in plastids.

We report here RNA‐guided genome engineering in tobacco by successfully targeting the Cas9 enzyme, expressed from a nuclear gene, to chloroplasts. We document the production of correctly sized sgRNAs from chloroplast minigenes. The sgRNAs were shown to function after incorporation in the Cas9 endonuclease by introduction of double‐strand breaks in the non‐essential ndhA and rpoC1 plastid genes. Subsequent sequencing of ptDNA confirmed microhomology‐mediated end joining (MMEJ) (Kwon et al. 2010) as the repair mechanism, consistent with the absence of non‐homologous end joining in chloroplasts.

Results

Design of sgRNA Genes

It is desirable to have uniform sgRNAs with well‐defined ends to guide Cas9 to the target site. Maps of minigenes tested for expression of sgRNAs in chloroplasts are shown in Figure 1A and Figure S1A. Defining the 5′‐ends was relatively easy since transcription start sites (TSSs) have been mapped for several plastid promoters. We tested the tobacco rubisco rbcL gene promoter, which initiates transcription from an A downstream of the ‐10 box (TACAATAATGA) (Shinozaki and Sugiura 1982; Shiina et al. 1998). We also tested the plastid ribosomal RNA operon PEP promoter (Prrn) from tobacco and maize. The tobacco promoter stutters, initiating from three different nucleotides (in bold) downstream of the ‐10 box (underlined) at TATATTTCTGGGAGCGA, whereas in maize, transcription initiates from a single G in the context TATACTGCTGGTG (Suzuki et al. 2003).

Defining sgRNA 3′‐ends was more complicated, since in chloroplasts, transcription termination is inefficient (Stern and Gruissem 1987). tRNAs in chloroplasts are processed by RNase P and RNase Z at the 5′ and 3′‐ends, respectively (Canino et al. 2009). We used a nuclear dicistronic tRNA‐snoRNA operon as a template for the processing of sgRNAs (Barbezier et al. 2009; Kruszka et al. 2003). The construct was created by adding the tobacco trnG gene downstream of the sgRNA either with or without the 11‐nt (linker) in between. We also employed a widely used self‐cleaving ribozyme strategy, incorporating a hammerhead (HH) ribozyme at the 5′‐end and a hepatitis delta virus (HDV) ribozyme at the 3′‐end of the sgRNA to achieve autonomous processing (Gao and Zhao 2014).

Altogether, seven sgRNA mini‐genes were constructed. They were then cloned into the pPRV111A plastid transformation vector (Zoubenko et al. 1994) and introduced in the plastid genome by the biolistic process (Maliga et al. 2021).

Processing of sgRNAs in Chloroplasts

To determine the sequence of sgRNA in chloroplasts, total cellular RNA was extracted from transplastomic tobacco leaves carrying the minigenes in the ptDNA. Total cellular RNA was sequenced, and the reads were aligned to the minigene sequences using the STAR alignment tool, then a pileup command was performed to visualize the coverage of each element of the minigene (Figure 1B and Figure S1B). The nucleotides at the 5′‐and 3′‐ends of the sgRNA are plotted on the histogram in Figure 1C and Figure S1C. The rbcL promoter (pCB101) yielded significantly less sgRNA (Figure S1D) than the Prrn promoters. The sgRNAs generated by Prrn promoters were longer than expected based on transcription start sites mapped by primer extension analyses. For example, though the maize Prrn promoter (pMM34, Figure 1C) initiates transcription from a single G (Suzuki et al. 2003). The sgRNA population included up to 3‐nucleotide‐long RNAs that matched the template. These longer transcripts were probably initiated by oligonucleotides using a primer‐dependent mechanism well characterized in Escherichia coli (Skalenko et al. 2021). The stuttering tobacco Prrn promoter generated more diversity of 5′‐ends, as expected. Of the tested ribozymes, the 5′ HH ribozyme was not processed at all (pCB110, Figure S1B). Based on these data, we believe that the Z. mays ribosomal RNA PEP operon promoter is the most suitable for the expression of the sgRNA in the chloroplast due to the more limited heterogeneity of the transcription start site.

As to the 3′ end, constructs encoding sgRNAs that are followed by trnG with an 11‐nucleotide linker showed processing of the tRNA, leaving some of the linker sequence behind attached to the sgRNA (Figure 1C and Figure S1C). Importantly, the construct with trnG without the spacer (pAH2) yielded an abundance of properly processed 3′ ends without additional nucleotides (Figure 1C). The 3′ HDV ribozyme efficiently self‐cleaved in both the pCB110 and pCB113 constructs (Figure 1B and Figure S1B), leaving 4Ts behind that were used as a linker. It remains to be seen if the sgRNA would be properly processed without the addition of the 4T linker.

Cas9 is Imported Into Chloroplasts

To ascertain that the Rubisco small subunit transit peptide delivers the large Cas9 protein (159 kDa) to chloroplasts, Cas9 was fused with the green fluorescent protein GFP (Figure 2A). First, we expressed four different fusion proteins in E. coli and inspected the culture for GFP fluorescence (Figure S2). The gene encoding the highest fluorescing Cas9‐GFP fusion protein was then transformed into the tobacco nucleus, and subcellular localization of GFP was tested by confocal microscopy. The fusion protein expressed from constitutive promoters (PSuper, 35S) was co‐localized with the chlorophyll signal, indicating import into chloroplasts (Figure 2B). Both Cas9 and GFP antibodies detected the correct size fusion protein on immunoblots (Figure 2C).

sgRNAs Guide Cas9 to Cleave Target Sites in the Chloroplast Genome

To document Cas9 activity in tobacco chloroplasts, both the Cas9 endonuclease and the sgRNA must be localized in the same cellular compartment. Cas9 is encoded in a nuclear gene, which was introduced into the tobacco nucleus by Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation (Figure 3A), while its product, the Cas9 endonuclease, is localized to chloroplasts (Figure 2). The Cas9 gene has an N‐terminal plastid‐targeting transit peptide (TP) and is linked to a gentamicin resistance gene (aacC1) in plasmid pSD7, a pPZP222 Agrobacterium binary vector derivative (Hajdukiewicz et al. 1994) (Figure 3A).

(A) Cas9 is expressed from a nuclear gene (TP‐Cas9) that incorporates an N‐terminal transit peptide (TP), which targets the encoded protein to chloroplasts. The sgRNA mini gene in the pCB101 vector inserts the mini gene in the ptDNA by homologous recombination. Cas9 binds the chloroplast‐expressed sgRNA and cleaves the target sequence. Tatt is a Terminator, obtained by fusing the Escherichia coli His‐Thr attenuators. (B) The genomic context of the target ndhA and rpoC1 genes in the ptDNA (GenBank Z00044). Genes on the top strand run 5′ to 3′ direction.

The targets for testing the CRISPR/Cas9 system in the ptDNA were the ndhA gene in the ndhH operon and the rpoC1 gene in the rpoB operon (Figure 3B). The sgRNA target is intron 1 of the ndhA gene. The ndhA gene is part of the ndhH operon, including the ndhH/A/I/H/E/D genes (Suorsa et al. 2009). If the deletion is relatively small and impacts only the ndhH genes, plants with a wild‐type/green phenotype are expected (Burrows et al. 1998; Shikanai et al. 1998). Rps15 encodes a non‐essential small plastid ribosomal subunit protein (Fleischmann et al. 2011). The essential genes flanking the ndhH operon are ycf5 (ccsA) (Xie and Merchant 1996) and ycf1 (Kikuchi et al. 2013).

The rpoB operon contains the rpoB, rpoC1, and rpoC2 reading frames encoding the β, β′ and β″ subunits of the plastid‐encoded multisubunit RNA polymerase (PEP) (Toyoshima et al. 2005). The coding region of three genes spans over 10.3 kb (Figure 3B). Deletion of any of the PEP subunits in tobacco yields the same non‐photosynthetic, pigment‐deficient phenotype (Allison et al. 1996; Serino and Maliga 1998). Such plants can be maintained as grafts on wild‐type plants or on sucrose‐containing medium.

To test sgRNA‐guided Cas9 activity in chloroplasts, we had to combine Cas9 and the sgRNA in one compartment. This was achieved by pollinating the minigene‐carrying maternal plant with the pollen of a tobacco carrying the Cas9 gene in the nucleus. (Note that in tobacco, as in most flowering plants, plastids are inherited by the maternal parent but the nuclear genes by both parents.) The seeds were then germinated on a medium containing spectinomycin and gentamicin (Figure 4A and Figure S3A). Spectinomycin selected for the presence of the minigene by the linked spectinomycin resistance (aadA) gene in the transformation vector. Gentamicin selected for the presence of the linked, chloroplast‐targeted Cas9 gene. In the plate, only the seed derived from the cross turned green because it carried both genes in the same cells. The plants, when grown bigger in culture, developed pigment‐deficient sectors, a sign of having deletions in essential genes.

Deletions in the ndhH operon were repaired by microhomology‐mediated end joining (MMEJ), which could be detected by PCR. A panel of PCR assays is shown in Figure 4B. On top are the samples taken from plants with the rbcL promoter driving the minigenes (pCB101 × pSD7). Below are the samples with the tobacco Prrn promoter driving the minigene (pMM51 × pSD7). Two abundant amplicons are the 1.3‐kb and 0.7‐kb bands on the gels. The 1.3‐kb fragment is the product of MMEJ between 14‐nt direct repeats (#1 in Figure 4C,D). When Cas9 introduced a double‐stranded DNA break, the ends were digested by exonucleases until the dsDNA break was resealed by MMEJ. Rejoining the broken ends was random. The most abundant amplicon is the 0.7‐kb band that resulted from recombination via the 15/1 nucleotide #2 repeats (Figure 4C,D). Importantly, in all cases of deletions, the microhomology sequences mediating DNA repair flanked the ndhA sgRNA target site, confirming the precision of sgRNA‐guided Cas9 cleavage in plastids.

In addition to ndhA, we successfully targeted the rpoC1 gene, an essential component of the plastid‐encoded RNA polymerase (PEP) complex, to ensure Cas9/sgRNA specificity and editing outcomes in chloroplasts. We confirmed deletions at the rpoC1 locus through sequencing PCR amplifications of ptDNA of seedlings germinating on dual antibiotic selection (Figure S3B). These deletions were flanked by short microhomology regions (9–14 nucleotides), consistent with the MMEJ repair mechanism. As with the previous targets, the Cas9‐induced double‐stranded breaks at the rpoC1 target site led to deletions repaired by MMEJ, further validating the system's targeting accuracy (Figure S3C,D). Thus, we have successfully extended Cas9‐mediated engineering to manipulate the plastid genome.

Off‐Target Mutations in the Plastid Genome

Our strategy was to regenerate green plants (Figure 5A) from the variegated leaves with deletions confined to the ndhH gene cluster, and then sequence the plastid genomes of each plant to identify off‐target mutations. These plants expressed Cas9 (Figure 5B) and carried the sgRNA minigene (Figure 5C). DNA gel blot and PCR analyses confirmed that the plants were homoplastomic for the deletions (Figure S4). Sequencing of complete plastid genomes revealed that each of the plants had a single deletion flanking the ndhA target site, and the size of the deletion was different depending on the location of microhomology that was involved in restoring the double‐stranded DNA (Figure 5D). DNA sequence coverage was uniform throughout the plastid genome, indicating the absence of off‐target mutations. The exceptions were deletions at the sgRNA target site in the ndhA gene, where the sequence coverage dropped from ~4000/~5000 to background levels (~200) (Figure 5E,F).

ELVd RNA to Target the sgRNA From the Nucleus to Chloroplasts

Extending plastid genome editing to all crops requires that plastid DNA editing be dependent only on nuclear genome transformation. In our plant chloroplast system, the Cas9 is already encoded in a nuclear gene. We needed a solution to target the sgRNA from a nuclear gene to chloroplasts, as schematically shown in Figure 6G. Plant viroids have been shown to target mRNAs to chloroplast when the viroid RNA is used as the leader of a chimeric gfp mRNAs that resulted in GFP accumulation in chloroplasts (Gómez and Pallás 2010; Baek et al. 2017). Viroids are small, single‐stranded, circular RNAs infecting plants. Composed of only a few hundred nucleotides and not encoding any proteins, viroids represent the lowest level of complexity for an infectious agent. Despite the relatively small size, viroids contain RNA structural elements required to interact with host factors involved in their infectious cycle (Navarro et al. 2021). The family of Avsunviroidae, like the Eggplant Latent Viroid (ELVd) are specifically targeted to chloroplasts where replication is based on symmetric rolling‐circle mechanisms that involves a self‐cleaving hammerhead motif (Giguère et al. 2014).

Proteins translated on cytoplasmic ribosomes are imported into chloroplasts only if they possess an N‐terminal extension termed transit peptide (TP) (Li and Chiu 2010). In the absence of a TP, the only explanation for GFP accumulation in chloroplasts is that the chimeric ELVd:gfp mRNA is translated on chloroplast ribosomes (Gómez and Pallás 2010; Baek et al. 2017). We duplicated this experiment using a stable transgenic system, fusing the ELVd sequence with the GFP mRNA and expressed it in a cauliflower mosaic virus 35S promoter‐terminator cassette. The gene design is shown in Figure 6A. We then cloned the construct into an Agrobacterium pPZP222 binary vector and introduced it into the tobacco nuclear genome by selection for gentamicin resistance (Hajdukiewicz et al. 1994) (Figure 6A). Construct pEW3 is a duplication of the construct tested by Gomez and Pallas in N. benthamiana in 2010 (Gómez and Pallás 2010). In our hands, in N. tabacum , the leaf extract of pEW3 plants did not contain any GFP when tested by Western blots and confocal microscopy. The pEW3 construct has an imperfect variant of the Kozak consensus sequence (GCNAUGC (Lyons et al. 2026)) that would not be suitable for chloroplast expression. In the pEW8 construct, we inserted the T7g10 phage leader sequence known to support high‐level protein expression in chloroplasts (Kuroda and Maliga 2001). Protein extracts of pEW8 leaves contain GFP protein (Figure 6C), and the GFP is localized to chloroplasts by confocal microscopy (Figure 6D).

Because we had tobacco plants accumulating Cas9 from a nuclear gene, we had the option to test sgRNA targeting from the nucleus to chloroplasts. We fused the Eggplant Latent Viroid (ELVd) leader sequence (Gómez and Pallás 2010) with the sgRNA targeting the ndhA gene, cloned it into a 35S promoter/terminator cassette (P35S:ELVd‐sgRNAndhA:T35S). The RNA sequence was then introduced into the tobacco nucleus in a pPZP212 Agrobacterium vector (Hajdukiewicz et al. 1994) by selection for kanamycin resistance (Figure 6E). Fifty independently transformed, kanamycin resistant shoots were isolated and tested for deletion in the ndhA plastid gene by PCR. Out of fifty independently transformed lines, five lines had the 0.7‐kb PCR fragment (Figure 6F), the most frequent product formed by MMEJ after cleavage of ndhA at the target site (Figure 4B). Specific cleavage of the ndhA gene and restoration of the double‐stranded DNA by microhomology‐mediated recombination is the first independent confirmation of RNA delivery from the nucleus to chloroplasts by the ELVd sequence in a system, when the Cas9 protein is already accumulating in chloroplast expressed from a second nuclear gene (Figure 6G). Five ELVd:sgRNA+Cas9 (pEW4 + pSD7) plants were obtained (Figure 6F), of which four survived in the greenhouse, where they flowered and produced seed. The seed was viable and germinated. However, none of the ELVd:sgRNA+Cas9 plants in the greenhouse expressed the ELVd:sgRNA gene, indicating the ELVd:sgRNA gene was silenced in the transgenic plants.

Discussion

In 2019, plastid (and mitochondrial) genome engineering has been extended to all crops based on a TALE‐repeat‐based methodology that requires transformation of the nuclear DNA. This methodology enabled the introduction of double‐strand breaks in the organellar DNA by TALENs and mutagenesis within a 12–18 nucleotide window. Because nuclear genome transformation is widely available, base editors could be applied to the economically important monocots maize and rice (Kim and Chen 2024; Nakazato and Arimura 2024). TALE‐based organelle editors require only a single nuclear transformation and no plastid transformation, making them faster to implement and applicable to crop species lacking efficient plastid transformation systems.

Here we report the successful production of properly sized sgRNA from chloroplast minigenes (Figure 1). We converted Cas9 into a plant nuclear gene by using a codon‐optimized variant and fusing the Cas9 N‐terminus with the chloroplast‐targeting, cleavable transit peptide of the Rubisco small subunit gene (Figure 2). Cas9 expressed from a nuclear gene then incorporated the chloroplast‐produced sgRNA and efficiently and specifically cleaved the ndhA and rpoC1 target sites. The current approach requires a separate plastid transformation event for every sgRNA target or combination of sgRNA targets. Compared to the nuclear CRISPR system, this limits design flexibility and multiplexing capacity. Cleavage of the dsDNA was repaired by MMEJ, as predicted (Kwon et al. 2010), which resulted in deletions in the ptDNA via microhomologies flanking the target site (Figure 4 and Figure S3). The leaves of plants carrying a Cas9/sgRNA were variegated due to deletions extending beyond the ndhH gene cluster. We regenerated green plants from the leaves of variegated ndhH operon plants. Some of the green plants regenerated from the green sectors were already homoplastomic for the deletions (Figure S4). Although Cas9 and sgRNA were still present in plants with deletions in the ndhH operon, no off‐target effect was found by sequencing the chloroplast genome (Figure 5).

Our results establish the foundation for RNA‐guided engineering of the chloroplast genome by overcoming the limitation of guide RNA delivery into chloroplasts. Future work will focus on chloroplast genome engineering through the implementation of precision editing systems, including base editing and Prime Editing, which introduce precise nucleotide changes without generating double‐strand DNA breaks. Base editors combine a catalytically impaired Cas nuclease with a nucleobase deaminase to introduce point mutations at target loci. Cytosine base editors (CBEs) convert C•G base pairs to T•A, whereas adenine base editors (ABEs) convert A•T base pairs to G•C (Rees and Liu 2018). Prime Editing employs a Cas9 nickase fused to a reverse transcriptase and a prime‐editing guide RNA (pegRNA) to introduce defined sequence changes into the ptDNA (Anzalone et al. 2019). Base editing and Prime Editing would first be tested in species in which the ptDNA can be directly transformed for expression of the guide RNAs, such as potato, tomato, soybean, and sugar beet. In addition, expression of both the Cas9 enzymes and guide RNAs from nuclear genes would enable extension of these technologies to crops amenable only to nuclear transformation. The tool proposed for guide RNA delivery from the plant nucleus to chloroplasts is based on plant viroid RNA sequences (Son and Park 2022) or the mRNA encoding the eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E (Nicolai et al. 2007). We have shown here that fusion of sgRNA with the Eggplant Latent Viroid RNA is suitable to target RNAs to chloroplasts where it specifically cleaved the plastid ndhA gene. The ELVd gene was silenced in mature plants. However, silencing of ELVd in a more refined system could be beneficial when guide RNAs are expressed in egg cells, enabling chloroplast genome editing at an early developmental stage. Once editing has occurred in the egg cells, continued persistence of the viroid would no longer be necessary, and silencing of ELVd could be an advantage by limiting its persistence in vegetative tissues.

Materials and Methods

Construction of the Nuclear Pt‐Cas9 and Chloroplast Minigene Vectors

Our nuclear Pt‐Cas9 gene is derived from plasmid pMD18, a PU18 derivative, which carries Cas9 as an NcoI‐BamHI fragment in PUbi1/TUbi1 cassette codon‐optimized for expression in the Arabidopsis nucleus (Liu et al. 2015). N‐ and C‐terminal nuclear localization signals have been removed using NcoI/BglII and EagI/BamHI restriction sites, respectively. To enable chloroplast targeting, the Cas9 N‐terminus was translationally fused with a Rubisco small subunit transit peptide (TP) using NcoI/BglII restriction sites to obtain the plastid‐targeted Cas9 coding sequence (Pt‐Cas9). The Pt‐Cas9 sequence was cloned into the cauliflower mosaic virus promoter/terminator expression cassette (P35S/T35S) (Timmermans et al. 1990) using the NcoI/XbaI restriction sites, yielding plasmid pSD2, and the expression cassette (P35S:Pt‐Cas9:T35S) was inserted into the pPZP222 Agrobacterium binary vector (Hajdukiewicz et al. 1994) as a HindIII/EcoRI fragment, generating pSD7. The DNA sequence of Pt‐Cas9 is listed in Table S1.

The sgRNA genes were obtained by fusing the spacer sequence with the sgRNA scaffold (Jinek et al. 2012) and expressing the RNA from a chloroplast promoter. Plastid‐expressed sgRNA minigenes were synthesized and assembled in pPRV111A derivatives (Zoubenko et al. 1994) as SacI/HindIII fragments. Promoters included the tobacco rbcL promoter (in pCB101), the tobacco rrn operon PEP promoter (Suzuki et al. 2003) (in pMM48, pMM51, pAH2, pCB110, and pCB113), and the maize rrn operon promoter (in pMM34). 3′ end processing elements were either the tobacco chloroplast trnG gene (±11 nt spacer) or a hepatitis delta virus (HDV) ribozyme. The minigene sequences are listed in Table S1.

Construction of Pt‐Cas9‐gfp Fusion Vectors

To evaluate the subcellular localization of Pt‐Cas9, four Pt‐Cas9‐gfp fusion constructs were initially tested in Escherichia coli ( E. coli ) to identify peptide linkers that preserved fusion protein conformation. The Pt‐Cas9 coding sequence was fused at its C‐terminus to GFP via one of four flexible linkers: AAALDLE (Park et al. 2021), (GGGGS)2 and (GGGGS)3 (Zheng et al. 2018), or ELVEGKLELVEGLKV (Khan and Maliga 1999). Constructs were assembled by cloning the Pt‐Cas9 coding region into the bacterial expression vector pET28a using NcoI and BamHI, followed by insertion of the linker‐gfp fragment using the MfeI and HindIII restriction enzymes. The (GGGGS)3 linker yielded the strongest GFP fluorescence signal in E. coli and was selected for use in plant studies. To generate the plant Pt‐Cas9‐gfp nuclear transformation construct, the (GGGGS)3 linker‐gfp sequence was subcloned downstream of Pt‐Cas9 in pSD7 binary vector using the MfeI/XbaI restriction sites, generating pMM88. As an alternative, Pt‐Cas9‐gfp was cloned in a 2′ super promoter (Lee et al. 2007)/Agroopine synthase terminator (Genbank CP033030) cassette. First, Pt‐Cas9 was cloned in the PSuper/Tagr cassette using NcoI/XbaI, yielding pMM74. The PSuper:Pt‐Cas9‐gfp:Tagr cassette was then inserted into pPZP222 binary vector via HindIII/EcoRI (pMM 75), followed by insertion of the (GGGGS)3‐gfp sequence to produce pMM79.

Construction of the ELVd‐sgRNA Nuclear Transformation Vector

We fused the Eggplant Latent Viroid (ELVd) leader sequence (Gómez and Pallás 2010) with the sgRNA targeting the ndhA gene, and cloned it into a 35S promoter/terminator cassette (P35S:ELVd‐sgRNAndhA:T35S). The RNA sequence was then introduced into the tobacco nucleus in a pPZP212 Agrobacterium vector (Hajdukiewicz et al. 1994) with a kanamycin resistance plant marker (Figure 6B). DNA sequence of the vector is included in Table S1.

Transformation of the Plastid and Nuclear Genomes

The transgenes were introduced into the nuclear and chloroplast genomes of Nicotiana tabacum cv. Petit Havana (tobacco) plants. Tobacco plastid transformation was performed as described (Maliga et al. 2021). Nuclear transformation was conducted as reported (Lutz et al. 2006).

Confocal Microscopy

Pt‐Cas9‐GFP expression in bacterial cells and its localization in chloroplasts of tobacco leaf cells were examined using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope in the Waksman Institute Shared Imaging Facility. GFP was excited at 488 nm, and chlorophyll autofluorescence at 586 nm. Emission was collected between 500–530 nm for GFP and 650–700 nm for chlorophyll.

PCR Analysis

PCR was performed with AccuStart II PCR SuperMix (Quantabio) to detect Cas9‐induced deletions at the ndhA and rpoC1 loci in the chloroplast genome. Total cellular DNA was extracted from leaf tissue using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol. Primer pairs were as follows: ndhA, MM46 (5′‐CAATACTCAACGCAATTACCACA) and MM47 (5′‐ATGGTCTAGACGTCGTAGAGAATTG); rpoC1, MM59 (5′‐TATGAATCGATTTACAAGTCCAATG) and MM60 (5′‐ATATGAAATTCAGACTCATGTGAC). Expected wild‐type amplicons were 4.9 kb (ndhA) and 4.9 kb (rpoC1). PCR products were resolved on 1% agarose gels. Deletion alleles appeared as smaller fragments and were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Microhomology‐mediated recombination at the deletion sites was validated by sequence analysis.

To confirm the presence of the plastid‐integrated ndhA sgRNA minigene in Nt‐ΔndhA lines, PCR was performed using sgRNA forward (5′‐GATTCTTGTACATGTTTTAGAGCTAG‐3′) and tRNAGly reverse (5′‐GGCGGATAGCGGGAATCGAA‐3′) primers, yielding a specific 0.17 kb amplicon.

To characterize deletions in the ndhH operon in homoplastomic Nt‐ΔndhA lines regenerated from variegated tissue, line‐specific primer combinations were employed. Nt‐ΔndhA#1 was analysed using primers MM86 (5′‐AAAACTAATAAATACGGGCTTCTG‐3′) and MM87 (5′‐CTATATCAGAGTCATTATGTTGGAG‐3′); Nt‐ΔndhA#2 with primers MM87 and MM88 (5′‐ACTTCTTGATCATTTCTACGAACG‐3′); and Nt‐ΔndhA#3 with primers MM87 and MM89 (5′‐TGCACCTATTAAAGCAACTAAAAG‐3′). PCR products were resolved by agarose gel electrophoresis and yielded fragments consistent with the expected deletion sizes for each independent line.

Southern Blot Analysis

Total leaf DNA (2 μg) was digested with DraI/NheI (Nt‐ΔndhA#1), ScaI/NheI (Nt‐ΔndhA#2), or MfeI/NheI (Nt‐ΔndhA#3) and separated on 1% agarose gels. DNA was transferred to Hybond‐N membranes (GE Healthcare) by capillary blotting. Probe labeling, hybridization, and detection were performed using the DIG Starter Kit II (Roche) according to the manufacturer's instructions. A 425‐nt ndhH probe was generated for Nt‐ΔndhA#1 and Nt‐ΔndhA#2 using primers MM84 (5′‐ACGTCGGATTTCTAAATTTTCG‐3′) and MM85 (5′‐TGATGCATAATTATTTTCGTATCG‐3′). A 723‐nt ndhI/ndhA probe was generated for Nt‐ΔndhA#3 using primers MM82 (5′‐ATAGTTACAGGTAAACGATTTGCG‐3′) and MM83 (5′‐AGTTGATATAGTGGAAGCGCAG‐3′).

Protein Extraction and Immunoblot

Total soluble protein (TSP) was extracted from tobacco leaves following the protocol of Lawrence et al. (2003). Cas9 and GFP proteins were detected by monoclonal anti‐GFP antibody (Living Color Peptide, Clontech; 1:1000 dilution) and monoclonal anti‐Cas9 antibody (clone 7A9, Sigma‐Aldrich; 1:1000 dilution).

Sequencing of Small RNAs and Data Processing

Total RNA was isolated from fully expanded leaves using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) and treated with TURBO DNase I (Thermo). RNA sample preparation, quality control steps, and small RNA sequencing were carried out by Admera Health, Plainfield, NJ, USA. Small RNA libraries were prepared using either the SMARTer smRNA‐seq Kit (Takara Bio) or the NEBNext Small RNA Library Prep Set for Illumina (New England Biolabs), following the manufacturer's protocol. Libraries were pooled and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus 10B 2 × 150 platform to generate 150 bp paired‐end reads, yielding approximately 15–25 million reads per sample. Initial data processing was also carried out by Admera. Raw sequencing reads were evaluated for quality using FastQC (v0.11.9). High‐quality reads were retained for downstream analysis and mapped to reference sequences of 500 nucleotides each beginning at the SacI cloning site for each minigene using STAR with the parameter –seedSearchStartLmax set to 50. This parameter restricts STAR from searching for mapping seeds beyond position 50 in short reads (such as tRNAs), effectively filtering out all tRNAs not fused to gRNA from the mapping results. Coverage graphs were generated using the pileup command and exported to Excel for visualization.

Chloroplast Genome Sequencing and Data Analyses

Total cellular DNA was extracted from leaf tissue using the CTAB protocol. RNAse treatment and library preparation were performed by Admera Health, South Plainfield, NJ, USA, using the KAPA HyperPrep Kit (Roche). The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina platform 2 × 150, with an estimated 150M PE reads per sample (75M each side). Data analyses involved Quality Control and Indel/SNP calling using the minigene‐containing plastid genome obtained by inserting the mini gene sequences in the tobacco ptDNA GenBank Z00044.
