Coexistence of anti-GluK2 and anti-LGI1 antibodies in a patient with autoimmune encephalitis: a rare case report and literature review
Authors: Zhuqing Luan, Zhigang Liang, Fuxia Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAutoimmune encephalitis (AE) is a neurologic dysfunctional disorder resulting from diffuse or multiple inflammatory lesions in the brain parenchyma mediated by autoimmune mechanisms. The diagnosis of AE relies on the detection of specific autoantibodies in serum or cerebrospinal fluid, which help define distinct clinical syndromes and guide immunotherapy. However, its clinical manifestations can be diverse and atypical, posing a great challenge for early diagnosis.Case presentationWe report the first case in Asia of an adult female with autoimmune encephalitis who tested positive for both serum anti-GluK2 and anti-LGI1 antibodies. Cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed abnormal signals in the right hippocampus. Her clinical presentation was atypical, characterized by paroxysmal nervousness and panic attacks. She showed a favorable response to immunotherapy with methylprednisolone and mycophenolate mofetil.ConclusionsThis case highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by atypical presentations of AE and the importance of antibody testing in such settings. We also review the relevant literature to improve the diagnosis and management of atypical AE.Keywords: Autoimmune encephalitis, Anti-GluK2 antibody, Anti-LGI1 antibody, Autonomic symptoms, Atypical symptoms

Introduction

Autoimmune encephalitis (AE) is a group of abnormal immune response disorders mediated by central nervous system-specific antigens, accounting for approximately 10%-20% of all types of encephalitis [1]. The clinical manifestations of AE are complex and diverse, ranging from typical limbic system-related symptoms (such as epileptic seizures, memory impairment), to rare manifestations including autonomic dysfunction and even isolated psychiatric abnormalities. These present a great challenge for the early diagnosis of AE, atypical AE is frequently misdiagnosed as psychiatric disorders, viral encephalitis, neurodegenerative diseases, and other related conditions, resulting in delayed treatment and poor prognosis.

Antibody detection is essential for AE diagnosis, with the continuous improvement of antibody detection technologies, an increasing number of AE-related antibodies have been identified. Among AE patients with positive antibodies, 1.88% of them exhibit “double-positive” antibodies [2]. Such “double-positive” cases tend to present with more complex and diverse clinical manifestations, which cannot be explained by a single mechanism. Further more, our understanding of the combined effects of “double-positive” antibodies and their corresponding clinical manifestations remains relatively limited, with few relevant case reports available. Notably, there have been no report for coexistence of anti-LGI1 and anti-GluK2 antibodies in Asian adult population with AE, and the combined effects of these antibodies on the clinical features, imaging findings, and treatment responses remain unclear.

To yield more information for clinical practice, we report the first case of AE in an Asian adult female with coexistence of anti-LGI1 and anti-GluK2 antibodies. We further summarize the previous reports for these two antibodies in AE patients to enhance the clinicians’ understanding and provide guidelines for AE treatment.

Case description

A 33-year-old female patient, generally healthy, had no history of emotional fluctuations or excessive fatigue prior to onset, and no special history of cold exposure, diarrhea, vaccination, or other relevant factors, presenting to a local hospital on October, 2024, with episodic nervousness, palpitations, and poor sleep lasting over 10 days. She reported episodic nervousness without clear triggers starting over 10 days prior, accompanied by subjective feelings of coldness and swelling in her hands and feet, cold intolerance, mild sleep-onset insomnia (difficulty falling asleep, approximately 2–3 h per night), poor sleep quality, and neck discomfort. She was diagnosed with anxiety, depression, sleep disorder, and sympathetic cervical spondylosis at the local hospital, where she received cervical spine rehabilitation therapy and oral zopiclone for sleep improvement. While her sleep improved with treatment, her nervousness and panic attacks persisted and gradually worsened. Half a month later, she developed non-rotational dizziness, occasional nausea, and vomiting of small amounts of gastric contents. A cranial MRI with Diffusion-Weighted Imaging (DWI) (3T) revealed abnormal signals in the right hippocampus and suspected abnormal signals in the right frontal lobe on DWI, prompting her transfer to our hospital for further evaluation and management.

On admission (November 7, 2024), her Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score was 30 (Supplementary File 1) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MOCA) score was 30 (Supplementary File 2), with an overall good general condition. She was alert and oriented, with normal mental status, judgment, and calculation abilities. She had no aphasia and spoke clearly. Physical examination showed normal neurological function.

Laboratory tests revealed mild abnormalities: hypoproteinemia (39.36 g/L, normal range: 40.00–55.00 g/L), elevated total bilirubin (27.9 µmol/L, normal range: ≤26 µmol/L) with elevated indirect bilirubin (23.9 µmol/L, normal range: 3–19 µmol/L), hypertriglyceridemia (1.77 mmol/L, normal range: 0.40–1.70 mmol/L), reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (0.95 mmol/L, normal range: 1.04–1.96 mmol/L, elevated immunoglobulin(Ig) M (3.36 g/L, normal range: 0.3–2.2 g/L), reduced thrombin time (13.6 s, normal range: 14.0–21.0 s), elevated D-dimer (1.39 mg/L, normal range: 0.0–1.0 mg/L), urobilinogen tested positive (+), elevated urine specific gravity (1.031, normal range: 1.003–1.030) and elevated neuron-specific enolase (NSE) (23.6 ng/ml, normal range: 0.0–17.0 ng/ml). No significant abnormalities were found in immune or rheumatoid-related laboratory tests including IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, C4, IgE, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein, anti-streptolysin “O”, rheumatoid factor, lupus anticoagulant, ANCA, MPO, PR3, antinuclear antibodies, anti-U1RNP, anti-Sm, anti-SSA, anti-SSB, anti-Jo-1, anti-Scl-70, anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies.

Lumbar puncture (performed on November 10, 2024) yielded clear, colorless cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) without clots. The opening pressure was 100 mmH2O. No significant abnormalities were found in routine CSF examination and CSF biochemistry was unremarkable. No bacterial growth was detected after 7 days of culture in CSF bacterial tests. As multiple sclerosis was not considered in this patient’s diagnosis, CSF oligoclonal bands and IgG index were not assessed. AE-related antibody testing for CSF and serum demonstrated a serum anti-LGI1 IgG antibody positive at 1:30 titer and anti-GluK2 antibody positive at 1:10 titer; both antibodies were negative in the cerebrospinal fluid.

A cranial Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA, 1.5T ) was performed with no significant abnormalities noted. A contrast-enhanced MRI (3T) with DWI (Fig. 1) revealed abnormal signals in the right hippocampus, suggesting possible inflammatory changes. Hippocampal magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS, 3T, Figs. 2) showed right hippocampal N-acetylaspartate/(choline+creatine) [NAA/(Cho + Cr)] ratio of 40.8%, left hippocampal NAA/(Cho + Cr) ratio of 62.8%, and a bilateral ratio asymmetry index of 22%. The results indicated decreased NAA/(Cho + Cr) in the right hippocampus.

Diagnostic assessment

Upon admission, the patient was initially administered methylprednisolone sodium succinate for injection (Milestone) 80 mg once daily (qd). Concurrently, symptomatic treatments were provided, including calcium and potassium supplementation to maintain electrolyte balance, and gastric mucosal protection to mitigate steroid-related side effects. This regimen was continued for three days.

After completion of relevant examinations, a comprehensive analysis was conducted, synthesizing key findings into a definitive diagnosis. Cranial MRI revealed structural abnormalities with non-enhancing signal changes in the right hippocampus, while MRS demonstrated metabolic disturbance evidenced by a decreased NAA peak in the same region. The pivotal and distinctive evidence came from serological testing, which confirmed the simultaneous presence of serum anti-GluK2 and anti-LGI1 antibodies—a combination not previously reported in an Asian adult. Having excluded neoplastic and ischemic cerebrovascular diseases, these findings collectively established the diagnosis of autoimmune encephalitis with the rare coexistence of anti-LGI1 and anti-GluK2 antibodies.

Based on the diagnostic findings, the treatment plan was adjusted accordingly. The patient received intravenous methylprednisolone (0.5 g daily), which was tapered to 0.25 g daily after three days and then switched to oral prednisone (60 mg daily). Mycophenolate mofetil (0.5 g twice daily) was added as a steroid-sparing agent. Following this regimen, the patient’s symptoms improved markedly: episodic nervousness and palpitations resolved completely, and sleep quality normalized. Prednisone was gradually reduced by 5 mg per week and eventually discontinued. The patient remains on maintenance mycophenolate mofetil (0.5 g twice daily) and continues to be in clinical remission.

At the most recent outpatient follow-up, neuroimaging (Fig. 4) did not reveal any significant abnormal signal intensities. Serum antibody testing revealed anti-LGI1 antibodies at a titer of 1:32, while anti-GluK2 antibodies were no longer detectable. Based on a comprehensive evaluation of the patient’s clinical status, imaging findings, and laboratory results, our multidisciplinary team decided to maintain mycophenolate mofetil therapy for an additional year. Discontinuation of immunosuppression will be reconsidered after this period provided that the patient remains clinical remission.

Discussion

AE is a group of autoimmune-mediated inflammatory diseases of the central nervous system induced by factors such as tumors and viruses [1], with complex and diverse clinical phenotypes [2]. When patients present with atypical combinations of manifestations profiles, the difficulty of diagnosis and differential diagnosis becomes particularly prominent [3, 4].

This patient represents the first reported case in Asia with coexistence of serum anti-GluK2 and anti-LGI1 antibodies. Her MRS indicated decreased NAA/(Cho + Cr) in the right hippocampus compared to the contralateral unaffected side, reflecting neuronal dysfunction and metabolic abnormalities, which confirmed the presence of abnormal inflammatory signals in the hippocampus as shown on MRI. This report is limited by the absence of CSF oligoclonal band and IgG index data. However, the diagnosis was firmly established based on clinical presentation, specific serum antibodies, and characteristic neuroimaging findings. Unlike typical hippocampal epilepsy [5], this patient’s main clinical manifestations were autonomic symptoms—episodic palpitations and sleep disturbances—leading to an initial misdiagnosis as somatic symptoms of anxiety disorder. This case highlights the diversity of clinical presentations in AE and its diagnostic challenges [6].

Anti-LGI1 antibodies predominantly affect the hippocampus, which correlates with the limbic system involvement observed in anti-LGI1 encephalitis⁷. Since its discovery in 2010⁸, it has been identified as a clinically significant antibody associated with autoimmune encephalitis⁹. Core manifestations of anti-LGI1 encephalitis are closely linked to limbic system impairment, including epileptic seizures, memory decline, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and characteristic faciobrachial dystonic seizures (FBDS)¹⁰. Imaging findings primarily involve the limbic system, with occasional basal ganglia or cerebral involvement¹¹. Initial symptoms are often insidious, and patients may be misdiagnosed with psychiatric disorders before antibody testing confirms the diagnosis¹². Unlike these typical presentations, our patient did not exhibit epileptic seizures or cognitive decline. Her prominent symptoms were autonomic: episodic palpitations, sleep disturbances, cold intolerance, and limb swelling—leading to initial misdiagnosis as anxiety disorder. Although 35.7% of anti-LGI1 patients show epileptiform discharges on EEG¹³, our patient’s EEG was normal, further highlighting the uniqueness of this case.

The anti-GluK2 antibody is a relatively rare member of the glutamate receptor antibody family, and its association with specific brain functional regions has gradually attracted clinical and research attention in recent years. Anti-GluK2 antibodies are classically associated with acute cerebellitis presenting with cerebellar ataxia and it is frequently considered a pathogenic antibody in encephalitis or cerebellitis of unknown etiology [7]. But it is noteworthy that anti‑GluK2 seropositivity does not invariably equate to cerebellar ataxia, nor does the presence of cerebellar ataxia necessarily imply anti‑GluK2 positivity. A recent study found that all patients with ataxia in anti‑NMDAR encephalitis tested negative for anti‑GluK2 antibodies, further confirming the non‑obligatory relationship between this antibody and cerebellar symptoms [8].

This patient lacks typical manifestations of cerebellar involvement, while atypical symptoms such as nervousness and palpitations are more prominent. This may be related to anti-GluK2 antibody affecting neural pathways in the basal ganglia and cerebral cortex [9]. Studies by Knight et al. have shown that there is potential mechanistic overlap between anti-GluK2 antibody and human neuropsychiatric disorders [10], providing theoretical clues for explaining antibody-mediated non-motor symptoms. However, animal models cannot fully replicate the complex functions of the human brain, and the correlation with clinical manifestations remains speculative [11]. The absence of typical symptoms, the presence of autonomic dysfunction, and the manifestation of paroxysmal anxiety and other emotional issues in this patient precisely validate this inference [12].

The previous case reports of anti-GluK2 antibody positive AE have been documented in Table 1. The first case in Asia involved an adolescent male presenting classic acute cerebellitis [13], with typical symptoms including lethargy, cerebellar ataxia, headache, and vomiting during the disease course [12], revealing the association between GluK2 antibodies and cerebellar injury. The second case reported by Zheng Ping Cheng et al. described an anti-GluK2 antibody positive AE patient with multiple myeloma, primarily manifesting as extrapyramidal symptoms and hysterical seizures, accompanied by psychiatric symptoms. This was the first report showing anti-GluK2 positive AE with atypical manifestations [14], suggesting that anti-GluK2 antibody may be associated with the “cortical-basal ganglia-cerebellar network” pathway. The landmark cohort study by Landa et al. systematically characterized eight patients with anti-GluK2 antibodies, establishing GluK2 as a pathogenic target and further confirming its strong association with acute cerebellitis, which ranged from mild ataxia to life-threatening obstructive hydrocephalus [15]. This cohort also expanded the recognized clinical spectrum to include opsoclonus-myoclonus, encephalopathy, and occasional mild temporal lobe involvement, providing a crucial reference for understanding both typical and atypical presentations.

These reports collectively illustrate that the clinical phenotype of anti-GluK2 associated encephalitis differs markedly depending on whether the antibody occurs alone or with other autoantibodies. Pure anti-GluK2 antibody-positive cases typically present as acute cerebellitis with cerebellar ataxia, predominantly in young males, and often show cerebellar MRI abnormalities. In contrast, cases with anti-GluK2 antibodies plus other autoantibodies usually exhibit clinical phenotypes driven by the accompanying antibodies, occur more frequently in females, and may be associated with tumors. Our patient belongs to the latter group, with the core limbic-autonomic syndrome attributable to the coexisting anti-LGI1 antibody. Unlike pure anti-GluK2 cases, she had no cerebellar ataxia. This comparison reinforces that in double-positive patients, the dominant antibody dictates the clinical phenotype, and further expands the spectrum of concomitant antibodies to include anti-LGI1.

Currently, there is no robust evidence confirming that anti-GluK2 antibody positive AE causes involuntary movements and psychiatric symptoms. However, a review of recent literature has revealed key experimental evidence related to anti-GluK2 antibody and striatal function. For instance, Chergui et al. found that kainate receptors play multiple roles in regulating striatal activity and promoting striatal circuit development [16], which is highly consistent with previous animal studies showing high expression of anti-GluK2 antibody in striatal neurons and its role in regulating motor function [10].

Multiple studies have shown that early diagnosis and standardized treatment for AE are crucial for improving prognosis. Currently, first-line treatment regimens recommended by domestic and international guidelines explicitly include glucocorticoids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and plasma exchange (PLEX)/immunoadsorption (IA). All patients should initiate first-line immunotherapy as early as possible after completing diagnosis and differential diagnosis [14, 17]. For patients with poor response to first-line treatment, severe cases, or those requiring continuous treatment, second-line treatments such as rituximab, cyclophosphamide for intravenous injection, or mycophenolate mofetil are often used sequentially, overlapping with hormone therapy for 3–6 months to achieve long-term disease control and prevent recurrence [14, 18, 19].

Although most patients experience significant improvement after first-line and second-line treatments, a small number of them still show poor response. Refractory AE is specifically defined as cases with no significant improvement after 2 weeks of first-line treatment. In recent years, several emerging therapeutic approaches have emerged for such patients. These include interleukin-6 (IL-6) receptor-targeted drugs represented by tocilizumab [20], which act on key factors in the inflammatory response [21]; the plasma cell depleting agent bortezomib, which can inhibit the production of persistent pathogenic antibodies [22]. In addition, novel monoclonal antibodies such as daratumumab and inebilizumab, targeting CD38 and CD19 respectively, have expanded the scope of targeted therapy. However, they have only been reported for use in 1 case with refractory anti-NMDAR antibody positive AE and 1 case of severe refractory anti-CASPR2 antibody positive AE to date [23, 24]. Furthermore, intrathecal injection of methotrexate is also being explored, though its neurotoxic side effects cannot be ignored [25–27]. Nevertheless, the clinical evidence for these therapeutic approaches mostly remains at the level of case reports or small-sample studies, and the efficacy and safety of their widespread application still require further verification.

Interpreting the clinical significance of multiple autoantibodies with discordant serum and CSF results remains challenging. In this case, both anti-LGI1 and anti-GluK2 antibodies were detected in serum but not in CSF. This pattern raises several possibilities: first, false positivity is a recognized limitation of commercial fixed cell-based assays (CBA), particularly at low titers; second, the core diagnosis may be anti-LGI1 antibody-mediated encephalitis (supported by its higher initial titer and persistence on follow-up), with the anti-GluK2 antibody (initial titer 1:10, negative on follow-up) representing a secondary or epiphenomenal immune response; third, this could represent a rare form of dual-antibody-associated autoimmune encephalitis with undetectable CSF antibodies. While all three remain plausible, the dynamic antibody changes and clinical phenotype lead us to favor the second interpretation. Future studies with larger cohorts and long-term follow-up are needed to better understand the clinical significance of such antibody combinations and to refine diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for similar rare cases.

Conclusions

This report represents the first documented case in Asia of AE with coexistence of anti-GluK2 and anti-LGI1 antibodies. Presenting predominantly with atypical autonomic symptoms, the patient underwent a series of misdiagnoses, underscoring the complexity of AE’s clinical phenotypes and diagnostic challenges, while enriching the spectrum of clinical manifestations of AE. Emphasis should be placed on the combined application of cranial imaging and antibody detection. Early standardized immunotherapy remains effective even for such rare cases. In the future, we need to conduct in-depth research regarding the pathogenic mechanisms of rare antibody combinations, clarifying their associations with clinical phenotypes and verifying the efficacy of emerging therapies in such cases.
