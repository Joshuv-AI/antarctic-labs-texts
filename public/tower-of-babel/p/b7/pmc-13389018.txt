Swallowing dynamics of burdock treated with a chelating agent in patients with dysphagia
Authors: Kazuya Maekawa, Takanori Tsujimura, Anna Sasa, Yuto Ochiai, Haruka Sakai, Taku Suzuki, Jin Magara, Ryuichi Inoue, Kana Umetani, Makoto Inoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFood modification is a common approach for patients with dysphagia. Treating vegetables with a chelating agent adjusts the hardness of food without altering its visual appearance. The current study aimed to evaluate the swallowing dynamics of burdock treated with a chelating agent (Chelate) compared with texture-modified soft burdock (SoB) and hard burdock (HaB) products in patients with dysphagia. Additionally, we investigated saliva secretion and physical properties of foods after chewing in healthy participants. We examined a total of 141 patients and 10 healthy participants. Swallowing function of patients was evaluated using videoendoscopy. In patients, Chelate left less pharyngeal residue compared with SoB and HaB, whereas the initiation and movement of swallowing, swallowing safety, and oral residue did not differ among the three foods. In healthy participants, the stimulated saliva volume during chewing for Chelate was higher than that for SoB, and was equivalent to that for HaB. The bolus properties of Chelate after chewing exhibited higher water content than other foods. Chelate had lower cohesiveness than SoB and tended to be softer than HaB. We speculate that Chelate shows better efficacy of swallowing in patients with dysphagia because of saliva secretion and physical properties of the food bolus after chewing.Keywords: Dysphagia, Chelating agent, Texture-modified food, Pharyngeal residue, Water contentSubject terms: Diseases, Gastroenterology, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Swallowing is defined by the World Health Organization as functions of clearing substances, such as food, drink, and saliva through the oral cavity, pharynx, and oesophagus into the stomach at an appropriate rate and speed1. Swallowing impairment is known as dysphagia, and can lead to aspiration pneumonia, which is the main cause of mortality in patients with neurodegenerative diseases2 and increases the risk of long hospital stay and adverse prognosis in stroke patients3. Dysphagia is also a cause of asphyxiation4, malnutrition5, dehydration6 and psychological problems related to quality of life7. Thus, there is a need for effective clinical intervention for patients with dysphagia.

Food modification is a common approach for enabling patients with dysphagia to eat safely, including chopped, minced, or boiled foods for a considerable period of time. High-pressure processing, high-hydrodynamic pressure processing, pulsed electric field treatment, plasma processing, ultrasound-assisted processing, and irradiation have been used to modify the texture of foods to make them more appropriate for these patients8. Various dietary guidelines for dysphagia have been developed worldwide, including the International Dysphagia Diet Standardisation Initiative, the National Dysphagia Dietary Guidelines, and the Japanese Dysphagia Diet 2021 (JDD2021). Clinical application of these guidelines is recommended9. However, texture-modified foods for patients with dysphagia have been reported to have reduced appeal in terms of visual appearance, taste, flavor and texture, which can lead to poor appetite and reduction of intake10. Among these characteristics, the appearance of food is likely to have a substantial impact on appetite, and a previous study reported that certain changes in the visual appearance of foods can improve food intake and satisfaction in older adults11. Thus, changing the physical properties of food without affecting its visual appearance may provide a useful approach for maintaining appetite among patients with dysphagia who require a texture-modified diet.

Root crops, such as burdock root, lotus rhizome, and bamboo shoot are relatively hard foods, and are difficult to soften even with prolonged boiling or high-pressure processing12. Chelating agents can change the cell wall structure of root vegetables, enabling the adjustment of the hardness of these foods without altering their visual appearance. Burdock is a commonly eaten vegetable in many parts of the world. Burdock is rich in water-soluble dietary fiber, including inulin and pectin, which resist digestion and absorption in the stomach. Many researchers have reported the benefits of burdock consumption for general health, including anti-inflammation13, anti-oxidation14, decreased body weight15, and decreased total cholesterol16. However, the difficulty of modifying the texture of burdock prevents patients with dysphagia from eating it.

The present study aimed to evaluate the swallowing dynamics of burdock treated with a chelating agent (Chelate) compared with commercially available texture-modified burdock in patients with dysphagia. In addition, we investigated saliva secretion and bolus physical properties of three test foods after chewing in healthy participants to elucidate the swallowing dynamics of Chelate.

Results

Physical properties of the test foods

Besides Chelate, we selected commercially available texture-modified soft burdock (SoB; Nihon Caremeal Co., Ltd., Hyogo, Japan) and hard burdock (HaB, Fukunao Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) products as test foods, both of which serve for patients with dysphagia (Fig. 1A). The hardness of Chelate, SoB and HaB was 4396 ± 2187, 5064 ± 947 and 14,257 ± 3574 N/m2, respectively; cohesiveness was 0.306 ± 0.053, 0.380 ± 0.050 and 0.408 ± 0.084, respectively; adhesiveness was 324 ± 155, 512 ± 153 and 713 ± 283 J/m3, respectively; and water content was 95.5 ± 1.5, 68.7 ± 2.9, 71.3 ± 0.8%, respectively (Fig. 1B). The hardness of Chelate was equivalent to that of SoB and significantly less than that of HaB (n = 10, p < 0.001). The cohesiveness of Chelate was significantly less than that of HaB (n = 10, p < 0.001) but not SoB. The adhesiveness of Chelate was significantly less than that of HaB (n = 10, p < 0.01) but not SoB. The water content of Chelate was significantly larger than that of SoB and HaB (n = 5, p < 0.001 for both). These results indicate that the physical properties of the Chelate exhibited a soft texture with low cohesiveness, low adhesiveness, and high water content compared with other foods.

Among them, five patients aspirated either Chelate or SoB during VE; therefore, we did not conduct further trials in these patients for safety reasons. Another five patients were excluded from the study for the following reasons: changing posture during evaluation, difficulty propelling the food bolus into the pharynx, and rumination before swallowing. Next, we compared each parameter among test foods (Fig. 2). The chewing duration for Chelate was equivalent to SoB and significantly shorter than that for HaB (n = 99, p < 0.001). The total number of swallows for Chelate was significantly lower than that for HaB (n = 141, p < 0.05). The position of the bolus head at the onset of first swallow (n = 141), whiteout duration (n = 141), penetration-aspiration scale (PAS) score (n = 140), and oral residue (n = 141) did not significantly differ among the three foods. Chelate left significantly less epiglottic vallecular residue than SoB and HaB (n = 137, p = 0.033 for SoB and p < 0.001 for HaB). Additionally, Chelate left significantly less pyriform sinus residue than SoB and HaB (n = 118, p = 0.002 for SoB and p = 0.005 for HaB). These data indicate that although Chelate had comparable values to those of HaB and SoB for the initiation of swallowing, swallowing movement, swallowing safety, and oral residue, Chelate left less pharyngeal residues than the other foods. Among the parameters, PAS scores tended to be low for Chelate compared with HaB (p = 0.105). The number of patients who showed lower PAS scores for Chelate compared with HaB was 24/140 (17.1%). Among them, the cause of disease was stroke for five patients, pulmonary disease for five patients, digestive disease for two patients, cardiovascular disease for two patients, head and neck cancer for two patients, and other causes for eight patients. Regarding oral condition, 10/24 (41.7%) and 16/24 (66.7%) patients were classified as A on the EI and modified EI, respectively. In the swallowing screening test, 11/24 (45.8%) and 10/23 (43.5%) patients were positive for RSST and MWST, respectively. As a result, this population is similar to the whole one, and we did not detect any specific background of these patients. We further compared the pharyngeal residues among three foods in each reclining angle. Chelate tended to leave less pharyngeal residues than other foods in all reclining angles. Although we observed some statistical differences of pharyngeal residues among foods in only 60° angle groups, it might be due to the large sample sizes for 60° angle (Epiglottic vallecula: p < 0.05 for Chelate vs. HaB in 60°; n = 11, 42, 48 and 36 for 30°, 45°, 60° and 90°, respectively; Pyriform sinus: p < 0.05 for Chelate vs. SoB in 60°; n = 9, 34, 43 and 32 for 30°, 45°, 60° and 90°, respectively). Given the potential influence of gravity on bolus transit and pharyngeal residue, we additionally performed a linear mixed-effects analysis including reclining angle as a covariate. For chewing duration, both food and reclining angle showed significant effects (p < 0.001 for food and p = 0.019 for angle), indicating that body position influences chewing behavior. In contrast, for pharyngeal residue, food showed a significant effect, whereas reclining angle did not reach statistical significance (Epiglottic vallecula: p = 0.003 for food and p = 0.068 for angle; Pyriform sinus: p < 0.001 for food and p = 0.761 for angle). These findings suggest that pharyngeal residue is less influenced by body position.

Saliva secretion and bolus properties after chewing in healthy participants

Because swallowing itself (initiation and movement) did not differ among the three foods, we speculate that saliva secretion and food physical properties after chewing are important factors in the difference in pharyngeal residue between Chelate and other foods. However, these evaluations were difficult to conduct in patients with dysphagia in the present study because of safety risks and physical burden. Instead, we examined this issue further in experiments with healthy participants. As with clinical data, the chewing duration for Chelate was equivalent to that for SoB and significantly shorter than that for HaB (Fig. 3A, n = 10, p < 0.01). The unstimulated saliva volume calculated for chewing duration was 0.081 ± 0.084, 0.080 ± 0.098, and 0.117 ± 0.146 g for Chelate, SoB, and HaB, respectively. The stimulated saliva volume during chewing was 0.945 ± 0.629, 0.568 ± 0.327, and 0.897 ± 0.590 g for Chelate, SoB, and HaB, respectively. The stimulated saliva volume for Chelate was significantly higher than that for SoB (Fig. 3B, n = 10, p < 0.05) and was equivalent to that for HaB. Finally, we evaluated the bolus properties after chewing (Fig. 4). The hardness for Chelate, SoB, and HaB was 533 ± 232, 428 ± 197, and 804 ± 557 N/m2, respectively; cohesiveness was 0.516 ± 0.097, 0.605 ± 0.032, and 0.541 ± 0.048, respectively; adhesiveness was 91 ± 40, 78 ± 33, and 116 ± 59 J/m3, respectively; and water content was 98.5 ± 1.3, 75.9 ± 3.4, and 81.3 ± 3.9%, respectively. Regarding hardness, Chelate was equivalent to SoB and tended to be smaller than HaB (n = 10, p = 0.118). Regarding cohesiveness, Chelate was significantly lower than SoB (n = 10, p < 0.05) but not HaB. Regarding adhesiveness, there was no significant difference among the three foods. Similar to the initial food properties, the water content of Chelate was significantly higher than that of SoB and HaB (n = 10, p < 0.001 for both). These data suggest that the bolus characteristics of Chelate after chewing exhibited higher water content than HaB and SoB, lower cohesiveness than SoB, and slightly softer texture than HaB.

Discussion

In the present study, we compared swallowing dynamics among three types of texture-modified burdock root in patients with dysphagia. Chelate resulted in less residue in the epiglottic vallecula and pyriform sinus compared with SoB and HaB. The chewing duration for Chelate was equivalent to SoB and significantly shorter than HaB. In healthy participants, the stimulated saliva volume during chewing for Chelate was higher than that for SoB and was equivalent to that for HaB. Regarding physical properties, Chelate had higher water content than HaB and SoB in both the before- and after-chewing conditions. Chelate after chewing had lower cohesiveness than SoB and tended to be softer than HaB. These results suggest that Chelate was associated with better swallowing efficacy in patients with dysphagia because of facilitated saliva secretion and the physical properties of the food bolus after chewing.

Burdock is known as a tough food, and changing its hardness is difficult. Freeze-thawing is a method used for changing physical properties of food by altering the internal structure of starch granules. Nakatsu et al. investigated the mechanical properties of burdock. The hardness of burdock following 10 min of boiling before and after freeze-thawing was approximately 1000 × 104 and 250 × 104 N/m2, respectively, indicating that hardness was reduced by only one-fourth after freeze-thawing12. In the current study, we treated burdock with a chelating agent followed by blanching, boiling, and heating by microwave. The hardness after chelating treatment was approximately 5 × 103 N/m2 (Fig. 1B), which is one-two thousandth of the reported hardness of burdock after 10 min of boiling. The consumer affairs agency in Japan developed permission criteria regarding foods for patients with dysphagia17. These criteria categorize foods into three classes on the basis of their hardness, cohesiveness, and adhesiveness. Chelate is classified into class Ⅱ, which has defined physical properties of 1 × 103–1.5 × 104 N/m2 for hardness, < 1 × 103 J/m3 for adhesiveness, and 0.2–0.9 for cohesiveness. This class is described as foods that can be swallowed after being squeezed a little in the mouth. SoB was also classified into class Ⅱ. HaB had a slightly greater hardness than the criteria of class Ⅱ and was classified into class Ⅲ, which is described as the food that can be swallowed after being chewed a little. The chelating method in this study changed the physical properties of burdock sufficiently to be suitable for patients with dysphagia without changing the visual appearance of the food. The mechanism underlying the change in physical properties caused by a chelating agent can be explained as follows. The textural characteristics of vegetables fundamentally depend on their cell wall structure. The cell walls of most vegetables are rich in pectin, which is a family of complex polysaccharides that can be categorized into four main groups: homogalacturonan (HG), rhamnogalacturonan-I (RG-I), rhamnogalacturonan-II, and xylogalacturonan18. HG and RG-I are the major components, contributing approximately 60% and 20–30% of total pectin, respectively. Pectin with a degree of methyl-esterification value exceeding 50% is classified as high methoxyl pectin and pectin with a degree of methyl-esterification value below 50% is classified as low methoxyl (LM) pectin19. LM pectin can form a gel by ionic linkages via calcium bridges between dissociated carboxyl groups20. After treatment with a chelating agent, the gel structure of LM pectin is destroyed by taking away Ca2+ ions. This makes it possible to soften foods with LM pectin21.

In the present study, we evaluated swallowing dynamics in patients with dysphagia. The patients were hospitalized in an acute hospital, and approximately half suffered from stroke, digestive disorders, or cardiovascular diseases. Additionally, more than half of the patients did not regularly eat foods before the evaluation, and over one-third of the patients were considered to be at risk of aspiration on the basis of screening tests. Regarding oral condition, approximately 60% of patients missed the partial or total contact of the posterior region, and over one-third of the patients missed it even while wearing dentures. These results indicate that some patients had a risk of aspiration and poor dental condition, suggesting that low masticatory function might increase the risk of aspiration for solid foods in these patients.

Most of the parameters of swallowing dynamics did not differ among the three foods. In clinical treatment of dysphagia, assessing the safety and efficacy of swallowing is critical22. Swallowing safety reflects airway protective function and is related to the risk of pneumonia and mortality23. The safety of swallowing was evaluated using PAS scores in the present study. PAS scores did not differ among the foods. However, the PAS score for Chelate tended to be lower than that for HaB (Fig. 2). Although we did not detect any specific background of patients who showed safer swallowing for Chelate than HaB, Chelate may be a safer food than HaB for some patients with dysphagia. Efficacy of swallowing reflects the ability of the pharyngeal passage of the food bolus during swallowing and is related to malnutrition and/or dehydration24. Efficacy was evaluated using Yale Pharyngeal Residue Severity Rating Scale in the present study. Interestingly, Chelate left less pharyngeal residue after the first swallow than SoB and HaB. This is likely to be related to the smaller total number of swallows for Chelate compared with HaB. Rofes et al. assessed the pathophysiology of oropharyngeal dysphagia in frail elderly patients25. The results indicated that impaired efficacy was related to weak tongue bolus propulsion forces and slow hyoid motion25. Although we did not evaluate those biomechanical parameters, the whiteout duration for Chelate was similar to that for SoB and HaB. An additional analysis for reclining angle did not reveal an obvious distinction across various reclining angles. Body position significantly influenced chewing duration, suggesting that oral processing is sensitive to postural changes. In contrast, reclining angle did not significantly affect either epiglottic vallecular or pyriform sinus residues, indicating that pharyngeal residue is less dependent on gravitational effects. The bolus head position at onset also did not differ among the foods. With these results and the fact that we evaluated the three foods under the same conditions, such as material weight and body posture, we speculate that swallowing initiation and movement did not differ among foods. Because we predicted that saliva secretion and the physical properties of food after chewing would result in less pharyngeal residue for Chelate, we further evaluated these factors in healthy participants. Mastication facilitates saliva secretion, which is called the masticatory-salivary reflex26. Because foods with a harder texture require a longer duration of chewing27, we hypothesized that the hard food would lead to greater saliva secretion by the masticatory-salivary reflex. HaB, which was the hardest among the three foods, was associated with longer chewing duration than SoB and Chelate (Fig. 2) and greater stimulated saliva volume than SoB (Fig. 3). However, the stimulated saliva volume for HaB was equivalent to that for Chelate. Tastants also facilitate saliva secretion via the gustatory–salivary reflex28. In the present study, Chelate was treated with a chelating agent and soy sauce, which have a slightly sour taste and a salty taste, respectively. These tastants might have facilitated saliva secretion during chewing.

Finally, we evaluated food bolus properties after chewing in healthy participants. The Chelate bolus tended to be less hard than the HaB bolus. Because a large number of patients in this study had poor dental condition, the difference in hardness between Chelate and HaB in these patients might be greater than that in healthy participants and might have affected pharyngeal residue. Although Park et al. reported that adhesiveness is related to swallowing difficulty and sense of residue in older adults using semi-solid foods29, adhesiveness did not differ among the three foods in the current study. On the other hand, the cohesiveness of the Chelate bolus was lower than that of the SoB bolus. Momosaki et al. reported that cohesiveness is an important factor for pharyngeal residue after liquid swallowing in patients with dysphagia30. In contrast, the present study evaluated chewed bolus, which differs in structure and rheological properties from liquids. Therefore, the applicability of findings from liquid-based studies to chewed bolus is limited, and this should be considered when interpreting the present results. Additionally, the water content after chewing was significantly higher for Chelate than SoB and HaB. In accordance with the findings of a previous study31, the food with higher initial water content had a higher water content after chewing. It has been reported that the water content affects the chewing activity and swallowing threshold31,32. Lubrication and flowability are important factors for ease and safety of swallowing33. Water content might affect the characteristics of foods after chewing. However, the higher water content observed in the Chelate bolus after chewing may largely reflect the high initial moisture content of the sample itself, rather than increased saliva incorporation.

The current study involved several limitations. First, we were unable to evaluate the physical properties of the test foods after chewing in patients with dysphagia. Although the reduction in pharyngeal residue for Chelate in patients can be discussed on the basis of results in healthy participants, significant differences in oral condition, chewing, and swallowing functions may exist between patients and healthy individuals. In addition, the increased stimulated saliva volume observed in healthy subjects cannot be directly extrapolated to patients with dysphagia, as differences in salivary function and oral motor control may influence the response. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Second, the taste differed among the three test foods, although all foods were slightly salty. This may have affected saliva secretion during chewing and subsequent swallowing. In particular, the Chelate sample was immersed in light soy sauce with glycine, which may have introduced stronger salty and umami/sweet taste stimuli compared with SoB and HaB. Because tastants trigger the gustatory–salivary reflex, the increase in saliva secretion observed with the chelate-treated food may be influenced not only by its physical properties but also by taste stimulation. Because these factors were not independently controlled in the present study, their relative contributions cannot be determined. It is therefore likely that the observed effects reflect a combined influence of both taste and texture. From a clinical perspective, distinguishing between these mechanisms is important. Third, the HaB condition was always conducted last in patients for safety reasons. However, this fixed order may have introduced an order effect, such as fatigue and adaptation, which could have influenced the outcomes. Therefore, the results related to the HaB condition should be interpreted with caution. Fourth, although we showed that the chewing duration for Chelate was shorter than that for HaB, the absence of chewing cycle data does not allow us to distinguish whether this effect is attributable to fewer chewing cycles, a faster chewing rhythm, or a combination of both. Furthermore, masticatory muscle activity (e.g., electromyography) would provide valuable insight into masticatory load. Although such measurements were not included in the present study, they represent an important direction for future research. Fifth, we did not evaluate the nutritional value of Chelate. Chelated burdock is likely to maintain some hardness in the fibrous parts, but the other tissues may have largely disappeared. This possibility is suggested by the finding that the moisture content of normal burdock is approximately 80%, whereas the chelating treatment increased it to 95% in this study. Because nutritional value is important for patients with dysphagia, the nutritional value of Chelate should be evaluated in future studies. Sixth, we did not investigate oral sensation for Chelate. A previous study described the importance of the oral sensation of texture-modified food in patients with dysphagia34. In future studies, it will be important to evaluate the oral sensation, including tactile and taste ones, to develop better food products for these patients.

In conclusion, we speculate that Chelate showed better efficacy of swallowing in patients with dysphagia because of facilitated saliva secretion and the physical properties of the food bolus after chewing. Treating food with a chelating agent may provide an effective approach for developing new food products for patients with dysphagia.

Methods

Clinical evaluation of patients with dysphagia

Participants

This prospective study employed data collected from September 2023 to May 2025. Data were obtained from patients with dysphagia who underwent VE at the Unit of Dysphagia Rehabilitation of the Niigata University Medical & Dental Hospital. The inclusion criteria were used to select patients who were able to swallow thickened water, jelly, and paste foods safely during VE. The exclusion criteria were (1) patients with allergies to the test food materials (2) patients who were unable to eat test food materials because of their preferences (3) patients who had a need to change their posture while eating different foods (4) patients who aspirated either Chelate or SoB. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Niigata University (2023–0008). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or authorized family members. The study was performed in accordance with Ethical Principles of the Declaration of Helsinki for Medical Research Involving Human Participants (2024).

General, oral, and swallowing screening characteristics of patients

Characteristics and data on age, sex, primary disease possibly causing dysphagia, and FILS score were collected from medical records. Primary diseases were classified into six categories: cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease (stroke), digestive disease, head and neck cancer, pulmonary disease, and others. The FILS was an observer-rating scale to assess the severity of dysphagia35. Levels 1–3 relate to varying degrees of non-oral feeding; Levels 4–6 relate to varying degrees of oral food intake and alternative nutrition; Levels 7–9 relate to varying degrees of oral food intake alone; and Level 10 relates to normal oral food intake. Dentition status was assessed using the EI and modified EI36, which were determined on the basis of the contact points of natural teeth with (as modified EI) and without (as EI) removable dentures between upper and lower premolar and molar regions. Class A: contact in four supporting zones; Class B: contact in one to three supporting zones or contact in the anterior region only; Class C: absence of occlusal contact. Swallowing screening tests, including the RSST and MWST, were performed to evaluate swallowing function before VE. In the RSST, each patient was instructed to swallow saliva as frequently as possible within a 30-sec interval37. A value of ≤ 2 suspects aspiration. In the MWST, each patient was instructed to swallow 3 mL of water administered into the floor of the mouth38. The assessment criteria were as follows: 1, no swallowing occur; 2, breathing changes after swallowing without cough; 3, cough or wet hoarseness after swallowing; 4, swallow successfully; 5, swallow successfully with additional two dry swallows within 30 sec. A score of ≤ 3 suspects aspiration.

Test foods

We used the following three test foods in this study: Chelate, SoB, and HaB. Chelate was processed as follows: 1. cutting the burdock (thickness: 5 mm, long diameter: < 3 mm, weight; approximately 1.5 g); 2. blanching with boiling water (100 °C) for 5 min; 3. immersion in the solution with a chelating agent (0.45% NEW F.C. RINSAN-F, FC Chemical Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan); 4. heating with hot water (90 °C) for 20 min; 5. after cooling, storing in the refrigerator for at least 1 day; 6. before eating, immersion in light soy sauce with glycine and heating in the microwave (500 W, 20sec); 7. serving to participants to eat 15 min later. The texture of each food was assessed using a modified two-bite test using the RE2-33005 s creep meter (Yamaden Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Food samples were positioned on a plate (15 mm diameter, 10 mm height) and moved towards a plunger (8 mm diameter, 22 mm height) at a speed of 10 mm/sec. Because the amount of chewed bolus obtained after expectoration was limited, a small plunger (8 mm diameter) was used to match the sample size. The plunger was connected to a load cell that compressed the sample twice (compressibility 66.7%). Subsequently, we calculated the values for hardness (the height of the first peak in the stress–strain curve), adhesiveness (the area of the first negative peak, representing the work required to pull the plunger away from the food sample) and cohesiveness (the ratio of the area under the second compressive peak to the area under the first compressive peak). Data were analyzed using the Creep Analysis software package version 2.0 (Yamaden Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The water content was measured by heating test foods at 140 °C until a drying rate of less than 0.2%/min was achieved using a moisture analyzer (ML-50, A&D Company Limited). These analyses were performed according to previously reported methods32.

Recordings and analysis of VE images

In patients, a fiberoptic endoscope (FNL-10RBS; PENTAX, Tokyo, Japan) or a digital fiberoptic endoscope (VNL8-J10; PENTAX, Tokyo, Japan) was inserted through the nasal passage and into the midpharynx. The posture was determined on the basis of the patient’s general condition and swallowing function. After confirmation of safe swallowing for 3 ml of thickened liquid and a teaspoon of jelly (JDD 2021 code 0j), three test foods were evaluated in the following order: Chelate then SoB, or SoB then Chelate, followed by HaB. The order was randomly assigned to patients. If a patient aspirated Chelate or SoB, we stopped further evaluation for safety reasons. The patients ate each food freely. The following eight parameters were evaluated: chewing duration, position of the bolus head at the onset of first swallow, total number of swallows, whiteout duration, PAS score, oral residue, epiglottic vallecular residue, and pyriform sinus residue. Chewing duration was defined as the time interval between the voice by clinical staff to inform the start of the chewing movement by visual inspection and the onset of whiteout by VE images. Because we began measuring chewing duration midway through the research, the sample size for chewing duration was smaller than that for the other parameters. The position of the bolus head was scored as follows; (1) above the epiglottic vallecula, (2) epiglottic vallecula, (3) between the epiglottic vallecula and pyriform sinus, (4) pyriform sinus. Whiteout duration was defined as the time interval between the onset and offset of whiteout during the first swallow. The PAS is a validated 8-point ordinal scale that quantifies episodes of airway invasion, evaluating the level of bolus penetration into the airway, the existence of residual bolus in the airway post-swallow, and cough response after laryngeal penetration or aspiration event39. Oral residue was assessed for its presence or absence by visual inspection performed by clinical staff. The residue at the epiglottic vallecula and pyriform sinus was scored using the Yale Pharyngeal Residue Severity Rating Scale as follows: (1) none, (2) trace, (3) mild, (4) moderate, (5) severe22.

Food physical properties and saliva secretion by chewing in healthy participants

Participants

Healthy participants were recruited through posters displayed within the university. All participants who volunteered and were enrolled in the study were staff members of our department. Although all participants were involved in the clinical management of patients with dysphagia, they were not informed of the results of the patient data at the time of participation. In addition, the study purpose was explained as an investigation of how the physical properties of Chelate and similar care foods change before and after intake. Therefore, participants were not informed of the specific study hypotheses related to chewing behavior, saliva secretion, or expected outcomes. Ten healthy participants (five men and five women, mean age ± standard deviation: 31.5 ± 4.9 years, range: 28–43 years) participated. A dentist verified that no participants had temporomandibular disorders, masticatory problems, or abnormalities in the number or position of their teeth. The participants did not report any coughing, swallowing, or speech problems, and none had a history of stroke, neuromuscular disorders, or respiratory disease. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Niigata University (2025–0004). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was performed in accordance with Ethical Principles of the Declaration of Helsinki for Medical Research Involving Human Participants (2024).

Data collection

All participants were instructed not to eat or drink for at least 1 h prior to each experiment.

Unstimulated saliva

A pre-weighed cotton roll was placed under the tongue for 30-sec while the participant kept their jaw and tongue as still as possible32. The cotton roll was reweighed, and the difference between the pre- and post-measurements was regarded as the unstimulated saliva volume.

Stimulated saliva and physical properties after chewing

Participants were asked to freely masticate and ingest the three test foods (Chelate, SoB, HaB; weight: approximately 1.5 g) in random order. The interval between trials was at least 1 min, and participants could rinse their mouths with distilled water between the trials whenever they wished. We measured chewing duration for each food from the onset of chewing to the first swallow by visual inspection. Next, participants were instructed to freely masticate each food during the recorded chewing duration and to spit it out in random order. These trials were repeated twice to collect enough volume for analysis of physical properties and water content. All participants completed another trial for each food on a different day to measure stimulated saliva volume. Saliva volume during chewing was calculated by subtracting the initial weight of the food from the weight of the spitting food bolus. The unstimulated saliva volume calculated for the chewing duration was subtracted from the saliva volume during chewing to determine the stimulated saliva volume. The physical properties and water content of the food bolus were assessed in accordance with the methods mentioned above.

Data analysis

One dentist (K.M) and one dental hygienist (H.S.) analyzed the VE images. These researchers were well trained, with > 7 years of clinical experience in dental practice and dysphagia rehabilitation. Inter-rater reliability was evaluated using the ICC. The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range). Tests for normality and equality of variances were initially performed for the statistical analysis to determine whether to use parametric or nonparametric tests. Parametric data were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or one-way repeated measures ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test. Non-parametric data were assessed using Friedman’s repeated measures ANOVA on ranks or the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Tukey’s test. In addition, we analyzed chewing duration and pharyngeal residue using a linear mixed-effects model. The dependent variable was outcome measure (chewing duration, epiglottic vallecular residue and pyriform sinus residue). Food (Chelate, SoB and HaB) was included as a fixed factor, and reclining angle (30°, 45°, 60°, and 90°) was included as a covariate. Subject was treated as a random effect to account for repeated measurements within individuals. Oral residue was compared among the foods using Cochran’s Q test. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.
