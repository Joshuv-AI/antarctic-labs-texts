Associations between kinesiophobia, pain, functional disability, balance confidence and transverse abdomen muscle activation in elderly individuals with chronic nonspecific low back pain: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ariela Torres Cruz, Priscila de Oliveira Januário, Mateus Dias Antunes, Ingred Merllin Batista de Souza, Mara Maria Lisboa Santana Pinheiro, Anice de Campos Pássaro, Elisa Frutos Bernal, Ana María Martín-Nogueras, Amelia Pasqual Marques
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic low back pain has a high prevalence in the elderly population. Psychosocial dimensions are possible causes of pain affecting the patient’s development and prognosis. Kinesiophobia is associated with a natural response to chronic low back pain and should be considered a key treatment target for physical therapists. The aim of this study was to investigate the association between kinesiophobia and pain intensity, functional disability, balance confidence and transversus abdominis muscle activation in elderly individuals with chronic nonspecific low back pain.MethodsEighty elderly people with chronic nonspecific low back pain participated in this study. Kinesiophobia was assessed using the Tampa Kinesiophobia Scale, pain intensity was measured using the Numerical Pain Rating Scale, functional disability was measured using the Oswestry Disability Index, balance confidence was measured using the Activities-Specific Balance Confidence Scale, and transversus abdominis muscle activation was measured using the pressure biofeedback unit. The data were analyzed in the SPSS Statistics program using Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlation coefficients.ResultsThe results show a correlation between kinesiophobia, pain intensity and functional disability, being negative for confidence in balance. The correlation between kinesiophobia and activation of the transversus abdominis muscle was not significant.ConclusionThe results suggest a positive correlation between kinesiophobia, pain intensity and functional disability and a negative correlation with balance confidence.

Background

Population aging is a response to changes in some health indicators, especially falling birth and mortality rates and increasing life expectancy. Brazil, a developing country, has 21.113.490 people aged 60 or over (15.6%), an increase of 56% compared to 2010 when it was 20.590.597 (10,8%) [1].

Chronic low back pain is very common in older adults, with prevalence estimates ranging from 10% to 20% in people aged 65 and over worldwide [2]. A systematic review indicates a prevalence of 21.7% to 75% of low back pain in the elderly, with 33.6% to 68.3% in Brazil, and also that low back pain impairs functionality and results in great dependence on carrying out activities of daily living and physical capacity [3].

Low back pain can be defined as any pain between the last ribs and the lower gluteal folds, with chronic pain being defined as pain on most days for at least three months [4].

In a systematic review carried out by Wong et al. [5] it was possible to observe that obesity, anxiety, depression and generalized pain were associated with a higher prevalence of chronic nonspecific low back pain in the elderly. Psychosocial dimensions are relevant and can be considered possible causes of pain affecting the patient’s development and prognosis [6]. Dysfunction in patients with chronic low back pain is highly associated with fear of pain and fear of movement [7].

The activity avoidance model suggests that when there is fear of pain, it leads to a conditioned response of increased fear, anxiety, and muscle tension [8]. Fear of movement or kinesiophobia is associated with a natural response to chronic low back pain and should be considered a key treatment target for physical therapists [9]. Fear is an important component of disability in patients, given its impact on rehabilitation strategies [10, 11]. Avoidance behaviors are associated with high levels of pain and can aggravate the pain experience and, in the long term, can lead to disuse and disability [12]. Prolonged restriction of painful movements in the lumbar joints can result in reduced recruitment of the lumbar and abdominal muscles, contributing to reduced strength and motor control in individuals with low back pain [13].

Pain-related psychological risk factors, such as kinesiophobia, are accepted as major drivers of persistent low back pain in younger populations, however, these factors have not been comprehensively evaluated in the elderly population [14].

Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate kinesiophobia, pain, functional disability, balance confidence and activation of the transverse abdominis muscle in elderly individuals with chronic nonspecific low back pain and to verify whether there are associations between kinesiophobia and the variables analyzed.

Methods

This cross-sectional study involved 80 elderly individuals with chronic nonspecific low back pain and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine of the University of São Paulo (protocol 3,0.70.664). We contacted participants in person at 3 health units affiliated with the Unified Health System. All participants had spinal imaging to exclude associated diseases and agreed to a consent form prior to participation. Participants were assessed by a previously trained blind evaluator.

The inclusion criteria were: elderly people aged 60 years or older, non-specific low back pain for at least 3 months and pain intensity equal to or greater than 3. The exclusion criteria were: people with specific low back pain, neurological involvement, previous spinal surgery, recent surgeries, being in treatment or having undergone non-pharmacological treatment for low back pain for three months and not having the cognitive capacity to undergo the proposed assessments according to the mini mental state examination [15] according to the cutoff score proposed by Brucki et al. [16].

Kinesiophobia was assessed using the Tampa Kinesiophobia Scale, which consists of a self-administered questionnaire composed of 17 questions that address pain and symptom intensity. Scores range from 1 to 4, with the answer “I completely disagree” being worth 1 point, “I partially disagree” being worth 2 points, “I partially agree” being worth 3 points, and “I completely agree” being worth 4 points. To obtain the final total score, the scores for questions 4, 8, 12 and 16 must be reversed. The final score can be a minimum of 17 and a maximum of 68 points, and the higher the score, the greater the degree of kinesiophobia [17]. A score of 37 is considered a high degree of kinesiophobia [18].

Pain intensity was assessed using the Numerical Pain Scale [19]. It is a scale from 0 to 10, with 0 meaning total absence of pain and 10 the maximum level of pain reported by the patient [20].

Functional disability was assessed using the Oswestry disability index. The index is calculated by adding the total score (each section is worth from 0 to 5) and the total points are equal to the sum of the points of the 10 sections. The interpretation is performed by means of percentages: 0% to 20% minimal disability, 21% to 40% moderate disability, 41% to 60% severe disability, 61% to 80% disability, 81% to 100% patient bedridden or exaggerating symptoms [21].

To assess balance confidence, the Activities-Specific Balance Confidence Scale was used [22]. This scale consists of 16 items rated on a scale ranging from zero (representing no confidence) to 100 (representing complete confidence) [23]. The total score for this scale is obtained by adding the ratings (0–1,600) and dividing by 16. Confidence scores are: >80% indicates a high level of physical functioning; 50–80%, a moderate level of physical functioning; and < 50%, a low level of physical functioning [24]. Values ​​<67% in elderly people are predictive of future falls [25].

The activation of the transversus abdominis muscle was assessed using the pressure biofeedback unit. This instrument (Stabilizer Pressure Biofeedback - Chatanooga Group Australia) consists of a pressure transducer with three inflatable bags, a catheter and a sphygmomanometer that ranges from 0 to 200 mmHg graduated every 2 mmHg. The bag is made of inelastic material and measures 16.7 × 24 cm. If changes in position occur, these will result in changes in the bag pressure which will be recorded by the sphygmomanometer [26]. Participants were instructed on how to adequately contract the transverse abdominis muscle and empty the bladder before the assessment. During the test, participants remained in the prone position on a rigid surface, with the pressure biofeedback unit positioned immediately below the anterior superior iliac spines, over the umbilical scar. Before starting the contraction, the bag was inflated to a pressure of 70 mmHg with the valve closed. Participants were instructed to perform two inhalations and exhalations using the abdominal region. The bag was then re-inflated to 70 mmHg. The participant was asked to contract the lower abdominal region, without moving the spine and pelvis, and to hold it for 10 timed seconds. Participants were instructed to perform slow inhalations and exhalations, and the contraction was performed together with the exhalation. Three measurements were taken, with 30-second intervals between them, and the average was calculated, according to the methodology used by França [27].

Data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables are presented in numbers and percentages and continuous variables in mean and standard deviation. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The association between clinical variables and kinesiophobia was measured using Pearson’s correlation coefficient and Spearman’s correlation coefficient, depending on the normality of the variables. Several multiple regression models were used to analyze the association between kinesiophobia and the measured clinical variables (pain intensity, disability, balance confidence, transverse muscle activation). Multiple regression modeling was performed with kinesiophobia as the dependent variable and clinical variables as independent variables. Model 1 was unadjusted, model 2 was adjusted for gender (0 = male, 1 = female), age in years, income, and pain duration in years.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to evaluate kinesiophobia, pain, functional disability, balance confidence and activation of the transverse abdominis muscle in elderly individuals with chronic nonspecific low back pain and to verify whether there are associations between kinesiophobia and the measured variables. Participants in this research demonstrated statistically significant associations of kinesiophobia with pain intensity, functional disability and balance confidence, however, there was no association with contraction of the transverse abdominis muscle.

Participants in this study scored an average of 41.52 on the Tampa Kinesiophobia Scale, which is considered a moderate level in people with low back pain [28]. In addition, they presented other symptoms such as pain, functional incapacity and low confidence in balance to perform specific activities. Other studies have found similar results in other populations [29–31], suggesting that people with low back pain have a worse prognosis due to fear of movement [32]. Other studies have suggested that low back pain may lead to difficulty or inability to perform functional tasks in older adults, further reducing mobility and balance [33–35]. Maintaining mobility is essential for older adults, as its reduction is associated with higher rates of morbidity, mortality, and poorer quality of life [36].

In the elderly, part of the functional limitations can be attributed to the normal aging processes [37], however, kinesiophobia in elderly people with low back pain can further aggravate the impairment of global functional performance due to the avoidance of physical activity that leads to reduced mobility and persistent pain [38].

Fear of movement contributes to a reduction in a person’s functional progression, increasing disability and decreasing quality of life [39]. Felício et al. [40] compared the evolution of the intensity of low back pain in people with and without kinesiophobia over 12 months. They found that elderly kinesiophobic participants presented greater pain complaints during the 12-month period.

Comachio et al. [32]. observed that there was a correlation between kinesiophobia and pain intensity, disability and limitation of physical and emotional roles in quality of life in patients with chronic low back pain. However, the correlations between kinesiophobia and physical limitations and pain duration were not significant. Emotions are powerful, sometimes harmful and sometimes beneficial drivers of decision-making [41]. According to Marciniak et al. [42], it appears that fear of movement is directly correlated with the intensity of pain and the patient’s emotional state, however, these authors did not observe in their research a correlation between kinesiophobia and depression. People with lower incomes and fewer years of schooling have a worse perception of their health, and even with health systems designed to be equitable, inequities still persist [43].

A study carried out with people over 55 years of age and with low back pain showed that women with less education, sedentary lifestyle, depressive symptoms, greater number of comorbidities, worse functional mobility, other clinical signs and symptoms associated with complaints, history of falls, worse self-efficacy in falls and higher level of kinesiophobia, had worse progression of low back pain over the 12 months of longitudinal follow-up. It was also observed that those who did not show clinical recovery had a worse biopsychosocial health profile [44]. Anxiety, depression and coping strategies can modify the severity and chronicity of low back pain. Psychological distress is a risk factor for persistent or debilitating low back pain in older adults [45].

Deficits in the lumbar muscles contribute to persistent low back pain, impacting functional limitations and physical performance [46]. Among the elderly, progressive degeneration of the musculoskeletal system, combined with deconditioning associated with low back pain, contributes to loss of lumbar strength [37]. In this age group, marked kinesiophobia can intensify muscle dysfunction [13].

This study represents the population of elderly people with low back pain in Brazil. As this was a cross-sectional study, it was not possible to establish relationships between exposure factors and the development of symptoms, which can be considered a limitation. However, the results found may be useful in clinical practice so that health professionals can consider the association of psychosocial issues in elderly people with chronic nonspecific low back pain.

Conclusion

The participants in this study presented kinesiophobia and it was possible to observe a correlation between kinesiophobia, pain intensity, functional incapacity and confidence in balance to perform specific activities. These results should be taken into account during the evaluation and planning of rehabilitation of elderly individuals with chronic nonspecific low back pain.
