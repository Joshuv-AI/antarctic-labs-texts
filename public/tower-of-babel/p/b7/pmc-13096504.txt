Filtration characteristics of coconut fiber filter used in emergency rescue of dike piping
Authors: Di Feng, Feng Xiang Luo, Sheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
After the occurrence of backward erosion piping (BEP) in dikes, timely emergency rescue is crucial. Based on the coconut fiber filter, a quick and eco-friendly method for protecting against dike BEP is proposed. To investigate the filtration characteristics of coconut fiber filters during BEP rescue, model experiments were conducted. The impact of filter pore size on its filtration performance was explored. The distribution of clogging particles within the filter was observed using an electron microscope, and the filtration mechanism of the coconut fiber filter was revealed. The results show that in continuous graded sand, there was no significant clogging of the coconut fiber filter. By the end of the experiment, the flow rate of the 300 g/m² coconut fiber filter was 52.4% higher than that of nonwoven geotextiles. Compared to nonwoven geotextiles, the coconut fiber filter exhibited better filtration performance. When the pore size of the coconut fiber filter exceeded a certain threshold, the clogging degree in the filter increased rather than decreased. Physical clogging of the nonwoven geotextile system mainly occurred near the filter, while for the coconut fiber filter system, it mainly appeared within the sand layer 3–25 mm below the filter.

Introduction

Dike engineering is an important water-retaining structure that plays a crucial role in protecting the lives and properties along its path. Backward erosion piping (BEP) is one of the most common forms of internal erosion in dikes1,2, and it poses a significant threat to dike safety. BEP occurs when concentrated seepage flows towards a local disruption of the downstream top layer. The high local hydraulic gradients near the defect cause upward forces on the sand grains, which may initiate an erosion process at that location. It is vital to promptly implement emergency protection measures for BEP. Otherwise, the swift progression of BEP may lead to dike failure3. In recent years, frequent extreme rainfall events have heightened the incidence of BEP, seriously threatening the safety of dikes.

The traditional method for rescuing BEP involves placing sand and gravel materials over the piping outlet for filtration (see Fig. 1). However, this method has limitations such as low efficiency, high cost, and the environmental damage caused by excessive extraction of granular materials4. Geotextiles, as a kind of common filtration materials, offer advantages such as lower cost and faster, easier construction compared to granular filters5–7. However, geotextile filters are prone to be clogged in several engineering applications8–11, which limits their effectiveness in BEP rescue. Koerner et al.12 reviewed 69 field failures of geotextile filters that performed unsatisfactorily, and found that the clogging of nonwoven geotextiles commonly occurs in cohesionless fine-grained soils. Miszkowska and Koda13 excavated nonwoven geotextile samples that had been used in Bialobrzegi dam in Poland for 23 years, and measured their permeability coefficient under the load of 0, 2, 20 and 200 kPa. Their findings indicated that the hydraulic performance of nonwoven geotextile under load decreased by factor of 4. Once clogging occurs in the geotextile filter during BEP rescue, the emergency response could fail. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a faster and more effective method of BEP rescue.

Schematic diagram of dike BEP and emergency rescue based on filters.

To achieve rapid and environmentally friendly rescue of BEP in dikes, a novel approach using coconut fiber filter (CFF) is proposed. CFF is a plant-based fiber filter made from processed coconut fibers. It has advantages such as high porosity, low cost, simple and efficient construction, and eco-degradability, making it highly promising for BEP rescue applications. As a plant fiber filter, CFF has already been widely used in areas like soil erosion protection14,15, wastewater filtration16,17, and air purification18,19, and CFF has shown effective results. Based on field experiments, Bhattacharyya et al.20 found that covering soil with CFF significantly boosted biomass production and reduced soil loss under diverse soil and climatic conditions. Ganesan and Nallathambi21 developed a biodegradable multifunctional filter with coconut fiber, achieving a 99% removal efficiency for microplastics and 94% for total organic content in water filtration. At present, the application of plant fiber filters in geotechnical filtration remains in the initial stage, and studies on the filtration performance of CFF in geotechnical engineering are insufficient. Therefore, in order to realize the rapid and eco-friendly treatment of dike hazards using CFF, it is imperative to investigate the filtration characteristics of CFF in BEP rescue.

To investigate the filtration characteristics of CFF in rescuing dike BEP, indoor experiments were conducted using an gradient ratio (GR) apparatus with typical sandy soil from the Yangtze River dike. By comparing with nonwoven geotextile filters, the impact of protected soil gradation and filter pore size on soil retention, clogging characteristics, and permeability of the CFF was analyzed in depth. Additionally, the distribution patterns and locations of clogging substances within the CFF system were explored, and the filtration mechanisms of CFF in preventing BEP were revealed. This research aims to provide a scientific foundation for the effective application of CFF in the emergency rescue of dike BEP.

Materials and methodology

Apparatus

The experiments were conducted using a GR apparatus, as shown in Fig. 2. The apparatus consists of an upper, middle, and lower part, which are connected by flanges and bolts, and the flanges are sealed with rubber pads. The apparatus can accommodate soil samples with dimensions of 100 mm height and 100 mm diameter, and the soil sample in the apparatus is used to simulate the foundation soil at the location of BEP water outlet. The flow direction of the water is vertically upward, simulating the water flow conditions at the BEP outlet. The opening in the upper part of the apparatus is used to represents the BEP outlet. To protect the sand sample from erosion by the inflow water, a perforated plate and a layer of nylon mesh are placed at the bottom of the sand sample. The filter is placed on the top of the sand sample to simulate the filtration process of filter preventing BEP in engineering sites, and the edges of the filter are fixed with flange plates. There are five pressure measurement ports distributed on the side of the apparatus to measure water heads at different positions of the apparatus, and 1# port is used to measure the water head at the water inlet. Compared to traditional GR apparatus, the experimental apparatus was improved by installing a pressure gauge on the lower side of the filter, which call port #5. The distance between the center of port #5 and the filter is 3 mm, and this port is more sensitive to the changes at the soil-filter interface due to being closer to the geotextile7,22. This port can capture the interaction between the protected soil and the filter, which is important to clarify filtration mechanism of filter. The water inlet is connected to the overflow system, which provides a constant water head for the test. The average hydraulic gradient of the system can be adjusted by changing the water head at the water inlet.

In 2020, a large-scale flood occurred in the Yangtze River basin, and 1439 piping occurred in the dykes in the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River. The sand used in the experiment is typical sand from the Yangtze River dike foundation. Two types of sand with different gradations were selected: continuous graded sand S1 and gap-graded sand S2. Experimental scheme

To examine the filtration characteristics of CFF used in BEP rescue and optimize the filter selection, six sets of experiments were designed, with the specific experimental scheme shown in Table 3. Two types of sand with different gradations were considered, and three kinds of filters were tested for each type of sand. During the filtration process, the pore size of the filter has a significant impact on its filtration performance. Based on the design specification of geotextile, the parameter of O95/d85 is used to characterize the relative size of the filter pore. Tests 1 to 3 were designed to investigate the filtration performance of filters in continuous graded sand (S1), while Tests 4 to 6 focused on the filtration performance of filters in gap-graded sand (S2). To ensure piping occurs in the absence of a filter, the average hydraulic gradient was set at 2.0 for all experiments, exceeding the hydraulic gradient at which the sand samples experience seepage failure.

Experimental procedure

Specimen preparation

Specimen preparation

Specimen preparation

To prevent sand intrusion into the pressure measurement tubes during the experiment process, the inlets of the five pressure measurement tube interfaces were plugged with lightweight geotextile (150 g/m2). A perforated plate and a layer of nylon mesh were placed at the bottom of the GR apparatus. Then the sand sample was filled underwater at a designed dry density to achieve saturation. The height of the sand sample was 100 mm, and it was filled in four layers with a thickness of 25 mm each. After the filling process, a saturated geotextile with a diameter of 120 mm was placed on top of the sand sample. Afterwards, the upper part of the apparatus was installed with bolts and sealed using rubber pads to ensure the tightness of the apparatus. The saturation process of geotextiles was to place them in a solution with a 0.1% volume fraction of sodium dodecyl benzene sulfonate for 12 h. Finally, the water inlet of the GR apparatus was connected to the overflow system, and the water level of the overflow system was adjusted to the height of the top surface of the sample. The pressure measuring ports were then connected to the pressure measuring tubes. After water heads of the five pressure measurement tubes were equal, close the water inlet, and the specimen preparation is completed.

Experimental process

Raise the upstream water level to the designed height, then open the water inlet to start the experiment. Based on the results of the preliminary experiments, the average hydraulic gradient was set to 2.0 in Test-1 to Test-6 to ensure the occurrence of piping. Through extensive research, it has been found that the time for emergency response to BEP is mostly within a few hours in practical engineering, and the rescue of piping generally does not exceed 12 h. The experimental process followed the guidelines of ASTM D5101, however, the total test time was adjusted from 24 h to 12 h based on the actual engineering conditions. To minimize the harm of BEP, the engineering rescue time for the BEP is generally kept within 12 h. The water heads of the pressure measurement tubes and the system flow rate were recorded at 2, 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 45, 60, 80, 100, 120, 240, 360, 480, 600, 720 min23. After the test, the sand particles that passed through the geotextile filter were collected, dried and weighed to analyze the soil retention of the geotextile. The geotextile was taken out for drying, weighing, and clogging analysis.

Filters utilized in emergency rescue of levee BEP must meet the requirements for soil retention, permeability, and anti-clogging properties. To assess the filtration performance of filters, key parameters such as the GR value, flow rate, and variations in geotextile mass, were compared across different tests. In the formula, i0−25 represents the hydraulic gradient from the geotextile filter to the 25 mm thick soil sample beneath it, while i0−3 represents the hydraulic gradient from the geotextile filter to the 3 mm thick soil sample beneath it. Filtration performance of CFF in continuous graded sand

During the service period of the filter, it is necessary for the filter to meet soil retention requirements. Soil retention refers to ensuring that the filter’s pore size aligns with the particle size of the protected soil, preventing the loss of soil skeleton particles that could cause seepage deformation. Generally, it is required that the pore size of the filter must be smaller than the control particle size of the protected soil, and the specific quantitative relationship varies according to different criteria. After the experiment, sand particles that passed through the filter were collected, dried, and weighed. In all tests, the mass of sand particles passing through both the CFF and the geotextile filter was nearly zero, indicating that all filters met the soil retention requirements in continuous graded sand.

Clogging characteristics

Figure 5 shows the variation curves of the GR25 over time for the three filters in continuous graded sand samples. The GR25 for all three filter systems remained below 2.0 and gradually stabilized by the end of the experiment, indicating that the filter structures eventually reached stability. At the end of the experiment, the GR25 values for the C300 filter and the nonwoven geotextile filter were similar, at 1.29 and 1.39 respectively, indicating that neither filter experienced significant physical clogging. The stabilized GR25 value for the C200 filter was the highest, at 1.76, greater than both the nonwoven geotextile filter and the C300 filter. It shows that the clogging degree of C200 filter was the greatest. The likely reason is that the larger pore size of the C200 filter allowed fine particles from the lower sand layers to gradually migrate upwards, reducing the permeability of the upper sand layer and filter. Before 360 min, the GR25 value of the C200 filter steadily increased, after which it remained relatively constant. While the rise in the GR25 value for the other two filter systems occurred mainly during the first 15 min of the experiment.

Fig. 5Variation curves of GR25 of the filter systems over time in continuous graded sand sample.

Variation curves of GR25 of the filter systems over time in continuous graded sand sample.

GR3 can be regarded as the ratio of the permeability coefficient of the sand layer 25–75 mm below the filter to the permeability coefficient of the filter and the sand within a 3 mm range beneath it. The changes in the permeability of the filter and nearby sand can be indirectly assessed by GR3 value. During the filtration process, the migration and accumulation of fine particles near the filter significantly impact filter’s filtration performance. To understand fine particles migration near the filter during rescuing BEP, the GR3 values in the experiments were analyzed. Figure 6 shows the GR3 and GR25 values of the three filter systems in S1 sand sample at the end of the experiment. The final GR3 values for the three tests were 0.64, 0.50, and 0.71, respectively, with minimal difference between them. These GR3 values of the filters were significantly smaller than the corresponding GR25 values, and they all were less than 1.0. This indicates that the permeability of the filter and the surrounding sand did not significantly decrease compared to the lower sand layer. Thus, the permeability loss in the filter systems mainly occurred in the sand layers farther away from the filter.

Flow rate characteristics

When filters are used for emergency rescue of dike BEP, the drainage volume is an important indicator of their performance. Figure 7 shows the variation of flow rate over time for filter systems in S1 sand sample. The flow rate for the three filter systems followed a similar variation pattern, peaking at the beginning of the experiment. As the experiment progressed, the flow rate gradually decreased and stabilized. By the end of the experiment, the flow rate of the C300 filter was the highest at 32 mL/min, and the nonwoven geotextile filter and the C200 filter both had flow rates of 21 mL/min. The flow rate of the C300 filter was 52.4% higher than that of the nonwoven geotextile, indicating that the drainage performance of the C300 filter was significantly better than that of the nonwoven geotextile. This may be attributed to the larger pore size and unique pore structure of the CFF. Feng et al.23 studied the filtration characteristics of traditional geotextile filter in rescuing BEP, and found a similar trend that increasing the pore size of the filter within a certain range can enhance the drainage capability of the filter.

Variation curves of flow rate of the filter systems over time in continuous graded sand sample.

At the end of the experiment, the flow rate of the C200 filter was 21 mL/min, significantly lower than that of the C300 filter (32 mL/min), further confirming the fact that the C200 filter experienced more severe clogging. The possible reason was that the pore size of the C200 filter was larger than that of C300. Having a larger pore size of the filter isn’t always advantageous, as it can lead to increased clogging in the filter once it exceeds a certain threshold. This finding is consistent with the research conclusions of Liu et al.24 Considering both the clogging and flow rate characteristics of the filter systems, the C300 filter demonstrated the best filtration performance in the S1 sand sample compared to the other filters tested.

Filtration performance of CFF in gap-graded sand

Test-4 to Test-6 aimed to investigate the filtration characteristics of filters in the gap-graded sand sample (S2). During the experiment, the C200 filter in Test-6 failed to satisfy the soil retention requirement. A large amount of fine particles passed through the filter (Fig. 8) and flowed out of the apparatus in Test-6. When the ratio of the O95 of the filter to the d85 of the protected soil was 1.60 in Test-6, the filter experienced soil retention failure. In contrast, For the continuous graded sand sample S1, when the ratio of O95 to d85 reached 4.18, the CFF could still satisfy the soil retention requirement. This indicates that for gap-graded sand, the risk of soil retention failure in filters significantly increases compared to more uniformly graded sand.

Clogging characteristics

Figure 9 illustrates the variation curves of the GR25 value of the filter systems over time in the gap-graded sand sample (S2). At the end of the experiment, the GR25 values of the nonwoven geotextile filter and the CFF were 1.12 and 5.39, respectively. The GR25 value of the CFF was significantly higher than that of the nonwoven geotextile filter. In the later stages of the experiment, the GR25 value of the CFF exceeded 3.0, indicating severe clogging in the filter system. Between 120 and 360 min, the GR25 value of the CFF dropped significantly before gradually increasing again. During this decrease, it was observed that fine sand particles passed through the filter, suggesting that the reduction in GR was primarily due to the flushing out of clogged particles in the filter system under hydraulic action. For the C300 filter, when the protected soil was the continuous graded sand sample and the gap-graded sand sample, the final GR25 values in the experiments were 1.29 and 5.39, respectively. The C300 filter was more prone to clogging when the protected soil has a higher coefficient of nonuniformity. This indicates that the grading of the protected soil had an significant impact on the filtration performance of the filter, consistent with previous findings25.

Variation curves of GR25 of the filter systems over time in gap-graded sand sample.

Figure 10 presents the GR3 and GR25 values of the filter system in sand sample S2 at the end of the experiment. It can be seen that there is a significant difference between the values of GR3 and GR25, indicating that it is insufficient to evaluate the clogging characteristics of the filter system based solely on the GR25 value. At the end of the experiment, the GR25 value of the nonwoven geotextile filter was 1.12, while the GR3 value was as high as 4.42. This suggests that the permeability coefficient of the filter and the sand layer within 3 mm below it in Test-4 was low, and the clogging of the filter system mainly occurred in this range. A low-permeability filter cake26 may have formed near the filter in Test-4. Conversely, the GR3 of the CFF in Test-5 was 0.66, but its GR25 value was 5.39, indicating that the clogging of the filter system primarily occurred in the sand layer within a range of 3–25 mm below the filter. And there was no significant reduction in the permeability of the filter and the sand layer within a range of 3 mm below it. This may be because the CFF had large pore sizes, making it difficult for fine sand particles to accumulate near the surface of the filter. Therefore, it is unfavorable for a filter cake to be formed in the CFF system.

To further analyze the filtration process of the CFF in the gap-graded sand and understand its filtration mechanism, the variation curves of the permeability coefficient for different sand layers over time in Test-5 are given in Fig. 11. These curves allow for indirect analysis of changes in the particle structure of different sand layers during the experiment. The sand layer structures were divided from the top surface downward. It was observed that the permeability coefficient of the 3–25 mm sand layer was lowest, while it was highest for the 25–50 mm sand layer. The reason may be that fine sand particles migrated from the 25–50 mm sand layer to the 3–25 mm sand layer during the filtration process, leading to clogging of the filter system primarily occurring in the 3–25 mm sand layer. By the end of the experiment, the permeability coefficient of the filter and the top 3 mm sand layer was significantly higher than that of the 3–25 mm sand layer, indicating that a low-permeability filter cake did not form near the filter. Compared to traditional nonwoven geotextile filters, the CFF has larger pore sizes, significantly reducing the risk of forming a low-permeability filter cake on the filter surface.

Flow rate characteristics

Figure 12 presents the variation curves of the flow rate of the filter system over time in the gap-graded sand sample. Throughout the experiment, the flow rate of the CFF was consistently higher than that of the nonwoven geotextile filter. By the end, the flow rate of the CFF was 48 mL/min, which was 71.4% higher than that of the nonwoven geotextile filter (28 mL/min). The CFF exhibited a stronger drainage performance compared to nonwoven geotextiles. The flow rate of the nonwoven geotextile filter experienced a slight increase between 80 and 240 min, and then gradually stabilized, indicating that the filter structure reached a stable state after the initial adjustment period. In contrast, the flow rate of the CFF varied significantly over time. Initially, the flow rate increased significantly, but it began to gradually decrease after 120 min. The reasons are as follows: in the early stages of the experiment, fine sand particles of gap-graded sand migrated under the action of seepage forces, leading to an overall improvement in the permeability of the filter system. As the experiment progressed, continued migration of fine sand particles caused clogging in the filter system, resulting in a decrease in the flow rate.

Fig. 12Variation curves of flow rate of the filter systems over time in gap-graded sand sample.

Variation curves of flow rate of the filter systems over time in gap-graded sand sample.

In the gap-graded sand, fine sand particles were prone to migration, resulting in the formation of a low-permeability filter cake on the surface of the nonwoven geotextile filter. On the contrary, there was no filter cake on the surface of the CFF due to its large pore size. The unique structure of the CFF significantly reduced the risk of forming low-permeability filter cakes in the filter system. For the gap-graded sand used in the experiment, the CFF exhibited significantly higher drainage capacity than the nonwoven geotextile filter, demonstrating superior filtration performance.

Clogging mechanism of CFF

To further investigate the clogging mechanism of CFF, an electron microscopy was used to observe the filters post-experiment. Figure 13 displays the pictures of the CFF after the experiment in Test-2 and Test-5, as well as their partial enlarged images with an magnification of 21 times. The ratios of the equivalent pore size of the filter (O95) to the characteristic particle size of the protected soil (d85) for Test-2 and Test-5 were 3.22, and 1.23, respectively. After the experiment, there were almost no sand particles within the filter in Test-2. However, in Test-5, a significant accumulation of fine particles was observed in certain areas of the filter (Fig. 13d). Due to the fact that the area with severe fine sand particle clogging in the filter occupied only a small portion of the entire filter in Test-5, the filter still exhibited considerable permeability after the experiment, as confirmed in Fig. 11. After the experiment, the filter was dried and weighed. The total sand mass inside the CFF of Test-2 and Test-5 was 0.19 g and 24.55 g, respectively, further confirming that more sand particles were embedded in the filter of Test-5. This may be attributed to the smaller pore size of the filter in Test-5 and the increased likelihood of sand particle migration in the gap-graded sand sample.

Conclusions

This study examines the filtration characteristics of the CFF in emergency rescue of dike BEP based on indoor experiments. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) In the continuous graded sand sample, the GR25 values for both the 300 g/m² CFF and the nonwoven geotextile filter system were similar, both being less than 1.5, indicating no severe clogging. By the end of the experiment, the flow rate of the 300 g/m2 CFF was 52.4% higher than that of the nonwoven geotextile, indicating better filtration performance. Compared to C300, C200 has a thinner thickness, and its GR value in the experiment was 36.4% higher than that of C300, indicating a worse anti-clogging performance.

(2) In the gap-graded sand sample, when the ratio of the equivalent pore size of the CFF (O95) to the characteristic particle size of the protected soil (d85) was 1.60, the filter experienced soil retention failure. The clogging of the nonwoven geotextile mainly occurred in the vicinity of the filter, while the clogging of the CFF primarily occurred within the sand layer 3–25 mm below the filter. At the end of the experiment, the flow rate of the CFF layer was 71.4% higher than that of the nonwoven geotextile, indicating that the CFF had stronger drainage capacity.

(3) Compared to nonwoven geotextile filter, the CFF has larger pores, making it less likely for fine sand particles to accumulate on its surface, which significantly reduces the risk of low-permeability filter cake formation. In contrast to continuous graded sand samples, when the protected soil was the gap-graded sand, fine sand particles were more prone to migration, significantly increasing the risk of soil retention failure and physical clogging of the filter. For the CFF in the S2 sand sample, some fine sand particles were embedded within the filter, but this had little impact on its permeability.
