The tangential growth of the human visual cortex and maternal smoking during pregnancy
Authors: Xavier Navarri, Zhijie Liao, James B Hengenius, Florian Bénitière, Sébastien Jacquemont, Zdenka Pausova, Tomáš Paus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExposure to maternal cigarette smoking in utero is a prenatal adversity due, in part, to the pharmacological effects of nicotine. During gestation, thalamocortical inputs—generated by cholinergic retinal waves—contribute to the development of the visual cortex (V1). First, we investigated the relationship between prenatal exposure to maternal cigarette smoking (PEMCS) and the tangential growth of V1. We observed a robust association between PEMCS and V1 surface area in the UK Biobank (n = 26,987 adults, 30% exposed) and replicated this relationship in the Saguenay Youth Study (n = 944 adolescents, 47% exposed). Subsequent in silico analyses suggested that PEMCS may influence V1 prenatal growth by disrupting the proliferation of intermediate progenitor cells and inhibitory neurons. Second, we explored the role of cholinergic retinal waves as a mechanistic pathway underlying this exposure–outcome relationship. To do so, we used causal variants in genes sensitive to retinal waves (identified in retinal ganglion cells). These variants were related to V1 surface area only among exposed individuals; deletions in these genes and putative loss-of-function single-nucleotide variants were associated with lower V1 surface area. These results suggest that PEMCS influences the prenatal tangential growth of the human V1 via the retina.

Introduction

The human visual cortex (V1) differs from other cortical regions both at phenotypic (i.e., spatial pattern of inter-individual variation) and genetic (i.e., molecular architecture) levels (Grasby et al. 2020; Shin et al. 2020). Before birth, retinal activity plays a critical role in the normal development of the V1 (Wiesel and Hubel 1963; Wiesel and Hubel 1974; Hubel et al. 1977). Spontaneous waves of neuronal activity are generated by the retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) and transmitted, via the lateral geniculate nucleus, to the subventricular zone of the developing V1 (Meister et al. 1991; Feller et al. 1996; Ackman et al. 2012). These thalamocortical afferents are known to play an essential role in the expansion of the progenitor pool in the outer subventricular zone, in particular in the nascent V1 (Dehay et al. 2001). The absence of retinal waves, caused by bilateral enucleation in nonhuman primates before birth, is associated with lower (vs. controls) cortical surface area (Rakic 1988; Bourgeois and Rakic 1996; Dehay et al. 1996). The generation of retinal waves by RGCs requires cholinergic signaling from amacrine cells. The knockout (KO) of the β2 subunit of the nicotinic receptor in the mouse impairs this signaling and reduces retinal waves (Xu et al. 1999; Bansal et al. 2000; Rossi et al. 2001).

Prenatal exposure to maternal cigarette smoking (PEMCS) is a common adverse exposure (Cui et al. 2014; Lange et al. 2018). Cigarette smoke contains thousands of chemicals, with nicotine being the main pharmacologically active molecule acting via nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (Rogers 2008; Shea and Steiner 2008). Although previous studies have pointed to the association between PEMCS and cortical growth (Paus et al. 2012; Salminen et al. 2019; Zou et al. 2022; Rivera et al. 2023), little is known about the underlying mechanisms. Here, we explore a possible mechanistic pathway involving the known relationship between cholinergic retinal waves and the development of the V1.

Materials and methods

Participants

We included participants from two community-based cohorts: the UK Biobank (UKB) and the Saguenay Youth Study (SYS) cohorts. These cohorts were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committees (UKB: 2022-3689, SYS: 2004-215), and consent was obtained from participants and/or their guardians in case of minors (see below).

The UKB cohort recruited around 500,000 adults aged between 40 and 70 years of age at enrollment across 22 centers in the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010 (Sudlow et al. 2015). All participants were genotyped and provided samples of blood, urine, and saliva. Multimodal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) imaging of the brain was obtained in a subset of these participants (n = 26,987) with available PEMCS status. Basic characteristics of this subsample are presented in Table S1.

The SYS cohort, which is a family-based (481 families) study, recruited 1,029 adolescents and 962 parents from the genetic-founder population (European ancestry) in the Saguenay Lac Saint-Jean region in Quebec, Canada, between 2003 and 2012 (Pausova et al. 2007; Pausova et al. 2017). Adolescents underwent neuroimaging and the mothers completed a semi-structured interview and questionnaires regarding their smoking behaviors during pregnancy. Additional information is described elsewhere (Pausova et al. 2007; Pausova et al. 2017).

The North West Multi-Center Research Ethics Committee as a Research Tissue Bank approved the UKB study. Consent from all UKB participants was electronically obtained to participate during their initial assessment visit. This study was approved by the UKB Resource Application Number 75946. Assent and consent from all SYS adolescents and parents, respectively, were obtained for this cohort. Ethics research board approval for this cohort was obtained at the Centre intégré universitaire de santé et services sociaux du Saguenay—Lac-Saint-Jean.

Prenatal exposure to maternal cigarette smoking

The “Maternal smoking around birth” variable (field 1787) was used to classify UKB participants: individuals who responded consistently to the questionnaire regarding their perinatal exposure across instances (until the neuroimaging visit) were classified as exposed or nonexposed. Participants with inconsistent answers across instances, in addition to those who did not know or did not answer, were excluded from the analysis.

The SYS cohort was designed a priori to study maternal smoking during pregnancy and included a similar number of exposed and non-exposed participants; at recruitment, non-exposed adolescents were matched to the exposed ones by maternal education (Pausova et al. 2007; Pausova et al. 2017). A daily consumption of at least one cigarette every day during the second gestational trimester was the definition of PEMCS in this cohort; effectively, the exposed individuals were exposed to smoking during the entire pregnancy.

Magnetic resonance imaging data acquisition, processing, and cortical surface area

T1-weighted anatomical images (1.0-mm isotropic resolution) were acquired using a 3T Siemens Skyra scanner in UKB and a 1T Phillips scanner in SYS. Additional information on the acquisition and processing steps for these cohorts were described elsewhere (Miller et al. 2016; Pausova et al. 2017). Images were then processed using the fully automated FreeSurfer software (Version 7.4.1 in both cohorts) to reconstruct regional surface area for 68 cortical regions using the Desikan–Killiany atlas as a reference in both cohorts (Desikan et al. 2006; Fischl 2012). Subsequently to the reconstruction, FreeSurfer was used to parcellate Desikan–Killiany into sub-regions to estimate the surface area for 11 cortical regions that match the regions in which the PsychENCODE Consortium collected fetal samples of the cerebral cortex to estimate bulk gene-expression. Additional information on the parcellation is described elsewhere (Patel et al. 2022). For each of the 11 cortical regions, surface area was summed across the 2 hemispheres. Global surface area was estimated by summing all 68 regions of the Desikan–Killiany atlas.

Genotyping of rare variants

In the UKB cohort, copy number variants (CNVs) and rare putative loss-of-function single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) were called (Bycroft et al. 2018). While genome-wide CNVs were called in the SYS cohort, there were not enough participants with CNVs associated with the differentially expressed genes in the β2KO mouse. Therefore, the analysis of rare variants was only performed in the UKB cohort.

In UKB, CNVs that were ≥50 kb and fully encompassed at least one protein-coding gene were detected using CNVs using PennCNV and QuantiSNP (see Liao et al. 2025 for details). There were 14 retinal genes deleted at least once in the population, and 17 participants had at least one “retinal” gene deleted (6 exposed and 12 non-exposed participants).

Next, we identified SNVs (minor allele frequency <0.001) from exome-sequencing data from UKB. SNVs were extracted from individual genomic variant call format (gVCF) files generated for the UKB cohort generated using DRAGEN 3.7.8 (Bycroft et al. 2018). Homozygous reference calls (0/0) and missing genotypes (./.) were excluded. Genotypes were filtered to retain those with read depth (DP) ≥20, genotype quality ≥30, and allele count ratios (the ratio of alternate allele depth to DP) between 0.2 and 0.8, consistent with true heterozygotes. To focus on rare, potentially pathogenic SNVs, variants were retained only if both their maximum allele frequency across gnomAD populations and within the UKB dataset were ≤0.001. The function of the SNVs was annotated using the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (release 113.3) (McLaren et al. 2016) with plugins of LoFtee and SpliceAI, aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome. The LoFtee identifies high-confidence loss-of-function variants (Karczewski et al. 2020) and the SpliceAI predicts the effects of variants on splicing (Jaganathan et al. 2019). Only LoFtee high-confidence frameshift and stop-gained variants, and splice variants with SpliceAI maximum delta score ≥0.8 were included as SNVs in the current study. The extraction and annotation workflows are available on Zenodo: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.16268986.

Out of 48,720 participants who had MRI, exome-sequencing, and maternal smoking data, 1,968 participants carried loss-of-function SNVs of retinal genes (encountered 172 retinal genes); 605 exposed participants carried SNVs of genes mapped to 136 retinal genes, and 1,363 non-exposed participants carried SNVs of genes mapped to 167 retinal genes.

Statistical analysis

First, we evaluated the phenotypic association between regional cortical surface area and PEMCS in the UKB cohort using multiple linear models. The models were adjusted for sex, age at scan, the first 10 genetic principal components, years of education, household income, smoking status, as well as cardiometabolic traits that showed differences between exposed and non-exposed participants (i.e., body mass index, diabetes mellitus status, and both systolic and diastolic blood pressures). Participants with a medical history of dementia-related conditions or strokes were excluded using 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases diagnostic codes as previously done (Said et al. 2018; Wilkinson et al. 2019). In the SYS cohort, linear models were also adjusted for sex, age at scan, and the first 10 genetic principal components. As done previously (Toro et al. 2008; Shin et al. 2020; Patel et al. 2022), we did not adjust for global surface area.

Next, we investigated the relationship between PEMCS and rare genetic variants in human homologs of genes sensitive to cholinergic retinal waves (in mice) vis-à-vis V1 surface area. A recent study (Somaiya et al. 2025) reported a list of 241 differentially expressed mouse genes (DEGs) in RGCs between β2KO mice and wild-type (WT) mice. These wave-sensitive DEGs in RGCs were classified as downregulated (KO < WT) or upregulated (KO > WT). Using the human homologs of the DEGs, gene ontology analysis of biological processes, cellular compartments, and molecular functions was performed using ClusterProfiler (Wu et al. 2021).

We used a two-pronged strategy to explore the relationship between rare genetic variants and PEMCS vis-à-vis V1 surface area.

First, we evaluated the association of CNVs of retinal genes vis-à-vis the V1 surface area as a function of PEMCS exposure. Since many CNVs encompass more than one gene, the counts of other genes (in addition to age at scan, sex, global surface area, and the first 10 principal components of genetic ancestry) were included as a covariate. In each exposure group (exposed, non-exposed), the association between surface area and deletions (present, absent) and duplications (present, absent) was evaluated, followed by an interaction analysis regarding the moderating role of PEMCS on the relationship between surface area and CNVs (carriers/non-carriers).

Second, we evaluated the influence of SNVs on V1 surface area by aggregating SNVs within each retinal gene and each participant as a function of PEMCS exposure. Since the magnitude and the direction of the effect of each retinal gene on V1 are likely to vary, Sequence Kernel Association Test-Optimal (SKAT-O) unified test (Lee et al. 2012) with a genes-by-participants matrix as the genetic input was used to test the relationship between retinal genes and V1. Age at scan, sex, global surface area, and the first 10 genetic principal components were included as covariates. To investigate the directionality between SNVs of downregulated and upregulated retinal genes and V1 surface area, a burden test was applied—for each of these two gene subsets—by running a linear regression between the number of genes with SNVs (as the predictor) and V1 surface area (as the outcome).

Virtual ontogeny

Virtual ontogeny examines the spatial relationship between inter-regional profiles of the surface area and the expression of genes specific to eight cell types (i.e., radial glia, intermediate progenitor cells [IPCs], excitatory and inhibitory neurons, microglia, oligodendrocyte progenitor cells [OPCs], endothelial, and mural cells) in the fetal cortex of the same 11 cortical regions (Patel et al. 2022). Using single-cell RNA sequencing data from each of the five fetal brains obtained between 6- and 22-weeks postconception, the 200 genes with the highest level of specificity for one of the eight cell types were identified (Bhaduri et al. 2020). The expression level of these cell-specific genes was then estimated for 11 cortical regions from 14 donors aged between 12- and 22-weeks postconception using the bulk gene expression data from the PsychENCODE Development dataset (Li et al. 2018). For each cell-specific gene panel, the correlation between each individual gene and the inter-regional profile of variations in cortical surface area was computed and averaged across the 200 genes. Significance was evaluated using a permutation approach to derive a null distribution over 10,000 iterations (Patel et al. 2022).

Results

Phenotypic associations

To evaluate the association between cortical surface area and PEMCS, we studied 26,987 UKB middle-aged adults (mean age at scan: 64.2 ± 7.62 years; 47% males; 30% with PEMCS) of European ancestry. Basic characteristics of UKB participants are provided in Table S1. We fitted multiple regression models with surface area (for each of 11 cortical regions) as the outcome and PEMCS as the predictor, and adjusted for several confounders (see Methods). Participants who were exposed (n = 8,087) or not exposed (n = 18,900) to maternal smoking during pregnancy are henceforth referred to as “exposed” and “non-exposed,” respectively. A False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied to the 12 associations (11 regional and 1 global) to account for multiple testing (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). In this sample (Fig. 1), exposed participants showed lower global surface area (β = −0.09, PFDR = 1.73 × 10−11). Regarding regional associations, V1 showed the strongest negative relationship (β = −0.17, PFDR = 9.25 × 10−26) between its surface area and PEMCS (see below for a formal evaluation of regional differences in the effect size).

Inter-regional profiles of the relationship between cortical surface area and PEMCS in UKB (middle-aged adults; left panel) and SYS (adolescents; right panel). Black dots indicate FDR-corrected significant associations (PFDR < 0.05). A1C, primary auditory cortex; DFC, dorsal frontal cortex; IPC, inferior parietal cortex; ITC, inferior temporal cortex; M1C, primary motor cortex; MFC, medial frontal cortex; OFC, orbitofrontal cortex; S1C, primary somatosensory cortex; STC, superior temporal cortex; V1C, primary visual cortex; VFC, ventral frontal cortex.

We then replicated the results in the SYS adolescent cohort, which was designed to study the relationship between PEMCS and various outcomes. At recruitment, it matched exposed and non-exposed participants by maternal education (Pausova et al. 2007; Pausova et al. 2017). We studied 944 adolescents (mean age at scan: 15 ± 1.8 years; 48% males; 47% with PEMCS) from a founder-effect population of European ancestry. Additional basic characteristics of SYS adolescents are provided in Table S2. We observed lower V1 surface area in exposed (vs non-exposed) participants (β = −0.40, PFDR = 2.91 × 10−9); this in the absence of a group difference in global SA. Although there are differences in the magnitude of the standardized β values between the cohorts, the degree of similarity (assessed by Pearson correlation) of the inter-regional profiles (11 regions) between the 2 cohorts is high (r2 = 0.77, P = 3.9 × 10−4). In each cohort, a Z-test (Clogg et al. 1995) confirmed that the effect of PEMCS on V1 surface area was the strongest effect of PEMCS across all examined cortical regions; summary statistics of the inter-regional profiles are provided in Table S3.

Owing to the family-based design of the SYS cohort (with two or more siblings per family), siblings who were discordant for their exposure status were compared. In the entire cohort, such discordance was rare, limited to 18 same-sex siblings (4 brother–brother pairs and 14 sister–sister pairs). Figure S1 and Table S4 provide the results for the sister–sister pairs; exposed sisters showed lower V1 area than non-exposed sisters in 11/14 pairs (paired sign-test P = 0.029).

Rare variants in retinal genes

To explore a possible mechanistic pathway, namely whether cholinergic retinal activity contributes to the relationship between PEMCS and V1 surface area, we adopted the following approach. First, we used experimental data generated by Somaiya et al. (2025) to select key genes sensitive to a KO of the gene critical for the generation of retinal waves, namely β2 subunit (β2KO) of the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor; this KO is known to inhibit the retinal waves (Xu et al. 1999; Bansal et al. 2000; Rossi et al. 2001). This step was accomplished by identifying 214 human homologs of 241 genes with a differential expression—in mouse RGCs—between β2KO and WT mice (Somaiya et al. 2025). Second, we tested the effect of rare variants (deletions, duplications, and putative loss-of-function SNVs) in these genes on V1 surface area in UKB individuals who were exposed and non-exposed to maternal smoking during pregnancy. Note that the SYS cohort is too small for applying this (rare variants) approach.

For CNVs, we identified 18 and 88 individuals with at least one deletion and one duplication, respectively, of retinal genes. We found that, in exposed individuals, participants with deletions of the retinal genes had lower V1 surface area (n = 6, β = −1.02, P = 0.00421, Fig. 2); this was not the case for deletions in the nonexposed participants (n = 12, β = −0.32, P > 0.05). With an a priori hypothesis that—among exposed individuals—deletion carriers would show lower V1 surface area than CNV noncarriers, the interaction term between PEMCS status (exposed, non-exposed) and retinal-gene deletion (present, absent) was significant (one-tailed P = 0.04987). No significant effects were observed for duplications (despite having a larger number of individuals in each PEMCS group; see Methods).

Relationships between V1 surface area among carriers of copy number variations, stratified by PEMCS in the UKB cohort. Deletions among individuals exposed to maternal smoking during pregnancy was the only significant predictor of lower V1 surface area since the 95% CI did not overlap with the non-effect line at zero. The thick and thin black lines reflect the mean and median, respectively.

The next analysis focused on rare SNVs with putative loss-of-function (i.e., stop-gain, frameshift, and splice-site). We stratified this analysis by the direction of the effect of β2KO on gene expression: downregulated (KO < WT, n = 124 genes) and upregulated (KO > WT, n = 90 genes) retinal genes. This is based on a gene ontology analysis indicating that downregulated (but not upregulated) retinal genes, differentially expressed in the β2KO mouse model, are enriched for cholinergic-signaling terms (Table S6). We evaluated the association between SNVs and V1 surface area by performing an optimal unified test (henceforth referred to as SKAT-O) and a burden test. Using a SKAT-O test, SNVs in downregulated retinal genes were associated with V1 surface area in exposed participants (n = 315 carriers, 79 genes, SKAT-O P = 0.0097); this was not the case for non-exposed participants (n = 698 carriers, 102 genes, SKAT-O P > 0.05). In contrast, SNVs in upregulated retinal genes were not associated with V1 surface area in exposed individuals (n = 298 carriers, 58 genes, SKAT-O P > 0.05), but they were associated with V1 surface area in non-exposed individuals (n = 690 carriers, 68 genes, SKAT-O P = 0.040). Using a burden test, we examined the interaction between the burden score for each set of genes (i.e., downregulated and upregulated) and exposure status vis-à-vis V1 surface area. Significant interactions were observed in both downregulated (n = 1013, β = −0.14, P = 0.016) and upregulated (n = 988, β = 0.14, P = 0.019) tests. To inform on the directionality and facilitate the interpretation, we stratified this analysis by exposure status: exposed participants with SNVs in downregulated genes showed lower V1 surface area (n = 315, β = −0.12, P = 0.012); this was not the case in non-exposed individuals (n = 698, β = 0.08, P > 0.05). For upregulated genes, we have not observed, with the burden test, any SNV effects in either exposed (n = 298, β = 0.02, P > 0.05) or non-exposed (n = 690, β = −0.05, P > 0.05) individuals (Fig. 3).

Burden test results of the relationship between V1 surface area and single nucleotide variants of downregulated and upregulated retinal genes, stratified by PEMCS. KO, knockout; WT, wild-type.

Virtual ontogeny

Finally, we have used an in silico approach developed previously (Patel et al. 2022) to explore the neurobiological underpinnings of the group differences (exposed vs. non-exposed) in surface area across the 11 cortical regions. This approach spatially correlates the MRI-based profiles with the expression of genes specific to various cell types (e.g., radial glia, IPCs, and excitatory and inhibitory neurons) in the fetal cortex during midgestation (see Methods) across the same 11 cortical regions. In the UKB sample, the inter-regional profile of the PEMCS-surface area associations correlated negatively with IPC, excitatory neurons and microglia panels, and positively with inhibitory neurons and OPC panels (Fig. 4). That is, regions with higher expression of genes specific to IPCs, excitatory neurons and microglia showed larger group differences as a function of PEMCS status, while cortical regions with high expression of genes specific to inhibitory neurons and OPCs during midgestation showed smaller group differences. Since V1 was the region with the greatest group difference in surface area observed between exposed and non-exposed participants, this region likely showed a high level of expression of IPC-specific genes during midgestation. We replicated the virtual ontogeny results in the SYS sample (Fig. 4). Summary statistics are provided in Table S5.

FDR-significant associations are annotated by a star (PFDR < 0.05). IPC, intermediate progenitor cell; OPC, oligodendrocyte progenitor cell.

Discussion

The surface area of the primary V1 was smaller in individuals exposed (vs. non-exposed) to maternal smoking during pregnancy in 2 independent cohorts. Among exposed individuals, those with rare variants in a set of retinal genes that are differentially expressed in the β2KO mouse showed lower V1 surface area; this was not the case in non-exposed participants. These findings support the proposed mechanistic pathway whereby cholinergic activity in the retina influences the relationship between prenatal exposure to maternal smoking and the tangential growth of V1.

Chronic exposure to nicotine may lead to a desensitization of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors: this adaptive response could change the number of functional receptors on RGCs (Quick and Lester 2002). Such functional changes could alter the generation of retinal waves and their propagation throughout the visual system. Considering that thalamocortical axons release a mitogenic factor (Dehay et al. 2001), a PEMCS-related change in the cell-cycle division rate of progenitor cells may decrease the number of ontogenetic columns and, in turn, the surface area (Dehay et al. 2001; Smart et al. 2002; Dehay and Kennedy 2023). This tentative mechanism aligns well with our virtual ontogeny results that point to the involvement of IPCs; it could reflect an early transition from neurogenesis to differentiation (Navarro et al. 1989). The same analysis also revealed a spatial correlation between PEMCS-related differences in surface area and expression of genes specific to inhibitory neurons. We speculate that this latter observation may reflect the known role of thalamocortical axons, which target the ganglionic eminence before birth and could, in turn, alter the density of inhibitory interneurons in the cerebral cortex (Métin and Godement 1996; Lavdas et al. 1999).

Overall, two observations support the involvement of the retina in underlying the association between PEMCS and V1 surface area. First, the PEMCS–V1 association was the strongest among all examined cortical regions in both cohorts. Second, it was moderated by rare variants in retinal genes. Note that SNVs of retinal genes downregulated by the β2 subunit KO were more strongly associated with lower V1 surface area, as compared with upregulated genes. A gene ontology analysis of these downregulated genes showed an enrichment for cholinergic-signaling terms; this was not the case for upregulated terms. Nonetheless, it is also possible that PEMCS interferes with nicotine binding directly in the developing cerebral cortex. In the (fetal) baboon brain, prenatal exposure to nicotine is associated with increased binding of a labeled molecule to nicotinic acetylcholine receptors in the occipital cortex; but note that other cortical regions were not examined in this study (Duncan et al. 2015). Single-cell spatial transcriptomics of the midgestational neocortex has revealed that genes coding for nicotinic acetylcholine receptor subunits (e.g. CHRNA7, CHRNA5, and CHRNB2) are similarly expressed in IPCs across all 6 examined neocortical areas (i.e., prefrontal, somatosensory, motor, temporal, parietal, and V1) (Bhaduri et al. 2021; Speir et al. 2021), thus arguing against (V1-specific) effects of PEMCS directly in the developing cortex. Thus, taken together, we suggest that retinal waves provide the most plausible explanatory mechanism.

Strengths

Here, we showed a robust relationship between PEMCS and the surface area of V1; exposed (vs. non-exposed) individuals had a smaller V1. In addition, we showed a high degree of similarity—between the two cohorts—of the spatial pattern of the PEMCS–surface area relationship. These results are consistent with previous reports that evaluated the relationship between maternal smoking and cortical surface area in adolescent and adult cohorts (Salminen et al. 2019; Zou et al. 2022; Rivera et al. 2023). Furthermore, we took advantage of the family-based design of the SYS cohort and showed that—in sister-sister pairs discordant for maternal smoking during pregnancy—the exposed sisters had lower V1 surface area than the non-exposed ones. Given that sibling-pairs share many possible genetic and family-level environmental confounders (Thapar and Rice 2021), this observation strengthens the causal nature of this exposure-outcome relationship.

Limitations

Although our results are consistent with the role of retinal waves in the PEMCS–V1 association, they are limited by the lack of experimental data on the relationship between retinal waves and the size of the V1, as assessed directly in animal models. Nonetheless, ample evidence exists with regard to the role of retinal waves in the development of the retinotopic organization of the visual pathway at the level of both the lateral geniculate nucleus (Penn et al. 1998; Rossi et al. 2001; Huberman et al. 2002) and the primary visual cortex (Cang et al. 2005; Cang et al. 2008; Ackman et al. 2012; Feller and Kerschensteiner 2020). In the UKB cohort, we cannot exclude the possibility of a recall bias, as these participants completed retrospective questionnaires and likely relied on information received, at some point, from their parents. We mitigated this by including only participants who provided consistent answers regarding PEMCS between the initial assessment and the neuroimaging visit. We also noticed that the results of the two cohorts differed in the magnitude of the standardized estimates of the association between PEMCS and surface area; the estimates are smaller in the UKB cohort, as compared with SYS. The difference in the magnitude of the PEMCS–surface area association between the two cohorts may reflect different strategies of their ascertainment: random vis-à-vis PEMCS in the UKB cohort, targeted in the SYS cohort in which we matched carefully exposed and non-exposed individuals by maternal education. Nonetheless, several prenatal confounders (e.g. alcohol, drugs, paternal smoking) were not included in the phenotypic modeling due to their limited measurements in the UKB cohort; similar linear models were used in the two cohorts to compare the estimates. In addition, analyses in the UKB cohort were limited to participants of European ancestry, limiting generalizability to other genetic ancestries. Lastly, the proposed retinal hypothesis would benefit from examining the V1–PEMCS relationship in a dose–response framework in murine models. The latter would allow oen to explore the heterogeneous relationship between SNVs in retinal upregulated genes and V1 growth, and provide an experimental validation of the proposed retinal hypothesis using an independent β2KO RGCs dataset.
