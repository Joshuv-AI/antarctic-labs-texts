Iron Complexes With Proteins, Carbohydrates, and Maillard Products in Dairy Systems: Implication for Fortification, Bioavailability, and Product Functionality
Authors: Ishara De Silva, Mary Ann Augustin, Thom Huppertz, Julia YQ Low, Arianna Dick, Jayani Chandrapala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe global prevalence of iron (Fe) deficiency anemia (IDA) continues to pose major public health challenges, necessitating the development of effective fortification strategies. Dairy‐based systems are promising vehicles for Fe fortification and delivery due to their widespread consumption and nutritional value, although their complex matrix presents significant challenges for maintaining Fe bioavailability and product quality. This review critically evaluates current knowledge on Fe fortification in dairy systems, integrating evidence on Fe forms, physicochemical interactions between Fe and the matrix, and their consequent effects on Fe bioavailability and physical functionality. Emerging fortification strategies are highlighted. Conventional Fe salts, including ferrous sulfate, ferrous fumarate, and ferric pyrophosphate, have varying degrees of solubility and absorption but are often associated with adverse sensory changes, lipid oxidation, and reduced product stability. Fe–ligand complexes, including protein‐, carbohydrate‐, and Maillard reaction product (MRP)‐based systems, can impart improved stability to the fortified product and bioavailability by minimizing interactions with inhibitors of Fe absorption. The incorporation of Fe into dairy matrices influences product pH and structural and functional properties while also accelerating lipid oxidation and sensory deterioration when inorganic salts are used. Strategies such as Fe chelation, microencapsulation, and co‐fortification with enhancers have shown potential to mitigate these limitations. In addition, the role of prebiotics, probiotics, and MRPs in modulating Fe absorption remains complex and context dependent. Despite promising advancements, gaps remain in understanding interactions between Fe, proteins, carbohydrates and MRP and their implications for Fe fortification of dairy systems. Future research should focus on developing integrated fortification approaches enabling the design of next‐generation functional dairy foods to effectively combat global Fe deficiency.Keywords: iron bioavailability, iron interactions, Maillard reaction products

Introduction

Iron (Fe) is an essential trace element in the human body. It plays a critical role in oxygen and electron transport, in multiple phases of cellular growth, such as division and differentiation, and in the regulation of gene expression. Consequently, Fe deficiency can lead to severe complications, including preterm birth, impaired growth, and cognitive and behavioral dysfunction in infants (Blanco‐Rojo and Vaquero 2019). In adults, Fe deficiency is associated with numerous adverse outcomes, such as fatigue, weakness, reduced productivity, and impaired concentration (Piskin et al. 2022). According to the World Health Organization (WHO), anemia affects approximately half a billion women aged 15–49 years, including 37% of pregnant women and 30% of nonpregnant women in this age group. In addition, 269 million children aged 9–59 months are reported to be anemic, with iron (Fe) deficiency anemia identified as the leading cause. Inadequate dietary Fe intake is recognized as the primary driver of IDA (WHO 2025). Although macrophages in the human body can recycle and reutilize Fe from erythrocytes, Fe cannot be synthesized de novo (Recalcati and Cairo 2021). Furthermore, Fe is continuously lost through various physiological processes, including menstrual blood loss, urinary and fecal losses, and desquamation of skin and endothelial cells (Moustarah and Daley 2024). Restoring Fe is therefore essential to maintain physiological functions.

The average daily dietary intake of Fe in humans ranges from 10 to 15 mg, yet only 1–2 mg is typically absorbed through the gastrointestinal (GI) tract (Piskin et al. 2022). The recommended nutrient intake differs according to age, gender, and physiological status (Table 1). Intestinal Fe absorption is regulated and influenced by several factors, including the form of Fe (heme or nonheme), the presence of dietary components, such as absorption enhancers (e.g., ascorbic acid [AA]), and inhibitors (e.g., phytic acid, polyphenols, calcium, and oxalic acid), as well as Fe status and health conditions (e.g., inflammatory bowel disease, Crohn's disease, and ulcerative colitis) (Hurrell and Egli 2010). Heme Fe complexes, found in animal‐based Fe sources such as meat and poultry, are absorbed intact without releasing Fe into the intestinal lumen and have a higher bioavailability than Fe from plant foods. Nonheme Fe, derived from plant‐based foods, is released into the lumen, and its absorption is strongly influenced by the presence of dietary enhancers and inhibitors (Rout et al. 2024). Although 95% of dietary Fe intake comes from nonheme sources, its absorption ranges from only 3%–20%, whereas heme Fe is absorbed at significantly higher levels of 15%–35% (Zijp et al. 2000). Dietary enhancers are compounds that can facilitate the release and absorption of the mineral in the intestinal lumen, often by binding to minerals in the food matrix, to form a soluble complex. In contrast, dietary inhibitors are compounds that form insoluble or nonabsorbable complexes with minerals, limiting their absorption (Clydesdale 1988). Moreover, individuals with lower Fe status achieve higher Fe absorption than those with higher Fe status, as the body regulates the Fe absorption efficiency to prevent adverse effects from high Fe content (Hurrell 2021).

To overcome IDA and achieve adequate Fe stores via dietary intake of Fe, strategies such as Fe fortification and supplementation have been developed over time. However, there are major barriers to successful Fe fortification of food. A significant challenge is identifying a form of Fe that exhibits high absorption efficiency without causing adverse sensory changes in the food carrier. Another is designing a carrier matrix that can overcome inhibitory effects on Fe absorption in the GI tract (Hurrell 2002). Although Fe fortification has been attempted in a variety of staple foods, including salt, rice, wheat flour, and maize flour, there have been limitations with these strategies due to sensory alteration and altered bioavailability (Hurrell 2022). While the intake of oral supplements is another option for combating Fe deficiency, it has been associated with side effects such as nausea, constipation, teeth staining, poor solubility, reduced bioavailability, bloating, impacts on gut microflora in the gut and limited effectiveness at physiological pH (Bloor et al. 2021; Rout et al. 2024). To mitigate these limitations, other strategies have focused on enhancing Fe bio‐accessibility through chelation or conjugation with biocompatible carriers such as polysaccharides, peptides, proteins, and Maillard reaction products (MRPs), which may have the potential to offer better product quality and bioavailability than inorganic Fe forms (Hu et al. 2023; Rout et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2024).

Milk is a staple component of the human diet in many countries and is considered an affordable food option for the global population (Miller et al. 2006). Milk is a biologically complex, multiphasic colloidal suspension composed of mainly water (85%–90% w/w) followed by lactose (carbohydrate), fat, proteins, minerals, and other micronutrients such as vitamins, enzymes, and nonprotein nitrogen compounds (Lucey et al. 2017). Although milk naturally contains a relatively low Fe concentration (0.2 mg/kg) (Flynn 1992), dairy products, including milk, yogurt, and milk‐based beverages, are ideal candidates for Fe fortification due to their widespread consumption and high nutritional value. However, the physicochemical and sensory properties of milk can be significantly influenced by the type and form of Fe used (Gaucheron 2000). In the context of Fe fortification, the functionality of dairy systems encompasses both the nutritional effectiveness of Fe, particularly in relation to bioavailability and absorption, and its technological performance within the food matrix, including physicochemical stability, structural integrity, and sensory quality after processing and storage. These aspects are closely interrelated, as interactions between Fe and dairy components such as proteins, carbohydrates, and MRPs can simultaneously influence Fe bioavailability and product quality.

Given this, the interaction between Fe–salts and Fe–chelate complexes formed with proteins, polysaccharides and MRPs, in milk‐based systems, offers a promising strategy to overcome some of the current limitations associated with Fe fortification. However, despite their potential, these systems remain insufficiently explored. Therefore, the aim of this review is to critically examine current knowledge on Fe fortification in dairy products, with a particular focus on the different Fe forms and delivery systems, including inorganic salts and protein‐, carbohydrate‐, and MRP‐based complexes, and their interactions with the dairy matrix. The review further evaluates factors influencing Fe bioavailability, including dietary enhancers and inhibitors and interactions with food components. In addition, it assesses the impact of Fe fortification on the physicochemical, structural, and sensory properties of dairy products, highlighting current challenges, potential strategies, and future directions for the development of effective dairy‐based Fe delivery systems.

Iron Forms for Fortification and Their Bioavailability

Iron fortification can be achieved by using various forms of Fe, including Fe salts, elemental irons and complexes of Fe with ligands (Gaucheron 2000; Piskin et al. 2022). These Fe sources can also be differentiated based on their absorption efficiency, that is, as freely water‐soluble, poorly water‐soluble but soluble in dilute acids, water‐insoluble and poorly soluble in dilute acid and protected compounds (Hurrell 1997). Higher Fe absorption levels can generally be achieved with freely water‐soluble Fe compounds, although they can cause adverse sensory effects compared to poorly soluble compounds. Therefore, the efficacy of the form of Fe used in fortification is determined by the cumulative effects of its solubility, stability, and bioavailability (Hurrell 2002; Hurrell 2022; Vatandoust and Diosady 2022).

Iron Salts and Ligands

Ferrous (Fe2+) and ferric (Fe3+) are the most common redox states of Fe in food. Ferrous ions predominate under acidic conditions, whereas higher pH promotes their oxidation to ferric ions. In addition, under conditions of elevated redox potential, Fe predominantly remains in its oxidized Fe3+ state (Mehansho 2006). Oxidation of Fe2+ ions generates free radicals, which can catalyze lipid peroxidation and cause off‐flavors and rancidity in the food carrier. Moreover, Fe2+ ions can react with phenolic compounds present in food, leading to the development of off colors (Mellican et al. 2003; Hurrell 2021). Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions can be bound to complexing agents such as sulfate, chloride, gluconate, fumarate, citrate, phosphate, and EDTA. These Fe salts and Fe chelates can be used to achieve a high fortification efficiency with varying bioavailability in a variety of food matrices while minimizing adverse sensory effects (Gaucheron 2000), depending on equilibria between Fe and the various components in the food system. Ferrous Sulfate

Ferrous sulfate (FeSO4) is regarded as the gold standard for Fe fortification, as it is readily soluble in water, cost‐effective and has the highest RBV (100%) among all the other Fe fortificant forms (Table 2) (Hurrell 2002). However, the chemical properties that enable the high absorption efficiency of FeSO4 also make it prone to interactions with food constituents, often resulting in sensory and stability issues (Hurrell 2021). During prolonged storage, FeSO4 can catalyze lipid peroxidation in fortified foods, leading to the development of rancid flavors. Some of the undesirable effects of Fe in food include unfavorable color changes in products rich in phenolic compounds, metallic off‐flavors and precipitation of peptides in beverages (Mannar and Hurrell 2018). These adverse effects of FeSO4 are primarily attributable to its oxidative conversion to Fe3+ ions, which are associated with reddish discoloration and off‐flavors (Vatandoust and Diosady 2022). The heptahydrate (20% Fe) and the anhydrous (33% Fe) form are the two forms of FeSO4 available. The heptahydrate form has a high water solubility and causes more unfavorable sensory effects than the anhydrous form. FeSO4 has been successfully used in bread, pasta, infant formula, cereals, and wheat flour (Hurrell 2002; Mannar and Hurrell 2018).

Ferrous Fumarate

Dark red‒brown colored and low‐cost ferrous fumarate (33% Fe) has a comparatively lower water solubility than FeSO4 and is soluble in dilute acids such as gastric juice (Mannar and Hurrell 2018). Due to its lower water solubility and lower reactivity with food vehicle matrices, ferrous fumarate causes minimal adverse sensory effects in fortified foods compared to the highly reactive FeSO4 (Mannar and Hurrell 2018; Hurrell 2021; Vatandoust and Diosady 2022). Furthermore, owing to its low water solubility, ferrous fumarate exhibits reduced susceptibility to dietary inhibitors such as phytic acids present within the food matrix, resulting in more effective absorption in fortified cereal sources than FeSO4 (Harrington et al. 2011). Therefore, ferrous fumarate has a RBV similar to that of FeSO4 (Table 2) in most age groups, whereas for Fe‐deficient infants, ferrous fumarate was reported to have a lower RBV (25%–35%) (Harrington et al. 2011). The latter is probably related to the fact that in Fe‐deficient infants, poor nutritional status leads to insufficient gastric juice production, which limits the solubility and effective absorption of ferrous fumarate (Hurrell et al. 2002). Ferrous fumarate is commonly used to fortify infant cereals, as an Fe supplement to fortify infant cereals, and occasionally for fortification of chocolate milk and cereal flours (Table 2) (Kaur et al. 2022). In contrast, its poor water solubility makes effective incorporation of ferrous fumarate into liquid systems challenging (Hurrell 2002; Harrington et al. 2011).

Ferric Pyrophosphate

Fortification of food matrices that are sensitive to sensory alteration can be successfully achieved using ferric pyrophosphate (FPP). FPP (25% Fe) has been reported to cause minimal color and flavor alteration compared to most water‐soluble Fe salts, including FeSO4 and ferrous fumarate (Mannar and Hurrell 2018). Despite its advantageous sensory characteristics, FPP is expensive, water insoluble, and poorly soluble in gastric juices, which reduces fortification efficiency and bioavailability (Table 2) (Mannar and Hurrell 2018; Kaur et al. 2022; Vatandoust and Diosady 2022). To absorb Fe from the digesta of FPP‐fortified foods in the intestine, FPP first needs to dissolve in gastric juice and Fe3+ ions and then needs to be reduced to the Fe2+ form (McKie et al. 2001). Due to this lower solubility, only approximately 50% of Fe of the added FPP is absorbed, resulting in a lower bioavailability (RBV) compared to FeSO4. To achieve the desired Fe fortification levels equivalent to FeSO4, FPP needs to be added at higher concentrations. Furthermore, to enhance absorption efficacy and RBV, reduction of FPP particle size (to increase surface area) is widely employed (Vatandoust and Diosady 2022). Commercially, three major types of FPP are available: regular FPP (20‐µm diameter), micronized ground FPP (2.5 µm), and micronized dispersible FPP (0.3 µm). Literature reports suggest that micronized ground FPP achieved approximately 70% RBV in rats (Moretti et al. 2006). Micronized dispersible FPP can be used in liquid products as agglomerates by incorporation into emulsions. Moreover, FPP has been reported as a successful Fe fortificant in extruded rice kernels without undesired color variations and has also been successfully used to fortify salt, margarine, chocolate drink powders, yogurt drinks, bouillon cubes, and complementary foods containing fruits and vegetables (Andersson et al. 2010; Hurrell 2021).

Elemental Iron Powders

Another Fe form used for fortification is powdered elemental iron (∼96% Fe), which can be manufactured by different processes, such as H‐reduction, CO‐reduction, atomization, and electrolytic and carbonyl processes (Gaucheron 2000; Hurrell 2002). The manufacturing process, particle size, shape, surface area, porosity, purity of the powder, composition of the diet, and physiological state of the individual's gut can greatly affect the solubility and absorption of these elemental iron powders (Lynch, Bothwell, and Powders 2007). The suitability and levels of different iron powders for Fe fortification in terms of efficacy, absorption and bioavailability have been broadly discussed over the years (Hurrell 2002; Mannar and Hurrell 2018). Consequently, the Sharing United States Technology to Aid Improvement of Nutrition (SUSTAIN) Task Force gathered expertise in the Fe fortification and nutrition sector in 2000 and reviewed available studies, including those using animal and human models (Hurrell et al. 2002). As outcomes of this review, among all iron powders, electrolytic iron has been recommended as the only iron powder that sufficiently fulfills human bioavailability needs (RBV 75%). Due to lower bioavailability, atomized (24% RBV) and CO‐reduced (12%–32% RBV) iron powders were not recommended. Although H‐reduced or atomized iron powders have been commonly used commercially, they were not recommended by the SUSTAIN task force at the time of the review due to the lack of available research studies (Hurrell et al. 2002). Low manufacturing cost and minimal sensory alteration during prolonged storage qualify elemental Fe sources as effective candidates for Fe fortification. Therefore, they have been widely used in cereal flour and cereal‐based functional food fortification (Allen et al. 2006; Kaur et al. 2022).

Iron Ethylenediaminetetraacetic Acid (Fe‐EDTA)

Fe‐EDTA (13% Fe) is a water soluble hexacoordinate complexing agent that can form covalent bonds with metal ions through four carboxylate and two tertiary amino groups, creating an octahedral complex. Among a variety of metal ions, including calcium, magnesium, sodium, zinc and copper, Fe3+ ions have been reported to have the highest stability constant (log k = 25.1). In addition to the stability constant, factors such as the molar ratio of EDTA: metal ions, pH, and the presence of competing complex‐forming ligands and ions can affect the efficacy of Fe‐EDTA complex formation (Bothwell and MacPhail 2004). Fe‐EDTA exhibits optimal binding affinity under acidic pH conditions, during which the complex remains stable (Sunda and Huntsman 2003). Owing to its higher stability constant and higher binding affinity at low pH conditions, Fe‐EDTA remains firmly bound under acidic conditions, preventing the formation of insoluble Fe–inhibitor complexes. In contrast, as the pH increases in the duodenum (∼pH 5), compared to the stomach, the binding affinity and stability of the Fe‐EDTA complex decrease, allowing Fe to be released for absorption (Heimbach et al. 2000). The free EDTA ligand may subsequently chelate other metal ions, which are then excreted from the body (Bothwell and MacPhail 2004; Mannar and Hurrell 2018). However, EDTA has not been reported to adversely affect the absorption efficiency of other minerals or trace elements (Davidsson et al. 1998; Kibangou et al. 2008). Due to its effective inhibitory capability against dietary inhibitors such as phytic acids, EDTA is recommended as an effective fortificant in phytic‐rich food components, including cereals and pulse products (WHO 2022). Although it is more expensive than FeSO4, Fe‐EDTA has been reported to enhance Fe absorption in food matrices containing dietary inhibitors by twofold to fourfold. In the absence of dietary inhibitors, Fe‐EDTA provides absorption similar to FeSO4 (Fidler et al. 2003; Vatandoust and Diosady 2022). Furthermore, Fe‐EDTA does not impact the flavor properties of the food matrix through lipid oxidation (Kaur et al. 2022). However, it may cause undesirable color changes in certain food matrices (Mannar and Hurrell 2018). Moreover, Fe‐EDTA acts as a promising Fe fortificant in fish sauce and soy sauce products, as it inhibits peptide precipitation (Hurrell 2002; Fidler et al. 2006).

Iron Complexes With Proteins, Carbohydrates, and MRPs

Fortification of food systems with inorganic Fe salts may cause numerous quality and nutritional issues. For example, it can induce lipid peroxidation, leading to changes in flavor, color, and appearance‐related sensory attributes, reduced solubility and bioavailability under physiological pH conditions, and in some cases, adverse effects such as vomiting and gastric mucosal inflammation (Gao et al. 2018; Kazemi‐Taskooh and Varidi 2021). To mitigate these adverse effects, promising approaches are being explored to ensure higher bioavailability and to enhance the quality of Fe‐fortified food products. Fe‐fortified chelate complexes are considered potential carriers for Fe across the human GI tract, as they possess favorable properties, including low toxicity, amphiphilicity, high nutritional value, and higher solubility and bioavailability, by minimizing Fe binding to dietary inhibitors owing to their presence as Fe–ligand complexes (Caetano‐Silva et al. 2018; Hu et al. 2023). Fe–protein‐based ligands, Fe–carbohydrate‐based ligands, and Fe–MRP‐based ligands are considered some of the viable fortification carriers for Fe.

Iron Fortification via Amino Acid‐ and Protein‐Based Carriers

Anionic carboxyl groups (─COO−), neutral amine groups (─NH2), and polar carbonyl groups (─CO) present in the protein structure are the major chelating sites for cationic Fe ions (Y. Wang et al. 2022). Fe–amino acid and Fe–protein chelation are pH‐dependent processes governed by the pK a values of coordinating functional groups. At low pH (below the pK a of carboxyl groups ∼2–4), protonation favors neutral ─COOH and cationic ─NH3 + forms, limiting Fe binding. In contrast, at higher pH, deprotonation restores negative charges on carboxylates (─COO−), enhancing the binding between carboxyl groups and Fe molecules. Simultaneously, amine groups (pK a ∼9–10) act as donor ligands, thereby enhancing Fe chelation through covalent or coordination bonds (S. Li et al. 2025). Fe–protein complex formation can be affected by pH, ionic strength, holding time and temperature (Sugiarto, Ye, and Singh 2009). Moreover, Fe–protein chelates form precipitates under acidic pH (∼2–4) in the stomach and avoid the release of Fe, which may prevent Fe‐dietary inhibitor interactions. Under intestinal alkaline pH, these complexes re‐solubilize, and soluble Fe can be absorbed by the human intestine (Shilpashree et al. 2018).

Moreover, the complexation stability of different amino acids varies with the source of Fe salt (Flynn et al. 1984). Protein hydrolysate (peptides and free amino acids) is reported as a promising carrier for Fe ions because hydrolysis exposes more Fe‐binding sites and increases the proportion of terminal amino and carboxyl groups (Y. Li et al. 2017; S. Li et al. 2025). Carboxyl groups of glutamic acid and aspartic acid, hydroxyl residues of serine, and the imidazole nitrogen ring of histidine and the ε‐amino group of lysine are considered potential Fe‐binding sites present in peptides (Figure 1b), and the chelation activity of peptides depends on their molecular weight, peptide structure, amino acid composition and steric effects (Y. Wang et al. 2022; Hu et al. 2023). Free amino acid forms of phenylalanine, methionine, and glycine are also capable of forming complexes with Fe through coordination links (Figure 1a) and improving the solubility and bioavailability of Fe compared to inorganic Fe salts (Hu et al. 2023). Ma et al. (2013) stated that the absorption rate of Fe–glycine chelates was reported to be two times higher than that of FeSO4.

Moreover, a range of protein sources (Ellis et al. 2013) and peptides (Caetano‐Silva et al. 2021) derived from a variety of food matrices have been reported to be effective for Fe chelation (Table 3). Among these, dairy proteins with Fe‐binding properties are considered potential Fe chelators (Vegarud et al. 2000). Hydrolysis of whey protein isolates (WPIs) into small peptides (< 5 kDa) using pancreatin, Alcalase, and Flavourzyme significantly increased the dialyzable Fe content, a simple in vitro indicator of potential Fe absorption, compared with FeSO4. This effect is likely due to the ability of whey protein (WP)‐derived peptides to form Fe–chelate complexes, thereby enhancing Fe stability and solubility (de la Hoz et al. 2014). Similarly, Ding et al. (2024) reported that WPI has the potential to chelate Fe, and with increasing Fe concentration (0.33–2.25 mmol/g protein), the available chelation sites in WPI can become saturated. Another study demonstrated that four peptide derivatives of caseins hydrolyzed by trypsin, CHP‐1, CHP‐2, CHP‐3, and CHP‐4, corresponding to αs1, αs2, and two β‐casein sequences, respectively, achieved Fe chelation rates of 11.1%, 8.0%, 7.6% and 59.8%, respectively, confirming their capacity to chelate Fe ions (Miao et al. 2019). Bouhallab and Bougle (2004) further reported that casein phosphopeptidess derived from β‐casein can bind 4–5 Fe ions, improving both affinity and Fe absorption and bioavailability compared with ferrous gluconate and FeSO4. In addition, chemically modified proteins such as succinylated caseins have shown enhanced potential for Fe–chelate formation (Caetano‐Silva et al. 2021). Succinylation introduces an additional carboxylic group to the casein molecule, which has been reported to significantly improve Fe binding, Fe retention and bioavailability compared to inorganic Fe salts and Fe–caseinate complexes (Shilpashree et al. 2018).

The addition of Fe complexing agents together with proteins also affects the physicochemical properties of the resulting complexes. For example, Mittal et al. (2018) reported that 3% (w/v) sodium caseinate forms Fe–caseinate precipitates when the Fe concentration exceeds 5 mM. They also stated that adding variable concentrations of orthophosphate (16–64 mM) causes redispersion of these casein precipitates. This redispersion improves Fe binding up to 3–4 mol per 1 mol of casein, owing to the negative charge of the orthophosphate molecules. Furthermore, Fe–protein chelates have been reported to possess antioxidant potential and mitigate lipid oxidation (Caetano‐Silva et al. 2021). Fortification using Fe–protein complexes improves Fe solubility and bioavailability while preventing lipid oxidation, thereby enhancing both the nutritional quality and functional properties of food compared with inorganic Fe salts.

Iron Fortification via Carbohydrate‐Based Carriers

Fe–saccharide complexes are also considered promising approaches to Fe fortification of dairy matrices, owing to their greater solubility, improved oxidative stability, enhanced bioavailability, and reduced cytotoxicity and side effects compared to inorganic Fe salts (Zhang et al. 2012; Gao et al. 2018; Kumari et al. 2023). Carboxyl, hydroxyl, and sulfate groups located in saccharide structures can interact with both Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions. The chelation capability and properties of the Fe–saccharide complex vary with the monosaccharide composition, molecular weight, functional group content and viscosity of the saccharide compound (Chi et al. 2018; Kazemi‐Taskooh and Varidi 2021; Hu et al. 2023). These Fe–saccharide complexes exhibit rapid release of bound Fe during intestinal digestion, while under gastric conditions, they may release a considerable proportion of bound Fe. Churio and Valenzuela (2018) reported that 32%–37% of bound Fe from FeSO4 encapsulated by maltodextrin microparticles was released at the end of gastric digestion, while at the end of the intestinal phase, 100% of bound Fe was released. Furthermore, within the initial 90 min of the intestinal phase, 80%–85% of bound Fe was reported to be released from the encapsulated microparticles. To improve Fe bioavailability and product quality in Fe–saccharide complexes, in addition to simple Fe–saccharide complexes such as lactose, sucrose, and gluconate (Figure 1c), a variety of complex Fe–polysaccharide complexes have been studied and developed (Sharma et al. 2017).

Zhang et al. (2012) reported that the low molecular weight soluble sulfated polysaccharide porphyran, extracted from the red algae Porphyra, exhibits enhanced stability under a wide range of processing conditions (50°C–235°C) and during storage when fortified with Fe. Moreover, this compound remains stable across a broad range of pH values (3–12), whereas inorganic FeCl3 and FeSO4 are stable only within pH ranges of 2.3–6.8 and 2.6–6.3, respectively (Zhang et al. 2012). Acidic polysaccharide extracted from soybean (soluble soybean polysaccharide [SSPS]) has been reported to form coordination links with Fe3+ via its hydroxyl, carboxyl and amino groups, which result in Fe complexes (Gao et al. 2018). To mitigate Fe release in the stomach and prevent the formation of insoluble Fe complexes, a pectin–Fe complex was developed from Assam lemon. This complex exhibited higher thermal stability, higher bio‐accessibility and improved absorption compared to conventional citrus pectin (Singhal et al. 2024). The nanoencapsulation of iron sulfate into citrus pectin resulted in an Fe loading of 7.5%, exhibiting an initial burst release at pH 2, followed by a more sustained release at higher pH values of 6 and 7.4 (Gholam Jamshidi et al. 2023). Minzanova et al. (2015) reported the capability of pectin to reduce Fe3+ to Fe2+ while enhancing hemoglobin growth in rats compared to inorganic Fe salts. Furthermore, the formation of Fe–polysaccharide gel complexes has also been investigated in several studies (Table 3), including Fe gels formed in the presence of polygalacturonate (Du Poset et al. 2018), alginate (Perez‐Moral et al. 2013), chitosan (Duffy et al. 2018), and oligosaccharides (He et al. 2018). In dairy‐based systems, lactose has been reported to form soluble Fe complexes that are stable, highly digestible bioavailable and less toxic than inorganic Fe salts (Sharma et al. 2017). Kumari et al. (2023) studied a novel soluble exopolysaccharide derived from Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus strain Kar1, which forms complexes with Fe through sulfate, carboxyl, and hydroxyl groups. This complex exhibited enhanced water‐holding capacity and viscosity with high stability and improved emulsifying properties in dairy‐based systems.

Iron Complexation With MRPs

The Maillard reaction (MR) is a nonenzymatic browning reaction that is initiated between a carbonyl compound from a reducing sugar and an amine group from an amino acid, a peptide or a protein. The MR occurs as a complex series of reactions and is divided into three stages: initial stage, intermediate stage and final stage (Figure 2) (Sunds et al. 2018). At the initial stage, sugar–amine condensation occurs in a reversible manner and produces colorless compounds. The amine group reacts with the carbonyl group of the open‐chained sugar in an equimolar ratio and forms an N‐substituted glycosylamine. This is followed by an isomerization referred to as Amadori rearrangement, producing Amadori products (APs), which can further undergo sugar dehydration and fragmentation to produce a range of intermediate compounds (Nursten 2005; Tamanna and Mahmood 2015).

Overview of the Maillard reaction process (Nursten 2005; Nooshkam et al. 2019; Hodge 1953).

In the intermediate stage, sugar dehydration, sugar fragmentation and amino acid degradation (Strecker degradation) can occur. Sugar dehydration is a pH‐dependent reaction. Under acidic pH conditions, with the loss of three water molecules and catalytic action of the amino acids, APs form furfural or hydroxymethyl furfural (HMF) (1,2‐enolization). At higher pH conditions, low water activity and higher amine concentrations, APs form reductones (2,3‐enolization), which may subsequently contribute to Strecker aldehyde and melanoidin formation (Nooshkam et al. 2019). In parallel, the AP can also undergo sugar fragmentation to generate fission products. These fission products, along with dehydroreductones from sugar dehydration, may undergo Strecker degradation, a reaction that is favored in the presence of α‐amino acids. This process produces Strecker aldehydes, which can further undergo condensation reactions with themselves, sugar fragments, dehydrated products, aldimines, and ketimines to form final‐stage MRPs or melanoidins. The type of α‐amino acid involved can alter the flavor profile of the final Strecker aldehydes (Hodge 1953; Cardoso 2023).

In the final stage of MR, via aldol condensation or aldehyde‐amine condensation, intermediates produced in the initial and intermediate stages polymerize and produce unsaturated, fluorescent, colored complex MRPs referred to as melanoidins. In aldol condensation, aldehydes are generated via sugar dehydration, sugar fission and Strecker degradation in the presence of amino compounds that react with each other to form melanoidin complexes. In contrast, in aldehyde‐amine condensation, simple aldehydes such as α/β‐unsaturated aldehydes and amines, including acetaldehyde and ammonia, react with each other at low temperatures and produce complex structural heterocyclic nitrogen melanoidin compounds (Hodge 1953; Nursten 2005; Cardoso 2023). The extent of the MR can be regulated by different factors, including pH, heating time and temperature, reactant type and concentration and water activity (Nooshkam et al. 2019; van Boekel 2001).

MRPs have the potential to chelate metal ions, including Fe2+ and Fe3+, and act as Fe carriers. Amino groups and carboxyl groups from the amino acids, as well as hydroxyl groups from the reducing sugars, form ligands with metal ions (Ramonaitytė et al. 2009; Zhou et al. 2024). Complexation of MRPs with Fe enhances the MR rate and induces nonenzymatic browning. A variety of ligands with different stabilities may result from Fe–MRP interactions depending on the Fe concentration, the presence of reactants in the mixture and the reaction conditions (O'Brien and Morrissey 1997; Nooshkam et al. 2019). Incorporation of Fe can enhance AP formation in the initial stage, promote the enolization of these APs into reactive intermediates, and ultimately lead to the formation of melanoidins. Maillard reaction intermediates (MRI) are reactive compounds formed during the initial and intermediate stages of the MR through degradation, dehydration and rearrangement reactions involving sugars and APs. These intermediates include a wide range of compounds, such as α‐dicarbonyl compounds, acrylamide, furans, pyrazines, and other reactive carbonyl species, which undergo further transformations, including condensation, polymerization, cyclization and rearrangement, to produce structurally complex MRPs (El Hosry et al. 2025). Based on their chemical nature and ability to interact with metal ions, MRIs have been categorized into four groups, namely, metal complexes with free amino acids and/or intact sugars; APs and their corresponding metal complexes; amino sugars and their corresponding metal complexes; and reactive sugar degradation products and their metal complexes. Furthermore, in the presence of Fe, not only reducing sugars such as glucose have the potential to glycate proteins, but smaller carbohydrate compounds such as glycolaldehyde, erythrose, and glyceraldehyde are also able to form MRIs (Ramonaitytė et al. 2009; Kim and Yaylayan 2021). Glycated proteins are reported to bind approximately three times higher levels of Fe than nonglycated proteins (Qian et al. 1998).

At the initial stage of the MR, APs have been reported to form chelates with Fe2+ (Figure 1d). Zhou et al. (2024) reported that AP, N‐(1‐deoxy‐D‐fructos‐1‐yl)‐amino acid forms complexes with Fe2+. The highest Fe chelation rates (∼85%–90%) were achieved by APs formed from basic amino acids such as arginine and histidine. This strong chelating ability is attributed to the guanidinium group in arginine and the imidazole group in histidine, both of which can donate electron density from nitrogen atoms to facilitate Fe binding. In contrast, only mild chelation rates are observed with aliphatic, aromatic, and polar amino acids due to the presence of weaker donor groups, while acidic amino acids exhibit the lowest Fe2+ chelation level (∼15%) due to their negatively charged side chains and oxygen donors (Zhou et al. 2024). In addition, Fe3+ can also be reduced to Fe2+ during the initial stage of the MR (Sakurai et al. 2007). MRs initiated with basic amino acids and those conducted with increasing concentrations of these basic amino acids have been reported to accelerate this reduction reaction (Zhou et al. 2024). Moreover, increased Fe concentrations (10, 20, and 50 mg Fe/L) have been reported to accelerate the MR and promote Fe incorporation during its initial stages. During the first hour of the experiment, the dialyzable Fe content increased with Fe concentration, reaching approximately 15, 18, and 23 mg/L, respectively (Ramonaitytė et al. 2009).

At the intermediate stage of the MR, both Fe2+ and Fe3+ have the potential to catalyze one of the enolization pathways and form corresponding Fe chelates. Fe2+ catalyzes the 1,2‐enolization pathway, whereas Fe3+ catalyzes the 2,3‐enolization pathway (Sakurai et al. 2007; Mossine and Mawhinney 2010). In the final stage of the MR, Fe2+ forms ligands with both high molecular weight (HMW) compounds and low molecular weight (LMW) compounds, with HMW compounds demonstrating the highest potential for Fe complex formation due to their higher content of electron‐donating groups and their extended structural conformation relative to LMW compounds. This chelation affinity is primarily due to the anionic nature of the melanoidins, arising from electron‐donating groups in their structures, including hydroxyl and pyrrole moieties (Figure 1e) (Nooshkam et al. 2019). Nevertheless, MRI that contribute to melanoidin formation, such as pyridine and pyrone, have also been reported to possess Fe‐chelating potential due to the presence of electron‐donating hydroxyl and ketone groups (Ramonaitytė et al. 2009; Nooshkam et al. 2019).

Fe–MRPs complex formation liberates proton ions (O'Brien and Morrissey 1997). Furthermore, these protein–sugar complexes have the capability to chelate Fe, shielding it from adverse conditions in the stomach, promoting controlled release of Fe, minimizing undesirable sensory alterations in the food matrix and enhancing both the bioavailability and stability of Fe (O'Brien and Morrissey 1997; Nooshkam et al. 2019; Hu et al. 2023). Despite the advantages of MRPs in Fe fortification, this area remains underexplored, and a considerable proportion of the interactions between MRPs and Fe compounds have yet to be investigated. In addition, the effect of Fe–MRP chelation on the bioavailability of Fe in dairy products has not yet been studied.

Bioavailability of Iron From Iron‐Fortified Dairy Products

Fe bioavailability is defined as the proportion of dietary Fe that is absorbed and utilized by the human body to support normal physiological functions, including hemoglobin synthesis and cellular metabolism (Hurrell and Egli 2010). In addition to the form of Fe used (Table 2), numerous host‐related and dietary factors have been reported to influence the absorption and overall bioavailability of ingested Fe. These include the Fe status of the host, certain genetic disorders and the composition of the diet (Figure 3), all of which can modify Fe bioavailability (Hurrell et al. 2002). In nutritionally deficient populations suffering from Fe inadequacy, higher Fe absorption and bioavailability have been reported, as their intestinal membrane receptors are upregulated, thereby enhancing Fe uptake from fortified diets (Sachdeva, Kaushik, Arora, and Kapila 2015). This Fe uptake can be further improved by the presence of dietary enhancers, such as AA, citrate or vitamin A. AA in particular is reported to reduce Fe3+ to Fe2+, thereby facilitating intestinal Fe absorption. Zhu et al. (2009); Pizarro et al. (2015) reported that the addition of AA to Fe‐fortified dairy products resulted in a 2‐3‐fold increase in Fe absorption and bioavailability in both in vivo and in vitro experimental settings. Conversely, dietary inhibitors such as phytates and tannic acids commonly present in plant‐based sources have a negative impact on Fe absorption and bioavailability by forming highly stable Fe chelates that hinder uptake by intestinal epithelial cells. Other divalent metal ions, including Zn2+ and Mn2+, compete with Fe2+ for entry into enterocytes via divalent metal transporter (DMT‐1) membrane proteins. Since both Zn2+ and Fe2+ share DMT‐1 for absorption, the inhibitory effect of Zn2+ on Fe uptake is more pronounced than that of other divalent metal ions (Hurrell et al. 2002; Zhu et al. 2009). Although DMT‐1 does not regulate Ca2+ uptake (Shawki and Mackenzie 2010), Ca2+ acts as a noncompetitive reversible inhibitor of Fe uptake. Ca2+ ions bind to the noncatalytic allosteric site present on DMT‐1 regardless of Fe binding, thereby forming catalytically inactive DMT‐1 complexes (Shawki and Mackenzie 2010; Cegarra et al. 2022). Owing to this noncompetitive inhibitory mechanism, the amount of elemental Ca2+ naturally present in bovine milk (∼450 mg/355 mL) is sufficient to exert an inhibitory effect on Fe absorption in the human intestine (Chon et al. 2018). Consistent with this mechanism, Hallberg et al. (1992a) observed a lower Fe absorption from bovine milk (19.5%) compared to human milk (48%) when consumed as a 200 mL milk portion by nine healthy individuals. Furthermore, they demonstrated that increasing the calcium content of human milk using calcium chloride to a level corresponding to that of bovine milk reduced Fe absorption from 39.1% to 21.3%, further confirming the inhibitory effect of calcium on Fe absorption (Hallberg et al. 1992a). Similar observations were reported by Walczyk et al. (2014), where the addition of 100 mg of Ca2+ to a fortified casein/whey‐based drink reduced Fe absorption by 18% in Fe‐repleted school children in India, and increasing Ca2+ to 200 mg further decreased Fe absorption by 27%. Importantly, this study also demonstrated that the addition of dietary enhancers such as AA could mitigate this inhibitory effect and improve Fe uptake from fortified foods. Moreover, Saarinen et al. (1977) also reported that 6‐month‐old infants absorbed 49% of the Fe administered during breast feeding, whereas only 10% Fe absorption was observed in cow‐milk‐fed infants under similar conditions. In addition, several other clinical trials have also reported the inhibitory effects of calcium on Fe absorption in vivo in human experimental settings (Hallberg et al. 1992b; Deehr et al. 1990). Beyond the factors discussed, the influence of prebiotics, probiotics and MRPs present in dairy foods on Fe absorption and bioavailability has been debated over the years (Sarria and Vaquero 2004; Silva et al. 2008; Christides et al. 2018; Scheuchzer et al. 2024).

Effect of Prebiotics and Probiotics on Iron Absorption

Dietary prebiotics are compounds that resist digestion and absorption in the human GI tract, withstand stomach acidity, and can be fermented by gut microbiota to selectively promote the growth of beneficial intestinal microorganisms and produce vital metabolites such as short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs) (Apte et al. 2025). Most dietary prebiotics are carbohydrate‐derived, including fructans, galacto‐oligosaccharides (GOS), glucose‐derived oligosaccharides (polydextrose), and pectin, while certain compounds, such as cocoa‐derived flavanols, are also classified as noncarbohydrate dietary prebiotic sources (Davani‐Davari et al. 2019). Microorganisms that are alive and, when ingested in adequate amounts, confer beneficial effects on host health are referred to as probiotics (Apte et al. 2025). Commonly administered probiotics include Lactobacillus, Bifidobacteria, and yeast species (Obayomi et al. 2024). Fe absorption may vary depending on the type of prebiotic or probiotic strain, the administered dose, and the duration of exposure (Scheuchzer et al. 2024). The effect of incorporating prebiotics and probiotics into dairy products on Fe absorption and bioavailability has been studied extensively in both in vivo and in vitro experimental settings. However, the findings remain inconsistent.

Studies using radiotracers have demonstrated that 60%–80% of absorbed Fe is taken up within the first 2–4 h of digestion, primarily in the duodenum of the small intestine, whereas the remaining Fe is absorbed more gradually over the subsequent 22–24 h, predominantly in the colon and cecum (Mikulic et al. 2021). Scheuchzer et al. (2024) reported that single‐dose consumption of human‐identical milk oligosaccharides had negligible effects on Fe absorption in Fe‐fortified infant follow‐up formula (8–14 months), showing 8.6% of Fe absorption compared with 8.1% for the reference nonprebiotic and probiotic formula, in a study where the majority of participants were nonanemic. In contrast, Mohammed et al. (2021) reported that anemic rats fed long‐ and short‐chain inulin–ferric sulfate biofortified yogurt for 4 weeks exhibited significant improvement in hemoglobin, hematocrit, and red blood cell counts compared with nonanemic rats or those fed FeSO4 alone. Moreover, long‐chain inulin complexes were observed to be more effective than short‐chain forms. In the context of selective stimulation, long‐term consumption of prebiotic compounds, rather than single‐dose intake, can increase the population of colonic microbiota and lower the physiological pH in the colon through microbial fermentation of these prebiotics, producing SCFAs such as propionate and butyrate, as well as metabolic acids including lactic acid. This enhanced the acidic environment in the large intestine compared to the small intestine and improved the solubility of poorly water‐soluble Fe sources, such as ferrous fumarate, thereby promoting Fe absorption. For instance, Paganini et al. (2017) investigated the effect of GOS incorporated into a micronutrient powder (MNP) containing different Fe sources, including isotopically labeled ferrous fumarate (57FeFum combined with Na58FeEDTA) and ferrous sulfate (54FeSO4), with and without GOS. Their findings demonstrated that the inclusion of GOS enhanced Fe absorption in infants, highlighting the importance of both the prebiotic‐induced colonic environment and interactions between Fe compounds and other nutrients within the MNP matrix. Prolonged prebiotic consumption may also modulate gene expression, enhancing the expression of DMT‐1, ferritin, and transferrin receptors that regulate Fe absorption while reducing intestinal inflammation that triggers hepcidin secretion, which otherwise inhibits Fe absorption. Furthermore, interactions between prebiotic–Fe complexes such as inulin–Fe and the intestinal mucosa may prolong the residence time of Fe within the GI tract, enhancing its absorption (Petry et al. 2012; Marciano et al. 2015; Mikulic et al. 2021; Scheuchzer et al. 2024). In the context of probiotics, they may enhance Fe absorption by lowering the pH in the large intestine through fermentation metabolites such as lactic acid and other organic acids, thereby increasing the soluble Fe available for absorption, similar to prebiotics. In addition, probiotic bacteria can mitigate intestinal inflammation and reduce hepcidin circulation, further promoting Fe absorption (Silva et al. 2008; Sazawal et al. 2010).

Although several studies have reported enhanced Fe absorption following the consumption of prebiotics and probiotics in vitro (Laparra et al. 2008) and in vivo (animal model) (Marciano et al. 2015; Mohammed et al. 2021) experimental settings with dairy products, human studies have generally indicated that Fe absorption is not significantly affected by prebiotic or probiotic consumption (Petry et al. 2012; Agustina et al. 2013). This discrepancy may be attributed to the proportion of Fe absorption occurring in the small intestine versus the large intestine. Fe absorption in the large intestine is considerably lower than that in the small intestine, and quantifying its contribution using conventional markers, such as erythrocyte incorporation, is challenging and often yields insignificant results in humans (Petry et al. 2012). In addition, the majority of reported studies were conducted in infants, children, or women, whose Fe requirements and mobilization from body stores are comparatively high due to physiological events such as growth or menstruation, leaving hematologic indicators at borderline levels without significant changes (Silva et al. 2008; Agustina et al. 2013). Christides et al. (2018), in an in vitro study using Caco‐2 cells and milk‐based young child formulas fortified with Fe–fructo‐oligosaccharides (FOS) and Fe–GOS (total 1200 mg/100 mL) along with AA (15 mg/100 mL), reported a 1.5‐ to 2.3‐fold increase in Fe absorption compared with a diet without prebiotics or with lower AA concentrations (10–12 mg/100 mL). Furthermore, this study demonstrated that only the combined effect of both prebiotics and AA significantly increased Fe absorption, whereas each compound alone did not exert a notable effect (Christides et al. 2018). A human clinical study conducted by Weinborn et al. (2017) reported that nonheme Fe bioavailability was not affected by the consumption of 2 g of a prebiotic mix containing inulin, polydextrose, gum arabic, and guar gum in 200 mL of yogurt over 12 days. In contrast, heme Fe bioavailability increased by 56% when yogurt fortified with the same prebiotic mix was consumed compared with nonfortified yogurt. The authors suggested that this effect may be attributable to lowered pH conditions in the colon and increased release of bound Fe (Weinborn et al. 2017).

Silva et al. (2008) reported that a Lactobacillus acidophilus‐incorporated, Fe–amino acid chelate‐fortified milk beverage was administered to children aged 2–5 years for 101 days. The probiotic‐fortified milk resulted in increased red blood cell saturation, reduced hypochromia, and improvements in weight and height, although no significant differences were observed in other Fe status indicators, including serum ferritin levels, mean cell volume, and mean cell hemoglobin levels. Moreover, a considerable increase in hemoglobin levels was reported in the probiotic group (38.2%) compared with the control group (30.2%). In another study, 5 × 108 CFU/day of Lactobacillus casei CRL 431 or 5 × 108 CFU/day of Lactobacillus reuteri DSM 17938, along with regular calcium content (440 mg/day), were incorporated into bovine milk and administered to children aged 1–6 years for 6 months (Agustina et al. 2013). The effects of these probiotics on Fe status and anthropometric parameters were evaluated. The study reported that both probiotic groups had a positive impact on weight gain. However, both experimental groups exhibited similar effects on Fe status compared with the regular calcium group and were associated with IDA, as indicated by reduced hematologic parameters (Agustina et al. 2013).

When both prebiotics and probiotics are incorporated into a food system in combination, they are referred to as synbiotics (Scheuchzer et al. 2024). A study involving children aged 1–4 years, in which Fe‐fortified milk with added Bifidobacterium lactis HN019 (probiotic) and a prebiotic oligosaccharide (1.7 × 107 CFU and 2.4 g/day, respectively) was administered for 1 year, resulted in a 45% reduction in anemia prevalence and a 0.13 kg/year increase in weight. However, individual Fe status indicators, including hemoglobin, serum ferritin, and serum transferrin, did not show significant improvement compared with the reference milk that did not contain prebiotics or probiotics. However, the consumption of milk fortified with probiotics and prebiotics reduced the prevalence of Fe deficiency in preschoolers (Sazawal et al. 2010). Scheuchzer et al. (2024) also reported that the synbiotic combination of GOS (4 mg/mL) and Limosilactobacillus reuteri (107 CFU/day) in follow‐up infant formula did not significantly affect Fe absorption (8.2%) compared with the control formula (8.1%). Another study incorporated two Bifidobacterium species, B. bifidum and B. longum, along with GOS, or the synbiotic combination of bifidobacteria and GOS in milk‐based infant formulas and administered them to weanling rats over 30 days to assess apparent Fe absorption, retention, and excretion via fecal and urinary output. This study reported improvements in apparent absorption and retention across all three test periods, with 10% prebiotic and synbiotic diets resulting in significant enhancement in each period (Pérez‐Conesa et al. 2007). Owing to their generally insignificant effects on Fe absorption and bioavailability in humans, the incorporation of prebiotics and probiotics into dairy products as a strategy to prevent IDA cannot be recommended universally. Further research on the effects of the addition of probiotics and prebiotics to Fe‐fortified food products is required to understand their effects on Fe markers and IDA (Sazawal et al. 2010).

Effect of MRPs in the Diet on Iron Absorption

MRPs act as anionic polymers capable of strongly chelating Fe, forming highly stable complexes relative to other metal chelates. The MR generates both LMW soluble compounds and advanced HMW insoluble compounds, which can form Fe complexes that are either absorbable or nonabsorbable in the human intestine (Delgado‐Andrade et al. 2008; Tamanna and Mahmood 2015). The impact of MRPs on Fe bioavailability is highly dependent on their solubility, molecular weight, and processing conditions. For example, an in vivo study reported reduced Fe digestibility in rats fed in‐bottle sterilized infant formula compared with the powdered milk formula, likely due to the increased production of insoluble, advanced MRPs that generate nonabsorbable Fe–MRP complexes (Sarria and Vaquero 2004). An in vitro assessment using Caco‐2 cells demonstrated that a glucose–lysine system heated at 150°C for 30 min produced predominantly soluble MRPs, enhancing Fe uptake, whereas extended heating at 150°C to 90 min resulted in insoluble melanoidins and lower Fe solubility (Delgado‐Andrade et al. 2004). Consumption of MRP‐rich brown diets by rats and male adolescents also led to reduced apparent Fe absorption and digestibility due to the reduced Fe solubility under intestinal conditions, consistent with the inhibitory effects of insoluble MRPs (García et al. 2009).

Under certain conditions, MRPs can enhance Fe bioavailability. LMW, soluble MRPs improve Fe solubility in the gut and act as carriers, facilitating Fe absorption (Delgado‐Andrade et al. 2004). In addition, MRPs can be fermented by gut microbiota to produce SCFAs, lowering colonic pH, promoting Fe3+ → Fe2+ reduction, and increasing Fe solubility and bioavailability. Heat‐processed heme and nonheme foods have been reported to contain higher MRP contents and exhibit improved Fe bioavailability in the lower intestine (Garcia‐Conde et al. 2025). Overall, the effect of MRPs on Fe bioavailability is determined by their molecular weight, solubility, and processing conditions. Soluble, LMW MRPs generally enhance Fe absorption, whereas insoluble, HMW melanoidins tend to inhibit it. Despite several in vivo and in vitro studies that have investigated the impact of MRPs on Fe bioavailability in various food systems (Delgado‐Andrade et al. 2004, 2016), the effects of MRPs on Fe bioavailability in dairy‐based systems remain underexplored, with only a few reports available (Sarria and Vaquero 2004).

Challenges for Iron Fortification in Dairy‐Based Systems

Dairy products are widely utilized as fortification vehicles due to their global consumption, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries (Siddique and Park 2019). The development of dairy‐based functional foods has gained considerable prominence within the dairy industry, as these products offer enhanced overall performance, including consumer acceptability, extended shelf life, and additional nutritional benefits compared with conventional liquid dairy systems (Bhat and Bhat 2010; Jalili 2016). Interactions between Fe and the dairy matrix can influence the carrier food matrix and its properties, either positively or negatively. Such interactions may modify casein and WP structures, alter salt equilibria, and accelerate lipid oxidation. Consequently, the physicochemical, structural, functional, and processing characteristics of the dairy matrix, including the secondary structure of proteins, texture, syneresis, and sensory attributes, may be affected. The extent of these effects and the quality of the final product depend on the Fe source and form, the incorporated amount, and the type of functional dairy product (Santillán‐Urquiza et al. 2017; Siddique and Park 2019).

Effect on Physiochemical and Structural Properties

When Fe is added to milk, ∼87% is distributed within the skim milk phase, while ∼12% is associated with the cream phase. Of the protein‐bound Fe, the majority (∼64%) binds to casein, with the remainder (∼23%) associating with WPs (Sachdeva, Kaushik, Arora, and Indumathi 2015). Fe predominantly interacts with casein molecules via clusters of phosphorylated serine (SerP) residues, which contain two, three, or four SerP units present in the casein micelles (Mittal et al. 2016). To a lesser extent, Fe also interacts with tryptophan and tyrosine residues in αs‐ and β‐caseins, as well as cysteine residues in κ‐casein (Sadiq et al. 2021). In SerP residues, Fe forms coordination bonds with oxygen atoms. Within the colloidal casein fraction, Fe binding is predominantly associated with αs1‐casein, followed by β‐casein and κ‐casein, which contribute approximately 72%, 21%, and 4% of the bound Fe, respectively (Sachdeva, Kaushik, Arora, and Indumathi 2015; Jalili 2016). These differences in Fe–casein binding reflect the relative abundance of SerP residues within each casein type, with αs1‐casein containing 8–9 SerP residues, β‐casein containing 4–5 SerP residues, and κ‐casein containing only one SerP residue (Hekmat and McMahon 1998). Accordingly, Fe–casein interactions are strongly governed by the type of casein molecule, its degree of phosphorylation, and its net charge (Broyard and Gaucheron 2015). Although the oxidation state of Fe does not significantly influence its overall distribution among milk proteins, it may affect binding affinity. Additional factors, including the Fe oxidation state (Fe2+ vs. Fe3+), the chemical form and particle size of Fe, added Fe concentration, counter‐ion type, and environmental pH, further modulate Fe–casein interactions, thereby affecting milk protein structure, mineral equilibria, matrix pH, thermal stability, texture, and sensory attributes of dairy products (Gaucheron 2000; Raouche, Dobenesque, et al. 2009). Raouche, Naille, et al. (2009) reported that increasing the added Fe concentration in milk from 2 to 20 mmol/kg resulted in increased colloidal Fe incorporation owing to the presence of excess free Fe present in the serum phase, which can facilitate colloidal Fe phosphate formation.

The partitioning of the added Fe between the casein micelle and the serum phase is significantly affected by pH. At the native milk pH of 6.7, approximately 67% of calcium is present within the casein micelle core as colloidal calcium phosphate (Holt and Carver 2024). As the pH decreases, organic and inorganic phosphate groups, including SerP residues and carboxylic groups of casein molecules, become increasingly protonated, thereby reducing the binding affinity of calcium for these sites. Moreover, mineral equilibria within the aqueous phase are disturbed, as calcium phosphate salts undergo dissociation in response to decreasing pH. To maintain equilibrium, micellar calcium phosphate progressively dissolves, and at approximately pH 5.2, a portion of calcium and the majority of the phosphate have been reported to dissociate from the casein micellar structure (Broyard and Gaucheron 2015). These two phenomena enhance the solubilization of colloidal calcium into the serum phase and reduce competition between Fe and calcium for SerP binding, thereby maximizing Fe incorporation (Figure 4) into casein micelle proteins as Fe phosphate (Raouche, Dobenesque, et al. 2009; Raouche, Naille, et al. 2009). From a practical perspective, these pH‐dependent changes are highly relevant to fermented dairy processing, where acidification during fermentation (e.g., yogurt and cultured milk production) reduces the pH to approximately 4.0–4.6. Under these conditions, the dissociation of micellar calcium phosphate and reduced mineral competition can promote greater incorporation of Fe into the casein matrix. This suggests that fermentation processes may enhance Fe retention and distribution within dairy systems (Drago and Valencia 2006).

Schematic diagram of casein micelle structural changes owing to iron fortification.

Of the incorporated Fe ions, a significantly lower proportion (26.4%) is bound to WP compared with the casein‐bound fraction (73.6%) on a mass basis, primarily reflecting the higher abundance of casein in milk. α‐lactalbumin (α‐La) and β‐lactoglobulin (β‐Lg) bind approximately 6 and 3.5 Fe2+ ions per WP molecule, respectively, primarily through carboxylic groups located on the side chains of glutamic and aspartic acid residues. The comparatively lower extent of Fe complexation with WP can be attributed to several factors, including the absence of molecular structures with high Fe‐binding affinity, such as clusters of SerP residues, and the fact that, unlike the open and flexible structure of caseinates, WPs possess compact, globular conformations (Sugiarto, Ye, and Singh 2009). The native compact globular structures of WPs, which are stabilized by intramolecular disulfide bonds, may restrict the accessibility of iron‐binding residues, thereby limiting Fe interactions (Cruz‐Huerta et al. 2016). In contrast to Fe salts, Fe–chelate complexes tend to modify the milk colloidal phase to a lesser extent under comparable conditions. Instead, Fe–chelate complexes influence the physicochemical and structural properties of dairy products through mechanisms that depend on the equilibrium constant between the Fe and the chelate, its concentration, and the system pH (Raouche, Dobenesque, et al. 2009; Banjare et al. 2019).

pH

The pH values of inorganic Fe‐fortified dairy matrices can be slightly reduced due to the inherent acidity of the Fe solutions and the interactions between Fe and casein micelles, wherein protons are released upon binding of Fe to casein (Gaucheron 2000). Several studies have demonstrated that water‐soluble Fe salts, including FeSO4, ferric ammonium sulfate, ammonium ferrous sulfate, FPP‐soluble ions, and ferrous gluconate hydrate, induce a slight reduction in pH when incorporated into milk (Kim et al. 2002; Sachdeva, Kaushik, Arora, and Indumathi 2015; Dabour et al. 2019; Darwish et al. 2021; Gandhi et al. 2022). Moreover, the type of counter‐ion also affects the extent of pH decline in dairy matrices. The relative basicity of the accompanying anion governs the degree to which the acidic properties of Fe ions are expressed. Very weakly basic anions such as chloride allow Fe ions to fully express their acidic character, resulting in a pronounced pH reduction. In contrast, highly basic anions such as gluconate and lactate undergo partial hydrolysis, which attenuates Fe‐induced proton release and consequently reduces the magnitude of pH decline (Y. Li et al. 2026). Nkhata et al. (2015) reported that yogurt fortified with FeSO4 reached the target pH of 4.6 within approximately 4.5 h, whereas yogurts fortified with ferrous lactate and ferrous bisglycinate required more than 6.5 h to attain the same pH value. Similarly, Y. Li et al. (2026) reported a concentration‐dependent pH reduction when increasing the Fe concentration in Fe‐fortified skim milk from 1 to 15 mmol/L. Moreover, at equivalent Fe concentrations, the final pH values followed the order ferrous gluconate > ferrous lactate > ferrous chloride owing to the basicity of the accompanying anions.

In contrast, Fe–chelates, including Fe–casein and Fe–WP complexes, as well as encapsulated Fe compounds, do not induce significant reductions in pH. This behavior is primarily attributed to the limited availability of free, exchangeable Fe ions in the dairy matrix, as Fe is tightly bound within protein complexes or physically entrapped within encapsulating materials. Consequently, interactions between Fe and casein micelles are minimized, reducing Fe‐induced hydrolysis reactions and proton release and thereby preserving the pH stability of the dairy system (Kim et al. 2002; Darwish et al. 2021).

Zeta Potential

A proportion of the incorporated Fe ions interact with negatively charged casein micelles, particularly glycosylated κ‐casein, which is located at the surface of the casein micelle (Broyard and Gaucheron 2015). These enhanced Fe interactions with negatively charged casein micelles shift their surface charge toward neutralization, substantially reducing the net negative charge of the casein micelle surface. This reduction in surface charge decreases electrostatic repulsion and initiates casein micelle aggregation (Gaucheron 2000; Raouche, Dobenesque, et al. 2009). This reduction in negative charge is reflected in increased zeta potential values in dairy matrices. For instance, Shilpashree et al. (2018) reported that the addition of FeSO4 to succinylated sodium caseinate increased the zeta potential from −36 to −31 mV at ∼pH 7. Raouche, Dobenesque, et al. (2009) observed that the addition of 20 mmol/kg FeCl3 and FeCl2 increased the zeta potential from −27 to −24 mV and that increasing the Fe concentration further increased the zeta potential. Similarly, Y. Li et al. (2026) observed increased zeta potential from −11 to −7 mV when increasing the Fe concentration from 1 to 15 mmol/L in skim milk fortified with ferrous gluconate, ferrous lactate and ferrous chloride.

Texture, Syneresis, and Gelation

When Fe salts are incorporated into dairy matrices to produce fermented products, including yogurt, cheese, and yogurt drinks, they improve firmness, increase syneresis and reduce gelation time (Dabour et al. 2019). As described above, a reduction in electrostatic repulsion among casein micelles modifies amino acid side‐chain exposure, decreases casein micelle hydration, promotes the formation of firmer structures, increases syneresis (Figure 5a) and reduces gelation time in acidified dairy matrices (Gaucheron 2000; Raouche, Dobenesque, et al. 2009). At approximately neutral pH ∼6.8, Fe3+ ions are more effective than Fe2+ ions in forming Fe–casein complexes due to their higher equilibrium constant (k ≈ 1017.5–20.5) of phosphoserine coordination (Hegenauer et al. 1979), whereas Fe2+ binds incompletely to casein, leaving a larger proportion in the serum phase to interact with milk fat (Hegenauer et al. 1979). Furthermore, increasing Fe concentrations up to an optimal level at which Fe incorporation efficiency reaches a maximum promotes preferential association with high‐affinity protein binding sites, such as caseins, thereby mitigating the formation of insoluble Fe phosphate precipitates (Raouche et al. 2009). Moreover, in cheese production, Fe–casein interactions can influence fat retention by modifying the casein network structure (Siddique and Park 2019). Fe binding promotes protein–protein interactions and crosslinking within the casein matrix, resulting in a more compact network that limits fat mobility and reduces fat losses during curd handling processes such as cutting, heating, and pressing, thereby contributing to increased firmness (Siddique and Park 2019). These phenomena were experimentally observed by Dabour et al. (2019) and Siddique and Park (2019), where fortification with FeSO4, ferric sulfate and FeCl3 in cheese and yogurt products enhanced firmness and cohesiveness. However, owing to neutralization effects arising from Fe–κ‐casein interactions and reduced pH conditions, contraction or collapse of the κ‐casein “hairy layer” can occur, diminishing micellar hydration and steric stabilization (Broyard and Gaucheron 2015). This collapse allows closer micelle–micelle interactions and the formation of a denser gel network. While such structural tightening enhances firmness, it simultaneously reduces the water‐holding capacity of the matrix, promoting serum expulsion and resulting in increased syneresis in Fe–salt‐fortified dairy systems (Raouche, Dobenesque, et al. 2009; Matumoto‐Pintro et al. 2011). Similarly, Dabour et al. (2019) reported that fortification of yogurt with FeCl3 (28%) and ferric sulfate (29%) increased syneresis compared to that of Fe‐free yogurt (27%). Moreover, the particle size of incorporated Fe can also influence physicochemical properties such as syneresis. A reduction in Fe particle size tends to preserve the gel‐like structure of the casein network by minimizing structural disruption and enhancing water retention (Santillán‐Urquiza et al. 2017). In this regard, Santillán‐Urquiza et al. (2017) observed that in fortified yogurt with 16% total solid content, ferric oxide microparticles exhibited the highest syneresis (55%), whereas ferric oxide nanoparticles resulted in lower syneresis (43%) owing to their reduced particle size.

Schematic diagram of the effect of different forms of iron on casein micelle aggregation and gel network formation. (a) Inorganic Fe–casein micelle interactions, (b) Fe–chelate–casein micelle interactions.

Fortification with Fe chelates, including WP concentrates and WP isolates, exhibits similar phenomena, such as enhanced firmness, as with inorganic Fe salt fortification, but in contrast, reduced syneresis (Figure 5b) through distinct mechanisms are as follows (Gandhi et al. 2022). These Fe–protein conjugates interact with milk proteins to form a gel‐like network, thereby improving water retention and firmness. In WP, β‐lactoglobulin contains free cysteine groups capable of forming disulfide bonds with milk caseins during acidification. Increasing the amount of WP enhances the number of thiol group‐associated bond formations, resulting in improved gel strength. Moreover, these Fe‐WP polymers can retain up to three times more water than conventional yogurt preparations (Matumoto‐Pintro et al. 2011). Gandhi et al. (2022) reported that incorporation of Fe–WP conjugates improved the firmness (from 2.76 to 4.25 N) and water‐holding capacity (from 74% to 78%) while reducing syneresis (from 5.95% to 4.63%) in curd samples. Furthermore, increasing the total solids content of the milk matrix can further reduce syneresis and improve the firmness, as higher solids enhance the microstructural protein matrix, thereby promoting greater cross‐linking and formation of a denser protein network (Arab et al. 2023). Amatayakul et al. (2006) reported that increasing the total solid content from 9% to 14% resulted in lower syneresis rates and increased firmness in set yogurt prepared using a variety of casein:WP ratios (4:1, 3:1, 2:1, and 1:1).

Viscosity and Heat Stability

In addition to texture and syneresis in gelled dairy products, Fe–protein interactions significantly influence the viscosity and heat stability of liquid/beverage dairy systems, which are critical parameters in both fermented and nonfermented products. These functional properties are closely linked to the Fe‐induced modifications in casein micelle interactions, mineral equilibria, and protein structural organization described in earlier sections. The increase in viscosity observed in Fe‐fortified dairy systems is primarily associated with reduced electrostatic repulsion and enhanced protein–protein interactions (Pougher et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2023), leading to aggregation. This aggregation leads to the formation of larger protein clusters, thereby increasing resistance to flow and resulting in elevated viscosity (Jing et al. 2017). This phenomenon was observed by Y. Li et al. (2026); upon Fe addition, it increased the apparent viscosity in FeSO4‐fortified skim milk, and it further increased with increasing Fe concentration (1–15 mmol/L).

Heat stability, commonly evaluated as heat coagulation time (HCT), is adversely affected by free Fe fortification. Fe disrupts colloidal calcium phosphate equilibrium and forms Fe–phosphate complexes that destabilize casein micelles, while Fe‐induced pH reduction further decreases thermal stability (Gaucheron 2000). Fe‐fortified milk thus exhibits reduced HCT and greater coagulation susceptibility during thermal processing. This was observed by Makwana et al. (2020), where the control buffalo milk HCT was reported as 31.40 min, while buffalo milk with casein hydrolysate fortified with 30 ppm FeSO4 resulted in an HCT of 28.75 min. This is of particular concern in high‐temperature processes such as UHT treatment, where Fe‐induced destabilization can lead to coagulation and sediment formation.

In contrast, Fe–chelate complexes, including Fe–casein and Fe–WP conjugates, exhibit improved stability with respect to viscosity and heat treatment. The chelation of Fe limits the availability of free Fe ions, thereby reducing their interaction with casein micelles and minimizing oxidative reactions (Kim and Yaylayan 2021; Darwish et al. 2021). Consequently, systems fortified with Fe–chelates can have improved viscosity and heat stability comparable to unfortified controls. Banjare et al. (2019) reported that incorporation of Fe (15 mg/L) into milk systems as a free form significantly reduced heat stability compared to the WPC–Fe complex and control milk across pH 6.4–7.0. This improved stability is attributed to the sequestration of Fe within protein matrices, which prevents micelle bridging and maintains the integrity of the colloidal system.

Overall, the effects of Fe on viscosity and heat stability are strongly dependent on its chemical form, concentration, and interaction with the dairy matrix. Free Fe salts promote aggregation, accelerate gelation, and reduce thermal stability through mechanisms involving micelle bridging, mineral imbalance, and oxidative reactions. In contrast, chelated forms of Fe mitigate these effects by restricting ion reactivity and preserving protein structure, thereby enhancing the functional stability of Fe‐fortified dairy systems.

Lipid Oxidation

Fe is considered a potent catalyst for lipid oxidation. When incorporated into a dairy matrix, Fe can initiate lipid oxidation through free radical chain reactions. Initially, Fe promotes the formation of highly reactive peroxyl radicals from lipid hydroperoxides, which are subsequently decomposed into alkoxyl radicals. These free radicals attack unsaturated fats present in the dairy matrix, generating additional radicals and ultimately forming secondary oxidation products, including aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols. These compounds contribute to off‐flavors and off‐odors (Cengiz et al. 2019). Milk proteins such as lactoferrin, casein, casein hydrolysates, and casein phosphopeptides are recognized as potential Fe chelators capable of mitigating Fe‐induced oxidation. Some of these proteins are naturally present in milk at relatively low concentrations. However, there can be a counteracting effect under conditions when they can act as prooxidants, depending on the redox potential of the Fe‐complex or when there is insufficient capacity to bind all the supplemented Fe. Increasing the Fe concentration intensifies lipid oxidation. Fe‐fortified dairy matrices with Fe present in inorganic Fe salt form exhibit a high potential to accelerate lipid oxidation (A. Wang et al. 2016). Al‐Shaikh et al. (2023) reported that FeSO4 fortification increased thiobarbituric acid (TBA) values, which quantify malondialdehyde formation during lipid peroxidation, from 0.046 to 0.053 in fortified yogurt systems. Similarly, Nkhata et al. (2015) observed that unfortified yogurt samples resulted in the lowest TBA values (< 0.05 TBA/g), whereas fortification with FeSO4, ferrous bisglycinate and ferrous lactate led to higher TBA values (> 0.05 TBA/g), indicating enhanced lipid oxidation.

Lipid oxidation in Fe‐fortified dairy systems can be minimized through two primary approaches. The first is the fortification of Fe in the form of Fe–chelate complexes such as EDTA, WP concentrate, or casein, while the second is the prevention of direct contact between lipid hydroperoxides and pro‐oxidants (Fe) (Cengiz et al. 2019). The latter strategy is typically achieved through Fe microencapsulation, which is widely employed in Fe fortification (Mehansho 2006). In addition, the incorporation of antioxidants such as AA can scavenge highly reactive free radicals, including alkoxyl radicals and singlet oxygen, thereby minimizing lipid oxidation (Khan et al. 2019). Jalili (2016) reported that feta cheese fortified with FeCl3 and FeSO4, whether in native or microencapsulated forms with or without added AA, exhibited higher lipid oxidation in both FeCl3 and FeSO4 treatments, while encapsulation reduced oxidation, and the presence of vitamin C further decreased it. Similarly, Kim et al. (2002) observed that yogurt fortified with encapsulated ferric ammonium sulfate, with or without vitamin C, showed lower TBA values compared with unencapsulated Fe. Furthermore, El‐Kholy et al. (2011) compared yogurts manufactured with milk fortified with ammonium ferric sulfate, ammonium ferrous sulfate, ferrous lactate, Fe–casein complex, or Fe–WP complex. The lowest TBA values were for yogurt containing Fe–casein and Fe–whey complexes, whereas Fe salts induced higher lipid oxidation. Similarly, Sugiarto, Ye, Taylor, et al. (2009) reported that TBA values for linoleic acid oxidation with Fe–caseinate, Fe–WPC and Fe–milk protein concentrates (MPC) were approximately 6‐, 1.5‐ and 4‐times lower, respectively, compared to linoleic acid oxidation with free Fe2+ ions. Moreover, compared to the WPC chelates, caseinate, and MPC–Fe chelates resulted in lower TBA values due to their pronounced ability to strongly complex with Fe. Similarly, Banjare et al. (2019) reported that 15 mg/L Fe concentration‐fortified FeSO4 and Fe‐WPC initially resulted in TBA values of 0.021 and 0.015, respectively, indicating that inorganic Fe salts induced higher lipid oxidation than Fe–chelated complexes. In addition, alterations in processing conditions such as homogenization and deaeration before Fe addition are potential strategies to reduce lipid oxidative rancidity in Fe‐fortified dairy systems (Gaucheron 2000). Homogenization alters the native milk fat globule structure by reducing the size of the fat globules and adsorbing milk casein micelles and WPs onto the fat globule surface (Garcia et al. 2014). This restructured membrane acts as a barrier between Fe and lipids, thereby reducing lipid oxidation (C. F. Wang and King 2006). Furthermore, deaeration before Fe addition limits the availability of oxygen, which aids lipid oxidation in milk. Moreover, Fe incorporation into milk has been reported to enhance the heat resistance of milk lipase, thereby reducing the effectiveness of low‐temperature (< 79°C) pasteurization. Therefore, increasing the pasteurization temperature to ∼81°C may inactivate lipase and result in more effective pasteurization, thereby reducing lipolytic rancidity (Edmondson et al. 1971).

Sensory Properties

Metallic and oxidized rancid flavors are the most challenging sensory attributes to overcome in Fe‐fortified dairy products. This metallic flavor originates from the intrinsic taste of Fe salts, whereas rancid flavor arises from lipid oxidation within the milk matrix (Nkhata et al. 2015). Increasing the concentration of Fe can intensify these flavor alterations, as elevated Fe levels enhance lipid oxidation by promoting the formation of reactive oxygen species and lipid‐derived oxidation products such as aldehydes and ketones and amplifying the perception of inherent metallic taste. Sachdeva, Kaushik, Arora, and Indumathi (2015) observed that the addition of FPP at concentrations above 25 mg/L caused significant adverse changes in the flavor and odor of pasteurized milk products. Similarly, Nkhata et al. (2015) reported that ferrous bisglycinate (63 mg/kg) and ferrous lactate (79 mg/kg) fortified yogurt received the lowest flavor and appearance scores compared to the unfortified yogurt by performing consumer affective sensory evaluation using 100 untrained panelists and a nine‐point hedonic scale. Abdulghani et al. (2014) also conducted a triangle test‐based sensory test on UHT processed milk fortified with Fe, magnesium, zinc and sodium. Fe‐fortified milk with 75% RDI reported significant alterations in taste, while other minerals resulted in negligible changes. When the FeSO4 concentration was reduced from 75% to 50% RDI per liter, the panelists could not perceive the taste differences after 7 days of storage, but after 30 days, they perceived the taste difference. In addition, the product color may also be affected depending on the Fe source due to its inherent pigmentation. Yogurt samples fortified with iron oxide showed lower L* and b* values along with increased a* values, indicating a deviation from the native yogurt color and a stronger shift toward red, as iron oxides are naturally red (Santillán‐Urquiza et al. 2017).

Microencapsulation and chelation of Fe can mitigate off‐flavor formation by minimizing the interaction between the Fe source and the milk fat, therefore mitigating lipid oxidation occurring in the dairy matrix (Hashemi Gahruie et al. 2015). In microencapsulation, Fe particles are protected by thin films or polymer coating materials such as milk fats, agar, and gelatin (Kwak et al. 2003). These coated Fe particles are designed to be released under different conditions, including mechanical force, variation in temperature or pH. Microcapsule stability and functionality can vary depending on the core and coating material type and form and encapsulation technique (Jackson and Lee 2006). Different microencapsulation techniques have been implemented and studied thus far, including spray drying, liposomal encapsulation, double emulsion, and complex coacervation (Ferreira et al. 2025). Jalili (2016) conducted a descriptive sensory analysis using seven trained panelists to evaluate the taste, flavor, texture, color, and overall acceptability of Fe‐fortified cheeses using a five‐point scale. This study reported that the flavor and color of Fe‐fortified feta cheese were significantly affected. For example, Fe salts (FeSO4 and FeCl3) yielded the highest metallic scores (∼3.54; 5 = high intensity), whereas microencapsulated forms encapsulated with vegetable fats exhibited lower metallic flavor (∼1.57; 1 = low intensity) and color scores comparable to control cheeses. Kim et al. (2002) showed that yogurt fortified with polyglycerol monostearate microencapsulated Fe achieved overall preference scores similar to unfortified samples, in contrast to products fortified with unencapsulated Fe. Although the incorporation of AA positively influenced flavor, it did not affect the color profile. These results were obtained through a nine‐point hedonic scale sensory analysis conducted with eleven semi‐trained panelists. El‐Kholy et al. (2011) and Gandhi et al. (2022) employed descriptive quality scoring using trained sensory panelists and reported that curd and yogurt produced from milk fortified with Fe–casein and Fe–WP conjugates achieved higher overall acceptance than those fortified with Fe salts.

Finally, the chemical composition of Fe‐fortified dairy products is largely not affected by the addition of inorganic Fe salts. Moisture, protein, ash, total solids, and fat contents remain statistically unchanged (El‐Kholy et al. 2011; Jalili 2016; Dabour et al. 2019). In contrast, Fe–chelates may increase protein, fat, and total solids content depending on the type of chelating agent employed, such as Fe–WPC or Fe–casein complexes (Gandhi et al. 2022; Al‐Shaikh et al. 2023). In conclusion, the impact of Fe fortification on the secondary structural modification of dairy matrix proteins has not been extensively studied. Although MRPs have been reported to possess antioxidant potential, the effects of Fe–MRP chelation on the physicochemical and structural properties of dairy matrices remain largely unexplored (Etcheverry et al. 2012).

Future Trends in Using Dairy Products for Iron Fortification

Some studies have argued that due to the modest effect on Fe status indicators and the inhibitory influence of casein and calcium present in milk on Fe absorption, dairy Fe fortification cannot be universally recommended as a potential preventive strategy for IDA (Ziegler 2011; Diniz Largueza et al. 2023). In contrast, numerous studies have reported that Fe fortification in the dairy matrix represents a promising approach to reduce IDA prevalence. A study conducted in Indonesia reported that the consumption of Fe‐fortified milk and noodles by children aged 5–59 months in rural and urban slum areas (30.1% and 40.1% milk and 22.6% and 48.9% noodle consumption, respectively) resulted in reductions in anemia prevalence of 55.9% and 60.8% from baseline prevalence rates of 59.7% and 64.2%, respectively, thereby reducing the odds of childhood anemia compared with children who did not consume Fe‐fortified milk. However, the study relied solely on hemoglobin as a biomarker for assessing anemia and did not include other relevant biomarkers, such as serum ferritin, or micronutrients, including vitamin B12, folate, or vitamin A. This limitation may reduce the validity of the study findings (Semba et al. 2010). El Menchawy et al. (2015) observed that consumption of multiple micronutrient‐fortified milk, including Fe, decreased Fe deficiency from 50.9% to 37.2% in Moroccan schoolchildren, with prevalence reduced by 27% in children living in high‐altitude rural regions. Consumption of Fe‐amino acid (biglycinate)‐chelated fortified milk reported a positive effect on improving low hemoglobin levels in children aged 6–24 months in Brazil, with 30 mg/day of Fe–amino acid chelate producing similar outcomes to 120 mg of inorganic Fe salt fortification (Iost et al. 1998). Afeiche et al. (2024) reported that the consumption of Fe‐fortified milk beverages reduced Fe deficiency by 7% in Pakistani school‐aged children, despite the inhibitory presence of casein and calcium in the dairy matrix. Kibangou et al. (2005) reported that casein‐bound Fe can be released during digestion via hydrolysis by intestinal phosphatase, with β‐casein‐bound Fe exhibiting higher absorption and bioavailability, whereas α‐casein reduced Fe absorption while promoting calcium uptake. Several studies have demonstrated that the inhibitory effect of casein and calcium can be mitigated by co‐fortifying the dairy matrix with enhancers such as AA, vitamin A and citric acid. Walczyk et al. (2014) reported a twofold to threefold and threefold to fourfold enhancement in Fe absorption when AA:Fe was added at 2:1 and 4:1 molar ratios, respectively, irrespective of the calcium concentration. Similarly, vitamin A was reported to reduce the odds of childhood anemia in Indonesia (Semba et al. 2010).

Some studies have raised concerns that the consumption of Fe‐fortified milk could adversely affect neural and cognitive development (Lozoff et al. 2006, 2012). However, other studies have demonstrated that Fe‐dairy fortification is either beneficial or neutral with respect to neurodevelopment, cognitive function and behavior. Iglesias Vázquez et al. (2019) reported that consumption of milk fortified with 0.4–1.2 mg/100 mL by Spanish children aged 6–12 months did not negatively impact neurodevelopment, while hematological indicators improved, and IDA decreased. Similarly, consumption of Fe‐fortified bovine milk for up to 18 months by approximately 8‐month‐old infants reduced IDA and improved psychomotor activities (Williams et al. 1999). Lozoff et al. (2003) reported that 6‐month‐old infants who did not receive Fe‐fortified milk for six months exhibited delayed development and behavioral outcomes, including lower social interactions and slower information processing, whereas these effects were not observed in infants fed Fe‐fortified milk. Overall, these findings indicate that the consumption of Fe‐fortified dairy products represents a promising strategy to prevent Fe deficiency and does not adversely affect neurodevelopment, cognitive function, or behavior.

The primary challenge in the development and manufacturing of Fe‐fortified dairy products lies in balancing product quality with Fe bioavailability. Conventional fortification using inorganic Fe salts often leads to undesirable physicochemical and sensory changes, including lipid oxidation, off‐flavors, color alterations, and compromised texture, particularly during prolonged storage of milk powders and value‐added products such as yogurt, yogurt drinks, and cheese (Mannar and Hurrell 2018; Kazemi‐Taskooh and Varidi 2021). In addition, interactions between inorganic Fe salts and milk constituents, notably casein and calcium, can inhibit Fe absorption in the human GI tract, limiting the overall efficacy of fortification strategies (Ziegler 2011). To address these limitations, recent advances have focused on the use of Fe–chelate complexes, protein‐based carriers such as casein and WP, carbohydrate‐based complexes, and encapsulated Fe systems. These strategies offer multiple advantages, such as minimization of direct interactions with dietary inhibitors, reduction of the pro‐oxidant activity of Fe in the dairy matrix, and preservation of sensory attributes, including flavor, color, and texture. Chelated or encapsulated Fe also demonstrates improved stability during storage and enhanced solubility and bioavailability under physiological conditions. Studies have reported that WP and casein‐based Fe complexes (El‐Kholy et al. 2011; Gandhi et al. 2022), as well as encapsulated Fe preparations (Gupta et al. 2014, 2015), effectively mitigate lipid oxidation and syneresis while maintaining desirable textural and sensory properties in yogurt and cheese. Given these advantages, chelation and encapsulation represent promising avenues for the development of next‐generation functional dairy products. Continued research on novel Fe–ligand systems, including Fe–MRP complexes, may provide innovative solutions that simultaneously optimize Fe bioavailability and product quality, offering a practical approach to combat Fe deficiency on a global scale.

Conclusion

Iron is an essential micronutrient vital for growth and health, yet its deficiency remains widespread. Various strategies, including oral supplementation, have been implemented to address this deficiency, but their effectiveness is often limited by adverse nutritional and quality effects. Consequently, food fortification has emerged as a promising alternative, with dairy products identified as suitable carriers due to their widespread consumption, nutritional value, and economic feasibility. Developing an effective dairy‐based Fe delivery system requires selecting an iron source with high bioavailability and designing a matrix capable of overcoming GI inhibitory effects. Conventional inorganic Fe salts, including FeSO4, FPP, and ferrous gluconate, have been associated with reduced Fe solubility, lower bioavailability, and sensory deterioration due to lipid oxidation. Fe–ligand complexes, such as Fe–protein, Fe–carbohydrate, Fe‐(protein‐carbohydrate blends) and Fe–MRP complexes, offer the potential to overcome challenges in the Fe fortification of foods. Fe–chelate complexes generally enhance texture and sensory properties while mitigating lipid oxidation, pH decline, and syneresis in acidified and fermented dairy products, whereas inorganic Fe salts may increase lipid oxidation and syneresis despite improving firmness. However, the secondary structural modifications in dairy matrices induced by Fe fortification remain largely unexplored. Moreover, despite their high Fe chelation capacity and potential to form both soluble and insoluble Fe complexes, the physicochemical properties and bioavailability of Fe–MRP complexes are underexplored compared to those of Fe–protein and Fe–carbohydrate complexes. The effects of Fe–MRPs on the physicochemical, structural, and sensory properties of dairy matrices have also not been extensively investigated. A comprehensive understanding of Fe–MRP interactions and their influence on the physicochemical, structural, sensory, and bioavailability characteristics of dairy products is therefore critical. Investigating these complexes may enable the development of novel functional dairy foods that effectively deliver Fe, improve product quality, and offer a viable strategy to mitigate global Fe deficiency.
