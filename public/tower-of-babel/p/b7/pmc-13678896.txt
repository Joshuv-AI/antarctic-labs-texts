A risk assessment model for gestational diabetes mellitus integrating maternal demographics and hematological indices in Thai women
Authors: Thaveesak Sai-ong, Donrawee Waeyeng, Tanaporn Khamphaya, Yanisa Rattanapan, Chutima Jansakun, Atsuko Ikeda, Supabhorn Yimthiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUniversal screening for Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) is the gold standard, but it can be difficult and expensive in resource-limited settings. Many current prediction models rely on specialized tests that are not available in primary care. This study aimed to create a cost-effective risk assessment model using common maternal demographic and blood test data to help identify GDM risk in Thai pregnant women.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study with 200 pregnant women (24–28 weeks of gestation) at a secondary care hospital in Southern Thailand. We analyzed routine antenatal data, including complete blood count and other lab results. A risk-scoring system was built using multivariate logistic regression. We then tested the model’s performance using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to determine the optimal cut-off point.ResultsSix independent predictors were found maternal age over 35 years, systolic blood pressure above 130 mmHg, glycosuria, hemoglobin at least 11 g/dL, platelets at least 260.50 × 103/µL, and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) of 2.89 or higher. The combined risk model performed better (AUC = 0.804; 95% CI: 0.74–0.87) than single-marker models. With a risk score cut-off of 2.5 (indicating three or more risk factors), the model had a sensitivity of 71.4% and a specificity of 78.1%.ConclusionThe new risk assessment model, which uses basic demographic and routine blood test data, offers a reliable and affordable way to screen for GDM. This method enables early risk identification and helps healthcare providers use resources more efficiently, potentially reducing unnecessary tests in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Gestational diabetes mellitus, Maternal demographics, Prediction model, Thai pregnant women, Pregnancy biomarkers

Background

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is the most common endocrine disorder in pregnancy, caused by insufficient insulin and metabolic problems [1, 2]. Its global prevalence ranges from 1% to 28%, depending on diagnostic criteria and population [1]. GDM is a major public health issue because it increases the risk of type 2 diabetes and obesity for both mother and child [3]. It is also linked to negative outcomes for the baby, including macrosomia, shoulder dystocia, neonatal hypoglycemia, hyperbilirubinemia, and higher rates of surgical delivery [4].

The Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT) is the standard screening test for GDM, usually performed between the 24th and 28th weeks of pregnancy, as recommended by the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) [5]. Although, this test requires fasting, and many pregnant women find the high-glucose drink hard to tolerate [6, 7]. Screening guidelines also differ widely between countries and organizations, making it hard to standardize [8]. In Thailand, many hospitals use a two-step process: a 50-g Glucose Challenge Test (GCT) followed by a 100-g OGTT. This approach takes time and puts pressure on healthcare resources and labs, often causing delays or incomplete screening in busy clinics [9, 10]. Importantly, there is no reliable way to predict GDM before this demanding test.

While the exact causes of GDM are still not fully understood, new research shows that changes in the body’s inflammatory response are important [11, 12]. Studies have found that GDM affects immune regulation, changing the levels of CD3+, CD4+, and B cells [13, 14]. As a result, blood markers from routine tests, like white and red blood cell counts, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), are being studied as useful indicators [13–15]. The lymphocyte/HDL-C ratio has shown promise for predicting GDM risk [12]. These markers provide a non-invasive way to look at the inflammation linked to GDM.

Although several prediction models use factors like age, BMI, obstetric history, and biomarkers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) or adiponectin/leptin ratios [16, 17], they are not widely used in practice. Many models work differently across ethnic groups, diagnostic standards, and available data [18]. Complex risk assessments, like those updated by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) in 2018, are difficult to implement in places with limited resources [19]. In Thailand, although GDM is becoming more common, universal screening is often not feasible due to budget and workload constraints in primary care [9, 20]. As a result, many hospitals still use risk-based screening, which can miss many GDM cases among Thai women [10, 21]. Western prediction models may not fit the Thai population well, since Asian women often develop insulin resistance and GDM at lower BMI levels than Caucasians [22, 23]. There is a clear need for a locally tested, affordable risk tool that uses readily available information to improve screening effectiveness.

Recent studies in other populations, such as those in Turkey [14, 24] and China [6, 25], have explored the utility of individual hematological inflammatory markers like the NLR and platelet counts in predicting GDM. However, a significant research gap remains: these markers have rarely been integrated with routine clinical data into a cohesive prediction model, and they have not been adequately validated in Southeast Asian populations, where women often develop insulin resistance at lower BMI thresholds. Existing risk-based strategies frequently lack diagnostic accuracy and do not adequately reflect the clinical and hematological characteristics of the Thai population. To address this literature gap, the present study developed a novel risk assessment model that integrates standard maternal demographic characteristics and routine hematological indices. This model, specifically tailored for Thai pregnant women, is designed to improve sensitivity and specificity, enabling routine prenatal data to serve as a practical tool for early and accurate GDM identification.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective comparative cross-sectional study was conducted at the Obstetrics and Gynecology Department of Thasala Hospital, Nakhon Si Thammarat, between August and December 2023. The study included 200 pregnant women: 49 with GDM and 151 as controls. Women were eligible if they were over 18 years old, more than 24 weeks pregnant and scheduled for GDM screening and did not have pre-existing type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Women with a history of hypertension, chronic kidney failure, thyroid problems, heart disease, HIV, or malaria were excluded.

Sample collection and laboratory measurements

Clinical and demographic data of pregnant women who met the inclusion criteria were retrospectively extracted from medical records at the antenatal care unit using a purposive sampling technique. A formal a-priori sample size calculation was not performed; instead, the sample size was determined by the total number of available medical records during the study period. Personal and obstetric histories were obtained from the hospital database. All laboratory tests, including the 100-g OGTT and routine blood tests, were performed as part of standard antenatal care. According to the hospital’s protocol, glucose levels were measured at the Thasala Hospital clinical chemistry laboratory with the Cobas 8,000 modular analyzer (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) using the hexokinase method.

Complete blood count parameters were obtained from routine antenatal laboratory tests performed during the GDM screening period at 24–28 weeks of gestation. The results were retrospectively extracted from medical records and used to calculate hematological indices, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio. All complete blood count analyses were performed using the Sysmex XN-3000 analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan).

Only medical records with complete maternal demographic data, obstetric information, OGTT results, urine test results, and complete blood count parameters were included in the study. Records with missing key variables were excluded during the screening stage.

Diagnosis of gestational diabetes mellitus

GDM screening and diagnosis followed the two-step approach consistent with the recommendations of ACOG guidelines. First, a non-fasting 50-g (GCT) was administered; if the 1-hour plasma glucose level exceeded 140 mg/dL, a diagnostic 100-g OGTT was performed. GDM was confirmed if two or more plasma glucose values met or exceeded the Carpenter-Coustan criteria: fasting > 95 mg/dL, 1-hour > 180 mg/dL, 2-hour > 155 mg/dL, and 3-hour > 140 mg/dL.

Construction of the GDM risk assessment score

To translate the multivariate model into a practical clinical tool, a simplified risk scoring system was developed. Based on the six independent predictors identified in Model 6 (maternal age > 35 years, SBP > 130 mmHg, positive urine glucose, hemoglobin > 11 g/dL, platelets > 260.50 × 103/µL, and NLR > 2.89), a binary score of 1 was assigned for the presence of each risk factor, and 0 for its absence. The total risk score ranged from 0 to 6. The diagnostic performance of this cumulative score was evaluated using ROC curve analysis to identify the optimal cut-off threshold for GDM prediction.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 28 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were compared between groups using the independent t-test for normally distributed data or the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Binary logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of GDM. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariable analysis were selected for the multivariable model. Additionally, SBP > 130 mmHg was retained in the final model based on its a priori clinical relevance, despite non-significance in univariable screening. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with all predictors showing acceptable values (VIF < 5).

Six risk assessment models were constructed using routine clinical data and known risk factors [26, 27]. Model performance was evaluated by ROC curve analysis, with an Area Under the Curve (AUC) ≥ 0.7 considered acceptable. Optimal cut-off points were determined using Youden’s J index [28, 29]. For continuous variables, thresholds for dichotomization were strictly determined either by established clinical guidelines (e.g., SBP > 130 mmHg) or, for hematological indices, by calculating Youden’s J index from the ROC curves to maximize sensitivity and specificity for the simplified clinical score.

Results

Maternal characteristics and biochemical parameters of GDM and non–GDM groups

A total of 200 women met the study criteria. The overall prevalence of GDM in our study cohort was 24.5%, with 49 women diagnosed with GDM and the remaining 151 (75.5%) classified as the non-GDM control group. The GDM group had a higher mean age (p = 0.002) and higher SBP (p = 0.050) and DBP (p = 0.045) compared to the non-GDM group. Hemoglobin (p < 0.001) and urine glucose (p < 0.001) also differed significantly between groups. Fasting plasma glucose and OGTT values at 1, 2, and 3 h were higher in the GDM group (p < 0.001). No significant differences were found in BMI, gravidity, or urine protein. Maternal characteristics and biochemical parameters are detailed in Table 1.

Multifactor logistic regression analysis and GDM prediction model

GDM prediction in the univariate analysis showed that higher age > 35 year. (cOR: 2.33; 95% CI: 1.12–4.84; p = 0.023) significantly predicted the incidence of GDM. Among the biochemical parameters, positive urine glucose (cOR: 4.78; 95% CI: 2.32–9.84; p < 0.001), Hemoglobin > 11 g/dL (cOR: 2.71; 95% CI: 1.14–6.48; p = 0.025), Platelets > 260.50 × 103 uL (cOR: 3.38; 95% CI: 1.66–6.88; p = 0.001) and NLR > 2.89 (cOR: 1.92; 95% CI: 1.00–3.72; p = 0.05) predicted the occurrence of GDM. In the comparison of various models, model 6 showed the highest predictive reliability for GDM (AUC = 0.80; 95% CI: 0.74–0.87). According to ROC analysis, a cut-off of 2.5 was the optimal cut-off for the onset of GDM, with 71.4% and 78.1% sensitivity and specificity, respectively (Fig. 1).

ROC curves of the 6 models for early screening of GDM

Based on the final multivariate logistic regression analysis (Model 6), a simplified clinical risk scoring system was constructed (Table 6). To facilitate practical application in routine prenatal care settings, a binary score of 1 was assigned to each independently significant predictor identified in the model. These risk factors included maternal age > 35 years, systolic blood pressure > 130 mmHg, positive urine glucose, hemoglobin 11 g/dL, platelets > 260.50 × 103/µL, and NLR > 2.89. Consequently, the total cumulative risk score for each participant ranged from 0 to 6. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using ROC curve analysis, which determined that a cut-off score of > 2.5 provided the optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity. Therefore, a total score of 3 or higher was defined as the threshold for high risk of developing GDM.

Model 6 demonstrated good calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow p = 0.399). The apparent calibration slope was 1.00 with an intercept of 0.00. Following internal validation via 1,000 bootstrap resamples, the optimism-corrected slope (0.86) and intercept (-0.11) indicated strong agreement between predicted and observed risks, with only minor overestimation at extremes. This alignment is visually corroborated by the calibration plots (Fig. 2).

Calibration of the GDM prediction and risk models

Table 7 details the diagnostic performance of the simplified scoring system at various cut-off points. The cumulative statistics reveal that a cut-off score of ≥ 3 provides the most balanced screening performance, yielding a Sensitivity of 73.5% and a Negative Predictive Value (NPV) of 87.1%. This implies that using a threshold of 3 points effectively identifies nearly three-quarters of all GDM cases, while a score below 3 gives a high assurance of ruling out the disease. Although a cut-off of ≥ 4 significantly improves Specificity (to 87.4%) and Positive Predictive Value (to 54.8%), it comes at the cost of reduced Sensitivity (46.9%), making it less suitable for primary screening but potentially useful for prioritizing resource-intensive diagnostics.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of sociodemographic parameters and routine hematological indices to identify potent predictors for GDM among Thai pregnant women. The multivariate analysis revealed that advanced maternal age (> 35 years), systolic blood pressure > 130 mmHg, glycosuria, higher hemoglobin levels, elevated platelet counts, and a high NLR were significant independent risk factors. Integrating these six variables into a unified predictive model demonstrated superior diagnostic performance compared to individual markers alone.

Consistent with established literature, our findings underscore maternal age as a critical determinant of GDM risk (p = 0.001). This aligns with reports by Yong et al. [31] and others indicating that older pregnant women face a significantly higher risk due to age-related declines in β-cell function and insulin sensitivity [30–32]. Vascular aging and alterations in glucose regulation likely contribute to these poorer pregnancy outcomes [33]. Furthermore, glycosuria emerged as the strongest predictor in our model (p < 0.001, aOR 5.30). While the association between glycosuria and hyperglycemia is physiologically expected, the inclusion of this parameter in our model is strategically driven by clinical utility and cost-effectiveness rather than biological novelty. In resource-limited settings where universal OGTT screening is often hindered by logistical constraints, laboratory costs, and patient burden, urine glucose analysis serves as a vital triage tool. Since urinalysis is already a mandatory, virtually zero cost component of routine antenatal care in Thailand, leveraging this data allows for the immediate identification of high-risk women without additional expenditure. This approach aligns with the practical necessity of prioritizing scarce diagnostic resources (i.e., OGTT) for those most likely to test positive, rather than applying a ‘blanket’ screening approach that may not be sustainable in primary care contexts [34–36].

A notable finding of this study is the significant association between hematological indices and GDM. We observed that elevated hemoglobin and platelet counts were associated with GDM, corroborating previous studies linking elevated hemoglobin to increased blood viscosity and insulin resistance [37]. Similarly, platelets are known to secrete inflammatory mediators that can impair insulin signaling. Moreover, the predictive value of NLR in our model highlights the role of chronic low-grade inflammation in the pathophysiology of GDM. A high NLR reflects a combined state of neutrophilia (inflammation) and relative lymphopenia (physiological stress), which collectively exacerbate insulin resistance [25, 38]. The predictive value of NLR and platelet counts observed in our Thai cohort aligns with findings from different ethnic groups. For instance, studies in Turkey [14, 24] have highlighted the usefulness of NLR as an independent marker for GDM due to systemic low grade inflammation. Similarly, research in China [6, 25] identified variations in complete blood count constituents as early indicators of GDM. While previous studies often evaluated these biomarkers in isolation, our model demonstrates that integrating them with routine maternal demographics such as age and glycosuria significantly enhances diagnostic performance, capturing the multifaceted pathophysiology of the disease more effectively.

Interestingly, our study did not find a significant association between BMI and GDM risk (p = 0.227), which contrasts with established prediction models where BMI is universally recognized as a primary predictor. This lack of statistical significance should be interpreted cautiously and is likely influenced by limited statistical power and sample size constraints. This finding may be attributed to the relatively high mean BMI observed in both the GDM (27.44 ± 5.07 kg/m²) and non-GDM (26.43 ± 5.08 kg/m²) groups. Since most participants in both groups fell into the overweight or obese categories based on Asian BMI cut-offs [39], overall BMI might have lost its discriminative power as a standalone predictor in this specific cohort. Furthermore, evidence suggests that in Asian populations, central adiposity (visceral fat) is often a more critical determinant of insulin resistance than overall BMI [22]. However, specific measures such as waist circumference were not evaluated in the present study.

Beyond identifying individual risk factors, a key strength of this study is the translation of these statistical associations into a simplified, clinically applicable risk-scoring system (Table 6). While we acknowledge that dichotomizing continuous predictors such as NLR and platelet counts into binary risk factors inevitably leads to some loss of statistical information and predictive power compared to retaining them as continuous variables, this methodological trade-off was deliberately chosen. Unlike complex prediction models that require specialized software or nomograms, our 6-point score was specifically designed for practical application in resource-limited settings where electronic health records may not be fully integrated. This approach aligns with recent global health initiatives, such as the development of the IN-SPIRE score, which emphasized that simple, non-invasive risk scores are essential for reducing the logistical burden of universal screening in low-income and middle-income countries [40]. However, the applicability of risk scores across different populations remains a critical challenge due to the heterogeneity of risk factors. While advanced maternal age and high BMI are universally recognized predictors appearing in models from both Western (e.g., USA, UK) and Asian cohorts (e.g., China, Japan) [1, 41] other parameters exhibit significant regional variation. For instance, hematological markers such as NLR and platelet indices, which were significant in our Thai cohort, are less frequently incorporated into European models but have shown strong predictive value in studies from Turkey and China, potentially reflecting genetic or environmental influences on inflammatory responses in Asian populations [22, 24]. Conversely, a history of macrosomia is a predominant factor in African and American models but may be less sensitive in South-east Asian contexts where baseline birth weights are generally lower [42]. This variation underscores the limitation of adopting one-size-fits-all international guidelines and validates our finding that a locally derived model integrating specific hematological thresholds relevant to Thai women outperforms generalized risk assessments.

In this study, the model included 49 events and 6 predictors. Thus, the events-per-variable (EPV) ratio is approximately 8.17. While this falls slightly below the traditional rule-of-thumb of 10, methodological studies have demonstrated that models with an EPV between 5 and 9 can still achieve robust and reliable performance without severe bias [43, 44]. Nonetheless, to mitigate the risk of overparameterization, we performed rigorous internal validation using 1,000 bootstrap resamples, which confirmed the stability and reliability of our findings. Moreover, the reliability of our proposed model is further supported by the calibration analysis using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (p = 0.399), which indicates strong agreement between predicted risks and observed outcomes. Calibration is a critical, often overlooked, component of prediction model performance; a well-calibrated model ensures that the estimated risk probability reflects the true underlying risk, preventing potential overtreatment or undertreatment [45]. Furthermore, the risk stratification analysis (Table 7) demonstrated a clear dose-response relationship. Notably, women with a score of ≥ 4 had a probability of GDM exceeding 50%. This distinct threshold enables risk-stratified care, allowing healthcare providers to prioritize high-risk women for immediate diagnostic testing while optimizing resources for the lower-risk group [46]. When compared to existing prediction models, our study addresses a common methodological gap. A systematic review by La-main-de Ruiter et al. (2016) revealed that although many GDM prediction models demonstrate acceptable discrimination (AUC), most fail to report calibration performance, often leading to an overestimation of risk when applied in clinical practice [47]. In contrast, our model demonstrated excellent calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow p = 0.399), suggesting that the predicted probabilities are reliable for individual risk assessment. Furthermore, unlike traditional risk-factor-based screening (e.g., NICE or ACOG guidelines), which treats risk as a binary outcome (High vs. Low) and suffers from low specificity [41], our scoring system provides granular risk stratification. This approach is comparable to the “PeRsonal” GDM model developed in the UK, which advocates for personalized risk thresholds to guide intervention intensity [46]. However, while the PeRsonal model requires historical data that may be subject to recall bias, our model leverages objective hematological indices (NLR, Platelets), offering a distinct advantage in reproducibility and objectivity for the Asian obstetric population.

Overall, the proposed model may be highly fitted to the current cohort. However, several methodological issues should be considered when interpreting the results of this study. The model was developed using univariable screening and sequential multivariable model selection. Although this approach is commonly used in clinical prediction, it may still introduce the risk of unstable predictor selection and some degree of model optimism. In addition, several continuous predictors, such as SBP, hemoglobin, platelet count, and NLR, were dichotomized to improve clinical interpretability and make the model easier to apply in routine practice; however, this may also reduce part of the predictive information contained within the original variables. The specific cut-off values for platelet count and NLR were empirically derived from our dataset, which may result in unstable threshold estimates and limit generalizability. Furthermore, as this was a single-center derivation study, although internal validation was performed, the model still lacks external validation. Therefore, its generalizability to other populations or clinical settings remains uncertain [48]. Future studies should consider alternative approaches, including the pre-specified predictors or the use of penalized regression techniques such as the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) [49], which could potentially improve model stability and robustness. In addition, validation in independent cohorts would help to improve and confirm generalizability. Despite these methodological considerations, the reliance on routinely collected, readily available, and low-cost antenatal data gives the proposed framework practical value and suggests that it may still be feasible for implementation in primary care settings, particularly where resources are limited.

Conclusions

This study developed and validated a practical risk assessment model for GDM using readily available clinical variables and identified several important predictors associated with GDM risk. The model demonstrated promising prediction performance and may be beneficial for early risk stratification in the study population, especially where universal OGTT screening is not feasible. However, the results should be regarded with caution due to methodological limitations such as a single-center design and internal validation only. Further multicenter prospective trials with external validation are needed to assess generalizability of the model, optimize its performance, and establish clinical utility before routine implementation.
