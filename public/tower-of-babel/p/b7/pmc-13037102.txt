Comparative analysis of sarcopenic obesity detection and associated mental–cognitive outcomes in community-dwelling older adults: impact of using body mass index versus waist circumference
Authors: Pengxin Dong, Haichen Wu, Yidan Chai, Ping Huang, Dongmei Huang, Caili Li, Xiao Pan, Pinyue Tao, Lichong Lai, Xiaoying Cao, Jie Peng, Xiaoling Feng, Jingyun Zeng, Zhixin Li, Haowei Liu, Huimin Zhou, Huiqiao Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcopenic obesity (SO), defined as the coexistence of sarcopenia and obesity, is associated with adverse physical, psychological, and cognitive outcomes in older adults. In community settings lacking advanced body composition measurement tools, simplified screening indicators are often used. The Ishii score, based on age, handgrip strength, and calf circumference, offers high predictive accuracy for sarcopenia. Body mass index (BMI) reflects overall adiposity, whereas waist circumference (WC) represents central obesity. However, evidence directly comparing SO prevalence and related characteristics using BMI versus WC in Chinese community populations remains limited.ObjectivesTo compare SO prevalence, agreement, and associated factors when using BMI or WC as the obesity index in combination with the Ishii score, and to examine differences in sociodemographic, psychological, and cognitive characteristics across SO phenotypes.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 880 community-dwelling older adults in Guangxi, China. Sarcopenia was defined using the Ishii score (≥ 105 for men, ≥ 120 for women), and obesity was defined by BMI (≥ 28 kg/m²) or WC (≥ 90 cm in men, ≥ 85 cm in women). Participants were categorized into four phenotypes: non-obesity/non-sarcopenia (NONS), isolated obesity (IO), isolated sarcopenia (IS), and sarcopenic obesity (SO). Cohen’s Kappa assessed agreement between methods. Group differences were analyzed using chi-square(χ²) tests, t tests, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and logistic regression. Depressive symptoms and cognitive impairment were assessed using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) and the Ascertain Dementia-8 (AD-8), respectively.ResultsSO prevalence was 3.2% (BMI method) versus 14.1% (WC method), with moderate agreement (Kappa = 0.572). Multinomial logistic regression revealed that WC-based SO was significantly associated with male sex, low monthly income, and lack of attention to nutritional information. BMI-based SO was primarily associated with older age and the presence of multimorbidity. For mental–cognitive outcomes, both SO phenotypes were associated with a higher risk of depressive symptoms and cognitive impairment. However, the strength of association with depressive symptoms was markedly greater for the BMI-defined SO group (OR = 7.823, 95% CI: 3.198–19.139) than for the WC-defined SO group (OR = 2.105, 95% CI: 1.097–4.042).ConclusionsThe choice of obesity index substantially influences SO detection, with WC identifying a much larger and likely earlier-stage at-risk population. The BMI-based definition, while identifying fewer cases, captured a severe SO phenotype with exceptionally strong associations with depressive symptoms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07145-y.

Introduction

With the accelerating pace of global population aging, the demand for health management among older adults is rising significantly. Sarcopenic obesity (SO), a condition characterized by the coexistence of reduced skeletal muscle mass and excessive fat accumulation, has garnered increasing attention in geriatric epidemiology and clinical practice in recent years. This condition not only combines the individual risks of sarcopenia and obesity, but may also lead to synergistically exacerbated adverse outcomes through mechanisms such as inflammation, insulin resistance, and oxidative stress [1]. Previous studies have confirmed that SO is significantly associated with increased risks of falls, functional impairment, hospitalization, and mortality [2–4].

In addition to impairing physical function, SO may also affect psychological and cognitive health in older adults. Evidence suggests that muscle loss is associated with reduced levels of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and degenerative changes in cognitive pathways [5], while obesity may alter brain structure and function through chronic low-grade inflammation and insulin resistance mediated by adipokines [6, 7]. Recent systematic reviews have reported significant associations between SO and multiple health conditions, such as cognitive impairment, depressive symptoms, and multimorbidity, although the underlying mechanisms remain complex and require further investigation [8–10].

Currently, there is no universally accepted diagnostic criterion for SO. In resource-limited community settings, the lack of advanced tools for precise body composition assessment often necessitates the use of simplified indicators. The Ishii score, a screening tool for sarcopenia based on age, handgrip strength, and calf circumference, has demonstrated high predictive validity and feasibility in multiple population-based studies [11]. Body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC) represent general and central obesity, respectively, capturing distinct patterns of fat accumulation that are differentially associated with health outcomes [12].

Critically, these adiposity indicators identify different at-risk populations with potentially distinct pathophysiological pathways. Studies show that abdominal obesity is substantially more prevalent than general obesity among Chinese older adults, with many exhibiting “normal-weight obesity”, characterized by normal BMI but excessive visceral fat [13]. Since visceral adipose tissue is a key source of pro-inflammatory cytokines and contributes directly to insulin resistance, neurodegeneration, and mood disorders [14, 15], WC-based SO definition may better identify community-dwelling older adults with metabolically high-risk profiles of combined muscle loss and visceral fat accumulation. Conversely, the BMI-based definition, with its lower sensitivity to body composition changes, might selectively identify a more severe SO subtype with pronounced metabolic dysregulation. However, direct comparative evidence regarding how these different definitions influence the observed associations between SO and neuropsychiatric outcomes remains limited. Understanding whether SO defined by BMI versus WC exhibits differential associations with cognitive impairment and depressive symptoms is crucial for elucidating distinct etiological pathways and identifying specific high-risk subgroups.

Based on a cross-sectional survey of community-dwelling older adults in China, this study employed the Ishii score combined with BMI and WC to classify participants into four mutually exclusive subgroups. The aims were to: (1) estimate the prevalence of SO and its subtypes under different obesity definitions; (2) examine differences in sociodemographic characteristics, cognitive function and depressive symptoms across the groups; and (3) evaluate the impact of using BMI versus WC on the interpretation of findings. The results are expected to provide practical evidence to support risk stratification, early identification, and targeted interventions for SO in community-based aging populations.

Methods

Participants

This cross-sectional study recruited a total of 880 community-dwelling older adults aged 60 years and above using a convenience sampling approach in five cities of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, China. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 60 years; (2) able to cooperate with physical measurements, including handgrip strength and calf circumference; and (3) able to complete a brief questionnaire. Exclusion criteria included: (1) severe acute illness or bedridden status; and (2) severe cognitive impairment rendering the individual unable to communicate effectively.

Definition of sarcopenia

Sarcopenia was initially screened using the Ishii score, a validated screening tool developed to identify older adults at high risk for sarcopenia [11]. The score is calculated based on sex, age, handgrip strength, and calf circumference. The formulas are as follows:

Males: Ishii score = 0.62 × (age − 64) − 3.09 × (grip strength − 50) − 4.64 × (calf circumference − 42); Females: Ishii score = 0.80 × (age − 64) − 5.09 × (grip strength − 34) − 3.28 × (calf circumference − 42).

According to the original validation study [11], which involved 1,971 functionally independent community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 65 years in Japan, sarcopenia was defined by low muscle mass (via bioimpedance analysis) combined with either low muscle strength or low physical performance. The final predictive model, consisting of age, grip strength, and calf circumference, demonstrated excellent discrimination, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.939 (95% CI: 0.918–0.958) for men and 0.909 (95% CI: 0.887–0.931) for women. At the optimal cutoff values (≥ 105 for men, ≥ 120 for women), the tool achieved high sensitivity (84.9% in men, 75.5% in women) and specificity (88.2% in men, 92.0% in women), with negative predictive values exceeding 93% in both sexes. In the present study, participants with Ishii scores ≥ 105 in men or ≥ 120 in women were classified as having sarcopenia.

Handgrip strength was measured using a standardized electronic dynamometer (CAMRY EH101, Xiangshan, China), with each hand tested twice and the maximum value recorded. In this study, handgrip strength was used as a continuous variable in the Ishii score calculation rather than for direct classification of sarcopenia. Calf circumference was measured in a standing position, with a non-compressive tape placed around the widest part of the calf; the average of two measurements was used. Participants with conditions that could interfere with accurate assessment—such as severe hand osteoarthritis, upper-limb musculoskeletal disorders, or lower-limb edema—were excluded from these measurements.

Definition of obesity

Obesity was defined using two indicators: (1) BMI: Calculated as body weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m²). According to the Chinese adult BMI classification, BMI ≥ 28.0 kg/m² was considered obese [16]; (2) WC: According to the Chinese Expert Consensus on Obesity Prevention and Control, central obesity was defined as WC ≥ 90.0 cm for men and ≥ 85.0 cm for women [16].

Group classification

Participants were categorized into four groups based on their obesity and sarcopenia status: (1) neither obesity nor sarcopenia (NONS), (2) isolated obesity (IO), (3) isolated sarcopenia (IS), and (4) sarcopenic obesity (SO).

Throughout this paper, “SO” is used both as a general term referring to the coexistence of sarcopenia and obesity, and as the name of one of the four phenotype subgroups (the SO group), depending on context.

Health-related variables

Measurement of attention to nutritional information

Participants were asked, “Have you ever paid attention to information related to dietary nutrition (including food nutrient content, nutritional functions, dietary balance, or food label interpretation)?” Responses were recorded as “Yes” or “No.”

Measurement of dietary supplement intake

Participants were asked, “Do you currently use any health-related nutritional supplements (e.g., vitamins, calcium tablets, protein supplements)?” Responses were recorded as “Yes” or “No.”

Measurement of multimorbidity

Participants were asked to report whether they had ever been diagnosed by a physician with any of the following chronic diseases: (1) hypertension; (2) heart disease/coronary heart disease; (3) diabetes; (4) cerebrovascular disease (including stroke); (5) chronic bronchitis or other respiratory diseases; (6) cancer or malignant tumors; (7) kidney disease (excluding tumors or cancer); (8) liver disease (excluding fatty liver, tumors, or cancer); (9) gastroenteritis or other digestive tract diseases; (10) tuberculosis; (11) rheumatoid arthritis or arthritis; (12) cervical/lumbar disorders; (13) reproductive system diseases; (14) prostate diseases; (15) urinary system diseases; (16) glaucoma or cataract; (17) osteoporosis; and (18) hearing impairment.

This list was developed based on prior epidemiological studies and the National Health Commission’s chronic disease surveillance priorities. To avoid potential overlap with psychological and cognitive assessments, neurological conditions (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease, cerebral atrophy, Parkinson’s disease) and emotional or mental disorders were excluded.

Multimorbidity was defined as the coexistence of two or more physician-diagnosed chronic diseases.

Measurement of cognitive impairment

We employed the Ascertain Dementia-8 (AD-8), a validated informant-reported questionnaire, to assess cognitive impairment through structured interviews with participants. Originally developed for community-based dementia screening, the AD-8 has demonstrated high diagnostic performance among Chinese older adults, with sensitivity of 93.9% and specificity of 76.0% for detecting mild cognitive impairment and dementia at the conventional cut-off score of ≥ 2 [17]. The tool evaluates decline across eight cognitive domains: memory, judgment, daily activities, initiative, orientation, visuospatial skills, and recall ability. Each item is scored as 0 (“no change”) or 1 (“decline observed”), yielding a total score range of 0–8, with higher scores indicating greater cognitive decline. The AD-8 is brief, easy to administer, and suitable for use in community settings, primary healthcare facilities, and general medical practice where specialized neuropsychological testing may not be feasible [18]. In this study, a total score ≥ 2 was considered indicative of possible cognitive impairment, and the variable was dichotomized accordingly.

Measurement of depressive symptoms

The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) was used to evaluate depressive symptoms based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)-V criteria. It consists of nine items assessing the frequency of depressive symptoms over the past two weeks, scored from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 27. The PHQ-9 is widely used in clinical practice, epidemiological studies, and community mental health screenings due to its simplicity, validity, and ability to capture both the presence and severity of depressive symptoms [19]. Conventionally, a score ≥ 5 indicates the presence of depressive symptoms, with higher thresholds corresponding to greater severity.

Data collection and quality control

All measurements and surveys were conducted by trained research staff. Questionnaires were administered via face-to-face interviews to ensure data accuracy and completeness. The survey was coordinated by the hospital research team and implemented in multiple community and village sites across five cities in Guangxi Province, China. Investigators from several hospital departments participated in fieldwork, assisting with participant recruitment, questionnaire administration, physical measurements, and data quality control.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The consistency between the two classification methods for SO [Method 1 (BMI + Ishii score) and Method 2 (WC + Ishii score)]—was evaluated using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient, with interpretation based on established benchmarks for agreement. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) after testing for normality, and categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Group differences among the four phenotypes—NONS, IO, IS, and SO—were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables and the chi-square (χ²) test for categorical variables.

To identify factors associated with each SO subtype, multinomial logistic regression analyses were performed, with the NONS group serving as the reference category. Independent variables included sociodemographic, behavioral, and health-related factors that showed significant associations in univariate analyses. Prior to inclusion in the regression models, multicollinearity among these variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and no significant multicollinearity was detected (VIF < 5 for all variables). The included variables were age, sex, residence location, marital status, educational level, monthly income, dietary supplement intake, attention to nutritional information, smoking status, and multimorbidity status. Results were reported as odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Depressive symptoms and cognitive impairment were analyzed as binary variables. Differences in their prevalence across body-composition phenotypes were examined using the χ² test. To further evaluate whether SO phenotypes were independently associated with depressive symptoms and cognitive impairment, separate binary logistic regression models were constructed with PHQ-9 and AD-8 as dependent variables, respectively. Covariates entered into these adjusted models were selected based on univariate screening.

All tests were two-tailed, and p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 880 older adults were included in the analysis, with a mean age of 72.27 ± 8.33 years; 236 (26.8%) were men and 644 (73.2%) were women.

Prevalence of SO based on BMI and waist circumference and agreement analysis

The agreement between these two classification methods was moderate, with a Kappa coefficient of 0.572. Method 1 identified 28 individuals with SO (3.2% of the total sample), whereas Method 2 identified 124 individuals (14.1%). Differences in sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics across SO subtypes

When classified using Method 1, significant differences across the four SO subgroups were observed in sex, age, residence location, marital status, education level, dietary supplement intake, attention to nutritional information, multimorbidity status, and monthly income (all p-value < 0.05). Under Method 2, subgroup distributions also differed significantly by sex, age, residence location, marital status, education level, smoking status, dietary supplement intake, attention to nutritional information, and monthly income (all p-value < 0.05). It is worth noting that smoking status did not differ significantly across subgroups under Method 1 (p-value = 0.299), and multimorbidity was also not significantly different across subgroups under Method 2 (p-value = 0.096). Discussion

Differences in prevalence and agreement of sarcopenic obesity using different obesity indicators

This study screened SO in community-dwelling older adults using the Ishii score in combination with two obesity indices—BMI and WC—and found notable discrepancies in SO detection between the two approaches: the SO prevalence was 3.2% (28/880) when using BMI and 14.1% (124/880) when using WC. The Kappa statistic was 0.572, indicating only moderate agreement between the methods and suggesting substantial differences in how BMI and WC identify obesity phenotypes relevant to SO.

These discrepancies stem from the distinct nature of the two indicators. BMI is a simple anthropometric measure of overall body size rather than a direct measure of body composition. As it cannot distinguish fat mass from fat-free mass (FFM), BMI may underestimate obesity-related risk in older adults who have significant central fat accumulation alongside muscle loss [20, 21]. Consequently, BMI is inadequate for characterizing the age-related redistribution of fat and FFM. In contrast, WC directly captures central/abdominal adiposity, which is strongly linked to the chronic inflammation and insulin resistance implicated in SO pathogenesis [7, 22]. The age-related shift of fat to central depots [23, 24]. makes WC a more sensitive and clinically relevant indicator for identifying metabolically high-risk SO.

Despite these fundamental differences, an important observation emerged. The SO prevalence identified by BMI (3.2%) was similar to the rates reported by Yang et al. [25], who used bioimpedance-based body fat percentage and muscle mass. This similarity suggests that the BMI-based definition, despite its limitations, likely captures a severe and advanced SO phenotype where substantial fat accumulation coexists with pronounced muscle loss, a profile also detectable by more precise body composition methods. In contrast, the WC-based classification appears to identify a broader spectrum of at-risk individuals, including those with central obesity and earlier stages of muscle decline.

Factors influencing subtype classification of sarcopenic obesity in older adults

Multinomial logistic regression analysis revealed that the distribution of SO subtypes was influenced by multiple sociodemographic, behavioral, and health-related factors, with notable variation between Method 1 and Method 2. These differences suggest that the two methods capture distinct distinct phenotypic profiles within the SO spectrum, rather than identifying identical populations.

Age was a significant positive correlate across all models, supporting the central role of aging in the coexistence of muscle loss and fat accumulation. Potential mechanisms include myofiber degeneration and atrophy, declines in anabolic hormones (e.g., testosterone, estrogen, IGF-1), mitochondrial dysfunction, and chronic low-grade inflammation; these changes drive reductions in FFM and increases in abdominal fat, thereby elevating SO risk [26, 27].

A pivotal finding was the differential association of sex and income with SO depending on the method used. Male sex and low monthly income (< 1000 CNY) were significant risk factors specifically under Method 2, but not Method 1. This discrepancy likely reflects the distinct phenotypes captured by each method. Waist circumference, used in Method 2, is a commonly used proxy for central (visceral) adiposity, a pattern more prevalent in men, whereas Method 1 may underestimate adiposity in men with central obesity but normal weight [28, 29]. The association between low income and SO observed exclusively with Method 2 may indicate that socioeconomic disadvantage is linked to a phenotype characterized by central fat accumulation. This association may co-occur with factors such as unhealthy dietary patterns or limited access to health resources [30], which might simultaneously influence both central obesity and muscle health. However, given the cross-sectional design, these observations represent associations rather than established causal pathways. They underscore that the WC-based criterion is more sensitive to sociodemographic gradients in identifying a high-risk SO phenotype centered on visceral adiposity.

A key observation that reinforces this phenotypic distinction is the more pronounced association between multimorbidity and SO under Method 1 compared to Method 2. This does not contradict the established role of waist circumference in predicting metabolic risk but rather suggests that the two methods may identify distinct expressions within the SO spectrum. Method 2 appears to capture a broader, more heterogeneous at-risk population, which likely includes many individuals with signs of metabolic dysregulation who have not yet developed diagnosed multimorbid conditions. Conversely, Method 1 tends to select for a subgroup with higher overall adiposity and more pronounced muscle loss, a phenotype that frequently coexists with multiple chronic diseases. Consequently, the significant association between multimorbidity and Method 1 SO may reflect the greater disease burden within this subgroup, not a superior predictive capability of the BMI metric itself.

Many individuals identified by Method 2, particularly older adults, may be characterized by a state of “normal-weight obesity”, where body weight remains stable or declines due to muscle loss while visceral adiposity stays high, potentially sustaining elevated metabolic risk. This population tends to be consistently captured by Method 2 but is likely to remain undetected by Method 1. On the other hand, Method 1 SO represents a phenotype in which clinical complexity appears to be more evident and sociodemographic gradients tend to be less pronounced. Together, these findings suggest that Method 2 is more sensitive to risk associated with sociodemographic factors, while Method 1 identifies individuals who tend to have a phenotype associated with higher levels of multimorbidity.

Association of sarcopenic obesity subtypes with depression and cognitive impairment

Binary logistic regression analyses revealed that both the IS and SO groups demonstrated significantly higher risks of depressive symptoms and cognitive impairment compared to the NONS group, irrespective of the classification method applied. Notably, the magnitude of these associations exhibited variation between Method 1 and Method 2, with the most pronounced differences observed for depressive symptoms.

The linkage between IS/SO and depression or cognitive decline likely arises from multiple interacting biological and behavioral mechanisms. Biologically, central adiposity secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) and adipokines, which increase systemic inflammatory burden and promote insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and reductions in neurotrophic factors such as BDNF—changes that impair neuroplasticity and are implicated in depression and cognitive impairment [9, 31–37]. Concurrently, muscle loss reduces the secretion of myokines, diminishes physical capacity and social participation, increases frailty and isolation, and thereby contributes to depressive symptoms and cognitive vulnerability.

The SO group defined by Method 1 demonstrated a substantially higher odds ratio for depressive symptoms (OR = 7.823; 95% CI:3.198–19.139) than that defined by Method 2 (OR = 2.105; 95% CI: 1.097–4.042). This pattern is consistent with the previously characterized phenotypic distinction: Method 1 identifies a more advanced SO stage, marked by greater overall adiposity and muscle loss, which is also reflected in its stronger association with multimorbidity. The markedly elevated depression risk in this group likely stems from the compounded physiological and functional burden of severe sarcopenia and obesity, which collectively exacerbate psychological distress. In contrast, the association strengths for cognitive impairment were more comparable between Method 1 (OR = 2.868; 95% CI: 1.214–6.773) and Method 2 (OR = 1.749; 95% CI: 1.031–2.965), suggesting that both definitions capture populations at risk for cognitive decline.

Limitations

This study adopted a cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inferences regarding the relationship between SO and psychological or cognitive outcomes. The use of convenience sampling may affect the representativeness and external validity of the findings. The measurement methods were relatively simplified: sarcopenia was determined using the Ishii score rather than direct assessment of muscle mass, and obesity was defined solely by BMI and waist circumference. Although these indices are widely used for preliminary obesity screening due to their simplicity, they do not fully capture the complexity of SO. In contrast, body fat percentage measured by bioelectrical impedance analysis provides more precise body composition information, but its application is limited by equipment availability and operational convenience. Moreover, the small sample size of SO cases defined by BMI reduced the statistical power for subgroup analyses. Potential confounders such as dietary patterns, physical activity levels, and inflammatory biomarkers were not included. Finally, the Ishii score used in this study was developed and validated in Japanese older adults. Future studies should validate the Ishii score and establish population-specific cutoff values for Chinese older adults, which would enhance diagnostic accuracy and cross-study comparability.

Conclusions

This study compared SO prevalence, agreement, and correlates using BMI and WC combined with the Ishii score in community-dwelling older adults. The key finding is that the obesity indicator choice defines distinct SO phenotypes. WC-based classification identified a substantially higher prevalence of SO than BMI (14.1% vs. 3.2%), capturing a broader spectrum of individuals with central adiposity and sarcopenia, and was more sensitive to sociodemographic risk factors like male sex and low income. In contrast, the BMI-based definition identified a smaller, more severe SO subgroup that demonstrated markedly stronger associations with depressive symptoms. Both definitions were significantly associated with cognitive impairment. These findings highlight that WC is a more sensitive tool for community-level SO screening, while BMI-defined SO may indicate a critical subgroup with compounded physical and mental health risks. Public health and clinical strategies should therefore incorporate central obesity measures and address socioeconomic determinants, while being aware of the severe psychological burden in advanced SO cases identified by high BMI.
