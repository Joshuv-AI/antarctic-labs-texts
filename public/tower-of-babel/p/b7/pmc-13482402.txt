A symbiotic skin hydrogel interface enabled by flexible hydrogel network with embedded enhancement structure
Authors: Jun Ma, Mingxu Wang, Yongfeng Wang, Qian Wu, Cunkai Zhou, Yuchen Zhou, Changlei Ge, Jixiao Guo, Feijun Zhao, Mingming Hao, Chonghui Fan, Feng Wen, Shuqi Wang, Mengyuan Liu, Yujie Liu, Hao Shen, Lianhui Li, Ting Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWearable electrophysiological monitoring based on hydrogel electrodes is pivotal for decoding the body’s “electrical language”, yet fundamentally hampered by the unstable mechano-electrical interface between flexible electrodes and the skin caused by dehydration and poor breathability. Here, we demonstrate a symbiotic interface between an embedded-interfacial enhanced breathable conductive hydrogel network (BCHN) and skin for high-fidelity long-term electrophysiological monitoring. By embedding sodium chloride-containing polyvinyl alcohol hydrogel into an oxidized electrospun 3D porous polylactic acid skeleton, a BCHN with embedded enhanced interface featuring dense ion transport pathways and multiple water molecule-adsorbing sites is constructed. Upon application, the breathable (1.85 kg·m⁻²·day⁻¹, ~3× skin perspiration) flexible conductive hydrogel network with bending stiffness of ~10−10 N·m² seamlessly conforms to the microscopic landscape of the skin, forming a symbiotic BCHN-skin interface, which allows BCHN to “breathe” in harmony with the skin to preserve stable hydration and conductivity by dynamically balancing sweat capture, permeation, and evaporation, evidenced by a sustained 55 Ω impedance even at 20%RH. Integrated into a wearable monitoring system, the BCHN electrodes maintain high-quality signals (SNR > 25 dB) for over 30 days, thereby permitting the quantitative assessment and early warning of driver fatigue through long-term electroencephalography analysis.Subject terms: Gels and hydrogels, Electronic devices, Gels and hydrogels

Introduction

Wearable electrophysiological monitoring breaks through the spatiotemporal constraints of traditional healthcare, enabling direct interpretation of the “electrical language” of nerves and organs for early assessing the functional status of the body preceding observable behavioral changes or peripheral physiological signals1–6. This holds critical value in scenarios such as prolonged driving, where fatigue accumulation often escapes conventional behavioral detection—yet according to the World Health Organization and global traffic statistics, it contributes directly to millions of accidents and tens of thousands of fatalities annually7,8. The acquisition of comfortable, long-term stable, and high-quality electrophysiological signals has long been a central focus of research in wearable electrophysiological monitoring, pursued through advances in material and structural design of flexible electrodes9–13. However, establishing an optimal mechano-electrical interface between flexible electrodes and the complex skin surface remains challenge, posing a major barrier to reliable, data-driven body state analysis through electrophysiological monitoring that urgently needs to be overcome6,14–18.

Compared to electronically conductive electrode systems that rely on ion-to-electron transition, flexible ion-conductive hydrogels exhibit innate compatibility with the ionic physiological environment of human skin, enabling low-impedance ion-to-ion conduction interfaces that significantly improve electrophysiological signal fidelity, making them ideal for next-generation wearable monitoring devices19–21. However, fundamental challenges inherent to the aqueous ionic system of hydrogels—including water loss22, interfacial shear slippage23, and insufficient breathability24—continue to persistently hinder their reliability and practicality as stable wearable electrophysiological electrodes25. The addition of high concentrations of glycerol is a widely adopted hydration strategy for hydrogels21,26, yet its high viscosity not only results in an undesirable sticky texture but also dilutes mobile ions and increases migration resistance, thereby raising electrical impedance, which compromises the detection of weak electrophysiological signals such as electroencephalography (EEG)27–30. Besides, the occlusive interface formed by poorly breathable conductive ionic hydrogels disrupts the natural physiological functions of the skin, along with shear motion between the hydrated hydrogel and the skin, resulting in a dual negative impact on both wearing comfort and signal quality5,8,19,31–33. Breaking through the aforementioned constraints of current ion-conductive hydrogels and constructing a breathable28,33–37, interfacially and electrically stable hydrogel electrode-skin interface is an urgent and necessary step toward transforming the advantages of hydrogels into reliable, high-fidelity electrophysiological monitoring5,6,38–43.

In this work, we demonstrate a symbiotic interface between embedded-interfacial enhanced breathable conductive hydrogel network (BCHN) and skin for high-fidelity long-term electrophysiological monitoring. By embedding a sodium chloride (NaCl)-containing poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) hydrogel into an oxidized electrospun 3D porous polylactic acid (PLA) skeleton, a breathable conductive hydrogel network featuring an embedded enhanced interface and abundant micro-indentations was constructed. The fabricated BCHN integrates the advantages of both hydrogel materials (softness and conductivity) and electrospun films (porous structure), achieving a bending stiffness of 10−10 N·m2, a breathability of 1.85 kg·m−2·day−1, and an electrical conductivity of 1.43 S·m−1. It is particularly noteworthy that owing to its soft, breathable, and embedded interface design, BCHN can seamlessly conform to the microscopic landscape of the skin, forming a symbiotic BCHN-skin interface, which allows BCHN to “breathe” in harmony with the skin to preserve stable hydration and conductivity by dynamically balancing sweat capture, permeation, and evaporation, resulting in sustained impedance of 55 Ω at 1 kHz even under 20% RH conditions. Based on conformal adhesion, combined with exceptional breathability and conductivity, the BCHN wearable electrophysiological electrode delivers high-fidelity signals during physical exertion and sweating, and even sustains a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) exceeding 25 dB over a 30-day monitoring period. Finally, integrated into a wearable EEG monitoring system, we realized long-term real-time specific features (e.g., Electroencephalographic oscillations across distinct frequency ranges) in the EEG signals to enable quantified assessment of driving fatigue levels and provide early-warning alerts.

Results

Structural and mechanical properties of the BCHN electrodes

The BCHN electrodes were fabricated by embedding a NaCl-containing PVA hydrogel into an oxidized electrospun 3D porous PLA skeleton, followed by rapid immersion in a 7.5 wt% glycerol solution (Figs. 1a and S1). This integrated structure provides continuous pathways for ion transport and water-vapor diffusion, while its ultrathin and compliant nature enables conformal contact with skin and suppresses interfacial slippage. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed the highly interconnected 3D porous PLA skeleton fabricated by electrospinning, where the hydrogel conformally wrapped around individual fibers rather than forming a continuous bulk film that would occlude inter-fiber channels, creating an interpenetrating network (Fig. 1b). Cross-sectional SEM images (Fig. S4) further confirmed the ultrathin configuration, with total thicknesses of only a few micrometers, underscoring the potential of this structure as a lightweight, transparent, and imperceptible bioelectronic interface. Following, FT-IR spectroscopy was utilized to examine the molecular bonding characteristics and verify the hypothesis of hydrogen-bonding interactions. As anticipated, for BCHN obvious blueshift of the peak assigned to O–H underwent from 3337 to 3300 cm−¹, and a similar blueshift from 1750 to 1747 cm⁻¹ was also observed corresponding to the stretching vibration of C = O (Fig. S5). These findings indicated the formation of chain entanglements and intermolecular hydrogen bonds within the polymer network, which is also consistent with the XPS analysis. C1s deconvolution of PVA@PLA exhibited distinct components at ~284.8 eV (C–C/C–H), ~285.6 eV (C–OH), ~286.3 eV (C–O), and ~288.5 eV (C = O) (Figs. S6 and S7). Taken together, the FT-IR shifts and the evolution of the C1s chemical state substantiate robust hydrogen-bond-mediated coupling at the fiber-hydrogel interface, which underpins the interface and mechanical functional stability of the BCHN electrodes. These interfacial bonds effectively prevented delamination or phase separation during hydration-dehydration cycles, thereby ensuring mechanical and functional stability under physiological conditions.

The degree of hydrogel coating could be finely tuned by adjusting the concentration of the PVA solution and the spin-coating speed. As shown in Figs. 1c and S8, both porosity and pore distribution were preserved when optimized conditions were applied, increasing the PVA concentration from 1 wt% to 7 wt% progressively reduced the membrane porosity from ~50% to ~18%, while an optimal value of 3 wt% PVA@PLA maintained ~41% porosity. Similarly, higher spin-coating speed promoted uniform spreading but partially densified the structure, with porosity decreasing from ~75% (uncoated PLA) to ~20% at 1000 rpm. Under optimized parameters (3 wt% PVA at 5000 rpm), the fiber morphology remained intact, with pore diameters preserved at 1–2 µm, indicating that the hydrogel conformally embedded into the 3D porous PLA skeleton without clogging vapor channels, thereby maintaining high breathability for skin-interface applications (Table S2). This delicate balance is key to the material’s dual function: the preserved open channels ensure high breathability, while the embedded hydrogel network provides abundant hydrophilic surfaces that offer additional adsorption sites for water molecules, thereby sustaining dynamic hydration equilibrium and preventing dehydration during long-term use. This structure enabled high water vapor transmission (WVTR, Figs. 1d and S9) of 1.85 kg·m⁻²·day⁻¹, which about three times that of human skin (~0.6 kg·m⁻²·day⁻¹), while outperforming previously reported hydrogel or polymer composite electrodes in breathability (Fig. S10) and maintaining stable hydration with only ~6% water loss after 14 days (Fig. S11). Systematic variation of film thickness reveals a thickness-dependent vapor transport behavior, with thinner membranes exhibiting higher WVTR (Fig. 1e), consistent with reduced diffusion length. As the thickness increases from 10 μm to 80 μm, the WVTR decreases but remains well above the physiological threshold, indicating sustained breathability under increased thickness. Structural characterization further shows that the porous architecture is preserved across thicknesses (Figs. S12 and S13), with the pore size distribution remaining in the micrometer–submicrometer range (~3–10 μm) and porosity decreasing moderately from ~45% to ~31%. Despite this ~14% reduction, the interconnected pore network remains continuous, enabling efficient vapor transport. Such exceptional breathability was complemented by high optical transparency (Fig. S14), allowing the ultrathin films to remain nearly invisible when laminated on skin.

The 3D porous skeleton structure and embedded fiber-hydrogel interfaces endowed the BCHN with remarkable mechanical compliance. Stress–strain analyses (Fig. S15) revealed that the tensile behavior of the composite could be rationally tuned by the spin-coated PVA concentration. The Young’s modulus increased from ~6.5 MPa (1 wt% PVA) to ~33 MPa (7 wt% PVA), indicating a progressive stiffening as hydrogel loading densified the fibrous network. This structural tunability endowed the electrodes with a low intrinsic modulus suitable for soft biointerface. Moreover, film thickness (Fig. S16) exerted a decisive influence on mechanical compliance: the modulus rose from ~12 MPa at 10 µm to ~85 MPa at 80 µm, showing that thinner films exhibited markedly reduced stiffness and enhanced flexibility. At a thickness of ~10 µm, the BCHN electrodes exhibited an ultralow bending modulus on the order of 10−10 N·m², achieving good softness and mechanical compliance consistent with the structural-mechanical synergy described above. The ultrathin BCHN electrodes conformed intimately to epidermal microtextures when laminated onto skin (Fig. 1f), remaining nearly invisible yet firmly interlocked within skin ridges to suppress lateral slippage and maintain stable, long-term adhesion under motion. This imperceptible integration, enabled by the embedded fiber-hydrogel topology, allowed the BCHN electrodes to conform seamlessly across multiple length scales from delicate neural tissues (10−9 N·m²) to macroscopic skin features (10−6 N·m²) such as fingerprints, palm ridges, and facial wrinkles (Fig. 1g, h). Such intimate lamination substantially enlarged the effective skin–electrode contact area and minimized interfacial impedance mismatches, ensuring stable, high-fidelity signal acquisition under natural motion. This structural-mechanical synergy established a direct link between mechanical compliance and electrophysiological fidelity. Furthermore, the integration of the embedded reinforced interface endowed the electrodes with high breathability, ensuring physiological comfort and long-term reliability for stable, high-fidelity bioelectronic monitoring.

Electrical properties and signal fidelity

As illustrated in Fig. 2a, b, the electrical performance of the BCHN electrodes was systematically evaluated to elucidate their high-fidelity conduction mechanism. In conventional smooth, non-porous fiber networks, ionic transport is confined to surface-bound hydrogels, where conduction proceeds mainly through segmental hopping along hydrogen-bonded PVA chains, resulting in long and tortuous pathways. In sharp contrast, the electrospun 3D porous PLA skeleton allows the hydrogel to infiltrate and embed within the interconnected pores, forming hybrid pathways that couple polymer-chain hopping with liquid-like ionic diffusion. This structure builds a three-dimensional hydrated network that increases the density of conductive pathways, which in turn shortens transport distance, enhances ion migration, and maximizes overall conductivity. Building upon this, the BCHN electrodes exhibited a low impedance of 19.93 Ω at 1 kHz (Fig. 2c), well within the range required for stable skin–electrode interfaces. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements revealed a clear frequency-dependent behavior (Figs. 2d and S17), from which the ionic conductivity was estimated to be 1.43 S·m⁻¹ (Fig. S18). To understand the origin of the reduced impedance, equivalent circuit modeling was performed (Fig. S19). The improved electrical performance arises from a combination of enhanced bulk ionic transport (Rs reduced from 0.87 to 0.67 Ω with increasing ionic content), decreased interfacial resistance (Rct reduced from 17.7 to 6.9 kΩ), and increased effective contact area enabled by the porous architecture, as evidenced by the consistently lower Rs and Rct compared to smooth counterparts (Fig. S20 and Table S3). Notably, comparison with previously reported electrodes21,44–51 (Fig. 2e) highlights that the BCHN electrode achieves a balance of ultralow impedance and high WVTR, surpassing previously reported designs and resolving the intrinsic trade-off between electrical and breathable performance in soft electrodes.

To assess functional relevance, voltage-tracking tests were conducted under square-wave stimuli with an amplitude of 1 Vpp across frequencies from 0.1 to 10,000 Hz, while an oscilloscope simultaneously recorded the input (reference) and output (measured) signals to mimic the spectral range of human electrophysiological activity. As shown in Fig. 2f, g, the BCHN electrodes accurately reproduced input square-wave signals across the full frequency range from 0.1 Hz to 10 kHz, with negligible phase delay (<0.5°) and amplitude attenuation (<0.1%). In contrast, nonporous counterparts exhibited pronounced distortion and signal decay, particularly at high frequencies (>1 kHz), where the measured amplitude dropped by nearly 5% (Figs. 2h, i and S21). In addition, representative frequency evaluations at 0.1, 10, 1000, and 100,000 Hz further confirmed this fidelity (Fig. S21), where the BCHN electrodes consistently reproduced the input waveforms with minimal phase delay and amplitude attenuation, in stark contrast to the distorted responses observed in nonporous electrodes. This fidelity arose from the markedly reduced impedance and the continuous ionic pathways provided by the hydrated porous network (Fig. 2a), which together enable rapid and accurate responses to dynamic electrophysiological inputs. More importantly, whereas existing epidermal electronics typically relied on electron or mixed ion–electron conduction, our electrodes exploit purely ionic transport. This design inherently avoided impedance mismatch at the bioelectronic interface (Fig. 2e), where skin-generated ionic currents must otherwise undergo ion–electron conversion, and thereby preserved high-fidelity signal transmission even under dynamic and sweat-rich conditions. In addition, the electrodes exhibited a high charge injection capacity (CIC) of 0.535 mC·cm-2, which exhibits stable performance without noticeable decay after 1000 cycles, underscoring their durability for long-term bioelectronic applications (Fig. S22). Collectively, these results confirmed that the BCHN electrodes deliver low-impedance, high-conductivity, and stable signal-tracking performance, thereby addressing a critical requirement for long-term and high-quality EMG, ECG, and EEG acquisition in real-world applications.

Symbiotic microenvironmental regulation for stable skin–electrode interfaces

Leveraging the embedded enhancement structure that couples a 3D porous skeleton with a hydrogel network, the BCHN electrode was innovatively engineered to form a symbiotic, breathable skin interface capable of maintaining dynamic microenvironmental equilibrium, as shown in Fig. 3a. Here, the symbiotic skin-hydrogel interface is defined as a self-regulated interfacial system, in which hydration and ionic conduction are maintained through continuous bidirectional exchange with the skin. Specifically, the embedded architecture establishes continuous ionic pathways and abundant hydrophilic adsorption sites, enabling efficient uptake of dermal perspiration (Jin), while the porous fibrous scaffold facilitates controlled vapor evaporation (Jout). This dynamic balance between moisture influx and evaporation (Jnet = Jin − Jout ≈ 0) sustains a stable hydration state at the interface, thereby ensuring consistent electrochemical performance during prolonged operation. Unlike conventional breathable or porous hydrogel electrodes that primarily rely on passive vapor transmission or bulk water retention, the present system couples vapor transport, ionic conduction, and mechanical compliance within a unified interfacial architecture, enabling stable functionality even under low-humidity and long-term conditions, embodying the essence of the “symbiotic interface” (Fig. 3a). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (Fig. 3b) revealed a frequency-dependent decrease in impedance with increasing hydrogel water content from 40 to 120%, dropping from 1600 to 35 Ω at 1 kHz, marking a nearly two orders of magnitude attenuation. A clear threshold emerged at ~80% hydration: below this point, impedance rose steeply with minor dehydration, whereas above it, further hydration caused little change. This transition indicates that ionic transport becomes significantly facilitated once continuous hydration pathways percolate through the porous network. On-skin impedance remained stably low (<3 kΩ @ 1 kHz) during both resting and exercise states, while optical images (Fig. 3c) confirmed seamless conformability to skin microtextures. Even under perspiration, the BCHN electrodes maintained breathable and stable conduction, preventing heat and moisture buildup.

To avoid the drawbacks of high-glycerol formulations, including ion dilution, migration resistance, and sticky occlusion, the glycerol loading was minimized to only 7.5 wt% of the total film mass, providing sufficient hydrophilicity for water retention and stabilized hydration, thereby maintaining both comfort and conductivity. Moisture capture analysis (Fig. 3d) revealed a symbiotic water exchange between the skin and the BCHN electrode, achieving dynamic equilibrium among evaporation (Jout), absorption (Jin), and perspiration, and maintaining hydration stability across 20-80% RH. Time-resolved tests (Fig. 3e) showed that water content and impedance stabilized within ~40 min, evidencing autonomous moisture regulation through vapor exchange rather than occlusive sealing. Consistent with this dynamic hydration equilibrium, ionic conductivity increased steadily with humidity, reaching ~0.6 S·m⁻¹ at 80% RH (Fig. 3f), and remained nearly unchanged over 150 min of continuous wear at 60% RH (Fig. 3g). Under varying humidity conditions, the humidity-dependent impedance change is reversible, the electrodes exhibit consistently lower impedance at high humidity (~45 Ω at 80% RH) and higher impedance at low humidity (~80 Ω at 30% RH), while maintaining minimal variation across repeated cycles. (Fig. S24). These findings establish the BCHN as a symbiotic skin–electrode interface that maintains long-term hydration-conduction balance, thereby laying the foundation for next-generation continuous, high-fidelity electrophysiological monitoring.

Electrophysiological signal acquisition: EMG and ECG

To evaluate the electrophysiological signal acquisition capability of BCHN electrodes, dynamic EMG and ECG signal monitoring was implemented. As shown in Fig. 4a–c, the porous fiber-hydrogel electrodes exhibited consistently stable ECG waveforms with well-defined P-QRS-T components, maintaining a mean amplitude of ~1.95 mV higher than that of commercial and smooth-structured counterparts. Moving-average heatmap analyses further revealed that the porous electrodes exhibited the smallest dispersion area (σ ≈ 0.05 mV, density >130), nearly twice as dense as commercial (density ≈70) and smooth electrodes (density ≈72), confirming temporal stability and minimal signal fluctuation during long-term ECG acquisition. In real-world monitoring, the BCHN electrodes delivered consistently stable ECG signals with amplitude fluctuations < ±0.1 mV and baseline drift <0.05 mV over 10,000 s (Fig. 4d), maintaining clear P-QRS-T waveforms across working, resting, and walking states, demonstrating good motion tolerance and high signal fidelity under dynamic conditions. Notably, owing to their intrinsic breathability, the electrodes sustained stable performance even under perspiration, ensuring uninterrupted acquisition and user comfort (Fig. S35). Extending the monitoring window further reinforced these advantages: after 3, 7, and 30 days of continuous use, ECG waveforms recorded with the BCHN electrodes remained essentially unchanged (Fig. 4e), confirming their long-term stability, reliable signal quality, and sustained wearing comfort. Quantitative SNR analysis further highlighted this durability, with the BCHN electrode achieving nearly 28 dB on day 3 about 10% higher than commercial gels (25 dB)-and maintaining >25 dB after 30 days, whereas commercial electrodes declined sharply to 18 dB (27% reduction) (Fig. 4f). Such long-term stability stems directly from the distinct hydration and breathability features of our embedded porous design. Traditional hydrogels are thick, non-breathable, and prone to sweat accumulation and dehydration, causing discomfort and conductivity loss during long-term use. In contrast, the embedded porous BCHN architecture precisely regulates water retention and vapor exchange, establishing a dynamic balance between hydration and trans-epidermal evaporation. This breathable equilibrium prevents ion concentration drift and dehydration-induced failure, ensuring stable, high-fidelity electrophysiological monitoring over extended durations.

Building upon the ECG performance, we further evaluated the electrodes in capturing dynamic sEMG signals to probe their signal reliability under muscular activity. As shown in Fig. 4g, recordings obtained with the BCHN electrodes during repeated fist-clenching exhibited markedly reduced background noise and a higher signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) compared with both commercial and nonporous counterparts. This arises from the electrode’s robust conformability to the skin across the strain range of natural skin and muscle motion (Fig. S26). The lower baseline amplitude of background noise in the BCHN electrodes directly translated into a higher signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), further substantiating this advantage and highlighting their capability for reliable and high-fidelity signal acquisition. Consistently, heatmap analyses (Fig. 4h) illustrated stronger effective signal intensity for the porous electrodes, thereby reinforcing their performance in dynamic sEMG monitoring. To evaluate electrode functionality under authentic perspiration conditions, sEMG signals were recorded in vivo before and after sweating (Fig. 4i). The BCHN electrodes maintained a high SNR of 28 dB with minimal noise increase (Noise power <1 × 10−3 mV²) even after sweating, in sharp contrast to commercial and nonporous electrodes, whose SNR dropped by over 40% (to 16–18 dB) accompanied by a twofold rise in noise power (Fig. 4j), and the microstructure on the 3D porous fibers will not be deformed due to the absorption of water or sweat by PVA (Fig. S27). These results confirm the moisture tolerance and signal stability of the BCHN architecture under real physiological conditions. These findings suggest that the reliable monitoring under perspiration arises primarily from the high breathability, intimate skin conformability, and structural stability of the BCHN electrodes. Finally, biocompatibility tests (Fig. 4k) confirmed that the BCHN electrodes are non-toxic and well-tolerated on the skin, with extracts from electrodes aged for up to 30 days also showing no cytotoxicity (Fig. S28), supporting their suitability for prolonged wearable use.

EEG acquisition: relaxation and concentration states

Having established the high performance of the BCHN electrodes in ECG and sEMG monitoring, we next focused on EEG acquisition, which is more challenging yet more informative, offering direct access to neural dynamics and fatigue-related cognitive markers. A custom EEG acquisition system was constructed by integrating a Bluetooth-based wireless recording module, commercially fabricated screen-printed electrodes, and the flexible BCHN electrodes (Fig. 5a). To ensure a fair comparison, the effective contact area of the BCHN electrodes was designed to be comparable to that of commercial electrodes, and all measurements were conducted under identical conditions (Fig. S29). To extend the applicability of the BCHN electrodes to central nervous system monitoring, EEG signals were recorded from the frontal region (FP1 and FP2 channels). As shown in Figs. 5a and S31–32, a custom-designed mobile interface enabled real-time wireless acquisition and visualization of two-channel EEG signals via Bluetooth, with the system configuration illustrated in Fig. S31. While real-time monitoring and basic feature extraction (e.g., band power estimation) were performed on the smartphone (Fig. S30 and Movie S1), the EEG data shown in Fig. 5 were processed offline for quantitative analysis of fatigue-related features. EEG recordings from FP1 and FP2 (Fig. 5b) demonstrated that the porous electrodes captured high-fidelity signals, with delta (δ), theta (θ), alpha (α), beta (β), and gamma (γ) bands clearly distinguished by frequency-domain analysis (spectral decomposition), confirming accurate spectral decomposition and signal fidelity (Fig. 5c).

Further evaluation of long-term performance was carried out over a 2-h monitoring period across different behavioral states, including rest, computer-based work, and relaxation (Fig. 5d). Power spectral density (PSD) analyses revealed distinct state-dependent modulation of EEG rhythms: during resting, α-band activity (~10 Hz) dominated with PSD, whereas computer-based work markedly suppressed α power by nearly 60%, accompanied by a relative increase in β-band activity (18–22 Hz). Relaxation restored α dominance, confirming the electrode’s ability to capture subtle cognitive-state transitions with high spectral fidelity. alongside enhanced β power, reflecting heightened attentional demand. Under relaxation, θ and α bands became dominant, indicative of reduced cognitive load and a transition toward a more restful state. These distinct spectral signatures provide mechanistic insight into the ability of the porous electrodes to resolve cognitive states such as focused attention versus relaxation. Importantly, when benchmarked against commercial gel electrodes, our porous electrodes demonstrated stable EEG acquisition throughout the monitoring period, whereas signals from commercial gels exhibited notable drift and instability over time (Fig. 5e). Taken together, the results establish the BCHN electrodes as a stable, high-fidelity platform for EEG acquisition, directly enabling exploration of cognitive dynamics between focused attention and relaxation. In addition, we extended the measurements to afternoon nap states, where the heatmap (Fig. S36) visualization also revealed characteristic spectral transitions, further confirming the electrode’s capability to capture state-dependent neural dynamics.

As shown in Fig. 5f–m, the BCHN electrodes enabled stable EEG acquisition at the FP1 and FP2 sites across distinct cognitive conditions. In the focused state, raw traces and spectrograms (Fig. 4g) revealed enhanced high-frequency activity, with PSD analysis showing β-band (~13–30 Hz) dominance accompanied by suppressed α rhythms (Fig. 5h). By contrast, the relaxed state exhibited attenuated high-frequency activity and pronounced θ (~4–8 Hz) and α (~8–13 Hz) power (Fig. 5k, l), consistent with reduced cognitive load. Notably, the frontal alpha asymmetry (FAA, FP1–FP2) remained directionally consistent and highly stable throughout 8 min of monitoring, with mean FAA values fluctuating within ±0.2 and standard deviations around 0.1–0.2 across both focused and relaxed states (Fig. 5i, m), confirming the electrode’s capability for long-term, drift-free EEG acquisition. This result reflects the neurophysiological principle that α power is inversely related to cortical activation: during focused states, enhanced engagement of right frontal attentional networks suppresses right-hemisphere α activity, yielding positive FAA values; whereas in relaxed states, reduced right frontal activation and relatively greater left frontal engagement result in elevated right-hemisphere α power and negative FAA values. These findings not only reinforce the capability of our electrodes to capture physiologically meaningful, state-dependent EEG signatures but also underscore their potential for cognitive state discrimination, continuous neural monitoring, and fatigue assessment in real-world applications.

Long-term driving test and fatigue evaluation

To further highlight the application potential of the BCHN electrodes, we conducted a 5-h continuous EEG monitoring test during real-road driving (Fig. 6a–d), illustrating four representative cognitive states experienced during driving: initial alertness, mild fatigue, music-induced relaxation, and severe fatigue. Throughout the entire session, our electrodes maintained perfect skin conformity and stable electrical contact, thereby enabling artifact-free, continuous dual-channel frontal EEG acquisition (Fig. 6e). This long-term stability stems directly from the electrodes’ high breathability, good conformability, and ionic conductivity, which not only prevented skin irritation and signal drift under sweating and motion but also enhanced ionic conduction to sustain a high stable signal acquisition over extended monitoring.

To elucidate the neural dynamics underlying prolonged driving, we quantified four established EEG-derived fatigue indices: the θ/α ratio (TAR), θ/β ratio (TBR), α/β ratio (ABR), and (θ + α)/β ratio (TPR). The physiological relevance of these indices was supported by concurrent subjective (Karolinska Sleepiness Scale, KSS) and behavioral (Stroop task) measurements (Note S1), which showed consistent trends with EEG-derived features (Figs. S33–35), indicating that the observed changes reflect genuine fatigue progression rather than artifacts of subject-wise normalization. Each index captures complementary features of fatigue progression: TAR and TBR reflect the gradual predominance of slow over fast rhythms; ABR indicates the suppression of α relative to β; and TPR simultaneously accounts for both θ elevation and α reduction, making it the most sensitive composite marker of drowsiness. In the simulated driving experiments, subject-specific reference ranges for these indices were established under controlled laboratory conditions (Fig. S38) based on each participant’s normalized baseline, and further validated with measurements from six participants (n = 6) to ensure robustness against inter-subject variability (Fig. S39). These ranges are not intended as universal thresholds, but rather as relative criteria for tracking intra-individual state transitions across alertness, mild fatigue, recovery, and severe fatigue. Fourier spectral analysis of time-resolved band power (Fig. 6f) revealed distinct state-dependent transitions across the four driving stages. As shown in Fig. 6g–j, EEG spectra revealed clear stage-dependent transitions during the driving task. In the alertness stage, α-β rhythms predominated, accompanied by minimal θ power and low TAR, TBR, and TPR but high ABR, indicative of optimal attentional focus. With mild fatigue, θ power increased and α decreased, yielding rising TAR/TBR and a declining ABR, marking the onset of drowsiness. During music relaxation, auditory stimulation partially restored cortical balance, reducing θ dominance and recovering α activity, with TAR and TPR decreasing by ~10–15% and ABR rebounding by ~8–12%. In the severe fatigue stage, slow-wave θ activity became dominant while α was strongly suppressed, driving TAR, TBR, and TPR to peak and ABR to its minimum, signifying deep fatigue and impaired vigilance linked to elevated accident risk.

In addition to spectral features, nonlinear dynamics of the EEG signals were further characterized using sample entropy and Rayleigh entropy (Figs. S38 and S39). Both metrics decreased consistently with increasing fatigue, indicating reduced signal complexity and enhanced neural regularity. Specifically, sample entropy decreased progressively from the alert to the severe fatigue state, reflecting diminished temporal irregularity, while Rayleigh entropy showed a parallel decrease, suggesting reduced phase variability and increased oscillatory synchronization. During the music relaxation stage, both entropy measures partially recovered, consistent with the restoration of α activity and reduced θ dominance. A comparison with commercial electrodes confirmed similar trends (Fig. S40). These nonlinear features are in strong agreement with the spectral indices, collectively reinforcing the robustness of the observed fatigue-related neural dynamics.

Finally, the composite fatigue score derived from four EEG indices (Fig. 6k) increased progressively with driving time, exceeding the threshold of 60 after 3 h and marking the onset of pronounced fatigue. These results highlight the high sensitivity of the BCHN electrodes in tracking state-dependent neural dynamics. By comparing with the existing reports, the BCHN electrode performed well in multiple key indicators (Table S1). Owing to their breathability, conformability, and low impedance, the electrodes maintained stability over 5 h of continuous monitoring, enabling precise mapping of neurodynamic transitions and real-time assessment of fatigue-mitigating interventions such as music. Collectively, this work demonstrates the electrodes’ capability for long-term, high-fidelity EEG acquisition and their promise in intelligent fatigue detection and traffic-safety monitoring.

Discussion

In summary, we developed a breathable conductive hydrogel network (BCHN) incorporating an embedded interfacial enhancement structure that forms a symbiotic, dynamically balanced interface with the skin. This structure fundamentally resolves the chronic challenges of conventional hydrogel electrodes including dehydration, poor breathability, and interfacial instability—by achieving self-regulated hydration through the dynamic balance of sweat capture, permeation, and evaporation, eliminating the need for high glycerol content. The BCHN electrodes exhibit ultralow impedance (~20 Ω @ 1 kHz), exceptional breathability (WVTR ≈ 1.85 kg·m⁻²·day⁻¹), and remarkable durability, maintaining SNR > 25 dB even after 30 days of continuous use. Integrated into a wearable EEG system, they reliably tracked cognitive transitions and quantified driver fatigue under real-world conditions, enabling high-resolution neurodynamic mapping and real-time fatigue assessment. By synergistically integrating breathability, mechanical compliance, and electrical stability within a unified porous-hydrogel framework, the BCHN design establishes a durable and symbiotic skin interface, paving the way for next-generation high-fidelity wearable bioelectronics in personalized healthcare and intelligent human–machine interaction.

Methods

Material

Poly (lactic acid) (PLA, weight-average molecular weight of 100,000; Zhejiang Hisun Biomaterials Co., Ltd.); Dichloromethane (DCM, Analytical purity), NaCl (Analytical purity) and N, N-Dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.5%) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China; glycerol (MW 92.09, 99.0%) and PVA (1799, alcoholysis degree 98–99%) were purchased from Shanghai Titan Scientific Co., Ltd., China. Deionized water was prepared with Milli-Q equipment (Merck, Germany).

Preparation of embedded fibrous hydrogel films

Poly (lactic acid) (PLA) microfiber networks were prepared by electrospinning. A 10 wt% PLA solution was obtained by dissolving PLA in a mixed solvent of DMF and DCM (1:9) and loaded into a 5 mL syringe. The PLA electro-spinning fiber membranes were fabricated using an electrospinning machine (YFSP-T) manufactured by Yunfan (Tianjin) Instrument Co., Ltd. Electrospinning was carried out under an applied voltage of 12 kV at a feeding rate of 0.1 mm·min⁻¹. The distance between the needle tip and the grounded glass collector was fixed at 15 cm. Electrospinning was performed for 1, 2, 5, or 8 min to obtain microfiber networks with different fiber densities and thicknesses.

The collected PLA microfibers were subjected to plasma treatment three times (1 min each) to generate hydrophilic surfaces (Figs. S2 and 3). A 3 wt% poly (vinyl alcohol) (PVA) aqueous solution was drop-cast onto the plasma-treated microfiber mats to allow complete infiltration, followed by spin-coating at 1.0, 3.0, or 5.0 krpm (Fig. S24). The resulting PVA-coated microfiber substrates were subsequently subjected to four freeze–thaw cycles using an ice-cream freezing machine (CAUKD, CBJF-1DC450), yielding embedded microfiber composite hydrogels (PVA@PLA).

For the preparation of BCHN, the obtained hydrogels were immersed in either a 30 wt% glycerol aqueous solution or a mixture of the 30 wt% glycerol solution and 0.9 wt% NaCl solution. The glycerol-saline mixtures were prepared by blending different volume ratios of the two solutions.

Preparation of PVA@PLA-GN-based electrodes

PLA microfiber networks were prepared using the same electrospinning parameters as described above, except that the electrospinning duration was set to 2 min. PVA was subsequently spin-coated onto the PLA microfibers at 5.0 krpm for 30 s. After four freeze–thaw cycles, the resulting PVA/MF/Gly-CH bioelectrodes were immersed in a mixed solution of glycerol and 0.9 wt% NaCl for 20 min to obtain PVA@PLA-GN fibrous hydrogel films. The hydrogel films were then laminated onto screen-printed electrodes with customized designs, yielding the final BCHN electrodes.

Characterization

The microscopic morphology of the hydrogel was examined by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, HITACHI, S4800). Elemental distribution was analyzed using an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS). The surface functional groups of the hydrogel were characterized by Fourier transform infrared microspectroscopy (FT-IR, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Nicolet 6700) in the range of 4000–500 cm⁻¹, enabling analysis of the chemical structure and functional group features. Further information on elemental composition, valence states, and chemical bonding was obtained by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Shimadzu, AXIS-ULTRA DLD, Japan), performed using non-monochromatized Cu Kα radiation as the excitation source.

The mechanical properties were evaluated using a universal testing machine (Instron 3365, Instron, Norwood, MA, USA). The specimens had dimensions of 10 mm × 2 mm, and at least three parallel replicates were performed for each group.

Electrophysiological recording

Electrophysiological monitoring was performed using a customized wireless acquisition system based on a Bluetooth-enabled module. The BCHN electrode was first cut into square patches (typically 1 × 1 cm²) on a glass substrate and then gently lifted using tweezers. The ultrathin electrode was subsequently placed directly onto the skin surface without the use of aggressive adhesives. BCHN electrodes and commercial electrodes attached to human skin with the same effective area (Fig. S25). Screen-printed BCHN electrodes were employed as the sensing interface, ensuring intimate skin contact and stable low-impedance coupling. For EEG recordings, two electrodes were integrated directly into a head-mounted cap together with the Bluetooth module, enabling compact, lightweight, and fully portable operation. Data acquisition was focused on the frontal region, specifically FP1 and FP2 channels of the international 10-20 system, which are particularly sensitive to attention and relaxation dynamics. The wireless system continuously transmitted dual-channel EEG signals at a sampling rate of 500 Hz, while on-board synchronization ensured temporal stability across extended recordings. During the experiments, participants underwent predefined conditions including resting, working, and relaxation tasks, allowing evaluation of EEG dynamics under multiple cognitive states.

Evaluation of biocompatibility

The in vitro biocompatibility of the BCHN electrodes was evaluated using mouse fibroblast cells (L929). L929 cells were purchased from National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures in China. L929 cells were seeded at a density of 4 × 10⁴ cells per well in 48-well plates containing the fiber-hydrogel samples leaching solution. After 24 and 72 h of culture, cell viability was assessed using a live/dead staining kit (Calcein-AM/PI, Beyotime). For proliferation analysis, L929 cells were seeded at a density of 8 × 10³ cells per well under the same conditions. Cell proliferation was quantitatively evaluated using a Cell Counting Kit-8 assay (CCK-8, Beyotime) after 24 and 72 h of incubation.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Office 2021) for initial calculations and preprocessing. Advanced signal processing and quantitative analysis were conducted in MATLAB (R2022b, MathWorks), including spectral decomposition, statistical evaluation, and custom algorithms where required. Graphical visualization and plotting of experimental results were carried out using OriginLab (OriginPro 2022, OriginLab Corporation). Statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA as appropriate, with a significance threshold of P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All reported results represent mean values with corresponding statistical measures, unless otherwise specified.

Ethics

The performance validation of the BCHN electrodes was conducted with human subjects. The participant (a 25-year-old male) participated in the study without compensation. Sex was self-reported. Due to the single-subject design, no sex- or gender- based analyses were performed, and disaggregated sex and gender data are not applicable. Written informed consent was obtained from the participant prior to the study. The study was conducted in accordance with ethical regulations and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Suzhou Institute of Nano-Tech and Nano Bionics (Approval ID: SINANO/EC/2022-075).
