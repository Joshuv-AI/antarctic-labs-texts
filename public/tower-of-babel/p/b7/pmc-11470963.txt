Evaluation of whole genome sequencing utility in identifying driver alterations in cancer genome
Authors: Takeshi Nagashima, Ken Yamaguchi, Kenichi Urakami, Yuji Shimoda, Sumiko Ohnami, Keiichi Ohshima, Tomoe Tanabe, Akane Naruoka, Fukumi Kamada, Masakuni Serizawa, Keiichi Hatakeyama, Shumpei Ohnami, Koji Maruyama, Tohru Mochizuki, Maki Mizuguchi, Akio Shiomi, Yasuhisa Ohde, Etsuro Bando, Teiichi Sugiura, Takashi Mukaigawa, Seiichiro Nishimura, Yasuyuki Hirashima, Koichi Mitsuya, Shusuke Yoshikawa, Yoshio Kiyohara, Yasuhiro Tsubosa, Hirohisa Katagiri, Masashi Niwakawa, Kaoru Takahashi, Hiroya Kashiwagi, Yoshichika Yasunaga, Yuji Ishida, Takashi Sugino, Hirotsugu Kenmotsu, Masanori Terashima, Mitsuru Takahashi, Katsuhiko Uesaka, Yasuto Akiyama
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn cancer genome analysis, identifying pathogenic alterations and assessing their effects on oncogenic processes is important. Although whole exome sequencing (WES) can effectively detect such changes, driver alterations could not be identified in 27.8% of the cases, according to a previous study. The objectives of the present study were to evaluate the utility of whole genome sequencing (WGS) and clarify its differences with WES in terms of driver alteration detection. For this purpose, WGS analysis was conducted on 177 driverless WES samples, selected from 5,480 fresh frozen samples derived from 5,140 Japanese patients with cancer. These samples were selected as primary tumor, both WES and transcriptome profiling were performed, estimated tumor content of ≥ 30%, and no driver alterations were identified by WES. WGS identified driver and likely driver alterations in 68.4 and 22.6% of the samples, respectively. The most frequent alteration type was oncogene amplification, followed by tumor suppressor gene deletion and small variants located outside the coding region. In the remaining 9.0% of samples, no such signals were identified; therefore, further investigations are required. The current study clearly demonstrated the role and utility of WGS in identifying genomic alterations that contribute to tumorigenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74272-0.Keywords: Cancer genome analysis, Whole genome sequencing, Gene expression profiling, Driver alterationSubject terms: Cancer genomics, Cancer genomics, Genome

Introduction

Advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) and related technologies have led to the widespread use of cancer gene panel testing in clinical practice. Whole exome sequencing (WES) and whole genome sequencing (WGS) are also becoming routine tests. Recent analyses of large cohorts, such as the International Cancer Genome Consortium, The Cancer Genome Atlas, and PanCancer Analysis of Whole Genome, have revealed the presence of large-scale copy number alterations, whole genome doubling, chromothripsis, and complex structural variants in many cancer types1–4. It is becoming clear that these genomic alterations, associated with prognosis and drug response, are more widespread and universal than previously thought, possibly enabling the stratification of cancer types5,6. The identification and interpretation of drivers and actionable alterations that contribute to tumorigenesis and drug response among the vast datasets generated by NGS are crucial and often limiting factors in cancer genome medicine. Overcoming these issues is essential for the clinical implementation of WGS.

In cancer genome analysis, cancer gene panel tests that sequence important target genes, WES of all genes, and WGS of whole genome regions have been widely utilized. WGS can be used to analyze the entire genome, including non-coding regions. Previous studies have reported that WGS has almost identical or slightly better performance when compared with WES in detecting germline and somatic single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions and deletions (indels)7–9. For larger changes, WGS reportedly detects germline copy number variants (CNVs) with higher accuracy and resolution because of its higher genome coverage and less variation in coverage depth than WES10,11. For germline structural variants (SVs), WGS is reportedly more sensitive12. Furthermore, WGS may also detect more somatic driver changes than WES; however, the extent of this ability and the differences between them have not yet been studied in detail.

We initiated the Project High-tech Omics-based Patient Evaluation (HOPE) in 2014 to catalog the structural, quantitative, and expression changes of all genes in Japanese patients with cancer, identify the factors that cause cancer based on these changes, and apply the results to drug discovery13. To date, multi-omics analyses, including cancer and fusion gene panels, WES, and microarray-based gene expression profiling (GEP), have been conducted for over 10,000 cases14–16. Some of the results have been released in the Japanese Cancer Genome Atlas (JCGA)17 and provide useful information in cancer genome medicine. The detection rate of driver alterations in 5,000 cases was 59.7% for the cancer gene panel and 72.2% for the combination of the four methods, including WES18. The remaining 27.8% were driverless cases, wherein no clear oncogenic changes were detected. Expanding the target region improves the detection rate of driver alterations and enables the detection of large-scale structural abnormalities at the chromosomal level.

The objectives of the present study are to evaluate the utility of WGS and clarify the differences in the detection ability between WGS and WES in detecting driver alterations in the cancer genome via WGS analysis for driverless WES cases. Our findings demonstrate the role and utility of WGS in identifying genomic alterations that contribute to oncogenesis.

Results

Selection of driverless WES samples for WGS analysis

We previously reported the multi-omics analysis results for 5,480 fresh frozen tumor samples derived from 5,143 Japanese patients with cancer who underwent surgery at Shizuoka Cancer Center Hospital18. Among these, no clear driver genomic alterations were identified in 27.8% of the samples using WES and/or GEP. To further investigate the driverless WES samples by WGS, we first re-evaluated the multi-omics dataset using an in-house analysis pipeline with the latest annotations. Next, samples that fulfilled all of the following criteria were selected: (1) primary tumor, (2) both WES and GEP analyses were performed, (3) WES-based estimated tumor content ≥ 30%, (4) no apparent or likely driver alterations were identified, (5) no apparent germline driver alterations were identified, and (6) enough residual DNA was available for WGS. In total, 177 samples were selected for analysis (Fig. 1a). The number of samples for each cancer type and their abbreviations are shown in Supplementary Table S1. Among these, stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) was the most common, followed by hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), breast invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), and colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) (Fig. 1b). Consistent with previous reports, these cancer types had low driver detection rates. The frequency of cancer types differed between the 177 and 2,845 WES driver-negative and -positive samples (Fig. 1c). Cancer types with low frequencies of driver alterations, such as STAD, HCC, and pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), were enriched in the former set.

WGS-based evaluation of genomic alterations in driverless WES samples

A total of 177 driverless WES samples were subjected to WGS. The mean (± standard deviation) depth of coverages for tumor and matched normal samples were 130.2 (± 11.1) and 36.8 (± 5.7), respectively. For these datasets, an in-house developed analysis pipeline designated “Shizuoka Multi-omics Analysis Protocol for WGS (SMAP-W)” (Fig. S1) was applied to evaluate the drivability of identified genomic alterations. This pipeline is an extended and optimized version of a previously established method for WES datasets. Five types of genomic changes were analyzed: (1) SNV and indels by WGS, (2) fusion genes by WGS, (3) oncogene amplification and tumor suppressor gene (TSG) deletion by integrative analysis of CNV (WGS) and GEP, (4) chromothripsis by WGS, and (5) oncogene overexpression and TSG suppression by GEP. The drivability of the somatic and germline variants was classified into five tiers, and the variants classified as tier1 were used in the drivability assessment. Chromothripsis, oncogene amplification, and TSG suppression supported by GEP only were classified as tier2 and regarded as likely driver alterations. Driver alterations were identified in 68.4% of the samples (121 out of 177) (Fig. 2a). Details of somatic and germline driver snv/indel are shown in Supplementary Table S2. Cancer types wherein drivers were most frequently detected included head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC) (100.0%) and renal clear cell carcinoma (CCRCC) (100.0%), followed by HCC (86.4%), gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) (80.0%), LUAD (78.6%), and rectal adenocarcinoma (READ) (75.0%). The least common cancer types for which no clear driver was identified were salivary carcinoma (SACA) (0.0%) and breast IDC (38.5%). When likely driver alterations were included, the detection rate reached 91.0% (161 out of 177). The type of genomic changes varied among the 302 alterations (Fig. 2b), with amplification being the highest (46.7%, 141 out of 302), followed by deletion (29.1%), snv/indel (18.9%), and fusion (5.3%). Structural changes, including amplification, deletion, and fusion, were the dominant alteration types in most cancer types, except for GIST. A higher coverage of structural changes (81.1%, 245 out of 302) indicated the superiority of WGS in detecting large-scale genomic changes. The number of driver alterations per sample varied among genes and cancer types (Fig. 3). The most frequently observed alterations were VHL frameshift variants in CCRCC (0.63 alterations per sample), followed by TERT promoter variants in HCC (0.59), CBLC amplification in LUAD (0.57), KIT indel variants in GIST (0.40), and CBLC amplification in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) (0.38). Four germline driver variants were identified in four samples, that is BLM in LUAD, BRCA2 in HCC, FANCG in HCC, and MRE11A in sarcoma, and all of them also had somatic driver alterations.

In terms of functional relevance of identified driver alterations, TERT promoter variants in HCC, hepatocellular carcinoma plus intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma and HNSC, KMT2A rearrangement in LUAD, CBLC amplification in LUAD and LUSC, and SLC34A2 deletion in STAD and PAAD were typical examples. A cytosine to thymine variant located 45 bp upstream from TERT transcription start site is a driver variant observed in many cancer types19. A 2,632 bp insertion that resulted in duplication of exon 9 and 10 of KMT2A was overlapped to a rearrangement hotspot region called major breakpoint cluster region20. KMT2A fusion is a prognostic marker and its frequency in LUAD was ranked the 2nd most frequent cancer type followed by acute myeloid leukemia according to AACR GENIE. Amplification of CBLC reportedly causes competition with CBL and inhibits EGFR degradation, thereby increasing the stability of activated EGFR and enhancing cell proliferation21. The suppressive effect of SLC34A2 on tumorigenesis and progression via down-regulation of MAPK and PI3K pathways has been reported22. Overall, WGS successfully identified driver alterations in SNVs in non-exonic region as well as, SVs and CNVs.

Difference between WES- and WGS-based driver identification

To further investigate the differences between WGS and WES, variants identified by WGS, but not WES, were manually inspected using an integrative genomics viewer23. The results are summarized in Supplementary Table S3. Among the 53 variants, 20 (37.7%) were missed because of their position in the genome. These were located outside the exon, which was not covered by WES. Another 17 variants (32.1%) could not be identified because of low variant allele frequency or sequence depth, although the mean (± standard deviation) coverage depth of WES for tumor samples was 127.6 (± 17.7). These results clearly demonstrate the importance of sequencing with sufficient depth for regions known to contain driver or actionable variants. The addition of a custom probe that captures these regions is the simplest solution to this problem. The remaining 16 variants (30.2%) were not detected because of technical issues, including strand bias, noise in normal samples, high variant allele frequency (VAF) in normal samples, variants located at the edge of amplicons, and stringent thresholds for variant filtering. In terms of the detection rate of driver alterations at the sample level, false negatives attributable solely to the sequencing platform were estimated to be 4.0% (7 out of 177 samples).

Characterization of driverless WGS samples

Although WGS-based evaluation of the cancer genome enabled the identification of oncogenic or likely oncogenic alterations in 91.0% (161 out of 177) of the samples, no such alterations were identified in the remaining 16 samples (9.0%). Of these, PAAD was the most frequent (four samples), followed by LUSC and STAD (three samples), COAD (two samples), and IDC, LUAD, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor and READ (one sample). To investigate the characteristics of these samples, the tumor content, number of small variants (SNVs and indels) calculated as tumor mutational burden (TMB), ploidy (mean copy number over the whole genome), and the number of SVs were examined. The samples were classified according to the status of driver alterations, i.e., driver-positive, likely driver-positive, likely driver-positive supported by transcriptome data only, and driver-negative. The results are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. The frequencies of the three types of somatic changes (snv/indel, CNV, and SV) were lower in the driverless samples. In addition, WGS-based tumor content estimates for five samples below 30%, although WES-based tumor content estimates for all samples exceeded ≥ 30%, which could be regarded as the lower bound of tumor content for identifying somatic variants24. To further inspect the factors that contribute to driver negativity, the possibility that the analyzed samples were derived from normal or non-tumor areas of specimens was evaluated by estimating the cancer type using somatic variants and transcriptome profiles. Among 16 samples, the cancer type for 13 (81.3%) and 2 samples (12.5%) were correctly and incorrectly estimated, respectively, and no results were obtained for the remaining 1 sample because the cancer type had ≤ 10 samples. The frequency of correct predictions indicated the possibility that these samples were derived from normal or non-tumor area of specimens to be low.

Discussion

The re-evaluation of the HOPE 5000 cohort based on the WES dataset with the latest annotations revealed that 19.2% was driver-negative. These samples were subject to technical challenges, such as low tumor content and sequencing platform issues, and somatic changes not detected by WES in principle, such as SVs and activating variants located in non-exon regions. Therefore, 177 samples that met multiple criteria were analyzed using WGS. Cancer type frequency in these samples differed among the 4,823 primary tumors, 3,089 samples with tumor content ≥ 30%, and 2,845 WES driver-positive samples. The former group was enriched in cancer types with a high percentage of driver-negative samples; however, no significant differences were observed among the three groups. WGS identified drivers in 68.4% of the samples. Driver changes were detected in all cases of HNSC and CCRCC and in more than 75% of HCC, GIST, LUAD, and READ. A total of 302 driver changes were detected in 121 samples. The average of 2.5 driver events per sample was similar to that of 2.3 reported previously18; however, the proportion of amplifications and deletions in the 302 alterations increased substantially from 21.7 to 75.8%. This percentage was almost similar to that of the WGS analysis (70.5%) for the WES driver-positive group. It is believed that the improved performance in detecting copy number changes contributes to an increase in the percentage of amplifications and deletions. Frequency of amplifications and deletions observed in WGS analysis results for the WES driver-positive group confirmed broader coverage and more uniform depth distribution was contributing factors for higher detection rate of structural changes. Taken together, WGS was useful in identifying drivers in more than two-thirds of driverless WES samples.

Of the remaining 56 samples, WGS yielded possible or suggestive changes in 40 samples (22.6%). The most likely changes included chromothripsis in 4 samples (2.3%), likely driver (Tier2) alterations in 6 samples (3.4%), and expression changes in 30 samples (16.9%). According to a previous study, chromothripsis is considered as a possible driver event25. The 4 samples wherein chromothripsis was detected comprised cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), COAD, IDC, and HCC. Large structural abnormalities were observed, in addition to a high frequency of SVs. CHOL, IDC, and HCC met at least two, whereas COAD met one of the following criteria: (1) a high homologous recombination deficiency score (≥ 42), (2) a high chromosome arm aneuploidy score (≥ 8), and (3) whole genome doubling. In five of the six Tier2 samples, multiple likely driver alterations and expression changes were observed, suggesting the contribution of multiple genes. Oncogene overexpression and TSG suppression were observed in 36 of 40 samples, wherein chromothripsis or Tier2 alterations co-occurred in 6 samples, and the remaining 30 samples showed expression changes only. The latter were amplification/deletion-independent expression changes; therefore, the presence of variants in the transcriptional regulatory regions was examined in 118 genes from 30 samples. However, this was considered unlikely because no somatic variants were detected in the promoter or enhancer within the 10-kb upstream region from the transcription start site. To investigate the involvement of SVs in altered expression, SVs in the surrounding 100-kb region of 118 genes, including the whole gene body, were searched and found to be inserted into TERT in one HCC case. The inserted sequence was estimated to be 648 bp, which contained the entire length of exon 1 of TERT and was 331 bp upstream of the transcription start site. This sequence is predicted to be inserted within the TERT transcription start site and the 39-bp upstream region. A recent study reported that duplicating the TERT promoter region has the same effect as that of well-known hotspot variants26, suggesting that the insertion identified in the present study may have a similar effect. No SV involvement was identified in the remaining 39 samples. Aberrations in epigenetic regulation, including methylation and chromatin modifications, should be investigated to further explore the possible mechanisms.

In the last 16 samples (9.0%), the drivers could not be identified even via an integrative analysis of the whole genome and transcriptome dataset. Of these, PAAD was the most frequent (four samples, 25.0%), followed by LUSC (three samples, 18.8%) and colon and rectal adenocarcinoma (three samples, 18.8%). The mean tumor content in the 16 samples was 33%, which was not extremely low but tended to be lower than that in the driver-positive samples. The possibility of non-tumor tissue was considered to be low because of the high frequency of correctly estimated cancer types (86.7%, 13 out of 15 samples) according to the somatic variant and expression dataset. Based on these results, low tumor content, complex structural rearrangements, and alterations at the non-genome and non-transcriptome levels were assumed to be the contributing factors in these samples. High-depth sequencing of hotspot regions, tumor enrichment methods, and long-read sequencing are indispensable for the further exploration of driver alterations in these samples.

To evaluate the utility of WGS in driverless samples, this study focused on samples that met multiple criteria. Therefore, the number of samples analyzed was a small fraction of the total sample, with an average of 5 samples per cancer type and only six cancer types ≥ 10 samples. More samples are needed to characterize driverless WGS cases and to search for potential new drivers among them. The data for the latter 5000 cases obtained from Project HOPE beyond the first 5000 cases will be one of the most important resources for this purpose.

Conclusion

In summary, WGS identified driver changes in 68.4% of the samples determined to be driverless using WES and cancer gene panels. It was also possible to identify likely or candidate oncogenic alterations in 22.6% of samples. The remaining 9.0% of samples were required for further exploration. WGS complemented with deep sequencing of hotspot regions, tissue suspension method for tumor cell enrichment, and long read sequencing for complex structural aberrations will be useful for further investigation of driverless WGS sample.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

All experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Shizuoka Cancer Center (Authorization Number: 25–33). Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants. All experiments using clinical samples were conducted in accordance with the approved guidelines. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical samples

To identify somatic and germline genomic alterations in patients with neoplasms, tumor and matched normal samples were subjected to WGS and GEP. Samples were obtained from patients who underwent surgery at Shizuoka Cancer Center Hospital. Fresh surgical specimens were evaluated under a microscope by a pathologist to confirm the presence of tumor cells. Adjacent tumor tissues were subjected to diagnosis and genome analysis. Samples with tumors weighing > 100 mg were analyzed. A blood sample from the same patient was used as a control for WGS, and the adjacent normal tissue was used as a control for GEP. WGS was performed using a NextSeq 6000 System (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and transcriptome profiling was performed using a SurePrint G3 Human Gene Expression 8 × 60 K v2 Microarray (Agilent Technologies, Canta Clara, CA, USA). Details of the experimental procedures are described in previous reports18,27.

Whole genome sequencing

DNA extracted from tumor and matched peripheral blood samples underwent whole genome 150-bp paired-end sequencing on a NovaSeq 6000 System (Illumina). Library construction was performed using a TruSeq DNA PCR-Free High Throughput Library Prep Kit (20015963; Illumina) from 1 µg DNA, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Bcl2fastq v2.20 (Illumina) was used to convert raw sequence data to FASTQ format. The DRAGEN Bio-IT Platform ver. 3.9 (Illumina) was used to map sequenced reads to the reference human genome (hs37d5), mark duplicated reads and call variants, and calculate quality metrics. The mapping results for tumor and normal samples were converted and stored separately as binary alignment map (BAM) files. These files were used for downstream analyses.

Variant calling and annotation

Small variants include SNVs and indels (≤ 50 bp), whereas SVs are greater than 50 bp. For SNVs and indels, somatic and germline variants were identified using the DRAGEN Somatic Variant Caller and Small Variant Caller (Illumina), respectively. Variants with a low sequence depth in normal and/or tumor tissues, low variant depth, and/or low variant allele frequency in tumors were discarded from the downstream analysis. The variants were annotated using Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor ver. 10428. The drivability of the variants was analyzed using an in-house pipeline. Details of the pipeline are described in the following sections.

Structural variants

The SVs were detected using the DRAGEN Structural Variant Caller. A Python script, convertInveresion.py, which is provided as part of Manta ver.1.629, was applied to extract the inversions. SVs flagged as PASS and IMPRECISE were extracted and discarded, respectively. In addition, SVs for which at least one breakpoint was located within 100 bp of the following genomic regions were excluded: (1) segmental duplications, (2) microsatellites, (3) simple repeats, (4) low-complexity regions, and (5) the ENCODE blacklist. For each breakpoint, the gene symbol, exon/intron number, transcript accession number (RefSeq and Ensembl), and the relationship to the frame for the coding exon were annotated using an in-house pipeline. SVs were categorized into five classes based on the mapping information of a read pair. These five classes were translocations, insertions, deletions, duplications, and inversions.

Copy number variants

CNVs were evaluated using Sequenza ver. 3.0.030. This method calculates the likelihood of copy number status among various combinations of ploidy and tumor content. In the default mode, 5,551 combinations [= 61 ploidy (= 1.0, 1.1, …, 7.0) × 91 tumor content (= 0.10, 0.11, …, 1.00)] are evaluated under a given variant allele frequency and depth ratio, and the most likely one is reported. BAM files for tumor and normal samples derived from the same patient were subjected to Sequenza analysis. CNV analysis was performed as follows according to the procedure described in the manual31: (1) bam2seqz was applied to a pair of BAM files to extract allele frequency, (2) seqz_binning was applied to perform binning of the result obtained in the first step, and (3) the sequenza.extract, sequenza.fit, and sequenza.results functions provided in the sequenza R package were sequentially applied. The first two steps were performed using the corresponding sub-commands in sequenza-utils ver. 3.0.0. According to a previous study32, “kmin” and “gamma” for the sequenza.extract function were set to 200. In the results file, the genomic region that was confidently assigned a copy number status was listed, but sometimes did not cover the entire genome. Therefore, if neighboring regions had the same total and minor copy numbers and their distance was < 1,000 bp, these regions were merged. The remaining regions that were not covered after this procedure were assumed to have a normal diploid status and the total and minor copy numbers were assigned 2 and 1, respectively. In cases where the percentage of homozygous deletions [HD(%)], calculated as the sum of genomic regions where the total copy number equals zero (HD rarely occurs in the cancer genome), exceeds ɑ, an alternative solution with the highest likelihood under the HD(%) < ɑ constraint was adopted. ɑ was set to 5% according to a previous study33.

Chromothripsis

Chromothripsis is a recently identified phenomenon in which thousands of clustered chromosomal rearrangements occur in one or a few chromosomes34,35. The status and region of chromothripsis were evaluated using ShatterSeek ver. 1.13, using the results obtained from SV and CNV analyses. Two types of inversions, head-to-head and tail-to-tail, were applied to the program, and this information was assigned according to the INV3 and INV5 tags in the VCF file. In addition, the strand information required for interchromosomal rearrangement (translocation) was determined by aligning the assembled contig sequence to the reference genome using BLAT ver. 3636. For this purpose, the sequence information stored in a VCF file with a CONTIG tag was utilized. Finally, a confidence level for each detected chromothripsis event was assigned based on the criteria described in the manual37.

Tumor mutational burden

The number of variants per megabase was defined as TMB, which was determined as follows: TMB = N/L, where N and L represent the number of somatic variants and the callable region size, respectively. A callable site was defined as a genomic position where the depth of coverage in normal and tumor tissues was ≥ 5 and ≥ 15, respectively. For this analysis, the DRAGEN “_somatic_callable_regions.bed” output file was used. The mean callable region size of the 177 samples was 2,800.4 Mb. All detected somatic variants were considered in determining the TMB, regardless of variant type (SNV and indel), effect of variant (synonymous and non-synonymous), and location of the genomic region (coding/non-coding exon, untranslated region, splice site, intron, and intergenic region).

Estimation of tumor content

In the current study, tumor content was defined as the fraction of cells derived from the tumor tissue in the analyzed sample, estimated from WGS data. Sequenza was used to analyze tumor content and CNV. The estimated tumor content, which was provided as part of the CNV analysis, was used. Except for clearly specified values, no cut-off value was applied for sample selection based on the estimated tumor content.

Construction of oncogene and tumor suppressor gene catalogs for expression-based driver assessment

To focus on alterations in the expression of oncogenes and TSGs, 914 genes annotated as cancer-driver genes were extracted from the previously reported 1,988 cancer-related genes18. Oncogene and TSG classifications were supported by multiple data sources. Consequently, 156 oncogenes and 152 TSGs were used to evaluate driver alterations based on gene expression. Overexpressed oncogenes were defined by a ≥ 10-fold increase and suppressed TSGs were defined by a ≥ 10-fold decrease in tumors when compared with matched normal samples and were annotated as likely driver alterations.

Evaluation of driver genomic alterations

In the present study, we evaluated the driver genomic aberrations that contribute to tumorigenesis. For this purpose, we used an in-house developed analysis pipeline designated “Shizuoka Multi-omics Analysis Protocol for WGS (SMAP-W)” (Supplementary Fig. S1). This is an extended and optimized version of a previously established method for WES datasets. Five types of genomic changes were analyzed: (1) SNV and indels by WGS, (2) fusion genes by WGS, (3) amplification of oncogenes and deletion of TSGs by integrative analysis of CNVs (WGS) and GEP, (4) chromothripsis, and (5) oncogene overexpression and TSG suppression by GEP. The drivability of somatic and germline variants was classified into five tiers in accordance with the reliability of supporting information by sequentially comparing alterations among multiple databases as follows:

Tier1: pathogenic or oncogenic variants registered in databases.

Tier2: likely pathogenic or likely oncogenic variants registered in databases.

Tier3: predicted to be pathogenic variants in oncogene and truncating variant ins TSG.

Tier4: non-synonymous variants.

Tier5: others.

A smaller tier number represents a higher confidence level of supporting data. Cancer Genome Interpreter (CGI)38, Database of Curated Mutations (DoCM)39, OncoKB40, IARC-TP5341, ClinVar42, Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD)43 and Functional Analysis through Hidden Markov Models (FATHMM)44 were utilized for somatic snv/indel. ClinVar, The Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD)45 and CADD were used for germline snv/indel. For Tier3 somatic snv/indel, single nucleotide polymorphism that was defined as variants with > 1% minor allele frequency (MAF) was discarded. gnomAD East Asian46, jMorp47 and Human Genetic Variation Database (HGVD)48 were used for this purpose. For germline snv/indel, this filter was applied to all Tiers and threshold for MAF was set to > 0.5%. When searching variants against databases, genome and amino acid sequences were compared separately and variants that satisfied one of following conditions were regarded as matched: (1) chromosome, position, reference and variant sequence, were identical, (2) gene symbol, amino acid position, reference and variant sequence were identical. To reduce false negatives due to ambiguity in indel description, genomic variants in databases and those identified in the present were normalized by vt normalize49 before comparison. For somatic CNV, oncogene amplification and TSG deletion were regarded as Tier 1, defined as follows: (1) oncogene with copy number gain and ≥ 5-fold increase in expression level, (2) TSG with copy number = 0 and ≥ 5-fold decrease in expression level. Here, gain was defined as follows: copy number ≥ g, where g was larger value of (1) mean + 2×s.d. and (2) 2×mean. Mean and s.d. represent average copy number over entire genome and standard deviation of copy number, respectively. For somatic SV, two breakpoints were annotated in relation to overlapped gene, transcript, frame, coding sequence and nearest gene if no gene overlapped the breakpoint. OncoKB and subset of FusionGDB50 which were considered to be involved in oncogenic mechanism were utilized. The SVs that had the same gene symbol and breakpoint located at exons or introns within coding regions were extracted and classified as Tier1. SVs that matched OncoKB only and registered as likely oncogenic, and TSG with out of frame SVs were classified as Tier2 and Tier3, respectively. Unless otherwise mentioned, the variants that were classified as Tier1 were used in the results and discussions.
