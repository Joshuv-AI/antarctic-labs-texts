Direct RNA sequencing in plants: Practical applications and future perspectives
Authors: Xi-Tong Zhu, Pablo Sanz-Jimenez, Xiao-Tong Ning, Muhammad Tahir ul Qamar, Ling-Ling Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The transcriptome serves as a bridge that links genomic variation to phenotypic diversity. A vast number of studies using next-generation RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) over the last 2 decades have emphasized the essential roles of the plant transcriptome in response to developmental and environmental conditions, providing numerous insights into the dynamic changes, evolutionary traces, and elaborate regulation of the plant transcriptome. With substantial improvement in accuracy and throughput, direct RNA sequencing (DRS) has emerged as a new and powerful sequencing platform for precise detection of native and full-length transcripts, overcoming many limitations such as read length and PCR bias that are inherent to short-read RNA-seq. Here, we review recent advances in dissecting the complexity and diversity of plant transcriptomes using DRS as the main technological approach, covering many aspects of RNA metabolism, including novel isoforms, poly(A) tails, and RNA modification, and we propose a comprehensive workflow for processing of plant DRS data. Many challenges to the application of DRS in plants, such as the need for machine learning tools tailored to plant transcriptomes, remain to be overcome, and together we outline future biological questions that can be addressed by DRS, such as allele-specific RNA modification. This technology provides convenient support on which the connection of distinct RNA features is tightly built, sustainably refining our understanding of the biological functions of the plant transcriptome.

Introduction

Overview of RNA sequencing techniques

Short-read RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) techniques have revolutionized the study of transcriptomes, enabling researchers to comprehensively analyze RNA molecules in specific biological backgrounds. These methods offer insights into the quantification of gene expression, the complexity of alternative splicing, and the potential functions of non-coding RNAs. They have dominated transcriptomic analysis in the last decade owing to increasingly lower costs and greater sequencing depth (Sedlazeck et al., 2018). Despite the dominance of RNA-seq methods, read length limitations and possible biases introduced during reverse transcription of RNA into cDNA for amplification in library construction (Hrdlickova et al., 2017) have made it more difficult to analyze several important aspects of co- and post-transcriptional processing events (Figure 1A). Various studies have shown the contributions of post-transcriptional events, including pre-mRNA editing, splicing, capping, poly(A) tailing, and modification, to normal cell functions (Petrillo et al., 2014; Hernando et al., 2017; Corbett, 2018; Vyse et al., 2020). Compared with other single-molecule long-read sequencing methods from Pacific Biosciences (PacBio), the first commercial nanopore-based direct RNA sequencing (DRS) technology developed by Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) has emerged as a powerful technology in the field of transcriptomics. It offers a direct and comprehensive view of the transcriptome, as it enables the sequencing of native full-length RNA molecules and can avoid amplification biases during PCR (Garalde et al., 2018) (Figure 1A). In addition, by distinguishing current shifts of modified bases from those of unmodified bases, DRS can enable simultaneous characterization of the RNA epitranscriptome, including base modifications such as N6-methyladenosine (m6A) and 5-methylcytosine (m5C), among many other rare types (Yu et al., 2023a; Acera Mateos et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024) (Figure 1B). In the post-genomic era, substantial increases in throughput and rapid declines in prices of third-generation DNA sequencing technologies have enabled the complete assembly of numerous plant genomes with high heterozygosity and polyploidy (Xie et al., 2024). DRS provides an efficient way to further decipher the function of plant genomes by characterizing gene expression patterns in various growth conditions or periods and multiple types of full-length RNAs (Kirov et al., 2020; He et al., 2024). A comprehensive full-length RNA profile can also facilitate the accurate identification of tissue- or condition-specific protein variants in proteomics experiments, thus enhancing the sensitivity and robustness of proteomes (Chen et al., 2020; García-Campa et al., 2023), especially for non-model plant species. DRS thus represents an unprecedented alternative for delineating multiple facets of RNA biology that collectively contribute to the diversity of the plant transcriptome, and it will shed more light on the connection between genomics and proteomics by taking into consideration the role of epitranscriptomic modifications.Figure 1Overview of the DRS protocol and its applications in plants compared with total RNA-seq.(A) Library preparation protocol for total RNA-seq and DRS.(B) Six main reported applications of DRS at the RNA molecular level in plants. Development of nanopore sequencing

The concept of nanopore sequencing emerged in the 1980s, but it was not until the 1990s that the first nanopore detected recognizable ionic current blockades by both RNA and DNA homopolymers (Kasianowicz et al., 1996; Akeson et al., 1999; Meller et al., 2000). Two key advances in its development were the engineering of wild-type α-hemolysin protein, which enabled the four DNA bases on oligonucleotide molecules to be distinguished (Stoddart et al., 2009, 2015), and the incorporation of specific processive enzymes to slow DNA translocation through the nanopore (Benner et al., 2007; Hornblower et al., 2007; Cockroft et al., 2008), which improved the signal-to-noise ratio. ONT announced MinION, the first nanopore sequencing device, in 2012 (Mason and Elemento, 2012) and made it accessible to early users later in 2014 (Jain et al., 2016). Three of the main challenges that hindered its widespread early adoption were low data throughput, low base-calling accuracy, and high input RNA quantity compared with those of short-read RNA-seq. Since then, ONT has continually refined the nanopore, motor protein, and sequencing kit and has optimized the base-caller architecture; ONT sequencing now provides greater raw-read yields (∼3 million reads over a 72-h run) with higher accuracy (98.8% median accuracy, London Calling, 2024) of DRS data (Liu-Wei et al., 2024). Similarly, because of improvements in library preparation protocols for stabilization of long fragments, read length has increased year after year, from kilobases to megabases, and can currently cover even the longest RNAs (Jain et al., 2022). These biological and technological advances, along with upgrades in the corresponding base-calling algorithms and bioinformatics software designed to utilize the unique superiority of nanopore data, have opened the door to numerous areas of RNA biology.

ONT has incorporated two main library construction methods to directly capture native RNA molecules (Figure 1A). In the first method, a poly(T) primer is ligated to the 3′ end of the RNA, followed by direct attachment of a special sequencing adapter bearing a prebound motor protein. In the second, a cDNA–RNA hybrid duplex is generated by synthesizing a cDNA strand using reverse transcriptase to relax the secondary structure of the RNA, and this is followed by ligation of the sequencing adapter to the RNA strand (Garalde et al., 2018). In both cases, only the native RNA strand passes through the nanopore in a 3′–5′ direction. The library produced by the latter strategy is much more stable than the former, enabling longer sequencing courses and therefore providing higher throughput, although the first method is experimentally easy and requires fewer molecular manipulations (Wang et al., 2021b). In addition, it is possible to expand the second strategy and perform direct cDNA sequencing. Nevertheless, DRS in plants provides valuable new insights into transcriptome dynamics, alternative splicing, RNA modifications, and other RNA-related features. At the beginning, a relatively large quantity of starting RNA (either total RNA or poly(A)-enriched RNA) is prepared from plant tissues (Parker et al., 2020). DRS is free from both the fragmentation and PCR that are required for short-read RNA-seq, and the size selection bias can also be avoided in the RNA extraction step (Stark et al., 2019). After essential quality checks for factors such as RNA length, quantity, and purity, the prepared RNA sample is loaded onto a nanopore sequencing device, and RNA molecules pass through the nanopores, generating electrical signals that are deciphered into RNA sequences in real time. The number of bioinformatics tools and computational resources centered on interpreting the specific characteristics and significances of DRS long reads continues to increase; and although some data processing can be computationally intensive, most modern laptops can handle the data relatively quickly (Chen et al., 2023b).

Applications of direct RNA sequencing in plants

A guide for DRS data analysis in plants

We categorized published papers that have used DRS according to the organisms studied (Supplemental Table 1) and found that DRS is becoming deeply embedded in the exploration of transcriptomes for both model and non-model plants. However, various methods and steps were used in these papers, and a complete and concise review of DRS data processing in plants is not yet available. To fill this gap, we propose a systematic pipeline for analysis of DRS data before discussing the biological insights gained by reviewing these collected papers. This pipeline consists of three major components: data pre-processing, alignment, and biological discovery, as illustrated in Figure 2. In brief, a Fast5 file that stores raw DRS current intensity and metadata information can be converted into conventional Fastq format through base-calling or into an alternative format, Slow5, that enables more efficient manipulation and storage of raw signal data by permitting parallel access (Gamaarachchi et al., 2022). If necessary, the Fast5 file can be visualized to trace the channel and time of base-called reads through the graphical viewer BulkVis (Payne et al., 2019) and can subsequently be manipulated to interrogate the embedded signals in SquiggleKit for further applications such as development of new algorithms (Ferguson and Smith, 2019). After quality controls that ensure the fidelity and reproducibility of the samples, base errors in DRS reads should be corrected, either with matched short reads (hybrid correction) or through a self-correction strategy. We recommend hybrid correction because the low cost of RNA-seq enables high coverage over long reads, resulting in better performance than self-correction methods in terms of correction quality and resource usage (Zhang et al., 2020a). The corrected DRS reads can then be aligned to the reference genome to obtain full-length transcripts or to the transcriptome when a reference is not assembled and modified RNA bases are called (Liu et al., 2019). The identified full-length transcripts are then used in numerous downstream analyses depending on the research subjects (Figure 2). One of the general applications is to quantify expression at both the gene and transcript level, which shows moderate correlation with that from short-read data (Hu et al., 2022). Using the unambiguous transcripts, poly(A) length and modified bases can be estimated from the raw signal, enabling a comprehensive investigation of the biological effects of dynamic RNA features under various conditions.Figure 2Flowchart summarizing DRS data processing in plants.The flowchart consists of three parts: data pre-processing, alignment, and biological discovery, with core steps highlighted in shaded boxes. For some essential steps in Figure 2, we recommend a number of analysis tools on the basis of three considerations (Supplemental Table 2). The first is whether their performance has been benchmarked using nanopore data from mammals. Base-calling for DRS data is computationally intensive. With a new architecture that makes full use of graphics processing units, Dorado is now the preferred base-caller, and benchmarking using human whole-genome sequencing data indicates a significantly higher speed of base-calling and a 3.8× performance increase when calling methylation compared with Guppy (https://github.com/aws-samples/basecaller-performance-benchmarking). Tools that were originally developed for DNA correction can also be applied to DRS data to reduce base error (Lima et al., 2020). A previous evaluation of tools for long-read correction showed that FM-index Long Read Corrector 2 (FMLRC2) (Wang et al., 2018), a hybrid correction method that leverages an FM-index built from a complementary short-read assembly, outperformed other approaches in terms of base-level accuracy, memory usage, and running time (Zhang et al., 2020a). However, new methods optimized for nanopore RNA data, such as isONcorrect (Sahlin and Medvedev, 2021), can achieve better performance on DRS data. A recent study pointed out that long-read quantification tools showed surprisingly poor performance on DRS data, presumably owing to the low throughput compared with other long-read protocols; instead, RSEM, which was designed for RNA-seq quantification (Li and Dewey, 2011), had the highest correlation coefficient between expected and observed abundance (Pardo-Palacios et al., 2024). However, this benchmarking did not include NanoCount, which was developed specifically for long-read DRS datasets and greatly improved the accuracy of isoform assignment with an expectation-maximization approach (Gleeson et al., 2022), similar to RSEM. Currently, Tombo is the main tool for discovery of m5C and other novel modifications from DRS data (Marcus et al., 2017), but new methods are emerging, such as CHEUI (Acera Mateos et al., 2024) and TandemMod (Wu et al., 2024), and the latter has been used successfully to predict m5C sites in rice under different growing conditions. For identification of m6A sites, benchmarking with DRS data in Arabidopsis thaliana indicates that many publicly available tools, such as Nanom6A (Gao et al., 2021) and m6Anet (Hendra et al., 2022), can achieve a high rate of true positives (>0.8).

Second, if no benchmarking had been performed, tools were selected according to the frequency of their use in the collected papers. For example, almost all studies used minimap2 for alignment of DRS data to the reference with splice mode (-ax splice), although we advise a separate evaluation of long-read mapping algorithms because many classical tools, such as GraphMap2 (Josip et al., 2019), have been tuned to optimize the alignment of long and error-prone reads. Similarly, Nanopolish (polya command) was frequently used to determine the poly(A) length of each read (Loman et al., 2015).

Third, many popular tools are also suitable for identification of differentially expressed genes, format conversion, and visualization of long-read coverage or base modification (Figure 2), and we selected them for the sake of practical usability. In addition to these three considerations, many statistical methods can be used to complement further analysis. Differences in poly(A) length between various biological backgrounds can be identified using the Mann–Whitney test (Xu et al., 2022). Many tools listed above can also perform versatile related analyses, such as the recognition of both m6A and m5C sites by TandemMod (Wu et al., 2024). Some of these tools are discussed in more detail in the corresponding sections.

Investigation of complex plant transcriptomes

Of the ∼400 000 known plant species, only around 0.3% (1575) have had their genomes sequenced to date (Sun et al., 2022b; Xie et al., 2024), despite the vital importance of plants for food, medicine, fuel, and ecology. Full-length transcripts have proven to be ideal for gene model prediction and identification of novel genes, as previous studies have suggested that more reads do not necessarily result in more transcripts; instead, read quality and length are key factors for transcript identification (Pardo-Palacios et al., 2024). DRS can generate a higher average read length than other long-read protocols (Ying et al., 2021), making it ideal for facilitating the identification of novel complex isoforms and resolving transcriptome dynamics that would be challenging to investigate using short-read RNA-seq, which depends primarily on computational algorithms for reconstruction of transcript sequences (Steijger et al., 2013; Wright et al., 2022; Prjibelski et al., 2023). Ideally, a full-length DRS read should consist of both a discernible coding sequence (CDS) and noncoding regions (5′ UTR, 3′ UTR, and poly(A) tail for mRNA). However, early release of the 5′ transcript end by the motor protein (Jain et al., 2022; Calvo-Roitberg et al., 2023), strong sequencing bias toward the 3′ end (Grünberger et al., 2022), and transcript truncation and degradation in the library preparation step (Soneson et al., 2019) collectively contribute to difficulty in obtaining truly full-length transcripts in DRS experiments. Instead, methods that calculate the coverage of reads over annotated transcripts by a certain threshold are frequently used to define nearly full-length transcripts (Gleeson et al., 2022). In plants, various ratios of full-length transcripts have been reported, ranging from ∼20% to 93%, presumably owing to the above factors or to different methods used to assign a flexible definition to full length (Puglia et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021, 2023).

The use of DRS for novel gene and transcript discovery has already been reported in numerous model and non-model plants: 22 360 novel transcripts in bamboo (Phyllostachys edulis) (Li et al., 2023), 16 432 in plane tree (Yan et al., 2023), 578 in Cynara cardunculus (Puglia et al., 2020), and 110 888 in strawberry (Chen et al., 2022). These novel transcripts greatly expand the repertoire of genomic sequences. In C. cardunculus, for example, long reads enabled an increase of 15% in complete assembled genes and 18% in transcript isoforms compared with short reads alone (Puglia et al., 2020). Although transcripts of A. thaliana are well annotated compared with those of other plants, 38 500 novel transcript isoforms and 8700 unique splicing variants that were not found in either the TAIR10 or AtRTD2 assembly were recently identified in two experiments (Parker et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020c). For important crops such as wheat (Kirov et al., 2020), maize (Zhang et al., 2020b), and rice (Li et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2023a), researchers have also reported many novel transcripts with complex origins (e.g., transcripts that overlap with exons on the opposite strand), some of which may be associated with environmental adaption, like the ZmHsf04-II isoform that is produced in response to heat shock in maize (Zhang et al., 2020b). Notably, these studies also attempted to dissect the prevalence and function of transposon-derived transcripts originating from the insertion of transposable elements (TEs) in intragenic regions (Kirov et al., 2020; Chen and Winefield, 2022). Berthelier et al. (2023) identified TE-gene transcripts generated from about 3000 gene loci in A. thaliana using long-read DRS data and showed that the presence of TEs in specific transcript regions (e.g., 3′ UTRs) could regulate the expression and stability of their hosts under environmental stresses, providing pioneering examples of how to fully utilize the advantages of DRS to gain insightful conclusions. Considering that TEs represent a large proportion of many plant genomes, it is reasonable to expect that DRS will continue to play important roles in studying the relationship between plant transcripts and their inserted TEs.

Although traditional short-read RNA-seq approaches offer great potential to refine existing plant transcriptomes, technical limitations of these methods have led to various computational challenges in de novo or genome-based transcript reconstruction, and a considerable reduction in reconstruction sensitivity was noticed from the gene to the transcript level (Steijger et al., 2013). DRS, however, avoids these issues by directly sequencing native full-length transcripts, thus enabling the precise detection of alternative splicing events. Traditionally, alternative splicing events can be classified into retained introns, skipped exons, alternative 5′ splicing sites, alternative 3′ splicing sites, and mutually exclusive exons (Vu et al., 2022). Numerous transcripts that combine these alternative splicing patterns have been reported in tea, maize, soybean, and rice (Zhang et al., 2020b; Wang et al., 2021a; Jia et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2023a). In A. thaliana (Parker et al., 2020), long reads facilitated the resolution of complex isoforms that were difficult to resolve using short-read sequencing (Zhang et al., 2020c). For example, the splicing patterns of 19 isoforms from the FLM gene in A. thaliana were consistent with known splice junction sites, but 11 of them had not been annotated previously. Numbers of newly discovered alternatively spliced isoforms derived from canonical splice-junction sites also exceeded expected estimates: 11 169 in C. cardunculus (Puglia et al., 2020) and 64 862 in strawberry (Chen et al., 2022). In nine Citrus species, from 1034 to 4467 novel isoforms were identified per species (Hu et al., 2022). Importantly, these new isoforms (e.g., serving as non-coding RNAs) and the proteins they encode have been found to possess diverse functions, cellular localizations, and regulatory roles. Their identification and characterization enhance our understanding of transcriptome complexity and may also lead to more discoveries of functional innovation beyond genome sequences.

DRS has also been used extensively to correct gene models previously predicted ab initio or from short reads. In C. sinensis, researchers confirmed that some long transcripts with up to 16 exons assembled from short reads actually consisted of two different non-overlapping transcripts. The significant difference in the expression levels of the two adjacent transcripts was also validated by RT–PCR (Wang et al., 2021a). Similarly, a total of 13 039 gene models in C. cardunculus were adjusted on the basis of DRS long-read alignment (Puglia et al., 2020). Another study in Camptotheca acuminata corrected 5692 alternative splicing events among 4746 genes and found 15 novel transcription factors (TFs) that potentially regulated the biosynthesis of camptothecin, whose derivatives are known to be effective anticancer agents (Zhang et al., 2023). The polyploidization of plant genomes, especially autopolyploidization, poses another challenge for transcriptome analysis, because sequence similarity between subgenomes hampers the accurate identification and quantification of homologous transcripts. In plane trees, 1854 different alternative splicing events between hexaploids and dodecaploids were documented, and those transcribed specifically in the dodecaploids were enriched in DNA repair or regulation of DNA recombination, indicating their involvement in pathways maintaining the stability of the polyploidized genome (Yan et al., 2023). In addition to its use in autopolyploids, the application of DRS to the allotetraploid Brassica napus has enabled the comparison of complex alternative splicing isoforms between the hybrid and its diploid parents, providing clues to the relationship between increased isoform number and adaptive evolution in polyploid species (Li et al., 2022).

The large number of transcripts precisely identified by DRS provides us with opportunities to elucidate the biological roles of these new events. For example, variations in the number of isoforms between orthologous genes in several closely related citrus species were characterized for the first time, enabling a systematic analysis of different types of isoforms and, most importantly, their potential functions as enzymes or receptors (Hu et al., 2022). In addition to identifying novel transcripts, DRS can also be used to quantify their expression levels, providing a method similar to that of short reads for detection of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between diverse biological conditions. Yan et al. (2023) found many genes with differential expression patterns in plane trees, which could be attributed to genome duplication. These DEGs were involved in many basic biological processes, such as cell wall organization and biogenesis, stress response, hormone signaling, and circadian rhythms. In A. thaliana, 3453 DEGs were identified between the seedling and the floral bud, but more than twice as many transcripts (7111) showed significant differences in expression (Zhang et al., 2020c). Similar results have also been observed in bamboo (12 061) (Li et al., 2023) and C. cardunculus (3424) (Puglia et al., 2020). Approximately two-thirds of differentially expressed transcripts between vegetative tissue and the inflorescence in C. cardunculus were categorized as TFs. In addition, to understand the role of the transcriptome in the successful establishment of a polyploid, researchers compared DEGs between resynthesized (i.e., a 1:1 mixture of parental full-length reads) and natural polyploidized plants such as B. napus (Li et al., 2022) and Nicotiana tabacum (Landis et al., 2020). They found large numbers of pre-mRNAs with alternative splicing events, which were enriched in a MYB-related TF family and may be related to the enhanced adaptability of polyploidized plants to various environmental stresses. The unique characteristics of DRS can help resolve not only the transcriptomic response of plants to specific stresses but also important agronomic traits. For example, 350 DE transcripts related to ammonium nutrition in pine roots (Ortigosa et al., 2022) and 802 DE transcripts related to drought stress in Populus trichocarpa (Gao et al., 2022) were identified. DRS clarified important flavor synthesis pathways in tea leaves (Wang et al., 2021a) and revealed the key transitional role of the white fruit stage for some unreported transcripts in strawberry (Chen et al., 2022). In summary, these findings demonstrate the ability of DRS to resolve the complex characteristics of plant transcriptomes and offer a unique perspective for understanding the biological roles of full-length transcripts during plant development.

Detection of long non-coding RNAs and circular RNAs

Genomic loci encoding RNA molecules that do not code for peptides were long considered to be “junk” sequences. However, the study of one such type of previously neglected RNA molecule, long non-coding RNA (lncRNA), has opened a broad area for decoding these unknown genomic regions and has shed light on the molecular basis of gene expression regulation. lncRNAs play pivotal roles in multiple plant processes through diverse mechanisms such as chromatin remodeling, transcriptional regulation, and post-transcriptional regulation (Wang and Chekanova, 2017). In addition to novel transcripts, DRS can also be used to identify new and structurally complex lncRNAs that are difficult to detect or assemble from short reads. Between 2613 and 3389 new lncRNAs were predicted in 9 citrus species, and their functions were enriched in enzyme classification, biosynthesis, modification of RNA and protein, and solute transport (Hu et al., 2022). In wheat, 796 new lncRNAs and 20 long terminal repeat retrotransposon-related transcripts were recently identified (Kirov et al., 2020), most located in intergenic regions and a few in intronic regions. This research highlighted the potential of DRS for unraveling the prevalence of lncRNAs in plants with complex polyploid and repeat-rich genomes. One notable characteristic of lncRNAs is that many of them are known to be associated with TEs (Fort et al., 2021). This was also found in wheat, in which 29% (111) of novel lncRNAs had exons with inserted TEs greater than 50 bp in length, more than half of which were derived from retrotransposons (class I TEs) (Kirov et al., 2020). lncRNAs have also been shown to participate in plant responses to various environmental stresses by modulating stress-responsive genes and pathways and in the regulation of plant developmental processes, including seed germination, organ development, flowering, and senescence (Marquardt et al., 2014; Jha et al., 2020; Urquiaga et al., 2020; Lucero et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023); however, it remains to be determined whether and to what extent TE-related lncRNAs are also involved in corresponding biological processes, leaving plenty of room for DRS to solve these puzzles. To this end, integration of newly discovered lncRNAs with functional genomics and systems biology approaches will provide a more comprehensive understanding of plant biology, ultimately aiding in predicting gene function, modeling biological networks, and deciphering complex regulatory interactions.

Another class of important non-coding RNAs are the circular RNAs (circRNAs), which have been demonstrated to be ubiquitous in plants and are implicated in the regulation of many essential cellular processes (Wang et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2021). Detection of circRNAs from DRS is not intuitive, because only individual linear stretches of bases can pass through the nanopore, but circRNAs generally form covalent closed-loop structures without poly(A) tails. Despite this restriction, some recently developed protocols and software specifically designed for detection of circRNAs in long-read data, such as CIRI-long (Zhang et al., 2021a), have attempted to overcome this problem by first enriching circRNAs, then generating circular cDNAs from their template circRNAs through reverse transcription (a procedure called circular reverse transcription), and finally identifying back-spliced junction sites by comparison with a reference genome or transcriptome to reconstruct the unbiased full-length circRNA sequences. Using a similar principle of library construction, Wang et al. (2020) identified 470 circRNAs in bamboo, the majority of which had an exonic origin; Gene Ontology enrichment analysis of their parental genes suggested that they were likely to participate in chromosome organization and chromosome segregation. Intriguingly, reads with back-spliced junctions were significantly longer than those without, especially for single circularized exons, emphasizing the potential utility of DRS for identification of large circRNAs. Although still in the nascent stage, protocols that take advantage of DRS hold great promise for advancing circRNA research, enabling a deeper understanding of circRNA functions and providing another rich resource that breeders can mine for crop improvement.

Identification of fusion transcripts

Fusion transcripts, which are also referred to as fusion RNAs, chimeric transcripts, or chimeric RNAs, are hybrid RNA molecules formed by the joining or fusion of two previously separate genomic regions (genomic rearrangement dependent) or RNA products of genes (genomic rearrangement independent) (Figure 3A). Fusion transcripts generated at the DNA level usually follow various genetic alterations, often tightly associated with abnormalities in the genomic sequence, including inter- and intra-chromosomal rearrangements (Sun and Li, 2022). By contrast, the molecular mechanisms that lead to fusion transcripts at the RNA level are still largely undetermined, although several possibilities have been proposed according to the classifications of these special RNAs: trans-splicing, transcriptional readthrough, and cis-splicing between adjacent genes. The recent identification of sense–antisense fusions, presumably from convergent transcription of annotated genes and their opposite-strand DNA, has further highlighted the complexity of the landscape of RNA-level fusions (Wang et al., 2021c). In addition, it has been estimated that up to 55% of all identified fusion transcripts may be attributable to template switching during the polymerase-based extension step (Yu et al., 2014) (Figure 3B); such fusions are frequently observed in short-read RNA-seq data as a result of cDNA amplification. Several key advances in long-read methods, such as reductions in base errors and sequencing costs, have promoted the discovery of potential fusion transcripts in diverse plant species (Wang et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2020c; Kirov et al., 2020). Identification of such transcripts was previously hampered by the relatively low abundance of fusion transcripts compared with those of their parental genes, as revealed in humans (Qin et al., 2017). The underlying principle for recognition of fusion transcripts is similar in both short-read and long-read methods: reads covering such transcripts should map to several genomic loci or transcripts or to a combination of both (Wang et al., 2016). This premise requires not only the precise alignment of reads but also a minimum length mapped to both parental loci. It is often heavily reliant on statistical inference for short-read methods like STAR-Fusion (Dobin and Gingeras, 2016), and a high proportion of identified fusion transcripts may therefore simply be artifacts. Several computer algorithms and tools designed specifically for fast and accurate detection of fusion events from long-read transcriptome data have recently been developed, such as LongGF (Liu et al., 2020), FusionSeeker (Chen et al., 2023c), and JAFFAL (Davidson et al., 2022). Dorney et al. (2023) reviewed the specific characteristics of these bioinformatic approaches and concluded that they generally outperform methods based on short reads in terms of minimizing false positives and constructing full-length fusion transcripts.

Compared with the striking finding that about 16.5% of human cancers are driven by fusion events (Gao et al., 2018), research on the functional roles of fusion transcripts in plants has largely lagged behind. Nevertheless, several notable features of their origins, structures, and expression patterns have been reported. First, both DNA- and RNA-level fusion transcripts have been reported and should be examined using separate protocols. For example, 310 fusion transcripts across four Oryza species were identified using a novel pipeline that included wild rice genomes for whole-genome comparison. These transcripts showed a high origination rate (63 per million years per species) but a modest fixation frequency (31.8% in O. sativa subsp. japonica), indicating a strong signal of purifying selection that was needed for rice to balance the negative effects imposed by these fusion transcripts (Zhou et al., 2022). Second, fusion transcripts tend to be expressed at a low level and display clear species- or tissue-specific expression. In rice, only 69 out of 157 fusion transcripts showed detectable expression, and 27 were expressed in the leaf compared with 52 in the panicle (Zhou et al., 2022). More fusion transcripts were also observed in the endosperm and embryo than in other tissues of maize (Wang et al., 2016). Third, the trans-splicing mechanism by which fusion transcripts are generated was evidenced by the observation that fusion breakpoints within the two parental genes corresponded to canonical splice sites that were found to be adopted in their normal transcripts via cis-splicing (Wang et al., 2016). This might explain the significant similarity in expression pattern between some fusion transcripts and their parental genes. DRS has not been widely used to study fusion events, and existing results focus mainly on the detection of complex fusion transcripts regardless of their mechanisms and potential biological functions. Six new types of fusion events were identified by comparison of DRS transcripts to Araport 11 annotations in A. thaliana and verified by RT-PCR and chop-RT-PCR (Zhang et al., 2020c). These new transcripts covered multiple loci, with or without splicing patterns consistent with those of the annotated genes, thus expanding the complexity of the A. thaliana transcriptome. However, the loci were adjacent to one another, raising the possibility that these unexpected transcripts may have been transcribed by a read-through mechanism or could simply be unannotated genes or isoforms from authentic loci and therefore not true fusions. Another DRS experiment in Vitis vinifera suggested that (long interspersed nuclear elements) LINE and terminal inverted repeat TEs were more likely to contribute to aberrant alternative splicing or gene–TE fusion transcripts (Chen and Winefield, 2022), highlighting the possible role of TEs in mediating the formation of fusion transcripts. Although few experiments in plants have focused on mining fusion transcripts, DRS now enables the identification of new types of fusions that are more reliable and often ignored by short-read methods, laying solid groundwork for further experimental analyses of these functionally important transcripts in plants.

Functional characterization of the poly(A) tail

Non-template poly(A) tails covalently added to the 3′ ends of RNAs vary greatly in length and are essential for assessment of the integrity of RNA molecules by cells (Passmore and Coller, 2022). The presence of poly(A) tails is tightly connected to the whole life cycle of RNA, and their important regulatory roles involve the control of RNA export, stability, translation, and cytoplasmic movement across developmental stages or under different environmental conditions (Jalkanen et al., 2014; Passmore and Coller, 2022). Notably, the length of the poly(A) itself is regulated. Nascent RNAs in the nucleus usually possess longer poly(A) tails of about 100–200 nt, almost twice as long as those in the cytoplasm, indicating a rapid shortening (or deadenylation) of the poly(A) tails before transport of RNAs out of the nucleus (Jia et al., 2020). By taking advantage of DRS to capture full-length transcripts, the poly(A) lengths in multiple tissues of A. thaliana were determined, revealing two prominent peaks in most tissues, one at ∼20 nt and the other at ∼50 nt, consistent with the footprint covered by one or two poly(A) binding protein cytoplasmic 1 (PABPC1) proteins (Subtelny et al., 2014; Parker et al., 2020). By contrast, poly(A) tails were much longer in pollen and seeds, mainly ∼55 and ∼80 nt, suggesting that although the length of this additional adenine chain was conserved across most tissues, specific purposes such as storing mRNAs or adjusting translational efficiency might require an overall alteration in poly(A) length (Zhu et al., 2023). In addition to its tissue-specific regulation, the length of poly(A) tails was also found to be gene specific; mRNAs with long half-lives had relatively short poly(A) tails (Jia et al., 2022). The overall length distributions of poly(A) tails differed among species; for example, rice shoots had more genes with extremely short tails than did A. thaliana. Nonetheless, the lengths of poly(A) tails of homologous genes exhibited significant similarity in shoots of A. thaliana, maize, soybeans, and rice (Jia et al., 2022), indicating that these genes, together with their intact poly(A) tails, are evolutionarily conserved in divergent plants.

Similar to alternative splicing, alternative polyadenylation (APA) is another widespread mechanism that increases the diversity and complexity of transcriptomes. APA can produce isoforms with different 3′ cleavage sites, which will not change the coding potential but will influence the fate of RNAs in several ways—for example, by altering microRNA binding sites or regulating RNA stability, thus ultimately regulating the outcome of gene expression (Abdel-Ghany et al., 2016; Tian and Manley, 2017; Wang et al., 2017; Arora et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021b). DRS data were first used to reveal an unexpectedly large number of new APA events. A total of 109 880 APA sites have been reported in A. thaliana (Zhang et al., 2020c), and APA events have also been characterized in plants with complex genetic constitutions, such as B. napus (Li et al., 2022). In bamboo, identification of new APA events enabled the discovery of global shifts in preferred polyadenylation sites from proximal to distal between the basal and middle shoot tissue (Li et al., 2023). Another study also concluded that APA played an important role in regulating gene expression under normal conditions during different stages of plant development, such as embryogenesis, flowering, and organ differentiation (Yang et al., 2021). A DRS experiment in A. thaliana demonstrated that transcripts in floral buds used more distal APA sites than those in seeds and were therefore enriched in long poly(A) tails (Zhang et al., 2020c). APA is also responsive to various environmental cues, contributing to stress-responsive changes in gene expression; therefore, studies using DRS have also concentrated on the dynamics of APA under stress conditions (Zeng et al., 2019; Yan et al., 2023). For example, this stress response was corroborated in P. trichocarpa: use of the distal poly(A) site increased during drought stress compared with control conditions, and the percentage of transcripts with long poly(A) tails also increased significantly (Gao et al., 2022). These results suggest that transcripts that show preferential use of distal APA sites are prevalent both in fast-growing tissues and in response to environmental stress; however, it remains to be determined whether there are common mechanisms, such as the involvement of master TFs. Alterations in APA sites were also observed to be correlated with increased m6A deposition in pine roots under ammonium treatment (Ortigosa et al., 2022), emphasizing that base modifications within transcripts were closely coupled to APA events. In summary, DRS provides a convenient way to study polyadenylation in plants compared with experimentally cumbersome methods such as poly(A)-tail length profiling by sequencing (PAL-seq) (Subtelny et al., 2014), offering many new insights into the dynamics and regulatory mechanisms of this 3′-end processing.

Exploration of RNA base modification

A major breakthrough of DRS is that it enables the rapid and accurate detection and characterization of RNA modifications in their native state without the need for additional chemical treatments compared with previous antibody-based technologies (Zhang et al., 2022). More than 170 covalent reversible chemical modifications added to different nucleotides of RNA molecules have been reported in eukaryotic organisms; these modifications, called the epitranscriptome, have emerged as a new layer of gene expression regulation (Arzumanian et al., 2022). Since the successful discrimination of ionic current signals of modified and unmodified bases in DRS data, this sequencing technology has rapidly been improved to detect a wide range of RNA modification types, including widespread m6A and m5C, as well as less frequent modifications found on non-coding RNAs, such as 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (hm5C), N7-methylguanosine (m7G), pseudouridine (Y) and 2′-O-methylations (Nm) (Garalde et al., 2018; Smith et al., 2019; Parker et al., 2020; Price et al., 2020; Begik et al., 2021; Jenjaroenpun et al., 2021; Leger et al., 2021). Even before the advent of DRS, many known RNA modifications were identified in plants by different methods (although not all could be mapped in a transcriptome-wide manner) (Xiao et al., 2016; David et al., 2017; De Almeida et al., 2018; Kumar and Mohapatra, 2021). These epitranscriptomic modifications were found to be key players that regulate multiple aspects of RNA metabolism in plants, such as splicing, stability, movement, translation efficiency, and secondary structures (Xiao et al., 2016; Shen and Yu, 2021; Shen et al., 2023) (Figure 4A), which cooperatively determine the function of RNA (Hu et al., 2019). In addition, modifications within the poly(A) tail can alter RNA properties by maintaining RNA stability. For example, mRNAs with phosphodiester modifications in the poly(A) tail were significantly less susceptible to degradation by 3′-deadenylase and maintained efficient expression (Strzelecka et al., 2020). m6A-dependent shortening of poly(A) tails to stabilize mRNA has also been associated with global transcriptome perturbation (Slobodin et al., 2020), providing new functional insights into the RNA modifications that can be mined by DRS, as it can detect RNA modifications and poly(A) tails simultaneously.Figure 4Multiple types of RNA modifications.(A) Multisite RNA modifications and their functional effects; (B) a full-length RNA with schematic secondary structure and modifications revealed by DRS simultaneously.

Compared with bioinformatics software that relies on statistical algorithms to perform alignment, quantification, and discovery of alternative splicing events or new transcripts from DRS data, advances in machine learning methods have boosted the rapid development of tools that are specifically designed for detection of various base modifications from raw DRS reads (Wang et al., 2021b; Chen et al., 2023b). These tools can be roughly divided into two categories according to the availability of pre-prepared modified training data: those that compare ionic current signals of native transcripts from specific conditions or tissues to transcripts with known modifications (comparative detection), and those that infer modifications directly from the shifted peaks produced by changes in current signals when transcripts pass through the nanopore (de novo detection) (Wan et al., 2022). The detection accuracy of both methods has increased substantially. For instance, for identification of m6A modification, Nanom6A was released with an accuracy of 97% at single-base resolution based on the XGBoost model, and it was further validated by methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing and m6A-sensitive RNA-endoribonuclease-facilitated sequencing (Gao et al., 2021). Later, a neural network-based method called m6Anet was developed and tested on both human and A. thaliana DRS data, achieving highly accurate predictions of candidate m6A sites (Hendra et al., 2022). These tools have greatly promoted the exploration of epitranscriptomes but are limited to known modifications, and the pre-required training data also increase the experimental costs. Tombo offers the possibility of detecting any kind of non-canonical base solely from DRS data and provides more accurate detection of specific modifications (Marcus et al., 2017). TandemMod is another new analytical tool that uses a transferable deep learning framework to detect RNA modifications and provides flexible modes for different purposes. In its transfer learning mode, users can fine-tune existing models with their own data and subsequently feed it to the prediction mode, which bypasses the need for pre-prepared training data and can be used for multiple types of RNA modification detection in a single DRS experiment (Wu et al., 2024). Although these tools have been tested primarily on mammalian data, especially human cell lines, a recent study comprehensively evaluating the performance of 10 computational methods indicated that, except for those trained on human samples, most achieved comparable or even better results when tested on A. thaliana for m6A mapping (Zhong et al., 2023). However, the improved modification calling likely benefited from higher read coverage in A. thaliana, as its genome is much smaller, rather than from any optimization for plant species.

Most modifications are located primarily on the body or 3′ UTR of mRNA (Qiu et al., 2023) (Figure 4B), and DRS in plants has been used to decipher their collaborative mechanisms in RNA function together with other properties such as APA under various conditions and to characterize the overall modification profiles of non-model species for which traditional experimental systems for RNA immunoprecipitation are difficult to establish. m5C modifications in A. thaliana accumulate in the CDS region and are found to be particularly enriched in mobile mRNAs, indicating a key role for m5C in long-distance mRNA movement such as transport via the phloem (Yang et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020c). Others studies in rice indicated that the number of m5C sites varied greatly in different tissues (Yu et al., 2023a), and more downregulated genes with m5C modification under high-salinity conditions were related to the resistance to abiotic stimuli (Wu et al., 2024). m6A is another abundant modification with multiple roles that is being extensively studied and is most closely related to the 3′ UTR. Previous m6A-seq in maize demonstrated that m6A sites are clearly confined to a consensus motif that is enriched in the 3′ UTR and are positively coordinated with APA (Luo et al., 2020). Further combined analysis with parallel polysome profiling suggested that mRNAs methylated to more m6A showed a reduction in translational efficiency (Luo et al., 2020). DRS experiments in A. thaliana confirmed this association, and sequencing of full-length RNAs enabled the discovery of many transcripts with significant defects in formation of their 3′ ends when the m6A sites were demethylated, which led to a decrease in their relative abundance (Parker et al., 2020). Another study in the model tree P. trichocarpa found that different APA usage was associated with a distinct ratio of m6A (Gao et al., 2022). The m6A level could also be adjusted in response to external stress. For example, salt-specific m6A deposition in the 3′ UTR was correlated with loss of RNA secondary structure in A. thaliana, resulting in enhanced stability of transcripts encoding abiotic stress-response proteins (Kramer et al., 2020). Although many known methyltransferases such as mRNA adenosine methylase are reported to be involved in the installation of m6A sites, they appear to write them primarily near stop codons or within 3′ UTRs (Růžička et al., 2017). A recent study using DRS expanded the repertoire of m6A writer proteins to include an ortholog of human METTL16 called FIONA1 (FIO1). FIO1 methylated adenosines in thecoding region, and A. thaliana mutants of this methyltransferase showed a global decrease in m6A methylation levels and an early-flowering phenotype, probably attributable to changes in the mRNA abundance of a central flowering integrator and its upstream regulators (Xu et al., 2022). The finding that m6A added by a new allele of FIO1 to the 3′ UTR of FLOWERING LOCUS C could protect its mRNA from rapid degradation indicates the versatile, position-dependent function of this modification (Sun et al., 2022a). In another study, transcriptome-wide m6A mapping showed that many transcripts, ranging from 276 to 2042 in 6 different rice tissues, exhibited clear tissue-specific m6A modification (Yu et al., 2023a). Advances have also been made in non-model plant species. Taking bamboo again as an example, Li et al. (2023) found that specific sites on some transcripts could be methylated to form recurrent m6A at different growth stages. In addition, ∼11% of circRNAs showed m6A methylation near the donor or acceptor splice sites, suggesting a role for m6A in the regulation of back-splicing for formation of this enigmatic type of RNA in bamboo (Wang et al., 2020). By taking advantage of DRS data, these studies were able to depict the complex interplay of different RNA features to some extent and to avoid troublesome experimental designs, which often comprise two or more different combinations of data types (Di Giammartino et al., 2011). However, distinguishing different kinds of modifications and precisely mapping their positions remains a challenge to comprehensively understanding the plant epitranscriptome and will certainly require further technological improvement of DRS and development of corresponding computational software without additional control samples. This will be especially helpful because many plant species lack relatively easily operated genetic transformation systems.

Future challenges and opportunities for DRS in plants

Technical and computational improvements to expand the application of DRS in plants

Broader applications of DRS in plants are poised to bring about transformative advances in dissecting the complexity and diversity of plant transcriptomes, and many puzzles related to the association of RNA features with their functions will be solved. However, some current technological and computational challenges remain to be overcome or optimized (Jain et al., 2022). Extracting high-quality RNA materials (300 ng poly(A) RNA according to the latest SQK-RNA004 kit, compared with 10 ng for Illumina RNA-seq) suitable for DRS to obtain sufficient read coverage is one of these challenges, especially for plant species that pose difficulties for tissue sampling such as those distributed in the wild. Advances in the use of lower DNA inputs for Nanopore devices have enabled in-field and real-time genomic surveillance, and lower RNA quantity may soon become achievable (Wang et al., 2021b).

One important application of reduced RNA input in the future is to monitor the dynamic transcriptomes of microorganisms that are beneficial or pathogenic to plant tissues, which play a critical role in healthy growth and fitness in plants (Haveman et al., 2021). The intimate connection between a plant and its microbiota leads to the view that they collectively form one functional entity, also referred to as the plant holobiont (Vandenkoornhuyse et al., 2015). DRS has not yet been applied to the plant metatranscriptome, but this represents an important alternative for exploring the interlinkages among different holobiont components from several broader perspectives. First, the investigation of taxonomic composition using plant metagenomic data might be insufficient, as not only the microbiome but also their expression is dynamically regulated in response to external changes (Shakya et al., 2019). DRS can improve both taxonomic classification and expression quantification of the microbiome by avoiding artifacts such as those from the assembly of short reads (Grünberger et al., 2022). Second, the capture of full-length reads will allow sensitive detection of novel expressed genes that code for valuable enzymes. These genes may share some sequence similarity but vary substantially in abundance within the microbiome (Shakya et al., 2019). Third, tracking the interactions within microbiotas or between the host and the microbiota is the key to expanding the concept of the plant holobiont in terms of evolution, during which the whole holobiont is under selection and mutual influences lead to co-evolution among holobiont components (Mesny et al., 2023). With fast and routine collection of metatranscriptomic DRS data from both plants and their microbiotas, complex regulatory networks can be efficiently resolved, assisting in the discovery of novel mechanisms that enable fine-tuning of plant holobiont fitness, especially in important crops (Chalupowicz et al., 2019).

Although a vast number of tools that handle DRS data specifically or compatibly are available, new methods that reduce the variability in experimental procedures and data analysis between different studies and platforms should be developed. This will permit the integration of existing DRS data from multiple plants to obtain greater insight into transcriptome evolution by building comprehensive databases and will simplify difficulties in future data processing by providing standardized protocols. In addition, many currently available tools for DRS data analysis have been tested only with mammalian datasets, and further benchmarking of their performance in plant species is needed. Whether special characteristics of complex plant genomes, such as high TE content and ploidy, should be taken into consideration also remains to be evaluated (Chen et al., 2023b).

Exploring the distribution, function, and evolution of TE-related transcripts in plants

When annotating gene structures de novo or with transcriptomic evidence, researchers often mask repeat sequences within the genome first, filtering out TE-related genes because their reliable structures can be hard to predict (Holt and Yandell, 2011). Many studies have highlighted the important role of TEs in driving genome evolution (Quesneville, 2020), but exactly how they influence gene expression at the transcriptional level is still poorly understood. Thousands of TE-gene transcripts have been reported in A. thaliana, and TE insertion can regulate RNA stability (Berthelier et al., 2023). With DRS data, not only TE-gene transcripts but also lncRNAs with TE-derived sequences can be precisely identified and quantified, independent of genome annotation quality in plants, enabling statistical analysis of their features such as the relationship between insert position and transcript abundance. This will also expand the repertoire of coding regions within the genome and lay the groundwork for further investigation of their biological functions, even through conventional approaches such as the construction of interaction networks.

Unraveling the biological functions of authentic plant fusion transcripts

Compared with intensive functional research on pathogenic mechanisms in human cancer, identification of reliable fusion events in plants, an essential prerequisite, seems questionable when using short reads only, let alone the interpretation of their functions. Nearly 80 000 fusion events from 3520 paired-end RNA-seq samples of A. thaliana were reported (Singh et al., 2019), compared with only about 1000 in maize (Wang et al., 2016). This substantial difference raises questions about the authenticity of identified fusion events in plants, which could presumably be attributable to template switching during the PCR step (Figure 3B). Further studies using DRS can remove such experimental by-products first and clarify many puzzles regarding the identity and classification of fusion transcripts in plants. For example, some sense–antisense fusions were categorized as antisense lncRNAs in human cells (Mukherjee and Frenkel-Morgenstern, 2022). A reliable repertoire of fusion events will also facilitate the exploration of their biological effects from a multitude of dimensions. The fact that fusion events at the DNA level may arise from chromosomal rearrangements means that they can provide new insights into the evolutionary history and genetic diversity of plant species. However, overlooked fusion transcripts at the RNA level, even in humans, may participate in complex interaction networks. For example, they may further guide the exchange of chromosomal segments by interacting with their parental genes and altering chromosomal DNA looping (Gupta et al., 2018), or they may mediate the formation of secondary fusion events (Vellichirammal et al., 2020). Therefore, profiling the landscape of fusion transcripts in plants will be important for understanding various aspects of plant biology during normal development and in response to various environmental stresses, providing unique clues about the intriguing adaptive mechanisms of plants.

Molecular functions of RNA modifications associated with multiple RNA features and heterosis in plants

Epitranscriptomics has emerged as a fascinating hotspot in plant research, but unlike epigenomic methods, methods such as RNA immunoprecipitation may fail to capture the intact sequences of transcripts and, more important, their secondary structures. DRS can, to some extent, offer an opportunity to explore the combinatorial interplay between RNA modifications and many other RNA features such as APA, secondary structures, and even other types of co-occurring modification (Figure 4B), as well as the possible roles of modifications within fusion transcripts and circRNAs (Zhou et al., 2021). Recent advances in the structural interrogation of single RNA molecules through chemical probing based on DRS and the coordinated regulation of gene expression by both m6A and m5C demonstrate the feasibility of at least some of these ideas (Bizuayehu et al., 2022; Alan et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2023a). Integrating data that detect specific RNA-binding proteins can facilitate the determination of some poorly understood mechanisms by which modifications influence RNA metabolism in plants. For example, a recent study suggests that DDX21, an RNA helicase, directs m6A modification on nascent RNAs to promote transcription termination (Hao et al., 2024). In addition, we previously examined several factors that contribute to allele-specific translational efficiency in rice, although prevailing RNA modifications were ignored owing to a limitation of available DRS data for the hybrid and its parents. Because modifications such as m6A have been reported to mediate multiple biological processes, including translation, it is expected that further studies will help to illustrate the role of RNA modifications in determining the divergence in translational efficiency between pairs of alleles, especially those that show differences only in the hybrid (Zhu et al., 2023). Likewise, it would be intriguing to investigate allele-specific APA and poly(A) length in hybrid plants on the basis of full-length transcripts that could easily be assigned to their parent of origin owing to a greater possibility of containing heterozygous SNPs (Workman et al., 2019); this could offer unique insights into the mechanism that contributes to allele-specific expression. Although research analyzing the potential association between base modifications and heterosis has been performed, this study focused on modifications at the DNA level, such as CG and CHG (Ma et al., 2021); whether modifications at the RNA level could also be key players in the complex mechanisms that underlie heterosis remains unclear and requires further investigation.

Capturing the complete landscape of RNA modification variations within species: A step toward the pan-epitranscriptome

Sustained reductions in sequencing costs and improvements in the resolution and accuracy of DRS can presumably open new avenues in the area of pan-transcriptomics. Transcriptomic studies serve as an important bridge linking genotypes with their phenotypic consequences, but increasing evidence suggests that capturing the diversity in transcriptomes between species will require studies to be scaled up to the pan-transcriptomic level, in which comparative analyses such as alternative splicing can be performed to gain more insight into phenotype variation and speciation. Related studies in maize and barley have revealed significant expression presence and absence variations, some located on important trait-related quantitative trait loci (Jin et al., 2016; Ma et al., 2019). A more recent study characterized dynamic changes in alternative splicing in response to cold stress in rice by constructing a pan-transcriptome using isoform sequencing data from cold-tolerant and cold-sensitive rice cultivars (Zhong et al., 2024). RNA modifications also participate in the regulation of gene expression, and with more accurate and quantitative modification calls from DRS data, it will be possible to generate an effective pan-epitranscriptome, defined as a collection of core and dispensable RNA modifications based on transcriptomes from multiple varieties of the same plant species (Figure 5). Profiling the comprehensive single-molecule transcriptome at the single-cell level in plants

Single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) overcomes the limitations of bulk RNA-seq, revealing the cellular heterogeneity of gene expression across tissues, empowering the investigation of developmental trajectories, and enabling the identification of new cell types in plants (Shaw et al., 2021). Plant single-cell transcriptomics is a burgeoning field, as indicated by the rapidly increasing number of studies that are extending scRNA-seq to diverse plant tissues beyond the root and leaf (Zheng et al., 2023) and to multiple RNA types such as lncRNAs (Cao et al., 2023), as well as the development of innovative methods for single-cell spatiotemporal gene expression analysis (Yu et al., 2023b; Yao et al., 2024). The nascent single-cell long-read sequencing of human cell lines that integrates 10X Chromium scRNA-seq libraries with PacBio or ONT platforms has demonstrated its ability to reveal isoform diversity, such as novel and cell-type-specific alternative splicing, at an unprecedented resolution (Kumari et al., 2024). The capture of full-length transcripts also overcomes the 3′ and 5′ sequencing bias caused by the fragmentation step in traditional scRNA-seq, which limits the latter technique to gene expression quantification rather than characterization of intact RNA molecules (Chen et al., 2023a). With similar combined approaches, an early study sequenced full-length RNAs at the single-nucleus level in A. thaliana, demonstrating that cell identities could be faithfully retained on the basis of long reads and that heterogeneity in alternative splicing and APA were ubiquitous among cell clusters (Long et al., 2021). Nevertheless, cDNA amplification is still required to obtain sufficient RNA materials from individual cells in such methods; thus, RNA modifications and structures are not measured directly. The application of DRS at the single-cell level in the near future is anticipated to efficiently resolve these challenges, although massive efforts will be required to optimize the library construction protocol for plants and the corresponding machine learning or statistical algorithms (MacKenzie and Argyropoulos, 2023). A comprehensive understanding of plant transcriptomes at the single-cell level could be achieved with the assistance of direct long-read scRNA-seq, which should encompass both RNA processing signatures and epitranscriptomic marks.

Concluding remarks

The potential applications of DRS in plants are not limited to those described above. In summary, existing studies highlight the use of DRS as a “scaffold,” tying together various RNA features that would otherwise be analyzed independently and thus facilitating the shift from descriptive to functional analyses of RNA metabolism in plants. Moreover, future characterization of the native and comprehensive RNA landscapes of important crop species using DRS will also accelerate the development of stress-tolerant, high-yielding, and nutritionally enhanced crop varieties.
