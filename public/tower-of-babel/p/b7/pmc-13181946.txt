An 18-month pilot microbiological evaluation of sterilized operating room supplies stored under routine clinical conditions at a single hospital in Japan
Authors: Kentaro Hara, Misa Tomooka, Reika Tachibana, Ryosuke Kumashiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTime-related sterility maintenance (TRSM) remains common in Japanese operating rooms (ORs), requiring routine re-sterilization of unopened supplies. Event-related sterility maintenance (ERSM), which defines loss of sterility based on contamination events rather than elapsed time, may reduce unnecessary reprocessing and associated burden. This study reports a pre-specified 18-month pilot interim analysis of a prospective observational evaluation of ERSM.ResultsThis single-center prospective observational study was conducted in a tertiary-care OR. Representative materials (titanium instruments, cotton, and latex) were sterilized using steam, ethylene oxide, or vaporized hydrogen peroxide, double-bagged and heat-sealed in ISO 11607-1–compliant sterile barrier systems, and stored in closed cabinets at three OR locations under routine clinical handling. Sterility testing followed the Japanese Pharmacopoeia (18th edition) sterility test (4.06), harmonized with USP < 71 > and Ph. Eur. 2.6.1. At 18 months, all 18 cultured items showed no microbial growth (0/18) across all sterilization modalities and storage locations. These interim findings are descriptive and suggest feasibility only; the ongoing protocol will continue to the planned 60-month evaluation.

Introduction

Many hospitals in Japan continue to apply time-related sterility maintenance (TRSM) to operating room (OR) supplies (e.g., fixed shelf lives of 3–6 months), which prompts re-sterilization of unused but unopened items [1, 2]. In contrast, international guidance increasingly endorses event-related sterility maintenance (ERSM), whereby sterility is considered compromised only when a contamination-related event occurs (e.g., package damage, moisture, or improper handling), rather than by elapsed time alone [3–5]. Transitioning from TRSM to ERSM has the potential to reduce unnecessary re-processing, staff workload, downtime, and costs, and may also lessen environmental burdens associated with repeated sterilization cycles, including energy and water consumption and packaging waste [6–9]. However, prospective clinical data from OR settings remain limited, particularly in Japan, which constrains evidence-based adoption of ERSM in routine perioperative practice [10].

Microbial ingress into packaged sterile goods is primarily driven by package integrity, handling events, and storage conditions, such as air cleanliness, temperature, relative humidity, and distance from the floor and walls, whereas duration per se appears to play a lesser role when package integrity is maintained [11–14]. Bench and clinical storage studies have generally reported very low contamination rates for properly packaged items stored for months to years [13–15]. Nonetheless, these investigations have largely been conducted outside Japan, vary in design and storage conditions, and may not be directly generalizable to contemporary Japanese OR environments and workflows.

To address this evidence gap, we initiated a prospective, single-center evaluation of sterilized, double-bagged surgical materials stored under guideline-compliant operating room (OR) conditions, including recommendations from the Association of periOperative Registered Nurses (AORN) for sterile storage and handling, with culture-based sterility testing at pre-specified time points based on ERSM principles [16, 17].

This report presents the pre-planned interim 18-month findings, testing the hypothesis that sterility of OR supplies can be maintained for at least 18 months under controlled storage and handling consistent with ERSM.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a prospective, single-center observational study in the operating room (OR) complex of a tertiary hospital. Reporting adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement, and the completed checklist is provided in the Supplement [18].

Items and sterilization processes

To frame the microbiological assessment a priori, sterility testing followed The Japanese Pharmacopoeia (JP), 18th edition, Sterility Test (4.06), which is interchangeable with USP < 71 > and Ph. Eur. 2.6.1 according to ICH Q4B Annex 8(R1) [19, 20].

Representative OR materials were selected: (i) titanium instruments, (ii) cotton products (e.g., gauze), and (iii) latex items. After processing, items were packaged within sterile barrier systems compliant with ISO 11607-1 [21].

Sterilization modalities and device/cycle details followed institutional standard operating procedures aligned with international standards:

Steam (moist heat): in accordance with ISO 17665-1 [22], using a bench-top wrapped-goods autoclave (Miura RG-32FVW, Miura Co., Ltd., Japan). The cycle consisted of a 135 °C, 10-minute exposure phase followed by a 30-minute drying phase.Ethylene oxide (EO): in accordance with ISO 11,135 [23], using an EO sterilizer (Miura E-32FW, Miura Co., Ltd., Japan). The cycle conditions were 100 kPa, 55 °C, with a 3.5-hour exposure phase followed by 12 h of aeration.Vaporized hydrogen peroxide (VH₂O₂): using the manufacturer’s standard cycle on STERRAD® 100NX (Advanced Sterilization Products, USA), with process characterization based on ISO 14,937 [24]. The standard cycle duration was 47 min.

Routine process monitoring used biological indicators (ISO 11138-1 compliant; manufacturer: 3 M, USA) and chemical indicators (ISO 11140-1 compliant; manufacturer: 3 M, USA) as appropriate to the load and cycle [25, 26].

Packaging and sterile barrier systems

Immediately after sterilization, items were double-bagged and heat-sealed within sterile barrier systems (SBS) that met ISO 11607-1 performance requirements [21]. Packaging materials included HALYARD® KR-10 pouches (O&M Halyard, USA) and STERRAD® regular rolls (19672; Advanced Sterilization Products, USA). Sealing was performed with a temperature-controlled impulse sealer (Fuji Impulse MS-451THP2, Fuji Impulse Co., Ltd., Japan), and seal–peel checks were conducted on representative lots. Lot IDs and cycle/date information were labeled on each package for traceability.

Storage environment and handling

Packages were stored at three distinct OR storage locations—A, B, and C—within closed cabinets with doors (Fig. 1).

Environmental controls reflected routine OR-core conditions: air cleanliness approximating Class 10,000, a temperature of 24 °C, and relative humidity of 50%. Shelving clearances followed institutional policy (≥ 20 cm above the floor, ≥ 45 cm below the ceiling, and ≥ 5.0 cm from exterior walls). Storage and handling procedures followed institutional policies consistent with AAMI ST79 [27]. Storage cabinets, including external surfaces and door handles, were included in routine environmental cleaning. Engineering services monitored environmental set points and logged any excursions.

Weekly package integrity checks were conducted every Friday as part of routine OR practice by trained operating room nursing staff, with oversight by the study investigators. Event-related incidents (e.g., visible damage, wetting, dropped items) were logged prospectively.

Sampling schedule and cohort

Sampling time points were pre-specified in the study protocol, including an interim culture at 18 months (reported here). Longer-term follow-up cultures are ongoing under the same storage and handling conditions.

At the 18-month time point, 18 items were cultured. Items were evenly distributed across the three storage locations (A, B, and C; n = 6 each) and across sterilization modalities (steam, EO, and VH₂O₂; n = 6 each).

The primary endpoint was binary (presence or absence of microbial growth after 14 days of incubation). The interim cohort size was predefined based on operational feasibility and the need to avoid disrupting routine inventory; no formal sample size calculation was performed.

Sterility testing procedures

All sterility tests were performed by an external microbiology laboratory in Japan, following JP 18th edition Sterility Test (4.06); per ICH Q4B Annex 8(R1), these methods are interchangeable with USP < 71 > and Ph. Eur. 2.6.1 [19, 20]. For this interim run, testing employed soybean–casein digest (SCD) medium only, incubated statically at 30 °C for 14 days under validated standard operating procedures. Visual determination of turbidity by trained staff served as the primary growth endpoint; any suspect bottles were re-examined and subcultured according to JP procedures and laboratory SOPs.

Controls and media preparation

The laboratory prepared a Bacillus subtilis NBRC 3134 spore suspension and adjusted it to 1 × 10³ cells/mL using a disposable hemocytometer (Bürker–Türk type). SCD medium was dispensed into 100 mL media bottles (≈ 80 mL each) and 1000 mL tall beakers (≈ 800 mL each), then autoclaved together with a biological indicator (BI) for cycle verification. Beaker mouths were covered with aluminum foil and secured with rubber bands. Controls were validated before testing specimens: positive controls (SCD plus spores) were required to show visible growth within 3 days, whereas negative controls were required to remain clear. BI readouts and pre- and post-process images were recorded.

Test article handling and inoculation format

Under a clean bench, each package was externally disinfected and then opened aseptically. Titanium forceps were immersed in 800 mL SCD beakers, whereas latex rubber bands and cotton gauze were immersed in 80 mL SCD bottles. If an item did not fully submerge, pre-sterilized SCD medium was added to ensure complete immersion. Pre-incubation (Day 0) and post-incubation (Day 14) states were photographed for documentation. Media prepared on the same day were treated as the same lot.

Incubation and endpoint

All vessels were incubated at 30 °C for 14 days under static conditions. After incubation, the laboratory recorded the presence or absence of visible turbidity for each item and reconfirmed positive-control turbidity within 3 days and negative-control clarity. Autoclave BI evidence was retained for each sterilization of media. The final deliverable from the laboratory comprised a PDF report with photographs and BI results.

Outcomes and data handling (descriptive only)

The primary outcome was the presence or absence of microbial growth at 14 days for each item. Secondary observations included package integrity and any event-related incidents during storage and handling.

Results are reported descriptively only, overall and stratified by storage location (A, B, and C), as counts and simple percentages; no inferential statistical analyses were performed.

Quality assurance

Sterilization cycles were run by certified staff, and indicator results and equipment logs were archived. Seal integrity was verified visually and by seal–peel checks on representative lots in accordance with ISO 11607-1 [21]. The external laboratory provided accreditation and method-validation summaries referencing JP 18 and ICH Q4B Annex 8(R1) [19, 20].

Ethical considerations

This project did not involve human participants or animals. The institutional review board determined that the activity did not constitute human subjects research (administrative determination on file). No patient-identifiable information was collected.

Reporting guideline statement

This report adheres to the STROBE statement, and the completed checklist is provided in the Supplement [18].

Results

At the pre-specified 18-month interim time point, 18 sterile packages were submitted for sterility testing using soybean–casein digest (SCD) medium with static incubation at 30 °C for 14 days. No turbidity was observed in any culture vessel, and microscopic examination detected no microorganisms; accordingly, none of the tested items showed microbial growth (0/18, 0%).

Negative findings were consistent across all pre-specified sterilization modalities—steam, ethylene oxide, and vaporized hydrogen peroxide—with six items tested per modality (0/6 each). Similarly, all items from the three storage locations (A–C; six per location) were culture-negative after the full incubation period.

Assay validity was confirmed using predefined positive and negative controls. Positive controls demonstrated expected microbial growth within 3 days, whereas negative controls remained clear throughout the 14-day incubation period. Biological indicators used during media preparation met acceptance criteria, confirming procedural reliability.

No compromised packaging (e.g., torn, wet, or improperly sealed) was identified upon laboratory receipt, and all specimens proceeded to testing as planned. Discussion

In this prospective interim analysis, all 18 sterile packages stored under guideline-compliant operating room (OR) conditions remained culture-negative at 18 months. This pattern aligns with event-related sterility maintenance (ERSM) principles, which emphasize package integrity and handling events as primary determinants of sterility rather than elapsed time alone [5, 11, 14, 15]. Our findings are directionally consistent with prior bench and clinical evidence demonstrating very low contamination rates for properly packaged items stored for months to years when environmental and handling controls are maintained [13–15, 28, 29].

The absence of microbial growth across all three sterilization modalities (steam, ethylene oxide, and vaporized hydrogen peroxide) suggests that once a validated sterilization process and packaging system are in place, the risk of time-dependent microbial ingress is low. This interpretation is consistent with earlier studies demonstrating maintained sterility over extended storage durations and with comparisons of single versus double barrier systems [29, 30]. The uniformly negative results across all three OR storage locations in this study further indicate that standard environmental controls (temperature, relative humidity, and air cleanliness), when combined with cabinet storage and routine integrity checks, provide sufficient mitigation of contamination risks under ERSM-consistent procedures and routine operating-room handling reflective of real-world clinical conditions [16, 27, 29].

ERSM implementation may reduce unnecessary reprocessing, labor demands, workflow interruptions, and environmental burdens associated with repeated sterilization cycles, including energy consumption, water use, and packaging waste [6–9]. Prior literature has noted substantial heterogeneity and limited generalizability in ERSM-related evidence, particularly outside North America and Europe [28]. The present study adds prospective, Japan-based microbiological data using harmonized pharmacopeial methods (JP 18/USP/Ph. Eur.) and prospectively logged package integrity, thereby contributing to the evidence foundation needed for ERSM adoption in Japanese OR settings.

Although our evaluation focused on unopened, fully packaged items, it is important to distinguish these findings from contamination risks associated with opened sterile fields. Several randomized and controlled studies have demonstrated measurable time-dependent increases in surface contamination for uncovered instrument tables and open trays, with substantial reductions when protective covers are used [30–32]. These observations reinforce that contamination is driven by discrete events such as opening and exposure, consistent with ERSM principles. Incorporating covering practices during idle periods can therefore complement ERSM-based inventory management without reverting to arbitrary shelf-life policies.

This interim evaluation has several strengths, including protocolized selection of materials, storage under controlled real-world OR conditions, routine integrity checks, and microbiological testing performed by an external accredited laboratory using validated pharmacopeial methods. Limitations include the descriptive nature of the analysis, modest interim sample size, single-center design, and restriction to selected materials and packaging configurations. Although environmental parameters were monitored, microclimatic variation within cabinets and rare handling deviations cannot be fully excluded.

Taken together, the 18-month findings suggest that sterility was maintained under the defined study conditions. Because this evaluation represents an interim dataset from a single-center protocol with selected item types, the results should be interpreted as preliminary. Ongoing follow-up under the same protocol will allow evaluation of longer-term sterility maintenance and broader applicability in perioperative settings. The longer follow-up period is intended for microbiological characterization rather than to imply routine clinical shelf-life extension.

Conclusion

In this pre-specified interim analysis, all tested operating-room supplies remained culture-negative at 18 months under the defined storage and handling conditions. These descriptive findings suggest that sterility was maintained in this setting when package integrity and routine monitoring were preserved.

Because this evaluation was conducted in a single center with selected item types and represents an interim dataset, the results should be interpreted as preliminary and not definitive. Ongoing follow-up under the same protocol will allow assessment of longer-term sterility maintenance and broader applicability.
