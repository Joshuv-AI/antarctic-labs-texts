Progress report on new epilepsy treatments: A summary of the Eighteenth Eilat Conference on New Antiepileptic Drugs and Devices (EILAT XVIII). II. Treatments in more advanced clinical development
Authors: Meir Bialer, Cecilie Johannessen Landmark, Matthias J Koepp, Emilio Perucca, Piero Perucca, Torbjörn Tomson, H Steve White, Elaine Wirrell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article summarizes data for 13 investigational treatments for which at least preliminary seizure outcome data in patients with epilepsy were reported at the Eighteenth Eilat Conference on New Antiepileptic Drugs and Devices held in Madrid, Spain, on May 3–6, 2026. The treatments reviewed include bexicaserin, a selective 5‐hydroxytryptamine (5‐HT, serotonin) type 2C (5‐HT2C) receptor superagonist investigated as a treatment for developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEEs); BMB‐101, a selective 5‐HT2C receptor agonist investigated for the treatment of absence seizures and DEEs; elsunersen, an antisense oligonucleotide designed for the treatment of early‐onset SCN2A‐DEE; EPX‐100 (clemizole hydrochloride), an antihistamine endowed with agonist activity at 5‐HT2A and 5‐HT2B receptors, repurposed as a treatment for DEEs; ES‐481, an antagonist of α‐amino‐3‐hydroxy‐5‐methyl‐4‐isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptors containing the transmembrane AMPA receptor regulatory protein γ8 (TARP‐γ8), under investigation for the treatment of drug‐resistant epilepsy; ETX‐101, a gene therapy in development for the treatment of SCN1A‐positive Dravet syndrome; PrevEp‐006 (intranasal seletracetam), a synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A (SV2A) ligand under investigation as an acute seizure rescue therapy; radiprodil, a negative allosteric modulator that binds to the GluN2B subunit of the N‐methyl‐d‐aspartate (NMDA) receptor, in development for the treatment of seizures and other symptoms associated with GRIN‐related neurodevelopmental disorder, tuberous sclerosis complex, and focal cortical dysplasia type II; RAP‐219, a selective negative allosteric modulator of TARP‐γ8, in development for drug‐resistant focal epilepsy; relutrigine, a selective disease‐state sodium channel modulator investigated as a treatment for SCN2A‐ and SCN8A‐DEE; Staccato Alprazolam, a breath‐actuated device that delivers alprazolam to the lungs for rapid seizure termination; vormatrigine, a functionally selective sodium channel modulator in development for the treatment of focal seizures; and zorevunersen, an antisense oligonucleotide engineered to restore endogenous Nav1.1 protein expression, investigated for the treatment of Dravet syndrome. Overall, the evidence reviewed justifies expectations for improved health outcomes for the millions of children and adults with epilepsy who do not fully benefit from existing therapies.Keywords: antiseizure medications, bexicaserin, BMB‐101, drug development, drug‐resistant epilepsy, elsunersen, EPX‐100, ES‐481, ETX‐101, PrevEp‐006 (seletracetam), radiprodil, RAP‐219, relutrigine, Staccato Alprazolam, vormatrigine, zorevunersen

INTRODUCTION

This article provides a review of preclinical and clinical data for 13 compounds presented at the Eighteenth EILAT Conference on New Antiepileptic Drugs and Devices (EILAT XVIII) in May 2026, currently under investigation for the treatment of a variety of seizure disorders. The methodology used for the preparation of EILAT progress reports has been described in detail previously 1 and explained briefly in an accompanying article, which included summaries of treatments in preclinical and early clinical development also presented at EILAT XVIII. 2 This report discusses compounds in more advanced clinical development, defined as those for which at least preliminary data on seizure outcomes in patients with epilepsy have been disclosed. The treatments reviewed include both small molecules and nucleic acid–based therapies, and they are presented in alphabetical order according to the standardized format used in EILAT progress reports.

BEXICASERIN

Tolga Uz1, Shikha Polega1, Sheryl Anne D. Vermudez1, Jesper Frank Bastlund1, Nuggehally R. Srinivas2

1H. Lundbeck A/S, Copenhagen, Denmark; 2Longboard Pharmaceuticals, Inc. (now part of H. Lundbeck A/S), La Jolla, CA, USA

Introduction and rationale for development

A growing body of preclinical and clinical evidence supports the role of 5‐hydroxytryptamine (5‐HT, serotonin) type 2C (5‐HT2C) receptors in modulating seizure activity. 3 , 4 Collectively, the developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEEs) comprise the most severe group of epilepsies, characterized by seizures that are generally resistant to available treatments, abundant background epileptiform activity, and developmental slowing or regression. 4 Accordingly, the “DEE” nomenclature represents an entry point in diagnosis, as a DEE can result from genetic, structural, metabolic, or immune causes, or the etiology may be unknown. Furthermore, given the prevalence of polypharmacy with multiple anti‐seizure medications (ASMs) as well as the myriad of comorbid conditions that accompany DEEs, there is a need for effective treatments with a low drug–drug interaction potential and minimal adverse effect burden. Bexicaserin is an oral, highly selective 5‐HT2C receptor superagonist currently in Phase 3 clinical development to evaluate its safety, tolerability, and efficacy in the treatment of seizures associated with DEEs in general, for the first time extending beyond the more well‐defined syndromes typically represented in clinical trials, such as Dravet syndrome (DS) and Lennox–Gastaut syndrome (LGS). 3 , 4 , 5

Pharmacology

Activity profile in animal models of seizures and epilepsy

Bexicaserin demonstrated anti‐seizure activity against different seizure types in multiple validated animal (zebrafish and rodent) models arising from diverse etiologies, as previously described in the EILAT XVII progress report. 3 In addition, in a dilute brown non‐agouti (DBA)/1 mouse model of sound‐induced seizures, bexicaserin significantly reduced the frequency of tonic–clonic seizures and respiratory arrest, and suppressed c‐Fos expression (a marker of neural activity) in regions involved in generalized tonic–clonic seizures and cardiorespiratory control, thereby protecting against motor seizures and the seizure‐induced respiratory arrest associated with sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP). 6 Bexicaserin also reduced the incidence and duration of spike–wave discharges associated with absence seizures in the Genetic Absence Epilepsy Rat from Strasbourg (GAERS) model. 7 Results from preclinical models suggest that the broad spectrum anti‐seizure effects of bexicaserin are mediated through activation of central 5‐HT2C receptors. 6 , 7

Other pharmacological properties

Bexicaserin exhibited high selectivity for 5‐HT2C receptors over other 5‐HT receptor subtypes, both in binding and functional inositol phosphate accumulation assays (more than 200‐fold over 5‐HT2A and 5‐HT2B), and no significant binding at other non‐5‐HT molecular targets at clinically relevant concentrations. 3 , 5 Hence, through this novel high selectivity, the potential for adverse effects linked with other receptor subtypes (e.g., 5‐HT2B receptors' association with cardiac valvulopathy and pulmonary arterial hypertension, and 5‐HT2A receptors' association with hallucinogenic activity) may be minimized. 4 , 5 In addition, the maximal activity of bexicaserin at 5‐HT2C receptors in vitro, as measured using both dynamic mass redistribution and inositol phosphate accumulation assays, exceeded that induced by the endogenous ligand (serotonin), consistent with its classification as a 5‐HT2C superagonist. 3 , 5

Mechanism of action

Preclinical and clinical data have shown that 5‐HT2C receptors are expressed in epileptogenic regions of the brain and are hypothesized to reduce hyperexcitability. 4 , 8 , 9 Bexicaserin, through its activation of 5‐HT2C receptors, is believed to act via a dual mechanism of action. The exact mechanism is still under investigation but likely involves direct modulation of both inhibitory γ‐aminobutyric acid (GABA)ergic and excitatory glutamatergic neuronal function, offering potential broad therapeutic efficacy in the treatment of seizures and non‐seizure symptoms associated with DEEs arising from diverse underlying etiologies. 4 , 5

Toxicology

Safety pharmacology and toxicology studies of bexicaserin across multiple species were outlined previously in the EILAT XVII progress report. 3 In addition, a complete reproductive toxicity package as well as an abuse liability study package have been finalized and the major human metabolite has been identified (glucuronide metabolite M20). No unexpected safety issues have been identified, and studies continue to offer comfortable safety margins toward human therapeutic doses.

Pharmacokinetic and metabolic profile

The pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profile of bexicaserin has been characterized in Phase 1 single ascending dose and multiple ascending dose studies in healthy adults (18–55 years of age), with an absorption profile previously reported in the EILAT XVII progress report. 3 Briefly, repeated oral dosing demonstrated dose‐dependent plasma accumulation and target engagement within the central nervous system (CNS), that is, central 5‐HT2C receptors. Bexicaserin is rapidly absorbed following oral administration of doses ranging from 3 to 24 mg three times daily (t.i.d.), with no clinically significant food effect, and reaches maximum plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) concentrations within 1 to 2 h and 2 to 3 h of dosing, respectively. 3 , 10 , 11 Bexicaserin is <10% bound to plasma proteins. 11

The mean half‐life of bexicaserin ranged from 4.8–6.5 h across dose levels (3 to 24 mg, t.i.d.). Glucuronidation by the uridine diphosphate glucuronosyltransferase (UGT) enzymes UGT2B7, UG2B15, and UGT2B17, plays a major role in the metabolism of bexicaserin, which is its primary route of clearance, whereas <5% of an administered dose is excreted in urine as unchanged drug. 3 , 11 , 12 , 13 The pharmacokinetics of several pharmacologically inactive metabolites (oxidative metabolites M9 and M12, and glucuronide metabolite M20) have been characterized in plasma. 11 , 13

Drug interactions

Drug interactions are a common concern due to frequent use of polypharmacy in patients with DEEs and have the potential to reduce drug efficacy and/or increase the risk of adverse effects. 4 Bexicaserin was designed to have reduced dependency on cytochrome P450 (CYP)–mediated metabolism. Being a substrate for metabolism primarily via UGT, bexicaserin is not expected to be an object (i.e., victim) of interactions caused by changes in activity of CYP enzymes. Consistent with this prediction, a clinical study in healthy participants demonstrated that the pharmacokinetics of bexicaserin is only minimally affected by treatment with multiple doses of quinidine (324 mg extended‐release tablets twice daily [b.i.d.] for five doses), an inhibitor of CYP2D6, CYP3A4, and the P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp) transporter. 12 However, because bexicaserin is a UGT substrate, interactions with inhibitors or inducers of UGT or transporters may occur. In healthy participants, the area under the plasma concentration–time curve (AUC) of bexicaserin increased by about 80% with co‐administration of the UGT inhibitor probenecid (1000 mg for 3 days). 12

Drug‐level measurements made in participants included in the double‐blind, placebo‐controlled Phase 1b/2a PACIFIC trial (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT05364021) showed a low potential for plasma bexicaserin levels to be affected by concomitantly administered ASMs. 14 In the PACIFIC double‐blind trial, the most common concomitant ASMs were clobazam, valproic acid, levetiracetam, cannabidiol, and lamotrigine. Overall, these data suggest that dosage adjustments are not necessary when bexicaserin is co‐administered with commonly prescribed ASMs. 14 Likewise, in this evaluation, bexicaserin did not change the plasma exposure of a wide array of co‐administered ASMs, and their levels remained stable during the study, suggesting a low potential for bexicaserin to act as a precipitant of drug interactions. 4

Efficacy data

The randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled PACIFIC trial evaluated the safety, tolerability, and efficacy of bexicaserin for the treatment of seizures associated with DEEs, and constituted the first regulatory‐endorsed study design to enroll participants with a variety of DEEs within a single trial. Study design and key findings were previously outlined in the EILAT XVII progress report. 3 Safety outcomes were assessed in 52 participants (bexicaserin, n = 43; placebo, n = 9), whereas efficacy outcomes were assessed in the full analysis set that comprised 44 participants (bexicaserin, n = 35; placebo, n = 9). In these participants, the dose of bexicaserin during the maintenance period ranged from 6 to 12 mg, t.i.d., according to tolerability. The proportions of participants in the bexicaserin group (n = 35) with ≥50%, ≥75%, and ≥90% reductions in countable motor seizure frequency were 60.0%, 31.4%, and 11.4%, respectively, compared with 33.3%, 0%, and 0%, respectively, in the placebo group (n = 9). Of note, countable motor seizure frequency reductions with bexicaserin were comparable across all prespecified participant subgroups (bexicaserin vs placebo: DS, −74.6% [n = 3] vs N/A [n = 0]; LGS, −50.8% [n = 17] vs −17.4% [n = 5]; and "DEE Other", −65.5% [n = 15] vs −32.2% [n = 4]), suggesting that the cumulative effect was not driven by a particular subgroup. In addition, the response to bexicaserin was consistent across two etiologically heterogeneous subgroups, LGS and "DEE Other", further supporting the rationale for a clinical trial approach that aggregates all types of DEEs in a single group. 3 , 4 , 15 Post hoc analyses showed that bexicaserin treatment resulted in a rapid onset of reduction in countable motor seizure frequencies compared with placebo that was observed by Weeks 1–2 (−49.6% vs 1.5%), followed by a consistent and sustained response at all other time points throughout the treatment period. 15

Participants who successfully completed the PACIFIC double‐blind trial were eligible to participate in the 12‐month open‐label extension (OLE; NCT05626634). All participants (100%, n = 41) who completed the PACIFIC double‐blind trial enrolled in the OLE; those who completed the OLE (92.7%, n = 38) had the option to enroll in the expanded access program (EAP; NCT06149663), allowing them to continue bexicaserin at the same dose. Of those who completed the OLE, 94.7% (n = 36) enrolled in the EAP. Reduction of countable motor seizure frequency during bexicaserin treatment compared with the pre‐treatment baseline was sustained over the 12‐month OLE (−59.3% [n = 40]), and at 18 months (−60.2% [n = 30]) and 24 months (−53.7% [n = 17]) in the EAP. 15 , 16 These results are comparable to overall outcomes reported in the PACIFIC double‐blind trial. In the OLE, over half of participants receiving bexicaserin (n = 22/40, 55.0%) experienced a ≥50% reduction in countable motor seizure frequencies from baseline for up to 1 year; 40% (n = 16) experienced ≥75% reductions; and approximately 1 of 6 participants (n = 7, 17.5%) were nearly free from motor seizures (≥90% reduction in countable motor seizure frequency). 15 Taken together, these findings underscore the consistent and sustained response to bexicaserin, suggesting no loss of long‐term therapeutic benefit. 16

Based on these data, bexicaserin received Breakthrough Therapy designation from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of seizures associated with DEEs, as well as a Rare Pediatric Disease designation for DS and Orphan Drug designations for DS and LGS. 4 Bexicaserin also received Breakthrough Therapy designation from China's Center for Drug Evaluation. 17

Adverse effects

In both the Phase 1 single ascending dose and multiple ascending dose studies, bexicaserin showed a favorable safety and tolerability profile in healthy adults across single doses ranging from 1 to 24 mg and multiple doses ranging from 3 to 24 mg, t.i.d. Treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were mild to moderate, with the most commonly reported (>20% in either study) being headache, somnolence, dizziness, postural dizziness, orthostatic hypotension, nausea, and micturition urgency. In the single ascending dose study (bexicaserin, n = 30), there were no TEAEs leading to discontinuation and no deaths or serious TEAEs reported. 11 , 13 Among the four multiple ascending dose cohorts (bexicaserin, n = 25), one participant discontinued after experiencing three TEAEs (sluggishness, bladder pain, and muscular weakness), and two participants experienced severe TEAEs of insomnia and vomiting; in the dose‐titration cohort (a separate cohort of the multiple ascending dose study where dose was escalated over time within the same participants up to a maximum of 24 mg, t.i.d.; bexicaserin, n = 8), two participants each experienced one TEAE leading to discontinuation (headache and vomiting) and another participant experienced three severe TEAEs: anxiety, insomnia, and euphoric mood. 11

In the PACIFIC double‐blind trial, bexicaserin had a favorable safety and tolerability profile in adult and adolescent participants with DEEs (bexicaserin, n = 43). 4 Details were previously outlined in the EILAT XVII progress report, with somnolence reported as the most common TEAE leading to discontinuations. 3 Discontinuations occurred predominantly during the 15‐day titration period (n = 7) compared with the maintenance period (n = 2), suggesting that a slower, more flexible titration strategy may potentially mitigate dose‐related TEAEs. Indeed, among the cohort of nine participants who transitioned from placebo to bexicaserin upon enrollment in the OLE (which allowed a dose‐titration rate according to the investigator's discretion), bexicaserin was well tolerated and there were no discontinuations. 4 Further reinforcing the tolerability of bexicaserin, 100% of participants who completed the PACIFIC double‐blind trial (n = 41) enrolled in the OLE, and 94.7% of participants who completed the OLE continued treatment in the EAP. 16

No new safety signals were observed in the 12‐month OLE and 12‐month EAP interim analysis. The most common TEAEs reported during the OLE (in ≥5% of participants; n = 41) were upper respiratory tract infections (14.6%), seizures (12.2%), coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19; 9.8%), decreased appetite (9.8%), lethargy (9.8%), pyrexia (7.3%), gait disturbance (7.3%), gastroenteritis viral (7.3%), pneumonia (7.3%), sinusitis (7.3%), vomiting (7.3%), weight decreased (7.3%), and rash (7.3%). Eight participants (8/41, 19.5%) reported 11 serious TEAEs: seizure (n = 4), pneumonia (n = 3), pneumonia bacterial (n = 1), gait disturbance (n = 1), small intestine obstruction (n = 1), and agitation (n = 1). In the OLE, only one participant discontinued treatment due to a TEAE of lethargy; one discontinued due to consent withdrawal; and one discontinued due to relocation. 4

Planned studies

The EAP, or “compassionate use”, is ongoing and includes participants who completed the 12‐month OLE of the PACIFIC double‐blind trial, as well as individuals who meet eligibility criteria and are naïve to bexicaserin treatment.

The global Phase 3 DEEp program is currently enrolling patients with all types of DEEs (diagnoses according to the International League Against Epilepsy [ILAE] definitions) to evaluate the potential safety, tolerability, and efficacy of bexicaserin for the treatment of seizures across DEEs (DEEp OCEAN, NCT06719141 [LGS and other DEEs] and DEEp SEA, NCT06660394 [DS only]). To maximize the tolerability of the dosing paradigm, the DEEp program implements a 3‐week titration period. Participants receive escalating doses of bexicaserin or placebo, progressing to their highest tolerated dose (6 mg, 9 mg, or 12 mg, t.i.d.). The starting dose of the titration is 6 mg, t.i.d., with stepwise increases up to a maximum of 12 mg, t.i.d. Each participant will enter the maintenance period with the same dose they achieved by Day 22, based on tolerability. Participants who are unable to tolerate the starting dose are withdrawn from receiving the study drug. For pediatric participants (weighing ≤40 kg), a comparable weight‐based titration protocol is followed. In both the DEEp OCEAN and DEEp SEA trials, the titration period is followed by a 12‐week maintenance period, and a 7‐ to 14‐day taper period. Participants who complete either DEEp OCEAN or DEEp SEA are eligible to enroll in the DEEp OLE (NCT06908226). The DEEp OLE comprises a 52‐week maintenance period with extension until discontinuation or study closure.

BMB‐101

Stephen Collins, Alex Vasilkevich, Ian McDonald, Joshua Johnstone, Jan Torleif Pedersen

Bright Minds Biosciences, New York, NY, USA

Introduction and rationale for development

BMB‐101 is a novel, potent, and selective 5‐HT2C receptor agonist under development for the treatment of drug‐resistant seizure disorders, including absence seizures and DEEs. BMB‐101 was designed based in part on the chemical scaffold of tranylcypromine, a well‐known antidepressant first approved for use in the UK in 1960 and in the United States in 1961, for the treatment of major depression. 18 The goal was to create a highly selective 5‐HT2C agonist devoid of activity on 5‐HT2A and 5‐HT2B receptors, which are responsible for causing hallucinogenic effects and cardiac toxicity, respectively. 19 BMB‐101 is biased for the Gq signaling pathway without β‐arrestin recruitment, which could provide a reduced potential for tolerance. 20 Based on available data, BMB‐101 is to our knowledge the only compound in clinical development that has been shown to be a “biased” or “functionally selective” 5‐HT2C agonist.

Positive topline results of a Phase 2 clinical trial suggesting meaningful improvement in seizure frequency in patients with absence seizures and DEEs were reported in January 2026. 21 Preparations for global registrational trials in both indications are underway.

Pharmacology

Activity profile in animal models of seizures and epilepsy

BMB‐101 has demonstrated activity in a number of seizure models. In the Scn1Lab zebrafish model of DS, BMB‐101 reduced locomotor activity and the cumulative duration of epileptiform events in the brain similarly to what was demonstrated with other 5‐HT2C agonists. 22

BMB‐101 was also effective in the 6‐Hz (44 mA) mouse model of drug‐resistant focal seizures, reducing seizure duration at both 30 min and 1 h after intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection. 23 At 1 h, BMB‐101 doses of 30 mg/kg and 45 mg/kg decreased seizure duration by 45% and 74%, respectively, and completely prevented seizures in 1 out of 9 and 5 out of 8 mice, respectively. BMB‐101 was also tested in the DBA/2 audiogenic seizure mouse model of reflex seizures and death in epilepsy. 23 In this model, the compound reduced average seizure severity score from 3.2 (vehicle; tonic seizure with hind limb extension phase) to 1.8 (wild running) at doses of 15 and 30 mg/kg per os (p.o.) (Figure 1). Furthermore, at these doses, BMB‐101 abolished deaths and tonic seizures with hind limb extension. The findings in the DBA/2 mice, coupled with activity demonstrated in the maximal electroshock seizure (MES) model in both mouse and rat 24 for BMB‐JJ42 (close analog of BMB‐101) suggest that BMB‐101 can be effective against generalized tonic–clonic seizures.

BMB‐101 dose‐dependently reduces seizure severity in the dilute brown non‐agouti (DBA)/2 mouse model. Seizures were scored by behavioral category, including wild running, tonic seizures, clonic seizures, tonic seizures with hindlimb extension (HLE), and death. BMB‐101 displayed a prominent dose–response in reducing the average seizure severity score from 3.2 (vehicle; tonic with HLE phase) to 1.8 (wild running) at the 15 and 30 mg/kg doses. The selective 5‐HT2C receptor agonist lorcaserin has been shown to reduce spike–wave discharges in the GAERS model. 25 This effect is blocked by the selective 5‐HT2C antagonist SB‐242084, which supports a 5‐HT2C receptor–mediated mechanism in seizure suppression.

Other pharmacological properties

The 5‐HT2C receptor is a key regulator of compulsive behavior and food intake. As part of the initial indication selection, BMB‐101 was tested in models of binge eating disorder, fentanyl self‐administration (substance use disorder), and aggressive behavior (resident intruder model).

In a rat model predictive of activity in human binge eating disorder, animals were habituated to intermittent access to high‐fat palatable food, mimicking binge episodes characterized by rapid consumption and compulsive overeating. BMB‐JJ42, a close analog of BMB‐101, suppressed binge food intake in a dose‐dependent manner from 3 to 30 mg/kg, i.p.; lorcaserin (1 mg/kg, i.p.) was used as reference. BMB‐JJ42 at 10 mg/kg reduced binge intake similarly to that observed for lorcaserin at 1 mg/kg. At 30 mg/kg, BMB‐JJ42 was superior to lorcaserin in reducing food intake.

In a rat model of fentanyl self‐administration, used to probe opioid addiction neurobiology, BMB‐101 reduced fentanyl infusions dose‐dependently at doses of 10 to 60 mg/kg, s.c., with over 50% suppression at 60 mg/kg, without any associated disruption of spontaneous behaviors. The effect was demonstrated to be 5‐HT2C mediated because it was blocked by the 5‐HT2C selective antagonist SB242084.

In the rat resident intruder model, BMB‐101 (10–25 mg/kg, i.p.) showed a dose‐dependent reduction in attack duration, with an 87.5% reduction at 25 mg/kg.

Preclinical data across these models support BMB‐101's broader profile in impulse control and substance use disorders.

Mechanism of action

BMB‐101 is a highly selective agonist of the 5‐HT2C receptor with no clinically meaningful activity at other 5‐HT receptor subtypes. The median effective concentration (EC50) at the 5‐HT2c receptor is 16.2 nM. 24 Furthermore, BMB‐101 is a G‐protein–biased agonist as demonstrated using the Bioluminescence Resonance Energy Transfer (BRET)–based reporter system. 26

Enhancing serotonergic signaling has been shown to exert anti‐seizure effects in several experimental and clinical settings. 27 , 28 Absence seizures are widely understood to arise from abnormal oscillatory activity within the cortico‐thalamo‐cortical network, a circuit linking the cerebral cortex, thalamic relay nuclei, and the thalamic reticular nucleus. 29 5‐HT2C receptors expressed on subsets of cortical pyramidal neurons projecting to thalamic nuclei appear to play a role in regulating cortico‐thalamic excitability. Pharmacologic stimulation of 5‐HT2C receptors suppresses absence seizure activity in rodent models. 25 , 27 Mechanistically, 5‐HT2C receptor activation appears to reduce seizure susceptibility by modulating cortical output to thalamic nuclei and indirectly influencing thalamic reticular inhibitory control of thalamocortical relay neurons. Through this mechanism, serotonergic signaling can disrupt the hypersynchronous oscillations that characterize absence seizures. Together, these findings support the hypothesis that targeting 5‐HT2C receptors within corticothalamic pathways represents a potential therapeutic strategy for modulating the cortico‐thalamo‐cortical circuitry underlying absence epilepsy.

Toxicology

The toxicological profile of BMB‐101 following oral administration has been thoroughly evaluated in adult animals. Completed repeated‐dose toxicology studies include 28‐day, 3‐month, and 6‐month studies in mice, and 28‐day, 3‐month, and 9‐month studies in dogs. Juvenile and reproductive toxicology studies are ongoing. Results from the safety pharmacology and toxicology package provide strong support for the continued development of BMB‐101 and its advancement into further clinical programs.

Pharmacokinetic profile and target engagement

In Phase 1 studies in healthy subjects, BMB‐101 showed a pharmacokinetic profile consistent with a Biopharmaceutics Classification System class 1 type compound, including a high oral bioavailability (>90%). High oral bioavailability was determined in mice, rat, and dog by oral, intravenous (i.v.), and i.p. administration and in humans by modeling from oral delivery in the Phase 1 study. BMB‐101 exhibits linear pharmacokinetics after single oral doses ranging from 20 to 150 mg, and multiple doses ranging from 40 to 120 mg/day, given as two divided daily administrations (b.i.d.). Co‐administration with food did not affect BMB‐101 AUC and was associated with a small (~27%) reduction in peak plasma drug concentration (C max).

The plasma protein binding of BMB‐101 in mice, rats, and humans ranged from 63.1% to 73.6%. Half‐life as determined in healthy subjects after multiple dosing at steady‐state on Day 7 ranged from 5 to 7 h, with a mean of 6 h. 30

Pharmacokinetic studies in mice, monkeys, dogs, and humans demonstrated that BMB‐101 is eliminated primarily metabolically, with minimal excretion of the parent drug in the bile and urine. In humans, at least five major CYPs (CYP3A, CYP2C8 and CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP1A2) as well as UGTs contribute to the metabolism of BMB‐101. The most important metabolites have been identified, and they do not have activity in primary pharmacology studies on 5‐HT receptors, or in secondary wide‐target screenings.

Target engagement of BMB‐101 was first evaluated by assessing its effect on plasma prolactin levels, an established pharmacodynamic biomarker of serotonergic activation in both preclinical and clinical studies. 27 Serotonergic drugs reproducibly stimulate prolactin release primarily through activation of 5‐HT receptor subtypes (notably 5‐HT2A/2C and 5‐HT1A receptors) within the hypothalamus, leading to transient suppression of dopamine release by tuberoinfundibular dopaminergic neurons. The transient nature of the prolactin response reflects the dynamic regulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–dopaminergic axis and the rapid homeostatic feedback mechanisms that control prolactin secretion. In the multiple ascending dose part of the Phase I study, BMB‐101 did produce a transient increase in plasma prolactin (about 50%). The effect was observed 2 h after dosing at doses between 40 and 80 mg, b.i.d., and demonstrated serotonergic activation.

CNS target engagement was further demonstrated by a quantitative electroencephalography (EEG) analysis, demonstrating that BMB‐101 (40 to 80 mg, b.i.d.) exhibits an EEG signature consistent with that of an ASM (depression of alpha and beta power), without driving slower power bands, implying less or minimal sedation, even at highest dose. 21 , 30

Drug interactions

Permeability data obtained in the Caco‐2 cell line showed that BMB‐101 is not a substrate of P‐gp or breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP). Therefore, the potential risk of drug–drug interactions involving these transporters is expected to be minimal. Involvement of different CYPs and UGTs in BMB‐101 metabolism suggests that BMB‐101 clearance is unlikely to be affected to a major extent by CYP or UGT inhibitors. In in vitro studies using concentrations of the compound several‐fold higher than projected human therapeutic exposure, BMB‐101 showed no potential to act as competitive inhibitor or inducer of specific CYP enzymes. The EC50 of BMB‐101 at the 5‐HT2C receptor is 16.2 nM, whereas inhibition at any CYP enzyme occurs at BMB‐101 concentrations >17 μM, which is well above plasma exposures observed in the Phase 1 studies.

Efficacy data

A Phase 2 open‐label, multicenter study (NCT06401538) evaluated the safety, tolerability, and efficacy of BMB‐101 in 24 adults (mean age, 30 years) with drug‐resistant absence seizures and DEEs (Table 1). The study included a 4‐week baseline, a 4‐week titration, and a maintenance period of 2 weeks for the absence seizure cohort (n = 15) and 4 weeks for the DEE cohort (n = 9). Maintenance dosage for either absence or DEE was based on the dose attained during the 4‐week titration period. Dosing began at .67 mg/kg/day (administered on a b.i.d. schedule) and ascended by .33 mg/kg/day weekly to a maximum dose of 2 mg/kg/day. Subjects were encouraged to attain the highest dose but could stay at lower doses for either adequate attained efficacy or tolerability.

Adverse effects

A total of 64 healthy adult subjects were included in the Phase 1 single‐ and multiple‐ascending dose studies, and the food effect study. These studies assessed single fixed doses ranging from 20 mg to 180 mg, and multiple doses ranging from 40 mg to 150 mg, b.i.d., for 7 days. Most TEAEs only appeared at the highest doses (180 mg in the single ascending dose study, and 120–150 mg, b.i.d., in the multiple ascending dose study), and consisted mostly of oral paresthesia, headache, nausea, balance disorder, and were mild to moderate, transient, and self‐limited in nature. No serious TEAEs were observed.

In the Phase 2 study, BMB‐101 was safe and well‐tolerated in patients in both the DEE and the absence seizure cohorts. Three patients discontinued in the DEE group due to fluctuation in pre‐existing behavior condition (n = 1, possibly treatment‐related), shoulder fracture (n = 1, not treatment‐related), and lethargy (n = 1, possibly treatment‐related). In the absence seizure cohort, three patients discontinued due to formulation taste intolerance (n = 1, treatment‐related, formulation updated since then), flu‐like symptoms, muscle ache and fatigue (n = 1, possibly treatment‐related), and dizziness (n = 1, possibly treatment‐related). There were no drug‐related serious TEAEs; the three non‐related serious TEAEs included a shoulder fracture, drowsiness (secondary to fentanyl delivered for shoulder fracture pain), and a case of pneumonia. The most common TEAEs were respiratory infections (n = 5, not treatment‐related), fatigue (n = 4; possibly treatment‐related), constipation (n = 4; 3 possibly treatment‐related), headache (n = 3; 1 possibly treatment‐related), and drowsiness (n = 3; 2 possibly treatment‐related). There were no significant changes in vital signs, safety laboratory tests, or electrocardiograms.

Planned studies

Bright Minds Biosciences has initiated preparations for global registration trials in patients with absence seizures and DEEs. BMB‐101 is also being evaluated in a Phase 2 trial for the treatment of patients with Prader–Willi syndrome (NCT07266324). 33

ELSUNERSEN

Brian Spar1, Kelley Del Real1, Silvana Frizzo1, John Peak1, Robert Horton1, Dharit Patel1, Henry Jacotin1, William Motel1, Archis Patel1, David Small1, Cliff Mason1, Brianna Murray1, Guillermo Fernandez1, Matias Wagner2, Ingo Borggraefe2, James Wheless3, Christopher Troedson4, Katherine Howell5, William Burns6, Marcio Souza1, Steven Petrou1, Walid Fazeli7

1Praxis Precision Medicines, Boston, MA, USA; 2Ludwig Maximilians University Hospital, Munich, Germany; 3Neuroscience Institute, Le Bonheur Children's Hospital, Memphis, TN, USA; 4The Children's Hospital at Westmead, Sydney, Australia; 5Department of Neurology, Royal Children's Hospital, Melbourne, Australia; 6Nationwide Children's Hospital, Columbus Ohio, USA; 7Department of Pediatric Neurology, Children's Hospital, University Hospital Bonn, Bonn, Germany

Introduction and rationale for development

Early‐onset SCN2A‐DEE is a rare, severe pediatric disorder caused by gain‐of‐function variants in SCN2A. It is characterized by neonatal or early‐infantile onset of frequent, often refractory seizures, profound developmental impairment, and high morbidity and mortality. 34 , 35 , 36 Current management relies on combinations of ASMs; however, seizure control remains inadequate for many patients and is frequently complicated by cumulative adverse effects. Even with intensive polypharmacy, seizures often persist, underscoring the limitations of symptomatic treatment approaches. 37 , 38

Approaches that reduce SCN2A expression represent a mechanistically distinct alternative to use of symptomatic ASMs. 39 , 40 Structural and functional biophysical studies have established NaV1.2 as a disease‐relevant therapeutic target, linking molecular channel architecture to neuronal hyperexcitability observed in pathogenic gain‐of‐function SCN2A variants. 41 , 42 Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), which selectively reduce expression of disease‐causing genes, have demonstrated translational feasibility and clinical benefit across multiple monogenic neurologic disorders, providing a rationale for evaluation in SCN2A‐DEE. 43

Elsunersen (PRAX‐222) is an intrathecally administered SCN2A‐targeted ASO designed to address the underlying genetic cause of early‐onset SCN2A‐DEE. Early clinical experience from Phase 1/2 studies and global expanded access programs suggests that gene‐directed reduction of SCN2A expression may translate into clinically meaningful seizure reduction with favorable tolerability in this population. 44 , 45 , 46

Pharmacology

In preclinical mouse models of SCN2A‐DEE, reduction of Scn2a expression using ASOs reduced spontaneous seizures and rescued the early mortality seen in untreated animals, demonstrating proof of concept for gene‐directed modulation of disease biology. 43 RNA knockdown using intrathecally administered ASO is associated with sustained target engagement characteristic of ASO pharmacology. 47 Together, these pharmacological features are consistent with disease‐modifying potential beyond symptomatic seizure suppression and support the clinical evaluation of elsunersen for early‐onset SCN2A‐DEE.

Toxicology

Nonclinical safety was evaluated in good laboratory practice (GLP)‐compliant repeat‐dose toxicity studies in juvenile mice and juvenile nonhuman primates, including chronic intrathecal administration. Across studies, elsunersen was generally well tolerated, with no evidence of systemic or developmental toxicity and no findings that precluded clinical dosing. Genetic toxicology assessments were negative. In chronic juvenile nonhuman primate studies, repeated monthly intrathecal administration was associated with minimal CNS histopathological findings, consistent with known, non‐limiting class effects of ASOs administered to the CNS and not associated with functional deficits.

The nonclinical safety profile supported selection of the initial clinical dosing regimen and provided an adequate safety margin for evaluation of elsunersen in pediatric patients with early‐onset SCN2A‐DEE.

Pharmacokinetic profile

The EMBRAVE trial (NCT05737784) was designed to assess the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and efficacy of ascending doses of elsunersen in male and female participants 2–18 years of age with a diagnosis of early‐onset SCN2A‐DEE. 44 The trial was designed to be conducted in four parts: preliminary safety Part 1 (open label, n = 4), dose‐escalation Part A (double‐blind), confirmatory Part B (double‐blind), followed by an OLE in Part C. Preliminary pharmacokinetic data are available from Part 1 of EMBRAVE and from patients included in the global EAP.

In Part 1, four participants (age range 2–14 years) received 1‐mg intrathecal doses monthly for up to 13 weeks. Systemic exposure was generally low, with plasma elsunersen concentrations detectable shortly after dosing through at least 24 h, but not measurable at monthly pre‐dose time points. During repeat‐dose treatment, elsunersen was measurable in the CSF prior to each dose, with concentrations in the low ng/mL range and no apparent accumulation during once‐monthly administration.

Additional supportive pharmacokinetic data were obtained from five EAP patients receiving long‐term monthly dosing at higher dose levels (dose range .5–8 mg), two of whom received elsunersen as an extension of Part 1 of the EMBRAVE trial. 45 In these patients, pre‐dose CSF concentrations measured 1 month after dosing were within the range previously observed in EMBRAVE Part 1, with no evidence of accumulation.

Efficacy data

In Part 1 of the EMBRAVE study, repeated intrathecal administration of elsunersen (1 mg at ≥4‐week intervals for up to 13 weeks) in pediatric participants with early‐onset SCN2A‐DEE (n = 4) was associated with a 43% median reduction in seizure frequency from baseline observation through four 28‐day periods. 44 Participants also demonstrated a 48% median increase in seizure‐free days relative to baseline, with treated participants being 1.6 times more likely to experience a seizure‐free day. The improvement in seizure frequency was observed on top of concomitantly administered ASMs and was maintained over successive dosing intervals in all patients, including the two patients who continued to receive treatment in the EAP.

EMBRAVE Part A enrolled nine patients randomized 3:1 to elsunersen or sham treatment for 24 weeks, followed by a blinded transition to elsunersen for up to 2 years in an ongoing OLE (Part C). EMBRAVE Part A is on track for topline results in the first half of 2026.

Further supportive evidence has emerged from the three additional EAP patients with severe, refractory SCN2A‐DEE, who showed substantial (up to 90% in one case) and sustained seizure reductions following elsunersen treatment, including marked decreases in seizure burden in a patient from Germany with prior status epilepticus or continuous seizures, alongside meaningful quality‐of‐life improvements. 45 , 46 In the patient from Germany, elsunersen administration was temporally associated with cessation of refractory status epilepticus and substantial reduction and stabilization of seizure frequency, permitting subsequent tapering of phenytoin without recurrence of status epilepticus or worsening of seizure frequency. 46 Collectively, early clinical experience suggests sustained seizure control and possible neurodevelopmental stabilization, with ongoing follow‐up poised to strengthen these findings.

Adverse effects

Across both the EMBRAVE study and the global EAP experience, elsunersen has shown a favorable safety and tolerability profile. In EMBRAVE Part 1, no TEAEs or serious TEAEs considered related to study drug were reported, and all observed TEAEs (mostly infections, which are common in this patient population) were resolved. 44 Independent monitoring supported continued dosing without protocol modification. Similarly, safety data from ~100 doses across the global EAP indicate that repeated intrathecal administration of elsunersen has been consistently well tolerated in pediatric patients, including infants with severe disease, with no TEAEs or serious TEAEs related to study drug reported to date. 45 These findings support continued clinical evaluation of elsunersen in early‐onset SCN2A‐DEE.

Planned studies

The EMBRAVE3 registrational study (NCT07019922) is currently recruiting and enrollment is accelerating following agreement with the FDA to change the trial design to a single‐arm, baseline‐controlled study, with all patients receiving elsunersen for 24 weeks, followed by an OLE. Topline results are expected in the second half of 2026. Clinical data from the EMBRAVE3 study, if successful, would serve as the basis for a New Drug Application (NDA) submission in 2027.

EPX‐100 (CLEMIZOLE HYDROCHLORIDE)

Amit Ray1, Robert Faulkner1, Scott C. Baraban2, Juby Philip1, Krystle Davis Rapchak1, Markiyan Doliba1, David S. Albers1, Kumar Budur1

1Harmony Biosciences, Plymouth Meeting, PA, USA; 2Department of Neurological Surgery & Weill Neuroscience Institute, University of California San Francisco (UCSF), San Francisco, CA, USA

Introduction and rationale for development

Clemizole hydrochloride (hereafter referred to as clemizole) is a first‐generation histamine H1 receptor antagonist that was first approved in the United States as an antihistamine in 1959, and marketed under the names of Allercur and Reactrol. The introduction of second‐generation antihistamines changed the landscape for drugs in this class by selectively targeting peripheral histamine receptors and offering a longer time course of pharmacological action per dose. For this reason, in around 1978, Allercur was voluntarily withdrawn from the market despite not having any significant safety concerns.

A renewed interest in clemizole emerged following reports characterizing its seizure suppressing properties in a scn1Lab zebrafish mutant model of DS. 48 EPX‐100 is a proprietary clemizole formulation now being developed as a potential treatment for rare DEEs, including DS and LGS. In the text below, we use the term clemizole when referring to the active moiety, and EPX‐100 when referring to the proprietary product administered in the listed studies.

Pharmacology

Activity profile in animal models of seizures and epilepsy

Phenotypic drug screening offers the potential to discover and develop new treatments for rare epilepsy disorders in a rigorous unbiased manner. This approach has demonstrated greater effectiveness in the identification of first‐in‐class drugs when compared to target‐based screening methodologies. 49 The zebrafish is particularly well‐suited for high‐throughput phenotype‐based drug screening applications. Zebrafish mutants harboring a loss‐of‐function missense mutation in the SCN1A orthologue, scn1Lab, are haploinsufficient for Nav1.1, thereby replicating the genetic defect underlying DS. This DS model recapitulates clinically relevant features, particularly spontaneous convulsive behaviors, electrographic seizure activity, shortened lifespan, sleep disturbance, metabolic deficit, and pharmaco‐resistance. 48 , 50 , 51 The scn1Lab zebrafish mutant model positively identified drugs used clinically in DS, including fenfluramine, stiripentol, cannabinoid derivatives, topiramate, potassium bromide, and valproate, as well as drugs that are ineffective or may worsen seizures associated with DS, like carbamazepine and phenytoin. 48 , 51 Using the scn1Lab zebrafish model and high‐throughput two‐stage phenotypic screening, clemizole was shown to significantly suppress brain electrographic seizure discharges as well as spontaneous convulsive behavior, manifested as decreased swim activity and velocity. 48 , 50 , 51 Clemizole exhibited anti‐seizure activity at bath concentrations between 100 and 400 μM. 51 Clemizole also significantly reduced seizure behavior elicited by pentylenetetrazol (PTZ) in wild‐type zebrafish. 48 Moreover, clemizole effectively controlled abnormal manifestations of scn1Lab mutant zebrafish that mimic comorbidities seen in DS patients, for example, increased locomotor activity during dark exposure (corresponding to sleep disturbances) and impaired exploratory behavior in a novel arena (corresponding to anxiety and movement disorders). 52 Clemizole was also submitted to the National Institutes of Health (NIH)–sponsored Epilepsy Therapy Screening Program for independent testing. In the MES test in mice, clemizole exhibited anti‐seizure activity at doses of 400 and 500 mg/kg, i.p.

Mechanism of action

To identify a potential mechanism for clemizole's anti‐seizure effect, a radioligand binding assay was initially performed on 132 targets, including various receptors, ion channels, transporters, enzymes, and second messengers. As expected, clemizole has a very high antagonist binding affinity for the H1 receptor. 50 , 51 The next highest affinity, as an agonist, was for two postsynaptic 5‐HT receptor subunits, 5‐HT2A and 5‐HT2B. Although the precise mechanism by which EPX‐100 exerts anti‐seizure activity remains an active area of investigation, currently available data strongly suggest a serotonergic mechanism of action. Studies are ongoing to establish the precise receptor binding as well as functional activity of EPX‐100 as well as its major metabolites (also see Section 5.4).

Toxicology

A full package of nonclinical safety pharmacology (CNS, cardiovascular, and respiratory) and toxicity studies (up to 6 months duration in rats and 9 months in dogs), male and female reproductive studies, and embryo–fetal development studies have been conducted for EPX‐100 via the oral route and in compliance with International Conference for Harmonization (ICH) M3(R2) guidelines. In addition, a definitive juvenile toxicity study in rats as well as in vitro and in vivo genotoxicity studies have also been completed. The EPX‐100 exposure data at the no‐observed‐adverse‐effect levels (NOAEL) from the chronic toxicity and juvenile toxicology studies show satisfactory safety margins relative to target clinical dose.

Pharmacokinetic and metabolic profile

The safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetics of EPX‐100 were evaluated in a double‐blind single and multiple ascending dose Phase 1 study. The study enrolled 24 healthy adults (18 in the EPX‐100 group and six in a placebo group) who received a single oral dose of 20, 40, or 80 mg EPX‐100, or placebo, in three groups of eight subjects each (six received EPX‐100 and two received placebo) in the fasting state. Following a 1‐week washout period, each subject received b.i.d. doses of the assigned dose levels (total daily doses of 40, 80, or 160 mg EPX‐100 or placebo) with a single dose on the morning of Day 5. After another washout period of at least 1 week, subjects received their assigned single dose of EPX‐100 following a high‐fat breakfast.

Clemizole was rapidly absorbed with a median t max (time to reach C max) of 1 h after a single oral dose. C max values were dose‐proportional. Following b.i.d. dosing for 4 days followed by a single dose on the morning of Day 5, plasma concentrations of clemizole were ~2.8‐ to 3.9‐fold higher than those observed after the single dose. Dose‐proportional increases were observed for clemizole C max and AUC0‐12 for the 20, 40, and 80 mg, b.i.d., doses, respectively. The accumulation ratio in C max and AUC0‐12 values (multiple dose vs single dose on Day 1) was ~3‐fold. Comparisons of C max and AUC0‐∞ values following a high‐fat meal showed no clinically meaningful change in plasma exposure compared with the fasting state.

Clemizole is bound extensively (98%) to human plasma proteins and is metabolized to two primary metabolites, a hydroxy metabolite (M6) and a dihydroxy metabolite (M6'). Based on results of in vitro studies using pooled human liver microsomes and recombinant human CYP enzymes, CYP3A4 was identified as the primary CYP isozyme responsible for clemizole metabolism. After administration of single oral doses of EPX‐100 in healthy participants, both M6 and the M6' appear rapidly in plasma, with a t max of ~1 h. Clemizole has a mean half‐life of 11.3 h, which supports b.i.d. dosing of EPX‐100.

A clinical study using 14C‐labeled clemizole to evaluate the excretion and metabolite profile is planned.

Drug interactions

Potential interactions of clemizole with CYPs, UGTs, and transporters were investigated in vitro. In these studies, that tested concentrations ranging from .05 to 100 μM, clemizole was shown to be an inhibitor of CYP2C19 (IC50 = .16 μM), CYP3A4 (IC50 = 13 μM), and CYP2D6 (IC50 = .51 μM) at concentrations that are many‐fold higher than the C max of unbound drug (~.01 μM) after 80 mg, b.i.d., doses in healthy adults, and an inducer of CYP3A4 and CYP2B6. Clemizole was not a substrate of organic anion transporter (OAT) 1, OAT3, organic cation transporter (OCT) 1, OCT2, organic anion transporting polypeptide (OATP) 1B1, OATP1B3, multidrug and toxic compound extrusion (MATE) transporter MATE1, MATE2‐K, BCRP, and P‐gp, whereas metabolites M6 and M6' are substrates of P‐gp.

An open‐label, three‐part, Phase 1 study was conducted in 18 healthy adult male and female participants to evaluate the potential for drug interactions with clemizole as a CYP3A4 substrate, and as a perpetrator for CYP2B6, CYP2C19, and CYP3A4. Multiple oral doses of EPX‐100, 80 mg, b.i.d., for 10 days, increased the AUC of omeprazole, a CYP2C19 probe substrate, by 3.7‐fold, indicating moderate inhibition, whereas no effect was observed on midazolam, a CYP3A4 probe substrate. Clemizole had a weak inductive effect on bupropion metabolism, a CYP2B6 probe substrate. 53 As common co‐administered ASMs are metabolized via similar pathways, a clinical drug–drug interaction study with clemizole and selected ASMs is planned.

The ARGUS study (NCT04462770) is an ongoing global randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, Phase 3 study to evaluate the safety and efficacy of EPX‐100 as adjunctive therapy in patients with DS. Patients who complete the double‐blind phase are eligible to enter an OLE. Treatment is administered as an oral solution (5 mg/mL) of EPX‐100 or placebo starting at 1 mg/kg, b.i.d., and escalating in 1 mg/kg, b.i.d., weekly increments to 4 mg/kg, b.i.d., with 80 mg, b.i.d., being the maximum dose. Patients who elect to enter the OLE undergo a double dummy titration during transition to OLE to maintain the blind.

Adverse effects

In the Phase 1 study in healthy subjects, a total of 27 TEAEs were reported in 16 subjects, which were mild or moderate in severity; no serious TEAEs were reported. The most commonly reported TEAEs in subjects receiving EPX‐100 were somnolence/drowsiness/grogginess in nine subjects and headache in two subjects. 55 One participant who received EPX‐100 had a borderline, asymptomatic QTcF (heart‐rate corrected QT interval, based on Fridericia formula) change from baseline of 30.67 ms with normal QTcF value of 433.66 ms at 6 h following a single 80 mg dose, which spontaneously resolved upon repeat electrocardiography (ECG) within an hour. Twelve serious TEAEs were reported in seven participants; only one (exacerbation of generalized tonic–clonic seizure) was determined by the investigator to be possibly related to study treatment and led to discontinuation of treatment. Ongoing and planned studies

In addition to the ARGUS study, the LIGHTHOUSE study is another ongoing global randomized, double‐blind placebo‐controlled Phase 3 study to evaluate the safety and efficacy of EPX‐100 as adjunctive therapy in patients with LGS (NCT05066217). The study includes a 4‐week observation phase followed by a 16‐week double‐blind period and an optional OLE period for up to 3 years (Figure 2). A total of 260 participants ages >2 to <55 years with a clinical and EEG diagnosis consistent with LGS and seizures not adequately controlled by current ASMs are eligible to participate. The primary outcome measure is the percentage change in CMS‐28 between the 4‐week observation phase (baseline period) and the end of the double‐blind phase. Additional clinical pharmacology studies are being conducted to support the clinical development program for a potential NDA filing.

ES‐481

Terence J. O'Brien1,2, Emma C. Foster1,2, Robert Niecestro3

1Department of Neuroscience, School of Translational Medicine, Monash University, Melbourne VIC 3004, Australia; 2Department of Neurology, Alfred Health, Commercial Road, Melbourne VIC 3000, Australia; 3ES‐Therapeutics, New York, NY USA

Introduction and rationale for development

ES‐481 ((S)‐6‐(1‐(1‐(5‐(2‐hydroxyethoxy)pyridin‐2‐yl)‐1H‐pyrazol‐3‐yl)ethyl)benzo[d]thiazol‐2(3H)‐one) is a novel ASM that acts as a potent and selective antagonist of α‐amino‐3‐hydroxy‐5‐methyl‐4‐isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptors associated with the transmembrane AMPA receptor regulatory protein γ8 (TARP‐γ8). TARP‐γ8–dependent AMPA receptors are highly expressed in the hippocampus and limbic system, enabling regionally selective modulation of excitatory neurotransmission, including the cerebellum. 56 This targeted mechanism is designed to preserve anti‐seizure efficacy while minimizing off‐target inhibition of AMPA receptors in other CNS regions, particularly the cerebellum. In contrast, non‐selective AMPA receptor antagonists such as perampanel are clinically effective but frequently associated with dose‐limiting adverse effects including dizziness, ataxia, and neuropsychiatric symptoms. 57 , 58 ES‐481 was developed to address this unmet need by selectively targeting hippocampal AMPA signaling.

Pharmacology

Activity profile in animal models of seizures and epilepsy

ES‐481 demonstrated robust anti‐seizure activity across a range of chemically, electrically, and genetically induced rodent seizure models, including amygdala and corneal kindling and PTZ‐induced seizures, without evidence of motor impairment at doses up to 100 mg/kg administered orally or intraperitoneally. 59 Translational modeling across these studies suggested a projected effective human dose range of ~75–150 mg/day.

In the rat PTZ seizure model, ES‐481 significantly reduced seizure activity without altering PTZ pharmacokinetics, that is, mean plasma PTZ concentrations were comparable with and without ES‐481 co‐administration. Neither the (R)‐enantiomer of ES‐481 (LSN 313048) nor the primary acid metabolite (LSN3179935) demonstrated anti‐seizure activity, confirming stereochemical specificity of the (S)‐enantiomer, that is, ES‐481. 59

Given the high hippocampal expression of TARP‐γ8, ES‐481 was evaluated in a mouse model of mesial temporal lobe epilepsy induced by intrahippocampal kainic acid infusion. ES‐481 (6, 11, and 23 mg/kg, i.p., n = 30/group) profoundly suppressed spontaneous hippocampal paroxysmal discharges (HPDs) within 5–25 min of administration. Complete suppression was observed at the two lower doses, with only a single HPD recorded at the highest dose. These findings, together with efficacy in a lamotrigine‐resistant amygdala‐kindled rat model, suggest activity against pharmacoresistant focal seizures. 59

ES‐481 also demonstrated efficacy in the GAERS model. At doses of 10 and 30 mg/kg, i.p., ES‐481 completely abolished spike–wave discharges between 30 and 130 min post‐dose, supporting a broad anti‐seizure spectrum that includes genetic generalized epilepsy with absence seizures. 59

Mechanism of action

ES‐481 is a highly potent antagonist of AMPA receptors containing the TARP‐γ8 subunit. In vitro, ES‐481 suppressed glutamate‐evoked responses in acutely dissociated hippocampal neurons (EC₅₀ = 66.2 nM) and cortical neurons, while sparing cerebellar neurons. This selectivity was confirmed using neurons from TARP‐γ8 knockout mice, in which ES‐481 showed no inhibitory activity. 59

Consistent results were observed in hippocampal slice electrophysiology, human epileptic hippocampal tissue, and in vivo iontophoretic studies in rats. ES‐481 inhibited AMPA‐induced excitation in hippocampal neurons expressing TARP‐γ8 but had no effect on neurons lacking this subunit, such as those in the red nucleus. 59

Collectively, these findings demonstrate regionally selective AMPA receptor antagonism, with preservation of cerebellar AMPA‐TARP‐γ2 signaling. This pharmacological profile is expected to reduce cerebellar adverse effects such as ataxia and vertigo, which are commonly associated with less selective AMPA antagonists.

Toxicology

Preclinical toxicity studies identified the main dose‐related toxicology effects of ES‐481 are on the CNS and cardiovascular functions. Nonclinical toxicology studies were conducted in rats, beagle dogs, and cynomolgus monkeys. CNS effects (i.e., sedation, ataxia, convulsions) were observed in dogs and monkeys, with mortality occurring in rats at the highest doses tested. The NOAEL was 100 mg/kg in rats, 3 mg/kg in male dogs, 5 mg/kg in female dogs, and 75 mg/kg in monkeys. Cardiovascular effects included increases in heart rate and blood pressure, without QT prolongation. 59

Dogs exhibited greater sensitivity to CNS effects (i.e., seizures, sedation, and ataxia) than monkeys. To mitigate potential human risk, the starting dose for the first‐in‐human single ascending dose study (3 mg, predicted C max 43.8 ng/mL) was conservatively based on plasma concentrations associated with convulsions in dogs. Dose escalation was conducted cautiously to remain below the NOAEL AUC in rats and NOAEL C max in monkeys.

Dedicated video‐EEG studies further characterized seizure liability. No clinical or electrographic seizures were observed in monkeys at any dose; clinical signs of ataxia emerged at 50 mg/kg/day, with a NOAEL of 15 mg/kg/day. In dogs, clinical signs occurred at 2 mg/kg/day, establishing a NOAEL of 1 mg/kg/day.

Pharmacokinetic and metabolic profile

A first‐in‐human, Phase 1 single‐center, open‐label, randomized, single ascending dose study was undertaken to evaluate the pharmacokinetics, safety, and tolerability of ES‐481 in healthy subjects. 60 The trial (Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry Identifier ACTRN 1269001591145p) was conducted in Melbourne, Australia (Nucleus Network), and approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) of the Alfred Hospital in Melbourne. Following single oral doses in healthy subjects, ES‐481 was rapidly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, with a t max of 1–4 h after dosing across the dose range examined (3 to 100 mg). An approximately dose‐proportional increase in ES‐481 C max and AUC was observed after doses of 3, 6, 12, 25, 37, 50, and 62 mg. Following single doses of 75, 87, and 100 mg, plasma ES‐481 concentrations increased less than dose‐proportionally. ES‐481 binds extensively to plasma proteins (97%–99%) and its half‐life in healthy subjects is ~8 h.

In the Phase 2a adjunctive‐therapy trial in patients with drug‐resistant epilepsy, pharmacokinetics was assessed using compartmental methods to determine t max, AUCo‐last, AUC0‐∞, half‐life, oral clearance (CL/F), and volume of distribution (V/F) on Days 1, 8, 15, 22, 28, 43, 50, 57, 64, and 70. Plasma ES‐481 concentrations increased rapidly, with a t max of ~2 h post‐dose. C max was 363 ng/mL after 25 mg once daily (q.d.) on Day 1/43, 543 ng/mL for 50 mg, b.i.d. (Day 15/57) and 1190 ng/mL for 75 mg, b.i.d., on Day 28/70. Median half‐life was 3.73 (range 3.59–3.87 h). Mean (SD) CL/F and V/F were about 6.4 (1.5) L/h and 34.1 (6.3) L, respectively, after the 25 mg dose on Day 1 (n = 21). An approximately dose‐proportional increase in C max was observed for ES‐481 doses of 25, 50, and 75 mg, b.i.d., whereas AUC tended to increase more than proportionally. Specifically, mean C max increased from 476 ng/mL at 25 mg, b.i.d. to 1317 ng/mL at 75 mg, b.i.d, whereas mean AUC0‐last increased from 720 ng h/mL at the 25 mg, b.i.d., to 3384 ng h/mL at 75 mg, b.i.d.

Drug interactions

Drug interactions with ES‐481 have not yet been evaluated and there are no formal drug interaction studies in humans planned at this time. However, in vitro studies suggest that human hepatic clearance is predominantly via non‐CYP metabolism mechanism.

Efficacy data

Proof of concept data for anti‐seizure efficacy in humans has been evaluated in a Phase 2a double‐blind, randomized, dose‐titration, crossover, placebo‐controlled trial followed by an OLE conducted across four Australian Comprehensive Epilepsy Centers. The study (NCT04714996; ACTRN12621000033842) was open to adults aged 18–70 years with drug‐resistant epilepsy (focal, generalized, or mixed focal/generalized) 61 who provided written informed consent to participate. Participants were taking a stable dose of 1–4 ASMs for at least 4 weeks prior to entering the 28‐day screening period. The primary outcomes were: (i) efficacy, measured via change in seizure frequency for each treatment week, (ii) safety and tolerability, and (iii) pharmacokinetics. Secondary endpoints were change in anxiety and depressive symptom scores. Following the baseline 28‐day observation period, eligible participants were randomized 1:1 to study drug‐ or placebo‐first groups. The first treatment period ran 28 days, with participants in the treatment group receiving four escalating doses of ES‐481 over a 4‐week period (week 1: 25 mg daily; week 2: 25 mg, b.i.d.; week 3: 50 mg, b.i.d.; week 4: 75 mg, b.i.d.), with participants in the placebo group receiving matching placebo doses. Participants in all groups had a study visit, 24‐h continuous EEG and pharmacokinetic testing prior to the start of each dose increment. This treatment period was followed by a 7‐day step‐down period and a further 7‐day washout period, and the groups then crossed‐over. The second treatment period ran for a further 28 days, following the same pattern as for the first treatment period, followed again by a 14‐day step‐down and washout period. Participants who completed the double‐blind treatment phase had the opportunity to participate in the OLE phase for up to 36 weeks treatment, titrated to a dose that was deemed most suitable by the treating neurologist (maximum dose 75 mg, b.i.d.). Seizure frequency during the double‐blind phase was assessed through seizure diaries at Days 1, 8, 15, 22, 28, 43, 50, 57, 64, and 70 time points. At these same time points, the change in the frequency of epileptiform/seizure discharges on a normalized 4–24 h EEG (normalized to a 4‐h period) was assessed as an additional primary efficacy outcome, as well as representing part of the safety assessment. The study was approved by Alfred Health's Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC number 688.20). First participant/first visit (assessment) occurred on January 29, 2021.

Twenty‐two participants (mean age 34.5 years, range 17–70 years, 13 male/9 female, 15 with focal epilepsy and seven with generalized epilepsy) were randomized. Seventeen (77.3%) completed the double‐blind phase, and 16 entered the OLE. Based on seizure diaries, ES‐481 treatment was associated with a 68%–80% reduction in seizure frequency compared with a 38%–49% reduction during placebo treatment (p = .097). At the highest dose (75 mg, b.i.d., n = 18), participants experienced an 80% seizure reduction (90% confidence interval [CI] 43%–97%) compared with 49% during placebo (90% CI 1%–74%; p = .047). No significant improvement in seizure control was observed at lower doses. There were no significant differences between ES‐481 and placebo in EEG epileptiform or seizure discharges >3 s. During the OLE phase, treatment with ES‐481 was associated with a reduced weekly seizure frequency relative to baseline by at least 70%, apart from Week 2 (60% reduction) and Week 5 (50% reduction).

Adverse effects

In the Phase 1 single‐center, open‐label, randomized, single ascending dose study of ES‐481 to evaluate the pharmacokinetics, safety, and tolerability of ES‐481 in healthy volunteers at doses up to 100 mg, there were no safety findings of concern, with no participants having a serious adverse reaction or discontinuing the trial. 60 The only TEAEs reported by more than one subject within a treatment group were headaches, fatigue, and anxiety reported by two subjects (33.3%) in the 87 mg ES‐481 group and tachycardia reported by two subjects (33.3%) in the 75 mg ES‐481 group. All other related TEAEs were reported as single episodes. Twenty‐four hour EEG recordings pre‐ and post‐dosing revealed no evidence of activation of de novo epileptiform or seizure discharges at doses up to 100 mg.

In the Phase 2a adjunctive‐therapy trial in patients with drug‐resistant epilepsy, safety assessments were performed at Days 1, 8, 15, 22, 28, 43, 50, 57, 64, and 70, and included screening for CNS and cardiovascular TEAEs, vital signs, hematology and biochemistry laboratory tests, neurological and physical examinations, ECG, and EEG. TEAEs of special interest were recorded, including anxiety, ataxia, agitation, behavioral change, dizziness, drowsiness, mood swings, insomnia, somnolence, paresthesia, feeling abnormal, disturbance in attention, gait disturbance, and dysarthria.

TEAEs of special interest were more common while taking ES‐481 than placebo, but the difference was not statistically significant (52.4% vs 19.0%, p = .052). TEAEs of special interest for which the occurrence rates on ES‐481 treatment were at least 5% higher than that on placebo treatment included dizziness (3 [14.3%] vs 1 [4.8%]), insomnia (3 [14.3%] vs 0), dysarthria (2 [9.5%] vs 0), feeling abnormal (2 [9.5%] vs 0), and gait disturbance (2 [9.5%] vs 0). Conversely, the TEAEs for which the occurrence rates on placebo were at least 5% higher than that on ES‐481 treatment included tinnitus (2 [9.5%] placebo vs 0 ES‐481), upper abdominal pain (2 [9.5%] placebo vs 0 ES‐481), nausea (2 [9.5%] placebo vs 0 ES‐481), and lethargy (2 [9.5%] placebo vs 0 ES‐481). Serious TEAEs were infrequent and comparable between groups (placebo 14.3% vs ES‐481 4.8%). Four participants (19%) experienced TEAEs leading to drug discontinuation, three while receiving placebo and one while receiving ES‐481. No participants died during the study. No significant changes in clinical laboratory or vital signs were noted.

TEAE profiles in the double‐blind treatment phase and OLE phase were similar. No new safety signals were noted during the OLE phase.

Planned studies

In the early clinical development program, ES‐481 demonstrated favorable safety, tolerability, and preliminary efficacy in adults with drug‐resistant epilepsy at doses up to 75 mg, b.i.d. These findings support continued clinical development. A Phase 2b randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled add‐on study in patients with drug‐resistant focal epilepsy is planned.

ETX101

Joseph Sullivan, Joshua J. Bear, Natalie Berger, Mustafa Hameed, Norman Huang, Maria Candida Vila, Emma S. James, Stephanie Tagliatela, Salvador Rico

Encoded Therapeutics, South San Francisco, California, USA

Introduction and rationale for development

ETX101 is an investigational gene therapy in development for the treatment of SCN1A‐positive DS (SCN1A+ DS). In more than 90% of cases, DS is caused by haploinsufficiency of the SCN1A gene, encoding the alpha‐1 subunit (NaV1.1) of the sodium channel. Children with DS typically exhibit normal early development followed by clinical worsening between 12 months and 5 years of age; this period is marked by a critical slowing in skill acquisition and cognitive development and a progressive increase in seizure frequency. 62 , 63 , 64 By ~4 years of age, children with DS typically attain a developmental quotient of a neurotypical 2.2‐year‐old, in contrast to the continued gains seen in neurotypical peers. 63 No approved disease‐modifying therapies exist for DS, and current treatment remains focused on seizure management rather than neurodevelopmental outcomes. ETX101 is delivered via a single intracerebroventricular (i.c.v.) administration, and may provide the first one‐time, disease‐modifying therapy for DS.

Pharmacology

Mechanism of action

ETX101 is a potential one‐time, disease‐modifying adeno‐associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9)–based gene regulation therapy for SCN1A+ DS. ETX101 is designed to target the underlying cause of DS by increasing SCN1A expression in GABAergic inhibitory neurons, thereby harnessing the gene's endogenous regulatory biology. 65 By addressing the mechanism of the disease, ETX101 aims to treat the full spectrum of DS symptoms. ETX101 has received Regenerative Medicine Advanced Therapy, Fast Track, Rare Pediatric Disease, Orphan Drug, Breakthrough Designations from the FDA, as well as Orphan Designation from the European Medicines Agency (EMA).

Activity profile in animal models of seizures and epilepsy

As reported previously, in neonatal mouse models of Scn1a deficiency, a single i.c.v. administration of ETX101 led to increased SCN1A messenger RNA (mRNA) transcripts, specifically within GABAergic inhibitory interneurons, and NaV1.1 protein levels in the brain. 65 This was associated with a significant decrease in the occurrence of spontaneous and hyperthermia‐induced seizures, and prolonged survival for over 1 year.

Toxicology

As reported in the EILAT XVII progress report, 1 a one‐time unilateral i.c.v. administration of ETX101 was well tolerated in non‐human primates with no adverse events during administration, no detectable changes in clinical observations, no adverse findings in histopathology, and no dorsal root ganglion‐related toxicity. 65

Pharmacokinetic profile

As reported in the EILAT XVII progress report, 1 administration of ETX101 by unilateral i.c.v. injection in non‐human primates led to widespread vector biodistribution and transgene expression throughout the brain, including key structures involved in epilepsy and cognitive behaviors, such as the hippocampus and the cortex. 65

Drug interactions

No formal drug interaction studies have been performed to date.

Efficacy data

As of November 2025, a total of 19 participants with DS 6 months to <7 years of age have received treatment with ETX101 in the first three clinical trials of the POLARIS program. Each trial involves a single i.c.v. injection into the lateral ventricle via stereotactic neuronavigation, with staggered dosing. Four ascending dose levels (DLs) are under evaluation (vector genomes [vg]/mL CSF): 5.0 × 1011 (DL1; n = 3), 1.9 × 1012 (DL2; n = 4), 3.3 × 1012 (DL3; n = 3), and 6.6 × 1012 (DL4; n = 9). Preliminary seizure outcome data, available for 10 participants who received the first three DLs, indicate dose‐dependent seizure reductions, with a 78% median reduction in frequency of countable motor seizures through Week 32 at DL3 (n = 3). Neurodevelopmentally, treated children demonstrated an increased rate of skill acquisition compared with natural history observations. Scores on the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales, Third Edition (Vineland‐3) scale at Week 18 (n = 10) and Week 52 (n = 4) demonstrated meaningful improvements across all sub‐domains—this was particularly marked in communication, with a 5‐fold improvement over natural history observations. Bayley‐4 assessments indicated that ETX101‐treated participants ≤24 months of age avoided early cognitive stagnation and approached neurotypical development as early as Week 16 (n = 4, DL1‐4). Preliminary data at Week 52 (n = 1, DL1) suggest sustained neurotypical trajectories.

Adverse effects

As of November 2025, ETX101 has been well tolerated with no treatment‐related serious TEAEs or dose‐limiting toxicities across DL1–4 (n = 19). The most common TEAE was transaminase elevation, a known AAV class effect, observed in four participants; all cases were clinically asymptomatic, resolving without treatment in three participants and following steroid therapy in one participant. No changes were observed on brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Planned studies

The POLARIS clinical development program for ETX101 in people with DS includes three ongoing Phase 1/2 open‐label, dose‐escalation trials (WAYFINDER [Australia, NCT06112275], ENDEAVOR [U.S., NCT05419492], Part 1A, EXPEDITION [U.K., NCT06283212]) in children with DS 6 months to <7 years of age. Enrollment in these studies has been completed. The ENDEAVOR trial also includes Part 1B, an open‐label study scheduled for initiation in 2026, aimed at evaluating one dose of ETX101 in older participants ≥48 months to <18 years of age.

In addition to the above studies, ENDEAVOR Part 2 (NCT05419492) is a randomized, double‐blind, sham delayed‐treatment control study in participants ≥6 months to <48 months of age. The primary efficacy endpoint is the change from baseline in monthly countable motor seizures. The key secondary endpoint is the change from baseline in the Bayley‐4 scales cognitive domain. The FDA has confirmed alignment on ENDEAVOR Part 2 as the pivotal study to support the planned Biologics License Application (BLA) submission for ETX101.

PrevEp‐006 (INTRANASAL SELETRACETAM)

Wolfgang Löscher1,2, Chris Rundfeldt2, Pavel Klein2,3

1Translational Epilepsy Research Group, NIFE, Department of Experimental Otology of the ENT Clinics, Hannover Medical School, Hannover, Germany; 2PrevEp, Inc., Bethesda, MD, USA; 3Mid‐Atlantic Epilepsy and Sleep Center, Bethesda, MD, USA

Introduction and rationale for development

Seletracetam is a second‐generation racetam derivative of the benchmark ASM levetiracetam, discovered in a drug discovery program conducted by UCB Pharma in the early 2000s. It was developed in parallel with another second‐generation racetam, brivaracetam, up to clinical Phase 2a, after which UCB stopped its further clinical development in favor of brivaracetam despite the favorable efficacy and safety profile of seletracetam after oral administration. The patent for seletracetam expired in 2021. Uniquely, the extremely high potency of seletracetam, unlike that of any other non‐benzodiazepine (non‐BDZ) ASMs, allows it to be formulated at therapeutic doses in a very low liquid volume suitable for intranasal administration and potential use as an acute seizure rescue therapy. PrevEp, Inc. filed a new U.S. patent application in 2024, followed by a worldwide Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT) application in 2025, for intranasal and orobuccal seletracetam formulations and new medical uses as the first potential non‐BDZ rescue treatment of acute repetitive seizures and rapid epileptic seizure termination. 66 Because seletracetam has never been approved for use in humans, it is a new chemical entity (NCE).

Pharmacology

Activity profile in animal models of seizures and epilepsy

The activity profile of seletracetam after systemic (i.p, p.o.) administration in animal models of seizures and epilepsy has been described in the EILAT VIII progress report. 67 As shown in Table 2, seletracetam is ~100 times more potent than levetiracetam and ~10 times more potent than brivaracetam in chronic models of seizures and epilepsy. 68 Furthermore, it has a higher protective index than the two other racetams (Table 2).

Other pharmacological properties

Dysfunction of synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A (SV2A), the main target of levetiracetam, brivaracetam, and seletracetam, has not only been discussed for epilepsy but also for Alzheimer's disease and other disorders associated with cognitive impairment, suggesting potential novel therapies for levetiracetam and its congeners. 68 Indeed, pro‐cognitive effects have been described for levetiracetam both preclinically and clinically. 69 It will be important to determine whether seletracetam shares this effect of levetiracetam.

Mechanism(s) of action

Like levetiracetam and brivaracetam, seletracetam binds to SV2A, which is thought to have a modulatory role on synaptic vesicle functions. 68 The binding affinity of seletracetam to human SV2A (Ki .032 μM) is ~100 times higher than that of levetiracetam. In addition, seletracetam blocks high voltage–activated Ca2+ currents much more potently than levetiracetam or brivaracetam, 70 which may add to its high anti‐seizure potency.

Toxicology

The low acute and chronic toxicity of seletracetam after oral administration has been described in the EILAT VIII progress report. 67

Pharmacokinetic and metabolic profile

The results of pharmacokinetic studies with oral seletracetam in healthy subjects have been described in the EILAT VIII progress report. 67 More recently, the pharmacokinetics of oral seletracetam from a Phase 2a trial in patients with photosensitive epilepsy were reported. 71 After each dose (.5–20 mg), C max was reached within ~2 h and was followed by a mono‐exponential decline. The concentration decreases after C max were parallel for all doses, as evidenced on the semi‐logarithmic plot, implying a similar terminal half‐life of ~8 h across doses. Plasma seletracetam concentrations appeared to increase in a linear proportion with the administered dose, as reported previously for healthy subjects. 72 The linear, time‐independent pharmacokinetics of the drug, combined with a rapid and almost complete absorption, indicate that seletracetam has a predictable pharmacokinetic profile.

Seletracetam is eliminated by metabolism and renal excretion. The major metabolic pathway consists of hydrolysis of the acetamide group to form the carboxylic acid metabolite ucb‐101 596‐1, which is pharmacologically inactive. 72 After oral administration, urinary recovery is primarily accounted for by ucb‐101 596‐1 (60% of the dose) and unchanged drug (30%).

Intranasal administration of 30 mg seletracetam results in rapid absorption with effective plasma seletracetam levels measured within 2 min. 73 The intranasal route offers the option for direct nose‐to‐brain absorption, bypassing the blood circulation. However, this pathway is currently only postulated; the availability of SV2A positron emission tomography (PET) ligands provides the possibility to evaluate this potential pathway in the future.

Drug interactions

Seletracetam has been reported not to inhibit or induce the major human drug metabolizing CYP isoenzymes, and not to inhibit human epoxide hydrolase, but details on the test systems used and concentrations tested were not reported. 72 It has low plasma protein binding (<10%), suggesting a low potential for plasma protein binding interactions. 72 However, because seletracetam, levetiracetam, and brivaracetam act by binding to SV2A, concomitant treatment may lead to pharmacodynamic interactions between these racetams. This was evaluated in a recent study in photosensitive patients with epilepsy. 74 Compared to patients not receiving concomitant treatment with levetiracetam, the effect of seletracetam on the photoparoxysmal response was reduced but not abrogated in patients on chronic therapy with levetiracetam. The unique combination of effects on both SV2A and high voltage‐activated Ca2+ currents may explain why seletracetam inhibits the photoparoxysmal response even in the presence of levetiracetam.

Efficacy data

The clinical development of the novel intranasal seletracetam formulation is de‐risked by the favorable oral seletracetam administration Phase 1 and Phase 2a clinical data, 75 which included three Phase 2a trials in patients with epilepsy, one in photosensitive patients 71 and two in patients with drug‐resistant focal epilepsy. 75

The limited volume of the nasal cavity allows for intranasal administration only if there is anti‐seizure potency at very low doses, precluding nasal administration of all licensed ASMs other than BDZs. For intranasal administration of seletracetam, a mucoadhesive phosphate‐buffered aqueous vehicle was developed that allows administering 30 mg of seletracetam in 150 μL. In a proof‐of‐concept study, intranasal administration of this formulation (PrevEp‐006) in a 42‐year‐old male patient with reading epilepsy under compassionate use regulations at the University of Salzburg, Austria, led to effective plasma seletracetam levels within 2 min and a rapid onset of anti‐seizure activity. 73 A second 30 mg dose fully prevented seizures during 25 min of reading. EEG spike frequency declined dose‐dependently after the first and second dose of 30 mg, with reduced spike propagation on magnetoencephalography (MEG). These findings support seletracetam as a promising non‐BDZ acute seizure prevention.

Adverse effects

In healthy subjects, seletracetam was well tolerated following single oral doses from 2 to 600 mg and following multiple oral doses of 200 mg, b.i.d. 67 Most commonly reported TEAEs were dizziness, somnolence, nausea, and headache. All TEAEs were of mild to moderate intensity and resolved quickly. A similar favorable tolerability was observed in the Phase 2a trials in patients with focal epilepsy, in which a dose range of 20–160 mg/day was tested. 75

Following intranasal administration in the individual with reading epilepsy, no sedation or other adverse effects were noted. 73 The expected high tolerability of intranasal seletracetam is a significant advantage because BDZs currently used for intranasal treatment of acute seizures, although effective, pose risks such as sedation, respiratory depression, and dependency. 76 Due to these risks, the FDA limits intranasal BDZs to one dose every 3–5 days and no more than five doses per month. These restrictions emphasize the need for non‐BDZ alternatives. Seletracetam does not share the safety risks associated with BDZs. 75

Planned studies

The favorable pharmacokinetic profile, high potency and high protective index in animal models, coupled with the favorable activity and tolerability profiles observed with oral administration in Phase 1–2a studies, make seletracetam an ideal candidate for non‐BDZ acute seizure rescue therapy. We are currently performing investigational new drug (IND)–enabling preclinical studies, to be followed by Phase 1 and proof‐of‐concept Phase 2a studies.

RADIPRODIL

Russell Chin1, Danielle Veenma2, Caroline Neuray3, Dayong Li1, Aneeta Saxena1, Michael Panzara1

1GRIN Therapeutics, Inc., New York, NY, USA; 2Department of Pediatrics, ENCORE, Erasmus Medical Center‐Sophia Children's Hospital, Rotterdam 3015, The Netherlands; 3NeurAI Consulting Ltd., London, UK

Introduction and rationale for development

Developmental dysregulation of N‐methyl‐d‐aspartate (NMDA) receptor activity can disrupt excitatory/inhibitory signaling in the brain, contributing to seizures and other neurodevelopmental symptoms in patients with GRIN‐related neurodevelopmental disorder (GRIN‐NDD), tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC), and focal cortical dysplasia (FCD) type II. 77 , 78 , 79 , 80 Pathogenic variants in GRIN1, GRIN2A, GRIN2B, and GRIN2D genes, encoding GluN1, GluN2A, GluN2B, and GluN2D NMDA receptor subunits, respectively, alter receptor activity and are the underlying cause of GRIN‐NDD. 79 In the context of GRIN‐NDD, pathogenic GRIN gene variants can be functionally characterized as gain of function (GoF), loss of function (Lof) or indeterminant. Overexpression of GluN2B subunit‐containing NMDA receptors in cortical tubers associated with TSC and dysplastic tissue observed in FCD type II may contribute to the seizures and other neurological symptoms observed in these disorders. 78 , 80 By modulating GluN2B subunit–containing NMDA receptor activity, radiprodil has the potential to be an effective treatment for GRIN‐NDD, TSC, and FCD type II.

Radiprodil is an orally available, investigational, negative allosteric modulator that specifically binds to the GluN2B subunit of the NMDA receptor. 77 Radiprodil has been evaluated extensively in pharmacokinetic and toxicology studies 81 (GRIN Therapeutics, data on file) and in preclinical models of epileptic seizures. 77 The ongoing clinical development program was designed to evaluate the potential benefit of radiprodil as a precision therapy targeting the underlying pathophysiology of GRIN‐NDD, TSC, and FCD type II. A description of the pharmacology, metabolic profile, mechanism of action, toxicity, and potential drug interactions of radiprodil were included in the EILAT XVII progress report, 3 so only relevant background information and updates are provided here.

Pharmacology

Activity profile in models of seizures and epilepsy

In male Wistar rats of postnatal (PN) ages 7 and 12 days, radiprodil (3–10 mg/kg p.o.) demonstrated dose‐dependent protective effects against the tonic phase of PTZ‐induced seizures. 77 The most potent effects were observed in PN12 rats, at an age that correlates with infant/early childhood in humans. In these rats, a 10 mg/kg dose of radiprodil completely eliminated tonic seizures. 77 Radiprodil (1–10 mg/kg, oral suspension) also showed dose‐dependent protective activity against generalized clonic convulsions in an audiogenic seizure mouse model. 77 In brain slices from transgenic mice expressing GluN2B‐S810R GoF variants, radiprodil reduced the enhanced amplitude of evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents. 82 In the same transgenic mice, radiprodil also delayed seizure onset following in vivo i.p. administration of PTZ. 82

Mechanism of action

Radiprodil is a selective, potent, negative allosteric modulator of GluN2B subunit–containing NMDA receptors. 82 Radiprodil binds at the interface between the GluN1 and GluN2B N‐terminal domains (NTDs) of the NMDA receptor. Its activity is unaffected by external pH 82 Radiprodil effectively inhibited 81% (22/27) of tested human missense GRIN1 and GRIN2B GoF variants within NTD, agonist binding and transmembrane domains, and linker regions. 82

Toxicology

All the available pharmacology, toxicology, and safety data for radiprodil support ongoing and future clinical trials at relevant doses for adult and pediatric patients. Toxicity was assessed using oral administration of radiprodil in rats (dosing: 28 days, 3 months, and 6 months) and dogs (dosing: 28 days, 3 months, and 9 months). Animal embryonic/fetal toxicity (rats and rabbits) and fertility (rats) studies were also conducted (GRIN Therapeutics, data on file). 3

Pharmacokinetic and metabolic profile

Several studies have characterized the pharmacokinetic profile of radiprodil, with additional studies underway. Briefly, oral administration of single and multiple doses of radiprodil in healthy adults demonstrated time‐ and dose‐independent pharmacokinetics. 81 After single oral doses (up to 45 mg) and multiple oral doses (up to 24 mg, t.i.d.), t max ranged from 1.5 to 4 h; variability in AUC and C max within and between subjects was low. The half‐life of radiprodil is ~8 to 9 h (GRIN Therapeutics, data on file). 3 Steady‐state plasma radiprodil concentrations were attained after ~3 days. Radiprodil protein binding in human plasma is 97.6%, and brain‐to‐plasma concentration ratios in rodents (mouse, 2.0; rat, 1.5) reflect a high brain penetration (GRIN Therapeutics, data on file). 3

The pediatric dose range of a radiprodil oral suspension was established using a physiologically based pharmacokinetic model correlated with expected GluN2B NMDA receptor occupancy. 77 , 81

Radiprodil is primarily eliminated independent of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP) metabolism, suggesting limited involvement of CYP enzymes (GRIN Therapeutics, data on file). 81 Further research is needed, and a clinical study is planned to address this question. After single‐dose oral administration of [14C]‐radiprodil in healthy subjects, 99% of the radioactive drug was recovered as metabolites. 81 Six metabolites were identified in urine, the two most abundant being an open‐ring radiprodil sulfate (a product of hydrolysis, decarboxylation, and sulfation) accounting for 42% of radioactivity, and 4‐(4‐fluoro‐benzyl)‐piperidinyl‐oxoacetic acid (FBPO). 81 No hydrolyzing enzymes specific to radiprodil were identified; in vitro analyses in human hepatocytes demonstrated that radiprodil was not affected by inhibitors of acetylcholinesterase, butyrylcholinesterase, fatty acid amide hydrolase, carboxylesterases 1/2, paraoxonase, and aldehyde oxidase. 81 The metabolic clearance of radiprodil normalized to body weight was similar in adults and pediatric patients 2 to 14 months of age. 81

Drug interactions

Studies assessing the potential for radiprodil to interact with concomitantly administered drugs, including ASMs, are ongoing. At plasma concentrations reached with .5 to .75 mg/kg, b.i.d., dosing, radiprodil is not anticipated to strongly interact with CYP‐dependent medications either as a victim or perpetrator (GRIN Therapeutics, data on file). 3 In human hepatocytes, radiprodil has been shown to not induce CYP1A2 mRNA, and at supratherapeutic concentrations radiprodil only modestly induced CYP2B6 and CYP3A4 mRNA. Radiprodil has been shown to be a weak inhibitor of CYP3A4 (inhibition constant [Ki]: 38 μM), CYP2D6 (Ki: 59 μM), and CYP2C9 (Ki: 79 μM) in human liver microsomes. In Caco‐2 cells, radiprodil demonstrated moderate inhibition (IC50, ~6.5 μM) of P‐gp GRIN Therapeutics, data on file. 3

Efficacy data

Evidence of radiprodil possessing anti‐seizure activity was obtained initially in three infants 5, 7, and 11 months of age with infantile spasms. 77 Subsequent clinical development has focused on patients with GRIN‐NDD, TSC, and FCD type II.

The Phase 1b/2a open‐label Honeycomb trial (NCT05818943; EudraCT Number: 2022–000317–14) initially enrolled 15 patients (age ≥6 months to ≤12 years) with GRIN‐NDD caused by GRIN1, GRIN2A, GRIN2B, or GRIN2D GoF variants, grouped into either a seizure cohort (n = 8; median baseline seizure frequency: 25.5 countable motor seizures/28‐days; resistant to ≥2 ASMs) or a behavioral symptom cohort (n = 7). 83 Patients underwent dose escalation with radiprodil starting at .05 mg/kg, b.i.d., and titrating over a period of ~8 weeks to reach an optimal dose, based on clinical response (median dose: .78 mg/kg, b.i.d.). In the seven patients in the seizure cohort who completed titration/maintenance, there was a median reduction of 86% in countable motor seizure frequency during the 8‐week maintenance period compared with baseline. In the behavioral cohort, a decrease in the Aberrant Behavior Checklist – Community (ABC‐C) irritability score over time was observed. For the clinician‐rated Clinical Global Impression of Change (CGI‐C), 28.6% of patients in the seizure cohort were rated as much improved at the end of the maintenance period. On the Caregiver Global Impression of Change (CgGI‐C), 28.6% of patients in the seizure cohort were rated as much or very much improved and 33.3% of patients in the behavioral cohort were rated as much improved at the end of the maintenance period. 84 In a subsequent analysis of 17 patients from both cohorts, EEG recordings (minimum of 8 h inclusive of wakefulness and sleep) obtained before radiprodil treatment showed highly abnormal background activity in 12 patients and a variable spike burden (including 5/17 patients with no spike activity). Following radiprodil treatment, median spike rate decreased, particularly in the five patients with a high EEG spike burden at baseline (from 474/h to 105/h), irrespective of phenotype (GRIN Therapeutics, data on file).

Adverse effects

In the three patients with infantile spasms, no sedation, behavioral changes, or clinically significant changes in vital signs or physical measurements were observed during treatment with radiprodil (starting dose, .04 mg/kg; maintenance doses: .1 mg/kg [n = 1] and .21 mg/kg [n = 2]). 77 Radiprodil was generally well tolerated in patients with GRIN‐NDD enrolled in the Phase 1b/2a Honeycomb trial. Commonly observed TEAEs were those associated with underlying disease and infections. Serious TEAEs were similarly associated with infections or the underlying disease, and all were assessed as unrelated to radiprodil. 83 , 84 Individual treatment‐related TEAEs occurred in no more than 3 of 17 individual patients (17.6%). The treatment‐related TEAEs that occurred in three individual patients were abnormal behavior, agitation, dystonia, and somnolence (GRIN Therapeutics, data on file).

The double‐blind maintenance periods for the RQS and RA cohorts are 12 and 24 weeks, respectively.

RAP‐219

William W. Motley1, Neal M. Nolan1, Swamy Yeleswaram1, Stephen Greene1, Arnold R. Gammaitoni1*, Jeffrey Sevigny1

1Rapport Therapeutics, Boston, MA, USA; *At the time of the studies

Introduction and rationale for development

Although AMPA receptor inhibition is clinically validated as a therapeutic strategy for the treatment of epilepsy, it has been associated with a high incidence of adverse effects due to ubiquitous expression of these receptors throughout the CNS. 87 , 88

Transmembrane AMPA receptor regulatory proteins (TARPs) are anatomically restricted AMPA receptor accessory proteins. TARPs have been found to modulate receptor function, alignment within the synapse, and gating properties. 89 TARPγ8 expression is high in the neocortex and the mesial temporal lobe, including the amygdala and hippocampus, key regions of the brain involved in the initiation and propagation of focal seizures, with minimal expression within hindbrain structures, including the cerebellum and brainstem, as reported previously. 1

RAP‐219, a selective and potent negative allosteric modulator of TARPγ8, is currently in preparation for Phase 3 clinical development as a potential treatment for drug‐resistant focal epilepsy. RAP‐219 is an azabenzimidazole‐based compound that exhibits excellent chemical and physical stability, including polymorphic stability, and has poor aqueous solubility. RAP‐219 is a white to off‐white solid formulated as immediate‐release tablets or capsules for oral administration.

Pharmacology

Activity profile in animal models of seizures and epilepsy

We previously reported that treatment with RAP‐219 prevented seizure activity in the corneal kindling model (CF‐1 mice) and increased the seizure threshold in the PTZ model (CF‐1 mice), with no impairing effects observed in the rotarod test. 1

Mechanism of action

The mechanism of action of RAP‐219 has been described in the EILAT XVII progress report. 1 Briefly, RAP‐219 partially inhibits AMPA receptors containing TARPγ8, providing selective negative modulation of AMPA receptors in regions of the brain associated with the onset and propagation of focal seizures.

Toxicology

We previously reported that the highest doses of RAP‐219 evaluated in IND‐enabling studies were considered NOAEL. 1

Pharmacokinetic profile and target engagement

Results from single and multiple ascending dose studies of RAP‐219 have been reported previously. 1 Additional studies were subsequently completed. In short, RAP‐219 is rapidly absorbed (relative to elimination) from the gastrointestinal tract with a t max of about 4 h, is extensively bound (free fraction of 1.2%) to plasma proteins, and is eliminated with a long half‐life (525 h, or ~22 days), consistent with q.d. dosing. RAP‐219 is primarily cleared through metabolism (Phase 2 conjugation and oxidation).

More recently, an open‐label study has been conducted to qualify and quantify the neuroanatomic expression of TARPγ8 in healthy adults 18–55 years of age. 90 Participants (n = 16) were enrolled in three cohorts and received at least one oral dose of RAP‐219, q.d., for 14 days (.75 mg/day for 5 days, followed by 1.25 mg/day for 9 days [n = 6]; .25 mg/day for 14 days [n = 6]; or .25 mg/day for 7 days, followed by .5 mg/day for 7 days [n = 4]) after ≥10 h of fasting. On Day 1 (pre‐dose) and on Day 15 (last day of treatment), PET scans were obtained to evaluate TARPγ8 expression after an intravenous (i.v.) injection of the TARPγ8‐specific tracer [18F]JNJ‐64511070. TARPγ8 mean distribution volume, a measure of TARPγ8 distribution, was greatest in the neocortex and mesial temporal lobe, including the hippocampus and amygdala. The pharmacokinetic profile of RAP‐219 observed in this study was consistent with that observed in previous multiple‐dose studies. Receptor occupancy was estimated using pre‐ and post‐dose TARPγ8 distribution volume and Lassen plots. Target receptor occupancy (at least 50%–70%, based on results from corneal kindling studies in mice) was achieved at a RAP‐219 plasma concentration range of ~2.0–3.5 ng/mL, suggesting that RAP‐219 at doses ≥.25 mg/day achieve the target receptor occupancy within 14 days, even though achieving steady‐state would require a longer treatment period. In the Phase 2a study described in Section 10.6, plasma RAP‐219 exposure at the end of the 8‐week treatment period was ~80% of steady state. Exposures approaching steady state during the Phase 2a study were achieved in Phase 1 studies and were overall well tolerated (Rapport Therapeutics, data on file).

Drug interactions

Results of in vitro investigations of potential drug interactions with RAP‐219 have been summarized in the EILAT XVII progress report. 1 No clinically significant drug interactions with RAP‐219 are known.

Efficacy data

The anti‐seizure activity of RAP‐219 was evaluated in a novel, Phase 2a proof‐of‐concept study (NCT06377930; FOS‐201) of participants 18–65 years of age with drug‐resistant focal seizures and an implanted responsive neurostimulator (RNS System, NeuroPace). 91 Participants were treated with RAP‐219, .75 mg/day, for 5 days, followed by 1.25 mg/day for the remainder of the 8‐week open‐label treatment period. RNS long episodes (LEs), which are defined as epileptiform activity exceeding a clinician‐prespecified duration threshold and often represent electrographic seizures, 92 were used as a measure of efficacy along with clinical seizures as recorded in seizure diaries.

Of 38 participants screened for eligibility, 30 received at least one dose of RAP‐219 (safety population); 26 completed the treatment period. LE analyses were conducted using data from the modified intent‐to‐treat (mITT) population, defined as those participants who received ≥3 weeks of treatment, who had treatment compliance of ≥70%, and who had no detection or stimulation setting changes to their RNS System during the baseline or treatment period (n = 27). Clinical seizures analyses were conducted using data from the 25 participants in the mITT population who had ≥1 clinical seizure in the 4‐week prospective baseline period.

The primary endpoint (≥30% responder rate, defined as percentage of participants with a ≥ 30% reduction in frequency of LEs during treatment compared with baseline), was met during the 8‐week treatment period, with 85.2% (p < .0001) of participants meeting responder criteria. In addition, the median percent change in LE frequency from baseline to Weeks 1–8 was significant (−71.0%, p = .0001). A significant change in LE frequency was observed during both Weeks 1–4 (median percent change: −74.7%, p < .0001) and Weeks 5–8 (median percent change: −74.7%, p = .0029), suggesting rapidity of onset and durability of efficacy across the 8‐week treatment period.

The median change in clinical seizure frequency from baseline to Weeks 1–8 was also significant (−77.8%, p = .0105). A significant change in clinical seizure frequency was observed during both Weeks 1–4 (median percent change: −85.0%, p = .0007) and Weeks 5–8 (median percent change: −77.2%, p = .0392). The ≥50% (clinically meaningful), ≥75% (profound), and 100% (seizure‐free) clinical seizure responder rates for Weeks 1–8 were also significant (72.0%, 56.0%, and 24.0%, respectively; all p < .0001).

Adverse effects

All TEAEs reported in the RAP‐219 PET study were Grade 1 (mild) or Grade 2 (moderate). 90 The most common TEAEs were headache (n = 7, 44.0%) and fatigue (n = 5, 31.0%). One participant discontinued due to a treatment‐related TEAE (obsessive rumination, Grade 2). 90

Of 30 participants who were treated with at least one dose of RAP‐219 in the Phase 2a proof‐of‐concept study, 25 (83.0%) reported TEAEs; all TEAEs starting during treatment were mild or moderate. 91 The most commonly reported TEAEs during the treatment period were dizziness (n = 8, 27.0%), headache (n = 6, 20.0%), fatigue (n = 4, 13.0%), fall, nausea, and somnolence (each n = 3, 10.0%). TEAEs led to study drug discontinuation in three (10.0%) participants (worsening of preexisting memory impairment [Grade 1, n = 1], panic attack [Grade 1, n = 1], and worsening of preexisting anxiety [Grade 2, n = 1]).

Planned studies

Participants who completed the Phase 2a proof‐of‐concept study for focal epilepsy (FOS‐201) are eligible to enter a long‐term safety study (NCT07219407, actively enrolling). Two Phase 3 randomized controlled trials of RAP‐219 for the treatment of drug‐resistant focal epilepsy are planned. In both trials, participants with focal seizures will be randomized after an 8‐week pretreatment baseline to either one of two RAP‐219 treatment doses or placebo. After completing the 8‐week randomized double‐blind treatment period, participants will continue into either an OLE study or an 8‐week post‐treatment follow‐up period. The primary endpoint will be a measure of change from baseline in clinical seizure frequency per 28 days, represented as either median percent change or 50% reduction responder proportion, depending on the regional regulatory standard. Safety and tolerability will be assessed primarily by incidence of TEAEs and clinically significant changes from baseline in vital signs, ECG recordings, complete blood count, and serum chemistry. A Phase 3 study of RAP‐219 for the treatment of primary generalized tonic–clonic seizures is also planned.

RELUTRIGINE

Samata Kamireddy1, Silvana Frizzo1, Brian Spar1, Poppy Guest1, Kelley Del Real1, Dharit Patel1, Henry Jacotin1, Archis Patel1, Cliff Mason1, Guillermo Fernandez1, Kimberly Crimin1, Steven Petrou1, Marcio Souza1, Linda Laux2, Douglas Smith3, Alvaro Bertran Corbellini4, Antonio Gil‐Nagel4, Eric Segal5

1Praxis Precision Medicines, Boston, MA 02110 USA; 2Ann and Robert H Lurie Children's Hospital of Chicago, Chicago, IL, USA; 3Minnesota Epilepsy Group, Roseville, MN, USA; 4Ruber International Hospital, Madrid, Spain; 5Northeast Regional Epilepsy Group, Hackensack, NJ, USA

Introduction and rationale for development

Although sodium channel blockers remain a cornerstone of anti‐seizure therapy for many DEEs, their utility is often constrained by dose‐limiting adverse effects. Advances in the genetic and mechanistic understanding of sodium channel–related epilepsies have highlighted the role of specific channel dysfunction in driving pathological neuronal firing, supporting the rationale for more selective therapeutic approaches. 93 , 94 , 95 Therapeutic agents designed to preferentially modulate aberrant sodium channel activity while preserving normal neuronal signaling have the potential to improve seizure control while reducing adverse‐effect burden, thereby addressing the efficacy–tolerability tradeoff commonly associated with existing therapies, including conventional sodium channel blockers. 94 , 96

Relutrigine (PRAX‐562) is a differentiated functional state sodium channel modulator designed to selectively target disease‐driven neuronal hyperexcitability in DEEs. 97 Unlike conventional sodium channel blockers, which may exacerbate seizures in DEEs such as SCN1A‐related epilepsies due to nonselective suppression of sodium channel activity, relutrigine preferentially modulates pathologic firing states while sparing physiologic neuronal signaling. By targeting a common pathway implicated in seizure activity across genetically diverse epilepsies, relutrigine has the potential to be effective across a broad range of DEEs. Preclinical and clinical data to date demonstrate a wide therapeutic window and support the potential for improved safety and efficacy over current treatments. 97 , 98 , 99 , 100 , 101 Notably, recent clinical data from the Phase 2/3 EMBOLD study show early onset and sustained improvements in seizure control and functional outcomes, supporting relutrigine's potential as a significant therapeutic advance for SCN2A‐ and SCN8A‐DEE. 101

Pharmacology

Relutrigine's seizure‐related activity profile in animal models, pharmacological properties, and mechanism of action were described previously. 97 Briefly, relutrigine preferentially inhibits persistent sodium current and use‐dependent sodium channel activity while having a lesser effect on peak transient current. In electrophysiologic studies, relutrigine demonstrated greater inhibition under conditions of repetitive, high‐frequency activation consistent with use‐ and frequency‐dependent inhibition of neuronal hyperexcitability. In established rodent seizure models, including MES and chemically induced seizure paradigms, relutrigine exhibited a clear separation between anti‐seizure efficacy and motor impairment, supporting a differentiated pharmacologic profile and favorable preclinical therapeutic window.

Relutrigine also demonstrated activity in a zebrafish model of DS (scn1a mutant). Preliminary findings showed that relutrigine significantly reversed seizure‐like locomotor behavior, including high‐velocity burst activity, with greater potency than fenfluramine. 100 Although relutrigine has not been evaluated directly in the Scn1a +/− mouse due to CNS exposure limitations that preclude sustained, interpretable pharmacology in this model, a fit‐for‐purpose tool compound with a closely‐matched state‐dependent sodium channel modulator profile demonstrated improved survival and reduced seizure frequency. 102 Together, these findings support the investigation of selective, disease‐state sodium channel modulation in SCN1A‐related epilepsies and continued evaluation of relutrigine across genetically defined DEEs.

Toxicology

Relutrigine has been evaluated in repeat‐dose toxicity studies of up to 6 months in rats and 9 months in dogs, safety genetic toxicology studies assessing mutagenic and clastogenic potential, standard safety pharmacology assessments, and reproductive and developmental toxicity studies, including embryo–fetal development, fertility, and pre‐ and post‐natal development assessments in rats and rabbits. The nonclinical safety data to date support continued clinical development and are consistent with the dosing strategies being evaluated for oral administration in pediatric patients.

Pharmacokinetic and metabolic profile

Relutrigine pharmacokinetics has been characterized across multiple Phase 1 studies in healthy adults (including data presented previously 103 , 104 ) and a Phase 2/3 study in children with SCN2A‐ and SCN8A‐related DEEs, 99 , 101 integrating rich adult sampling with sparse pediatric data using population and physiologically based pharmacokinetic modeling. Across populations, analyses demonstrated predictable, body weight–dependent systemic exposure, with dose‐proportional increases in plasma AUC over the clinically relevant dose range (2.5 to 90 mg) and less‐than‐proportional increases in C max at higher doses (120 and 150 mg).

Following oral administration of single doses (2.5 to 150 mg) and multiple q.d. doses (30 to 120 mg), relutrigine was rapidly absorbed, with C max occurring ~1.75–3.5 h after dosing in adults and 2 h after dosing in patients 1–18 years of age. Plasma concentrations were quantifiable throughout the dosing interval following q.d. administration (30 to 120 mg, 14 days) and remained measurable for up to 120 h after the last dose. Relutrigine was highly bound to human plasma proteins (~95%), with binding occurring primarily to albumin. Relutrigine exhibited extensive tissue distribution, with a population median steady‐state volume of distribution (Vss) of ~182 L. Relutrigine has a prolonged half‐life (median ~110 h in pediatric patients; 129 h in adults), resulting in substantial accumulation with q.d. dosing. Steady state was achieved by ~28 days, with a median accumulation ratio (Rac) for AUC of ~5 and minimal incremental increases in C max, trough concentration (C min), and AUC beyond Day 28. Food intake delayed absorption without affecting overall plasma exposure, supporting dosing without regard to meals (16 healthy participants receiving a single 90 mg dose). Body weight was the primary determinant of plasma exposure variability, supporting weight‐based dosing, whereas age and sex had no clinically meaningful influence. Weight‐based dosing in pediatric patients yielded steady‐state exposures that were generally comparable to those observed in adults.

Based on human mass balance and in vitro studies, relutrigine is eliminated primarily through metabolic pathways, with negligible renal excretion of unchanged parent drug. Metabolism is mediated predominantly by CYP1A2. Principal biotransformation pathways across species include O‐dealkylation and related secondary reactions. One circulating human metabolite (~10% of total systemic relutrigine‐related plasma exposure) was identified in a mass balance study and was pharmacologically inactive; no other circulating metabolites were present at clinically meaningful levels.

Drug interactions

The drug–drug interaction potential of relutrigine was evaluated using a combination of in vitro and clinical assessments. In vitro CYP phenotyping studies conducted at concentrations encompassing anticipated therapeutic plasma exposures identified CYP1A2 as the predominant enzyme mediating relutrigine metabolism, with a secondary contribution from CYP3A4. Transporter assessments indicated that clinically relevant interactions are unlikely at anticipated therapeutic plasma exposures.

In clinical drug–drug interaction studies, relutrigine (120 mg, q.d.) demonstrated moderate inhibition of CYP1A2 and CYP2D6, as assessed using caffeine (100 mg) and dextromethorphan (30 mg), respectively, whereas no clinically meaningful effects on CYP3A4 activity were observed, as assessed using midazolam (2 mg). Marker substrates were administered as single doses at baseline and after 14 days of relutrigine q.d. dosing.

Co‐administration of a single 120 mg dose of relutrigine with the CYP1A2 inhibitor ciprofloxacin (500 mg, b.i.d., for 18 days) resulted in modest increases in relutrigine exposure (AUC ~31%), whereas co‐administration with the strong CYP3A4 inhibitor itraconazole (200 mg, q.d., for 17 days following a loading dose) had no clinically meaningful effect on systemic exposure. Overall, available data indicate a low risk of clinically meaningful drug–drug interactions affecting relutrigine exposure.

Efficacy data

EMBOLD is a Phase 2/3 multicenter, double‐blind, randomized, timing‐masked placebo‐controlled clinical trial (NCT05818553) 99 , 101 evaluating the safety, tolerability, and efficacy of relutrigine in pediatric participants 1–18 years of age with genetically‐confirmed early‐onset SCN2A‐ or SCN8A‐related DEEs, followed by an OLE. Briefly, the study enrolled participants with a documented severe DEE due to mutations in SCN2A or SCN8A. Eligible participants were required to have ≥8 countable motor seizures during the 4 weeks prior to screening and during a prospective 28‐day baseline observation period captured via daily seizure diaries. Participants were also required to be on stable doses of background ASMs.

The trial was initially planned to enroll up to 20 participants (Cohort 1, initial double‐blind phase) to assess safety and tolerability and to explore pharmacokinetic and seizure outcomes. Based on early evidence of substantial seizure reduction, periods of seizure freedom, favorable tolerability, and clinically meaningful benefit observed in this initial phase, 99 EMBOLD was expanded to include up to 80 additional participants (Cohort 2, registrational expansion) to further evaluate efficacy, safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetics in a larger population. 101

All participants followed the same trial design, consisting of a 16‐week double‐blind treatment period followed by an OLE. In the double‐blind period, participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to one of two treatment arms, receiving either q.d. relutrigine for 16 weeks (the relutrigine treatment arm) or relutrigine for 12 weeks and 4 weeks of placebo (the placebo/relutrigine treatment arm). The study employed a timing‐masked placebo design in which participants randomized to the placebo/relutrigine arm received placebo during a single, pre‐specified 4‐week interval within the 16‐week double‐blind phase. Although all parties were aware of the possibility that placebo would be given for 4 consecutive weeks within the 16‐week period, to preserve blinding integrity, the timing of placebo administration remained fully masked to investigators, caregivers, and participants for the entire study duration. In Cohort 1, relutrigine was administered at a starting dose of .5 mg/kg/day with dose adjustment permitted up to 1.0 mg/kg/day. In Cohort 2, relutrigine was administered at 1 mg/kg/day, with optional increase to 1.5 mg/kg/day in the OLE. All treatments were administered q.d., without titration.

The primary endpoint was change from baseline in monthly motor seizure frequency. Key secondary endpoints included motor seizure‐free days and assessments of global clinical improvement, as measured by the Clinical Global Impression–Improvement (CGI‐I) and Caregiver Global Impression–Improvement (CgGI‐I) scales. The registrational portion of EMBOLD included an interim analysis agreed with the FDA, after which the Data Monitoring Committee recommended stopping the study early for efficacy.

All efficacy analyses were conducted in the ITT population, defined as all randomized participants who received at least one dose of study drug and were analyzed according to randomized treatment assignment. 101 Baseline log‐transformed motor seizure frequency per 28 days was similar between groups, with a mean (SE) of 5.04 (.3) in the placebo group and 4.72 (.19) in the relutrigine group.

Primary efficacy analyses demonstrated significantly lower seizure frequency in the relutrigine treatment arm compared with placebo. Model‐based estimated marginal means were 196.6 seizures in the relutrigine group and 418.4 seizures in the placebo group. The estimated incidence rate ratio (IRR, relutrigine/placebo) was .47 (n = 51; 95% CI .32–.70; p = .0002), where model‐derived IRR represents the ratio of seizure rates between treatment groups, accounting for baseline seizure frequency and exposure time; this corresponds to a 53% reduction in monthly motor seizure frequency (calculated as [1 − IRR] × 100). Treatment with relutrigine was further associated with statistically significant improvements in seizure‐free days. Estimated marginal means were 17.34 seizure‐free days in the relutrigine group and 10.43 seizure‐free days in the placebo group. The estimated IRR was 1.66 (95% CI 1.04–2.66; p = .034), corresponding to a 66% increase in seizure‐free days (calculated as [IRR − 1] × 100).

Global assessments of clinical status demonstrated improvement among relutrigine‐treated patients, as assessed by both investigator‐rated CGI‐I and caregiver‐reported CgGI‐I measures, with improvements observed in seizure severity and intensity (78% of participants on both clinician and caregiver report), alertness (78% on both), communication (67% on caregiver report and 53% on clinician report), and disruptive behavior (35% on caregiver report and 27% on clinician report).

Collectively, these data demonstrate robust seizure reduction accompanied by broader functional benefit in this severely affected pediatric DEE population.

Adverse effects

Relutrigine was initially evaluated in two randomized, placebo‐controlled Phase 1 studies in healthy adult participants. 103 , 104 In PRAX‐562‐101 (n = 112), single ascending doses ranging from 2.5 to 150 mg and multiple q.d. doses up to 120 mg for 14 days were assessed. 103 Relutrigine was generally well tolerated across all dose levels, with most TEAEs mild in severity, the most commonly reported including headache (16.7% at 90 mg, q.d.; 33.3% at 120 mg, q.d.) and site‐related reactions to the i.v. catheter used for blood sampling. In PRAX‐562‐102 (n = 48), relutrigine was evaluated at 90 mg, q.d., for 28 days without other concomitant treatments, and at 120 mg, q.d., for 28 days in combination with oxcarbazepine 600 mg, b.i.d. 104 Relutrigine was generally well tolerated at 90 mg, q.d., with the most common TEAEs being dizziness and headache (both 16.7%). In the 120 mg cohort in combination with oxcarbazepine, the number of participants with TEAEs increased, and TEAEs were predominantly neurologic and consistent with sodium channel modulator class effects (e.g., dizziness 42.9%, tremor 35.7%, headache 57.1%, hypoesthesia 28.6%, fatigue 21.4%), with additional gastrointestinal events (e.g., nausea 50%). Pharmacokinetic assessments supported q.d. dosing and showed no meaningful alteration of relutrigine exposure with oxcarbazepine coadministration.

EMBOLD study results involving controlled and open‐label treatment extending over 24 months in participants with SCN2A‐ or SCN8A‐related DEEs demonstrated that relutrigine was generally well tolerated, with no evidence of discontinuations or study withdrawals due to tolerability. 99 , 101 There were no relutrigine‐related serious TEAEs identified. In the double‐blind period, exposure‐adjusted incidence rates (per 100 patient‐months) were similar between relutrigine and placebo. The most commonly observed TEAEs included pyrexia (11.7% vs 13.4% on placebo), upper respiratory tract infection (9.8% vs 4.4% placebo), and somnolence (9.1% vs 13.4% placebo). Overall, TEAEs were mostly mild to moderate, manageable through optimization of background medications, and did not result in relutrigine discontinuation or necessitate dose modification. Five participants had temporary treatment interruptions due to TEAEs, all of which resolved and were considered unrelated to relutrigine.

Planned studies

Following the pre‐specified interim analysis of the EMBOLD study and subsequent planned discussion with the FDA in December 2025, Praxis confirmed submission of an NDA for relutrigine for SCN2A‐ and SCN8A‐DEE. The EMERALD registrational study (NCT07010471) evaluating relutrigine in phenotypic DEEs irrespective of etiology is rapidly enrolling and expected to be completed in the second half of 2026.

EMERALD is a randomized, multi‐center, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled clinical trial comprising a 16‐week double‐blind treatment period followed by a 32‐week OLE. Eligible participants are individuals 2–65 years of age with a documented diagnosis of a DEE with seizure onset before 12 years of age. Key inclusion criteria include ≥4 countable motor seizures during a 4‐week baseline observation period and stable concomitant ASMs, with no restrictions on the number of sodium channel blockers permitted. Participants are randomized 1:1 to receive q.d. relutrigine (1.0 mg/kg/day, with option to increase to 1.5 mg/kg/day) or placebo during the double‐blind phase. The study is designed to enroll ~160 participants. Assuming successful initial NDA approval of relutrigine, the EMERALD study, if positive, would serve as the basis for a Supplemental NDA submission by 2027, expanding the potential of this compound to address the significant unmet meet across a broad range of DEEs.

STACCATO ALPRAZOLAM

Robert Roebling1 and Hugues Chanteux2

1UCB, Monheim am Rhein, Germany and 2UCB, Braine‐l'Alleud, Belgium

Introduction and rationale for development

Seizures that fail to self‐terminate can become prolonged or progress in severity, including progression to seizure clusters or status epilepticus. Prolonged seizures can be more difficult to treat the longer they last and can lead to significant morbidity and mortality. 105 Rapid and Early Seizure Termination (REST) is a new treatment approach, with the goal of terminating an ongoing seizure in patients at risk of progressing to a more severe seizure or seizure emergency. 106 Rapid termination of prolonged seizures is critical to prevent progression to seizure emergencies, protect the brain, and help patients maintain their quality of life.

Alprazolam is a well‐known and highly characterized 1,4‐benzodiazepine. It was approved in 1981 in the United States as an oral tablet for the treatment of anxiety and panic disorders. 107 Staccato alprazolam is a portable, handheld, drug–device combination that can provide rapid systemic delivery of alprazolam through the intrapulmonary route. 108 The device is actuated by a single normal inhalation in the mouthpiece, leading to rapid heating of a thin film of excipient‐free alprazolam to form pure‐drug vapor (Figure 6). 109 This condenses into aerosol particles that are delivered to the deep lung. The inhaler does not require priming, coordination between actuation and breath, or forceful inspiration. Staccato alprazolam is under investigation as a REST medication for the treatment of stereotypical prolonged seizures and is now in the final stages of the Phase 3 program.

(B) A single normal inhalation actuates the Staccato inhaler and formation of the drug aerosol is obtained within 500 ms by rapid heating and vaporization of a pure thin film of excipient‐free alprazolam coated on a metallic plate (heat‐package). Pharmacology

Activity profile in animal models of seizures and epilepsy

Alprazolam has demonstrated potent anti‐seizure activity in various animal models, including audiogenic seizures in DBA/2 and DBA/2J mice. 110 , 111 Alprazolam had higher potency in reducing audiogenic seizures compared with diazepam and clonazepam after i.p. administration in DBA/2 mice; median effective dose (ED50) values for alprazolam, diazepam, and clonazepam were .009, .28, and .016 μmol/kg, respectively, for inhibition of the clonic phase of audiogenic seizures, and .005, .24, and .01 μmol/kg, respectively, for inhibition of the tonic phase. 111

The effects of alprazolam have also been assessed in vivo in rats with chemically induced seizure activity. Alprazolam .5 mg/kg, i.p., had a strong anticonvulsive effect in male Wistar rats with seizures chemically induced by 50 mg/kg, i.p., N‐(3,5‐dimethoxy‐4‐propoxyphenylethyl)‐aziridine or 100 mg/kg, s.c., PTZ. 112 In anesthetized male Sprague–Dawley rats, .4 mg/kg alprazolam given i.p. was able to inhibit in vivo epileptiform burst activities in hippocampal CA1 neurons induced by 5 μmol, i.c.v., cyclothiazide or 383 mg/kg, s.c., PTZ. 113

Mechanism of action

Alprazolam binds with high affinity to the BDZ binding site of the GABAA‐BDZ receptor–chloride channel complex. 114 By binding at this site, alprazolam potentiates GABAergic inhibition, resulting in a general dampening of neuronal excitability.

Toxicology

To support inhalation as the intended route of administration in humans, the potential toxicity of alprazolam has been evaluated by the inhalation route in accordance with the FDA guidance on Nonclinical Safety Evaluation of Reformulated Drug Products and Products Intended for Administration by an Alternate Route. 115 To ensure similar chemical and physical properties between the toxicology and clinical study material aerosols, aerosols in toxicology studies were generated using the same process as that employed in clinical trials: namely heating alprazolam to form a vapor that subsequently cools, condenses, and forms aerosol particles that are inhaled. This results in similar aerosol purity and particle size distribution between toxicology and clinical study materials.

The effects observed after administration of alprazolam by inhalation to rats and dogs were limited to clinical signs related to the known pharmacology of alprazolam. There were no new relevant or unexpected toxicological findings. The doses administered to rats and dogs were considered to have adequately assessed the potential for local toxicity and cover the expected human exposure for the projected clinical doses.

Pharmacokinetic and metabolic profile

The systemic delivery and pharmacokinetics of Staccato alprazolam have been assessed across a range of studies in both adults and adolescents. Following administration with the Staccato inhaler, alprazolam was rapidly absorbed with t max achieved within 2 min in most participants, compared with 45 min for oral alprazolam. 108 , 116

Variability during the absorption phase has been observed following administration with the Staccato delivery system. Although the Staccato inhaler has been designed to provide an aerosol of optimal particle size (mass median aerodynamic diameter between .5 and 3 μm) to target the deep lung and alveolar space, 117 it cannot be ruled out that a fraction of the aerosolized alprazolam can be deposited at different levels of the respiratory tract or swallowed with saliva, which can lead to slower absorption. 108 , 116 Despite this observed variability, clinically relevant plasma alprazolam concentrations, comparable to the C max achieved after oral administration, can be reached within 2 min post‐dose with the Staccato inhaler (Figure 7). 108 , 116 An evaluation of published data concerning different routes of BDZ administration suggested that pulmonary alprazolam may have the fastest rate of absorption, similar to a 2‐min i.v. infusion. 118

The lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) for alprazolam is .200 ng/mL. In specific populations assessed, Staccato alprazolam was rapidly absorbed to a similar extent in smokers and non‐smokers. 108 Pharmacokinetic profiles were generally similar across Japanese, Chinese, and Caucasian participants, suggesting that dose adjustment would not be needed across different ethnicities. 108 Based on results of population pharmacokinetic simulations, plasma alprazolam exposure (C max and AUC) following a single 2 mg dose of Staccato alprazolam was similar between adolescents (12–17 years of age) and adults with epilepsy, which supported the use of a 2 mg dose in adolescents in the Phase 3 trials. 119

Alprazolam is primarily metabolized in humans by CYP3A4 to two main metabolites: 4‐hydroxy‐alprazolam and α‐hydroxy‐alprazolam. 107 Following administration of Staccato alprazolam in healthy adults, the mean half‐life of alprazolam was similar to oral alprazolam, ranging from 12 to 15 h. The AUC of 4‐hydroxy‐alprazolam and α‐hydroxy‐alprazolam accounted for <10% and <5% of the AUC of unchanged alprazolam, respectively, 108 which was similar to that of oral alprazolam.

Drug interactions

Risks of drug interactions with Staccato alprazolam are expected to be similar to those with oral alprazolam. 107 Specific dosing recommendations are under evaluation considering the intermittent use of Staccato alprazolam.

Efficacy data

Staccato alprazolam has demonstrated anti‐seizure activity in clinical trials consistent with the mechanism of action of BDZs. A Phase 2a proof‐of‐concept randomized, double‐blind, crossover, placebo‐controlled trial (NCT02351115) assessed the ability of Staccato alprazolam to rapidly suppress the photoparoxysmal EEG response in five patients with photosensitive epilepsy. 120 The three dose levels of Staccato alprazolam (.5, 1, and 2 mg) reduced the photoparoxysmal response at 2 min; this effect was sustained through 4 h for the .5 mg dose, and 6 h for the 1 and 2 mg doses.

A Phase 2b proof‐of‐concept dose‐ranging trial in an inpatient setting (NCT03478982) demonstrated the efficacy, feasibility, and safety of Staccato alprazolam as a REST medication in adults with a history of seizure episodes with a predictable pattern. 121 A single dose of Staccato alprazolam (1 or 2 mg) at seizure onset terminated seizures within 2 min of administration without recurrence within 2 h in 65.8% of patients in both the 1 and the 2 mg dose groups (n = 38 for each group), compared with 42.5% with Staccato placebo (n = 40; p = .0392). Consistent with the rapid onset of clinical activity, the median (95% CI) time to seizure cessation was 30 s (19, 59) for Staccato alprazolam (n = 76) vs 60 s (30, 120) for Staccato placebo (n = 39). In patients with prolonged focal seizures, these values were 60 s (42, 93) for Staccato alprazolam (n = 19) vs 120 s (60, 360) for Staccato placebo (n = 17). Alprazolam was successfully dosed in patients with focal impaired consciousness (awareness) seizures, indicating that an active breath is not required for the Staccato inhaler.

Adverse effects

Several trials have assessed the safety and tolerability of Staccato alprazolam. TEAEs most commonly reported with Staccato alprazolam across clinical trials included somnolence, cough, dizziness, dysgeusia, and sedation. 108 , 109 , 119 , 120 , 121 In the double‐blind part of the randomized, Phase 2b, dose‐ranging trial, the most common TEAEs reported with Staccato alprazolam were cough (Staccato alprazolam 1 mg: 10.5%; 2 mg: 18.4%), somnolence (1 mg: 18.4%; 2 mg: 10.5%), and dysgeusia (1 mg: 15.8%; 2 mg: 10.5%). 121 Across all trials, TEAEs were mostly mild or moderate in intensity. Lower body weight (<50 kg) did not appear to increase the risk of sedation or other TEAEs in adolescents with epilepsy who received a single dose of Staccato alprazolam, 2 mg. 119

For drugs delivered via inhalation, it is important to assess the risk of drug‐induced bronchospasm, particularly in patients with airway hyperresponsiveness. The pulmonary safety of two consecutive doses of Staccato alprazolam administered 72 h apart was assessed in a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, Phase 1 trial in healthy adults (n = 30) and participants with mild asthma (n = 48). 109 Cough was reported more frequently in participants with mild asthma compared with healthy participants. There was no increase in the incidence of TEAEs with repeated dosing, and no evidence of clinically relevant airway obstruction or bronchospasm related to the administration of Staccato alprazolam. Availability of these data supported enrollment of patients with mild asthma in the Phase 3 trials.

Subjective assessments completed by participants (using the sedation visual analog scale) indicated a rapid increase in sedation with Staccato alprazolam compared with Staccato placebo, which generally resolved within 6 h post‐dose. 119 , 120 , 121 Sedation reported within 2 min of Staccato alprazolam administration indicates that effective plasma alprazolam levels are already reached within 2 min post‐dose. 119

Staccato alprazolam was generally well tolerated and displayed a safety profile consistent with that known from other alprazolam applications. The adverse event profile was predictable based on the known adverse effects of benzodiazepines.

Ongoing studies

A head‐to‐head comparison trial assessed the time to onset of effect (based on quantitative EEG) of Staccato alprazolam vs other benzodiazepines. This was a Phase 1 open‐label, single‐dose, randomized, active‐comparator, crossover trial in healthy adults (NCT07020988). The objectives were to compare the time to onset of effect of Staccato alprazolam 2 mg vs i.v. midazolam 5 mg administered over 2.5 min (primary objective), and of Staccato alprazolam 2 mg vs intranasal diazepam 10, 15, or 20 mg (secondary objective). Safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetics were assessed as secondary outcomes. This trial was recently clinically completed; 55 participants were enrolled, and analysis of the results is ongoing.

Phase 3 trials are currently ongoing to evaluate the efficacy and safety of Staccato alprazolam in patients ≥12 years of age with stereotypical prolonged seizures in the outpatient setting.

A Phase 3a, double‐blind, randomized, placebo‐controlled trial is assessing the efficacy and safety of Staccato alprazolam for the treatment of a qualifying prolonged seizure event (EP0162; NCT05077904; EudraCT 2021–002686‐18). Planned enrollment is up to 350 patients. The primary efficacy objectives are to compare the success of a single administration of Staccato alprazolam 2 mg vs Staccato placebo in: (1) rapidly terminating a seizure episode within 90 s, and (2) rapidly terminating a seizure episode within 90 s with no seizure recurrence up to 2 h after trial drug administration and no initiation of seizure rescue treatment from 90 s to 2 h. Safety, tolerability, healthcare resource utilization, treatment satisfaction, and pharmacokinetics are also being assessed.

A Phase 3b, OLE trial is ongoing to assess the long‐term safety and efficacy of multiple intermittent administrations of Staccato alprazolam 2 mg for the treatment of a qualifying prolonged seizure event (EP0165; NCT05076617; EudraCT 2021–002637‐42). Depending on the date of enrollment, trial duration for each patient can be up to ~6.5 years. The planned enrollment is up to 300 patients with epilepsy who completed a previous trial of Staccato alprazolam (NCT04857307, NCT03478982, NCT05077904). The primary outcomes are the incidence of TEAEs, TEAEs leading to discontinuation, and serious TEAEs.

Within the EP0165 trial, a Phase 3a sub‐study is being conducted to assess the consistency of effect of Staccato alprazolam over several seizures. This involves a double‐blind, randomized, placebo‐controlled, crossover design in a subset of study sites in Europe. Patients are randomized to one of four treatment arms, and over four qualifying prolonged seizure events, three seizures will be treated with Staccato alprazolam 2 mg and one seizure will be treated with Staccato placebo, after which all patients can enter the OLE and receive Staccato alprazolam 2 mg.

VORMATRIGINE

Karl Hansen1, Bashar Mohsen2, Maribel Hernandez1, Megan Sniecinski1, Silvana Frizzo1, Henry Jacotin1, Dharit Patel1, Noam Epstein1, Archis Patel1, Cliff Mason1, Brianna Murray1, Guillermo Fernandez1, Kimberly Crimin1, Minao Tang1, Steven Petrou1, Marcio Souza1, Orrin Devinsky1,3, Ángel Aledo‐Serrano4

1Praxis Precision Medicines, Boston, MA, USA; 2Assertive Research Center, Miami Lakes, USA; 3NYU Langone Comprehensive Epilepsy Center, New York, NY, USA; 4Epilepsy Unit, Clinical Neuroscience Institute, Campus Blua Sanitas Valdebebas Hospital, Madrid, Spain

Introduction and rationale for development

Although sodium channel blockers are central to current anti‐seizure therapy, their clinical utility is frequently limited by tolerability concerns, prolonged titration requirements, and complex dosing paradigms driven by drug–drug interactions. As a result, many patients are unable to achieve or maintain doses required for optimal seizure control. In some cases, dose‐limiting adverse effects emerge before a fully effective dose can be reached. 122

The development of sodium channel modulators with more selective targeting of sodium channels involved in pathological neuronal hyperexcitability represents a promising therapeutic strategy. By preferentially acting during periods of disease‐related hyperexcitability while sparing physiological neuronal activity, such therapies have the potential to improve the balance between efficacy and tolerability, addressing key limitations of existing therapies.

Vormatrigine (PRAX‐628) is a functionally selective sodium channel modulator in advanced development as an oral, q.d. treatment for the treatment of focal seizures in adults. Emerging preclinical and clinical data indicate that vormatrigine achieves plasma exposures exceeding predicted therapeutic levels with rapid onset of pharmacological activity, while remaining well tolerated without the need for titration. Collectively, these data are consistent with vormatrigine being differentiated from currently available standard‐of‐care sodium channel therapies and support its potential to improve seizure control without compromising tolerability. 123 , 124 , 125 , 126 , 127 , 128 , 129

Pharmacology

Vormatrigine's activity profile in animal models of seizures and epilepsy, other pharmacological properties and mechanism of action have been described in the EILAT XVII progress report. 1

Toxicology

A comprehensive nonclinical toxicology package has been completed, which included toxicity studies in rats (up to 6 months of dosing) and dogs (up to 9 months of dosing), along with a pre‐carcinogenicity range‐finding study in mice up to 28 days duration. A full genetic toxicology assessment with in vitro and in vivo assays, and phototoxicity studies were negative.

Safety pharmacology assessments including cardiovascular, respiratory, and CNS function were performed, including a dedicated EEG study in dogs. Reproductive and developmental toxicity evaluations, including a combined male and female fertility study in rats and embryo–fetal development studies in rats and rabbits, were also completed.

Overall, vormatrigine demonstrated a favorable and well‐characterized nonclinical safety profile across all studies. Observed findings were generally dose‐dependent, monitorable, and occurred at exposures exceeding those anticipated to be effective clinically. Of note, safety margins were broader than those reported for the recently approved ASM cenobamate. 130 These data provide robust support for continued clinical development.

Pharmacokinetic profile

Vormatrigine's pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profile was previously described in the EILAT XVII progress report 1 and has been further characterized through additional Phase 1/2 studies, and interim population pharmacokinetic analyses.

Following single oral dosing (5 to 45 mg), C max increased less than proportionally at the highest dose, whereas AUC0‐inf values increased in an approximately dose‐proportional manner across the evaluated range. 125 , 131 Geometric mean half‐life values in healthy subjects range from 45 to 66 h, consistent with q.d. dosing. After repeated q.d. dosing of 20, 30, and 45 mg for 10 days, AUC0‐tau increased proportionally across dose levels, with apparent oral clearance values at steady state (CL/F) of 7.0 to 7.7 L/h (geometric means) and no evidence of time‐dependent changes in elimination. Accumulation with q.d. dosing was moderate and predictable, with mean AUC accumulation ratios of ~2.6 to 2.8 and near steady state achieved by Day 7 to 9. At the highest evaluated dose of 45 mg, q.d., steady‐state plasma C max values were ~2000% of the human‐equivalent mouse MES‐EC50 (~60 ng/mL) while remaining well tolerated, demonstrating the ability to achieve plasma exposures exceeding preclinical efficacy benchmarks with acceptable tolerability.

In an open‐label, crossover design food effect study, 16 healthy subjects received two single 30 mg doses in the fasted and fed states (in random order), separated by a 7‐day washout. 131 Dosing with food had no clinically significant impact on C max or AUC. Modeling and simulation with a population pharmacokinetic model of the effect of fed/fasted states on drug exposure at steady state showed that a modest decrease in fed C max is within the bioequivalence range. The effectiveness and safety profile of vormatrigine are thus likely to remain unchanged, regardless of whether it is taken with or without respect to food.

The plasma protein binding of vormatrigine is ~97%, based on in vitro studies. Vormatrigine is eliminated primarily by hepatic metabolism. In in vivo studies conducted in animal and human samples, unchanged vormatrigine was a minor component in urine. Consistent with these findings, vormatrigine was undetectable in urine in rat radioprofiling studies.

Drug interactions

Effect of other drugs on vormatrigine pharmacokinetics

The PRAX‐628‐102 Phase 1 study in healthy adults evaluated how vormatrigine pharmacokinetics is affected by co‐administration with commonly used metabolic inhibitors: fluvoxamine (CYP1A2/2C19 inhibitor); itraconazole (CYP3A4 inhibitor); and probenecid (non‐selective UGT inhibitor). 132 Across all study parts, 43 subjects were enrolled and dosed. Subjects received first a single 10 mg dose of vormatrigine in Period 1 (reference) followed by a 9‐day washout. In Period 2, probe drugs were administered as follows: fluvoxamine for 17 days (100 mg, q.d., with protocol‐defined reduction to 50 mg, q.d., later in the dosing period), itraconazole for 13 days (200 mg, q.d., following a loading dose), and probenecid for 12 days (1000 mg, b.i.d.). The second 10 mg dose of vormatrigine was given after multiple doses of fluvoxamine, itraconazole, and probenecid treatment, in accordance with the predefined study design for each probe drug. Vormatrigine pharmacokinetics were evaluated using non‐compartmental analysis with linear mixed‐effects modeling.

Co‐administration with fluvoxamine (n = 17) increased plasma vormatrigine exposure by ~17% based on C max and by 30% based on AUC, suggesting that CYP1A2 is involved in vormatrigine metabolism and that strong CYP1A2 inhibitors have a weak effect on vormatrigine exposure. Co‐administration with itraconazole had no significant impact on plasma vormatrigine AUC, indicating that CYP3A4 has no major role in vormatrigine clearance. A reduction in Cmax was observed, consistent with a transient effect on the rate rather than on the extent of absorption. This finding is most likely attributable to gastrointestinal interaction with components of the itraconazole formulation (such as cyclodextrin) and is not expected to be clinically meaningful. Probenecid did not affect plasma vormatrigine exposure, suggesting that vormatrigine is not significantly metabolized by UGTs.

Interim comparisons of plasma exposures between healthy subjects and patients enrolled in the RADIANT study (n = 65) suggest that vormatrigine pharmacokinetics is consistent across populations. Following 30‐mg dosing, Day 1 vormatrigine plasma exposures in patients were comparable to those observed in healthy subjects, and steady‐state plasma exposures in patients fell within the range predicted from healthy subject data. The most frequently used concomitant ASMs in RADIANT included levetiracetam, lacosamide, lamotrigine, and cenobamate. Vormatrigine plasma exposures in the presence of these background ASMs were comparable to those observed in healthy subjects receiving the same doses. Collectively, these findings support the use of healthy volunteer pharmacokinetic data to inform exposure projections at higher doses and suggest no meaningful impact of concomitant ASMs on vormatrigine pharmacokinetics.

Effect of vormatrigine on the pharmacokinetics of other drugs

Preclinical studies indicated that vormatrigine has a low metabolic turnover in human hepatocytes, with minor involvement of CYP1A2 and CYP3A4 and UGT‐mediated pathways in its clearance. In in vitro tests conducted with human CYPs, weak inhibition of CYP2D6 and intestinal CYP3A was observed only at supratherapeutic concentrations (≥7.37 μM), with no evidence of time‐dependent inhibition. Transporter interaction risk was considered minimal, with some in vitro inhibition of BCRP and P‐gp not expected to be clinically relevant. Based on available evidence, vormatrigine exhibits a favorable drug–drug‐interaction profile. To date, no clinically meaningful interactions have been observed with major CYP and UGT pathways, and preliminary Phase 2 experience 129 suggests that vormatrigine can be combined with commonly used ASMs without the need for dose adjustments.

Efficacy data

The Phase 2 RADIANT study was an open‐label, single‐arm, multicenter trial (NCT06908356) designed to evaluate the efficacy, safety, and pharmacokinetics of vormatrigine in adults with focal seizures or generalized tonic–clonic seizures. 128 , 129 Participants 18–75 years of age received vormatrigine 30 mg, q.d., for an initial 8‐week treatment period, following a prospective baseline observation phase of up to 4 weeks. Based on local regulations and site availability, some participants continued treatment beyond 8 weeks. The primary efficacy endpoint was median percent change in monthly (28 days) seizure frequency from baseline to treatment period, with secondary efficacy endpoints including the proportion of subjects experiencing seizure freedom, defined as 100% reduction in seizure frequency from baseline during consecutive 28‐day periods.

Sixty‐two of the patients included in the study had focal seizures, with a median baseline 28‐day seizure frequency of 9.0 (interquartile range [IQR] 4, 21). In these patients, vormatrigine treatment was associated with a rapid, consistent, and sustained reduction in seizure frequency, with an overall median seizure reduction of 54% across the initial 8‐week treatment period. 129 Further weekly analyses including participants at sites participating in the OLE showed that median seizure reduction improved steadily, reaching a median reduction of ~100% at Week 8, and remaining at ~100% from Week 10 through Week 16 (n = 26 at Week 16). Fifty‐eight percent of patients achieved at least a 50% reduction in seizures by Week 1, increasing to 61% by Week 8 and 70% by Week 16. Three patients with generalized epilepsy included in the cohort also showed a rapid and durable seizure reduction.

Seven of the 62 patients with focal seizures (11.3%) experienced seizure freedom throughout the entire 8‐week treatment period, and approximately one third of patients achieved seizure freedom for at least 28 consecutive days. Greater reductions in seizure frequency were observed at Week 8 in patients receiving 1 or 2 background ASMs compared with patients receiving ≥3 concomitant ASMs, whereas clinically meaningful seizure reduction was maintained across increasing background ASMs and sodium channel blocker burden. These findings support the anti‐seizure activity of vormatrigine and its potential utility as both an adjunctive therapy and in monotherapy settings. 129

Adverse effects

Vormatrigine's preliminary safety profile was described in the EILAT XVII report. 1 Building on previously reported findings from the Phase 1 PRAX‐628‐101 study, vormatrigine has continued to be generally well tolerated at all doses evaluated to date, including single doses up to 45 mg and multiple doses up to 45 mg, q.d., administered for 10 days. 126 , 131 Cardiac safety assessments demonstrated no clinically meaningful changes in ECG parameters and no dose‐related trends. 126

Recent data from the Phase 2 RADIANT study further support a favorable tolerability profile, with low rates of TEAEs. 128 , 129 The majority of TEAEs were mild to moderate in severity and transient in nature. CNS‐related events were the most frequently reported, primarily dizziness (32%), headache (14%), and somnolence (12%). All severe and serious TEAEs resolved. Sixteen patients (25%) discontinued treatment before completing the 8‐week treatment period, 14 (22%) of whom were due to TEAEs.

Together with updated results from the PRAX‐628‐101 study, demonstrating that vormatrigine can be safely dosed in healthy participants at exposures greater than 20‐fold, the human equivalent of the rodent MES‐EC50, these data support a wide therapeutic window and are consistent with the potential for improved tolerability relative to current ASMs.

Planned studies

Plasma exposure response modeling based on data from the RADIANT study supports meaningful seizure reduction across a range of doses, validating the study designs and dosing regimens in the ongoing vormatrigine development program. Praxis has completed recruiting for the POWER1 pivotal study (NCT06999902) in adults with focal seizures, enrolling ~230 participants 18–75 years of age taking 1–3 ASMs. Participants were randomized 1:1 to receive vormatrigine (20 mg, q.d., for 6 weeks followed by 30 mg, q.d., for 6 weeks) or matching placebo over a 12‐week treatment period, with safety follow‐up or option to enter an OLE. Topline results are expected in the first half of 2026.

POWER2 (NCT07505004), the second registrational study of vormatrigine in adults with focal seizures, has been initiated, with enrollment expected to complete in the second half of 2026, and topline results expected in 2027. The study is a randomized, placebo‐controlled trial (n ≈ 400) evaluating doses of 20 mg, 30 mg, and 40 mg, q.d., over 12 weeks, followed by safety follow‐up or entry into an OLE. Clinical data from the POWER1 and POWER2 studies, if successful, would serve as the basis for an NDA submission in 2027.

The POWER3 study to evaluate vormatrigine as monotherapy is expected to initiate in the first half 2026. It will assess conversion to vormatrigine monotherapy in patients with refractory focal seizures receiving 1–2 concomitant ASMs, including a 4‐week downtitration of background ASMs, with subsequent safety follow‐up or OLE.

ZOREVUNERSEN

Carolyn Reever1, Marija Geertsen2, Ivan Bozin2, Kimberly A Parkerson3

1Biogen, Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA; 2Biogen, Baar, Switzerland; 3Stoke Therapeutics, Bedford, Massachusetts, USA

Introduction and rationale for development

Dravet syndrome (DS) is a genetic DEE caused predominantly by heterozygous LoF pathogenic variants in the SCN1A gene. The resulting reduction in neuronal voltage‐gated sodium channel (Nav1.1) protein disrupts inhibitory interneuron firing and establishes a lifelong imbalance between excitation and inhibition. Although several ASMs are now approved for DS, patients continue to experience frequent major motor seizures, cognitive and behavioral impairment, and significant functional limitations. 133 Because available therapies primarily target seizures and not the genetic cause, there is a clear need for disease‐modifying strategies.

Zorevunersen is an investigational ASO engineered to increase productive SCN1A transcripts and restore endogenous Nav1.1 protein expression. More details can be found in the previous EILAT progress report. 3

Pharmacology

Activity profile in animal models of seizures and epilepsy

In the Scn1a+/− mouse model of DS, a single i.c.v. dose of zorevunersen at postnatal Day 2 or D14 markedly increased Nav1.1 protein in the brain. 134 Protein levels approached those measured in wild‐type animals and remained elevated for more than 3 months following treatment. In the same model, zorevunersen reduced susceptibility to spontaneous seizures and significantly improved survival by reducing mortality due to SUDEP. These effects were durable and maintained across several post‐treatment timepoints, indicating that the restored protein expression translated into meaningful functional rescue. 133 , 134 The combined data demonstrate that reinstating Nav1.1 expression can counteract spontaneous seizure activity and substantially improve survival outcomes. 134

Mechanism of action

Zorevunersen employs targeted augmentation of nuclear gene output (TANGO) technology to enhance SCN1A mRNA levels from the functional gene copy. 134 , 135 The ASO binds to a regulatory region of SCN1A pre‐mRNA, increasing productive mRNA to near normal levels that can be translated into functional Nav1.1 protein.

Raising Nav1.1 levels in inhibitory interneurons could support more reliable GABAergic firing, thereby reducing network hyperexcitability. Because interneuron impairment drives seizures and contributes to broader neurological dysfunction in DS, restoring Nav1.1 could produce benefits beyond seizure control. 3 , 133

Toxicology

A full non‐clinical toxicology program—including single‐dose, sub‐chronic, and long‐term studies in nonhuman primates—has been completed. More details of the findings can be found in the EILAT XVI progress report. 133

Pharmacokinetic profile

A large, multi‐compartment nonhuman primate pharmacokinetic model for zorevunersen was adapted for children with DS and accurately predicted plasma, CSF, and extrapolated brain concentrations. In patients, observed pharmacokinetic parameters closely matched model simulations, and higher simulated brain exposure was associated with greater seizure reduction. 3 , 136 In the Phase 1/2a studies, zorevunersen levels in plasma increased with increasing dose, and zorevunersen levels in CSF were detectable for up to 6 months after the last dose. In the OLE studies, zorevunersen levels in CSF increased with each dose administered for the first year before nearly reaching steady‐state levels. 137

Drug interactions

Interactions of zorevunersen with drugs metabolized by CYP enzymes are not expected at the metabolic level. 3 , 133

Efficacy data

The clinical efficacy profile of zorevunersen is supported by data from two completed Phase 1/2a studies (MONARCH [US, NCT04442295]; ADMIRAL [UK, 2020–006016‐24]) together with long‐term, ongoing observations from the SWALLOWTAIL (US, NCT04740476) and LONGWING (UK, 2021‐005626‐14) OLE studies. These trials enrolled children and adolescents 2–18 years of age with genetically confirmed DS, who remained highly refractory despite receiving multiple concomitant ASMs. Across the Phase 1/2a program, 81 patients received at least one dose of study drug, with the majority receiving ≥3 ASMs at baseline. The most common concomitantly administered ASMs were clobazam (70%), fenfluramine (49%), cannabidiol (44%), and valproic acid (44%). Their median baseline major motor seizure frequency was high (17 per 28 days, n = 77), reflecting the severe and persistent disease burden characteristic of this population. 137

Zorevunersen was administered intrathecally, in doses ranging from 10 mg to 70 mg, with patients receiving either a single dose or multiple doses depending on cohort assignment. Patients completing these trials were eligible to enter the OLE studies, where dosing transitioned to a maintenance regimen of up to 45 mg every 4 months. Nearly all (75/81, 92.6%) enrolled participants continued in the OLEs, providing a robust longitudinal dataset now extending up to 4.5 years of cumulative follow‐up and with more than 800 doses administered as of November 2025. 137 , 138

Consistent reductions in major motor seizure frequency were sustained through Month 36 of the OLEs, with the greatest decreases in patients who received at least one 70 mg loading dose before transitioning to long‐term maintenance dosing (n = 15–17, depending on the duration of follow‐up over a period up to 36 months). Reductions in median major motor seizure frequency per 1‐month (28‐day) intervals ranged from 58.82% to 90.91% from naive baseline through Month 20 of the OLEs in patients who received at least one 70 mg loading dose followed by 45 mg maintenance doses (n = 15–17). 137 The clinical relevance of these reductions is underscored by comparisons with natural history trajectories. Propensity score‐weighted analyses leveraging data from the BUTTERFLY study 139 demonstrated clear divergence between zorevunersen‐treated patients (two 70 mg loading doses +45 mg once every 4 months, n = 5) and natural history patients, with the treated cohort showing significantly lower convulsive seizure rates by Month 6 and sustained until Month 24. 138

In addition to seizure outcomes, improvements across multiple functional domains were assessed using Vineland‐3. 3 The Vineland‐3 is a standardized assessment used to measure adaptive behavior (everyday functional skills such as communication, daily living skills, socialization and, optionally, motor skills and maladaptive behaviors) across individuals from early childhood through adulthood. It is administered through parents or caregiver interviews and is used widely to diagnose and plan interventions for developmental and intellectual disabilities, including comorbidities such as autism spectrum disorder. Results of the Phase1/2a and their respective OLE studies revealed substantial improvements in the expressive and receptive communication, interpersonal relationships, play and leisure, and personal, coping, and motor skills subdomains of the Vineland‐3. These improvements were observed as early as 12 months into the OLE studies and continued through 24 and 36 months for patients with longer follow‐up. 137 , 138 Propensity score‐weighted comparisons with the BUTTERFLY natural history study demonstrated that patients treated with zorevunersen (2 doses 70 mg or 3 doses 45 mg in Phase 1/2a and 45 mg every 4 months in the OLEs) showed significantly greater improvements across multiple Vineland‐3 adaptive behavior domains over time. Improvements were seen in cognition and behavior domains at both 18 and 24 months from naive baseline, contrasting with the plateauing observed in natural history cohorts. 138 , 139

Overall, patients treated with zorevunersen on top of current standard of care experienced substantial and durable reductions in major motor seizure frequency as well as improvements in cognition, behavior, and communication. Propensity score‐weighted analyses of the high‐dose cohort were significantly different from those observed in the natural history cohorts with respect to convulsive seizure rates and multiple Vineland‐3 subdomains. These results support ongoing Phase 3 evaluation.

Adverse effects

Across both Phase 1/2a and OLE programs, patients were treated up to 4.5 years. 138 As of May 30, 2025, no new safety concerns emerged since the previous EILAT report. 3 Thirty percent of patients (24/81) experienced a TEAE considered related to study drug in the Phase 1/2a studies, with the most frequent being CSF protein elevation (13.6%, 11/81 patients; defined as ≥1 CSF protein value >50 mg/dL) and procedural vomiting (4.9%, 4/81 patients). 137 , 138

In the OLE studies, CSF protein elevation occurred in 86.1% of patients (62/72 patients with at least one post‐baseline CSF protein value in the OLE studies) yet was reported as a treatment‐related TEAE in only 44.0% of patients. There were no reports of hydrocephalus, and one patient discontinued treatment because of CSF protein elevation. 137 , 138 One patient had experienced suspected unexpected serious adverse reactions (SUSARs) attributed to zorevunersen after receiving three doses in the 70‐mg cohort. 137 During the program, two patients died due to SUDEP and one due to malnutrition; all deaths were assessed unrelated to zorevunersen. 137 , 138 The overall safety profile supported advancement into the Phase 3 trial.

Planned studies

Zorevunersen is currently being evaluated in the global Phase 3 EMPEROR study (NCT06872125). This is a randomized, double‐blind, and sham‐controlled trial, enrolling patients with DS 2 to <18 years of age with confirmed SCN1A variants. 140 EMPEROR is designed to evaluate changes across multiple outcomes, including seizures, cognition, and behavior, to understand the impact of zorevunersen as a potential disease‐modifying therapy. 140 Approximately 70 sites globally are planned for participation in the United States, UK, European Union, Japan, and China.

Eligible participants are assigned randomly to receive either zorevunersen or sham control during an initial 52‐week treatment period (Treatment Period 1). Participants randomized to zorevunersen in Treatment Period 1 will receive two intrathecal 70 mg loading doses followed by two 45 mg maintenance doses. Regardless of initial treatment assignment, all participants will receive zorevunersen during a 48‐week Treatment Period 2, consisting of either three 45 mg maintenance doses or two 70 mg loading doses followed by a single 45 mg maintenance dose for those randomized to zorevunersen or sham procedures in Treatment Period 1, respectively. The primary endpoints for Treatment Period 1 are the percent change from baseline in major motor seizure frequency from Week 16 to Week 28 (Europe‐specific) and the change from baseline in log‐transformed major motor seizure frequency at Week 28 (non–Europe‐specific). Key secondary endpoints include percent change from baseline in major motor seizure frequency from Week 16 to Week 52 (Europe‐specific), change from baseline in log‐transformed major motor seizure frequency at Week 52 (non‐Europe‐specific), as well as functional outcomes using the Vineland‐3 scale across multiple subdomains at Week 52. 140 Patients who complete the study may enroll in a long‐term extension.

CONCLUSIONS

The treatments reviewed in this article encompass a wide range of chemical entities, from novel small molecules (bexicaserin, BMB‐101, ES‐481, radiprodil, RAP‐219, relutrigine, seletracetam, vormatrigine) to ASOs (elsunersen, zorevunersen), gene therapies (ETX‐101), and compounds previously approved for other indications (clemizole, alprazolam) and being repurposed for the management of seizures and epilepsy. These treatments also show a remarkable diversity of mechanisms of action, including interactions with novel targets as well as greater selectivity when traditional targets are involved.

Two of the 13 treatments discussed are rescue medications for seizure emergencies, with significant innovations in term of a non‐BZD mode of action in the case of PrevEp‐006 (seletracetam) and pulmonary route of administration for Staccato Alprazolam. Of the remaining 11 compounds, 8 are being developed for the treatment of DEEs, and only 3 (ES‐481, RAP‐219, and vormatrigine) have common (focal) epilepsies as their primary indication. This shift in targeted indications confirms a trend that has been ongoing for the last decade, as shown by the fact that of six ASMs approved by the FDA since 2018, five (cannabidiol, everolimus, ganaxolone, fenfluramine, and stiripentol) target orphan indications (mostly DEEs) and only one (cenobamate) has focal seizures as its primary indication. The increased focus on DEEs has been facilitated at least in part by an improved understanding of the pathophysiology of these disorders, 141 , 142 , 143 which is a prerequisite for the development of precision treatments directed at the underlying etiology. 144 , 145 , 146 In line with some preliminary findings presented in this article, many of the treatments targeting the etiology or the specific mechanisms of the disease have the potential to have direct beneficial effects not only on seizure control but also on non‐seizure outcomes. Some of these treatments also bear promise for a disease‐modifying effect, that is, a positive impact on the natural course of the disease. To what extent will this promise be fulfilled, and the conditions under which disease modification can be achieved (e.g., choice and modalities of treatment, age at treatment onset, etiology types) will become clear as results from ongoing and future trials become increasingly available. Of note, the design of studies in this area is fraught with methodological challenges, from optimal patient selection to choice of control group, blinding procedures, outcome measures, duration of follow‐up and strategies to control for the influence of confounders. 147 , 148 , 149 , 150 , 151 , 152 , 153 , 154

Overall, the findings presented at EILAT XVIII show that treatment development in epilepsy is thriving. As many as 21 different treatments were included in the 2026 EILAT progress report (which comprises an accompanying article on compounds in earlier development), 2 compared with 14 in 2024, 1 , 3 12 in 2022, 133 , 155 and 13 in 2020. 156 , 157 The growing number of investigational agents being evaluated is a welcome development, particularly because these treatments generally target severe drug‐resistant epilepsies where unmet therapeutic needs are greatest. Promising results from clinical studies are already available, with data in some cases being generated in the setting of randomized controlled trials.

Some limitations inherent in the methodology used in the preparation of progress reports need to be acknowledged. EILAT reports are limited to treatments for which sponsors are willing to provide a summary for review and publication. Specifically, of 37 sponsors known to the EILAT XVIII organizing committee to be involved in the development of investigational epilepsy treatments, 25 accepted the invitation to present at the EILAT XVIII Conference but ultimately an extended summary for inclusion in this report was provided for only 21 compounds. Although we cannot claim that these fully reflect the current state of epilepsy treatment development, at least for agents in clinical development, the overall scenario as highlighted in this article is highly consistent with that emerged from recent more comprehensive reviews of the epilepsy development pipeline. 146 , 158 , 159 , 160 , 161 An important added consideration is that summaries included in the progress report are largely based on information provided by sponsors, and the possibility of reporting bias should be taken into consideration. Some of the findings presented are entirely novel and some had been reported previously, mostly in the form of press releases or congress presentations, but only a small proportion of these data has been published before in peer‐reviewed journals. Members of the EILAT Organizing Committee interacted intensively with sponsors to ensure that the presented information was adequately supported by data, while accepting that the level of detail disclosed could be limited by the need to protect the sponsors' proprietary rights. Despite these limitations, we are confident that this report provides a valuable glimpse into ongoing efforts aimed at bringing improved therapies to market. Drug development is a high attrition process, particularly in the CNS area, 162 but the results reviewed justify the expectation that in the next few years the therapeutic armamentarium for epilepsy will be further expanded, with added benefit for the millions of people with severe epilepsies unresponsive to current medications.
