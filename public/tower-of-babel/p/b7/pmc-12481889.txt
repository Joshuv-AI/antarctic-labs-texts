Effect of fasting duration on mortality after COVID-19 infection: A retrospective analysis at a single institution
Authors: Naoko Takaku, Koji Hayashi, Hiromi Hayashi, Toyoaki Miura, Kouji Hayashi, Yasutaka Kobayashi, Mamiko Sato, Ippei Sakamaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Fasting duration was independently associated with mortality in post-COVID patients.•Longer fasting and older age were associated with higher mortality risk.•Cognitive decline was observed in all patients who died of COVID-19.•Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) and care level were not independent predictors of COVID-19 mortality.•Avoiding prolonged fasting may improve outcomes during COVID-19 rehabilitation.Keywords: COVID-19, Fasting duration, Rehabilitation, Mortality, Dysphagia, Nutritional support

Introduction

COVID-19 is a viral infection caused by SARS-CoV-2.1 The main symptoms of COVID-19 include fever, cough, myalgia, and fatigue.2 In some cases, the infection primarily presents as respiratory symptoms caused by viral pneumonia.3 The severity of symptoms varies from asymptomatic and mild to severe and requiring treatment in the intensive care unit (ICU).4 Patients with moderate to severe COVID-19 experience dysphagia or difficulty swallowing; 3 %–62 % of patients develop dysphagia after severe illness, which is associated with longer hospitalization and increased risk of reintubation, pneumonia, and mortality.5 Dysphagia is considered a complication of COVID-19, with an increased risk of developing aspiration pneumonia after infection.

Fasting can also exacerbate swallowing disorders. A prolonged fasting duration in patients with sepsis can potentially lead to swallowing dysfunction.6. Fasting is often necessary in the treatment of aspiration pneumonia,7 and the longer the duration of fasting, the more difficult it becomes to recover swallowing function. A previous study reported that prolonged fasting during the treatment of aspiration pneumonia can negatively affect the treatment duration, with a decline in swallowing function and recovery of this function taking up to 4.8 ± 4.9 days.8

Some studies have examined factors influencing clinical outcomes in patients with COVID-19, such as age, comorbidities, nutritional status, and rehabilitation interventions.9, 10, 11 However, the impact of fasting during COVID-19 and its subsequent effects on patient outcomes remain unclear. Although studies on aspiration pneumonia and community-acquired pneumonia suggest that the fasting duration affects swallowing function and outcomes, few studies have specifically focused on COVID-19. Additionally, evaluation of the swallowing function in these patients is lacking. Thus, the effect of the fasting duration on patients' swallowing ability and nutritional status remains unclear.

In this study, we aimed to analyze the relationship between the duration of fasting and subsequent outcomes in hospitalized patients with COVID-19.

Materials and methods

Patients

In this study, we included patients who underwent post-COVID-19 rehabilitation treatment (hereinafter, rehabilitation) at our facility (Fukui General Hospital) between March 1, 2021 and June 30, 2023. We excluded patients who were transferred to other hospitals owing to worsening COVID-19 symptoms. Additionally, following our facility's protocol, patients requiring oxygen therapy were transferred to other institutions, thereby limiting the study population to patients with mild COVID-19.12

Data collection

Patient data were retrospectively collected from the medical records of individuals registered with post-COVID-19 syndrome, code U09, according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10 (ICD-10).13 Study participants were categorized into two groups: those who were admitted to our hospital specifically for rehabilitation after COVID-19 and those who had contracted COVID-19 while hospitalized for other conditions. For the former group, data were extracted from the time of transfer to our facility; for the latter group, data were taken from the time of infection so as to maintain consistency.

The extracted variables included age at admission and age at COVID-19 onset (ICD-10 code U071), sex, albumin levels at admission or disease onset, fasting duration from the time of admission or disease onset, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) at admission or onset,14 Revised Hasegawa Dementia Scale score,15 required level of long-term care at admission, total length of hospital stay, time from disease onset, time to initiation of speech therapy (ST), time until death, and ability to consume food at both onset and discharge (including the presence or absence of alternative feeding methods).

The mortality group included patients who received end-of-life care; those who were registered as deceased at the time of data extraction were classified into this group. The timing of death included deaths occurring during hospitalization, after discharge, and after transfer to another facility. The hospitalization period was defined as the duration of stay at our facility. For patients transferred to our hospital for post-COVID-19 rehabilitation, the first day of hospitalization was set as Day 1. For those who contracted COVID-19 during hospitalization for other conditions, the day of disease onset was set as Day 1.

Cognitive level

The Revised Hasegawa Dementia Scale cutoff value was set at 20 points, with scores ≤ 20 indicating cognitive decline. Alternative feeding methods for food intake included enteral nutrition via a gastrostomy or nasogastric tube; intravenous fluid administration alone was classified as an inability to consume food.

Care level

The required level of long-term care was determined according to Japan's long-term care insurance system in which insurance, welfare, and health care experts assess the degree of care required.16 Care levels are broadly categorized into “required support” and “required care” and are further divided into eight stages: Support Level 1, Support Level 2, and Care Levels 1–5, along with “independent” (non-applicable). Support levels indicate lower care needs than care levels, and higher numerical values correspond to a greater decline in physical function. In this study, levels were converted to points, as follows: independent = “0″, Support Level 1 = “1″, Support Level 2 = “2″, Care Level 1 = “3″, Care Level 2 = “4″, Care Level 3 = “5″, Care Level 4 = “6″, and Care Level 5 = “7″.

Statistical analysis

Fisher's exact test was used to analyze categorical variables. For continuous variables, we assessed normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and checked for homogeneity of variance using the F-test. If the data followed a normal distribution with equal variance, a two-sample t-test was conducted. If the data were not normally distributed, the Mann–Whitney U test was performed.

To examine the association between the fasting duration with COVID-19 infection and outcomes, we performed logistic regression analysis using outcome (survival vs. death) as the dependent variable. Also, logistic regression analysis was performed to examine whether the fasting duration, categorized into two groups according to the number of days, was associated with clinical outcomes. Based on the typical treatment duration of 5 days for hospitalized patients with COVID-19, the fasting duration was classified into two groups: ≤ 5 days and > 5 days. Age, CCI, and required care level were included as independent variables in the adjustment. Multicollinearity was ruled out by ensuring a variance inflation factor < 3 for all independent variables.

A post hoc power analysis was conducted using R version 4.4.1 with the “pwr” package. This analysis was performed to yield estimates based on the observed effect sizes, assuming a Type I error rate (α) of 0.05 and a two-tailed t-test. Effect sizes were specified as Cohen's d for t-tests and Cohen's w for chi-square tests, based on the observed sample sizes. The post hoc power values obtained are reported in the Results section. Statistical analyses were performed using EZR (version 1.54; Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Number of patients

During the study period, 103 patients underwent post-COVID-19 rehabilitation at our hospital. After excluding one patient that met the exclusion criteria, 102 patients were included in the analysis. Among them, 71 patients were classified into the survival group and 31 patients were classified into the mortality group (Fig. 1).

Patient outcomes of the 103 individuals initially enrolled in the study, one patient was excluded. Among the remaining 102 participants, 71 survived and 31 died during the study period.

Patients characteristics

The characteristics of patients in the survival and mortality groups are shown in Table 1. No significant differences were observed between the groups regarding sex (p = 0.52), time to ST intervention (p = 0.28), total length of hospital stay (p = 0.173), or length of hospitalization from disease onset (p = 0.102). By contrast, age (p < 0.001), cognitive impairment (p < 0.001), and fasting duration (p < 0.001) were significantly different between the two groups. Fasting duration was significantly longer in the mortality group, with a median of 11.00 days (interquartile range [IQR] 1.65, 25.50), compared with 0.00 days (IQR 0.00, 0.00) in the survival group. In most patients, fasting began on the same day as admission or symptom onset; fasting that newly developed after a certain period following admission or onset occurred in very few patients. Notably, the prevalence of cognitive impairment was 56.3 % in the survival group and 100 % in the mortality group. CCI, which reflects medical comorbidity, was statistically significant (p = 0.014). By contrast, the level of care required, which reflects physical function, was significantly higher in the mortality group, with a median of 5.00 (IQR 4.00, 6.00), as compared with 3.00 (IQR 0.00, 5.00) in the survival group (p < 0.001).

Multivariate analysis

Multivariate analysis was conducted among 101 patients, after excluding one patient with missing values. The results identified fasting duration (p = 0.00826, odds ratio [OR]: 1.06, 95 % confidence interval [CI]: 1.01–1.11) and age (p = 0.0377, OR: 1.07, 95 % CI: 1.00–1.05) as significant independent factors (post hoc power = 0.99) (Table 2). The sample size was 102, and the exclusion of one patient owing to missing data was considered to have a limited impact on the results, as the statistical power remained sufficient (≥ 0.80).

Discussion

The findings of this study suggested that in patients with COVID-19, both age and fasting duration are independently associated with mortality. Furthermore, analysis based on dichotomized fasting duration indicated that a fasting duration of more than 5 days may have a significant impact on patient outcomes, particularly mortality. A study on aspiration pneumonia found that patients with a longer fasting duration (4.8 ± 4.9 days) had a prolonged treatment duration and decreased swallowing function.8 Examination of the effects of fasting on swallowing function in older patients with community-acquired pneumonia revealed that those with persistent dysphagia had a significantly longer fasting duration than the group without dysphagia.17 Although the impact of fasting duration has been considered in conditions such as aspiration pneumonia and community-acquired pneumonia, no prior studies have specifically examined fasting duration in the context of COVID-19. Consequently, the influence of fasting and its association with patient outcomes in COVID-19 remains unclear. Therefore, the present study represents an important contribution, addressing the relationship between COVID-19 and fasting duration.

From 2021 to 2023, patients with COVID-19 in Japan were required to undergo a 10-day isolation period. During hospitalization, infection control measures limited direct dietary interventions by speech therapists and other professionals, possibly leading to prolonged fasting of more than 5 days. This extended fasting duration may have caused a disuse-related deterioration in swallowing function, further prolonging fasting and ultimately influencing patient outcomes. Based on the present findings, initiating oral intake within 5 days and early rehabilitation intervention by a speech therapist may be important for improving mortality outcomes following COVID-19 infection.

COVID-19 has been reported to cause a 5 %–10 % reduction in body weight over approximately 2 weeks, negatively affecting nutritional status.18,19 It has been suggested that requiring nursing care is not an independent factor associated with COVID-19, although this is expected to be associated with frailty and sarcopenia. Also, the development of COVID-19 could further deteriorate the patient's general condition, making adequate oral intake more difficult.20 By contrast, when we dichotomized the fasting duration, care level was identified as an independent factor associated with prolonged fasting of more than 5 days. In univariate analysis, the level of care dependency was significantly higher in the mortality group, which may be related to cognitive decline. In the present study, we found that the prevalence of cognitive decline in the mortality group was 100 %.

The CCI has been investigated in relation to COVID-19 outcomes. Studies have shown that compared with a CCI score of 0, scores of 1–2 and ≥ 3 are associated with increased mortality and poor prognosis.21 The same study found that each one-point increase in the CCI score increased the mortality risk by 16 %, and higher average CCI scores were significantly correlated with increased mortality and disease severity.21 Comoglu and Kant also reported a positive correlation between CCI scores and mortality in patients with COVID-19, with each one-point increase in the CCI score raising the mortality risk by 2.5 %.22 A CCI score ≥ 4 was found to predict mortality, with 87.2 % sensitivity and a 97.9 % negative predictive value.22 Although the CCI appears to be a useful score for estimating COVID-19-related mortality, this was not identified as an independent risk factor in our study. Several factors may explain why this score did not show a significant association. The primary reasons include reduced statistical power owing to an insufficient sample size, differences in the study population, and the stronger influence of other factors. Comorbidities are known to contribute to the risk of severe COVID-19.9, 10, 11 However, because this study focused exclusively on mild cases, the impact of the CCI score may not have been prominently observed.

In the present study, we only assessed the presence or absence of nutritional intake, without evaluating specific nutrients. Several studies have investigated the relationship between COVID-19 and nutritional factors. For instance, multiple reviews and observational studies have suggested that deficiencies in essential vitamins and minerals are associated with impaired immune function, which may increase susceptibility to infections and worsen the clinical course of COVID-19.23, 24, 25, 26, 27 In particular, adequate intake or supplementation of vitamin D has been clinically associated with reduced disease severity and a lower risk of hospitalization.24,26 These findings suggest that deficiencies in specific nutrients may also influence clinical outcomes, highlighting the need for future research focusing on fasting duration and nutritional aspects.

This study has several limitations. First, owing to sample size constraints, only a limited number of independent variables could be included in the analysis. A larger sample size would allow for a more comprehensive investigation of the risk factors related to mortality outcomes. Owing to the small sample size, there is a high risk of statistical errors. Subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and adjustments for multiple confounding factors were not performed; therefore, potential confounding factors may not have been adequately addressed. Although it would be ideal to stratify or examine the interaction effects of factors such as age, comorbidities, cognitive function, and baseline nutritional status on clinical outcomes, the limited sample size restricted the number of variables that could be included in the multivariable analysis, making such investigations difficult. Therefore, the generalizability of the present findings to specific high-risk populations is limited, and further validation in larger-scale studies is warranted. Second, because this study only targeted patients with mild COVID-19 infection who were treated at our facility, the findings may not be generalizable to all patients with COVID-19. Third, there was an age difference between the survival and mortality groups. The mean ± standard deviation age was 80.14 ± 13.61 years for the survival group and 89.10 ± 6.96 years for the mortality group, indicating that we primarily analyzed order patients. Thus, the present findings may be applicable only to older populations. The treatment for COVID-19 has undergone considerable changes over the study period. In Japan, favipiravir28 was used initially, but antibody preparations and mainstream antivirals29 have since been approved. Many patients received some form of treatment, but the heterogeneity of treatments owing to timing could not be controlled. Therefore, medications were not analyzed in this study. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that medications may have potentially affected the study outcomes. This study was conducted exclusively in a Japanese population, which may limit generalizability of the findings to individuals of other racial or ethnic backgrounds. Further studies in more diverse populations are warranted to confirm the applicability of these results across different ethnic groups. Finally, the assessment of swallowing function was insufficient. Our study suggests that efforts should be made to minimize unnecessary fasting. However, further investigation that incorporates swallowing function assessment is required. Patients who underwent swallowing function evaluation that includes numerical assessment, such as the Repetitive Saliva Swallowing Test30,31 or Hyodo Score32, had substantial missing data and could not be included in the analysis. Moreover, we did not conduct objective assessments using imaging modalities, such as videofluoroscopic swallowing studies or fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing, as well as standardized scales or biomarkers. These limitations may have affected the validity of the study's primary outcomes. Future research incorporating standardized and objective assessments of swallowing function is needed to enable more precise investigation and enhance clinical applicability. To address the above limitations, further validation using a larger sample size is necessary. Additionally, to clarify the impact of fasting duration on outcomes across all patients with COVID-19, study designs should include patients with mild infection, as well as moderate and severe infection. Furthermore, to examine swallowing function, it is essential to establish a system in advance, in collaboration with STs, to enable the assessment of swallowing function in all patients with COVID-19.

One question emerging from this study is whether the significantly higher prevalence of cognitive decline in the mortality group affected oral intake ability and prognosis. Further research examining outcomes based on cognitive decline is warranted.

In conclusion, this study is the first to investigate the relationship between COVID-19 infection and fasting duration. Our findings suggested that the fasting duration during treatment is a significant factor associated with mortality in patients with COVID-19. Furthermore, a fasting duration of more than 5 days may be strongly associated with mortality. Therefore, early nutritional intervention through rehabilitation within 5 days after disease onset or hospital admission is likely related to improved prognosis in patients with COVID-19. Although this study provides evidence suggesting an association between COVID-19 infection and fasting duration, further evidence is needed in larger, multicenter prospective studies to clarify causality and validate the clinical utility of the present findings.
