Experience in the Treatment of Male Prolactinomas: A Single-Center, 10-Year Retrospective Study
Authors: Xiaoan Ke, Xiaoxue Chen, Linjie Wang, Lian Duan, Hongbo Yang, Yong Yao, Kan Deng, Hui Pan, Fengying Gong, Huijuan Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMale prolactinomas are uncommon and typically macroadenomas with difficult treatment and management. The purpose of this study was to summarize the treatment and management experiences of 254 male prolactinoma patients at a single center.MethodsThis was a 10-year retrospective study conducted at a single center. A total of 254 male prolactinoma patients were included. Clinical data for all subjects were collected using an electronic medical record system.ResultsA total of 254 male patients with prolactinoma were studied. Their median age at onset was 28.8 years, and median disease duration was 28.5 months. The median PRL levels were 582.0 ng/mL at diagnosis. Their median maximum tumor diameter was 23.0 mm, with macroadenoma accounting for the majority (76.7%). After treatment, the biochemical remission rate with monotherapy was 36.6%, but significantly increased to 60.6% with multidisciplinary treatment (p < 0.001). Knosp 0–2 patients had significantly higher rates of biochemical remission compared to Knosp 3–4 (all p < 0.05). In addition, the maximum diameter of adenoma (B = −0.110, p = 0.008) and cavernous sinus invasion (B = −1.741, p = 0.023) were negatively correlated with postoperative biochemical remission. The maximum diameter of the adenoma (B = − 0.131, p < 0.001) was a negative correlation factor, while treatment duration (B = 0.034, p = 0.002) was a positive correlation factor for biochemical response to medication.ConclusionMale prolactinoma has a low biochemical remission rate when treated alone, but multitherapy can improve it even more. Surgery may also be considered for male prolactinoma with a micro, and noninvasive tumor after a thorough evaluation.

Introduction

Prolactin-secreting pituitary adenomas (prolactinomas) are the most common functional pituitary adenomas, accounting for approximately 53% of all functional pituitary adenomas [1, 2], while prolactinoma in men is relatively uncommon [3]. The female-to-male incidence ratio is estimated to be 5:1 to 10:1 between the ages of 20 and 50 years, whereas the incidence of unselected prolactinoma is similar in both sexes after the fifth decade [2]. The average age of diagnosis is 35–50 years in men and 28–44 years in women [3]. Male prolactinomas often were macroprolactinomas while diagnosed.

Furthermore, prolactinomas are generally more aggressive in men than in women, with increased proliferation (as measured by Ki-67), cellular atypia, angiogenic and proliferative characteristics, and greater invasiveness [4]. Men have already been more likely to develop pharmacological resistance [5]. In World Health Organization 2017 Classification of Pituitary Tumors, lactotroph adenomas in men were classified as “high-risk” pituitary adenomas [6]. Therefore, male prolactinoma is difficult to treat and manage in clinical practice. However, there is a scarcity of large-sample clinical studies and guidelines for treating and managing for male prolactinoma patients, both domestically and internationally. As a result, we retrospectively summarized the therapeutic approaches and patient management of 254 male prolactinoma cases in a single center over 10 years to provide evidence for clinical practice.

Subjects and Methods

Subjects

A retrospective study was performed. This study included a total of 254 male prolactinoma patients in Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH) between June 2012 and April 2022. The diagnosis of prolactinoma met the following criteria (1) through (3) [4], or there was a previous diagnosis of prolactinoma: (1) clinical symptoms, such as hypogonadism, headache, vision loss, and breast development; (2) hyperprolactinemia (>200 ng/mL); and (3) pituitary MRI indicating a pituitary adenoma. Patients with hyperprolactinemia (<200 ng/mL) and pituitary microadenoma who experience tumor shrinkage and lower serum PRL levels after DAs treatment may have prolactinoma. Patients diagnosed with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1), McCune-Albright syndrome, mixed somatotroph-lactotroph tumors, or drug-induced hyperprolactinemia were excluded.

The treatment strategy was as follows: the initial dose of bromocriptine was 0.625–1.25 mg/day, which was increased by 1.25 mg every 2 weeks until it reached 5 mg/day or 7.5 mg/day 2–3 times orally. Cabergoline was initially administered at a weekly dose of 0.25–0.5 mg, gradually increasing to 0.25–0.5 mg monthly. The maximum tolerated dose for each patient was determined by titrating the dose based on changes in serum prolactin levels and tumor size on pituitary MRI, as well as the maximum therapeutic dose of bromocriptine or cabergoline. Some patients who were resistant to bromocriptine were treated with cabergoline (if available). Surgical treatment was chosen if the following criteria were met: drug resistance, drug intolerance, refusal to take medication, cerebrospinal fluid rhinorrhea, pituitary apoplexy, and other acute complications. Biochemical remission was defined as biochemical control with prolactin levels below the normal range (below 25 ng/mL) [7] (Beckman Coulter Access 2). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of PUMCH (K24C1963).

Data Collection

Medical records of all subjects were documented, including the chief complaint, current history, clinical manifestations, past medical history, vital signs, physical examination, laboratory examinations, and imaging information. The vital signs were sex, age, height, weight, blood pressure, and heart rate. All subjects had laboratory tests during the follow-up, to determine their anterior pituitary hormone levels, liver and kidney function, and blood lipids. The maximum diameter of tumor and the Knosp grade of the pituitary MRI were also recorded. Anterior pituitary hormones were prolactin (PRL), luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone, testosterone, thyroid-stimulating hormone, free tri-iodothyronine, free thyroxine, adrenocorticotropic-hormone, cortisol, growth hormone (GH), and insulin-like growth factor 1.

Diagnostic Criteria

Hypopituitarism was diagnosed in the manner previously described [8]: hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (HH) was identified in the following situations: (1) serum testosterone levels were less than 10.4 nmol/L (3 ng/mL) with low or low-normal serum LH and follicle-stimulating hormone levels (<10 IU/L) [9]; (2) for boys over 14 years, the peak LH level was below 4 IU/L (indispensable tests for delayed or absent puberty) after gonadotropin-releasing hormone analog (triptorelin) stimulation tests [10]. GH deficiency was diagnosed with a peak serum GH level below 5 µg/L in both insulin tolerance tests and l-arginine tests or serum insulin-like growth factor 1 levels below the lower normal reference range matched for age and sex, coupled with other pituitary hormone deficiencies in pituitary lesions [11]. Secondary glucocorticoid deficiency was suspected when cortisol concentration was <80 nmol/L at 8:00 and was confirmed when the peak serum cortisol response was lower than 497.5 nmol/L in an insulin-induced hypoglycemia (<40 mg/dL) test [11]. Secondary hypothyroidism was defined as a serum-free thyroxine level below the lower reference range combined with a low or normal thyroid-stimulating hormone level in the presence of pituitary or hypothalamic disease [11].

Patients with polydipsia and polyuria should consider diabetes insipidus. Diagnoses of vasopressin deficiency (central diabetes insipidus) were based on an inappropriately high serum osmolality (>295 mOsm/kg) with the corresponding urine osmolality lower than 2.5 times of serum osmolality and a high serum sodium (children and adolescents: >143 mmol/L; adults: >145 mmol/L) in water deprivation tests, with polyuria and hypotonic urination improving after the administration of vasopressin [12, 13].

DAs resistance was defined as patients with prolactinoma treated with adequate DAs (daily dose of bromocriptine is ≥15 mg, weekly dose of cabergoline is ≥2 mg) for at least 3 months, and serum PRL levels did not fall to normal and/or tumor shrinkage below 50% [7].

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was carried out with SPSS.25 software. All charts created using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 software. Nonparametric tests are used for comparing continuous variables. χ2 and Fisher tests were used to compare rates across groups. Kendall bivariate correlation analysis was used to investigate the relationship between biochemical remission from medication or surgery alone and other variables. Stepwise bivariate logistic regression analysis was used to investigate the factors that influence remission following surgery or medication alone. p < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. CMH statistics were used to compare the incidence of complications following surgery.

Results

Basic Information of Male Patients with Prolactinoma

The study included 254 male patients with prolactinoma, as shown in Table 1. The median age at onset and diagnosis was 28.8 and 30.0 years, respectively. Male prolactinoma was most commonly diagnosed between the ages of 20 and 30 years, followed by 30 and 40 years (Fig. 1). The median diagnosis duration was 3.2 months (range: 0.0–300 months), and disease duration was 28.5 months (range: 9–60 months). The median prolactin level at diagnosis was 582.0 ng/mL, while the median testosterone level was 1.3 ng/mL. The median maximum tumor diameter in these patients was 23.0 mm, with the majority being macroadenomas (76.7%, 188/245). The most common clinical manifestations were sexual dysfunction (41.7%), visual impairment (35.8%), headache (35.0%), and gynecomastia (18.9%). For male children and adolescents, growth retardation is a distinct clinical manifestation.

Furthermore, patients were divided by treatment they received: “surgery as the first-line treatment” and “medication as the first-line treatment.” The first-line treatment groups were surgery (103 participants) and the medication (151 participants). Compared to the initial surgery group, patients in the initial medication group had a younger age of onset (median: 27.4 vs. 31.6 years, p = 0.015) and a longer median disease duration (36.0 vs. 18.0 m, p < 0.001). However, patients in the initial medication treatment group had lower prolactin levels at diagnosis compared to those in the initial surgery group (median: 470.0 vs. 963.0 ng/mL, p = 0.004). In terms of adenoma size, the proportion of patients with microadenoma was higher in the medication as the first-line treatment group was higher than that in surgery as the first-line treatment group (20.9% vs. 3.1%, p < 0.001), while the proportion of macroadenoma was lower than in the surgery as the first-line treatment group (68.2 vs. 89.7%, p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in proportion between tumors with Knosp 3–4 and Knosp 0–2. The proportion of adenoma with cavernous sinus invasion (33.8 vs. 51.5%, p = 0.005), optic chiasmatic compression (21.9 vs. 49.5%, p < 0.001), and cystic adenoma (6.0 vs. 21.4%, p < 0.001) in medication as the first-line treatment group were significantly lower compared to that of surgery as the first-line treatment group.

Treatment and Biochemical Remission Rates in Male Patients with Prolactinoma

Table 2 and Figure 2 show the treatment and biochemical remission results for all subjects. Medication was administered to 151 patients as a first-line treatment (Fig. 2). Fifty-six patients responded to DAs. Furthermore, 8 of them underwent surgery for DAs resistance or refusal to take long-term medication. Six of them achieved postoperative remission, and one was prescribed medication, which resulted in biochemical remission. Of those who were not in remission after one type of DAs, 33 switched to another, and 8 achieved remission with medication alone. Furthermore, 72 patients who received medication initially underwent surgical treatment after DAs, primarily due to DAs resistance (23 cases, 31.9%), intolerance (14 cases, 19.4%), and refusal of long-term drug therapy (7 cases, 9.7%). The postoperative biochemical response rate was 50.0% (36/72). Of those who were not in remission after surgery, 6 out of 14 achieved final biochemical remission, accounting for 42.9%. The serum PRL levels at the final follow-up were significantly decreased after surgical debulking subsequently with medical therapy (19.1 [17.0, 28.5] vs. 58.5 [12.6, 116.8] ng/mL, p = 0.035). In conclusion, the biochemical remission rate at the last follow-up was 60.9% (92/151) in male prolactinoma treated with medication.

Hundred three patients underwent surgery as a first-line treatment (Fig. 2). Surgery was the primary treatment for 51 (49.5%), 20 (19.4%), and 6 (5.8%) patients who refused medication, had pituitary apoplexy, or had a tumor recurrence. As shown in Figure 2, 36.9% (38/103) of patients experienced immediate biochemical remission following surgery. Therefore, the biochemical remission rate at the final follow-up was 60.2% (62/103) in the surgery as the first-line treatment group.

Subgroup Analysis of Biochemical Remission Rate in Male Patients with Prolactinoma

Table 2 shows the difference in biochemical response rates between the surgery and the medication first-line groups. The biochemical response rates were 28.2% (29/103) and 42.4% (64/151) for those who received only surgery and medication, respectively. There was no discernible difference in biochemical response rates between the two groups. The biochemical response rate for Knosp grade 0–2 in surgery as the first-line group was 82.9% higher than in medication as the first-line group (72.7%), but there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups. Furthermore, the biochemical response rate of Knosp grade 0–1 in the surgery as the first-line group was higher than in medication as the first-line group (89.5 vs. 71.4%, p = 0.133), but no significant difference was found between the two groups.

In terms of Knosp grade (Fig. 3), patients with Knosp 0–2 had significantly higher biochemical remission rates at the last follow-up than those with Knosp 3–4 in total (75.9 vs. 36.6%, p < 0.001), surgery as the first-line group (82.9 vs. 28.6%, p < 0.001), and medication as the first-line group (72.7 vs. 41.9%, p = 0.001). Table 2 shows that the biochemical remission rates for microadenomas, macroprolactinoma, and giant prolactinoma were 76.5% (26/34), 59.0% (111/188), and 54.5% (12/22), respectively (p < 0.001). However, there was no significant difference in biochemical remission rates across adenoma sizes (p = 0.128). In this study, 31.8% (7/22) of patients with giant adenoma who received medication alone experienced biochemical remission, which increased to 54.5% (12/22). Although there was no significant difference, the biochemical remission rate of giant adenoma for medication alone was higher than for surgery alone (37.5 vs. 16.7%).

Biochemical remission rates in male prolactinoma with different Knosp grades.

The biochemical response rate among all subjects receiving monotherapy was 36.6% (93/254). Ninety-eight patients underwent additional multidisciplinary treatment, and biochemical remission significantly increased to 60.6% (154/254). Furthermore, additional multidisciplinary treatment significantly improved biochemical remission rates in the first-line surgery group (60.2% vs. 28.2%, p = 0.001) and the first-line medication group (60.9% vs. 42.4%, p = 0.001). Therefore, multidisciplinary treatment can significantly increase the biochemical response rate of male prolactinoma.

Correlation Analysis of Biochemical Remission after Receiving Medication or Surgery Alone

Furthermore, factors associated with biochemical remission through medication or surgery alone were investigated. A total of 147 patients receiving bromocriptine were included. In Table 3, bivariate correlation analysis revealed that treatment duration (r = 0.421, p < 0.001) and total dose (r = 0.216, p = 0.023) were positively correlated with biochemical remission after bromocriptine treatment, while adenoma size (r = −0.203, p = 0.012), maximum diameter of adenoma (r = −0.294, p = 0.001), cavernous sinus invasion (r = −0.177, p = 0.032), Knosp grade (r = −0.311, p = 0.001), PRL level at diagnosis (r = −0.208, p = 0.018), and maximum dose of bromocriptine (r = −0.303, p < 0.001) were negatively correlated factors. Furthermore, bivariate logistic regression analysis showed that the maximum diameter of adenoma (B = −0.131, p < 0.001) was a negative independent influencing factor, while treatment duration (B = 0.034, p = 0.002) was a positive independent influencing factor of biochemical remission with bromocriptine alone. The findings remained unchanged when BMI and age were included as independent variables (Table 4).

Comorbidities and Complications of Treatment

82.6% (176/213) of male adult prolactinoma patients had HH, with median testosterone levels of 1.3 ng/mL at diagnosis. Among male prolactinoma patients with normal serum PRL levels after treatment, 42.1% (56/133) still had HH, with only 12 (21.4%, 12/56) receiving testosterone replacement therapy. At the last follow-up, the median testosterone levels of all male adult prolactinoma were significantly increased to 2.6 ng/mL, while the percentage of patients with HH (50.7%, 108/213) was significantly decreased (50.7% vs. 82.6%, p < 0.001). Furthermore, 34.3% (87/254) of all subjects had visual impairment at diagnosis, which had improved in 42.5% (37/87) of patients at the most recent follow-up visit.

In this study, 175 male patients with prolactinoma underwent surgery, with 36 patients having tumor residual after surgery, accounting for 20.8% (Table 5). The incidence of postoperative anterior hypopituitarism was 21.7% (38/175), while the incidence of permanent diabetes insipidus was 4.0% (7/175). Only 1 patient had cerebrospinal fluid rhinorrhea. The effects of preoperative medication on postoperative residuals and complications were studied further. All patients were divided into two groups: those who underwent surgery and those who received medication after the surgery. The tumor residual rate was 21.8% (22/101) in the direct surgery group and 19.4% (14/72) in the medication followed surgery group, with no significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.709). The rate of postoperative anterior hypopituitarism was significantly higher in the direct surgery group than in the medication-assisted surgery group (30.1 vs. 9.7%, p = 0.001). Despite adjusting for adenoma size, there was a significant difference in the incidence of postoperative anterior hypopituitarism between the two groups (p < 0.001), particularly in patients with macroadenoma (25.3% vs. 8.3%, p = 0.009). There was no significant difference in the incidence of postoperative anterior hypopituitarism between the two groups based on the Knosp grade (p = 0.209). Meanwhile, the rates of permanent diabetes insipidus were 3.9% (4/103) in the direct surgery group and 4.2% (3/72) in the medication-assisted followed surgery group, respectively (p = 0.925). In addition, 11.9% (18/151) of patients receiving medication as a first-line treatment reported side effects, such as headache, dizziness, nausea and vomiting, pituitary stroke, and cerebrospinal fluid rhinorrhea.

Discussion

Dopamine agonist drugs (DAs) are the most common treatment for prolactinoma and mainly include bromocriptine and cabergoline [14]. The biochemical remission rates of male prolactinoma with bromocriptine treatment varied from 65% to 100% [15–20]. Delgrange found that PRL levels normalized in 65% (26/40) of male prolactinoma cases, with 25% macroprolactinoma [15]. Also, 8 male prolactinoma patients treated with bromocriptine achieved biochemical remission at their last visit, as previously reported [16]. Another study found that 70% of male prolactinoma patients achieved biochemical remission (PRL <575 mU/L) with eight macroprolactinoma [17].

In this study, biochemical remission was lower at 42.4% (64/151) with medication alone, but increased to 60.9% (92/151) with multitherapy. First, male prolactinoma is typically associated with macroprolactinoma and frequently presents as invasive [4]. As previously stated, the normalization rate of serum PRL with bromocriptine was lower in macroadenomas than in microadenomas [21]. In this study, the proportion of macroprolactinoma and giant prolactinoma was high (86.1%), which could lead to a lower biochemical remission rate. Second, previous studies used different criteria of biochemical remission, such as less than 575 mU/L [17] and less than 30 μg/L [22], which may have contributed to higher remission rates. In this study, a stricter serum PRL threshold of 25 ng/mL was used, which may result in lower remission rates. Furthermore, this study was the largest cohort of male prolactinoma patients treated with bromocriptine as primary DAs, so the results may be more objective.

Furthermore, normalized PRL levels were achieved in 51.9–87.0% of female prolactinomas treated with DAs, as previously reported [15, 23–25], which was higher than the rate of male prolactinoma in this study (42.4%). Thus, the biochemical remission rate in male prolactinoma patients receiving medication was lower in this study.

Surgery is the secondary treatment for prolactinoma in men. Success rates vary depending on tumor size and invasiveness, ranging from 0 to 83% [26]. In this study, the biochemical remission rate of male prolactinomas with surgery alone was 28.2% (29/103) and that of male prolactinomas with additional multimodal therapy was 60.2% (62/103), both of which were lower than previously reported [22, 27, 28]. In the study (macroadenoma accounting for 82%) by Andereggen et al. [22], prolactin levels were normalized (<30 µg/L) in 47% and 80% of male prolactinoma patients in a surgical cohort at early (3 months) and late follow-ups (>12 months), respectively. Postoperative PRL normalized (<13 ng/mL) in 52.0% (57/174) of male prolactinomas with 78.3% suprasellar or parasellar adenoma in a different study [27]. Also, the PRL levels in 13 (59.1%, 13/22) male prolactinoma patients (macroadenoma and giant adenoma accounting for 31.8%) returned to normal within 2 months of surgery [28].

As previously stated, the proportion with 86.1% (211/245) of macroadenoma and giant adenoma in this study was higher than previous studies [22, 27, 28]. Also, nearly half of the patients had invasive procedures, with 40.9% (104/254) being cavernous sinus invasion and 39.1% (72/184) being Knosp 3–4. As we all known, the invasiveness and size of adenoma are negatively correlated with surgical results [3]. Thus, the higher proportion of macroadenoma and invasiveness may have contributed to the lower biochemical remission observed in this study. Furthermore, our study’s lager sample size and stricter criteria of biochemical remission (below 25 ng/mL) contribute to a lower biochemical remission rate. Therefore, the biochemical remission following surgical treatment was lower than in previous studies and more multicenter studies are needed to confirm the findings.

In recent years, surgery for prolactinomas has proven to be particularly effective in small and noninvasive tumors, making it a viable first-line treatment option in such patients [29]. However, regardless of tumor size or Knosp grade, there was no significant difference in biochemical remission rates between the surgical treatment as first-line and medication as first-line groups in this study (p all >0.05). Nonetheless, we observed that when compared to Knosp 3–4, the biochemical remission rate of Knosp 0–2 preferred surgery tended to increase (82.9% vs. 72.7%, p = 0.341). Also, Knosp 0–1 demonstrated a biochemical remission rate of up to 89.5%.

Abou-al-Shaar et al. [30] found that Knosp 0–2 (83.8%) had a significantly higher postoperative short-term remission rate than Knosp 3 (58.8%) and Knosp 4 (41.7%), which was consistent with our findings. Our previous study found that intrasellar adenoma was a predictor of remission (p < 0.05) and had a significantly higher initial remission rate (87.5%, 35 cases) [27]. Similarly, in our study, maximum adenoma diameter (B = −0.110, p = 0.008) and cavernous sinus invasion (B = −1.741, p = 0.023) were independent negative predictors of postoperative biochemical remission. In summary, postoperative outcomes of male prolactinoma were strongly related to the size and invasiveness of adenoma.

In addition, medication for prolactinoma had a low discontinuation rate, ranging from 0% to 37.5% [31]. Additionally, medication as a first-line treatment resulted in a significantly longer treatment duration (median: 36 vs.18 m, p < 0.001) in this study, indicating the importance of high compliance. Furthermore, a higher rate (51.0%) of DAs side effects was previously reported by us [32]. In short, medication treatment usually lasts for a long time with a low withdrawal rate, requires high patient compliance, and frequently results in adverse drug reactions. Therefore, DA treatment was still the first treatment for males prolactinoma, and surgery may be an option for those with micro and noninvasive adenoma (Knosp 0–1 and without a cavernous sinus invasion.

Multidisciplinary approaches, such as surgery and radiotherapy, as well as continuous medical treatment, are an important in the treatment and management of prolactinoma, particularly in male patients [14, 26]. In a study with a mean follow-up of 9 years, only 1 patient with giant prolactinoma was able to discontinue DA treatment, and PRL returned to normalize in 7/16 (43.7%) patients with multimodal treatment [33]. Additionally, irrespective of initial tumor size, a response rate of 84% in male patients with medical treatment and further improvement of 91% following multimodal therapy were reported by Rudman et al. [34]. Similar to previous studies [14, 26, 33, 34], patients with monotherapy (surgery or medication alone) had a remission rate of 36.6% (93/254) and significantly increased to 60.6% (154/254) with multimodal treatment (p < 0.001) in this study. Thus, multidisciplinary treatment can significantly improve the biochemical remission rate of male prolactinoma.

Furthermore, we found that 31.8% (7/22) of prolactinoma patients with giant adenoma who received medication alone achieved biochemical remission, which increased to 54.5% (12/22) after multimodal therapy. Several studies found that the normalization rate of PRL in giant prolactinomas treated with multimodal therapy ranged between 44% and 65% [33, 35–38], which was consistent to our findings. Thus, multidisciplinary therapy was required for the treatment and management of male prolactinoma, particularly giant adenoma.

Men with prolactinomas often experience hypogonadism, which may require testosterone replacement therapy if persisted after 3–6 months of normal prolactin levels [26]. In this study, 82.6% (176/213) of male adult prolactinoma patients had HH when they were diagnosed. As previously reported, the LH-testosterone axis recovered in 26.7% of men with macroprolactinoma who achieved normal prolactin on cabergoline monotherapy [39]. A total of 15 male prolactinoma patients (53.6%, 15/28) had normalized testosterone levels in a study by Shimon et al. [40]. Another study found that 84% of male prolactinomas had HH at diagnosis, but only 17% had recovered of their gonadal axis at the most recent follow-up [41]. Consistent with the above, in male prolactinoma with normal prolactin levels, HH persisted in 42.1% (56/133) of them with only 12 undergoing testosterone replacement therapy. Therefore, the gonadal axis should be evaluated and male prolactinoma replacement therapy administered during the follow-up.

Our study summarized the experience of treating and managing 254 male prolactinomas in a single center, but it has several limitations in this study. First, the findings need to be confirmed in a large multicenter study. Second, the subjects’ clinical data were partially missing, resulting to the failure of comprehensive analysis; further prospective research and more complete data are required.

Conclusion

This large single-center retrospective study found a biochemical remission rate of 60.6% in male prolactinoma patients. Mono-treatment has a low biochemical remission rate, but multidisciplinary therapy can improve it even more. In addition, surgery may be considered as the first option for male prolactinoma with micro- and noninvasive tumor after a thorough evaluation.
