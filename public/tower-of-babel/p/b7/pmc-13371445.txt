Gender differences in relationships among parenting dimensions, disordered eating, and psychological factors in adolescents: a network analysis
Authors: Mengting Wu, Yunshu Zhang, Ruijiao Liu, Di Wu, Mengdi Zhou, Ping Zhang, Zewei Wang, Kejin Ma, Bing Li, Lili Zhang, Yanmei Guo, Jiashuang Qin, Yingda Huang, Jie Huang, Mingyao Qu, Yunyan Luo, Jue Chen, Keqing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDisordered eating (DE) is prevalent among adolescents and often linked to parenting dimensions. Proximal psychological factors such as self-esteem, depression, anxiety, and sleep quality are associated with DE. However, the interplay between parenting dimensions and these psychological factors remains unclear. This study used a network approach to examine how parenting dimensions are associated with proximal psychological factors in relation to DE in Chinese adolescents, and to explore potential gender differences.MethodsA total of 5148 adolescents aged 12–20 were included in the analysis. Network analysis was employed to construct basic networks of parenting dimensions and DE, and extended networks incorporating proximal psychological factors. Bridge strength indices were calculated to identify bridge nodes. Network comparison tests were used to explore gender differences in network structure and strength.ResultsIn the basic networks, maternal rejection showed the strongest association with DE symptom “self-induced vomiting”. Gender differences were observed in the basic network, with boys’ DE symptoms linked to both maternal and paternal parenting dimensions, while girls’ DE symptoms were more associated with maternal parenting dimensions. When psychological factors were included, self-esteem and sleep quality emerged as key bridge variables connecting parenting dimensions and DE. Gender-specific patterns were observed: in boys, self-esteem was the primary bridge node, whereas in girls, sleep quality was the strongest bridge node. No significant global differences were found between gender networks, but several edge variations revealed distinct gender-specific associations.DiscussionParenting dimensions are closely linked to adolescents’ disordered eating through psychological factors such as self-esteem and sleep quality. Gender-specific associations suggest that prevention efforts should focus on both parental influences and self-esteem for boys, and maternal influences and sleep quality for girls.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40337-026-01641-7.Keywords: Disordered eating, Parenting dimensions, Network analysis, Adolescents, Sleep quality, Self-esteem

Background

Disordered eating (DE) refers to a range of inappropriate attitudes, behaviors, and perceptions about eating and body weight [1], such as dieting to lose weight, binge eating, or eliminating calories through self-induced vomiting, misuse of laxatives or diuretics, and/or excessive exercise [2]. In China, the prevalence of DE has been increasing over the past decade [3]. Although DE does not meet the diagnostic threshold of eating disorders (ED), it is often considered a precursor to ED [4] and is highly prevalent among adolescents [5]. Research generally indicates that girls are at higher risk for DE than boys [6]. Understanding the risk factors for DE in adolescents, and how these factors may differ by sex, is critical for early prevention and intervention.

Parents play a critical role in shaping children’s eating-related attitudes and behaviors [7]. Children’s dietary habits, physical activity, body satisfaction, and eating-related cognitions are influenced by the quality and pattern of parenting behaviors [8, 9]. The general pattern of parenting behaviors and attitudes has been characterized into some key parenting dimensions [10], such as emotional warmth and control. Building on these dimensions, researchers have also classified parenting styles such as authoritative, authoritarian, and permissive [11]. A growing body of evidence has demonstrated associations between parenting dimensions and DE. Low parental responsiveness to children is associated with the development of various ED diagnoses [12], and adverse parenting characterized by high levels of control and low levels of responsiveness or warmth is associated with a range of DE symptoms in youth [13]. Longitudinally, adolescents with authoritarian mothers showed a higher probability of engaging in extreme weight control behaviors and binge eating five years later compared to those with authoritative or permissive mothers [14].

Parenting serves as a foundational factor in children’s development, and it may exert a distal influence on DE via some proximal psychological factors. The transdiagnostic theory of eating disorders proposes self-esteem and mood intolerance as two maintaining mechanisms [15]. Self-esteem is an individual’s subjective evaluation of his or her worth as a person [16], and low self-esteem can drive eating disorder individuals to pursue achievement in the domain of weight and shape [15]. Lower self-esteem has often been associated with higher levels of DE [17, 18]. Intolerance to negative emotional states like depression and anxiety can serve as a proximal trigger for DE behaviors, such as binge eating [15]. Studies have also demonstrated that depression and anxiety can significantly impact eating behaviors [19–21]. In addition, sleep quality and eating patterns are highly related, empirical findings suggest that delayed and unstable sleep phases are associated with irregular eating patterns [22]. Sleep disturbances are associated with both DE symptoms and body dissatisfaction [23].

Existing research suggests that the association between parenting and DE is often indirect rather than direct [13], with parenting operating distally through proximal psychological factors. For example, prior studies have shown that this association may be mediated by emotional distress [24], self-competence [25], and heightened emotional reactivity [26]. These indirect pathways have typically been examined using mediation models or structural equation modeling approaches. However, these models rely on a priori specification of paths among a limited set of variables, which may constrain the ability to capture the interconnected nature of these factors. As a result, it remains unclear how parenting dimensions, psychological factors, and DE symptoms associate within a broader system, and which pathways play a relatively more central role in linking these domains.

Network analysis provides a promising approach to address these gaps by examining the interrelationships among variables at a more integrated level. Rather than imposing a priori pathways, network analysis simultaneously estimates pairwise associations among all variables, allowing the full pattern of associations to emerge from the data. It is a novel approach that conceptualizes mental disorders as the result of associations between symptoms, forming a disease symptom network consisting of nodes (symptoms) and edges (relationships between symptoms) [27]. Bridge symptoms are defined as key nodes that strongly connect two distinct symptom clusters, facilitating the spread of symptoms across two disorders [28]. Compared to traditional mediation models or structural equation modeling approaches, bridge centrality allows for the identification of specific variables that connect symptom clusters without requiring predefined pathways. The bridge centrality can quantify the relative importance of each variable in connecting different clusters in the network. These bridge symptoms may play a critical role in the development of comorbidity by increasing the risk of one disorder triggering symptoms shared with other disorders. Bridge symptoms are often considered potential targets for intervention.

There has been a growing number of studies that use network analysis to investigate core symptoms of eating disorders and their comorbidity with other psychiatric disorders [29]. Several studies have examined the comorbidity networks of eating disorders with anxiety and depression, and identified the bridge symptoms such as “suicide”, and “low self-confidence” [30, 31]. To date, no studies have explored how parenting dimensions associate with proximal psychological factors in relation to DE through network analysis.

Therefore, this study first applied the network approach to investigate how proximal psychological factors are linked to parenting dimensions and DE in a large sample of Chinese adolescents at risk for DE. Furthermore, given that DE is significantly more prevalent in girls than in boys, and that the influence of parenting dimensions on DE has been shown to differ by gender [32], this study also separately examined the networks for boys and girls. The study hypothesizes that parenting dimensions are associated with DE through specific bridge nodes, and these patterns may vary between boys and girls.

Method

Participants and procedures

Participants aged 12–20 were recruited by convenience sampling from six schools across three districts of Hebei Province. The participating schools were identified and contacted through collaboration with local education authorities and school administrators. The survey was conducted as part of a school-based mental health and eating behavior screening program. All participants volunteered to take part in the survey after receiving a clear explanation of the study’s purpose, and the participants and their guardians provided informed consent. No financial or material incentives were offered for participation. All surveys were checked by researchers, and samples with excessively short response times or significant missing data were excluded. The project was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Hebei Provincial Mental Health Center (No.202335). The survey was conducted between October to December 2023. A total of 5169 questionnaires were distributed and 5148 valid responses were collected.

Measures

Demographic characteristics

Demographic characteristics included gender (male/female), age, weight, height, residence (urban/rural), grade (junior high/senior high), monthly household income, and whether the participant is an only child (yes/no).

Disordered eating

Disordered eating was measured by the Sick, Control, One, Fat, Food Questionnaire (SCOFF) in the study. The SCOFF is a brief screening tool for identifying potential eating disorders [33]. The scale consists of 5 yes/no questions addressing the core symptoms of eating disorders: Self-induced vomiting after feeling full (“Do you make yourself Sick because you feel uncomfortably full?”), Loss of control (“Do you worry you have lost Control over how much you eat?”), Weight loss (“Have you recently lost more than One stone in a 3 month period?”), Feeling fat (“Do you believe yourself to be Fat when others say you are too thin?”), and Food dominates life (“Would you say that Food dominates your life?”). With the exception of the weight loss item, which specifies a three-month period, the remaining items do not define a fixed recall period, consistent with the original scale design. Responses are scored as “yes” = 1 and “no” = 0. A score of ≥ 2 on the SCOFF scale is commonly used as a cut-off indicating a positive screen for a potential eating disorder [33]. The Chinese version of the scale has demonstrated acceptable reliability [34]. Given that the SCOFF includes only five dichotomous items, relatively low internal consistency is expected, as Cronbach’s α is sensitive to both the number of items and restricted response variance [35]. The α coefficients reported in previous studies ranged from 0.44 to 0.57 [34], which is comparable to the present study (α = 0.574).

Short form of Egna Minnen Betraffande Uppfostran (s-EMBU)

The s-EMBU [36] was used to assess participants’ perceived parental rearing behaviors. The questionnaire includes both father and mother versions, each containing 21 items scored on a 4-point Likert scale (“never” = 1 to “always” = 4). Each version includes three dimensions: emotional warmth (e.g., “My parents praise me”), rejection (e.g., “My parents get angry with me without letting me know the reason”), and overprotection (e.g., “I wish my parents would worry less about what I am doing”). Emotional warmth represents a positive parenting dimension, while rejection and overprotection represent negative parenting dimensions. The s-EMBU has demonstrated good reliability and validity among Chinese adolescents [37]. In this study, all participants completed both the paternal and maternal versions. Scores for each dimension were computed as the average of the corresponding items. In this study, the α coefficients for the paternal and maternal versions were 0.732 and 0.786, respectively.

Proximal psychological factors

Proximal psychological factors include self-esteem, depressive and anxiety symptoms, and sleep quality. Self-esteem was measured by the Rosenberg Self-esteem Scale (SES) which consists of 10 items with a 4-point scale [38]; an example item is “On the whole, I am satisfied with myself ”. In this study, we excluded the item “I wish I could have more respect for myself.”, due to its ambiguity in the Chinese context [39]. This resulted in the total scores range from 9 to 36 with higher scores indicating higher self-esteem. The α coefficient in this study was 0.878. Depressive symptoms were assessed by Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [40], with 9 items rated on a 4-point scale; an example item is “Feeling tired or having little energy”. Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) [41], which contains 7 items rated on a 4-point scale; an example item is “Feeling nervous, anxious, or on edge”. Both PHQ-9 and GAD-7 have demonstrated good reliability and validity in Chinese adolescents [42, 43], with α coefficient of 0.910 and 0.941 in this study. Sleep quality was measured by the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [44], a widely used self-report questionnaire to evaluate sleep quality; an example item is “During the past month, how often have you had trouble sleeping because you cannot get to sleep within 30 minutes”. The PSQI consists of 18 items grouped into seven components. Each component is scored according to the original PSQI guidelines [44], yielding a score ranging from 0 to 3. The PSQI global score (range: 0–21) was computed as the sum of the seven component scores, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality. The α coefficient of PSQI in this study was 0.731.

Statistical analysis

Network estimation

All network models were analyzed using R software (version 4.3.1). First, the basic networks were established only for parenting dimensions and DE to investigate the direct relationship between parenting dimensions and DE. This network consisted of 11 nodes: 6 dimensions of parenting and 5 items of SCOFF. To explore the bridge function of other proximal psychological factors, the total scores of PHQ-9, GAD-7, SES, and PSQI were incorporated into the basic network to create an extended network. This extended model included 15 nodes: 6 nodes for parenting dimensions, 4 nodes for the depression, anxiety, sleep quality and self-esteem, 5 nodes for the SCOFF. All networks were developed for the whole samples and separately for boys and girls.

As the data included both continuous variables and binary variables from the SCOFF questionnaire, we estimated the network using the Mixed Graphical Model (MGM) via the R package ‘mgm’. Both the basic and extended networks were estimated by the Mixed Graphical Model (MGM) using the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC) and regularized via the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) method to ensure sparsity and interpretability of the network [45]. The R package “qgraph” was used to visualize the networks. In the networks, green edges represent positive correlations, red edges represent negative correlations, and the thickness of the edges indicates the strength of the correlation. The R package “networktools” was employed to estimate bridge strength. The bridge strength indicates a node’s total connectivity with all nodes from other communities [28]. The communities in the network represent the variables of interest: parenting dimensions, disordered eating, and other psychological variables (self-esteem, depressive and anxiety symptoms and sleep quality).

Network stability

The stability of the networks was calculated using bootstrapping procedures with 1000 iterations. A case-dropping bootstrapping procedure was conducted to calculate the correlation stability coefficient (CS-coefficient) of both bridge strength and strength centrality. According to the guidelines of Epskamp [46], stability coefficients above 0.5 are considered to indicate good stability, while values below 0.25 suggest instability.

Network comparison

In this study, separate networks were developed for boys and girls. To investigate the gender differences in the networks, we conducted the Network Comparison Test using the R package “Network Comparison Test”. We examined the gender difference in network structure, global strength, and all edges of the basic networks and extended networks.

Participants with a SCOFF score of ≥ 2 were classified as screening positive for potential eating disorders. The overall detection rate of potential eating disorders among adolescents was 10.8%. The detection rate among girls was significantly higher than that among boys (p < 0.001). Moreover, girls reported significantly higher levels of anxiety and depression, poorer sleep quality, and lower self-esteem than boys (all p < 0.001). Compared with girls, boys reported higher scores on paternal parenting dimensions, including emotional warmth, rejection, and overprotection (all p < 0.001).

Bivariate correlations among all study variables are presented in Supplementary Figure S1. Parental rejection and overprotection showed moderate positive associations with depression, anxiety, and poor sleep quality (r = 0.24 to 0.41), and negative associations with self-esteem (r = − 0.24 to − 0.35), whereas emotional warmth showed associations in the opposite direction. Among proximal psychological factors, depression and anxiety were strongly intercorrelated (r = 0.84), and both showed moderate associations with sleep quality (r = 0.55 to 0.59) and self-esteem (r = − 0.50 to − 0.54). SCOFF items showed small-to-moderate associations with all other variables.

Characteristics of the symptom network

Network stability

The strength stability for the basic networks (only parenting dimensions and DE) was good for the whole samples (CS-coefficients = 0.750), boys (CS-coefficients = 0.517) and girls (CS-coefficients = 0.516). After incorporating proximal psychological variables, the whole sample network also showed good stability for both strength (CS-coefficients = 0.672) and bridge strength (CS-coefficients = 0.594). The boys’ network demonstrated good stability for both strength (CS-coefficients = 0.594) and bridge strength (CS-coefficients = 0.672). The girls’ network showed relatively lower stability for bridge strength (CS-coefficients = 0.361) and strength (CS-coefficients = 0.439) but the valueswithin acceptable ranges. The graph for the bootstrap test is shown in Figs. S2, S3.

Network comparison

In basic networks, no significant differences were observed in network structure (p = 0.63) or global strength (p = 0.29) between the boys and girls. The centrality invariance test showed no significant gender difference for strength centrality. The edge invariance test showed significant differences in 4 edges. Among the results between DE symptoms and parenting dimensions, maternal rejection was connected to “food dominates life” more strongly in girls than in boys (p = 0.03).

Similarly, the network comparison test revealed no differences in network structure (p = 0.62) and global strength (p = 0.27) between the extended networks of the two groups. The bridge strength of “paternal rejection” was significantly greater for boys (0.16) than for girls (0, p = 0.03), primarily driven by its stronger connection with self-esteem, depression, and “loss of control”. Although the bridge strength of sleep quality appeared higher in girls than in boys, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.08). The edge invariance test showed significant differences in 10 edges. The results involving DE symptoms showed that: paternal rejection was more strongly correlated to “loss of control” in boys than in girls (p = 0.01); self-esteem was more strongly connected to “feeling fat” in boys than in girls (p = 0.001); sleep quality was more strongly associated with “feeling fat” (p = 0.001) and “food dominates life” (p = 0.007) in girls than in boys. Discussion

This study used network analysis to explore the relationships between parenting dimensions and disordered eating (DE) in adolescents, with self-esteem, depression, anxiety, and sleep quality incorporated as proximal psychological factors. The network findings identified the specific psychological bridge nodes through which parenting is most strongly linked to disordered eating, and explored gender differences.

In the basic network, maternal rejection showed the strongest and consistent connection to DE symptoms “self-induced vomiting after feeling full” across all three networks. This pattern is consistent with prior research demonstrating that parental rejection is a significant predictor of DE [47, 48]. Prior studies have also suggested maternal rejection may be more consistently linked to adolescent disordered eating than paternal rejection [49]. Furthermore, in gender-stratified basic networks, DE symptoms were prominently linked to both maternal and paternal dimensions in boys, while in girls, DE symptoms were more associated with maternal parenting dimensions. Maternal rejection was also connected to “food dominates life” more strongly in girls than in boys. These findings are consistent with previous findings that girls with DE reported more maternal parenting issues compared to their relationships with fathers [47], and that maternal criticism was found to be a significant predictor of DE symptoms for both boys and girls, while paternal criticism only significantly predicted DE symptoms in boys [50].

When proximal psychological factors were incorporated, self-esteem and sleep quality emerged as the strongest bridge nodes in the overall networks. These findings can be interpreted within an attachment theory framework, which posits that caregiving experiences shape adolescents’ internal working models of the self and others, relating to their psychological functioning such as self-evaluations and regulation [51]. From this perspective, parenting dimensions can relate to DE through more proximal psychological processes.

Self-esteem emerged as the most prominent bridge node, primarily connecting parental emotional warmth to the DE symptom “feeling fat”. Consistent with attachment theory, warm and responsive parenting, which is associated with the development of secure attachment, has been linked to healthier self-esteem [52, 53]. Self-esteem is also closely related to body image [54], and low self-esteem has consistently been identified as a strong predictor of DE symptoms [17, 55]. Moreover, self-esteem is an important mediator in the association between parenting dimensions and DE, in which self-esteem was linked to DE risk both directly and indirectly via body shame [56]. The present findings are consistent with the possibility that self-esteem may represent a key pathway linking parental emotional warmth to body image concern.

Sleep quality also emerged as a key bridge node, primarily connecting parental emotional warmth and rejection to DE symptoms “self-induced vomiting”. This finding is relatively novel and warrants further attention. Previous studies have shown that sleep quality is influenced by parenting behaviors [57, 58]. Parental emotional warmth is associated with better sleep quality in children, while parental rejection is linked to poorer sleep outcomes [59]. Poor sleep quality has been associated with DE through multiple mechanisms, such as disrupting appetite regulation and emotional stability [60]. In particular, sleep disturbances have been shown to undermine emotional regulation [61], which may be associated with maladaptive eating behaviors such as self-induced vomiting. Furthermore, individuals with DE have been shown to exhibit worse sleep quality compared to healthy controls [62, 63], suggesting a bidirectional relationship between sleep disturbances and eating pathology.

Although the network comparison test revealed no significant differences on overall structure and global strength of boys’ and girls’ extended networks, there were gender differences in bridge strength and edge strength for some nodes. Parenting dimensions may be connected to DE symptoms through different pathways across genders.

In the boys’ network, self-esteem was the strongest bridging node and was most closely connected to the DE symptom “feeling fat” and “maternal emotional warmth”. This finding is consistent with previous findings indicating that boys may be more sensitive to the effects of low self-esteem on emotional and behavioral problems [64]. Notably, we further found the connection between self-esteem and “feeling fat” was higher for boys than for girls. Although prior research has typically suggested that females are more closely associated with body dissatisfaction such as “feeling fat” [65], recent studies have shown changes in male body ideals in China, and increasing pressure for thinness among young men in China [50]. Among Chinese male university students, nearly half desire a slimmer body type [66]. Our findings highlight the need for increased attention to body image concerns among males. Additionally, the bridge strength of paternal rejection was significantly greater in boys than in girls, consistent with evidence that boys may be more sensitive to the quality of paternal relationships for their emotional and behavioral development [64].

In the girls’ network, both sleep quality and self-esteem showed high bridge strength. Sleep quality emerged as the strongest bridging symptom, closely linked to the DE symptoms of “self-induced vomiting”, “feeling fat”, and “food dominates life” and to parental emotional warmth on the parenting side. The network comparison further showed that sleep quality was more strongly associated with “feeling fat” and “food dominates life” in girls than in boys. The prominence of sleep quality as a bridge in girls likely reflects sex-specific physiological and hormonal factors. Prior research has consistently shown that women report poorer sleep quality than men [67, 68]. Poor sleep has further been strongly associated with emotional distress and behavioral difficulties in girls than in boys [69, 70], which are closely linked to disordered eating symptoms [71]. Conversely, higher sleep quality has been shown to buffer against stress-related eating problems specifically in women [72]. Self-esteem similarly functioned as a bridge node in the girls’ network, connecting parental emotional warmth to DE symptoms including “loss of control” and “feeling fat”, a pattern consistent with the overall network findings discussed above.

Finally, it should be noted that the stability of the girls’ network was relatively lower than that of the boys. Although the coefficients remained within acceptable ranges, this may limit the reliability of edge strength and bridge centrality estimates. This aspect should be taken into account when considering the observed patterns across groups.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be noted. First, for the convenience of a large-scale school survey, DE was assessed using the SCOFF scale, which only contains five items. This brevity may limit the comprehensiveness of the DE assessment, as it does not capture the full range of DE behaviors and cognitions. Future studies should consider scales with more comprehensive DE measures to capture more symptom profiles. Second, the cross-sectional study precludes causal inferences regarding the associations observed among parenting behaviors, proximal psychological factors, and disordered eating symptoms. Although network analysis identifies bridge symptoms, the directionality of these associations cannot be established from the present data. Future research should employ longitudinal designs to explore causality. Third, although the sample size was large, participants were drawn from a specific region of China. Cultural norms surrounding eating behaviors and parenting practices may vary across regions, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Fourth, while this study focused on adolescents, the sample may include both clinical and subclinical cases. This may limit the ability to determine whether the observed associations differ across levels of symptom severity. Additionally, although household income and rural/urban residence were not incorporated as covariates in the network models, these socioeconomic factors represent important contextual variables that may shape the associations examined in the present study. Future research should further examine how socioeconomic context moderates the relationships between parenting behaviors and disordered eating in adolescents.

Despite these limitations, this study offers valuable insights into the early prevention of DE in adolescents. By examining the connections between parenting dimensions, DE, and psychological variables through network analysis, key bridge symptoms were identified, such as self-esteem and sleep quality. These bridge symptoms may serve as important targets for early DE intervention. Additionally, the results suggest that intervention strategies may need to be tailored to different gender groups.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study applied network analysis to investigate the complex interplay between parenting dimensions, proximal psychological factors, and disordered eating (DE) symptoms in adolescents. The results suggested maternal rejection as the most influential parenting dimension associated with DE symptoms in the basic network. In boys, DE symptoms were prominently linked to both maternal and paternal dimensions, while in girls, DE symptoms were more associated with maternal parenting dimensions. When psychological factors were incorporated, self-esteem and sleep quality emerged as central bridge variables linking parenting dimensions to DE. Gender-specific patterns were identified: in boys, self-esteem emerged as the primary bridge variable linking parenting dimensions to DE; in girls, sleep quality served as the most prominent bridging factor, followed by self-esteem. These findings offer practical implications for early intervention. Programs that strengthen adolescents’ self-esteem and promote healthy sleep may help prevent DE. Moreover, the gender-specific pathways suggest tailored approaches: for boys, addressing both paternal and maternal influences and enhancing self-esteem; and for girls, focusing on maternal parenting and sleep quality.
