Serotype and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns of Vibrio cholerae in cholera outbreak areas of East and Central Oromia Region, Ethiopia, 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCholera is an acute diarrheal infection caused by the consumption of food or water contaminated with Vibrio cholerae serotypes Ogawa and Inaba. Historically, Vibrio cholerae has remained susceptible to several key antibiotics. However, resistance to older antibiotics has been increasingly reported worldwide. The objective of this study was to assess the distribution of Vibrio cholerae serotypes and their susceptibility patterns in the cholera outbreak areas of the East and central Oromia region, Ethiopia, 2024.MethodThe study was conducted on isolates recovered from stool specimens collected from Cholera Outbreak areas of East and Central Oromia Region in 2024. The study design used was a laboratory-based retrospective cross-sectional study. Of these Ogawa accounts for 62% and Inaba for 38%. Complete (100%) resistance was observed for cefepime, ceftazidime, piperacillin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. Additionally, very high resistance rates were found for cefuroxime sodium (93.0%) and ampicillin-sulbactam (93%). Tetracycline also exhibited significant resistance at 76.2%, while cefoxitin and ampicillin showed substantial resistance at 56.1% and 40.5%, respectively. Ciprofloxacin (69.0%) and levofloxacin were moderately effective, but also displayed notable resistance rates 31.0% and 26.2%, respectively.Conclusion and interpretationAll Vibrio cholerae isolates identified in this study belong to serogroup O1, with the Ogawa serotype being more prevalent than Inaba. The isolates demonstrated extensive resistance to multiple commonly used antibiotics, including a complete resistance to several cephalosporins, piperacillin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, alongside high resistance to tetracycline and other agents. Introduction

Cholera is an acute diarrheal illness caused by toxigenic Vibrio cholerae serogroups O1 and O139. It is primarily transmitted through ingestion of food or water contaminated with fecal matter from infected individuals. The pathogen can persist in contaminated environments, and its spread is associated with inadequate sanitation, poor hygiene, and limited access to safe drinking water, particularly in settings such as peri-urban slums and refugee or displaced persons’ camps [1, 2].

There are more than 200 serogroups of Vibrio cholerae, based on the presence of somatic O antigens. However, only Vibrio cholerae serogroups O1 and O139 are responsible for epidemic and pandemic cholera, while all other serogroups typically cause sporadic, non-epidemic diarrheal illness [3, 4].

In Ethiopia, cholera outbreaks are primarily caused by Vibrio cholerae serogroup O1, with both Ogawa and Inaba serotypes reported [5, 6]. The ongoing outbreak since August 2022 has resulted in over 58,000 cases and more than 700 deaths by early 2024 [5]. Molecular analysis shows that the circulating strains belong to a multidrug-resistant clade linked to outbreaks in Eastern and Sub-Saharan Africa, including countries such as Kenya, Somalia, Tanzania, the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), and neighbouring areas of Sudan and South Sudan, sharing resistance genes against fluoroquinolones, trimethoprim, chloramphenicol, aminoglycosides, β-lactam antibiotics, macrolides, and sulfonamides [5, 7, 8].

A Significant global increase in cholera cases and their geographical spread was observed recently. In 2021, 23 countries reported cholera outbreaks, primarily in the WHO African and Eastern Mediterranean regions. This trend intensified in 2022, with over 29 countries reporting cases or outbreaks, and 16 experiencing prolonged outbreaks by November 2022. Many countries reported higher case numbers and case fatality rate (CFR) than in previous years, with the global average CFR in 2021 at 1.9% (2.9% in Africa), exceeding the acceptable threshold of less than 1% and marking the highest level in over a decade [9].

In a study conducted by Bitew et al., in Ethiopia in 2024, Vibrio cholerae infections were predominantly caused by the O1 serogroup. In the same period, 43.2% of Vibrio cholerae isolates during outbreaks in Ethiopia originated from Oromia, with the Ogawa serotype accounting for 86.4% of these cases [10].

Moreover, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) among circulating Vibrio cholerae strains in Oromia remains a significant and emerging public health concern. While isolates have historically remained fully susceptible to the recommended first-line treatment, ciprofloxacin, resistance to commonly used older antibiotics such as trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and ampicillin is high, with reported resistance rates reaching 62.4% and 56.8%, respectively. This escalating resistance necessitates continuous, local surveillance to inform evidence-based empirical treatment guidelines for the region.

This study identified circulating Vibrio cholerae serotypes and assessed their antimicrobial susceptibility, providing essential information for guiding public health interventions, treatment decisions, and strengthening cholera surveillance in the Oromia region and Ethiopia.

While previous surveillance efforts have been conducted, there remains a critical lack of recent, published data detailing the circulating serotypes (Ogawa vs. Inaba) and their contemporary antibiotic susceptibility profiles from the specific epidemic areas within the Oromia region. This gap limits the ability of clinicians and public health officials to prescribe effective empirical first-line therapy and effectively track emerging resistance trends.

The main objective of this study was to assess the distribution of Vibrio cholerae serotypes and their antimicrobial susceptibility patterns in the cholera outbreak areas of the Central and Eastern Oromia region, Ethiopia, 2024.

Methods and materials

Study design

A laboratory-based cross-sectional study was conducted.

Study setting

The study was conducted using the stool samples collected from the Central and Eastern parts of Oromia regional state. With a total population of 18,424,536 in 2024, the region represents a considerable proportion of Ethiopia’s national population [11]. Isolates were analyzed from stool specimens collected during January- December 2024 outbreak in the study area.

For the study on Vibrio cholerae serotyping and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) in Central and East Oromia, all laboratory analysis procedures were conducted at Adama Public health research and Referral Laboratory Center.

Adama Public health research and Referral Laboratory Center (APHRRLC) (https://www.aphrrlc.gov.et/en/) is Oromia regional government research and Referral facility that serves as referral and research center for East and Central Oromia region and beyond. This laboratory center is a nationally recognized institution accredited for excellence in a range of critical diagnostic services. The laboratory holds accreditation in clinical microbiology culture, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST). Notably, the laboratory has successfully transitioned to and achieved accreditation under the latest ISO 15189:2022 standard, affirming its commitment to continuous quality improvement and adherence to globally accepted laboratory practices [11].

Study sample

During the 2024 outbreak in Central and Eastern Oromia regions, a total of 4,440 suspected cases were reported. Data from Oromia health office showed that the case fatality rate for this outbreak was reported to be 1.5%. Stool specimens were collected from individuals aged ≥ 5 years who met suspected cholera standard case definitions. A total of 76 stool samples were received in recommended triple package (UN3373) and Carry-Blaire transport media (HiMedia, India). In the outbreak periods of January 2024- December 2024, a total of 42 (Positivity rate = 55.26%) tested positive for Vibrio cholerae species [12].

The study included all 42 culture-confirmed Vibrio cholerae O1 clinical isolates that were successfully recovered, identified, and subjected to antimicrobial susceptibility testing at the Adama Public Health Research and Referral Laboratory Center (APHRRLC). The sample size for this study was determined by the complete enumeration of stool specimens collected during cholera outbreaks between January and December 2024 in the East and Central Oromia regions of Ethiopia. All 42 specimens were processed at APHRRLC and every available isolate was included in the analysis.

Operational definition

Multi drug resistant: resistant to more than one antimicrobial agent.

Laboratory test protocols

Sample collection and transportation

Stool specimens were collected from patients meeting the standard case definition for suspected cholera using clean, leak-proof containers with Cary Blair transport medium (HiMedia, India). This process ensured minimal contamination and an adequate volume of 2–5 ml. Specimens were labeled with patient identifiers, stored at 2–8 °C, and transported to the Adama Public Health Research and Referral Laboratory Center (APHRRLC) within a maximum of 24 h. Stool specimens were first inoculated into alkaline peptone water (APW) and incubated at 37 °C for 6 h to selectively enrich Vibrio cholerae, then sub-cultured onto Thiosulfate Citrate Bile Salts Sucrose (TCBS) agar (Liofilchem s.r.l., Italy) and MacConkey agar (Oxoid, UK) for isolation.

Isolation and presumptive identification of Vibrio cholerae serogroups O1 and O139

Isolation and presumptive identification of Vibrio cholerae O1 from fecal specimens follow APHRRLC standard operating procedures. In line with the above-mentioned protocols, the process begins with quality control measures and continues through enrichment, selective plating, and preliminary identification techniques. To ensure diagnostic reliability, all culture media and reagents used in the identification of Vibrio cholerae undergo strict quality control procedures. Each batch of media prepared from a dehydrated base was assessed for sterility, growth-supporting ability, growth-inhibiting behavior, and biochemical functionality. This is done by performing contamination checks on uninoculated plates, inoculating American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) reference strains to confirm optimal growth performance, and verifying that expected biochemical reactions (e.g., color change on Thiosulfate-Citrate-Bile Salts-Sucrose or blood agar hemolysis) are observed [3, 4].

For laboratory isolation of Vibrio cholerae, Thiosulfate-Citrate-Bile Salts-Sucrose (TCBS) (HiMedia, India) agar was used as the selective medium for stool samples. From this TCBS agar, large yellow (sucrose-fermenting) colonies were identified within 24 h at 37 °C, picked with a sterile loop, and inoculated onto blood agar (Oxoid, UK) for purity plating and further oxidase and other biochemical testing. The oxidase test was performed using fresh 24 h cultures grown on blood agar. All isolates from this study demonstrated a positive oxidase reaction, which is characteristic of Vibrio cholerae, and were taken into further processing.

Screening tests for suspected Vibrio cholerae isolates

Before serologic identification of Vibrio cholerae O1 and O139, biochemical screening tests were performed for better characterization of the bacteria. The screening tests included positive oxidase (HiMedia, India), positive string test, acid production without gas in triple sugar iron agar (Oxoid, UK), small Gram-negative curved bacilli on Gram stain, and small curved bacilli with darting motility in wet mount [3, 4]. In addition, other biochemical tests were performed following standard procedures. Citrate utilization was assessed using Simmons citrate agar (Oxoid, UK) to determine whether the isolates could use citrate as the sole carbon source after incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. Urease activity was tested by inoculating urea agar slants (HiMedia, India) and observing color change from yellow to pink, indicating urease production. Indole production was evaluated by growing isolates in tryptone broth (Oxoid, UK) and adding Kovac’s reagent (HiMedia, India) to detect the development of a red layer. Quality control was ensured by including known V. cholerae reference strains 14,033 as positive controls and non-Vibrio strains such as Escherichia coli as negative controls.

Serologic identification of Vibrio cholerae O1 and O139

Hemolysis of sheep red blood cells and a positive Voges–Proskauer (VP) (Hardy Diagnostics, USA) test were used to identify O1 and O139 biotypes. Slide agglutination testing with polyvalent O1 or O139 antisera (Hardy Diagnostics, USA) was performed for the presumptive identification of Vibrio cholerae serogroups [3, 4].

A fresh 24 h culture grown on blood agar was used for the slide agglutination test. The isolates were tested using O-group-specific antisera for both O1 and O139 serogroups (Deben Diagnostics, UK). No agglutination was observed with saline alone, ruling out self-agglutinating strains and validating the agglutination reactions. Positive agglutination with the anti-O1 serum confirmed the isolates as belonging to the Vibrio cholerae O1 polyvalent serogroup [3, 4].

Serotyping of Vibrio cholerae O1 isolates was performed using monovalent Inaba and Ogawa antisera. Serotype determination was based on agglutination in monovalent antisera to type-specific O antigens. A positive reaction in either Inaba or Ogawa antiserum was used to classify the Vibrio cholerae O1 isolate as Inaba or Ogawa. Isolates that agglutinated weakly or slowly with serogroup O1 antiserum but did not agglutinate with either Inaba or Ogawa antiserum were not considered to be Inaba or Ogawa [3, 4].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of the Vibrio cholerae O1 isolates was performed using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method on Mueller-Hinton agar (HiMedia, India). A standardized volume of medium was dispensed into 150 mm Petri dishes to achieve an approximate agar depth of 4 mm. Bacterial inocula were standardized to a 0.5 McFarland turbidity using a calibrated DEN-1 McFarland densitometer (BioSan, Latvia) before application to the agar surface. Antimicrobial agents and their disk concentrations were selected based on CLSI 2023 Guideline and Ethiopian national guideline for cholera surveillance and outbreak response 3rd Edition, 2022 G.C recommendations to include tetracycline (30 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), ampicillin (10 µg), and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (1.25/23.75 µg), cefepime (30 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), piperacillin (100 µg), cefuroxime (30 µg), ampicillin/sulbactam (10/10 µg), piperacillin/tazobactam (100/10 µg), imipenem (10 µg), meropenem (10 µg), levofloxacin (5 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), cefoxitin (30 µg). Extended antimicrobial panels beyond first-line agents were also included to support local antimicrobial resistance (AMR) surveillance and enable detection of emerging resistance patterns. After incubation at 37 °C for 18 h, zones of inhibition were measured and interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI guidelines, (M45, M02). Since the testing laboratory implements laboratory quality management system, quality control was routinely performed weekly, monthly, and with each new batch of antibiotic disks and culture media using standard reference strains American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) to ensure accuracy and reliability of results. Based on these criteria, isolates were classified as resistant, intermediate, or sensitive to the panel of antibiotics tested.

Antibiotic disks and reagents were stored at − 20 °C to 8 °C according to manufacturer’s instructions, and all were checked for expiration prior to use. Control strains, including Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29,213, were included to ensure test validity. After incubation at 37 °C for 18 h, the diameters of the zones of inhibition were measured using a metric ruler on a white background with sharp black lines. Interpretation of susceptibility was performed according to the 2025 CLSI guidelines, categorizing each isolate as sensitive, intermediate, or resistant to the tested antibiotics [13].

Results

Serotypes and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns of Vibrio cholerae

Serotype of Vibrio cholerae

Vibrio cholerae was isolated from stool specimens. On TCBS agar, large yellow sucrose-fermenting colonies were observed after 18–24 h of incubation, consistent with the characteristic morphology of V. cholerae. Growth was also observed on MacConkey agar, supporting its identification among Gram-negative organisms. These phenotypic characteristics were consistent with the presence of Vibrio cholerae in the clinical samples.

Biochemical testing showed that all isolates exhibited hemolysis on sheep blood agar and were negative for the Voges–Proskauer (VP) test. Oxidase activity was positive in all isolates. The isolates were string test positive and demonstrated motility. On triple sugar iron (TSI) agar, an acid/acid reaction was observed without gas or hydrogen sulfide production. Further biochemical characteristics included indole positivity, citrate utilization, and urease negativity. These findings were consistent across all isolates identified as Vibrio cholerae.

Serological confirmation demonstrated that all 42 isolates agglutinated with polyvalent O1 antisera, verifying their classification within the Vibrio cholerae O1 serogroup. This serogroup is recognized as the most clinically significant type associated with cholera outbreaks worldwide. The uniformity of serotype among the isolates indicates the predominance of a single strain lineage during the outbreak period covered by the study.

Serotype determination of the Vibrio cholerae O1 isolates collected in the APHRRLC catchment area during the 2024 cholera outbreak revealed that, of the 42 isolates tested, 26 (62%) belonged to the Ogawa serotype, while the remaining 16 (38%) were classified as Inaba (Fig. 1).

Antimicrobial susceptibility patterns

Vibrio cholerae isolates were found to be completely resistant to five antibiotics. These included cefepime, ceftazidime, piperacillin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. For these drugs no isolates were found sensitive indicating a complete ineffectiveness against the circulating strains in the study area. A significant number of isolates also exhibited high resistance to other antibiotics. Resistance to cefuroxime sodium was observed in 97.6% (41/42) of the isolates, followed by ampicillin–sulbactam at 92.8% (39/42) and tetracycline at 76.2% (32/42). Additionally, 57.1% (24/42) of the isolates were resistant to cefoxitin. Ampicillin resistance was detected in 40.5% (17/42) of the isolates, while 21.4% (9/42) demonstrated intermediate susceptibility. In contrast, 69.0% (29/42) of the isolates were susceptible to ciprofloxacin, although 31.0% (13/42) showed resistance. Similarly, levofloxacin demonstrated activity against 69.0% (29/42) of the isolates, with resistance observed in 26.2% (11/42).

Conversely, several antibiotics demonstrated good activity against circulating Vibrio cholerae isolates in the study area. Meropenem showed complete effectiveness, with 100% (42/42) of isolates being susceptible. Similarly, imipenem exhibited high activity, with 97.6% (41/42) of isolates classified as sensitive. Piperacillin–tazobactam also displayed substantial effectiveness, with 88.1% (37/42) of isolates susceptible and a low resistance rate of 7.3% (3/42). Ciprofloxacin remained effective against 69.0% (29/42) of the isolates, although resistance was observed in 31.0% (13/42) of the isolates (Table 1).

Based on the isolate level antimicrobial susceptibility results, all 42 isolates of Vibrio cholerae O1 isolates demonstrated multidrug resistance. Most isolates showed concurrent resistance to several β-lactam antibiotics, including ampicillin, ampicillin–sulbactam, cefepime, ceftazidime, cefotaxime, and cefuroxime sodium. In addition, resistance was frequently observed to tetracycline and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, indicating limited effectiveness of several commonly used antimicrobial agents against the circulating strains.

The resistance pattern was therefore mainly characterized by simultaneous resistance to penicillin, cephalosporins, tetracyclines, and folate pathway inhibitors.

Overall, the findings indicate that the circulating Vibrio cholerae serotype polyvalent isolates exhibited substantial multidrug resistance, highlighting the importance of continuous antimicrobial resistance surveillance and careful selection of effective antibiotics for cholera treatment.

Discussions

All isolates from the study area were classified within the Vibrio cholerae O1 serogroup, with serotype distribution limited to the polyvalent Inaba and Ogawa types. The confirmation of Vibrio cholerae O1 is particularly significant, as this serogroup is recognized as the primary cause of the ongoing seventh cholera pandemic and accounts for nearly all major outbreaks worldwide.

The finding that 100% of isolates in this study belonged to the O1 serogroup unequivocally confirms that the observed infections were caused by the life-threatening lineage of Vibrio cholerae. This result is consistent with global epidemiology, as the O1 serogroup is the principal driver of the ongoing seventh pandemic. Recent studies have similarly reported that cholera outbreaks across Africa, including Ethiopia, have been consistently attributed to Vibrio cholerae O1 serogroup (Thapa Shrestha et al., [14]; Frank et al., 2025; [15, 16]).

The absence of other serogroups, namely O139 or non-O1/non-O139 strains, is noteworthy. Although Vibrio cholerae O139 has been responsible for major epidemics in Asia, it has largely remained geographically confined and has not established endemicity on the African continent [8]. In contrast, non-O1/non-O139 Vibrio species are typically associated with sporadic gastrointestinal illness and are not implicated in large-scale epidemics [9, 14].

In this study, the prevalence of serotypes within the Vibrio cholerae O1 serogroup was determined. Based on classical culture results performed at APHRRLC, 62% of isolates were identified as Ogawa serotype. This finding contrasts with reports from other regions of the world. For example, a study conducted in 2020 documented the Ogawa prevalence rates of 1.8% globally, 39% in Nepal, 86% in Ethiopia, and 100% in Karachi, Pakistan. Such variation highlights the geographic differences in serotype distribution and underscores the importance of local surveillance in understanding cholera epidemiology [10, 17–19].

The findings from this study revealed concerning pictures of high proportions of resistant isolates were noted. This pattern is critical for guiding effective clinical management and public health strategies in this local context of central Oromia, Ethiopia, especially considering the ongoing cholera outbreaks in Ethiopia.

The alarmingly high resistance rates to ampicillin, cefepime, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, cefuroxime sodium, and piperacillin are highly consistent with recent global and regional trends of increasing β-lactam antibiotic resistance in Vibrio cholerae. This phenomenon is primarily driven by the acquisition and dissemination of β-lactamase enzymes, particularly extended-spectrum Β-Lactamases (ESBLs) and, to a lesser extent, carbapenemases. The 100% resistance to third and fourth-generation cephalosporins (cefepime, cefotaxime, ceftazidime) and broad-spectrum penicillins (piperacillin) strongly suggests the presence of ESBL-producing Vibrio cholerae strains in the study sample [20, 21].

This finding of 41.46% resistance to ampicillin aligns with its declining efficacy as a historical cholera treatment, widely reported in recent literature [15]. Most importantly, the 93.0% resistance to ampicillin-sulbactam is highly concerning. While sulbactam aims to inhibit common β-lactam antibiotics, its ineffectiveness suggests the presence of β-lactamases not inhibited by sulbactam (e.g., certain ESBL variants or AmpC β-lactamases), or the involvement of alternative resistance mechanisms such as efflux pumps or changes in outer membrane permeability. This specific pattern of high ampicillin-sulbactam resistance, even in the presence of an inhibitor, is being increasingly reported, reflecting evolving resistance mechanisms [14, 21, 22].

In contrast to other β-lactam antibiotics, the relatively high sensitivity to piperacillin-tazobactam (87.80% sensitive) is a positive outcome. Tazobactam, a potent β-lactamase inhibitor, effectively overcomes the resistance produced by Vibrio cholerae. This suggests that for cases requiring broad-spectrum coverage, piperacillin-tazobactam remains a potentially valuable therapeutic option in this setting. Studies have similarly shown varying but often higher susceptibility to piperacillin-tazobactam compared to other cephalosporins in Vibrio cholerae isolates, highlighting its continued utility in areas with high resistance [16, 23].

Fluoroquinolones, particularly ciprofloxacin, have been widely recommended as first-line oral agents for treating severe cholera. While this data shows a substantial proportion of sensitive isolates, the presence of resistance in approximately one-third of this strain is a significant and growing concern that mirrors a global trend of increasing fluoroquinolone resistance in Vibrio cholerae.

Despite the emergence of resistance, the fact that over two-thirds of these isolates remain sensitive suggests that fluoroquinolones may still be a viable option for many cholera cases in this specific area, especially in non-severe cases or when susceptibility is confirmed. However, the rising resistance necessitates vigilant local surveillance and careful consideration of alternative treatments for severe or unresponsive cases. The escalating global threat of antimicrobial resistance in Vibrio cholerae highlights fluoroquinolones as a critical class whose efficacy is increasingly challenged, emphasizing the importance of local AST to guide therapy [10, 15, 22].

The remarkably high proportion of isolates susceptible to gentamicin (95.12%) in these Vibrio cholerae isolates is a significant and promising finding. Aminoglycosides, which inhibit bacterial protein synthesis, generally exhibit lower resistance rates in Vibrio cholerae compared to other antibiotic classes, as evidenced by recent scientific literature.

Multiple recent studies from cholera-endemic regions also founds the effectiveness of aminoglycosides against Vibrio cholerae species. A 2024 study from Uganda, for instance, reported high susceptibility rates to gentamicin among Vibrio cholerae isolates collected during outbreaks [10]. Similarly, a 2023 retrospective analysis of clinical Vibrio cholerae isolates in Bangladesh reported a sustained high proportion of isolates susceptible to gentamicin over several years [22]. However, despite this preserved in vitro susceptibility, gentamicin is not routinely recommended for the treatment of cholera, and such findings should be interpreted in the context of established clinical management guidelines rather than as a therapeutic alternative [16].

This study demonstrated that the resistance patterns of imipenem was 0.00% resistance 2.44% intermediate, and 97.56% sensitive. In the same case meropenem was found to be 0.00% resistance, 0.00% intermediate and 100.00% sensitive. These results demonstrating nearly complete susceptibility to carbapenems (meropenem 100% sensitive, imipenem 97.56% sensitive) are exceptionally positive and crucial. Carbapenems are broad-spectrum β-lactam antibiotics often reserved as last-line agents for treating severe infections caused by multidrug-resistant gram-negative bacteria, including Vibrio cholerae. [24] overall resistance to carbapenems in Vibrio cholerae remains fortunately low compared to other widespread resistance mechanisms. A comprehensive review in 2024 on carbapenem resistance in Vibrio species highlighted that Vibrio cholerae is less frequently found to be carbapenem-resistant compared to other Vibrio species, though surveillance is vital the consistent sensitivity in this isolate suggests that these highly potent antibiotics remain effective for this local Vibrio cholerae strains [24].

The continued susceptibility to carbapenems is of immense clinical significance. It provides an indispensable therapeutic option for managing the most severe and complicated cholera cases, especially those that might be resistant to all other commonly used antibiotics. This underscores the critical importance of careful antimicrobial stewardship to preserve their efficacy and prevent the emergence of carbapenem resistance. This aligns with WHO’s guidance on essential medicines, which emphasizes preserving critical last-resort antibiotics [9].

Tetracyclines were among the first antibiotics effectively used against cholera, but their widespread and often unmonitored use has unfortunately led to a pervasive decline in efficacy. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing for this antibiotic shows 75.6% resistance to Vibrio cholerae species which suggests treating the current Outbreak in the study area becomes ineffective and unhelpful.

In a study of the Emerging Antibiotic Resistance in Vibrio cholerae: A Study of Cholera Prevalence and Resistance Patterns in Zambia’s Copperbelt Province in 2024 showed contrasting result. In this study by Chisompola and et al., Vibrio cholerae serotype was found to be more sensitive (74.2%) than our study which is way much less 24.4% sensitive [25].

The finding of 100% resistance to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (SXT) in this isolate is a critically important observation, indicating that this antibiotic combination is entirely ineffective for treating cholera in this specific setting. This extreme level of resistance is a pervasive issue globally, particularly in areas with a history of extensive SXT use for various enteric bacterial infections.

In a similar study by Chisompola D. et al., on Vibrio cholerae isolates In Zambia Copperbelt found similar antimicrobial susceptibility patterns for trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (SXT). In his study, all isolates were found to be resistant to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (SXT) which is almost the same resistance pattern with our study [25].

The finding of 100% resistance to multiple classes of antibiotics specifically cefepime, ceftazidime, piperacillin, and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (SXT) resistance is most likely a reflection of the successful local circulation of a single, highly evolved, multi-drug resistant clone that has been shaped by widespread antimicrobial pressure, rendering these critical antibiotics ineffective for empirical treatment in the study area.

Conclusion

This study characterized 42 Vibrio cholerae isolates obtained from 42 patients during the 2024 cholera outbreak in East and Central Oromia, Ethiopia. All isolates belonged to the O1 serogroup, with 62% identified as Ogawa and 38% as Inaba. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed high levels of resistance to several commonly used agents, while carbapenems and piperacillin–tazobactam showed strong in vitro activity against the isolates. These findings reflect laboratory susceptibility patterns and highlight the importance of continuous surveillance of circulating serotypes and resistance profiles to inform appropriate antibiotic selection and outbreak control strategies.

Recommendation

Empirical treatment options: The observed resistance to ciprofloxacin (31.0%) raises questions about its continued suitability as a first-line empirical agent for cholera treatment in the study area. While ciprofloxacin may remain useful where susceptibility testing is available, its role as a primary empirical option could benefit from further evaluation in light of these resistance levels.

Tetracycline resistance: The high resistance proportion observed for tetracycline (76.2%) may indicate reduced effectiveness of this agent in the local context. This finding highlights the importance of continuous antimicrobial resistance surveillance to inform cholera treatment strategies, particularly regarding commonly used tetracycline-class antibiotics such as doxycycline.

Limitation of the study

The relatively small sample size (n = 42) may limit the statistical power of the results. In addition, the absence of molecular characterization of resistance mechanisms restricts deeper understanding of the genetic determinants underlying the observed antimicrobial resistance patterns. Furthermore, the use of isolates obtained from a limited geographic area may reduce the generalizability of the findings to other settings.
