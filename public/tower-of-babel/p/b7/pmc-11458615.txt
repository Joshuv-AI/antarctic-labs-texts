Mid- to long-term radiological and respiratory outcomes following surgery for older early-onset scoliosis patients with Marfan syndrome
Authors: Yuki Taniguchi, Ryoji Tauchi, Teppei Suzuki, Haruhisa Yanagida, Kei Watanabe, Takachika Shimizu, Yoshitaka Matsubayashi, Noriaki Kawakami
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to elucidate mid- to long-term radiological and respiratory outcomes in patients aged 7–11 years at index surgery with Marfan syndrome and early-onset scoliosis (EOS) in a retrospective multicenter study. Primary outcomes were final thoracic height and final percentage of predicted vital capacity (%VC) at or after 16 years of age. We identified 21 (6 male and 15 female) patients with a mean age of 9.9 years and mean follow-up period of 149.3 months. Fifteen patients underwent primary fusion, whereas six underwent growth-friendly surgery (GFS). The mean preoperative and final T1–T12 heights were 204.0 mm and 248.0 mm, respectively. Final pulmonary function tests were available for 16 patients, and the mean final %VC was 54.0% with 10 patients exhibiting a final %VC < 60%. A significant moderate association was observed between the final T1–T12 height and final %VC. The predicted final T1–T12 height required for a final %VC of 60% was approximately 260 mm. Although most older patients with Marfan syndrome and EOS acquired a considerably large final T1–T12 height, a larger thoracic height was required for satisfactory respiratory function in many cases; hence, GFS may be indicated even in this population.Keywords: Marfan syndrome, Early-onset scoliosis, Thoracic height, Respiratory function, T1–T12 heightSubject terms: Paediatrics, Musculoskeletal system, Paediatric research, Connective tissue diseases, Musculoskeletal abnormalities

Introduction

The Marfan syndrome (OMIM: #154,700), initially described by Antoine-Bernard Marfan in 1896, is an autosomal dominant heritable disorder of the connective tissue characterized by several clinical manifestations, including dilatation of the aortic root, ectopia lentis, and distinctive skeletal features1,2. Among the various musculoskeletal manifestations in patients with Marfan syndrome, spinal deformities cause pain and restrictive ventilatory impairment and can be the main issue affecting patients’ daily activities. Because patients with Marfan syndrome are potentially at high risk of further impairment of cardiopulmonary function following thoracotomy and/or recurrent pneumothorax, preventing the progression of spinal deformity and further deterioration of respiratory function is crucial. Additionally, the life expectancy in these patients has improved over the last few decades owing to better medical and surgical management of cardiovascular conditions; thus, appropriate control of spinal deformities is increasingly important3.

Spinal deformity in Marfan syndrome often presents as early-onset scoliosis (EOS); however, the management of patients with Marfan syndrome and EOS is challenging. Conservative treatments, including casting or bracing, are rarely effective in halting curve progression in EOS coexisting with Marfan syndrome4–6. Conversely, surgery for EOS in Marfan syndrome is reportedly accompanied by a high incidence of complications5,7,8. Historically, patients with Marfan syndrome who required early cardiac surgery were at a greater risk of early death, and spinal surgery was avoided until later childhood; however, recently, surgery has been aggressively indicated for those with Marfan syndrome and EOS because of the improved management of coexisting aortic dilatation and valvular lesions5,7,8.

For patients with EOS, growth-friendly surgery (GFS), including growing rods, vertical expandable prosthetic titanium ribs, or the Shilla procedure, can be indicated to acquire sufficient thoracic height at skeletal maturity, because the final thoracic height correlates with the final forced vital capacity (FVC)9,10. GFS is especially favored in younger patients with EOS, even at the cost of a high incidence of complications because early definitive fusion in these patients leads to restrictive pulmonary diseases11. However, some controversies have emerged in recent years regarding the treatment strategy for older patients with EOS, considering the high incidence of complications in GFS and potential loss of thoracic-height growth in primary definitive fusion (PF)12–14. Consequently, the optimal treatment for older patients with Marfan syndrome and EOS remains unknown. Hence, to gain insight into the optimal treatment for this population, this study aimed to elucidate the middle- to long-term radiological and respiratory outcomes in patients with Marfan syndrome and EOS of 7–11 years of age at the time of index surgery who underwent GFS or PF at multiple centers.

Results

Demographic, surgical, and radiographical data

We identified 21 patients in this multicenter cohort, comprising 6 male and 15 female patients with a mean age of 9.9 years at initial surgery. The demographic, surgical, and radiographical data of the study population are shown in Table 1. The mean age at the final follow-up was 22.4 years with a mean follow-up period of 149.3 months. Fifteen patients underwent PF, whereas six underwent GFS, comprising four patients with traditional growing rods and two with the Shilla procedure. All patients were skeletally immature at initial surgery; the triradiate cartilage was open in 12 patients, and among the remaining nine patients, the Risser grade was 0 in eight and 1 in one patient. The main curve was identified in the main thoracic region in 19 patients and in the thoracolumbar/lumbar region in two. Ten patients exhibited preoperative pectus excavatum, including seven undergoing prophylactic pectus correction before spinal surgery.

Incidence and causes of unplanned return to the operating room

Eight unplanned returns to the operating room (UPROR) in three cases were identified among the six patients who underwent GFS, whereas six UPROR in five cases were identified among the 15 patients who underwent PF (Table 2). UPROR tended to be identified more frequently in the GFS than in the PF group, although the difference was not significant (P = 0.053). Implant failure and surgical site infections were the primary causes of UPROR in the GFS group, while junctional problems were the primary cause of UPROR in the PF group. Surgical site infections were more frequent in the GFS group; however, there were no cases with comorbidities associated with the occurrence of surgical site infections in either group. Regarding comorbidities related to Marfan syndrome, one patient in the GFS group had undergone cardiovascular surgery at the time of the initial surgery.

Radiographic parameters and pulmonary function test results

The radiographic parameters of the patients are listed in Table 3. The mean preoperative and final Cobb angles were 77.8° and 35.4°, respectively. The average number of fused vertebrae was 14.3 with the final lowest instrumented vertebra at or above L3 in nine patients, at L4 in seven, at L5 in two, and at S1 or the pelvis in three. The mean preoperative and final T1–T12 heights were 204.0 mm and 248.0 mm, respectively, and mean preoperative and final T1–S1 heights 355.3 mm and 430.9 mm, respectively. The mean preoperative percentage of predicted vital capacity (%VC) in all 21 patients was 65.1%. Final pulmonary function test (PFT) results were available in 16 patients, and the mean preoperative and final %VC in these patients were 67.4% and 54.0%, respectively. Ten (62.5%) of 16 patients exhibited a final %VC of < 60%. (Fig. 1). Upon plotting the final T1–T12 height and final %VC in these 16 patients, a significant moderate association was observed between the two parameters (R2 = 0.48, P = 0.003). The predicted final T1–T12 height required for a final %VC of 60% was approximately 260 mm (Fig. 1).

Growth-friendly surgery vs primary definitive fusion

Comparing between the GFS and PF groups, the age at initial surgery was lower in the GFS group, as expected, by approximately one year (Table 4). Although the final Cobb angle of the main curve was significantly higher in the GFS group than in the PF group (50.8° vs. 29.2°, P = 0.03), the final T1–T12 height was comparable between the two groups (248.5 mm vs. 247.7 mm). In the GFS group, an average gain in T1–T12 height of approximately 14 mm from 193.5 mm to 207.8 mm during the initial surgery was observed. Subsequently, an additional gain of about 41 mm was observed by the time of the final fusion, resulting in a total gain of approximately 55 mm in T1–T12 height (Table 4). The gain in T1–T12 height was approximately 15 mm more in the GFS group than in the PF group, although the difference was not significant (55.0 mm vs. 39.5 mm, P = 0.09), suggesting a potential benefit for GFS, especially in younger patients, albeit the high incidence of UPROR (Table 4). A scatter diagram clearly demonstrated that the final T1–T12 heights were especially short (< 200 mm) in patients undergoing PF at or under eight years of age, which also suggested a potential benefit for GFS in younger patients (Fig. 2). In terms of respiratory function, final PFTs were performed in 13 out of 15 patients in the PF group and in 3 out of 6 in the GFS group. Although the final %VC tended to be lower in the GFS group than in the PF group, this difference was not significant (57.7% vs. 37.3%, P = 0.19) (Table 4). Additionally, there was no significant difference in the prevalence of pectus excavatum between the PF and GFS groups, suggesting that the differences in final respiratory function between the two groups was not owing to the concomitant pectus deformity (Table 4).

Discussion

This study yielded three novel findings. First, it demonstrated that most older patients with Marfan syndrome and EOS had considerably large final T1–T12 heights. Second, the final respiratory function was unsatisfactory in most of the older patients with Marfan syndrome and EOS. Third, the predicted final T1–T12 height required for a final %VC of 60% was approximately 260 mm.

Since Karol et al. demonstrated the correlation between respiratory function and final thoracic height, the goal for the management of patients with EOS has been to gain as much thoracic height as possible9. In their report, they proposed a thoracic height of 18 cm as the minimum threshold, which corresponded to a percentage of predicted FVC (%FVC) of approximately 50–60%9. Recently, many surgeons have adopted 22 cm as a thoracic-height goal because patients who achieved 22 cm of thoracic height presented near-normal pulmonary function in their reports9,15. Conversely, because these previous studies did not consider the impact of different etiologies or the magnitude of residual deformities, recent studies have raised doubts about such threshold10. Especially in Marfan syndrome, other factors, including a history of pneumothorax or thoracotomy, pectus deformity, and sarcopenia, supposedly impact respiratory function; therefore, applying these thresholds as goals for patients with Marfan syndrome and EOS may be premature. However, no report has elucidated a goal for the management of EOS in Marfan syndrome in terms of respiratory function, which motivated us to conduct this study.

In the present study, most patients acquired a considerably large final T1–T12 height with an average of 248.0 mm, which far exceeded the previously reported threshold of 22 cm and was consistent with the findings of a previous study that reported a final mean T1–T12 height of 238 mm in 42 patients with Marfan syndrome and EOS7. However, the final respiratory function was not satisfactory in many cases in the present study with an average of 54.0% of final %VC in 16 patients. Although a significant correlation between the final thoracic height and final %VC was observed in the present study, as reported in previous studies, the predicted thoracic height required for a final %VC of 60% was calculated to be approximately 260 mm, which was much larger than 22 cm. Considering that the final thoracic height in the PF group was 247.7 mm in the present study, GFS might be indicated even in older patients with Marfan syndrome and EOS. Additionally, surgeons may be motivated to perform GFS in these patients because the typical law of diminishing returns, by which progressive stiffness of the spine leads to a reduced ability to distract over time, was not observed during GFS in this population in a previous study, albeit the high incidence of complications7. Considering that the final T1–T12 heights were less than 200 mm in patients undergoing PF at or under 8 years of age, PF apparently should be avoided, at least in those aged 8 years or younger, although it is unavoidable due to comorbidities in some cases.

The reason for poor final respiratory function in many patients in the current study is unknown. Few reports have demonstrated respiratory function in patients with Marfan syndrome. Kolonics-Faredrkas et al. reported that the mean %FVC in 32 patients with Marfan syndrome was 87.0%, which was slightly below the reference values16. Additionally, Cipriano et al. demonstrated that the mean %FVC in 46 patients with Marfan syndrome was 88.3%17. These previous reports indicated that Marfan syndrome alone did not largely impact respiratory function. Residual spinal and/or pectus deformities might have affected respiratory function in the present study population. Furthermore, patients with Marfan syndrome presenting with EOS may exhibit more severe muscle weakness than those without EOS, which could lead to a worse expiratory volume. However, the effect of each phenotype on respiratory function requires further investigation.

This study has a few limitations. First, the data were collected retrospectively and not prospectively; therefore, information bias might have occurred. Second, only 21 participants were included in the present study; thus, the sample size might have been insufficient to determine the optimal treatment for this population. Third, the final PFT results were not available in five patients, which might cause selection bias; however, the impact of these five patients might be small because the mean preoperative %VC in all 21 patients and that in 16 with final PFT results available were comparable (65.1% vs. 67.4%). Fourth, the choice of surgical procedure, either GFS or PF, was left to the discretion of each surgeon, which might have introduced indication bias. Fifth, the impact of each phenotype on respiratory function, including a history of pneumothorax, sarcopenia, pectus deformity, or cardiac function, was not considered. Sixth, the genotype–phenotype correlation was not considered in the present study because of the limited number of eligible cases. Because several studies to date have demonstrated genotype–phenotype correlations in Marfan syndrome, the phenotype of respiratory muscles may also differ among cases with different FBN1 variants18–21.

In conclusion, we investigated the mid- to long-term surgical outcomes in older patients with Marfan syndrome and EOS. Although most patients had acquired a considerably large final T1–T12 height with an average of 248.0 mm, the final respiratory function was not satisfactory in many cases. Because the predicted final T1–T12 height required for a final %VC of 60% was approximately 260 mm, GFS may be indicated even in older patients with Marfan syndrome and EOS, albeit with a high incidence of complications.

Methods

Data source and patients

We obtained retrospective data from six institutes in Japan, all of which are members of the Japan Spinal Deformity Institute (JSDI), namely, the University of Tokyo Hospital, Meijo Hospital, Kobe Medical Center, Fukuoka Children’s Hospital, Niigata University Medical Center, and Gunma Spine Center.

We included all patients with Marfan syndrome between 7.0 and 11.9 years of age at the time of index surgery who underwent GFS or PF for EOS with a final follow-up at or after 16 years of age, which is considered to be the cut-off for skeletal maturity. The diagnosis of Marfan syndrome was established according to the original or revised Ghent nosology. Surgical procedure, either GFS or PF, was determined by each surgeon based on the individual patient’s background.

Collected covariates

The collected covariates consisted of patient demographic data, radiographic parameters, surgery-related data, and PFT results. Patient demographic data included sex, age at initial surgery and final follow-up, follow-up period, body mass index at initial surgery and final follow-up, and coexisting pectus deformities. Radiographic parameters included preoperative and final Cobb angle of the main curve, preoperative maximum kyphotic angle, preoperative and final thoracic height (defined as the height between T1 and T12), and preoperative and final spinal height (defined as the height between T1 and S1, measured on standing whole-spine posterior-anterior and lateral radiographs). Preoperative skeletal maturity was also assessed on preoperative whole-spine radiographs using triradiate cartilage and the Risser grade. Surgery-related data included the type of surgery performed, number of fused vertebrae, and incidence and cause of UPROR.

Pulmonary function test

All patients underwent preoperative PFTs, and the %VC was selected for analysis. In patients with PFT conducted at or after 16 years of age, the final respiratory function was determined by the final %VC. In this study, %VC was used because some of the old cases lacked %FVC data. In cases without obstructive ventilatory disorders such as asthma, %VC and %FVC values are almost identical. We have confirmed that the cases included in this study did not have obstructive ventilatory disorders as an underlying condition.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using t-test, and Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical data. The association between the final thoracic height and final %VC was assessed using linear regression analysis. The threshold for significance was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro 16 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).
