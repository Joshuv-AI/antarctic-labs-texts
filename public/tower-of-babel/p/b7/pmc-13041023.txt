The development of a model of acculturation and health practices among sub-Saharan African Men (MAPS) in the United States: a grounded theory study of prostate cancer early detection
Authors: Ernest Kaninjing, Motolani Ogunsanya, Gladys Asiedu, Damian Francis, Kaitlin Van Voorhis, Whitney Heppner, Sabrina L. Dickey, Ashley Taylor, Jebessa Mijena, Getachew Dagne, Mary Ellen Young, Clement Gwede, Folakemi Odedina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe past two decades has seen a steady rise in the number of immigrants from sub-Saharan Africa to the United States (US). While this population represent one of the fastest growing segments of the US immigrant population, research examining the impact of migration on the health practices and behaviors among this population has lagged. Acculturation is the modification of an individual’s beliefs, values, and behavior upon contact with a different culture. This study aimed to enhance understanding of the links between acculturation and health practices among sub-Saharan Africa immigrants in the US.MethodsConstructivist Grounded Theory methodology was employed to develop a process model that explains the relationship between acculturation and health behaviors of sub-Saharan African immigrant men. This qualitative approach included in-depth interviews with 36 participants and a follow-up interview with 10 participants. Purposive sampling was used to recruit participants and audio recordings of interviews were transcribed, verified, and analyzed using an iterative constant comparative approach that comprised open, axial, and focused coding. Emerging constructs were identified and synthesized into a process model which was further refined with input from an advisory panel and participant checking.ResultsThe Model of Acculturation and Health Practices among sub-Saharan African immigrant men (MAPS) in the US can be categorized into four main pillars: inputs, contexts, mechanisms, and outcomes. Inputs conceptualize pre-immigration experience including reasons for migration. Contextual factors describe prevailing conditions in the US including the political and economic aspects surrounding immigration. Mechanisms depict how acculturation impacts health behaviors. Outcomes illustrate the proximal and distal outcomes.ConclusionThis Model of Acculturation and Health Practices among sub-Saharan African immigrant men identifies key constructs that should be considered when studying acculturation and health practices among sub-Saharan Africa immigrants in the US. Notably, MAPS can aid in providing healthcare providers with a comprehensive lens through which to understand the complexities of sub-Saharan African Immigrant men’s health behaviors. This study fills the void of dearth in theoretical models that elucidate behavioral adaptation and preventive behavior among sub-Saharan Africa immigrants in the US.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26613-z.

Introduction/background

Sub-Saharan African immigrants (SSAIs) in the United States (US) represent considerable geographic, linguistic, religious, and cultural diversity that reflect the varied histories of the African continent. The size of this population has increased 16-fold since 1980 [34]. As of 2018, 81% of all SSAIs in the US came from Eastern and Western Africa, and the leading countries of birth include Nigeria, Ethiopia, Ghana, Kenya, and Somalia [5, 24, 56]. The impetus for immigration among this population varies from seeking refuge from conflict and war to academic and economic opportunities. Despite their growing numbers and the diversity among this population, there is a dearth of research focusing on the impact of migration on SSAIs, particularly with regards to chronic diseases such as prostate cancer (CaP) [7, 41, 48].

It is estimated that in 2025, 313,780 men in the US were diagnosed with CaP, the second most common cancer among men. While the disease impacts men of all races and ethnicities, Black men bear a disproportionate burden, experiencing a 70% higher incidence [4]. Since African ancestry is an established risk factor for CaP, it is important to understand the attitudes and health practices of SSAI men relative to early detection of this disease. This is particularly challenging given the lack of unanimity among medical advisory panels about when to screen for CaP. While the US Preventive Services Task Force recommends that men aged 55 to 69 have a conversation with their physician about the risks and benefits of screening for CaP [29], the American Urological Association recommends baseline screening for CaP between ages 45 to 50 for men at average risk and from ages 40 to 45 for men at higher risk (African ancestry) [54]. The lack of unified guidelines places the burden on men to be aware of the potential risk of this disease and to advocate for themselves during their visits with clinicians.

Regrettably, research about the health practices and behaviors among SSAI men regarding early detection of CaP has been lacking [35, 39, 40]. Therefore, there is growing need for research to better understand the drivers of poor outcomes among SSAI, and to illuminate the influence of acculturation on CaP health-seeking behaviors. For example, a recent study found the proportional incidence ratio for CaP among sub-Saharan African immigrants to be 1.5 times higher compared to US born Blacks [36]. Elucidating the health needs, health seeking behaviors, and drivers of disparities in health outcomes among SSAI is essential to identify specific opportunities for culturally sensitive interventions aimed at addressing disparities among this population. To that end, this study aimed to develop a process model that explains the relationship between acculturation and health behaviors such as screening for CaP among sub-Saharan African immigrant men.

Acculturation and health behaviors

Acculturation refers to the modification of an individual’s beliefs, values and behavior upon contact with a different culture [11]. The acculturative process and its effects on behavior can vary widely among different individuals within the same ethnic group [8, 14]. While some immigrants easily assimilate to the culture of the host country, others straddle both their heritage and receiving cultures [47]. Some immigrants may prefer to live in ethnic enclaves and maintain minimal contact with the culture of the host country [9, 46, 47]. Some studies have assessed behaviors and attitudes related to acculturation via unidimensional markers such as length of residence in host country, language(s) spoken at home, adopted values, social affiliation, and cultural identification [1, 14, 21, 43, 47]. Invariably these measures of acculturation make different assumptions about how a person may acculturate and it is unclear whether these measures are sensitive to cultural differences [47, 55].

Since the acculturation process involves physical, psychological, cultural, and social changes as individuals adjust to a new culture and create new connections, [53] it is important to have robust frameworks that inform the exploration of constructs. Constructivist Grounded Theory (CGT) and The National Institute of Minority Health and Health Disparity (NIMHD) framework informed the exploration of cultural identity, separation, biculturalism, assimilation, and integration in this study. CGT is an effective theoretical tool that focuses on creating conceptual frameworks or models through inductive analysis. It facilitates the uncovering of social relationships, processes, and behaviors of groups and the conceptualization of hidden social and collective patterns in the area of interest [32]. In this study, we employed CGT in a substantive area as the NIMHD framework has never been applied to examine the impact of acculturation among SSAI in regard to CaP screening and lifestyle factors. Moreover, illuminating the influence of acculturation on CaP early detection behaviors is essential to inform the design of culturally sensitive interventions aimed at addressing disparities among this population.

Study objectives

To enhance scientific knowledge on socio cultural factors that may contribute or exacerbate health outcomes among SSAI men, the primary objective of this study was to develop a conceptual model elucidating acculturation-related behavioral health factors among SSAI men. We aimed to uncover a new conceptual model for identifying essential constructs and categories involved in the acculturation process among African immigrant men in the US and to foster a deeper understanding of the relationships among these constructs and categories. We hope the conceptual model will inform future opportunities for targeted, culturally sensitive CaP interventions among African immigrant men.

Methodology

Constructivist Grounded Theory (CGT) methodology was chosen as the best approach to develop a process model to explain the relationship between acculturation and health behaviors of SSAI, a phenomenon about which little is known [17]. CGT is a qualitative approach used to systematically uncover social relationships, processes, and behaviors of groups and to conceptualize the hidden social and collective patterns and constructions in the area of interest [32]. Key elements of this approach include: (a) the co-construction of the explanation (theory, model) between the researcher(s) and the participants; (b) the interview guide as a beginning point for data collection that evolves as data analysis proceeds concurrently with data collection; (c) data collection and analysis processes are captured through extensive memoing, (d) data collection continues until saturation is met, with sound decisions about purposeful and theoretical sampling to achieve saturation, (e) the development of a process model that illustrates study findings, supported by participant quotes, (f) testing the emerging model with subsequent data collection and analysis confirming its robustness, (f) describing the reflexivity of the researchers and how they engaged with their participants to co-create the research findings, and (g) describing the role of a community advisory board (CAB) members and other members of the research team in the development of all stages of the research [16, 18], p. 305, 2014a, [28]. In this study, we employed CGT in a substantive area to examine the impact of acculturation among SSAI on CaP early detection behaviors. The consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) 32-item checklist was followed [51] and further validation was achieved by examining the study in light of Braun and Clarke’s value-based guidelines for evaluating qualitative research [13].

Reflexivity

The study team engaged in personal and team reflexivity at all stages of the research process. Four members of the research team are SSAI, including the two interviewers and a third coder. A five-member Community Advisory Board (CAB), two of whom are SSAI men was engaged at the beginning of the research and participated in the development and modification of the interview guide, data analysis throughout the study, and confirmation of the model that emerged from the data. Researchers, being members of the group of interest, brought the emic perspective and opened the possibility of full engagement as the interviewers and participants readily established rapport and trust which are considerable barriers to overcome in studying this population. Other members of the research team brought an etic or outsider’s perspective, but all had experience working with Black men with CaP. Varying perspectives were regularly discussed at research team and CAB meetings adding breadth to the phenomenon of interest [15, 22].

Interview guide and data collection

Congruent with a CGT approach [13, 18], p. 305 [19, 30, 52], this study employed a framework that informed the interview guide for initial interviews. This framework identified during literature review aligned with the study goal of illuminating the influence of acculturation on CaP early detection behaviors among SSAI men. The NIMHD research framework depicts broad areas of health determinants that are important to understand health outcomes among minority populations [3]. This framework provided a launching path to exploring health behaviors and practices among study participants within the context of structural factors. The conceptual domains of influence offered by this framework (behavioral, physical or built environment, sociocultural environment, and health care system) were explored during in-depth interviews in relation to different levels of influence including individual, interpersonal, community and societal (see supplemental file). The interview guide was piloted with members of the CAB and recommended changes incorporated.

Examples of topic areas covered during interviews include:a) Health seeking behaviors and activities that participants were involved with; b) Participant’s willingness to openly discuss preventive measures against cancer with family members; c) How participants identify themselves and what aspects of their identity are important to them; d) Participant’s experience integrating to life in the United States; e) Social network and social support; f) Access and utilization of health services; and f) Interaction with health care providers.

A key focus area during the interviews consisted of the changes or adjustments that participants had to make post immigration, their strategies for coping, their support system (if any), psychological stress, and any barriers they may have encountered in seeking screening for CaP or treatment and care of CaP and how they navigated those challenges.

In line with CGT procedures [18], p. 305), the interview guide was iteratively modified as noteworthy experiences in earlier interviews were queried in subsequent interviews to explore emerging concepts and categories. For example, as participants in earlier interviews described how they felt when visiting their country of birth (some described the experience as feeling as an outsider, and another described it as being of a double mind or torn between allegiance to both country of birth and current residence), we incorporated new questions to further explore how connected or unconnected participants felt about their country of origin. During focused coding this concept was further developed into a sub construct called “Restless Mind” to depict the tension of belonging to two worlds. Field notes were taken during each in-depth interview and captured non-verbal cues such as body language, affect, and tone of voice. Interviews lasted approximately 60 min in a private setting or via zoom and were audio recorded.

Study participants and location

The study participants were SSAI men regardless of CaP diagnosis residing in the US, including residents of Georgia (GA), Florida (FL), and Minnesota (MN). According to the Migration Policy Institute's tabulations of the US Census Bureau American Community Survey and Decennial Census, in 2018 there were 114,396 African-born immigrants living in GA, 71,673 in FL, and 131,832 in MN [37]. These states represent a rich pool of potential participants to recruit from and a mixture of diverse source countries in sub-Saharan Africa. Sampling and recruitment

Informational flyers about the study were sent to potential participants identified through African cultural groups. Flyers were also posted at barber shops frequently visited by SSAI men. When a potential participant indicated interest by calling the number on the flyer or sending an e-mail, a screening call was set up to further explain the goals of the study and inquire about the potential participants’ country of birth, and demographic characteristics. Based on this screening call, appointments were set up for in-depth interviews. A sequential sampling approach was employed, starting with purposeful sampling and moving to theoretical sampling when constructs, and concepts began to emerge [50]. For example, our initial interviews purposefully targeted men who migrated from different geographic regions in Africa to explore similarities and differences in their experiences. Researchers also purposefully recruited men of different religious backgrounds and recent immigrants as well as those who have lived in the US for over 10 years. Theoretical sampling was used when we sought out participants with different experiences. For example, instead of men who had migrated for economic or educational opportunities men who had migrated due to violence or regional conflicts were sought for participation in the study [23, 44].

The initial set of 7 interviews provided a launching path for theoretical sampling guided by emerging data. Emerging constructs from the initial interviews led to iterative modification of the interview guide to follow up on recurring constructs from participant data. Confirmatory follow-up interviews were conducted with 10 participants during data analysis to verify emerging data about the conceptual model.

Data analysis

Each interview was transcribed and verified for accuracy before uploading into NVivo 13 (QSR International Inc., Burlington MA) for data management and coding. An iterative, constant-comparative approach, [12, 27] was employed in data analysis by a team of five trained qualitative researchers. The first step in this approach was open coding whereby each transcript was read line-by-line and independently coded by three researchers. Memos written after each interview informed initial interpretations and enhanced theoretical sensitivity. The second step was axial coding and consisted of grouping initial codes into constructs and constructs into categories. Simultaneously, variational sampling [23] was conducted to examine and validate the relationships among categories. Emerging concepts were compared with more data from fresh interviews and follow-up interviews to sharpen definitions and to refine properties. Additional interviews were conducted to further explore constructs and categories until saturation was achieved [28]. The third step involved focused coding to refine and rearrange codes to identify core categories or pillars. The core pillars laid the foundational structure to formulate theory. Discriminate sampling with key participants to gather more data on the categories or pillars was used to verify the theory developed. Theoretical saturation was achieved when the richness of the acculturation experiences of diverse men from SSAI was fully understood and there was nothing else to explore on this topic among this population. This was the culmination of an iterative process of data collection, analysis, exploration, and comparing data with more data from participants. The final model is comprised of four core categories or pillars: Inputs, Mechanism, Outcomes and Contextual Factors.

Rigor

To minimize bias, three researchers independently coded and analyzed each transcript. Additionally, follow-up interviews with 10 participants was employed to verify the accuracy of transcripts and preliminary constructs [51]. During axial coding, if there was less than 85% agreement among the research team, the transcripts were reviewed to resolve any lack of congruence.

Data source triangulation

Triangulation of data sources included the use of field notes recorded by the two interviewers during each interview to contextualize observations. Field notes were included in the analysis and interpretation of data [44] which led to enhanced understanding of the context of acculturation and health behaviors among SSAI men. Moreover, confirmatory interviews were held with 10 participants who had been interviewed previously, to review and provide feedback and critique of the emerging explanatory model. This process enhanced trustworthiness of the final model [15]. Notably, the confirmatory interviews added different voices to the analysis and interpretation of data which is in sync with the co-construction approach of CGT [32].

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

Theoretical saturation was achieved after 36 interviews and 10 follow-up interviews. Of the 36 participants, 33% were CaP survivors and 67% were healthy men. Participants’ age ranged from 40 to 70 years old, and 44% of participants were born in West Africa while 56% were born in East Africa. Seventy-two percent of study participants had lived in the US for at least 10 years and 77% had education beyond the high school level. Similarly, 75% of the participants reported having a routine doctor’s visit within the last 12 months.

Sixty-two percent of participants reported being screened for CaP. In terms of religious affiliation, 50% of the study participants identified as Christians while 44% identified as Muslims. The conceptual model comprises fifteen constructs and four main pillars (core categories) rooted in the experiences of the study participants.Fig. 1Model of Acculturation and Health Practices among sub-Saharan African Immigrant Men (MAPS)

Model of Acculturation and Health Practices among sub-Saharan African Immigrant Men (MAPS)

Pillar 1 comprises the inputs, which are the starting conditions including push and pull factors towards immigration. These include individual or personal characteristics that influence decisions about migration, such as age, and family structure. Additionally, the precursors of migration or factors that provide the impetus for individuals to leave their homeland and the factors attracting them to the new country are part of the inputs. Pillar 2, context, represents the political, economic, and social conditions that frame the model. Pillar 3 is the mechanism of how acculturation impacts health behaviors. The relationship between the constructs in the mechanism section (middle of the model) are depicted by the unidirectional arrows between pre-immigration and transition period and between the transition period and adaptation. Bidirectional arrows represent the relationship between adaptation and assimilation, and between assimilation and integration. The bidirectional feedback loop indicates that an individual can regress from assimilation or integration back to adaptation due to life circumstances such as having a divorce or the rejection of their petition for permanent status in the US. Such adverse circumstances can also cause an individual to be stagnant as opposed to moving from adaptation to assimilation or to integration. Unlike the pre-immigration and the transition periods, the adaptation, assimilation and integration phases are fluid and non-linear. Pilar 4 is outcomes or health impacts. In the middle of the model are factors that explain how acculturation impacts health among SSAI while to the right of the model are the health outcomes. Key findings related to the four pillars of the MAPS model

MAPS inputs

The basis for understanding the lived experiences and health behaviors of any immigrant starts with examining a key question: “Why did they leave their native country?” While there are varied reasons for migrating, they can be broadly categorized into two constructs: Push and Pull factors. Push factors are the conditions that cause an individual to consider leaving their country of birth. Examples of push factors include political conditions such as civil unrest, persecution, and wars that can cause an individual to feel unsafe and desire to physically leave that environment. Furthermore, limited economic conditions that made life difficult for an individual and inability to provide for themselves and their family were common reasons for leaving native country in search of prosperity abroad. Similarly, the desire to further one’s education, particularly in subject areas not available in the native country prompted some participants to migrate to the US. Pull factors are the conditions and circumstances that lure or attract someone to move to a new destination. These include peace, political stability and freedom of speech and association, legal pathways to obtain the necessary documentation to work and earn a living and opportunities to enhance health, education, and economic well-being. Personal characteristics was another construct within the inputs category as participants were more inclined to migrate at a younger age among those who voluntarily migrated. Having immediate or distant family relatives in the US was a big draw for men considering migration.

MAPS context

Our data analysis yielded five constructs under the pillar of contextual factors of acculturation. They consist of events and circumstances preceding immigration and the political and social climate of the US upon arrival. These factors are the foundational underpinning of the context in which acculturation occurs. Emerging constructs under contextual factors are listed at the bottom of the model and depicted by dotted lines around the entire model to indicate the continuing influence of these factors. They include US laws and policies like the 1965 Immigration act that ushered equality of entry to the US regardless of country of birth. Another example is the 1990 Diversity Immigrant Visa Program that aimed to foster legal immigration from countries other than major sending countries.

Additional constructs include the political and economic conditions, and enforcement of immigration laws. Discrimination and or racism experienced (or perceived) by participants at the workplace, healthcare setting, or social encounters also provide context for understanding the lived experiences of SSAI. Similarly, mistrust of healthcare providers or the health care system expressed by participants was often a by-product of perceived or genuine discrimination and had the chilling effect of creating reluctance among participants to seek care or preventive screening. These contextual factors contribute to determining the extent to which individuals adapt to their new setting in the US and influence their participation (or lack thereof) in health behaviors like seeking annual screening examinations.

MAPS outcomes

Our data analysis showed that the health consequences of acculturation and health behaviors can be either proximal (short term) or distal (long term) and these two outcomes make up the final pillar. Proximal outcomes among participants were manifested in terms of low perceived susceptibility to CaP diagnosis. Additionally, there was low priority given to screening and seeking care after onset of symptoms was not always by choice. Immigrants, particularly during the transition phase, prioritize economic opportunity and becoming financially independent over taking time off work for preventive health visits.

The main distal outcome was advanced presentation of CaP at the time of diagnosis. Primarily, this was the consequence of proximal action or inaction. In some cases, structural (contextual) factors like inadequate insurance contributed to delayed diagnosis.

Discussion

This study aimed to illuminate the effects of acculturation on CaP health behaviors among SSAI men and to offer a new conceptual framework for understanding the acculturation process among this population. To the best of our knowledge, the model presented in this study is the first to capture the acculturation experiences and provide insights about health practices regarding early detection for CaP among a diverse sample of SSAI men.

The MAPS provides structure and identifies important features of acculturation and health behaviors to be considered when studying SSAI men in the US. We illustrate the multidimensions of acculturation via four pillars that represent a phenomenon taking place over time. The model focuses on changes that occur at the individual level, while identifying structural or contextual factors that significantly impact the acculturation experience of SSAI men. Elucidating the social and contextual factors including conditions in country of birth provides insights into factors that shape health behaviors. For example, over reliance on symptomatic care among SSAI men in the US is a carryover from the immigrant’s lived experience in Africa. This health compromising behavior gets compounded by psychological stress experienced during the transition period when finding a job, securing housing, transportation and obtaining legal status are prioritized over wellness checkup. These acculturation experiences affect health behavior in the sense that preventive health screenings receive low priority and contributes to delays in seeking care and ultimately, poor health outcomes.

Our findings are akin to the work of Riedel and colleagues [42] in the development of the integrated acculturation theory that combines elements of Berry’s acculturation model [10] with Antonovsky’s salutogenic theory [6]. The inputs and contextual pillars in MAPS mirror the socio-cultural, historical and biographical constructs in the salutogenic model [6] which depict the personal characteristics and the social environment that create hurdles or opportunities for health enhancing behaviors. The sense of coherence construct in the salutogenic model is aligned with adaptability to stressors of life and problem solving.

Findings from this study identify two constructs that align with Berry’s [10] acculturation strategies, namely: assimilation and integration. Our study participants consisted of immigrants who voluntarily moved to the US, as well as refugees who fled civil war and political unrest in their native country. However, the strategies of separation outlined by Berry [10] whereby immigrants prioritize heritage (afro-centric) values while avoiding interaction with others was not prominent in the experiences of participants in this study. Instead, study participants were engaged in their local communities, learning to speak English, interpreting for newly arrived immigrants while retaining important characteristics of their culture such as speaking the native tongue, listening to music from home country and wearing traditional dress. Similarly, the marginalization strategy espoused in Berry’s acculturating strategy was not prominent in the current study. While some participants mentioned experiencing discrimination and encounters where they perceived being treated disrespectfully or with malice, they were quick to move on and purposefully avoid dwelling on these negative encounters or experiences. This was not surprising given the fact that participants who had experienced the trauma of a civil war in their home country, and other adverse life circumstances coped by focusing on the positive benefits of their new life in the US. Moreover, the positive attributes of their new life in the US (job, quality health care, educational advancement for their children) outweighed the negative encounters with prejudice and discrimination.

Another strength of the MAPS is the universality of constructs that can be used across other immigrant groups to better understand behavioral patterns that influence health outcomes. For example, in the pre-immigration phase, we note remote acculturation via movies, food, family and interactions with Americans abroad. These aspects of remote acculturation influence health behaviors before the immigrant arrives in the US. The Outcomes construct adds a public health perspective to this acculturation study. The proximal and distal outcomes highlight factors that contribute to or exacerbate disparities in the burden of CaP among SSAI.

Conceptual contributions of MAPS

Despite the growing evidence of links between acculturation and health behavior, [2, 14, 20, 36, 38] no study has proposed an explanatory theory or model that elucidates contributing factors to poor health outcomes among SSAI. Moreover, there is a dearth of theoretical models with a public health perspective that explore individual, structural, and cultural factors among SSAI and identify links between acculturation and health outcomes. Given the complexities involved in migration and the adjustment process in a new environment, theoretical models could provide useful structures and identify interrelated concepts leading to a better understanding of drivers of poor health outcomes. This study adds to existing literature by presenting a theory-driven model that illuminates health behaviors such as delayed utilization of screening and preventive services among SSAI. The mechanism construct explains how acculturation influences health behaviors and health outcomes. This model allows for the conceptualization of factors relevant to acculturation and CaP lifestyle factors such as physical activity and diet which are important from a public health perspective, as the number of SSAIs in the US continues to rise.

Implications

Findings from this study have important implications for health interventions targeting SSAI men, particularly in addressing CaP-related disparities. By mapping out the pillars of acculturation—inputs, context, mechanisms, and outcomes—the MAPS can aid in presenting healthcare providers with a comprehensive lens through which to understand the complexities of SSAI men’s health behaviors. Of specific importance to health interventions are the mechanisms of acculturation (pre-immigration, transition period, adaptation, assimilation, integration). Individuals in the assimilation and integration stages are likely to have higher familiarity with the US, health system and higher health literacy compared to immigrants in the transition period or adaptation state. Therefore, health messages can be targeted to individuals at specific stages. For instance, a study examining the influence of health literacy and acculturation on cancer screening behaviors among older Chinese Americans found that higher levels of acculturation and health literacy were associated with increased likelihood of cancer screening [33]. This highlights the importance of considering acculturation in health interventions to enhance screening behaviors.

Additionally, healthcare professionals can use the MAPS to identify cultural, structural, and psychological factors that may influence the decision-making process regarding CaP screening and early detection. The MAPS can also guide future research by highlighting the need to explore how acculturation influences not only health behaviors but also health outcomes within immigrant populations [25, 26]. It offers a novel perspective for studying the burden of CaP among SSAI men and could serve as a foundation for larger studies that investigate the role of acculturation in other health conditions.

The cultural concordance between interviewers and participants in this study contributed to the richness of the data generated as both interviewers were immigrants from sub-Saharan Africa with the cultural competence to build trust and rapport with participants that allowed participants to share their lived experiences. Moreover, these trained interviewers were able to use their nuanced understanding of the context and cultural experiences of the participants in the data analysis and interpretation. However, the insider perspective has the potential risk of bias that can affect objectivity during data analysis. This risk was mitigated in this study by having coinvestigators with an etic experience and an advisory board that provided additional outsider perspectives and contributed to the data analysis and development of the MAPS model.

Limitations and further directions

Our study participants comprised of first generation SSAI men, most of whom immigrated to the US as adults. These immigrants had stronger and more direct ties to their country of birth and therefore were more nostalgic towards afro-centric values. The views and experiences of second or third generation immigrants or SSAI men who immigrated as children or adolescents may be different. Moreover, participants in this study were recruited from three states, Georgia, Florida, and Minnesota and their experiences might not be generalizable to other SSAI men living in different states.

The transition period was characterized by major life events contributing to psychological stress. This is an area that requires further research to explore the effects of stress during the transition period to better understand how actual or perceived stress impact the psychosocial health of SSAI men.

Conclusion

The MAPS offers a comprehensive understanding of the relationships between specific mechanisms of acculturation and how they impact health outcomes among SSAI. It offers a new conceptual framework for understanding the acculturation process among SSAI men in the US and adds to the growing literature on acculturation and health behaviors by providing insights into overlooked factors such as contextual factors prior to and during migration (social, political, and ecological environment) that influence health behaviors among immigrants. Furthermore, it identifies factors that influence preventive health behaviors and the outcomes. For example, it could inform the design of effective, culturally sensitive interventions aimed at promoting asymptomatic CaP screening and early detection among SSAI men. Early detection of cancer improves opportunities for better outcomes, including cures and long-term survival. Behavioral factors have been shown to offer the single greatest opportunity to reduce incidence in cancer [31, 45, 49]. The MAPS promotes understanding of acculturation and health practices among SSAI men and provides a guide to better understand and address behavioral factors that lead to detrimental health outcomes.
