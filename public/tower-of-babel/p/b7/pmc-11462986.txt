The relationship between childhood trauma and mental health status among Chinese vocational high school adolescents: the mediating effect of poor self-control and internet addiction
Authors: Yuhan Su, Zhuohui Chen, Ziwei Teng, Mengdong Liu, Yuanguang Yang, Jindong Chen, Xiaoman Liu, Jing Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMental health problems among adolescents are a common concern globally. However, its relationship with childhood trauma is not clearly understood from the existing studies. Therefore, this study aims to explore the relationships among childhood trauma, mental health, self-control, and internet addiction in Chinese vocational high school students.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among vocational high school students in China from October 2020 to December 2020. Standardized questionnaires were used to collect basic information regarding childhood trauma, self-control, psychological state, and social demographics. A structural equation model was used to study the relationships among internet addiction, self-control, childhood trauma, and mental health.ResultsA total of 3368 individuals participated in the study. The results revealed the mediating effects of poor self-control and internet addiction on the association between childhood trauma and mental health.ConclusionsInternet addiction and low self-control play mediating roles in childhood trauma and mental health. Clarifying these relationships will help formulate better-targeted interventions to improve the mental health of Chinese vocational high school students and aid in interventions to treat and prevent mental health problems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20271-9.Keywords: Childhood trauma, Mental health, Self-control, Internet addiction, Adolescents

Introduction

Adolescents’ mental health problems have always been a global study topic in recent years because of its high prevalence rates and serious consequence. Across hundreds of studies, the prevalence rate of mental health problems is up to 35% among college students [1]. Their mental health problems are generally manifested as depression, anxiety and stress. In addition, the prevalence rates of severe anxiety (15%), depression (11%), and stress (11%) symptoms reported by American college students are also high [2]. In addition, from a Chinese survey, 35.27%, 42.32%, and 21.87% high school students from Guangdong province report symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress [3]. The high prevalence rates may be caused by childhood adversity, psychiatric problems in their parents and bullying [4–7]. Besides, the diagnostic frequency of bipolar disorder in adolescents is increasing [8], which makes bipolar disorder also plays significant role in mental health problems of adolescents. Moreover, these mental health problems also bring many adverse effects. They may disturb adolescents’ academic achievement and interpersonal relationships [9], which may further worsen their mental condition. Additionally, mental health problems also might be associated with increased risk for some negative behaviors, like suicide, substance abuse disorders and unhealthy diets [9, 10]. These behaviors not only harmful to physical health also life threatening. Furthermore, the mechanisms by which teenagers and adults cope with stress are basically the same, but the consequences of “self-medication” are more challenging for teenagers than adults because their brains are still developing [11]. The high prevalence rates and serious consequence both emphasize the importance of intervening in adolescent mental health.

Studies have identified multiple factors related to mental health problems of adolescents. Childhood trauma, a general term encompassing unwanted psychological or physical harm [12], may be related to the mental health disorders. Adverse childhood experience may increase the probability of mental health diagnosis [13, 14]. Additionally, self-control, the ability to delay immediate gratification in order to achieve better results in the future and to inhibit inappropriate emotions, desires, and behaviors [15], may be implicated in the positive psychological regulation [16]. It means that poor ability of self-control may be correlated with the occurrence of mental health problems. Moreover, internet addiction (IA), also known as Pathological Internet Use, Internet Addiction Disorder, and Problematic Internet Use that affects daily life and leads to stress in social relationships [17], also is found the significant correlation with mental health problems. Multiple cross-sectional studies have shown that increased symptoms of depression, anxiety, stress, and bipolar disorder are strongly associated with IA [18–21]. Longitudinal studies have also shown that the severity of anxiety symptoms is positively associated with IA, and that baseline depression and anxiety status are independent predictors of the incidence of new IA [22]. Besides, it seems that IA problem needs more attention among Chinese high school students compared with that in other countries, because its prevalence of adolescents, which is range from 3.0% to 26.8% in Hongkong, is higher than other regions of world [23]. Overall, childhood trauma, self-control and IA are associated with mental health problems.

Furthermore, there are also some correlations between those factors related to mental health problems. There is strong evidence that suggests childhood trauma is associated with vulnerability to IA [24] and traumatic experience increases the possibility of abusing internet [20, 25–27]. It may be related to IA being regarded as a method of compensating for childhood trauma [28], and being used as an escapism-related way to dissociate from unbearable psychological states when dealing with traumatic events [26, 29]. Studies showed that low self-control, which may be affected by childhood trauma [30], was a risk factor for gaming addiction in adolescents [31, 32] and was related to the severity of IA [33, 34]. Overall, childhood trauma, self-control and IA not only may promote mental health problems in adolescents, but also have a mutual influence between each other, therefore this study will focus on these potential determinants to explore the direct and indirect effect between them and mental health of adolescents. However, previous research has only explored the pairwise relationship between childhood trauma, mental health, IA and poor self-control, without comprehensively discussing whether some of these factors will influence those pairwise relationships.

Compared to adulthood, adolescence is the most significant period of brain psychological development. In this period, the brain’s circuitries that are related to cognitive control, emotion and reward are substantially developing, and these circuitries are associated with the development and perseverance of mental health problems like depression and anxiety [35–38]. It means that these developing circuitries may be unstable and easily influenced by the factors mentioned earlier. Moreover, adolescents are more prone to addictive behaviors due to an immature prefrontal cortex, inadequate self-control, and poor judgment [15], which means that mental health problems mediated by IA are more likely to occur during adolescence. Therefore, schools and parents should pay more attention to the mental health and IA behaviors of adolescents. In addition, this study is aimed to the vocational students who is mostly in adolescent. Different from regular Chinese high school students, Chinese vocational high school students are expected to learn technical skills for the labor market [39]. It means that they may need to learn additional professional knowledge while completing regular academic content, which may cause that they have different performance in mental health, self-control and IA. Previous studies have shown that the score of Depression Self-Rating Scale for Children (DSRSC) of Chinese vocational school students is higher than that of Chinese normal high school [40] and vocational school students may have a higher rates of self-injurious behavior and probability of suicidal ideation [41]. At present, there is a lack of survey on childhood trauma, self-control, mental health and IA among Chinese vocational high school students. Therefore, study focus on the relationships among these factors of vocational high school students is necessary.

Based on this, we hypothesized that IA, bipolar symptoms (defined as the occurrence of 7 or more items of the 13 proposed in a self-report measure of manic episodes in this study [42]) and low self-control were associated with stress, depression and anxiety and that they provided an explanation for the relationship between childhood trauma and mental health problems. Therefore, we conducted this study using structural equation modeling to: (1) explore the association between mental health (including stress, depression, anxiety, and bipolar symptoms) and IA among Chinese vocational high school adolescents, (2) identify whether there were direct effects of childhood trauma on mental health, and (3) investigate whether IA and self-control mediated the relationship between childhood trauma and mental health (Fig. 1). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the relationship between childhood trauma, IA, self-control, and mental health among Chinese vocational high school adolescents.

Materials and methods

Settings and participants

Participants

A cross-sectional design was used. A total of 3650 individuals, selected from two vocational high schools by convenient sampling method in Hunan Province, China between October 2020, and December 2020, participated in this study. Two hundred eighty-two were excluded because they either refused to participate, failed to finish all the questions, or answered too fast. Therefore, 3368 adolescents participated in this study.

Procedures

The questionnaire was distributed in November 2020. We distributed questionnaires simultaneously to all the participants and collected the responses within the limited time. The questionnaire contains guidelines and teachers were trained by psychiatric professionals on how to give guidance to students regarding filling out the questionnaires, making sure they knew the exact meaning of the questions, and providing help to students if they became confused. We exclude the students whose submission time was less than 600 s. The study was anonymous, and informed consent was obtained from the participants and their parents or guardian of the child before they received the questionnaires. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulation.

Settings

Students in this study were from Lixian Vocational High School in Hunan Province and Taoyuan Vocational High School in Hunan Province, and there are a total of 257 public vocational schools in Hunan Province. Most students in these two schools are not admitted to high school and choose to attend vocational high schools. Unlike regular high schools, vocational high schools start choosing majors such as clothing design, mold design, auto repair, etc. Additionally, the age ranges of students in the vocational high schools in China is generally 15–18.

Measures

Basic characteristics

Basic sociodemographic information was collected from the students, including age, gender, community type (urban or rural), family income/year (in yuan), parents’ education levels, family type, and whether the participants smoked, drank, colored their hair, or had tattoos.

Questionnaire measurement of mental health

In this study, the evaluation of mental health mainly includes depression, anxiety, and stress-related symptoms.

Stress and symptoms of depression and anxiety

This study used the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21), which included three 7-item self-report subscales, to measure depression, anxiety, and stress in the students [43] over the preceding seven days and it showed a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.94 in the present study [44]. DASS-21 items are scored on a four-point (0–3) Likert scale; the individual score of each subscale was calculated by summing the corresponding items and then multiplying them by 2. The cut-off score is nine for depression symptoms, seven for anxiety symptoms, and fourteen for stress symptoms. Additionally, Cronbach’s alpha test will be used to measure the reliability of the data of DASSS-21 in this study [45].

Bipolar symptoms

The Mood Disorder Questionnaire (MDQ) is a widely used self-rated screening instrument for bipolar disorders [46] and it showed a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.83 in the present study. The MDQ contains 13 items and uses a yes/no response format, and a score of 7 or more suggests a positive result that requires additional comprehensive assessment for bipolar disorder [47].

Questionnaire measurement of childhood trauma

The Childhood Trauma Questionnaire-Short Form (CTQ-SF) consists of 28-items (25 clinical items and 3 validation items) that are each rated on a five‐point scale, ranging from 1 (never true) to 5 (very often true). The CTQ-SF is a self-reporting measure that assesses five forms of childhood trauma: emotional abuse (items 3, 8, 14, 18, 25), physical abuse (items 9, 11, 12, 15, 17), sexual abuse (items 20,21,23,24,27), physical neglect (items 5, 7, 13, 19, 28), and emotional neglect (items 1, 2, 4, 6, 26) [48] and it showed a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85 in the present study. Items 2, 5, 7, 13, 19, 26, and 28 require reverse scoring. The sum scores of the CTQ-SF ranges from 25 to 125 points, with a higher score indicating a higher severity of Childhood trauma. The evaluation criteria were the following: emotional abuse (score ≥ 13), physical abuse (score ≥ 10), sexual abuse (score ≥ 8), physical neglect (score ≥ 15), and emotional neglect (score ≥ 10) [49]. If one of the subscales meets the above conditions, it is considered a person with moderate or severe childhood trauma.

Questionnaire measurement of self-control

The Dual-Mode of Self-Control Scale (DMSC-S) consists of 21 items intended to measure a participant’s "good control" (problem solving, future time perspective) and "poor control" (impulsivity, distractibility, and poor delay of gratification) [50] and it showed a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.82 in this study. The scale gives participants options to rate each item on a five-point scale, ranging from 1 (not at all true for me) to 5 (very true for me). In this study, only the poor control subscale was used. Higher scores indicate less control of the student.

Questionnaire measurement of IA

The Chinese Internet Addiction Scale-Revision (CIAS‐R) measures participants' IA [51] and it showed a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.96 in this study. The CIAS‐R consists of 19 self-reported items, and it is rated on a four-point (1–4) Likert scale. Total scores can range from 0 to 76. In this study, a CIAS‐R score > 53 was considered indicative of IA [52].

Statistics

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS 26.0, M‐Plus 7.4, and MATLAB for MAC software and plotted using GraphPad Prism 8. A Kolmogorov–Smirnov one-sample test was used to measure the normal distribution of the continuous variables. Since the continuous data did not fit the normal distribution, the reported scores were shown as the first quartile, the median, and the third quartile. The categorical variables and continuous variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test and the Mann–Whitney U test, respectively. The correlations between IA and the symptoms of stress, depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, and childhood trauma (including emotional abuse, emotional neglect, physical neglect, and physical abuse), and the correlation between IA and self-control (including impulsivity, distractibility, and poor delay of gratification) were calculated using Spearman's correlation.

Structural equation model (SEM) was used to evaluate the hypothesis regarding the mediating effects of IA and self-control on the relationship between childhood trauma and mental health. The demographic variables (i.e., gender, hair coloring, smoking, and drinking) were significant between the two groups (from the two different vocational schools) in the univariate analysis and they were covariates in SEM. The unstandardized (B) and standardized (β) coefficients were calculated to measure potential direct and indirect relationships among the variables of interest. The following indices were evaluated to test the model for the satisfactory goodness of fit: a comparative fit index (CFI) > 0.90 (> 0.95 great, > 0.90 acceptable), a Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) > 0.90 (> 0.95 great, > 0.90 acceptable), a root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) < 0.10 (< 0.05 good, 0.05–0.10 moderate), and a standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) < 0.09 [53–56]. The significance level of the α threshold was 0.05 (two-tailed).

Demographic characteristics

According to the Cronbach’s alpha test, the results of DASS-21 in this study is reliable (Cronbach’s alpha is 0.945). Table 1 and Table S1-S3 showed the basic socio-demographic data and characteristics of the participants. The proportion of participants reported the symptoms of stress, anxiety and depression is 21.0% (706/3368), 49.2% (1657/3368) and 35.9% (1210/3368) respectively. 78.4% of the participants were between 15 and 18 years old, 43.9% were male, and 89.6% were from rural areas (Table 1). Family income and parents’ education levels were found no statistically significant differences between the participants, regardless of the presence of mental health disorders (all p > 0.05). Additionally, the mental health problems is related to the higher risk of IA (stress: 28.3%, anxiety: 18.6%, depression: 22.6%), bipolar symptoms (stress: 31.7%, anxiety: 28.3%, depression: 29.0%), emotional abuse (stress: 19.8%, anxiety: 11.2%, depression: 14.6%), physical abuse (stress: 19.3%, anxiety: 14.5%, depression: 18.8%), sexual abuse (stress: 22.7%, anxiety: 17.7%, depression: 22.6%) and physical neglect (stress: 70.7%, anxiety: 66.7%, depression: 69.5%). Additionally, it is worth noting that emotional neglect is only associated with higher risk of anxiety (43.4%) and depression (50.0%) (p < 0.05, Table S1-S3, the first and third quartiles are shown in Fig. 2). Additionally, from Table S5, only drinking may influence the mental health problems among those potential confounders (p < 0.05).

Structural equation modelling

Figure 3 shows the results of the final SEM, and the full data of SEM is shown in figure S1 and table S5. The observable variables are represented by rectangles, while the latent variables are represented by ovals in SEM. To testify the hypothesis of the complicated interactions among variables, this study approached each latent variables by their observable variable (using subfactors of scales), which is continuous. Additionally, There were direct effects of childhood trauma (β = 0.577, p < 0.001), poor self-control (β = 0.410, p < 0.01), and internet addiction (β = 0.351, p < 0.001) on mental health. Childhood trauma also had indirect effect on mental health through poor self-control and mental health (β = 0.577, p < 0.001). The goodness-of-fit indices (i.e., CFI = 0.936; TLI = 0.917; RMSEA = 0.052; SRMR = 0.054) indicated that the SEM was a satisfactory fit.

Discussion

This is the first study which elucidates the direct and indirect relationships between childhood trauma and IA, and emphasizes the importance of the potential mediating roles of mental health and self-control in IA in Chinese vocational high school adolescents. Based on a sample of 3368 Chinese vocational high school adolescents, this study revealed three main findings: (1) the proportion of participants reported stress, anxiety and depression symptoms is 21.0%, 49.2% and 35.9%, respectively, while 28.3% of the participants with stress symptoms, 18.6% of them with anxiety symptoms and 22.6% of them with depression symptoms reported IA problem, 31.7% of the participants reported stress, 28.3% of the participants with anxiety symptoms and 29.0% of them with depression symptoms showed bipolar symptoms. (2) Childhood trauma, self-control, and IA are associated with mental health. (3) Childhood trauma had a significant impact on mental health in the study population. Based on the results, we conclude that IA and low self-control play mediating roles in childhood trauma and mental health. After adjusting for the sociodemographic factors to conduct the SEM analysis, we examined the direct and indirect effects of childhood trauma on mental health. We determined that the indirect effects of childhood trauma on mental health were influenced by low self-control and IA.

The rates of mental health problems in this study is similar to that from other Chinese survey (3), but slightly higher than that found in a national study of Jordanian high school students, in which reporting prevalence of anxiety and depression are 18.3% and 34.2% respectively [57]. This difference may be caused by students from different country and the different sample size that the national study includes 200,000 participants. In addition, academic pressure may also lead to higher prevalence of anxiety and depression among Chinese vocational high school students. Vocational high school students not only need to learn the basic courses as normal high school students, but also have to learn their major courses. It may lead high academic pressure, which might cause increased prevalence of anxiety and depression. In contrast, a study reports 65% high-school adolescents in Bangladesh experience stress symptoms [58], and this prevalence is higher than that of in this study. This difference may be related to different ways to regulate students. Vocational high school students are more like college students in the way of training, as vocational students choose their own majors, are under less control of teachers and parents, and are not under strict supervision.

The overall model revealed childhood trauma is could positively predict mental health problems which is consistent with previous studies [59, 60]. The positive relationship between mental health and childhood trauma suggests that if vocational students are with adverse childhood experience, they might be at high risk of suffering from mental disorders. In addition, childhood trauma may increase the risk of serious mental health problems by effecting the development of central nervous system. It has been proved that adverse childhood experience is identified as toxic stressors that may negatively influence the brain development and increase the lifelong risk for mental disorders, and this harmful effect could be reduced by supportive relationships [59]. It reveals the importance of early intervention in childhood trauma and suggests that teachers and public institutions could take some interventions in order to protect injured teenagers from the toxic stressors and prevent them from the harm of mental disorder. However, an interesting result of this study is that among all three mental health problems in this study, emotional neglect was only positively correlated with an increased risk of depression. This positive relationship, which is also proved in other studies [61, 62], may be related to emotional neglect involved negative self-referential processing, which is detrimental to the depressive cognition style [62]. Different from this study, anxiety and stress are also found a positive correlation with emotional neglect [63]. This difference may be caused by the different assessment instrument that emotional neglect is assessed by the Family Health History Questionnaire in the latter study. Additionally, the positive correlation between IA and mental health problems also be found in this study, and it suggests that adolescents with IA may be more likely to report psychiatric problems compared to those without IA. From many previous studies, the relationship between IA and mental health problems are bidirectional [64, 65]. IA is always considered as a solution to escape from negative emotions [66], which means that a person who has been in negative emotions for a long time is more likely to be addicted to the internet. This situations is similar in a Chinese study that depression significantly predicted IA in 1 year among boys in middle schools and it is not significant among girls [67]. Conversely, IA also increase the risk of depression [68]. For example, people with IA were approximately 2.5 times more likely to report depression compared with those without IA [68, 69], and it may be caused by IA reducing their sleep and exercise time [70]. In addition, maternal care is found it may directly affect adolescents’ IA problems [71]. It suggests that a nice family care may play a significant role in solving those mental health problems caused by IA problems.

Furthermore, this study also finds IA and low self-control play mediating roles in childhood trauma and mental health. IA may influence neurobiological pathway of how adverse childhood experience influence mental health problems. Exposure to childhood adversity may increase the risk for psychopathologies related amygdala-prefrontal cortex (PFC) circuits [72], and orbital PFC also have been proved that its the cortical thickness is reduced in adolescents with IA [73]. It means that the development of IA may change the structure of PFC, which may affect the PFC related pathway leading mental disorders. Moreover, unlike adulthood, adolescence is a time when the ability to exhibit self-control is weak, as the circuits in the brain that control cognition are still developing [15]. As the result, adolescents with low self-control may increase the risk of IA, which is also found among Korean junior high school adolescents [31]. It suggests that self-control may also play a significant role in childhood trauma and mental health by influencing the development of IA.

Additionally, the relationship between self-control and mental health may be also bidirectional. People with low self-control tend to report higher levels of mental disorder symptoms as their IA increases, while people with high self-control tend to report lower levels of mental disorder symptoms as their IA increases. The reason may be that people with low self-control lack the ability to regulate their emotions and behaviors and are vulnerable to IA, but for people with high self-control, they adopt the right methods (e.g. Divert attention to work or asking help for doctors) to deal with IA. Although there is significant evidence that an individual’s mental state plays an important mediating role between emotional trauma and IA, most studies have ignored bipolar disorder (BD) related symptoms, and instead, focused on depression, anxiety, and stress. Park et al. [21] reported that the incidence of BD increased among individuals with behavioral addictions, although the sample size in the study was small. However, similar results can be obtained from a larger sample size. Depressive symptoms, such as decreased interest and willpower, in patients, can distract clinicians from further screening for manic symptoms [74]. For example, only 20% of BD patients with depressive episodes were diagnosed with BD in the first year [75]. This study included BD in the context of mental health and added clarity to the existing literature.

This study had several limitations. Firstly, participants were selected only from two vocational schools, which would influence the generalizability of the results. Secondly, the data providers of family income are adolescents, which may influence the data accuracy. Thirdly, this study was a cross-sectional study, so it could not reveal causality or changes in the students' emotions and coping styles as they aged and it not possible to disentangle causal relationship among self-control, IA, and mental health in adolescents. In future, it will be necessary to include a larger and more diverse longitudinal sample. In addition, the participants were non-clinical individuals, and all the measures were self-rated. Additional studies in a clinical setting are needed to investigate the effects of patients’ emotional states and self-control on the association between childhood trauma and mental health. Finally, this study exclude post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) that may be associated with childhood trauma IA [76].

Conclusion

Our results show that mental health problems are common among Chinese vocational high school students, and it is associated with a high incidence of IA, and bipolar symptoms. Additionally, IA and low self-control plays mediating roles in the relationship between childhood trauma and mental health problems. These results will help to increase the public awareness and education regarding the mental health of teenagers. Professional psychological counseling services and more targeted interventions should be established to improve the mental health of Chinese vocational high school students [77]. Additionally, they will provide a beneficial direction for the development of clinical treatments and school prevention protocols for mental health problems.
