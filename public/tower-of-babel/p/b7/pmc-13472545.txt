Evaluation of Pharmacovigilance Reporting Patterns and Adverse Drug Reactions in a Tertiary Care Teaching Hospital
Authors: Dipika Baria, Ishita Nishad Gogdani, Akash Dubey, Naresh Parmar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Pharmacovigilance is crucial in the safety of drugs and it is here that adverse drug reactions (ADRs) are observed, especially in a tertiary care environment where polypharmacy is prevalent. This paper set out to assess the patterns of pharmacovigilance reporting and examine the nature of ADRs in a tertiary care teaching hospital.Methods and Materials:The study was prospective observational and was carried out after 12 months involving 250 patients with suspected ADRs. The sources of data included the spontaneous reporting forms, patient records, and laboratory results. The World Health Organization (WHO) causality assessment scale was used to determine ADRs. Descriptive statistics were presented in the form of mean ± standard deviation and percentages, and Chi-square and t-tests were used in the inferential statistics.Results:The average age of the patients was 47.8 (18.5) years, and the patients were mostly male (58%). The largest percentage of ADRs reported was in the medicine department (42%), then surgery (22) and pediatrics (16). The most commonly involved classes of drugs were antibiotics (36%) and antiepileptics (18%). Type A reactions were found in 64 percent of the cases, whereas Type B reactions were found in 36 percent. WHO causality assessment indicated that 52% of ADRs were possible, 34% probable, and 14% certain. ADRs were serious in 28% of the patients. Underreporting was observed and only 68 percent of ADRs were reported in writing. The incidence of ADRs was significantly greater in patients who were on polypharmacy (five or more drugs) than in those on fewer drugs (P = 0.01).Conclusion:The article identifies some gaps in pharmacovigilance reporting and presents the necessity to involve better awareness, training, and regular reporting systems to improve drug safety in clinical practice.

INTRODUCTION

Adverse drug reactions (ADRs) are a serious global burden of morbidity and mortality and a burden to healthcare systems.[1] An ADR is defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as any noxious, unintended, and undesired effect of a drug used at a normal dose for prophylaxis, diagnosis, or therapy.[2] Pharmacovigilance programs play a crucial role in the identification, evaluation, and prevention of these reactions, particularly in tertiary care settings where patients are at risk of receiving multiple treatments and medications.[3]

Although national pharmacovigilance programs have been put in place, reporting rates for ADRs remain low worldwide. The research indicates that reporting rates for ADRs range from 6 to 10%, resulting in an underestimation of the true safety profile of drugs.[4] This is due to factors, such as awareness gaps, lack of training, fear of legal implications, and time constraints among healthcare providers.[5]

Recent research has shown that antibiotics, antiepileptics, and cardiovascular medicines are the most common classes of drugs involved in ADRs, while the most common clinical manifestations are cutaneous and gastrointestinal.[6] Further, polypharmacy has been shown to be a major factor in the occurrence of ADRs, especially in the elderly.[7]

However, little is known about the reporting pattern of ADR and its association with clinical characteristics in tertiary care teaching hospitals in resource-limited settings. The assessment of pharmacovigilance reporting is important for effective monitoring of ADR and patient safety.

Hence, this study was undertaken to assess pharmacovigilance reporting practices and to study the profile of ADRs in a tertiary care teaching hospital.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corps, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as percentages. Chi-square test was used for categorical comparisons, and t-test for continuous variables. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The study included 250 patients with a mean age of 47.8 ± 18.5 years. Males constituted 58% (n = 145), while females accounted for 42% (n = 105).

DISCUSSION

This study assessed the pharmacovigilance reporting and ADR profile in a tertiary care teaching hospital. This study has highlighted the burden of ADRs and the problems associated with reporting.

The high proportion of ADRs in the medicine department (42%) has been reported in earlier studies, possibly due to increased patient contact and polypharmacy.[1] Drugs from the antibiotic class were most frequently reported (36%), consistent with previous studies showing a high propensity of ADRs with antibiotics.[2]

Type A reactions predominated (64%) as these reactions are dose-related and predictable. This finding is consistent with reports from pharmacovigilance studies that highlight the preventability of many ADRs.[3]

Our research found that polypharmacy was a risk factor for severe ADRs (P = 0.01). This finding is consistent with other studies, which show a greater risk of ADR with increasing drug use.[4] Polypharmacy is a common feature of tertiary care due to multiple comorbidities.

The results of the causality assessment indicated that the majority of ADRs were rated as probable (52%), suggesting a temporal association between drug exposure and effect. Other inhospital studies have reported similar findings.[5]

ADRs underreporting (32%) is a significant issue. This is consistent with reports from other studies, which have attributed underreporting to the lack of awareness and training of health care professionals.[6] Improvements in pharmacovigilance through training and mandatory reporting are important.

This study also reported that serious ADRs (28%) were clinically important. Timely detection and management of ADRs is vital to reduce the burden of morbidity and mortality.

In summary, the study highlights the need for better pharmacovigilance, such as active monitoring, periodic training, and incorporating electronic reporting.

CONCLUSION

The study demonstrates a substantial burden of ADRs and highlights significant gaps in pharmacovigilance reporting in a tertiary care teaching hospital. Antibiotics and polypharmacy were major contributors to ADR occurrence, with severe reactions significantly associated with multiple drug use. Strengthening pharmacovigilance systems, promoting awareness, and ensuring timely reporting are essential to improve patient safety and optimize drug therapy.
