Dislocated Dual-Mobility Hip Implants Are Unlikely to Be Successfully Closed Reduced and Are More Likely to Require Revision Than Standard Articulation Implants
Authors: Janyne Mallender, Kendall Schwartz, Christian Leber, Tony Huyhn, Paulo Castaneda, J Brock Walker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe natural history of dislocated dual-mobility (DM) total hip arthroplasties remains unclear, particularly regarding closed reduction success and revision rates compared to standard articulations (SAs). This study compared closed reduction success and subsequent reoperation rates between dislocated DM and SA hips.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted across a large health system, identifying patients with dislocated total hip arthroplasties using International Classification of Disease, Tenth Revision codes. Inclusion required an attempted closed reduction. Patients were stratified by implant type (DM vs SA). Data collected included demographics, prior surgeries, reduction attempts, intraprosthetic dissociation (IPD), subsequent instability, and revision surgery. Outcomes were compared between groups.ResultsSeventy-four patients met the inclusion criteria (26 DM, 48 SA). Successful closed reduction was significantly lower in the DM vs SA group (34.6% vs 90%; P < .001). Among the 26 dislocated DM hips, 15 experienced IPD, including five initially missed. Revision surgery was more frequent in the DM group compared to SA (79.2% vs 53.4%; P < .004). Among those with initially successful closed reductions, rates of recurrent instability requiring revision surgery did not differ significantly (DM 44.4% vs SA 41.9%; P = .84).ConclusionsDislocated DM implants are significantly less likely to be successfully closed reduced and more likely to require revision surgery than SA implants, largely due to the high rate of IPD. Missed IPD contributes substantially to the increased reoperation risk and highlights the importance of accurate diagnosis following dislocation.Keywords: Dual-mobility, Closed reduction, Intraprosthetic, Dislocation, Dissociation

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) remains one of the most successful procedures in orthopaedics today, offering significant pain relief and functional improvement to patients with degenerative hip disease. As the population ages and the demand for hip replacements increases, it is critical to not only perfect the surgical techniques but also continually advance implant technology to reduce complications, such as dislocation. Dislocation following THA is one of the most common and challenging complications, often leading to patient dissatisfaction, reduced quality of life, and costly reoperations. With the goal of reducing the risk of dislocation, newer implant designs have emerged over the last few decades, one of the most promising being the dual-mobility (DM) articulation [1].

DM implants were first introduced in France by Bousquet in 1974 and have since gained popularity worldwide. The design of the DM implant consists of a smaller femoral head articulating within a larger polyethylene head, which in turn articulates with the acetabular component. This dual articulation concept theoretically combines the benefits of both larger head sizes, which increase the range of motion and reduces impingement, with the reduced wear and improved stability [2]. By incorporating these advantages, DM implants have demonstrated a reduced risk of dislocation compared to standard articulation (SA) implants [2,3]. Given that dislocation remains a leading cause of revision surgery, the role of DM implants in both primary and revision hip arthroplasty is increasing, particularly in high-risk populations such as those with previous hip or spine surgeries, neuromuscular disorders, or a history of recurrent dislocations.

Recent literature supports the utility of DM articulations in reducing the incidence of dislocations in both primary and revision THA. A large systematic review found that patients with DM implants had a significantly lower dislocation rate (0.9%) compared to those with standard bearings (6.8%) [4]. Similarly, another review of over 10,000 DM hips found the rate of intraprosthetic dissociation (IPD) was 1.1% and the incidence of extra-articular dislocation was 0.46% [5]. These studies highlight the potential benefits of DM systems in reducing the risk of dislocation.

Despite these promising results, DM implants are not without complications. One of the most significant concerns specific to DM systems is the risk of IPD, a complication unique to the design of these implants. In IPD, the femoral head dissociates from the polyethylene liner, often during an attempted closed reduction of a dislocated DM hip. This complication necessitates revision surgery, as closed reduction alone cannot address the mechanical failure of the implant. IPD rates vary across the literature, but it has been reported to occur in up to 2-5% of cases following a dislocation [6]. Furthermore, while DM hips are less likely to dislocate compared to SAs, when they do dislocate, they can present significant challenges in management. Closed reduction, the preferred initial treatment for a dislocated hip, may not be as effective in DM implants as it is in SAs [7]. Studies have shown that a failed closed reduction attempt in a dislocated DM implant is often due to the presence of an IPD [6]. This is a critical consideration for surgeons, as missed IPDs can result in prolonged pain and disability for the patient, leading to a higher likelihood of revision surgery [7].

The natural history of a dislocated DM hip implant is not well understood, particularly regarding the success rates of closed reduction and the long-term outcomes following dislocation. Given the increasing use of DM implants in both primary and revision THA, it is essential to further investigate the outcomes of patients who experience a dislocation. Specifically, the rate of successful closed reduction, the incidence of IPD, and the long-term need for revision surgery must be explored to provide a clearer understanding of the risks associated with these implants. While it is generally accepted that DM implants offer a reduced risk of dislocation, the management of patients who do experience a dislocation remains a topic of concern and requires further clarification [3,7].

Dislocations among those with DM implants present unique challenges in management due to the risk of IPD and the potential for failed closed reductions [7]. This study aims to describe the likelihood of successful closed reduction and the risk of revision surgery in DM and SA hips. Additionally, this study aims to address these gaps in knowledge by comparing the outcomes of dislocated DM and SA implants in a large cohort of patients treated within a single healthcare system.

Materials and methods

Study design and data extraction

This was an Institutional Review Board approved retrospective cohort study performed at a large health system. Patients who presented to a hospital with a dislocated THA between January 2014 and December 2023 were identified using International Classifications of Diseases codes for dislocation of joint prosthesis, unspecified dislocation of hip, and presence of artificial hip. Inclusion criteria were those who had undergone an attempted closed reduction and patients over the age of 18. Exclusion criteria include patients with no documentation of an attempted closed reduction, patients with a hemiarthroplasty, and those for which no implant record could be obtained.

Variables of interest included patient demographics, implant type (DM vs SA), approach, previous surgical history, implant manufacturer, closed reduction attempts, IPD event, time to dislocation, subsequent instability, and rate of revision surgery. IPD was primarily identified via imaging review, emergency department reports, orthopaedic consult notes, and operative reports. Our primary outcome was the rate of successful closed reduction. Secondary outcomes included subsequent instability and revision surgery assessed by chart review for future dislocation events and operative intervention.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis consisted of descriptive statistics for demographic factors and comparative statistics for categorical and continuous data as appropriate. Multiple variables were compared using Mann-Whitney U test and post hoc chi-square test was conducted when appropriate. Continuous data were evaluated for normality with a Quantile-Quantile graphical analysis and subsequently analyzed using Student’s t-test. Comparisons included successful reduction, subsequent instability, and revision surgery between DM and SA implants. All P values <0.05 were considered significant. Statistical analysis was performed using SAS software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Demographics and implant factors

A total of 74 patients were included in this study (26 DM, 48 SA) (Table 1). Mean age was not significantly different between cohorts at 65 and 66 for the DM and SA, respectively (P > .5). There were more female patients in the SA group at 77% (n = 37) vs 50% (n = 13) of DM patients (P = .018). Time to dislocation also differed at 6.5 weeks for DM vs 8 weeks for SA, although this was not significant (P = .273). Of the DM cohort, 46.2% (n = 12) of dislocators were from revisions, 33.3% (n = 16) for SA implants (P = .278). The majority of both DM and SA THAs were performed through a posterior approach (DM: 92.3%, n = 24; SA: 75%, n = 36). The most common implant manufacturer was Stryker for both DM and SA hips (Table 2).

Successful closed reduction

Overall, the successful closed reduction rate was 34.6% for DM hips vs 89.6% for SA hips (P < .001) (Table 3). Also subclassified to emergency room vs operating room (OR) reductions, 23.1% of DM THAs were successfully closed reduced in the emergency department compared to 72.9% of SA THAs. Of patients who were taken to the OR for closed reduction, 16.7% DM were successfully closed reduced vs 61.5% SA. Of the 26 patients in our cohort with dislocated DM THAs, 15 of them (57.7%) ultimately failed closed reduction due to having an IPD. Of note, this was missed in 5 patients who were discharged from the hospital with an IPD, 4 from the emergency department, and 1 from the operating room (Fig. 1). The implant manufacturers which sustained an IPD were 8 Stryker, 5 Depuy, 1 Smith & Nephew, and 1 Enovis.

Revision and persistent instability

Overall, there was a 79.2% (19/24) revision rate for DM dislocators vs 53.4% (23/43) for SA hips (P < .004) (Table 3). This includes patients with IPD and those who required revision for persistent instability. Of note, periprosthetic infection was identified in 1 DM and 2 SA patients and was excluded from final revision analysis. Also 1 DM and 3 SA patients were excluded due to insufficient follow-up. Additionally, of the patients with a successful closed reduction, there were no differences in patients with persistent instability events leading to the need for revision surgery (44.4 vs 47.3%, P = .84).

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that dislocated DM implants are less likely to be successfully closed reduced and have a higher likelihood of requiring revision surgery compared to SA implants. These outcomes highlight the unique challenges posed by DM implants in the context of dislocation and underscore the importance of recognizing DM implants and IPD, a potential complication that has not been extensively studied. The high incidence of IPD observed in our cohort (57%) suggests that even though DM designs are intended to reduce overall dislocation rates, they may lead to unique challenges when dislocation occurs. This aligns with previous reports on IPD as a distinct concern in DM implants, particularly during closed reduction attempts [8].

The likelihood of failing to achieve a successful reduction, coupled with the high rate of subsequent revisions, suggests that orthopaedic surgeons should approach dislocated DM implants with caution. In contrast, the successful closed reduction rate of SA hips (90%) indicates that SAs may offer more reliable closed reduction outcomes in the setting of instability. This difference is attributed to the unique design of DM implants, and their propensity toward IPD during reduction attempts.

A systematic review by Pai et al (2021) noted that DM implants face a notable risk of dislocation and highlighted that failure to achieve a successful reduction could lead to significant morbidity [9]. Our study corroborates this, as one out of 3 patients with a dislocated DM were discharged home with a missed IPD leading to subsequent hospital visits, revision surgery, and increased morbidity. Figure 1 illustrates five examples of patients who were discharged from the hospital with an unrecognized IPD, despite clear radiographic evidence of femoral head eccentricity. These cases highlight the challenge of diagnosing IPD, even when subtle signs are present on postreduction imaging. In our cohort, 3 cases were initially managed by emergency medicine attendings without orthopaedic consultation, while the remaining 2 were missed by an orthopaedic resident and an attending orthopaedic surgeon. This underscores that orthopaedic providers are not immune to overlooking this complication. As awareness of IPD continues to grow, we hope that early recognition will become more routine and prevent delays in appropriate management. Lim et al. (2024) have similarly noted that complications in DM implants often lead to reoperation, thus reinforcing the need for selective indications for the use of DM implants [10]. Our study showed no significant difference in the risk of subsequent instability between the DM and SA groups after successful closed reduction (44.4 vs 47.3%). This finding suggests that the increased revision rate for DM hips is primarily driven by the high incidence of IPD.

Our study has several limitations. As a retrospective review conducted within a single healthcare system, it is limited by the data available in medical records. We do not have data regarding why the treating surgeon selected a DM implant at the time of surgery; there is a possibility toward bias of only selecting high-risk patients. Additionally, long-term follow-up data were not included. While the cohort sizes are small, dislocations remain a relatively rare complication. Despite the limited sample size, we observed a notable number of IPDs. To our knowledge, we report one of the largest numbers of IPDs to date.

Given the findings of our study, we propose several clinical recommendations to guide clinicians in recognizing and managing dislocated DM implants. Surgeons should maintain a high index of suspicion for DM implants and possible IPD. A thorough history, examination, imaging, and implant identification should be performed prior to any closed reduction attempt. Physicians should consider the low rate of success of closed reduction when deciding whether to put the patient through an anesthetic event to attempt closed reduction alone (either in the emergency room or OR) without being ready for open revision. Ideally, if revision arthroplasty staff and OR is readily available, we recommend the patient by taken to the OR where closed reduction can be attempted under controlled conditions, under general anesthesia with adequate sedation or full paralysis, and surgeons should be prepared for conversion to revision under the same anesthetic event. Postreduction radiographs should be scrutinized for eccentricity within the acetabular component or the polyethylene shadow indicative of an IPD [11]. Closed reduction should be aborted if there is any indication of IPD, and the patient should be prepared for revision surgery. Lastly, in deciding whether or not to use DM implants, the lower dislocation risk should be weighed against the increased risk of revision should a dislocation occur.

Mallett et al. emphasize the importance of performing closed reductions under general anesthesia by an orthopaedist to maximize the likelihood of success, given that most attempted reductions in their study were unsuccessful and resulted in complications such as iatrogenic IPD and periprosthetic fractures [12]. If possible, closed reduction should be performed by a member of the orthopaedic surgery team with the patient under general anesthesia, especially in the setting of a dislocated DM. However, this is not possible or feasible for many orthopaedic surgeons and emergency departments. We do not routinely obtain prereduction computed tomography scans, even in the setting of known DM implants, however computed tomography scan is usually obtained if there is eccentric reduction on postreduction radiographs.

Conclusions

Our study highlights the significant challenges associated with dislocated DM implants. In the setting of dislocation, DM implants have significantly lower likelihood of successful closed reduction and higher likelihood of requiring revision surgery compared to SA implants. Additionally, closed reduction of DM implants carries a substantial risk of IPD, which is frequently undetected on postreduction radiographs. As DM implants become increasingly common in both primary and revision THAs, it is essential for orthopaedic surgeons to recognize and be prepared to manage the unique complications associated with their use.
