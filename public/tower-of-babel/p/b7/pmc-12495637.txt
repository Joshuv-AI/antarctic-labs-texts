A new lens on football match intensity: from the most demanding passage to extremely demanding passage
Authors: Zunqi Niu, Jingyun Yang, Jiewei Long, Xinyi Zhang, Zhaoyang Wang, Iyán Iván-Baragaño, Hongyou Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study examines positional differences in physical and technical performance during the extremely demanding passage (EDP) in the Chinese Super League (CSL), using a 5-minute time window and a high-intensity running (HIR) threshold of ≥ 19.8 km/h. Data from 176 matches (n = 109 players, 438 observations) in the 2019 season were analysed using generalised mixed linear models. Results showed that: (1) The mean HIR distance during the EDP was 50%–60% higher than during the most demanding passage (MDP). (2) During the EDP, central defenders (CD) achieved the fewest total distance (695 ± 51 m) and HIR distance (251 ± 15 m) but excelled in ball control metrics, including the longest running with the ball average length (6.9 ± 8.7 m) and the highest forward passes (1.5 ± 1.2). (3) Central midfielders (CM) covered the most total distance (796 ± 46 m) and HIR distance (343 ± 27 m). These findings underscore the specific physical and technical demands of CDs and CMs compared to other positions during the EDP, necessitating role-specific strategies to optimise performance.

Introduction

Football is a sport characterised by complex, intermittent running patterns in which high-intensity actions such as sprints and high-speed runs are interspersed with lower-intensity activities like jogging and walking [1]. This intermittent structure highlights the need to understand the physical demands players encounter, especially during the most demanding periods of a match. To address this issue, the concept of the ‘most demanding passage (MDP)’ [2], or sometimes referred as ‘worst case scenario’ [3, 4], ‘peak demands’ [5], ‘peak locomotor demand’ [6, 7], or ‘maximal intensity period’ [8], has been developed.

MDP is typically identified as a period (e.g., 1, 3, or 5 min) during which a player covers the most high-intensity running distance, calculated using either static or rolling averages [9, 10]. The rolling average method, in particular, has been favoured for its precision in identifying fluctuations in demand across match phases [9]. Through this framework, MDP provides valuable baseline data to researchers and practitioners helping them gain insights into the peak physical capabilities required of players, facilitating more accurate training and performance optimisation. The strength of MDP lies in its ability to identify the most demanding period for a player within a single match. Importantly, previous studies have shown that replicating MDPs in training prepares players for peak demands and helps prevent injuries by building tolerance to extreme loads [11].

However, there is considerable match-to-match variation in player’s average high intensity running distance [12, 13]. For example, Thoseby et al. (2022) [13] reported that the coefficient of variation (CV) for high-speed distance ranged from 20.6% to 29.8%, depending on the length of the analysed epoch, with longer epochs showing greater variability. Meaning that MDPs of a player can vary notably from one match to another. This variability challenges MDP’s ability to fully capture the extreme physical demands players face across different matches. Therefore, an approach that identifies the most demanding periods across multiple matches for a player is needed. This is where the concept of ‘extremely demanding passage’ (EDP) comes into play. EDP refines the MDP framework by focusing on the most intense periods of running and exertion across multiple matches, offering a broader and more accurate view of a player’s peak physical demands over time. Various approaches exist to identify EDPs, including the aggregation of multiple MDP exposures per player across matches. This approach captures continuing high-intensity phases but may favour players who play more match minutes, resulting in bias and making comparisons across players or positions less consistent. This study utilises a statistical frequency-based approach to identify the top 5% of HIRD values, typically considered extremes [14, 15]. A threshold of the position-specific mean plus 1.65 standard deviations (SD) was used, statistically corresponding with the top 5%. By identifying these critical periods, coaches can develop more targeted training programs to prepare players for the most unpredictable and demanding phases of a match, thereby optimising performance under these high-stress conditions [16, 17].

It is well-documented that there are substantial positional differences in the physical demands encountered during MDP conditions [18]. Therefore, it is important to investigate whether similar positional distinctions are observed in EDP, as this could offer further insights into the specific demands of the most intense, short-duration phases of play across multiple matches.

Accordingly, this study primarily aims to: (1) identify the EDP within a 5-min window for players in the Chinese Super League (CSL), and (2) analyse differences in running and technical performance among elite players in various positions during the EDP phase.

Materials and methods

Design

An observational methodology was employed in this study. Utilizing a nomothetic (analysing each outfield player), a punctual (focusing on analysing different time windows over time), and a multidimensional (capturing responses across various levels using a professional tool) design, this study corresponds to the third observational quadrant proposed by Anguera et al. [19]. The local university’s ethics committee granted approval for this study.

Participants

The analysis included 176 matches from the 2021 season of the CSL, with inclusion restricted to professional outfield players who started in at least three matches and had complete match data, while goalkeepers and players with incomplete records were excluded. A total of 133 players were identified with EDP, and their descriptive information, including age, height, weight, and BMI, is summarised in Table 1. Based on the Participant Classification Framework [20], these players are categorised as elite-level professionals (Elite/International level), as they competed in the Chinese Super League during the data collection period. The running and technical performance data of the players for each match were captured using a multiple-camera computerized tracking system (STATS Pro®, Sport-Universal Process, Nice, France). The movement of players was observed during the match at a sampling rate of 25 Hz by cameras installed at the roof level [21, 22]. The signals and angles generated by the sensors were sequentially turned into digital data and stored on computers for post-match analysis. All data were downloaded after the match using the appropriate proprietary software (STATS Viewer, version 3.6.0.3). The validity and reliability of the data collected by this tracking system have been previously verified [23–25].

Identification of MDP and EDP

High-intensity running distance (HIRD) efforts were defined using a running speed threshold of above 19.8 km·h− 1 [9, 26]. A rolling average approach was then applied to locate the 5-min period with the greatest distance covered at HIR for each player, thereby identifying the MDP. The 5-minute interval was selected as it offers a more stable representation of sustained high-intensity demands and captures a wider range of technical actions, whereas shorter windows (1–3 min) are more affected by transient interruptions [26].

The mean and SD of the 5-min MDP values were calculated for each position (CD, FB, CM, WM, and FW respectively). Any MDP value surpassing the average MDP value by more than 1.65 SDs was classified as an EDP. The selection criterion produced a final sample of 109 players, resulting in a total of 438 observations (some players played different positions in various matches). Players were divided into five tactical positions: central defender (CD, 34 players, 114 observations); full-back (FB, 19 players, 77 observations); central midfielder (CM, 28 players, 112 observations); wide midfielder (WM, 30 players, 53 observations) and forward (FW, 22 players, 82 observations).

Variables

In accordance to prior studies of MDP analysis [9, 26], nine physical performance-related and twenty-four technical performance-related variables were selected to quantify the physical and technical demands encountered by the players during MDP conditions. The grouping and operational definition of these variables can be found in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

In the university version of the Statistical Analysis System (SAS Studio version 3.6), generalised mixed models were conducted separately for each dependent variable, according to their data type. Specifically, Poisson regression was applied to count variables, while linear regression was used for continuous variables that followed approximately normal distributions. Player position had 5 levels: CD, FB, CM, WM, and FW. Player ID was treated as a random effect to account for repeated measures within individuals across different matches, thereby addressing intra-individual correlations and improving the reliability of fixed-effect estimates.

As part of the quality control for the model, each dependent variable underwent diagnostic checks appropriate to its model type. For Poisson regression, model convergence and the ratio of Pearson chi-square to degrees of freedom (χ²/df) were evaluated to assess overdispersion, with a threshold of χ²/df < 2.0 adopted as an inclusion criterion [27]. For linear regression models applied to continuous variables, residual diagnostics were conducted, including checks of the linearity, homoscedasticity, and normality of the residuals. These were evaluated using scatterplots of residuals versus fitted values and normal Q–Q plots.

To ensure the robustness of the reported findings, variables that failed to meet one or more diagnostic criteria were excluded from inferential analyses. The following technical variables were left out because they had problems with convergence or too much variation in the Poisson models: GainPosse, LossPosse, BallLost, BallWon, AvTimeIndPoss, N.ShortPass, and S.ShortPass. No variables analysed using linear regression were excluded, as they all passed the diagnostic checks.

Means and standard deviations of each dependent variable for players of each position can be estimated from the mixed model, accounting for repeated measures of player ID. Effect sizes were calculated by dividing estimated difference in the means of different positions by the standard deviation among players as derived from the mixed model. Non-clinical magnitude-based inference was employed to evaluate the effect sizes in all the physical and technical variables across the five positions, accompanied by a supplementary materials package for generalised mixed modelling provided using SAS Studio [28]. Non-clinical magnitude-based inference was employed to evaluate the effect sizes in all the physical and technical variables across the five positions, accompanied by a supplementary materials package for generalised mixed modelling provided using SAS Studio [29]. Effects were considered clear if the 95% confidence interval (CI) did not include both positive (> 0.2) and negative (<-0.2) values simultaneously [29]. Clear effects were reported, and qualitative likelihood was used to determine whether the true effects were substantial or trivial, utilising the following scales: < 0.5% most unlikely, 0.5–5% very unlikely, 5–25% unlikely, 25–75% possible, 75–95% likely, 95–99.5% very likely, and > 99.5% most likely [29].

Discussion

This study analysed the MDP across multiple matches for a player by introducing an approach of EDP. The primary results showed that: (1) the ratio between the maximum and minimum HIR distance during the MDP phase across multiple matches for players of the same position ranged from 3.5 to 6.2, while the mean HIR distance during the EDP was 50%-60% higher than the mean HIR distance during the MDP phase. (2) there are clear differences in the physical and technical demands for players of different positions during the EDP phase.

The results indicate that the MDP phase has been characterised by a substantial variation in HIR distances among player positions, reflected by an overall maximum-to-minimum ratio of 5.8 across all positions combined. This variability is most pronounced in CM, who exhibited the largest maximum-to-minimum ratio, highlighting those players encountered significant extremes in HIR distance during the MDP phase. Moreover, the recorded HIR distances show considerable fluctuations across different matches and among different players [30–32], further emphasising the variation in MDP. These differences underscore the necessity of focusing on extreme MDP scenarios, as being based only on typical MDP may not be enough to reflect the most demanding physical demands that can occur during different matches or for different players.

To address this issue, the concept of EDP was introduced, utilising a frequency-based statistic to define extreme MDP cases [33]. A key finding showed that the mean HIR distance during the EDP was 50%–60% higher than the mean HIR distance during the MDP phase. This significant increase in HIR distance during the EDP reflects the increased physical demands faced by players in the most extreme match periods. This highlights the necessity of identifying between the general high-intensity efforts exhibited by MDP and the truly extreme demands that define the EDP phase. This distinction enhances the training framework, emphasising the need for players to prepare for these intense periods in matches, which will challenge their physical capacities to the limit.

As noted earlier, the EDP identifies the extreme physical demands encountered during a match. In line with this, prior research has shown that notable differences in running performance exist among players in various positions during the MDP phase [18, 34, 35]. This study extends this by comparing positional differences in running and technical performance during the EDP phase. By analysing data based on playing positions, it was possible to identify how the extreme demands captured by the EDP influence players differently depending on their tactical positions.

As with prior studies [36], in the EDP, CD exhibits the lowest total distance (TD, ES = 1.87 vs. CM; 1.43 vs. FB; 1.31 vs. FW; 1.48 vs. WM) and HIRD (ES = 4.07 vs. CM; 3.23 vs. FB; 3.04 vs. FW; 3.56 vs. WM) among all positions, while achieved the highest walking and jogging distance (WJD, ES=-1.21 vs. CM; -0.69 vs. FB; -0.62 vs. FW; -0.84 vs. WM). The situation could possibly be related to their defensive responsibilities, which necessitate them to occupy effective defensive positions on losing possession [37]. These tactical demands may lead central defenders to focus on short bursts of high-intensity efforts, such as positioning and defensive interventions, rather than covering long distances [38], and these tactical requirements are consistent across different match phases [39]. Furthermore, although CDs covered the lowest total distance, their distance of running with the ball (RunBallAvgLen, ES=-0.29 vs. CM; -0.50 vs. FB; -0.78 vs. FW; -0.20 vs. WM) was the highest. While the main tactical actions for CDs during the MDP phase are “covering” and “recovery run” [40], they may have utilised different tactical strategies during the EDP phase, adopting more aggressive approaches [31], which could partially elucidate their pronounced running patterns.

For technical performance, CDs demonstrated superior technical performance during the EDP phase, particularly in the number of passes (N.Pass, ES=-0.34 vs. CM; -0.50 vs. FB; -0.94 vs. FW; -0.73 vs. WM), successful passes (S.Pass, ES=-0.45 vs. CM; -0.43 vs. FB; -1.01 vs. FW; -0.59 vs. WM), the number of forward passes (N.FwdPass, ES=-0.63 vs. CM; -0.81 vs. FB; -0.99 vs. FW; -0.65 vs. WM) and total time in individual possession (TotTmIndPoss, ES=-0.44 vs. CM; -0.40 vs. FB; -0.70 vs. FW; -0.43 vs. WM). Significantly, previous research found that [41–43], during a match, CDs possess limited ball control, with fewer touches and dribbles than players in other positions. In contrast, in the EDP phase, CDs exhibited superior ball control in both quantity (BallTch, ES=-0.28 vs. CM; -0.25 vs. FB; -0.63 vs. FW; -0.35 vs. WM) and quality (S.BallTch, ES=-0.33 vs. CM; -0.25 vs. FB; -0.63 vs. FW; -0.35 vs. WM) compared to FB, FW, and WM, clearly showing differences from previous research. This can be attributed to the tactical evolution of modern football, where CDs increasingly adopt roles traditionally occupied by midfielders, including passing responsibilities [12]. The increased ball possession and distribution responsibilities indicate that central defenders are no longer limited to passive recycling functions. They help break through the opponent’s defence by keeping the ball for a long time and making longer passes forward, which is especially useful in crowded central areas when facing tight defences or specific strategies to handle pressure [44]. This conclusion corresponds with the observation that CDs achieved the greatest ball-carrying distances during the EDP phase, further suggesting that their function transcends mere defence. During the EDP phase, CDs seemed to participate more proactively in offensive organisations, advancing to higher areas and connecting with other players. These findings reflect the increasing designs of modern football, wherein defensive players have become the starting point for attacking play [45].

Consistent with earlier research [17, 46], CMs achieved the highest TD during the EDP phase across all positions (ES = 1.87 vs. CD; -0.46 vs. FB; -0.41 vs. WM; -0.58 vs. FW). As CMs have to participate in both offensive and defensive roles, they must traverse longer distances during crucial phases of the game [47]. Previous studies [34, 47] discovered that WMs exhibit the highest intensity of running during the MDP phase, or with no notable positional differences observed. Conversely, during the EDP phase, our study indicates that CMs exhibit the highest HIRD (ES = 4.07 vs. CD; -0.96 vs. FB; -0.59 vs. WM; -1.18 vs. FW), showing a clear difference compared to players in other positions. During the match, the main tactical actions of CMs involve maintaining possession, passing, and occupying strategic positions on the field, which typically limits their ability for high-velocity sprinting [38]. However, during the EDP phase, CMs may participate in organisational and transitional activities while also pushing into the opponent’s area in search of offensive opportunities [48]. The change in responsibilities likely accounts for the increased HIRD, as CMs may have to cover more areas during these high-intensity phases [3]. These changes in their role could be linked to broader adjustments in team tactics and formations [49] or even individual player characteristics [50]. Further investigation with video recordings is necessary to examine the tactical behaviours exhibited by CM during the EDP phase that resulted in such extreme running outcomes.

Forcher et al. [38] found that CDs achieved more passes, and Yi et al. [51] reported that, during a match, CMs exhibit superior passing quantity and quality. Our results showed that, during the EDP phase, CMs exhibited clearly higher passing quantity (N.pass, ES=-0.35 vs. WM; -0.55 VS FW) compared to WMs and FWs; however, no substantial differences were observed between FBs and CDs. The CMs provide an important tactical role by controlling the game’s tempo and facilitating ball distribution, which naturally results in increased passing participation [45]. In addition, CMs exhibited clearly higher long passing quantity (N.longpass, ES=–0.50 vs. WM; − 0.88 vs. FB; − 0.60 vs. FW) and quality (S.longpass, ES=–0.45 vs. WM; − 0.96 vs. FB; − 0.53 vs. FW) compared to WMs, FBs, and FWs. These findings highlight the tactical value of CMs in orchestrating offences and maintaining structural balance during the EDP. Their frequent use of long passes indicates a tactical function in starting advances forward and accelerating tempo changes [52]. In addition to functioning as connectors, CMs frequently serve as directional hubs, reallocating possession to change defensive shape and create space through central control [53].

Liu et al. [45] reported that FW generally exhibit a higher frequency of shot (shot, ES = 0.42 vs. CD; 0.36 vs. CM; 0.29 vs. FB; -0.61 vs. WM) during a match compared to players in other positions, as their main role is to create and capitalise on goal-scoring opportunities. During the EDP phase, similar results were noted, with FW exhibiting a clearly increased shooting frequency relative to players in different positions. This is a result of their tactical position within the team, as they are situated closer to the opponent’s goal and are expected to be involved more and take chances during attacking scenarios [31].

Limitation

This study utilised a 5-minute window to analyse the EDP phase, excluding shorter durations of 1, 2, or 3 min. This limitation may introduce a potential bias, as shorter windows could be more sensitive in capturing peak running demands that are temporally condensed, especially in high-intensity transition moments [36]. While considering the possible interruptions in play related to a 5-minute window [38], consequently, future studies ought to expand upon this study by including different time intervals for EDP analysis. Additional contextual factors were not integrated, such as tactical formation changes during the match, halftime score, red cards, home versus away conditions, or whether the team was leading or trailing [54]. Importantly, the analysis compared players across different teams rather than within the same team, which may introduce additional variability, as team-specific tactical approaches, physical preparation, and match contexts can differ substantially. Future research should therefore explore within-team comparisons to reduce this source of heterogeneity. In addition, the study also did not integrate video-based or behavioural contextualisation to validate tactical interpretations, limiting the capacity to directly link physical and technical metrics to observed match behaviours. Employing video analysis or qualitative coding in future research could enhance tactical contextualisation and strengthen inference.

Additionally, although the current classification of EDPs using a position-specific mean plus 1.65 standard deviations is grounded in statistical convention, this approach remains exploratory. Future studies should examine how this threshold compares with other classification methods, such as percentile-based cutoffs or repeated MDP exposures, to validate and improve the identification of extreme match demands.

Practical applications

The position differences observed during the EDP phase offer helpful information for practitioners aiming to improve training plans and match preparations. The increased running loads noted in CMs and FBs indicate a necessity for personalised conditioning exercises that match their substantial aerobic and anaerobic requirements. At the same time, the increased technical involvement of CDs, particularly in forward distribution and ball carrying, suggests that players need to incorporate build-up plays and pressure-based decision-making into their training routines. In contrast, the playing profile of FWs, which is characterised by frequent shots and high sprint activity, suggests that their training should prioritise repeated, high-intensity efforts followed by finishing actions. These findings may help coaches and technical staff to develop position-specific strategies for conditioning, recovery, and tactical planning, thereby improving player readiness for the extremely demanding phases of competition.

Conclusions

The EDP phase, characterised by substantially greater HIR demands compared to the MDP, imposes clearly increased physical demands on players, and is clearly influenced by player position. Particularly CDs and CMs, who showed specific differences in running, particularly in running with the ball distance. CD exhibits clearly differences in passing and ball control relative to FB, WM, and FW. CM has variations in passing compared to WM, FB and FW.
