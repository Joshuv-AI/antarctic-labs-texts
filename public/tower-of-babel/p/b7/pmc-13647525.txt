The role of health literacy and attitudes toward artificial intelligence in the acceptance of telemedicine services among adults in Turkey: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Furkan Şakiroğlu, Ertuğrul Çoruh, Muhammet Doğan, İdeal Beraa Yılmaz Kartal, Mehmet Akif Nas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe digital transformation in healthcare has led to an increase in the use of telemedicine and artificial intelligence (AI)based applications. This study aims to examine the associations between e-health literacy, attitudes toward AI, and acceptance of telemedicine, and to test whether attitudes toward AI statistically mediate the association between e-health literacy and acceptance of telemedicine.MethodsOur study was conducted between January 1 and March 1, 2026. This cross-sectional study included 277 participants who visited a family health center. Data were collected using a sociodemographic information form, the General Attitude Scale toward Artificial Intelligence in Health Services, the Attitude Scale toward Telemedicine, and the e-Health Literacy Scale. Hierarchical regression analysis and mediation analysis using the bootstrap method (5,000 resamples) were performed.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 30.52 ± 9.95, and 57.4% were female. In the hierarchical regression analysis, e-health literacy (β = 0.28, p < .001) and a positive attitude toward AI (β = 0.23, p = .010) were found to positively predict telemedicine acceptance, while a negative attitude toward AI (β = -0.28, p = .002) was found to negatively predict it. In the mediation analysis, it was determined that e-health literacy has a strong direct effect on telemedicine acceptance (β = 0.545, p < .001), but AI attitudes do not play a mediating role in this relationship (p > .05).ConclusionE- health literacy is a strong and direct determinant of telemedicine acceptance, independent of attitudes toward AI. Negative attitudes toward AI were associated with lower acceptance of telemedicine services. The results of our study suggest that strategies aimed at improving e- health literacy and addressing AI related concerns may be effective in promoting the widespread adoption of telemedicine, particularly in primary care settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-026-03470-8.Keywords: Telemedicine, Artificial intelligence, Health literacy, Technology acceptance, Primary care, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

The healthcare sector is undergoing an unprecedented transformation driven by rapid technological advancements, evolving patient expectations, and changing policy frameworks. The digital transformation of healthcare has emerged as a key driver in reshaping the delivery structure of healthcare services, with a focus on enhancing their efficiency, accessibility, and quality [1]. Telemedicine, a key component of this transformation, refers to the use of digital communication technologies and telecommunications to provide and facilitate healthcare services such as patient education, medical care, health-related information services, and self care [2]. In particular, the COVID-19 pandemic has fundamentally transformed the delivery of healthcare worldwide and accelerated the widespread adoption of telemedicine [3]. Telemedicine, whose use is becoming increasingly widespread in primary care, offers various advantages. In particular, it significantly improves the accessibility and availability of care in rural areas where patients often have to travel long distances to receive treatment; it facilitates access for underserved populations, such as those living in remote areas or with mobility issues; it reduces travel time and costs; shorter wait times for appointments, which in turn makes accessing healthcare services easier, and the availability of flexible scheduling and the ability to schedule appointments outside of regular business hours are key advantages that stand out [4]. In addition to these advantages, there are various factors that make its use difficult due to individual, socio-structural, or technological limitations [5].

Another key stakeholder in the digital transformation of healthcare is artificial intelligence (AI) technology. Its transformative power stems from its ability to redesign processes, decision-making mechanisms, and even workforce dynamics within the healthcare sector [6]. AI encompasses a broad range of technologies that enable machines to perceive, understand, act, and learn. Machine learning, a subset of AI, involves the development of algorithms that learn from data and can make predictions or decisions based on that data [7]. AI has been successfully used in various telemedicine applications. AI powered chatbots and digital assistants are improving mental health support and patient triage, while AI enabled wearable devices facilitate the continuous monitoring of cardiovascular and respiratory conditions [8]. Despite the countless benefits AI offers to healthcare systems, its widespread adoption and implementation also present various challenges. These challenges include patient data privacy, cybersecurity, data ownership and sharing, medical ethics, and the risk of system failure. Given the nature of healthcare delivery, ethical concerns are particularly prominent. The use of AI technology can pose a threat to patient safety and privacy and may potentially lead to a crisis of trust and various risks. Such issues can create significant barriers to the acceptance of AI powered healthcare assistants by users [9].

Another factor influencing the use of telehealth services is health literacy [10]. Health literacy is linked to literacy and encompasses the knowledge, motivation, and skills people possess to access, understand, evaluate, and apply health information [11]. The concept of health literacy has evolved to include the dimension of e-health literacy, encompassing individuals’ ability to access, evaluate, and use health information on digital platforms [12]. Studies show that individuals with low levels of health literacy are less likely to use health information technology applications, perceive these services as less beneficial, and experience greater difficulties when using digital health technologies [13]. Particularly, the fact that disadvantaged groups in terms of digital health literacy such as the elderly, lowin come individuals, and marginalized communities have less access to telehealth services and experience worse health outcomes [14] supports this finding.

With the ever-increasing development of technology and its integration into users’ private and professional life, a decision regarding its acceptance or rejection still remains an open question [15]. The Technology Acceptance Model, introduced by Fred Davis, has become a dominant model for investigating the factors affecting users’ acceptance of technology [16]. Based on this theoretical framework, the present study hypothesized that attitudes toward AI (positive and negative) may play a mediating role in the effect of health literacy on telemedicine acceptance. AI attitudes were tested as a mediator rather than a direct predictor or moderator because health literacy is expected to influence telemedicine acceptance indirectly through individuals’ perceptions of AI.

Although telemedicine, AI, and health literacy have been studied separately, little is known about how they interact within a single model, particularly in primary care settings in Turkey. This study addresses this gap by testing whether attitudes toward AI mediate the relationship between health literacy and telemedicine acceptance.

There are numerous studies that address the concepts of telemedicine applications, AI-supported healthcare services, and health literacy [17–19]. This study aims to evaluate these three concepts which are often treated independently in the literature within a comprehensive model by testing the mediating role of AI attitudes on the effect of health literacy on attitudes toward telehealth services.

The model proposes that e-health literacy is directly associated with telemedicine acceptance, and that this association may be mediated by attitudes toward AI (both positive and negative). Methods

Research design

This study was designed using a cross-sectional and correlational survey model to examine the underlying mechanisms linking individuals’ levels of health literacy, their general attitudes toward AI, and their attitudes toward telemedicine services. Verbal and written informed consent were obtained from the participants, after which the questionnaire was administered to them in person.

Sample and sample size

Our study was conducted between January 1 and March 1, 2026. The study sample consisted of 277 individuals who visited the family health centers affiliated with the Faculty of Medicine at Atatürk University, which serve as training family health centers and are located at different campuses (three centers), volunteered to participate in the study, and met the inclusion criteria. During the data collection period, a total of 1,800 patients presented to the Family Health Centers. To minimize selection bias, every fifth consecutive patient who met the inclusion criteria was invited to participate in the study, reaching a total pool of 360 eligible individuals. Of these, 304 patients agreed to participate, yielding a face-to-face response rate of 84.4%. Following a preliminary data screening, 27 questionnaires were excluded due to missing data or failure to strictly meet the inclusion criteria. Consequently, the final statistical analysis was conducted with a valid sample of 277 participants.

To ensure a homogenous and representative study population, specific selection criteria were applied. Eligible participants were adults aged 18–65 years who visited the family health centers as outpatients during the study period, volunteered to participate by providing written informed consent, were literate in Turkish, and possessed the cognitive capacity to comprehend and respond to the survey items as assessed by the clinical researcher during face-to-face interviews. Conversely, individuals fell outside the 18–65 age bracket, exhibited severe communication impairments (e.g., profound hearing, speech, or visual deficits), or self-reported diagnosed cognitive disorders (such as dementia or Alzheimer’s disease) or severe psychiatric conditions (including schizophrenia and active psychotic episodes) were excluded from the study. Additionally, active healthcare professionals and medical students were excluded to prevent expert bias regarding technology acceptance and e-health literacy metrics.

The required sample size was evaluated using GPower software (v3.1.9.7). For the multi-variable models (hierarchical regression and parallel mediation analyses involving up to six predictors), an a priori power estimation indicated that a minimum sample of 146 participants was required to detect a moderate effect size (f2 = 0.15) with a significance level of α = 0.05 and a statistical power (1-) of 0.95. Data collection was continued until the end of the pre-specified study period (January 1 – March 1, 2026), which resulted in the final sample of 277 participants. The finalized sample size of 277 participants exceeded this threshold and provided adequate statistical power for the planned analyses.

Data collection tools

In addition to the sociodemographic information form, the following scales were used during the data collection process.

General attitude scale toward artificial intelligence

The General Attitude Scale toward Artificial Intelligence (GAAIS) was used. The Turkish version of the scale, adapted by Kaya and İşçi [20] from the original version developed by Schepman and Rodway [21], was used. The scale consists of two subscales: “Positive Attitude” and “Negative Attitude.” In the Turkish adaptation, item 20 of the original scale (“Artificial intelligence is used to monitor people”) was excluded due to structural inconsistency; therefore, the analyses were conducted using 19 items. GAAIS is 7point Likert scale comprising two subscales: ‘Positive Attitude’ and ‘Negative Attitude’. Each subscale is evaluated independently based on the cumulative scores. Higher scores obtained from the Positive Attitude subscale indicate a higher level of optimistic and favorable attitudes toward artificial intelligence. Conversely, higher scores on the Negative Attitude subscale reflect elevated levels of fear, anxiety, and negative perceptions regarding artificial intelligence technologies. In the present sample, the Cronbach’s alpha values were 0.88 for the Positive Attitude subscale and 0.82 for the Negative Attitude subscale.

Attitude scale for the use of telemedicine services

The Attitude Scale for the Use of Telemedicine Services was developed in Turkish by Yücel & Uyar in 2024 [22]. It is an 5point Likert scale and has 18-item, unidimensional scale that measures individuals’ attitudes toward telemedicine and remote consultation services. Higher scores on the scale (range 18–90) indicate a positive attitude toward telemedicine. Item 8 of the scale (“I do not trust the treatment provided via telemedicine”) was reverse coded prior to analysis because it is semantically negative. In the present sample, the Cronbach’s alpha for the scale was 0.91.

e-health literacy scale

The e-Health Literacy Scale (e-HEALS) [12], developed by Norman and Skinner to measure participants’ ability to access, understand, and evaluate health information in electronic environments, and for which a Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Uskun et al. [23], was used. Consisting of a total of 8 items and exhibiting a unidimensional structure, the scale is a 5-point Likert-type scale scored on a scale from ‘1: Strongly Disagree’ to ‘5: Strongly Agree.’ The total score range for the scale is between 8 and 40, and a higher score indicates a higher level of e-health literacy. In the present sample, the Cronbach’s alpha for the scale was 0.84.

For construct validity, all three scales have undergone rigorous validation within the Turkish cultural context. The GAAIS Turkish adaptation confirmed a solid two-factor structure via confirmatory factor analysis (CFA); the Telemedicine Scale demonstrated strong unidimensional fit statistics in primary care populations; and the e-HEALS has consistently proven its robust single-factor construct stability in adult groups.

Data analysis and assumptions of normality

Data analysis was performed using SPSS v27.0 and Jamovi v2.3 software. The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests, as well as measures of kurtosis and skewness. Considering the excessive sensitivity of the Shapiro-Wilk test in large samples (n > 100), the skewness and kurtosis values within the ± 1.5 range proposed by George and Mallery [24] were accepted as the basic criteria for parametric tests.

The following methods were used in the relational analyses. Group comparisons were performed using an independent samples t-test to assess differences between two groups. For categorical variables consisting of three or more groups (employment status, occupation, AI typology), the homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test) was checked; when variances were not homogeneous, Welch’s ANOVA was used, and for multiple comparisons, Games-Howell or Tukey post-hoc tests were employed. Hierarchical regression analysis was conducted to identify the predictors of attitudes toward telehealth (USH_S). A three-block hierarchical regression analysis was performed in which [1] demographic variables [2], health literacy, and [3] attitudes toward AI were added to the model step by step. Multicollinearity was checked using VIF values (< 2.0). Mediation analysis was performed using the Jamovi jAMM module to test the mediating role of AI attitudes in the relationship between e-health literacy and attitudes toward telemedicine. Significance tests were conducted using the bootstrap 95% confidence interval (CI) method with 5,000 resamples. A bootstrap confidence interval that did not include zero was considered evidence of a significant mediating effect. The hypothesized mediation model was tested using path analysis via the jAMM module, which allowed for the simultaneous estimation of direct and indirect effects of e- health literacy on attitudes toward telemedicine through AI attitudes.

In this study, ‘student status’ was operationalized within the employment variable to comprehensively capture the functional daily timeallocation and labour force status of the participants, as fulltime students in Turkey face distinct economic and timemanagement paradigms compared to active labour market participants. To address potential confounding bias introduced by age and socioeconomic status (monthly income), these variables were explicitly entered as covariates in the first block of the hierarchical regression analysis; however, monthly income was not a significant predictor in any model and is therefore not shown in the final table. This statistical control ensures that the observed effects of employment status and technology attitudes on telemedicine acceptance are independent of age related or economic differences.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Erzurum Atatürk University Faculty of Medicine (Approval No: B.30.2.ATA.0.01.00/779) on 28.11.2025. All participants were provided with detailed information about the study procedures and gave their written informed consent. The confidentiality and integrity of the data were maintained throughout the research process, and the study was carried out in compliance with the revised principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of participants

An analysis of the demographic distribution of the 277 participants in the study sample revealed that 57.4% (n = 159) were female and 42.6% (n = 118) were male. 52.3% of the participants (n = 145) were single, and 47.3% (n = 131) were married. In terms of educational level, a large majority (60.3%, n = 167) held a bachelor’s degree, followed by high school graduates at 17.3%. 51.6% of the participants (n = 143) were actively employed, while 32.5% (n = 90) were students. The sample’s mean age was 30.52 ± 9.95 (Min: 18, Max: 65), and the mean monthly income was calculated as 51,818.39 ± 60,001.01 TL. 83.4% of the participants (n = 231) reported having no chronic illnesses. The sample was predominantly young and highly educated, with a mean age of 30.5 years and 60.3% holding a bachelor’s degree. Therefore, the findings primarily reflect the views of younger, educated adult primary care attendees rather than the general adult population in Turkey.

E- health literacy has a massive direct effect on predicting attitudes toward telehealth services, and this effect is independent of technological attitudes.

E- health literacy directly and strongly predicts attitudes toward telehealth without requiring any other variables (β = 0.545). The parallel mediation analysis was executed utilizing a bias-corrected bootstrap resampling method (5,000 iterations) to evaluate the indirect effects of e- health literacy on telemedicine attitude via AI positive and negative attitudes. The total standardized effect of e-health literacy on telemedicine attitude was statistically significant (β = 0.285, 95% CI [0.299, 0.888], p < .001). The standardized direct effect (c’) remained significant (β = 0.263, 95% CI [0.255, 0.840], p < .001), confirming that e- health literacy maintains a strong, independent predictive power on technology acceptance. Crucially, the total indirect effect was statistically non-significant (βtotal_indirect = 0.022, 95% CI [-0.063, 0.117], p = .357), indicating the empirical absence of a functional mediation mechanism. Looking at the specific indirect pathways, the indirect effect channeled through AI positive attitude was nonsignificant (β = -0.009, 95% CI [-0.114, 0.057], p = .611), as was the path through AI negative attitude (β = 0.032, 95% CI [-0.013, 0.189], p = .124). In both instances, the 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals successfully spanned across zero, structurally confirming the lack of mediation. This breakdown occurs because e- health literacy does not significantly predict AI positive (β = -0.031, p = .608) or AI negative attitudes (β = -0.101, p = .092). However, independent of health literacy, both AI positive β = 0.307, p < .001) and AI negative attitudes β = -0.314, p < .001) emerged as powerful internal determinants of telemedicine acceptance.

AI attitudes (M1 and M2) do not mediate this effect of e- health literacy (Fig. 4). In other words, being health literate fosters acceptance of telemedicine not by altering one’s view of AI, but by enhancing the individual’s own knowledge capacity.

Discussion

This crosssectional study examined the associations between health literacy, AI attitudes, and telemedicine acceptance among 277 adults in Turkey.

It is well established that the level of e-health literacy is associated with the acceptance of digital health services and the ability to utilize them [25]. In our study, a strong direct association was found between e- health literacy and attitudes toward telehealth services, a finding that supports this established body of research. The mediation analysis revealed a strong direct association between e- health literacy and telemedicine acceptance. The indirect effects via AI attitudes were not statistically significant, suggesting that AI attitudes may not mediate this relationship in our sample. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as non-significant indirect effects do not rule out the possibility of mediation that could be detected with different or context-specific measures of AI attitudes. The finding that AI attitudes did not mediate this relationship was quite striking. It is speculated that various factors may account for this. The fact that the AI scale we used was not specifically developed for health-related issues may have prevented us from identifying specific assessments in this area; it should not be overlooked that various environmental and social factors influencing the assessed factors could have masked the mediating effects. In particular, the relatively young and highly educated nature of our sample may have influenced the results. Young and educated individuals may have more positive or flexible attitudes toward AI compared to the general population. Demographic factors, including age, have been shown to influence attitudes toward AI in previous research [26]. This situation may have masked the mediating effect. According to the results of our study, individuals with high e-health literacy reported more positive attitudes toward the use of telehealth services, regardless of their general attitudes toward AI. This may be because healthliterate individuals are aware of the conveniences that telehealth services can provide. The study by Qiu et al. [27], which states that individuals with high e-health literacy have a clearer understanding of their health needs, possess higher informationseeking and filtering skills, and that this was associated with a higher likelihood of recognizing the advantages of internetbased medical services and developing an intention to actively use them, supports our findings. There are various studies examining the role of health literacy in the use of health services. Studies have shown that individuals with low levels of health literacy are less likely to take advantage of health education and disease prevention services [28]. In addition, there are studies examining the relationship between health literacy and the adoption of health information technologies. For example, in their study evaluating the relationship between health literacy and the adoption of health information technologies, Mackert et al. found that patients with low health literacy were less likely to use health information technology tools or perceive them as easy or beneficial [13].

It is well established that trust in AI is strongly associated with the willingness to use AI [29]. In our study, hierarchical regression analysis revealed that the strongest negative predictor of attitudes toward telehealth was a negative attitude toward AI. This finding may be explained by individuals’ concerns and anxieties regarding AI. It is observed that even healthcare professionals’ negative perceptions of AI come to the forefront in technological applications within the healthcare field [30]. Technological systems providing telemedicine services utilize AI algorithms across various domains, ranging from diagnostic support to patient monitoring and data analysis [31]. Individuals with negative views toward AI also approach telemedicine service providers that use these AI algorithms with suspicion. A study examining patient concerns regarding AIbased voice analysis technologies found that a significant portion of participants approached the technology with scepticism and expressed privacy concerns [32]. Studies examining AI anxiety in the context of healthcare also support this finding. In their study assessing individuals’ resistance to AIbased healthcare services, Longoni et al. found that people exhibited resistance due to a “sense of unique invulnerability.” Individuals resist AIbased systems because they believe AI does not reach a conclusion by evaluating their own unique circumstances [33]. In another study related to this topic, Yokoi et al. found that patients trust AI less than they trust a doctor. It is speculated that this situation may stem from perceived differences in care and value, rather than the AI system’s capabilities [34]. These studies suggest that such distrust and anxiety could lead to a negative attitude toward AIbased telemedicine services, which aligns with the findings of our study.

The two main findings of our study are that e- health literacy was independently and directly associated with higher acceptance to telemedicine services, and that a negative attitude toward AI were associated with lower acceptance to telemedicine services. When these two findings are considered together, it suggests that the willingness and ability to use technological tools depend on various factors. This multivariate model explains why an individual with a negative attitude toward AI may still adapt to telehealth servicessetting aside their negative viewsif they possess a high level of health literacy, particularly when it comes to the tangible benefits of telehealth. This finding may suggest that e-health literacy is associated with telemedicine acceptance independently of AI related concerns, possibly by enhancing individuals’ ability to recognize the practical benefits of telehealth. Our study found statistically significant differences in attitudes toward telemedicine based on participants’ sociodemographic characteristics. In the analysis based on employment status, it was found that the telemedicine attitude scores of employed individuals were statistically significantly higher than those of students. This finding may be related to working individuals’ desire to use their time more effectively. Regarding marital status, it was found that the remote healthcare attitude scores of married individuals were statistically significantly higher than those of single individuals. This finding may be related to married individuals’ awareness of their responsibility for family health. In their study, Quintal et al. found, similarly to our findings, that married individuals prefer telemedicine services more than single individuals, and working adults prefer them more than students [35]. In comparisons across occupational groups, it was found that teachers’ AI positive attitude scores were significantly higher than those of students. One possible explanation for this finding is that teachers may use AIsupported materials in their professional lives.

This study contributes to the literature by suggesting that e- health literacy is associated with attitudes toward telehealth services independently of attitudes toward AI. Furthermore, the fact that a negative attitude toward AI were the strongest negative predictor associated with telemedicine acceptance suggests that that affective factors (such as anxiety) are just as important as cognitive factors in the use of health technologies.

This study has several limitations. First, the crosssectional design precludes causal inferences. Second, the predominantly young and educated sample limits generalizability, although this group represents the primary future users of digital health services in Turkey. Additionally, several potential confounders (e.g., income, digital literacy, internet access, prior telemedicine experience) were not measured and may have influenced the results. Finally, the modest explanatory power of our model suggests that other variables (e.g., trust in physicians) also affect telemedicine acceptance and should be explored in future studies. Additionally, the exclusion of healthcare professionals and medical students, although methodologically justified to prevent expert bias, limits the generalizability of our findings to these important groups, who are major users and influencers of telemedicine and AI applications in healthcare.

Conclusion

In this cross sectional sample, e-health literacy was positively associated with telemedicine acceptance, and negative attitudes toward AI were inversely associated with acceptance. AI attitudes did not statistically mediate the association between e-health literacy and telemedicine acceptance. These findings suggest that e-health literacy may be a key target for interventions aimed at promoting telemedicine adoption, independent of individuals’ general views about AI. The direct nature of the association observed in our mediation model may indicate that e-health literacy operates through cognitive and informational capacities rather than through affective evaluations of AI, although this interpretation requires further investigation. The finding that negative AI attitudes were inversely associated with telemedicine acceptance suggests that concerns about AI, including ethical and privacy issues, may also influence the adoption of technology mediated health services. In clinical practice, it is crucial for family physicians to develop patient centered information strategies that enhance patients’ e-health literacy and reinforce trust in AI based systems for the success of the digital health transformation. Additionally, policymakers should consider legal regulations that ensure the transparency of artificial intelligence. Future studies are encouraged to examine the association between e-health literacy and adaptation to telemedicine using longitudinal or qualitative methods, as well as to investigate other variables that may influence the process.Based on our findings, we offer several recommendations to healthcare professionals and policymakers for the widespread adoption of telehealth services. The first of these is the development of educational programs and public service announcements aimed at improving e-health literacy. Although our cross-sectional design does not allow us to test these outcomes directly, the association between e-health literacy and telemedicine acceptance suggests that improving e-health literacy could potentially contribute to more efficient use of primary care resources and better patient access to specialist consultations. Indeed, Chisolm et al. demonstrated the importance of this recommendation by finding that digital health literacy training increased adults’ confidence in and participation in telehealth [14]. To address negative attitudes toward AI, assurances regarding data security and privacy must be provided, and awareness campaigns should be conducted. In this way, efforts should be made to address the negative attitudes toward AI that are associated with lower acceptance of telemedicine services.
