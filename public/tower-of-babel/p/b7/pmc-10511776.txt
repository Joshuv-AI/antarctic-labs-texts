Radical local excision of the mons for treatment of recalcitrant lymphangioma circumscriptum
Authors: Ruth Baker, Elizabeth O. Ferreira, Peter A. Argenta
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Acquired lymphangioma circumscriptum may arise in adulthood as a result of blunt trauma.•Large scale surgical excision may effectively treat recalcitrant lymphangioma circumscriptum.•Patients with large lymphangioma circumscriptum lesions may benefit from earlier surgical intervention.

Introduction

Lymphangioma circumscriptum (LC) is a benign vascular lesion involving impaired drainage of lymphatic vessels in deep dermal and subcutaneous layers. These lesions are rare, accounting for 4 % of all vascular tumors. LC often presents at or shortly following birth, with 90 % occurring before 2 years of age, but may be acquired later in life due to pathologies or interventions that cause impaired lymph flow. Congenital lymphatic malformations may be related to syndromes such as Turner syndrome or Downs syndrome but their pathogenesis remains poorly understood. This condition typically presents as edema, papules or vesicles, and may have associated itching, bleeding or pain. The appearance of LC may mimic a variety of infections or tumors leading to misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis. Case reports describing acquired LC often link its emergence to previous surgical intervention, trauma, or radiation therapy of the same area (Ghaemmaghami et al., 2008). Genital involvement is uncommon but has been described following hysterectomy, pelvic lymphadenectomy, hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) sinus tract removal, radiation, and in association with Crohn’s disease and tuberculosis (Chang et al., 2016, Marous and Mercurio, 2021). Complications related to vulvar LC include drainage, secondary infection, swelling, discomfort, and psychosexual dysfunction (Amouri et al., 2007).

Case

A 42 year-old non-smoking Caucasian female presented to gynecologic oncology clinic with a complaint of vulvar pain and swelling compromising her ability to perform routine activities. She notably had a history of a subcutaneous abscess on the mons measuring 44 mm × 23 mm at age 31 which was first noted after a motor vehicle accident during which she suffered trauma to the affected area from the seat belt restraint. After failing antibiotic therapy, she underwent incision and drainage of this abscess and 6 months of antibiotic therapy. Despite closure of the initial wound, she began to develop vulvar and mons varicosities as well as additional superficial draining sinuses which left her tender and wet. Physical exam during that presentation describes a nodular, tender rash over the mons and low abdomen described as possible cellulitis, candidiasis or hidradenitis. She began following with dermatology for ongoing lesions and initial biopsy of the mons at age 33 described a superficial dermal varix. Clinical concern for a diagnosis of LC by dermatology began the same year and was later confirmed by biopsy. Lesions consistent with hidradenitis and LC involved her groin, low back, and buttocks, without involvement of her axillae or inframammary folds. A magnetic resonance arteriogram performed to exclude arterial-venous malformation was negative; and both HPV and Quantiferon gold tuberculosis testing were negative.

She was subsequently treated with six courses of Sotradecol sclerotherapy with transient improvement of symptoms, but was referred for pulsed dye laser (PDL) therapy less than 6 months after completing sclerotherapy. She completed 14 (q2 month) treatments of PDL therapy, again noting transient benefit without resolution. She was also evaluated by two independent plastic surgeons during her treatments for possible panniculectomy, both agreed that the condition was largely confined to the mons pubis and would not likely benefit from panniculectomy. Repeat biopsy was performed to exclude alternate pathology but again was felt to be consistent with verrucous lymphangioma circumscriptum. Owing to persistent symptoms she was referred for hyfrecator tissue ablation and was treated twice before observing a symmetrical enlargement of her mons.

She subsequently required inpatient admissions for treatment of cellulitis of the mons and anterior abdominal wall, which both resolved with prolonged antibiotic therapy.

In the year prior to presentation to our clinic she had been further treated with 5 courses of interventional radiology-directed deep tissue ablation using Sotradecol and doxycycline, with admission for ulcerated abscess and cellulitis following the first of these. She had had multiple incision and drainage procedures, as well as high dose steroid injections, and had been treated with topical Sirolimus for multiple months without alleviation of her symptoms. After discussing options at length, the patient elected for a radical local excision under general anesthesia. She received a single dose of antibiotics pre-operatively. The mons was excised to the level of the underlying fascia (pathologic specimen size 22 × 17 × 11 cm) and the superior margin was elevated and advanced across the defect and fixed with PDS and Vicryl suture without drain placement. The procedure took two hours and twenty seven minutes.

The pathology report confirmed persistent LC. Histologic sections demonstrated ectatic benign lymphatic channels situated in the superficial dermis resulting in a verruciform-like epidermal hyperplastic reaction. Features of HPV-associated disease were absent. The lesion was situated in an area of abundant chronic lymphoplasmacytic inflammation, which extended to deep subcutaneous tissue. The skin was closed with widely placed staples and dressed with a vacuum wound dressing. The patient was observed overnight and discharged without event on post-operative day one, reporting complete resolution of her discomfort. Her wound was evaluated twice weekly for the first two weeks after which her staples were removed. She had a 30 × 5 mm wound separation without evidence of infection on post operative day 14 (Fig. 1c).

At her 4 month post-operative evaluation she was without recurrent swelling and her wounds had healed well (Fig. 1d–e). She remains well at 10 months without evidence of recurrent swelling.

Discussion

This case demonstrates the effectiveness of aggressive surgical management of LC recalcitrant to less invasive approaches. This patient was symptomatic from mass effect caused by progressively worsening edema of the mons. While initially hoping to avoid more aggressive intervention, she reported “significant improvement” in her quality of life following surgical excision and was able to sit and ambulate without discomfort within 1 day. Hidradenitis lesions outside the surgical excision site persist, but after a brief delay in healing, she remained without evidence of LC recurrence at 4 and 10 month follow up.

There is no standard treatment for LC (Ghaemmaghami et al., 2008). Both non-interventional (external compression garments and exercise) as well as localized interventions (sclerotherapy, laser therapy, electrocautery, cryotherapy, or surgical “unroofing” surgeries) have all been applied with varying degrees of success (Ghaemmaghami et al., 2008, Piernick et al., 2017). Recurrence after attempt at definitive treatment is common unless the entire lesion including the deep communicating lymphatic system is removed or destroyed (Bond et al., 2008).

Carbon dioxide (CO2) laser treatment has been described as a successful method of managing LC in that it vaporizes some of the communicating vessels to deeper cisterns with a more acceptable cosmetic result than surgical removal, however this method may result in relapse, and the development of keloids has also been described (Puri, 2015). CO2 laser energy delivered to deeper structures also requires large amounts of local anesthesia (Puri, 2015). Pulsed dye laser targets chromophore hemoglobin in and around vessels without damaging surrounding tissues so treatment in LC may be limited by the minimal hemoglobin (dilated lymphatic channels contain serosanguineous fluid) (Puri, 2015). Cryotherapy uses low temperatures to cause vasoconstriction leading to cellular necrosis with the intention of promoting healing by secondary intention. Intralesional sclerotherapy is a minimally invasive method of treatment that may be successful in superficial LC with rates varying from 58 to 100 % (Puri, 2015).

Surgical excision, typically radical with resection of the dermis and underlying subcutaneous tissue to the underlying fascia, is considered the most definitive treatment option with highest chance of cure (Vignes et al., 2010). The success of surgical management depends on the extent of disease, its location, and the depth of lymphatic cisterns within the dermis (Piernick et al., 2017). The acute risks of surgical excision are greater than other treatment options described and include recurrence, scarring, keloid formation, hematoma, wound infection, nerve injury, and disfigurement (Puri, 2015). In our patient the long-term excess weight of the distended mons acted as de facto tissue expansion, leading to her symptoms but ultimately providing adequate healthy tissue at the surgical resection margin to allow for a tension-free closure despite the large excision. Delayed complications of surgical resection include worsening disease due to further disruption of lymphatic drainage (Piernick et al., 2017). The degree to which local ablative or scar-inducing strategies contribute to secondary or tertiary aberrant lymph flow is unknown as there are no controlled trials from which to draw comparisons. Recurrence is common following even radical surgical excision (as high as 23 % in some studies), but serial excisions (up to three in some reports) appears to have resulted in symptom resolution in the majority of cases (seven out of eight) at a median of 53 month follow up in one previous review (Vignes et al., 2010).

Ultimately, confirming the diagnosis and minimizing associated pathologies (infection, trauma, HS, and surgical or ablative tissue destruction), are key in preventing the development of new lesions. Our patient remains physically active and continues treatment of her HS with biologic therapy; and has not required any incisional, excisional, or ablative interventions since surgery.
