Climate emotions, parenting plans, and racial difference in the United States
Authors: Jade S Sasser, Emily Klancher Merchant
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIncreasing attention is being paid to the relationship between climate change, emotional and mental distress, and feelings about raising children. These studies often overlook the role of demographics, race in particular, despite evidence that racial minority groups in the U.S. experience more detrimental climate impacts and express more climate concern than do white communities. This survey was conducted to elucidate the relationship between race, climate emotions, and parenting plans in the U.S.MethodsWe surveyed 2489 people in the U.S. between the ages of 22 and 35 in September 2021. Because we were interested in the role of race, we oversampled those who identified as non-white/people of color. The survey assessed participants’ emotions with respect to climate change, and their emotions with respect to birthing and raising children (parenting) in the context of climate change.ResultsNonwhite respondents were more likely than white respondents to report feeling traumatized as a response to climate change, and to plan to have fewer future children than they wanted as a result of their climate-related emotions. They were also more likely to report feeling more optimistic and hopeful in response to climate change and in response to raising existing children in the context of climate change. White women were least likely to report any positive emotions with respect to parenting during climate change. White men were most likely to report non-feeling emotions such as numbness, indifference, or being checked out. Christian respondents were more likely to report feeling positive emotions overall.ConclusionThis study highlights race as a statistically significant factor in the reporting of climate emotions and parenting plans. It demonstrates that climate change has contradictory impacts on the emotions of nonwhite people of color, while having a negative impact on childbearing plans. We call for further research into the role of race in climate-related emotions and parenting plans, particularly with respect to the impacts of traumatized feelings, as well as the roles of religion and positive emotions.Keywords: Climate, Emotions, Climate anxiety, Parenting plans, Race

Introduction

Popular media is increasingly focusing on mental and emotional distress in response to climate change, and how this distress impacts reproductive plans [[1], [2], [3]]. Young climate activists have also emphasized this perspective, notably in campaigns focusing on not having children until government leaders develop stronger policies supporting climate change mitigation and adaptation [4]. Despite this trend, few research studies situate reproductive plans in the context of emotions about the environment and climate. The lack of research is problematic, as the increase in public attention signals a growing concern. Systematic research into the issue could provide a base of evidence for the scope of the concern and shed light on the broader dynamics linking climate emotions and parenting concerns.

Academic studies that have addressed reproduction and environmental and climate concerns highlight generational differences and gendered norms [5]; a growing interest in childfree lifestyles among people who support an anti-population perspective [6]; the well-being of existing or hypothetical children in a world impacted by climate change [7]; the desire to link responsible environmental behavior with responsible parenting [8]; and the role of environmental pollution concerns in attitudes toward childbearing and ideal number of children [9]. In each study, gender is analyzed as a central demographic category, while race is not. The present study fills this gap by analyzing the role of race [10] in climate emotions and parenting plans among people in the United States.

Race is an important demographic factor in the American context, where racial minorities are more likely than white Americans to express concern or alarm in response to climate change [11]. This concern extends to reproductive decision-making. A national poll conducted in 2020 found that younger Hispanic and black respondents were more likely to plan to forego having children because of climate change [12]. This may be due in part to social vulnerability. Social vulnerability—particularly the vulnerability produced by racial inequality—is understudied in environmental emotions and environmental psychology research, both in the U.S. and globally [13,14]. Climate-related mental health stressors have the potential to exacerbate and multiply the effects of such other existing stressors as poverty, systemic racism, and racial violence [15,16], and may be associated with such negative mental health impacts as anxiety, mood disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), mental illness, and suicide [17].

The dearth of studies of racial and cultural differentials in environmental emotions make it difficult to understand the relationships between race, climate emotions, and reproductive plans. This omission may have the unintended effect of centering the experiences of racially privileged individuals [18]. To address this knowledge gap, we surveyed more than 2500 Americans aged 22–35 to explore whether race plays a role in climate-related emotions, reproductive desires, and reproductive plans in the United States. Given that the literature shows that racial minority communities in the United States are particularly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change [19], we examine whether and how climate emotions may impact how people of color feel about parenting, now or in the future.

Material and methods

This study used a questionnaire we developed on a nationally representative sample of respondents in the panel managed by the survey company Qualtrics. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of California, Riverside (Ref. no. 21126)

Participants

Participants were recruited to our study by Qualtrics if they were between the ages of 22 and 35 and if they answered affirmatively when asked if they believed that climate change is real. We excluded individuals who said that climate change is not real because the primary aim of the study was to assess the effect of race on climate emotions, and from there the effect of climate emotions on parenting plans. In order to assess climate emotions, one must profess a belief that climate change is real. We were not interested in climate denial. Further, our aim was to study the effect of racial difference on expression of climate emotions and related parenting plans between non-Hispanic white and racial minority respondents in the United States. To do this, Qualtrics recruited equal numbers of non-Hispanic white and Hispanic or non-white participants to the survey. Participants were recruited through random, independent sampling. When the desired number of non-Hispanic white participants was attained, we ceased recruitment of this demographic. The same is true of non-white participants.

Survey design and data collection

We designed the survey to collect information on participant demographics (race/ethnicity, gender, age, income, education, parent status, and religion); political orientation; emotions experienced in response to climate change; and childbearing plans.

To classify participants’ race and ethnicity, we asked respondents whether they were of Hispanic, Latino, or Spanish origin, and then asked them to identify with one of the following racial categories: American Indian/Alaska Native/Indigenous, Asian/Asian American, Black/African American, Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander, Middle Eastern/Arab, non-Hispanic white, mixed race, and other race. We collapsed these categories into two: white and people of color, classifying someone as white if they identified themselves as “non-Hispanic white” and not of Hispanic, Latino, or Spanish origin. We collapse racial categories in this way in order to consider the relationship between a person's climate emotions and their location in the racial power structure of the United States. While it is true that each group has a different history and experience of racial discrimination, people of color occupy a subaltern status in the United States that is specific to race and cannot be reduced to any other category of alterity. We classified people who identified as mixed race as people of color because people who are mixed race are typically considered people of color in the United States and therefore experience racial discrimination.

To classify participants’ gender, we asked respondents to identify as either man, woman, or trans/non-binary/gender non-conforming. Since the vast majority (over 98 %) of our respondents identified as either a man or a woman, we limited our analysis to these two groups.

To examine respondent characteristics other than race and gender that might influence climate change emotions, we asked respondents to identify their age (22–25, 26–30, or 31–35), income level (less than $26,500/year, $26,500–$39,999/year, $40,000–$119,999/year, or $120,000/year or more), educational attainment (high school, Associate's degree, Bachelor's degree, Master's degree, or Doctorate), whether they have children or are currently expecting, and religion (dichotomized as Christian or not). We report religion in this way because Christianity is the majority religion in the United States. However, we recognize that there is heterogeneity within the Christian faith, and that this heterogeneity may potentially impact climate emotions. In addition to these demographic characteristics, we asked participants to identify their political identity on a spectrum from very conservative to very liberal, though respondents could also identify as “other” or “not political at all.”

To assess the emotions respondents experienced in response to climate change, we compiled a taxonomy of emotions from existing studies. These studies typically mentioned fear/worry/anxiety, sadness/grief, guilt/shame, and hope/empowerment [20]. Previous studies have also organized climate emotions based on self-condemning, other-condemning, self-praising, other-praising categories, and more [21], as well as emotion categories based on closeness between emotions [20]. Our survey presented respondents with a list of emotions they may have experienced with regard to climate change, and another list of emotions they may have experienced with regard to parenting in the context of climate change (see supplemental material). For each list, respondents were required to choose at least one emotion or to state that they had no feelings; they were permitted to choose as many emotions as they wanted. Some emotions were only available in response to climate change in general, others were only available in response to parenting in the context of climate change, and the rest were available for both questions. The reason different emotions were available is because we wanted to arrive at some specificity of the emotional response to parenting. The emotions included in the questions about parenting were selected based on analysis of informal qualitative interviews with young people who are concerned about parenting in the midst of climate change.

Parenting plans were assessed only for a sub-sample of respondents who did not yet have children and were not expecting (we refer to this as the non-parent sub-sample). We asked respondents in this sub-sample how many children they planned to have or adopt, and how many they would want to have or adopt if climate change were not happening. We did not distinguish between having biological children and adopting because we are trying to understand how people feel about raising children in the midst of climate change, not how they feel about adding to the number of people on the planet.

Statistical analysis

All analysis was performed using R version 4.4.1 for Mac. We used descriptive statistics to analyze respondent characteristics and the emotions reported by respondents. To examine the effects of demographic characteristics and political identity on the emotions experienced with regard to climate change in general and parenting in the midst of climate change, we fit a separate logistic regression model for each emotion (using the glm function in base R with the family = binomial(link = “logit”) option), with the outcome being whether or not a respondent reported the given emotion. Independent variables were race, gender, age, income, education, parenting status, religion, and political identity.

To understand who was taking climate change into account in their parenting plans, we constructed four logistic regression models for our non-parent sub-sample (again using the glm function with the family = binomial(link = “logit”) option). The first used only race as an independent variable. The second used political identity and demographic characteristics (race, gender, age, income, education, and religion) as independent variables. In the third model, independent variables were the emotions reported in response to parenting in the midst of climate change, grouped into categories (see supplemental material). A person was considered to have reported an emotion category if they reported at least one emotion in that category. The fourth model used all independent variables from the previous models. In all regression models, we assumed that independent variables were independent of one another.

Climate emotions

Fig. 1 shows the percentage of respondents reporting each emotion, with standard errors. Emotions reported with respect to climate change in general are graphed on the left; emotions reported with respect to parenting in the midst of climate change are graphed on the right.

Percent of survey respondents who reported feeling various emotions with respect to climate change in general and parenting in the midst of climate change, with standard errors.

Overall, the most frequently reported emotions were uncertainty and concern, with over 40 % of respondents reporting these feelings with respect to climate change in general and over 30 % with respect to parenting in the midst of climate change. Fewer than 6 % reported feeling indifferent, numb, or checked out. As Fig. 1 indicates, when thinking about climate change in general, respondents reported feeling negative emotions (anger, resentment, depression, sadness, guilt, trauma, powerlessness, anxiety, fear, and overwhelm) in greater numbers than positive emotions (optimism, hope, and motivation). However, when respondents were asked to consider parenting in the midst of climate change, they reported positive emotions in greater numbers than negative emotions.

Emotions by race and gender

Fig. 2a, Fig. 2b show the proportion of women of color, men of color, white women, and white men experiencing each emotion, along with standard errors. Most emotions exhibited clear patterning by race and gender.

Percent of survey respondents in each race/ethnicity and gender category who reported feeling various emotions with respect to climate change in general, with standard errors.

Percent of survey respondents in each race/ethnicity and gender category who reported feeling various emotions with respect to parenting in the midst of climate change, with standard errors.

With respect to both climate change in general and to parenting in the midst of climate change, respondents of color were more likely than white respondents, and men more likely than women, to report positive feelings. With respect to climate change in general, differences were statistically significant (p < 0.05, Welch two-sample t-test using the t.test function in base R) between respondents of color and white respondents for hope, and between men and women for optimism, hope, and motivation. With respect to parenting in the midst of climate change, differences were statistically significant between respondents of color and white respondents for hope, happiness, excitement, and determination, and between men and women for optimism, hope, happiness, excitement, determination, and motivation.

With respect to climate change in general, respondents of color were more likely than white respondents to report feeling traumatized (10 % as compared to 6 %, p < 0.05), but less likely to report feeling powerless (31 % as compared to 38 %, p < 0.05). Women were more likely than men to report negative feelings. With respect to climate change in general, gender differences were statistically significant for sadness, guilt, anxiety, fear, and overwhelm; with respect to parenting in the midst of climate change, gender differences were statistically significant for sadness, anxiety, and fear.

At the intersection of race and gender, women of color were least likely to report feeling powerless with respect to climate change in general (29 %, p < 0.05 for difference from all other groups), while white women were most likely to report feeling powerless (43 %, p < 0.05 for difference from all other groups). White women were considerably more likely to report feeling anxious, uncertain, and concerned with respect to climate change in general than were any other group (52 %, 56 %, and 54 %, p < 0.05 for all differences). With respect to parenting in the midst of climate change, white women were by far the least likely to report any of the positive emotions (optimism, hope, happiness, excitement, determination, and motivation), and the most likely to report uncertainty, with differences from all other groups statistically significant. White men were more likely than anyone else to report non-feeling emotions (indifference, numbness, being checked out, or having no feelings), both with respect to climate change in general and with respect to parenting in the midst of climate change, though the differences from other groups was not always statistically significant.

Fig. 3 compares the emotional experiences of respondents of color to those of white respondents, adjusting for the gender disparities in the sample. It shows the rate at which we would expect respondents of color and white respondents to report feeling each emotion, if each race category had equal numbers of men and women.

The rate at which we would expect respondents in each race/ethnicity category to report each emotion if each group had equal numbers of male- and female-identified respondents, with standard errors.

The patterns in Fig. 3 are quite clear with respect to climate change in general: controlling for gender, respondents of color were more likely than white respondents to report feeling optimistic and hopeful (p < 0.05, Welch two-sample t-test using the wtd.t.test function from the weights package), and were less likely than white respondents to report feeling most of the negative emotions or non-emotions: anger, resentment, depression, powerlessness, anxiety, overwhelm, uncertainty, concern, or indifference, or to report feeling checked out or having no feelings (p < 0.05). The major exception, as also reflected in Fig. 2a, is that respondents of color were nearly twice as likely as white respondents to report feeling traumatized (11 % for respondents of color as compared to 6 % for white respondents, p < 0.05). Respondents of color and white respondents reported feeling motivated, sad, guilty, scared, and numb in similar proportions.

With respect to parenting in the midst of climate change, respondents of color were again more likely than white respondents to report feeling the positive emotions (optimistic, hopeful, motivated, determined, happy, and excited, all statistically significant), but there was little difference in the frequency with which respondents of color and white respondents reported feeling the negative emotions (angry, disgusted, depressed, sad, guilty, anxious, scared, and concerned). Respondents of color were significantly less likely than white respondents to report feeling uncertain or having no feelings.

Many of these race gaps in emotions about climate change continue to hold when income, education, political identity, parenthood, age, religion, and gender are controlled through logistic regression. The signs and significances of coefficients for logistic regression models predicting the reporting of each emotion (one model for each emotion) are shown in Fig. 4 (signs shown in black were statistically significant at p < 0.05; signs shown in gray were not; p-values were not adjusted for multiple hypothesis testing). Net of all demographic and political characteristics, respondents of color were significantly more likely than white respondents to report optimism and hope with respect to climate change in general, and all of the positive emotions (optimistic, hopeful, motivated, determined, happy, and excited) with respect to parenting in the midst of climate change. At the same time, with respect to climate change in general, respondents of color were significantly more likely than white respondents to report feeling traumatized, but less likely to report feeling angry, resentful, depressed, powerless, overwhelmed, or uncertain, or to report feeling checked out or having no feelings. With respect to parenting in the midst of climate change, respondents of color were significantly less likely than white respondents to report feeling angry or uncertain, or to report having no feelings. Race has no statistically significant effect on the odds of reporting disgust, sadness, guilt, anxiety, fear, concern, confusion, indifference or numbness.

Religion had a statistically significant effect on some emotions, with respondents identifying as Christian generally being more likely than other respondents to report feeling positive emotions and less likely to report feeling negative emotions. Respondents who already had children (or were expecting at the time of the survey) were more likely to report feeling optimistic and hopeful with respect to climate change in general and more likely to report feeling determined with respect to parenting in the midst of climate change. However, they were also more likely to report feeling sad, anxious, scared, concerned, and confused with respect to parenting in the midst of climate change. Political identity had a strong effect on emotions, with respondents identifying as more liberal being more likely than those identifying as less liberal to report most of the negative emotions. Income had no effect on most emotions, though people with more income were more likely to report feeling uncertain or concerned, and less likely to report feeling numb, with respect to climate change in general. They were more likely to report feeling hopeful, anxious and concerned, and less likely to report feeling numb, with respect to parenting in the midst of climate change.

Childbearing plans

When respondents without children (our non-parent sub-sample) were asked how many children they planned to parent, and how many children they would want to parent if climate change were not happening, a clear pattern emerged. The most popular answer to both questions for respondents in both race categories was two or more. However, a small number of respondents who would ideally want to parent two or more children if climate change were not happening planned to parent fewer than two children under current conditions.

Despite being more likely to report positive emotions with respect to climate change in general and with respect to parenting in the midst of climate change, respondents of color who did not already have children were more likely than white respondents to report that they would want to parent more children if climate change were not happening. Nearly 30 % of respondents of color would want to parent more children than they are currently planning if climate change were not happening, as compared to only 20.4 % of white parents.

Table 3 shows regression coefficients for our four models of childbearing plans, along with standard errors, 95 % confidence intervals, and p-values (not adjusted for multiple hypothesis testing). Coefficients in bold face were statistically significant (p < 0.05). Model 1 shows that, when no other variables are controlled, race is a significant predictor of a person reporting that they would want to parent more children than they are currently planning if climate change were not happening, with respondents of color more likely than white respondents to answer this way. Model 2 indicates that, even when all demographic and political variables were controlled, respondents of color were still more likely than white respondents to report that they would want to parent more children than they are currently planning if climate change were not happening. The regression coefficient changes—which indicates the difference being white makes—changes only from −0.506 to −0.471. In terms of odds ratios, the univariate model (Model 1) predicts that a white respondent is only 60 % as likely as a respondent of color to say that they would want more children if climate change weren't happening, while the model with all demographic and political variables (Model 2) predicts that a white respondent is 62 % as likely as a respondent of color to say that they would want more children if climate change weren't happening. This finding suggests that climate change is having more of a negative effect on the parenting plans of respondents of color than on the parenting plans of white respondents, and that most of this difference can't be accounted for by differences in income, education, political identity, age, religion, or gender.

Fear about parenting in the midst of climate change also predicts a negative effect of climate change on parenting plans, both when all other emotions are held constant (Table 3, Model 3), and when demographic and political variables are also held constant (Table 3, Model 4). Race and fear both retain their significance and most of their magnitude in Model 4, which includes emotions as well as demographic and political variables, indicating that climate change is more likely to have a negative effect on the parenting plans of people of color even in the face of the same climate-related emotions.

Discussion and conclusions

Study implications

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the role of race in climate emotions and climate-related reproductive plans. We explored race as a factor in climate emotions and reproduction because the United States has a particular, context-specific history of racial privilege and power being afforded to non-Hispanic whites and denied to non-white racial minorities. This history, and its ongoing legacy, shapes exposure to climate impacts as well as a range of social, political, and economic vulnerabilities that are relevant to both climate emotions and raising children in the U.S.

Our study results suggest that racial minority status is a contradictory factor in the identification of climate emotions, and in the relationship between climate emotions and parenting plans. Respondents of color are significantly more likely than white respondents to report feeling traumatized by climate change, which correlates with other research demonstrating the disproportionate climate impacts across all communities of color [22]. At the same time, respondents of color are significantly more likely than white respondents to report optimism, hope, and other positive emotions with respect to climate change and parenting during climate change. As we state below, the more positive emotions could be a protective effect of religion, particularly Christianity (both Protestant and Catholic). However, and despite being more likely to report positive climate emotions, respondents of color were more likely to report that they would want to have more children if climate change were not happening. In other words, despite reporting more positive climate emotions, and generally regardless of reported emotion, climate change was a negative factor in the parenting plans of people of color in this study. This could simply be an indicator that, despite respondents of color reporting more emotions at two opposite poles (trauma and happiness/joy), it is the more negative emotions that have a stronger impact on people of color's parenting plans, as their emotions are mediated by the material reality of disproportionate climate harm in the U.S. Race was the only statistically significant factor in this association, regardless of other demographic variables such as education level, political affiliation, religion, or income. This indicates that, while race and class are tightly correlated, they are not reducible to one another.

This study builds and expands on previous research on climate change-related concerns and reproductive plans in North America that do not analyze race as a key variable. A previous U.S.-based study of “eco-repro concerns” [7] found that respondents’ primary concerns included the well-being of existing or hypothetical children in a world impacted by climate change and the carbon footprint of reproduction. Similar to a previous Canadian study [9], survey participants were racially homogenous (88 % were white); further, they represented a narrow segment of American society in terms of politics (91 % identified as liberal or very liberal), gender (73 % identified as women), and education (93 % had a bachelor's or graduate level degree). It is noteworthy that previous research found that environmental and climate concerns have an impact on parenting plans among a particular segment of white American women. At the same time, it is impossible to understand the impact of racial differences in other studies whose populations were homogenous and primarily white.

Religion was associated with more positive climate emotions in this study. Given that higher proportions of Black and Latino individuals in the United States identify as Christian [23,24], and most of the non-white study participants identified in these racial groups, having a Christian religious affiliation likely accounts for some of the positive emotional responses. However, despite these positive emotions, respondents of color were more likely to plan to have fewer children than they want, because of climate change. We recommend future research on the relationship between religion, including intra-Christian religious belief, and climate emotions (which are different from climate change related values). We propose building on existing studies of religion and climate denial [25]; and religion and climate polarization [26] to explore that intersection further, particularly with respect to parenting plans.

Study limitations

The selection criterion for the study excluded those who believe that climate change is not real. This means that a larger proportion of our sample will express emotions about climate change than we would expect to find in the United States as a whole. Further, with this survey instrument, we can only measure feelings reported by respondents, which may not accurately reflect what they actually felt. In addition, we organized respondents into the racial categories of “non-Hispanic white” and “Other.” While this categorization reflects structural-racial power dynamics of the United States, it adopts the risk of assuming homogeneity in the social experiences of those in the “Other” category. Finally, our survey instrument did not distinguish between Protestant Christians and Catholics, or Evangelicals and other Protestants, because our primary goal was to assess the effects of race (not religion) on climate emotions, and the effect of climate emotions on parenting plans. Our results indicate the possibility that identifying as Christian has a correlation with more positive emotions about parenting during climate change. Future research should examine this effect, as well as examining whether climate emotions and related parenting plans differ between intra-group Christians.

Recommendations for future research

We recommend future research to illuminate the specific perspectives of various non-white racial groups with respect to climate emotions and parenting plans. We also recommend qualitative research on race, climate emotions, and parenting plans in order to elucidate the reasons why respondents of color were more likely to report emotions at the two poles of positive emotions and trauma. Such research would also further explore the role of religion in actual plans to have children, emotions around raising those children, and the role of climate change. Our data also suggest the importance of additional research into the role of race in climate trauma. Climate trauma is a unique category of trauma in that it is “continually triggering all past traumas—personal, cultural, and intergenerational” [27]. The specific traumas of racial vulnerability in the U.S. may yield useful insights into climate trauma among racial minority groups.

Further research on race and climate emotions may inform policy and programming, including that focused on the equitable distribution of mental and emotional health resources in communities differentially burdened by climate impacts. We align with Ingle and Mikulewicz's [28] call for researchers to illuminate the emotional and mental health impacts of climate change among socially vulnerable communities, as a component of climate justice.
