Antifungal potential of silver and copper nanoparticles in suppressing Tilletia indica for Karnal bunt resistance in wheat
Authors: Muhammad Jabran, Muhammad Sohaib Shafique, Amjad Abbas, Weng Han, Muhammad Amjad Ali, Li Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractKarnal bunt disease, caused by the fungus Tilletia indica, poses a real threat to wheat grains and trade, emphasizing the need for effective management. We investigated the antifungal activity of silver (Ag) and copper (Cu) nanoparticles (NPs) against T. indica. The NPs were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), dynamic light scattering (DLS), and UV-vis spectroscopy, which confirmed uniformity in size distribution and well dispersion. TEM analysis presented spherical particles around 5–15 nm in size, while DLS measurements displayed average diameters of 12.1 nm for Ag-NPs and 8.7 nm for Cu-NPs. UV-vis spectroscopy examination confirmed the formation of nanoparticles, with plasmon bands at 440 nm for Ag and 405 nm for Cu. Both Ag and Cu-NPs exhibited dose-dependent inhibition effects, with Ag-NPs showing a higher inhibition rate than the others at all concentrations. In the minimum inhibitory concentration assessment, Ag- and Cu-treated samples at 400 µg/mL exhibited a highly significant suppression of mycelial growth compared to the control. The inhibition of radial growth (IRG%) assay showed a significant reduction in colony size at 100 µg/mL, with Ag and Cu-NPs inhibiting growth of 75% and 65%, respectively. We further analyzed spore germination assays, which showed that Ag-NPs achieved the most substantial inhibition at 200 µg/mL, with suppressing germination over 80% compared to a 70% reduction by Cu-NPs. Furthermore, nanoparticles significantly increased the expression of defense-related genes, with Ag-NPs causing a much stronger response compared with Cu-NPs. Specifically, PR1 and PR6 showed the most considerable fold change observed at 96 h, indicative of improved plant defense. Thus, these findings demonstrate that Ag and Cu-NPs are potent antifungal agents, with Ag-NPs emerging as a promising and effective strategy for controlling T. indica infections and inducing plant resistance in wheat plants.Keywords: Antifungal activity, Copper and silver nanoparticles, Gene expression, Karnal bunt of wheat, PR-proteins

Introduction

Karnal bunt (KB) disease is a devastating fungus that affects wheat worldwide, resulting in significant yield losses and a substantial economic impact on wheat production. This disease infects wheat kernels, lowers quality, and diminishes market value, thereby posing a considerable threat to global food security [1]. KB has been reported in developing countries, including India, South Africa, Australia, Iran, Iraq, Pakistan, Mexico, and the USA [2]. T. indica is a biotrophic fungal pathogen that produces teliospores that infect wheat grains, causing the characteristic “bunted” appearance and spread by teliospores in contact with wind, rain, or soil [3, 4]. Primary sporidia germinate from teliospores and give rise to secondary sporidia, which infect wheat floral parts, leading to mycelial colonization [5]. Resistance to Karnal bunt is complex due to the pathogen’s ability to persist in soil and the limited understanding of wheat’s defense mechanisms. Genetic resistance in wheat to T. indica has been challenging to achieve due to the scarcity of durable resistance genes and the pathogen’s ability to evolve rapidly [6]. Although efforts have been made to control KB, its recurrence in different wheat production areas highlights the need for effective management strategies. Chemical treatments are often not fully effective and raise concerns over sustainability in agriculture. Biological control and eco-friendly alternatives, such as nanoparticle-based treatments, are emerging as promising options to reduce dependence on harmful chemicals. Nanoparticles are projected to significantly address challenges against plant pathogens in crops in the future [7, 8]. For example, metal nanoparticles, particularly silver and copper [9], show promise in their antifungal properties, providing environmentally sustainable alternatives to chemical fungicides [10, 11]. Several studies on silver nanoparticles (Ag-NPs) have demonstrated their ability to inhibit fungi, including Penicillium citrinum, Aspergillus niger, Aspergillus flavus, Aspergillus fumigatus, Botrytis cinerea, Colletotrichum capsici, and Candida albicans [12–14]. Very recently, Ag-NPs were used to significantly suppress Pestalotiopsis psidii, a pathogen associated with guava stem cracking [15]. Similarly, copper nanoparticles (Cu-NPs) have been studied for plant disease management, including Fusarium spp., Phoma destructiva, Curvularia lunata, Alternaria alternata, Fusarium oxysporum, Penicillium italicum, Penicillium digitatum, and Rhizoctonia solani [16–18]. Additionally, metal oxide nanoparticles offer several benefits compared to their bulk counterparts. They are more stable in severe environmental circumstances, show antimicrobial activity at lower concentrations, and are non-toxic to humans [19].

Moreover, pathogenesis-related (PR) genes play a crucial role in plant defense mechanisms. PR1-PR6 genes have been reported to be expressed in response to pathogen attack, which facilitates the plant’s immune response through the production of antifungal proteins and enzymes. Studies have shown that nanoparticle treatment can activate the PR genes, leading to enhanced plant defense against fungal infections [20]. Ag and Cu-NPs can act on these PR genes, a finding supported by other studies [17, 21]. For example, the study reported that PR1, PR2, and other pathogenesis-related proteins were modulated in response to pathogen infection, as well as upon exposure to nanoparticle-treated wheat plants infected with Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici (the causal agent of stripe rust) [22]. The impacts of these nanoparticles on PR gene expression are mediated through direct and indirect pathways, such as the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) or activation of systemic acquired resistance (SAR) and induced systemic resistance (ISR) [23]. While these nanoparticles offer various advantages, their potential environmental impact must be thoroughly assessed. It is essential to optimize nanoparticle concentration and delivery methods, in conjunction with conducting long-term ecological assessments, to ensure their safe and effective integration into sustainable agricultural practices [14]. However, the rationale behind this study lies in the need to explore practical, eco-friendly alternatives to traditional chemical fungicides, particularly for managing T. indica. While the antifungal properties of Ag and Cu-NPs are well-documented, there is limited research on their effects on PR gene expression in wheat. This study aimed to evaluate the antifungal efficacy of silver and copper nanoparticles against T. indica in vitro and under greenhouse conditions, with a focus on the expression of the PR1-PR6 genes in wheat. By investigating the molecular response of wheat to nanoparticle treatment, this research aims to provide insight into how nanoparticles can enhance plant immunity and support green farming practices.

Materials and methods

Identification and purification of Tilletia indica

Bunted wheat grains were collected from the Department of Plant Pathology, Ayub Agricultural Research Institute, to isolate the pathogen. Following established protocols, teliospores were isolated from infected seeds [24, 25]. Approximately 20–30 wheat grains showing signs of infection were placed into a test tube filled with sterile water (10 ml) and vortexed for 2 min. The suspension was passed through a 60 μm sieve and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 min. The pellet was suspended in a 1% sodium hypochlorite solution and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 s. Sterilized distilled water was used to wash the spores through three rounds of centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 2 min, followed by the resuspension of the pellet. Washed spores were plated on water agar (20 g of agar dissolved in 1 L) using a 1000 µl micro-pipette. Plates were incubated at 20 ± 2 °C for 10 days. The teliospores sprouted, and the primary sporidia became visible on the surface of the water agar medium, resembling star- or thread-like structures. After this, secondary sporidia were produced on potato dextrose agar using the primary sporidia culture. Colonies were examined under a stereomicroscope and isolated on potato dextrose agar (PDA) for morphological identification of T. indica. For multiplication and inoculum preparation, a small disk from a fresh T. indica culture on water agar was placed on the upper edge of the PDA flasks. Flasks were incubated at 20 ± 2 °C for two weeks. This process involved taking approximately 100 mL of autoclaved PDA in a 250 mL flask and solidifying the media in a slant formation to produce colonies exhibiting a brittle-like appearance. Microscopic examination of slides prepared from the culture revealed the presence of numerous sickle-shaped secondary sporidia. The resulting cultures were stored at 4 °C for use in subsequent experiments assessing the antifungal activity.

Evaluation of copper and silver nanoparticles as antifungal agents against the growth of Tilletia indica

Characterization of copper and silver NPs

The Department of Microbiology at Government College University, Faisalabad, Pakistan, provided the copper and silver nanoparticles used in the current study. The technique for synthesizing Cu-NPs was performed by [26] and utilized a chemical reduction method. Copper sulfate (CuSO4) was employed as the precursor, and sodium borohydride (NaBH4) was the reducing agent. The reaction was conducted at room temperature, resulting in the reduction of Cu2+ ions by NaBH4 and leading to the formation of Cu-NPs. In the synthesis of Ag-NPs, the method established by [27] was used, which involved a chemical reduction procedure. Silver nitrate (AgNO3) was utilized as the precursor and sodium borohydride (NaBH4) as the reducing agent. NaBH4 reduced the Ag+ ions to form Ag nanoparticles. The nanoparticles were characterized using TEM, DLS, and UV–Visible spectrophotometry methods. All stock solutions of antifungal compounds were prepared (mg/mL) in sterilized distilled water and aseptically added to sterilized growth medium before inoculation. Before incorporation into growth media, suspensions of nanoparticles were sonicated for 30 min in a Transonic 420 sonicator (Elma, Germany).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The samples were prepared for TEM analysis by placing a drop of nanoparticle solution on a carbon-coated copper grid and leaving it to dry. A JEOL JEM-1400 microscope (Jeol LTD, Tokyo, Japan) was used to visualize NPs and determine their size. For the TEM samples, carbon-coated nickel grids were organized by ultrasonically dissolving the samples in ethanol, and the ensuing solution was then frozen [26]. After determining the size, TEM imaging revealed the nanoparticle’s size and shape, which can be spherical, rod-like, or irregular.

Dynamic light scattering analysis (DLS)

DLS analysis was performed to evaluate the dispersion size of the synthesized nanoparticles in the final solution after ultrasonic treatment. It provides polydispersity index (PDI) data, which indicates the breadth of the sample’s size distribution [28]. The synthesized nanoparticle samples were dissolved in deionized water, and ultrasonication was achieved to reduce aggregation and ensure uniform dispersal. The suspension was then filtered using Whatman™ 1 filter paper (GE Healthcare UK Limited) to eliminate large aggregates or contaminants. The filtered suspension was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to separate the nanoparticles from the solution. The supernatant containing the distributed nanoparticles was carefully collected and diluted 4–5 times with deionized water to obtain the applicable concentration for particle size analysis. The diluted supernatant was added to a computer-controlled particle size analyzer that utilized dynamic light scattering to quantify the hydrodynamic diameter of the nanoparticles in suspension. The software analyzed data information and generated a histogram and a distribution curve that represent the relative abundance of nanoparticles within the range. The received length distribution information was then analyzed to decide the mean particle length, polydispersity index, and other applicable parameters characterizing the nanoparticle sample.

UV–Visible spectrophotometry

UV-Vis spectroscopy was used to measure the UV-Vis absorption spectra of the nanoparticle solution. The depth of the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peak inside the spectra provided insights into the nanoparticle size, shape, and concentration [29]. Baseline correction of the spectrophotometer was executed with the use of a water blank reference. Two milliliters of the pattern were transferred directly into a cuvette. The pattern’s UV-Vis absorption spectra were recorded, and numerical information was collected. The synthesized silver and copper nanoparticles have been characterized using various techniques, including analysis and determination of the mean particle length. These characterizations have been performed to confirm the nanoparticles’ potential for transitioning from laboratory-scale to pilot-scale research, ensuring their realistic feasibility for combating fungal diseases in wheat.

Fungal growth inhibition (FGI) assay of Tilletia indica

In this study, infective cultures of T. indica were developed. Stock solutions of silver and copper nanoparticles were prepared by dissolving the nanoparticles in sterile distilled water to a concentration of 1 mg/mL. Serial dilutions of the stock solutions were performed to achieve final six concentrations of 12.5, 25, 50, 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL. These concentrations were selected based on preliminary studies for their effectiveness in inhibiting fungal growth.

Microtiter plates were prepared by filling the wells with the nanoparticle solutions, and purified T. indica cultures were added to each well. The working nanoparticle solutions were freshly prepared just before the assay to ensure their stability and biological activity. The plates were incubated at 24 °C for 1, 3, and 5 days, and fungal growth was measured using a spectrophotometer at 605 nm.

Additionally, the microbial inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined using the broth microdilution method, adapted from previous antifungal assays [30, 31]. A spore suspension of T. indica was prepared in potato dextrose broth (PDB), and the inoculum concentration was adjusted to 1 × 10⁶ spores/mL using a hemocytometer. Serial dilutions (100 µL) of each nanoparticle suspension were distributed into the wells of a 96-well microplate, and a standard growth medium was added to each well. Positive controls (with inoculum but without nanoparticles) and negative controls (without inoculum) were also included. The microplates were sealed and incubated at 24 °C for 48 to 96 h.

After incubation, optical density (OD) was measured at 605 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Tek, 800 | TS, Highland Park, Winooski, Vermont, USA) to monitor fungal growth [32]. The percentage growth inhibition at each nanoparticle concentration was calculated from the OD values. MIC values were determined based on dose-response curves and visual inspection of fungal growth. The inhibitory effects of Ag-NPs and Cu-NPs on T. indica were compared based on their MIC values, with results presented as the mean/range of MICs from three independent experiments performed in triplicate.

Assessment of antifungal activity of copper and silver NPs on mycelial radial growth

The antifungal effect of Ag and Cu-NPs was assessed against Tilletia indica. The pathogen was isolated and purified at the Plant Pathology Research Institute, AARI, Faisalabad, Pakistan. To determine the inhibition rate of mycelial growth, potato dextrose agar (PDA) was prepared and mixed with different concentrations of Ag and Cu-NPs, resulting in a final concentration of nanoparticles in the media of 25, 50, 75, and 100 µg/mL. A suspension of 1 × 106 CFU/mL cells of the fungal pathogen was placed in the middle of each petri dish containing fresh PDA solid medium with nanoparticles, after which the suspension plate was incubated at 24 ° C for 14 days. Control PDA plates were used without nanoparticles, and pathogenic fungi were grown under the same circumstances. Every treatment was done thrice. 14 days after incubation, data on the growth of fungal colonies were recorded, and the diameter of radial colony expansion was assessed. The fungal inhibition index was calculated based on the inhibition of radial growth percentage [26].

R1 is the growth of the radial dimension of the control (without nanoparticles), and R2 is the growth of the radial dimension of each treatment. Data on IRG were analyzed using the mean and standard deviation.

Antifungal properties of nanoparticles on spore germination of Tilletia indica

The inhibition rate of spore germination by Ag-NPs was calculated as described by [20], and the impact of Cu-NPs was examined as explained by [26]. Pure cultures were taken, and spores were isolated using sterile distilled water. The suspension was filtered to eliminate the mycelial remains, and the concentration of spores was attuned to the desired level at 1 × 10⁶ spores/mL. A hemocytometer was used to calculate the spores. To assess the antifungal effect, different concentrations of NPs (50, 100, 150, and 200 µg/mL) were prepared, along with negative controls (without NPs and PDA). Furthermore, a fixed amount of spore suspension was distributed on agar plates, and the respective NPs treatment was added to each plate. The plates were then incubated for 48 h at an optimal temperature of 24 °C. After the incubation, the slides were examined under the microscope (OLYMPUS BX51, Olympus Corporation, Japan), and the number of germinated and non-germinated spores in each treatment was counted. The pathogen abnormalities in structure and size were evaluated, and the spores were quantified in percentage. The spore germination percentage was judged using the method described by [33].

The experiment was performed two times with three replications to confirm the consistency and reproducibility of the results. Finally, the inhibiting rate of spore germination was compared to checking the efficacy of different NPs concentrations [34].

Expression analysis of PR genes in nanoparticle-treated wheat under Tilletia indica infection in greenhouse conditions

The impact of Ag and Cu-NPs on the expression of pathogen-related proteins (PR1, PR2, PR3, PR4, PR5, and PR6) in wheat plants was assessed under greenhouse conditions against Tilletia indica. Susceptible seeds of variety WL-711 to Karnal bunt disease were sterilized with 70% ethanol for two minutes, followed by three rinses with distilled water. They were divided into two groups: a control (untreated and uninfected) and a nanoparticle-treated infected group (seeds coated with silver or copper nanoparticles before sowing, followed by T. indica inoculation at the booting stage). Each group was performed with three replications, with ten plants per replicate. Seedlings were grown in pots containing a sterile mixture of soil and vermiculite (2:1) under greenhouse conditions. The conditions were maintained at a temperature of 18–24 °C and 80% relative humidity, with lighting conditions adjusted to provide 16 h of light and an 8-hour dark period. This condition is optimal for growth and pathogen development, as previously established in a study by [35].

In addition, Ag and Cu-NPs were adjusted to a concentration of 400 µg/mL, which had previously demonstrated effective anti-fungal activity in vitro tests against T. indica in the current study. Then, NPs were sonicated for 30 min, added to Petri plates with distilled water for seed treatment, and soaked overnight before sowing. The artificial inoculation of T. indica was carried out at the booting stage of wheat (~ 45–50 days post-emergence, DPA), a suitable time for T. indica infection. The inoculum for injection was prepared and suspended in sterile water to attain a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ spores/mL. Each plant was inoculated with 5 ml of the spore suspension directly into the boot. This process was adapted from earlier studies that emphasized the importance of seed treatment and has been validated as an effective method for assessing susceptibility to wheat cultivars to improve plant resistance to pathogens [36]. After inoculation, the plants were checked for disease symptoms. Additionally, samples were collected routinely from both infected and uninfected plants for gene expression studies.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed to examine the expression levels of pathogenesis-related genes and genes activated in plant defense responses to nanoparticles [37]. For this purpose, leaf samples were collected at different time points after inoculation for qRT-PCR, specifically at 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 96 h for gene expression analysis. The samples were then frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C. RNA was isolated from 100 mg of leaf samples by Qiagen RNeasy Plant Mini Kit and quantified with a Nanodrop spectrophotometer, followed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis [35]. This total RNA was used to make cDNA through a reverse transcription kit (reverse transcriptase PCR kit). Expression of defense-related genes, including PR1, PR2, PR3, PR4, PR5, and PR6, was analyzed by qRT-PCR using gene-specific primers obtained from the NCBI database and the housekeeping gene (actin). Reactions were performed using SYBR Green Master Mix in a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR system, following thermal conditions of 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^ΔΔCt method [38], with untreated plants serving as the calibrator. The primer sequences used in the analysis are listed in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

The experiments were conducted following a completely randomized design. The antifungal test was performed three times, and the results were presented as mean ± standard error (SE). Statistical analysis was done using Prism software (version 9.1.3, GraphPad Software). Significance was determined using ANOVA, and to compare the treated group with the controls (without Ag and Cu NPs), Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) test was applied [9].

Results

Characteristic bunted symptoms were observed in infected wheat grains after harvest, with kernels appearing blackened and filled with teliospores of Tilletia indica. When cultured on water agar, the teliospores germinated to produce star-shaped or thread-like primary sporidia. Subsequent culturing led to the formation of abundant sickle-shaped secondary sporidia, the main infective propagules of the pathogen. Microscopic examination revealed the reticulate ornamentation and thick walls of the teliospores. Both filiform and allantoid types of secondary sporidia were also observed. These morphological features are consistent with previously reported characteristics of T. indica (Fig. 1).

Efficacy of nanoparticles against Tilletia indica

The results showed that a dose-dependent reduction in growth was observed in most fungal strains treated with nanoparticles (NPs) at varying concentrations. A stock solution of both nanoparticles at a concentration of 1000 µg/mL was initially prepared and subsequently incorporated into growth media at various initial concentrations ranging from 12.5 to 100 µg/mL. All solutions were freshly prepared and used for the experiment. Applying both copper and silver nanoparticles significantly controlled the in vitro growth of T. indica. The findings demonstrate the impact of varying nanoparticle concentrations on the MIC for T. indica over five days. The MIC was approximately 400 µg/mL on day 5, at which point a significant inhibition of fungal growth was observed.

Characterization of silver and copper nanoparticles

TEM analysis revealed that the synthesized nanoparticles were uniformly shaped and well-separated, with no observable agglomeration. Silver and copper nanoparticles appeared predominantly spherical and displayed faceted particles ranging from 5 to 15 nm (Fig. 2A, D). DLS analysis provided size distribution profiles, polydispersity, and insights into potential aggregation. Silver nanoparticle characterization revealed a mean diameter of 12.1 nm and moderate dispersion (PDI = 0.8) (Fig. 2B). Similarly, copper nanoparticles showed a mean diameter of 8.7 nm with a polydisperse nature (PDI = 0.18) (Fig. 2E). UV-visible spectroscopy analysis confirmed nanoparticle formation with the absorbance spectrum, with silver nanoparticles exhibiting a sharp plasmon band at 440 nm, indicating of smaller particle sizes (Fig. 2C). Copper nanoparticles displayed peak absorbance at 405 nm, highlighting their efficient light absorption (Fig. 2F). The specific wavelength indicates where the nanoparticles absorb light most efficiently.

Inhibitory effects of nanoparticles via broth microdilution assay

The effect of silver and copper nanoparticles on the mycelial growth of Tilletia indica was quantified by measuring optical density at 605 nm over a 5-day incubation period. The findings present the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values for nanoparticles determined through a broth microdilution assay. The MIC, the lowest concentration inhibiting visible fungal growth, was assessed through serial dilutions of NPs in a 96-well microtiter plate inoculated with the pathogen. Six Cu and silver NPs concentrations were tested (12.5, 25, 50, 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL), with maximum inhibition observed at 400 µg/mL.

In Ag-NP-treated samples, fungal growth was substantially suppressed at concentrations ≥ 100 µg/mL, with a statistically significant reduction (p < 0.05) observed at 200 µg/mL and highly significant inhibition (p < 0.01) at 400 µg/mL compared to the control. On Day 3, the absorbance values dropped sharply from ~ 2.0 (control) to < 0.5 at concentrations ≥ 100 µg/mL, confirming time-dependent efficacy (Fig. 3). Similarly, Cu-NPs also demonstrated strong inhibitory activity, with a noticeable decrease in fungal growth starting from 25 µg/mL. On Day 5, 400 µg/mL Cu-NPs resulted in a highly significant (p < 0.01) reduction in absorbance compared to the control, while 200 µg/mL exhibited significant inhibition (p < 0.05). However, the inhibition levels were slightly lower than those seen in Ag-NPs at corresponding concentrations, particularly at early time points (Fig. 3). The ANOVA results support these findings. For Ag and Cu-NPs, both incubation days (and concentrations had a significant effect on fungal growth, with a significant interaction between time and concentration (Table 3). However, the interaction effect is not statistically significant (p = 0.20). These results indicate that both nanoparticles effectively reduce fungal biomass in a dose- and time-dependent manner, with Ag-NPs showing slightly higher antifungal potency compared to Cu-NPs at equivalent doses.

Inhibition of radial growth (IRG%) by nanoparticles against Tilletia indica

The inhibition of radial growth (IRG%) of Tilletia indica by silver and copper nanoparticles was evaluated at various concentrations (25, 50, 75, and 100 µg/mL). Both Ag-NPs and Cu-NPs exhibited dose-dependent inhibition of mycelial growth, with higher nanoparticle concentrations leading to greater IRG% (Fig. 4). At the highest concentration of 100 µg/mL, both Ag and Cu-NPs showed over 75% inhibition of fungal growth. Statistically significant differences were observed between all treatments and the control group (0 µg/mL), with Ag-NPs consistently demonstrating higher IRG compared to Cu-NPs at all concentrations. At 25 µg/mL, Ag-NPs induced a significant IRG of 14%, while Cu-NPs had a lower IRG of 11.67%. At 50 µg/mL, both nanoparticles demonstrated moderate inhibition, with Ag-NPs showing 32% inhibition and Cu-NPs showing 35.33%. At 75 µg/mL, Ag-NPs inhibited 60% of radial growth, while Cu-NPs exhibited 40% inhibition. The highest concentration of 100 µg/mL resulted in 75.33% inhibition for Ag-NPs and 65% inhibition for Cu-NPs. However, concentrations of 75 and 100 µg/mL resulted in a significant reduction in colony size, with 75 µg/mL achieving the desired level of fungal inhibition (Table 4).

Spore germination inhibition assay

The spore germination inhibition assay demonstrated that both silver and copper nanoparticles significantly inhibited spore germination of Tilletia indica in a dose-dependent manner. Manual spore germination counts using a hemocytometer revealed abnormal spore development under nanoparticle treatments, with many spores failing to germinate. This suggests that nanoparticles interfere with the spore germination process, thus limiting fungal growth. Evaluation of T. indica spore germination after 48 h of incubation with varying concentrations of silver and copper nanoparticles showed significant inhibition by both treatments (Fig. 5). At the highest concentration of 200 µg/mL, Ag-NPs achieved the most substantial inhibition, with over 80% of spores showing inhibition, which was significantly higher than that of the control and lower concentrations (p < 0.01). At 150 µg/mL, Ag-NPs inhibited approximately 60% spore germination, with significant differences compared to the control (p < 0.05). At concentrations of 100 µg/mL and 50 µg/mL, Ag-NPs showed moderate inhibition, approximately 40% and 25%, respectively, with statistically significant differences observed at all concentrations. For Cu-NPs, the inhibition of spore germination was also important but less pronounced compared to Ag-NPs. At 200 µg/mL, Cu-NPs inhibited spore germination by around 70%, significantly higher than the control (p < 0.01). At 150 µg/mL, inhibition reached 50%, and at 100 µg/mL, it was approximately 30%. The lowest concentration of 50 µg/mL resulted in about 20% inhibition. However, both nanoparticles exhibited significant dose-dependent inhibition of spore germination, with Ag-NPs proving to be a more effective candidate for controlling T. indica spore germination.

Expression profiles of defense-related PR genes in wheat following Ag and Cu-nanoparticle treatments

The relative expression of six pathogen-related (PR) genes (PR1–PR6) in wheat was compared with control (untreated) and nanoparticle-treated infected plants (Ag and Cu-NPs at 400 µg/mL), using Actin as the normalized housekeeping gene. The results show that both Ag and Cu-NPs induced a significant increase in PR gene expression, with Ag-NPs generally having a more substantial effect across all time points. Ag-NPs induced a marked increase in PR1 expression, with the highest fold expression observed at 96 h (~ 12-fold). At 0 h, there was no significant expression in Ag-NP-treated plants, but the expression steadily increased over time, reaching a significant peak at 96 h. In contrast, Cu-NPs also showed upregulation in PR1 expression, but to a lesser extent (~ 5-fold at 96 h), with statistically significant differences between treatments observed at 72 h and 96 h (Fig. 6). Like PR1, PR2 showed significant upregulation in response to both Ag and Cu-NPs, with Ag-NPs producing the highest fold change (~ 6-fold at 96 h). The expression was increased progressively at 24 h and 48 h, and peaked at 96 h, with significant differences compared to the control. Cu-NPs also induced PR2 expression (~ 3.5-fold at 96 h). Statistically significant differences were observed between 72 h and 96 h. The fold change for PR3 expression increased substantially at 72 h and 96 h in Ag-NP-treated plants (~ 6-fold), indicating a strong nanoparticle effect. Cu-NPs also induced upregulation in PR3 (~ 4-fold at 96 h), but the fold change was lower than that of Ag-NPs. Ag-NPs produced the highest PR4 expression (~ 8-fold at 96 h), with significant differences from the control and Cu-NPs at 72 h and 96 h. Cu-NPs also induced an increase in PR4 expression (~ 5-fold at 96 h). In addition, PR5 expression was significantly higher in Ag-NP-treated plants (~ 7-fold at 96 h), with a steady increase from 0 h to 96 h. Statistically significant differences were observed between Ag and Cu treatments at 72 h and 96 h. Ag-NPs induced the highest expression of PR6 (~ 12-fold at 96 h), followed by Cu-NPs (~ 7-fold at 96 h). Both nanoparticles showed significant upregulation of PR6, with the most substantial fold change observed at 96 h. The strong upregulation of PR genes in response to nanoparticle treatments highlights the role of these nanoparticles in enhancing plant defense mechanisms. The expression of PR genes is known to be activated by biotic stresses such as Karnal bunt infection (Table 5), and the nanoparticle-induced expression suggests that Ag and Cu-NPs may act as elicitors of the plant’s immune system.

Discussion

Nanoparticles can be used as antifungal agents and have been reported to enhance natural defense responses in plants against pathogens [17]. The current study highlights the potential of silver and copper nanoparticles as effective agents for controlling T. indica growth and enhancing wheat plant defense mechanisms. Chemically synthesized Ag and Cu-NPs were obtained and characterized by transmission electron microscopy, dynamic light scattering analysis, and UV-Vis spectroscopy, as reported by [39]. TEM and DLS analysis verified the uniform size of the synthesized nanoparticles and the fine dispersion. At the same time, UV-Vis spectroscopy revealed well-formed nanoparticle absorption peaks at 405 nm and 440 nm, respectively, indicating the strong synthesis of nanoparticles against plant pathogens [40], as described in previous studies [41]. The smaller sizes reported by DLS are due to the measurement of the hydrodynamic diameter, which includes the nanoparticle surface and the surrounding solvation layer. However, the TEM images reveal the actual particle size, whereas DLS measures the size in solution.

Both Ag and Cu-NPs demonstrated dose-dependent antifungal activity, with higher concentrations resulting in more significant inhibition of mycelial growth and spore germination. For example, the use of Ag-NPs as antimicrobial agents has been shown to adversely affect the growth of pathogenic fungi and their conidial development [11]. The application of Ag-NPs efficiently inhibited at 75 μg/mL mycelial growth and spore germination of four kiwifruit rot pathogen [20, 42]. Additionally [26], synthesized Cu-NPs in an aqueous solution and investigated the fungicidal potential against different pathogens, including Fusarium solani, Neofusicoccum sp., and Fusarium oxysporum, which showed significant inhibition of soilborne pathogen growth. Conversely, Ag-NPs showed consistently higher IRG than Cu-NPs throughout the test, according to our results.

The inhibition of radial growth (IRG%) assay showed that at 100 µg/mL, both Ag and Cu-NPs inhibited T. indica growth by over 75%, with Ag-NPs consistently demonstrating higher efficacy across all concentrations. These findings are consistent with recent studies, in which nanoparticles, particularly Ag-NPs, have been reported to effectively control fungal growth [14]. The dose-dependent effect observed in this study suggests that nanoparticles interact with fungal cells, possibly disrupting cell membranes or interfering with enzyme function, as reported earlier [43]. Ag-NPs were more effective at inhibiting growth, likely due to their smaller size and higher surface area, which enhances their interaction with fungal cells [44].

The spore germination inhibition assay further confirmed the antifungal potential of Ag and Cu-NPs, with Ag-NPs showing the most substantial inhibition, particularly at 200 µg/mL, where more than 80% of spores were inhibited. Cu-NPs also significantly inhibited spore germination but to a lesser extent (~ 70% at 200 µg/mL). These results agree with the findings of [9], who observed significant inhibition of spore germination by metal nanoparticles, including Ag and Cu. The higher efficacy of Ag-NPs in inhibiting spore germination suggests a more potent antifungal activity, which may be attributed to the enhanced reactivity and smaller size of Ag-NPs compared to Cu-NPs.

In addition to their direct antifungal activity, both Ag and Cu-NPs were found to enhance the expression of defense-related (PR) genes in wheat plants. Ag-NPs induced a stronger response than Cu-NPs, particularly at 72 h and 96 h, where significant upregulation of PR1, PR2, PR3, PR4, PR5, and PR6 was observed. The highest fold change in gene expression was observed for PR1 and PR6, with Ag-NPs inducing approximately 12-fold increases at 96 h. This suggests that Ag-NPs may stimulate more robust and immediate defense responses compared to Cu-NPs. The upregulation of these PR genes is consistent with the enhanced resistance observed in other studies using metal nanoparticles, where PR gene expression was linked to the activation of plant defense mechanisms [45]. For example, Zn-NPs have been found to up-regulate genes involved in the reaction to oxidative stress, which is essential for pathogen defense [46]. This suggests that Ag-NPs can stimulate a more attentive state in plants, making them better able to resist pathogen attacks. Furthermore, the differential expression of PR genes in response to nanoparticle treatments may vary depending on the pathogen type and the specific PR genes involved. For example, in wheat, PR1, PR2, PR4, PR5, and PR8 genes were significantly upregulated at the initial stage of infection by Blumeria graminis f.sp. tritici (Bgt), the causal agent of powdery mildew in wheat [47]. This upregulation is a component of the defense mechanism in plants, which is essential in the early phase of pathogen resistance. For example, PR1 is related to systemic immunity and shows resistance against plant pathogens. Hence, the noticeable upregulation of PR1 in Ag-NP-treated plants at 96 h is consistent with earlier results, which display that Ag-NPs can activate early and robust immune responses in plants [48]. In contrast, Cu-NPs induced a slower and less intense expression of PR1, particularly at 72 h, suggesting that Cu-NPs may trigger defense mechanisms more gradually. This difference in the temporal response among Ag and Cu-NPs could be attributed to their differing bioactivity, with Ag-NPs likely inducing a more instant and widespread activation of plant defense genes [49]. Furthermore, the time-course fold increase in PR gene expression reflects that the nanoparticle treatments triggered a temporal and dynamic defense response. The larger fold changes recorded at 72 h and 96 h indicate that both Ag and Cu-NPs exhibit immediate antifungal action, contributing to long-term resistance defenses [40]. Hence, the results indicate that Ag-NPs were more effective than Cu-NPs in inhibiting fungal growth, reducing the spore germination rate, and enhancing the defense response through PR gene expression. The concentration used in this study falls within a safe and environmentally friendly range, demonstrating its potential for use under any field conditions. These outcomes suggest that Ag-NPs have the potential to be a viable alternative to conventional chemical fungicides, serving as a sustainable and environmentally friendly control measure for Karnal bunt disease. Future research will investigate the specific mechanisms by which nanoparticles interact with fungal cells, and understanding the plant defense mechanisms will be crucial for enhancing their utility in agricultural applications.

Conclusion

The findings on silver and copper nanoparticles showed significant antifungal potential in suppressing Tilletia indica growth, with Ag-NPs being more effective in inhibiting fungal growth, spore germination, and enhancing plant immunity. The dose-dependent inhibition of radial growth rate and spore germination showed that the nanoparticles were able to inhibit T. indica growth at both doses of 100 µg/ml and 200 µg/ml. Additionally, the results indicate that the induction of defense gene expression (PR1 and PR6) in the Ag and Cu-NPs groups compared to the control groups suggests that both Ag and Cu-NPs activated the defense genes. Our study suggests that Ag and Cu-NPs can likely be used as non-toxic alternatives to chemical fungicides for managing Karnal bunt disease on wheat. However, further work is still required to clarify the exact modalities of the nanoparticles, enabling their more effective use in the field for environmentally safe crop protection.
