Bistable Perception Discriminates Between Depressive Patients, Controls, Schizophrenia Patients, and Their Siblings
Authors: Elahe Arani, Simona Garobbio, Maya Roinishvili, Eka Chkonia, Michael H Herzog, Richard J A van Wezel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and HypothesisIndividuals with schizophrenia have less priors than controls, meaning they rely less upon their prior experiences to interpret the current stimuli. These differences in priors are expected to show as higher alternation rates in bistable perception tasks like the Structure-from-Motion (SfM) paradigm. In this paradigm, continuously moving dots in two dimensions are perceived subjectively as traveling along a three-dimensional sphere, which results in a direction of motion (left or right) that shifts approximately every few seconds.Study DesignHere, we tested healthy controls, patients with schizophrenia, siblings of patients with schizophrenia, and patients with depression with both the intermittent and continuous variants of the SfM paradigm.Study ResultsIn the intermittent variant of the SfM paradigm, depressive patients exhibited the lowest alternation rate, followed by unaffected controls. In contrast, patients with schizophrenia and their unaffected siblings displayed significantly higher alternation rates. In the continuous variant of the SfM paradigm, patients with schizophrenia showed the lowest mean percept durations, while there were no differences between the other three groups.ConclusionsThe intermittent SfM paradigm is a candidate endophenotype for schizophrenia. The aberrant processing in the patients may stem from alterations in adaptation and/or cross-inhibition mechanisms leading to changes in priors, as suggested by current models in the field. The intermittent SfM paradigm is, hence, a trait marker that offers the great opportunity to investigate perceptual abnormalities across the psychiatry spectrum, ranging from depression to psychosis.Keywords: visual perception, ambiguity, schizophrenia, endophenotype, phenomenology

Introduction

Schizophrenia is strongly influenced by genetic factors, and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified numerous single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with the disorder.1 However, the contribution of each individual SNP to schizophrenia is relatively small, highlighting the complex nature of the disorder.2,3 This emphasizes the importance of endophenotypes, or traits rather than state markers, which provide a more direct window into the underlying genetic predisposition.4 Deficits in endophenotypes may represent the cumulative impact of genetic abnormalities. Endophenotypes must meet specific criteria.5,6 For example, unaffected first-degree relatives of patients should display abnormalities compared to healthy controls. Additionally, tests should exhibit a high signal-to-noise ratio, be readily administered, and be sensitive to at least the psychosis spectrum. However, only a limited number of endophenotypes with these characteristics have been identified (but see Iacono et al., 20147). A particular challenge for endophenotypes is to avoid floor or ceiling effects for all the populations involved. This means that test must have a large range of performance values. Here, we show that a specific motion perception paradigm shows the characteristics of an endophenotype and allow to differentiate between patients, their relatives, healthy controls, and depressive patients.

In the Structure-from-Motion (SfM) paradigm, random dots displayed on a 2D computer screen appear to move left or right in a sinusoidal pattern.8 The human brain integrates these 2D movements into a perceived 3D rotating sphere that moves in one direction. When the SfM stimulus is presented continuously, the perceived rotation direction is bistable, shifting approximately every 2-3 seconds. When the SfM stimulus is presented intermittently, with intervals between the dot movements, the perceived rotation direction alternates at shorter intervals (<1 sec) and stabilizes at longer intervals.9,10

Previous studies have tested these bistable paradigms on patients with schizophrenia with mixed results: some studies have found a reduced number of perceptual switches in the patients compared to healthy controls,11 while others have reported an increased number of switches.12 More recent studies utilizing the SfM paradigm with intermittent dots motion presentation have demonstrated significantly higher alternation rates in patients with schizophrenia compared to healthy controls.11,13 Additionally, it has been shown that perceptual stability exhibits a negative correlation with delusional ideation.13

Here, we measured percept switches to the intermittent and continuous variants of the SfM paradigm. While the percept-switch variant assesses the frequency of perceptual switches during intermittent presentation, the continuous variant measures the stability of the perceived motion direction over time. By incorporating both variants, we gain a more comprehensive understanding of perceptual stability across different timescales. To preface our results, we found that the intermittent SfM paradigm can effectively differentiate between patients with schizophrenia, their unaffected siblings, healthy controls, and depressive patients. Both patients with schizophrenia and their unaffected siblings exhibited significantly higher alternation rates compared to healthy controls. Conversely, depressive patients demonstrated significantly lower alternation rates compared to healthy controls. These findings underscore the potential of the intermittent SfM paradigm as a trait marker for schizophrenia.

Participants

These participants consisted of 45 patients with schizophrenia (SCZ), 23 first-degree relatives of the schizophrenia patients (REL), 26 depressive patients (DEP), and 51 healthy controls (CTR). Participants showing no button presses were excluded, which was the case for 10 participants in the continuous SfM and 23 participants in the intermittent SfM.

Patients were recruited from either the Tbilisi Mental Health Hospital or the Acute Psychiatric Departments of Multi-Profile Clinics. General exclusion criteria included drug or alcohol abuse, as well as neurological or other somatic illnesses that could affect participants’ mental states. Patients were diagnosed according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV) through structured clinical interviews. Out of the 45 schizophrenia patients, 29 had paranoid, 2 had residual, 2 had disorganized, and 12 had undifferentiated type of schizophrenia. All depressive patients have diagnoses of major depressive disorder (MDD). More specifically, out of the 26 patients with MDD, 16 had moderate, 6 had mild, and 4 had severe MDD. Psychopathology was assessed by an experienced psychiatrist (EC) using the Scales for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms and Scales for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms (SANS,15 SAPS16), the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS17), and the 17-items Hamilton scale.18 Chlorpromazine equivalents were recorded. Relatives participated with the consent of the patients. Ten siblings of included patients participated in the study. All relatives and controls were free from axis I psychiatric disorders and completed the Schizotypal Personality Questionnaire-Brief (SPQ).19 Age-matched healthy controls were recruited from the general population. A family history of psychosis was an exclusion criterion for the control group. Group characteristics are presented in Table 1. All participants had normal (or corrected to normal) vision. Participants provided informed consent, and the experiments adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki (except for pre-registration) and were approved by the local ethics committee.

Stimuli and Apparatus

The stimuli were presented on a 24-inch LCD (ASUS VG248QE) with a screen resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels and a refresh rate of 120 Hz. An ambiguously rotating SfM sphere was presented, comprising white dots moving either to the left or right on a black background, following a sinusoidal speed profile (Figure 1A). Each dot measured 2.8 arcmins and moved with a peak angular speed of 60 °C/second. The dot density was set at 40 dots per square visual degree, with the luminance of the white dots at 21.7 cd/m2 and the background luminance at 0.13 cd/m2. The overall stimulus size was 2.4 °C. The dots had an infinite lifetime, but at the beginning of each stimulus presentation, they were randomly positioned to prevent tracking individual dots across trials. This display induced a vivid impression of a three-dimensional rotating sphere.8,20–22

Participants viewed the stimuli passively from a distance of 100 cm in a completely dark room. A chin rest was used to maintain a constant distance from the screen. Observers were instructed to fixate on a red dot (4.2 × 4.2 arcmins) at the center of the screen and report the perceived motion direction by pressing the left or right arrow on the keyboard. The experiments were programmed in MATLAB (The MathWorks Inc. 2014b) using PsychToolbox_3.23

Experimental Protocol

The experiment included both the percept-choice paradigm with intermittent stimulus presentation (Figure 1B) and the classic percept-switch paradigm with continuous presentation (Figure 1C). Task instructions for each paradigm were displayed on the screen before its initiation.

In the percept-choice paradigm, participants were instructed to report their percept after each stimulus onset (Figure 1B). Given the minimal impact of stimulus duration (Ton) on alternation probabilities, a fixed one-second Ton was used to prevent spontaneous switches during Ton.9 Interstimulus duration (Toff) of 250, 500, and 1000 milliseconds were used, as previous studies9,24 demonstrated changes in alternation rates within this range, dropping towards zero at interstimulus durations exceeding 1 second.

In the percept-switch paradigm, participants reported their perceived percept at the beginning of stimulus presentation and whenever the perceptual switch occurred (Figure 1C).

Each of the four conditions (intermittent presentation with Toff of 250, 500, and 1000 ms, and continuous presentation) was presented for two minutes, with the order randomized for each participant. Participants were instructed to always report the most dominant percept. In the case of perceptual uncertainty, participants were instructed to randomly choose between left or right.

Data Analysis

In the percept-choice paradigm (Figure 1B), alternations were defined as two subsequent stimulus presentations with different responses, and the alternation probability was calculated as the ratio of the total number of alternations to the total number of responses. In cases where multiple key-presses were recorded, only the first response was considered, and subsequent responses (<2%) were excluded. Trials where participants failed to respond, along with their preceding and subsequent trials, were also excluded (approximately 2.5% trails were excluded in total for all participants). Reaction times were calculated from the onset of each trial in the percept-choice paradigm.

In the percept-switch paradigm (Figure 1C), the average percept duration was computed. A key press identical to the previous one was considered a continuation of the percept. The last registered key response was disregarded as it was truncated at the end of the block and contained no reliable information about the duration of the percept. Additionally, the cumulative distribution function (CDF) was calculated to determine the probability of a percept duration being lower than a certain value. We have previously shown that changes in inhibition strength change the convergence of CDF to 1,25 with a faster rise to a value of 1 for strong inhibition. It is calculated by sorting the percept durations in ascending order, dividing it into intervals (bins) and counting how many percept durations fall into each interval. The cumulative frequency is calculated by summing the subsequent frequencies of all intervals and normalizing to the largest value. Cumulative percept duration histograms for the four groups were also analyzed. Following the approach discussed by Arani et al. (2018),25 changes in inhibition strength were considered by dividing the data into 10-second window sizes, and the number of switches within each window for each participant was calculated. This shows how the dynamics of percept-switch change over time. Statistics

For the percept-choice paradigm, we conducted a 3 (Toff) × 4 (group) repeated-measure ANOVA (rm-ANOVA). Subsequently, to investigate the group effect on alternation probabilities, six (i.e., 3 Toff x 2 group) rm-ANOVA were performed, and P-values were corrected with the Bonferroni-Holm (p-BHcorrected) method for multiple comparisons.28 Additionally, a 3 (Toff) × 4 (group) rm-ANOVA was conducted to explore the effect of group on reaction times.

For the percept-switch paradigm, an ANOVA was initially conducted to test any group effect on the mean percept duration. Following this, to test the statistical differences in the CDF of percept duration and the fitted Gamma distributions between the four groups, a two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was conducted. This test provides a decision on the null hypothesis that percept duration data of groups A and B come from the same continuous distribution, while the alternative hypothesis suggesting that CDF or Gamma distributions of percept duration data of groups A and B are from different continuous distributions (at a 5% significance level). Finally, a 12 (bins) × 4 (groups) rm-ANOVA was conducted to investigate the dynamics of percept-switch change over time.

Finally, for patients with schizophrenia, we have computed Spearman’s correlations between performances in the bistable stimuli (mean percept duration and alternation rates) and patients’ symptoms characteristics (CPZ, SANS, SAPS, and Illness duration).

Percept-Choice Results

The alternation rate decreases with longer Toff (F(2365) = 11.47, η2 = 0.06, P < .05) and is consistent across all four groups (i.e., no significant interaction Toff*group (F(6365) = 0.34, η2 = 0.00, P = .92). The rm-ANOVA showed a significant effect of group (F(3365) = 7.01, η2 = 0.05, P < .05). Thus, we conducted pairwise rm-ANOVA tests to explore group differences. No significant difference was found between SCZ and REL (F(1176) = 0.13, η2 = 0.00, p-BHcorrected = 0.72) nor between DEP and CTR (F(1188) = 2.6, η2 = 0.01, p-BHcorrected = 0.22). Additionally, a significant difference was observed between REL and DEP (F(1119) = 11.02, η2 = 0.08, p-BHcorrected = 6e-3). The comparison between CTR and REL was significant only before correcting for multiple comparisons (F(1200) = 5.7, η2 = 0.03, p-BHcorrected = 0.06), which may be attributed to a lack of power due to the smaller number of REL compared to SCZ and CTR.

Discussion

Schizophrenia has a high heritability. More than 100 genes were identified contributing to schizophrenia, each explaining, however, only a small amount of the variance. Endophenotypes try to capture the susceptibility for schizophrenia on nongenetic levels. One major problem of a behavioral endophenotype is to clearly distinguish between the different populations, e.g., patients, their siblings, unaffected controls, and clinical controls from, such as depressive patients. Often tests cannot host so many populations since the noise is high in each population and performance range is low.

Here, we tested for phenomenological distortions of motion with two variants of the SfM paradigm: percept-choice (i.e., intermittent dots motion) and percept-switch (i.e., continuous dots motion). As in previous studies, we found that the alternation rates of patients with schizophrenia are significantly higher than in healthy controls for both paradigms.11,13 In addition, our results show that the percept-choice paradigm is a candidate trait marker as unaffected siblings of patients with schizophrenia also exhibit motion distortions, performance does not depend on symptoms’ severity, and it is specific to schizophrenia as depressive patients perform similarly to healthy controls.

The percept-choice and the percept-switch paradigms are different.24,29–32 On one side, the percept-switch paradigm tests how long it takes for a spontaneous reversal to occur, expressed as the mean percept duration. This mechanism is thought to be based on an interplay between adaptation, noise, attention and possible memory signals. The most dominant model is that percept switches are thought to occur due to a gradual accumulation of neural adaptation in the neurons representing the dominant percept. This adaptation causes the dominant percept to become less stable until any small noise signal can trigger a switch to the opposite percept. On the other side, the percept-choice paradigm is used to study percept alternation in a more controlled manner, because lapses can be determined as participants need to indicate their percept each time the stimulus is presented. In the continuous paradigm, there is no such control for lapses, which are omissions to respond, because the changes of percept are entirely subjective. In the percept-choice paradigm, participants are presented with an ambiguous stimulus for a short period of time and then asked to report which interpretation they saw. This allows to measure the frequency and timing of percept alternations under different conditions. Studies have shown that percept alternation are more likely to occur when stimuli are presented for shorter durations, when attention is directed to certain parts of the stimulus, and when the stimulus is primed with stimuli that favor one interpretation over the other. Percept choices are thought to be driven by a decision-making process that takes into account both the current activation of the two percepts and the overall neural baseline activity.24 This baseline activity is thought to bias the decision towards one percept or the other.

In our study, patients with schizophrenia had a significantly higher number of percept-choice switches compared to controls in both paradigms. Importantly, unaffected siblings of patients with schizophrenia performed similarly to patients in the percept-choice paradigm but like controls (and significantly different from patients) in the percept-switch paradigm. These results support mechanistic models showing that results from continuous and intermittent paradigms are not correlated.24 One possible explanation is that the intermittent paradigm is more affected by serial choice biases, which are known to be reduced in psychosis-prone individuals and schizophrenia.33–35 Since the siblings were unmedicated, it may be well that also the abnormal percepts in the schizophrenia patients in this paradigm do not come from medication but come from the same traits as in the siblings.

In our experiments, we did not record eye movements; thus, we cannot exclude the possibility that the observed differences are (partly) due to different eye movements or blink patterns in the different groups.36,37 However, we instructed participants to fixate on a dot on the screen. In addition, in previous studies, under very similar conditions, it has been shown that eye movements do not play a crucial role for the alternations of awareness during bistable perception.38,39

Perception, in particular of weak and ambiguous stimuli, does not depend on the stimulus only but also on the expectation we have about it. For example, in the case of ambiguous stimuli, we may see the variant that is expected to occur more often than the less expected variant. These aspects are usually well modeled by Bayesian, predictive coding approaches.40 It has been proposed that schizophrenia patients have altered error-dependent updating in a hierarchical Bayesian structure, leading to disruptions in predictive coding that require the system to change inferences more often.41 This was suggested to explain hallucinations and delusions because of a continued sense that the world is not well predicted or modeled by previous beliefs.42 Similarly, it has also been argued that patients have a “failure to attenuate sensory precision,” which means they cannot call upon their prior experiences to interpret the current stimuli.43 In line with that literature, we find that both patients with schizophrenia and their unaffected siblings display significantly higher alternation rates in the intermittent paradigm. The fact that we do not show higher alternation rates in the relatives for the continuous stimulation condition suggests that those alternation rates are not related to changes in priors of previous percepts.

The SfM paradigm is a versatile method to show differences in depressive patients, patients with schizophrenia, and controls. Many paradigms do not have the range of performance scores to discriminate differences between populations. For example, when the percentage of correct responses is used in a 2AFC task, mean performance differences of three populations should be optimally at 60%, 75%, and 90% taking floor and ceiling effects into account. The variance in the data of a population is usually rather large, leading to poor discrimination between the populations. The subjective nature of the SfM allows for an unlimited range of scores since bias could, in principle, go to infinity. Importantly, as the SfM paradigm measures a perceptual bias, not accuracy, the results are not due to diminished attention or other higher cognitive factors, and the paradigm can help determine how observers subjectively perceive the world. Moreover, the SfM task is largely language-independent, it takes only two minutes per condition, and yet it delivers results with a high signal to noise ratio. All these characteristics make the SfM a promising tool to investigate various patient populations within one paradigm. Such paradigms are, for example, crucial to investigate whether the various psychiatric disorders lie on a continuum and share common mechanisms.
