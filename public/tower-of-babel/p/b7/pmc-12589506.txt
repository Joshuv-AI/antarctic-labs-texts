Development of a machine learning-based interface for insulin dependency prediction using clinical data
Authors: Vinod Kumar Yata, Om Pritam Das, B. V. S. Lakshmi, M. Vaishnavi, Mohd Arif Uddin, Aparna Srikantam, Sarad Pawar Naik Bukke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Diabetes mellitus is a major global health burden, and early identification of insulin dependency is important for timely intervention. This study developed an artificial intelligence-based diagnostic system using a real-world clinical dataset of 100 anonymized patient records, collected with ethical approval and informed consent. The dataset included demographic, lifestyle, and biochemical variables such as glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), fasting blood sugar (FBS), and postprandial blood sugar (PPBS). After preprocessing to handle missing values, normalize continuous variables, and encode categorical features, four machine learning models were implemented: Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM, along with ensemble based, combined approaches. Model evaluation was performed using 5-fold cross-validation with accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score as metrics. XGBoost achieved the highest performance (accuracy 0.88, precision 0.86, recall 0.90, F1-score 0.88), followed by LightGBM (accuracy 0.85, F1-score 0.84), Random Forest (accuracy 0.82, F1-score 0.81), and Logistic Regression (accuracy 0.76, F1-score 0.74). The most predictive features were PPBS and HbA1c, consistent with clinical understanding. While results are promising, they reflect a single-center dataset of 100 records, and should be interpreted as preliminary, further study will include external validation on larger, multi-site cohorts prior to clinical adoption.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a heterogeneous group of metabolic disorders characterized by chronic hyperglycemia due to defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both1,2. It is one of the most widespread non-communicable diseases globally, currently affecting over 500 million people, with incidence expected to rise due to urbanization, sedentary lifestyles, and aging populations1,3. The condition manifests mainly as type-1 diabetes, caused by autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β-cells, or type-2 diabetes, which arises from insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction4,5. Determining when diabetic patients require exogenous insulin therapy is a critical clinical decision. Insulin dependency often indicates progressive β-cell failure or suboptimal glycemic control despite oral antihyperglycemic agents3,6. Timely initiation of insulin can significantly reduce the risk of diabetes-related complications, including retinopathy, nephropathy, and cardiovascular diseases, ultimately leading to better quality of life and lower healthcare costs4,7,8. Traditionally, decisions regarding insulin initiation are based on physician judgment supported by clinical and biochemical parameters such as glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), fasting plasma glucose, and patient symptoms4,5,9. However, these methods often fail to fully account for the multifactorial and nonlinear relationships among demographic, behavioral, biochemical, and physiological variables involved in diabetes progression10–12. Additionally, the high variability across patient profiles complicates standardized treatment protocols1,2. Recent developments in artificial intelligence and machine learning (ML) offer promising solutions for analyzing complex healthcare data and supporting evidence-based clinical decisions13–18. Machine learning (ML) models can identify hidden patterns in structured and unstructured datasets and have shown considerable success in diabetes care, including risk stratification, treatment optimization, and complication prediction. Notably, supervised learning algorithms such as logistic regression, decision trees, random forests, XGBoost, and LightGBM are widely used in clinical prediction tasks6,13–16. They can identify hidden patterns in structured and unstructured datasets, which has led to considerable success in diabetes care, including risk stratification, treatment optimization, and complication prediction. Recent research has shown the utility of ensemble learning and machine learning models in various aspects of diabetes care17,19–21. For instance, machine learning applications have been used for data-driven modeling and prediction of blood glucose dynamics in type 1 diabetes11.

Despite these advances, current ML research in diabetes remains limited in key ways. Most prior studies have focused on predicting the risk of type 2 diabetes onset or secondary complications (e.g., retinopathy, neuropathy) rather than on insulin dependency itself, which is a clinically decisive treatment threshold. Furthermore, many studies rely on synthetic datasets, lack rigorous interpretability analyses, or stop at model development without demonstrating practical deployment. This study addresses these gaps by developing and rigorously evaluating multiple ML models, including Random Forest, Logistic Regression, XGBoost, LightGBM, and ensemble combinations, to predict insulin dependency using a real-world, ethically approved clinical dataset. We systematically compare model performance across standard classification metrics, analyze feature importance to provide clinically interpretable insights, and deploy the best-performing model as a web-based decision support tool. By explicitly linking predictive features such as HbA1c, blood glucose levels, and duration of diabetes with pathophysiological evidence, this work aims to bridge the gap between technical ML performance and clinical applicability, contributing toward personalized and timely diabetes care.

Data collection and preprocessing

The dataset used in this study was derived from real-world clinical records of diabetic patients, comprising individuals both dependent and non-dependent on insulin therapy. Data collection was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee for Biomedical and Health Research, Malla Reddy Institute of Medical Sciences, Hyderabad, India (certificate number: MRIMS/DHR-Msc-CREM-4024/176). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and all procedures adhered to established ethical standards for biomedical research involving human subjects. The dataset consisted of 100 de-identified and anonymized patient records with 11 core features that are age, gender, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, duration of diabetes, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), fasting blood sugar (FBS), and postprandial blood sugar (PPBS). Insulin dependency served as the binary target variable. A detailed description of the features is provided in Table 1.

Since the dataset was directly obtained from curated hospital records, no missing values were present. Preprocessing therefore focused on ensuring consistency and model readiness. Continuous variables were normalized to reduce scale differences, and categorical features were encoded into binary numerical representations. Outliers were examined using interquartile range thresholds and adjusted where necessary. The final dataset was divided into training and test sets using an 80:20 stratified sampling strategy to preserve the distribution of insulin-dependent and non-dependent patients.

Exploratory data analysis (EDA)

Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) was conducted to examine both the statistical distribution and structural characteristics of the dataset, with the aim of identifying patterns associated with insulin dependency. Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations, were calculated for continuous variables after stratification by insulin dependency status. Consistently higher values of HbA1c, FBS, and PPBS were observed among insulin-dependent patients, confirming trends of poorer glycemic control in this group. Categorical features were assessed using frequency distributions, which revealed a greater prevalence of male patients and smokers among insulin-dependent individuals. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to quantify inter-feature associations, and a correlation heatmap demonstrated strong positive relationships between glycemic indicators (HbA1c, FBS, PPBS) and BMI, suggesting their central predictive importance. To further explore multivariate interactions, pair plots were generated to visualize clustering trends between dependent and non-dependent groups. All these analyses were performed using Python libraries, specifically Pandas for data manipulation, and Matplotlib and Seaborn for data visualization27,28,30.

Model development, training, and evaluation

Six supervised machine learning models were implemented to predict insulin dependency from the preprocessed dataset. These included Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, a Voting Ensemble that aggregated the four base classifiers, and an Averaged Model that combined the probability outputs of Random Forest and LightGBM. Logistic Regression was used as a baseline linear classifier, Random Forest as a bagging-based ensemble of decision trees, and XGBoost and LightGBM as gradient boosting frameworks designed to sequentially reduce residual errors22,23,31–33. The ensemble approaches were included to evaluate whether combining base learners could yield more stable predictions.

To ensure comparability, all models were trained on the same dataset. A stratified 80/20 train–test split was applied to preserve class distribution, and 5-fold cross-validation was used during evaluation to mitigate sampling bias and improve robustness. Default hyperparameters were employed across models, as no manual tuning was performed. Model evaluation relied on multiple performance metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC). ROC curves and confusion matrices were generated to assess classification behavior and misclassification tendencies. Feature importance values were derived from tree-based models to confirm alignment with clinical understanding, and learning curves were plotted to monitor training dynamics and detect possible overfitting. The models were trained with their standard, default hyperparameters to serve as a baseline for performance evaluation, as detailed in Table 2.

Results

Descriptive statistics and demographics

comprehensive demographic and clinical characterization of the study cohort was conducted to inform model development and interpretation. Table 1 presents these features stratified by insulin dependency status. The cohort included both insulin-dependent and non-insulin-dependent diabetic patients, highlighting key differences consistent with established diabetes epidemiology3,9. Insulin-dependent patients were generally older (55.5 ± 7 vs. 54.4 ± 10 years) and predominantly male (74% vs. 47%), aligning with prior findings associating male sex with more severe diabetic phenotypes and increased insulin requirements3,8. Additionally, insulin-dependent individuals exhibited higher mean height, weight, and body mass index (BMI), factors known to influence insulin sensitivity and the progression of type 2 diabetes8,16. The prevalence of smoking was also notably higher in this group (21% vs. 8%), consistent with evidence that tobacco use exacerbates insulin resistance and impairs glycemic control37.

A significant disparity was observed in diabetes duration; insulin-dependent patients had a longer disease history (9 ± 9 vs. 4 ± 3 years), supporting literature linking prolonged diabetes duration with β-cell dysfunction and the necessity for insulin therapy38,39. Group differences were formally assessed using independent t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables, confirming the observed disparities (Table 3). Variable names in Table 3 were standardized for consistency. Statistically significant differences were observed between insulin-dependent and non-insulin-dependent groups in gender distribution (p = 0.013), diabetes duration (p = 0.0006), HbA1c levels (p = 7.6 × 10−6), and PPBS values (p = 1.0 × 10−5). Other features such as age, height, weight, BMI, smoking status, and fasting blood sugar showed no significant group differences (Table 3). Glycemic control markers, including HbA1c, fasting blood sugar (FBS), and postprandial blood sugar (PPBS), were significantly elevated in the insulin-dependent group, reflecting poorer metabolic regulation and heightened insulin dependency. These findings emphasize the importance of these clinical and demographic variables in predicting insulin dependency and justify their inclusion as key features in the machine learning models developed herein (Table 1).

Model training and performance comparison

Six machine learning models were trained and evaluated for insulin dependency prediction: Random Forest, Logistic Regression, XGBoost, LightGBM, a Voting Ensemble (all four models), and an Averaged Ensemble (Random Forest + LightGBM). Training was conducted with an 80–20 stratified split, and hyperparameters were tuned through cross-validation to ensure optimal performance. As shown in Table 4, Random Forest, Logistic Regression, and XGBoost each achieved 85% accuracy, but their discriminatory ability varied (ROC-AUC: 0.9396, 0.8462, and 0.8901, respectively). LightGBM achieved the highest accuracy (90%) and a ROC-AUC of 0.9341, while both ensemble approaches (Voting and Averaged) matched LightGBM in overall accuracy and AUC. Statistical comparison of AUC values using DeLong’s test confirmed that Logistic Regression performed significantly worse than LightGBM (p = 0.02), whereas Random Forest (p = 0.41), XGBoost (p = 0.08), Voting Ensemble (p = 0.92), and Averaged Ensemble (p = 0.94) did not differ significantly. These results demonstrate that while multiple models achieved strong performance, Logistic Regression was less reliable for capturing insulin dependency risk.

To assess whether performance differences were statistically significant, pairwise comparisons of ROC-AUC were conducted using DeLong’s test, and McNemar’s test was applied for classification errors. LightGBM demonstrated significantly higher AUC compared to Logistic Regression (p < 0.05), while its difference with Random Forest and XGBoost was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Similarly, ensemble methods did not significantly outperform LightGBM (p > 0.05). Confusion matrices in Fig. 4 visualize these results, confirming robust class-specific predictions. Together, these analyses suggest that while multiple models achieved high accuracy, LightGBM provided the best balance of predictive performance and statistical reliability without being significantly outperformed by ensemble methods. Model interpretation and feature importance

Feature interpretation of the best-performing LightGBM model provided insights into clinically relevant predictors of insulin dependency (Fig. 5C). The model consistently identified HbA1c as the strongest determinant, followed by fasting blood sugar (FBS), postprandial blood sugar (PPBS), and duration of diabetes. These variables are directly aligned with clinical knowledge that poor glycemic control and longer disease duration increase the likelihood of requiring insulin therapy. Anthropometric and demographic features, including BMI, weight, age, and height, showed moderate contributions, while lifestyle factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption had relatively minor influence. This ranking underscores that metabolic and disease progression markers, rather than lifestyle variables alone, are the primary drivers of insulin dependency risk.

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (Fig. 5B) demonstrated excellent discrimination with an AUC of 0.9341, confirming the model’s ability to distinguish between insulin-dependent and non-dependent patients. In addition, the learning curve (Fig. 5A) reflected stable performance across training and validation sets, with no indication of overfitting. Together, these analyses reinforce the robustness, interpretability, and clinical credibility of the LightGBM model as a decision support tool. Together, these findings confirm that the LightGBM model not only achieves high predictive performance but also provides clinically interpretable insights aligned with established diabetes pathophysiology.

Model comparison and selection

A systematic comparison across performance metrics (Table 3) revealed that LightGBM and ensemble-based methods (Voting and Averaged) consistently outperformed baseline classifiers. LightGBM achieved the highest accuracy (90%), with an AUC of 0.9341, and demonstrated strong balance between sensitivity and specificity. The F1-scores of 0.9167 (non-insulin group) and 0.875 (insulin group) further highlight its reliability in identifying both patient classes. Although ensemble approaches achieved comparable accuracy and AUC, they offered no substantial improvement over LightGBM while introducing additional computational complexity. Logistic Regression showed good calibration but lower sensitivity in detecting insulin-dependent patients, while Random Forest and XGBoost performed well but were slightly less stable across folds. Given its combination of competitive performance, interpretability, computational efficiency, and deployment simplicity, LightGBM was selected as the final predictive model. The decision aligns with both statistical evidence and practical considerations for clinical translation, ensuring that the tool can be readily integrated into real-world decision support systems.

Deployment and interface testing

The final LightGBM model was deployed as an interactive clinical decision-support tool using the Gradio framework and hosted on Hugging Face Spaces. The user interface (Fig. 6) enables clinicians and researchers to input relevant clinical and demographic variables: age, gender, anthropometric measurements, glycemic markers, lifestyle factors, and diabetes duration, to obtain real-time predictions of insulin dependency. The deployment pipeline included model serialization (joblib), ensuring reproducibility and portability across environments, and seamless integration with the GUI, which presents predictions alongside contextual information. The tool was further tested with synthetic and held-out patient records to validate input–output consistency, system robustness, and usability.

To ensure clinical transparency, the interface highlights the features most strongly influencing predictions (as derived from model interpretation in Sect. 3.4). In addition, disclaimers clarify that the model is an experimental research tool and should complement, not replace, professional medical judgment. The modular design allows for easy updates, including retraining on larger or more diverse datasets, expanding feature sets, or integrating into hospital electronic health record (EHR) systems. Collectively, the deployment demonstrates how advanced machine learning models can be translated into usable clinical tools while maintaining a balance between performance, interpretability, and practical applicability.

Discussion

This study developed and evaluated machine learning models to predict insulin dependency in diabetic patients using routinely available demographic and clinical features. Among the tested algorithms, LightGBM demonstrated superior performance (accuracy 90%, AUC 0.9341) compared to Random Forest, Logistic Regression, and XGBoost. While ensemble methods performed comparably, LightGBM was ultimately favored for its interpretability, computational efficiency, and streamlined deployment. The superior performance of LightGBM is likely due to its ability to model complex, non-linear feature interactions and capture subtle threshold effects that are clinically relevant in diabetes progression25,40. This is particularly important for the interplay between glycemic markers and disease duration, as the model can effectively discern the point at which a patient’s condition may necessitate insulin therapy9,41,42. Feature importance analysis consistently identified HbA1c, FBS, PPBS, and diabetes duration as the most influential predictors41,42. This finding is directly aligned with established clinical guidelines that recommend HbA1c and blood glucose monitoring as primary indicators for intensifying therapy, including insulin initiation. Elevated HbA1c reflects long-term glycemic control, while FBS and PPBS provide complementary short-term insights into glucose homeostasis9,19. The well-known association between longer diabetes duration and increased β-cell exhaustion, which often necessitates insulin therapy, further validates this finding38,39. The convergence of model-derived feature importance with established pathophysiological mechanisms strengthens confidence in the model’s clinical relevance31,43. In contrast, demographic factors (age, sex) and lifestyle variables (smoking, alcohol consumption) exerted weaker effects, which is consistent with their indirect influence on glycemic regulation rather than a direct treatment indication9,44.

From a clinical standpoint, despite its limitations, this model has practical implications as a decision-support tool. By identifying patients at high risk of requiring insulin, clinicians can prioritize close monitoring, patient counseling, and timely therapy adjustments. For example, in patients with borderline HbA1c or rising PPBS, an AI-assisted prediction could prompt earlier consideration of insulin therapy, reducing delays in achieving glycemic targets. Moreover, integration into a web-based interface allows rapid risk stratification at the point of care, potentially reducing clinician workload and improving patient engagement. Transparency through feature importance enhances trust, addressing a major barrier to clinical adoption of AI systems. Nevertheless, several limitations warrant careful consideration. The dataset was modest in size and drawn from a single institution, which may constrain the generalizability of findings across different healthcare settings43,45. Additionally, the model did not incorporate potentially important covariates such as medication history, comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, cardiovascular disease), or socioeconomic factors, all of which can influence insulin requirement. The cross-sectional design also prevents assessment of temporal dynamics such as, whether predicted “high-risk” patients actually progressed to insulin therapy over time. While stratified sampling helped address class imbalance, external validation on multi-center and ethnically diverse cohorts remains necessary to establish robustness11,12. Prospective validation in clinical workflows will be essential to determine usability, safety, and real-world impact43,44.

Future work should focus on scaling this approach to larger and more heterogeneous populations, incorporating longitudinal data from electronic health records and continuous glucose monitoring to capture disease trajectories more accurately. Integration with omics data, such as metabolomics or genomics, could provide additional insights into individual variability in insulin requirement. Finally, systematic evaluation of fairness and bias will be required to ensure equitable application across demographic subgroups, along with prospective trials to evaluate clinical utility. Such steps are critical before translating this promising proof-of-concept into a reliable and widely adopted clinical decision-support tool.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that machine learning models, particularly LightGBM, can accurately predict insulin dependency in diabetes using standard clinical and demographic variables. LightGBM achieved the highest performance (accuracy 90%, AUC 0.9341), with HbA1c, fasting and postprandial glucose, and duration of diabetes emerging as key predictors, consistent with established clinical guidelines. The translation of this model into a web-based application illustrates its potential to support early identification of patients requiring insulin therapy, thereby aiding treatment planning and patient stratification. However, the modest sample size, single-center origin, and omission of potential confounders such as comorbidities and medication use limit generalizability. External, multi-center validation and longitudinal testing are essential before clinical deployment. Future work should focus on expanding datasets, integrating models into electronic health record systems, and addressing fairness and interpretability to ensure safe and equitable application. In summary, this work provides a proof-of-concept for AI-driven tools in diabetes care, showing promise for enhancing clinical decision-making, but requiring further validation to establish reliability and clinical utility.
