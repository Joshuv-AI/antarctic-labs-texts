Cost-Effectiveness of AF Screening With 2-Week Patch Monitors: The mSToPS Study
Authors: Matthew R. Reynolds, Amy B. Stein, Xiaowu Sun, Evangelos Hytopoulos, Steven R. Steinhubl, David J. Cohen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The mSToPS study (mHealth Screening to Prevent Strokes) reported screening older Americans at risk for atrial fibrillation (AF) and stroke using 2-week patch monitors was associated with increased rates of AF diagnosis and anticoagulant prescription within 1 year and improved clinical outcomes at 3 years relative to matched controls. Cost-effectiveness of this AF screening approach has not been explored.METHODS:We conducted a US-based health economic analysis of AF screening using patient-level data from mSToPS. Clinical outcomes, resource use, and costs were obtained through 3 years using claims data. Individual costs, survival, and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) were projected over a lifetime horizon using regression modeling, US life tables, and external data where needed. Adjustment between groups was performed using propensity score bin bootstrapping.RESULTS:Screening participants (mean age, 74 years, 41% female, median CHA2DS2-VASC score 3) wore on average 1.7 two-week monitors at a mean cost of $614/person. Over 3 years, outpatient visits were more frequent for monitored than unmonitored individuals (difference 190 per 100 patient-years [95% CI, 82–298]), but emergency department visits (−8.3 [95% CI, −12.6 to −4.1]) and hospitalizations (−15.2 [CI, −22 to −8.6]) were less frequent. Total adjusted 3-year costs were slightly higher (mean difference, $1551 [95% CI, −$1047 to $4038]) in the monitoring group. In patient-level projections, the monitoring group had slightly greater quality-adjusted survival (8.81 versus 8.71 QALYs, difference, 0.09 [95% CI, −0.05 to 0.24]) and slightly higher lifetime costs, resulting in an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of $36 100/QALY gained. With bootstrap resampling, the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio for monitoring was <$50 000/QALY in 64% of study replicates, and <$150 000/QALY in 91%.CONCLUSIONS:Using lifetime projections derived from the mSToPS study, we found that AF screening using 2-week patch monitors in older Americans was associated with high economic value. Confirmation of these uncertain findings in a randomized trial is warranted.REGISTRATION:URL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: NCT02506244.

With its rising prevalence, the availability of effective preventive therapies for stroke, and the emergence of improving diagnostic technologies, atrial fibrillation (AF) has attracted considerable interest as a target for population screening. Multiple studies have now demonstrated that sub-clinical AF can be detected more frequently than with usual clinical care using a variety of electrocardiographic monitors and personal electronic devices.1–5 However, only limited evidence has been generated that AF screening translates into improved clinical outcomes. Consequently, empirical evidence that AF screening programs are cost-effective is also limited.

The mSToPS study (mHealth Screening to Prevent Strokes) was a pragmatic trial of AF screening that randomized older US adults at high risk for AF or its consequences (due to an elevated CHA2DS2-VASc score) to either immediate or delayed monitoring with 2-week patch monitors. After the 4-month primary end point, outcomes to 3 years were compared between the pooled randomized patients who were all actively monitored and matched, observational, usual care controls. As previously reported, the screening strategy utilized in mSToPS was associated with more frequent AF diagnosis as well as the initiation of oral anticoagulation in the first year of follow-up compared with usual care3,6 with some shifts but no major increase in overall resource utilization.7 At 3 years of follow-up, the screening group was observed to have a lower incidence of stroke and the composite of death, stroke, systemic embolism, or myocardial infarction than the control group.6 Given these findings, we performed a study to assess the cost and potential cost-effectiveness of AF screening with patch monitors, based primarily on empirical patient-level data from the mSToPS study.

METHODS

Data, Analytic Methods (Code), and Research Materials Transparency

The source data for this research study were derived from a private insurance claims database (Aetna, currently owned by CVS Health). These data, by contract, cannot be shared with outside investigators.

mSTopS Overview

The mSToPS study design has been described previously.3,8 In brief, mSToPS was a pragmatic matched cohort study in which US residents aged ≥75 years (or younger with certain comorbidities) without a prior history of AF or baseline anticoagulant use were recruited from Aetna commercial and Medicare Advantage plans. Those opting to participate were asked to wear two 2-week patch monitors (Zio XT, iRhythm, San Francisco, CA) 3 months apart. The monitored group was randomly divided into immediate and delayed monitoring subsets with monitoring initiated immediately or 4 months after enrollment, respectively. For analytic purposes, these groups were combined and compared with an unmonitored control group matched (2:1) on age, sex, and CHA2DS2-VASc score. For the current analysis, we adopted the convention used in 2 prior mSToPS analyses6,7 to use the date ECG screening began as the analytic start time for each study subject and their 2 matched controls.

Clinical outcomes and costs for mSToPS participants were determined through 3 years, or the date of death or administrative censoring, using health insurance claims. mSToPS was originally approved by the Scripps Office for the Protection of Research Subjects. Participants in the monitoring group provided informed consent. Control group participants’ claims data were kept deidentified. The current analysis was designed by the academic coauthors, with biostatistical support from CVS Health (who acquired Aetna in 2018) and financial support from iRhythm. The current study received Institutional Review Board approval from Advarra.

Analytic Overview

We assessed the cost-effectiveness of AF screening using patch monitors, compared with usual care, from a US third-party payer perspective, over a lifetime horizon. We performed a patient-level analysis using the mSToPS per-protocol population, as defined previously.6,7 Figure 1 provides a conceptual overview of our analytic approach. Survival, clinical outcomes, resource utilization, and health care costs were determined using insurance claims through 3 years or the date of censoring. Patient-level projections of costs, life expectancy, and quality-adjusted life expectancy beyond 3 years (or the date of administrative censoring) were made as described below. Specific clinical events (eg, stroke) were not modeled beyond 3 years. Future costs, life years, and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) were discounted at 3% per year consistent with current recommendations.9

Analytic overview. For both study groups, clinical outcomes, resource utilization, and costs were tracked through 3 y using insurance claims data. Thereafter, patient-level costs and life expectancy were projected with a lifetime horizon using regression formulae and US life tables, respectively. Quality of life adjustment, for the calculation of quality-adjusted life years, was applied across both periods using external inputs. Long-term survival was adjusted for strokes/systemic embolism (SE) events observed during initial follow-up. AF indicates atrial fibrillation.

Determination of Costs

All costs are reported in constant 2021 US dollars. The cost of patch monitoring was estimated based on average Medicare reimbursement for current procedural terminology codes 93246-93248. The technical fee for local monitor application and physician fee for study interpretation were obtained from the 2021 Medicare Fee Schedule. An estimate of the average technical fee currently paid to the vendor by Medicare ($311 per monitor) was provided by iRhythm.

Payments for office visits, emergency department encounters, hospital admissions related to cardiovascular causes, stroke or bleeding, and prescription drugs were obtained from Aetna claims from baseline through 3 years, representing the cost to the insurer for these services. Linked pharmacy claims were available for 55% of mSToPS participants. For individuals without pharmacy claims, drug costs were separately imputed for oral anticoagulant and antiarrhythmic drugs by constructing multivariable logistic regression models to predict the likelihood of prescription, and then applying the average cost for those categories. The aggregate mean cost for all other prescription drugs was estimated with linear regression.

Using available follow-up data through 3 years, per-person average monthly costs were summed across the categories described above. To project long-term costs beyond 3 years, a linear regression model of average total monthly costs was constructed. Covariates included baseline demographics and comorbid conditions, Charlson comorbidity index, measures of baseline resource utilization, observed 3-year events (stroke and myocardial infarction), AF diagnosis, and the method of AF diagnosis (by patch only, or clinical) when applicable. Lifetime costs beyond the 3-year period were calculated for each surviving patient as the product of the estimated monthly costs and projected survival.

Estimation of Survival

Observed mortality in the mSToPS population was low (<1 per 100 person-years) through 3 years.6 Survival time was directly observed up to 3 years, with censoring only for disenrollment from the insurance plan (22% of monitoring group and 32% of control group at 3 years). Beyond 3 years, individual age and sex-adjusted long-term survival was estimated using US life tables.10 Adjustments to the long-term hazard rate were made for participants with observed stroke or systemic embolism events (long-term hazard ratio 1.94).11 Although a statistically borderline difference in mortality was observed through 3 years (adjusted hazard ratio, 0.61, P=0.047),6 we assumed no ongoing survival benefit from the monitoring intervention after 3 years (ie, no adjustment to life table estimates based on group).

Determination of QALYs

No quality of life data were collected as part of mSToPS. Baseline utility estimates were taken for age-matched members of the US population from published literature.12 Utility decrements were applied for patients observed to experience stroke or systemic embolism, also based on published literature.12,13 Although utility decrements associated with a clinical diagnosis of AF have been published, the great majority of patients in the monitored arm of mSToPS were observed to have a very low burden of AF at the time of diagnosis (<1% on average) and only a small minority were treated with rhythm control interventions (antiarrhythmic drugs or ablation). We therefore did not apply a utility decrement to all patients diagnosed with AF. At the end of the 3-year observation period, each patient’s utility was assumed to decline at a rate of ~0.003 per year, as reported in prior research.12,14

Projected QALYs beyond 3 years were calculated by summing the age-adjusted utility for each year, or fraction of a year, of projected survival. Lifetime QALYs were determined as the sum of the 3-year and projected long-term QALYs.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data are reported as mean±SD and were compared with 2-sample t tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Categorical data are reported as frequencies and were compared with the Fisher exact test. For costs and QALYs, group means and between-group differences with 95% CIs were generated by bootstrap resampling with 1000 replicates.

Observed 3-year costs and QALYs were adjusted for censoring with the Bang/Tsiatis method based on inverse probability weighting.15 Given the nonrandomized design of mSToPS, we also adjusted for potential imbalances in baseline characteristics using propensity score bin bootstrapping.16,17 Propensity scores for the probability that a given patient was in the monitored versus unmonitored group were calculated with multivariable logistic regression. Covariates for this model included age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, heart failure, COPD, chronic renal failure, diabetes, hypertension, obesity, stroke, prior myocardial infarction, sleep apnea, and ER visits, baseline PCP visits, and hospitalizations in the 12 months before the study start date. Propensity score bin bootstrapping was then used to generate adjusted group mean and variance estimates for resource utilization, and discounted lifetime costs, life expectancy, and QALYs.

Cost-Effectiveness Analysis

Incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) comparing the monitored with the matched, unmonitored group were calculated as the ratio of incremental costs to incremental effectiveness, with QALYs serving as the principal effectiveness measure. ICERs were summarized as means and 95% CIs from the bootstrapped samples.

Uncertainty in the join distribution of incremental costs and QALYs was visually represented by plotting the results of each bootstrap iteration on the cost-effectiveness plane.18 Additionally, the likelihood that the monitoring strategy was cost-effective across a range of potential cost-effectiveness thresholds (also derived from the bootstrap iterations) was displayed graphically using cost-effectiveness acceptability curves.19 Based on current recommendations, an ICER of < $50 000 per QALY gained was considered high economic value, while values between $50 000 and $150 000 per QALY gained were considered intermediate value, and values > $150 000 per QALY gained were considered poor value within the US health care system.20

Sensitivity, Subgroup, and Scenario Analyses

One-way sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the impact of potentially important assumptions and external inputs on overall study results. Parameters varied in sensitivity analysis included the discount rate, reimbursement for patch monitors, the disutility of stroke events, and the excess long-term hazard associated with stroke/systemic embolism. We additionally performed a scenario analysis in which age/sex-adjusted mortality rates (from the US life tables) were assumed to increase by 25%, as might be expected in a higher-than-average risk population. ICERs were calculated for the following prespecified subgroups: age (<75 versus ≥75), sex, and CHA2DS2-VASc score (≤3 versus >3). Finally, we conducted 2 scenario analyses in which the timeframe by which the hazard ratio for death reached neutrality was extended from the base case assumption of 3 years to 5 and 10 years.

DISCUSSION

In this health economic evaluation of the mSToPS study, we found AF screening with patch monitors to be cost-effective, with an ICER of ≈$36 000 per QALY gained, which is considered high value by US cardiac professional societies.20 This finding resulted from a small (≈0.09 year) projected gain in lifetime QALYs and a small (≈$3300) projected increase in lifetime health care costs, about 20% of which came from the screening intervention, itself. These gains in quality-adjusted survival were driven primarily by observed reductions in adverse clinical events over the initial 3-year follow-up period with the patch monitor strategy as well as projected impacts on life expectancy from observed stroke events.6

For any population screening strategy to be cost-effective, it must not only succeed at diagnosing a condition earlier than otherwise but also facilitate interventions that improve clinical outcomes. In the case of AF, several screening strategies, including daily hand-held EKG recordings,5,21 patch monitors,1,8 smartwatches,2 and insertable cardiac monitors4 have met the initial bar of increasing AF detection. Two studies of patch monitors, including mSToPS and a smaller randomized trial, have additionally observed increased rates of oral anticoagulation use after AF screening.1,8

It has been much harder to prove that screening improves clinical outcomes, however. For this reason, the US Preventive Services Task Force has recently reiterated its position that there remains insufficient evidence to recommend for or against AF screening.22 The systematic review underlying that position considered only randomized evidence. At present, mSToPS is unique among AF screening studies in its empirical observation of clinical benefit. Our study extends the mSToPS results by showing that if the observed clinical benefits are accurate, AF screening with patch monitors is likely to provide high value from a health economic perspective.

There are several possible explanations for the benefit seen with patch monitor screening in mSToPs. In addition to increasing the utilization of anticoagulation, earlier detection of AF could have led to health benefits by raising awareness of the condition’s presence among older adults and their health care providers. With earlier institution of rate control and rhythm control interventions, and knowledge of potential warning signs that would prompt medical attention, additional adverse consequences of AF (such as progressive heart failure) may have been avoided. This hypothesis is supported by the observed shift of care from the inpatient to the outpatient setting in mSToPS, and the lower rate of adverse events near the time of AF diagnosis when accomplished via screening rather than routine clinical care.6 Earlier knowledge of an AF diagnosis could also prompt healthier behaviors and better management of potential AF triggers (eg, weight loss, reduced alcohol intake, treatment of sleep apnea, etc) with cascading health benefits.

Data on the potential cost-effectiveness of AF screening programs are limited. STROKESTOP, a randomized study of AF screening using hand-held, single-lead EKGs (self-collected twice daily for 14 days) in a large Swedish cohort aged 75 to 76 years, reported a marginal reduction in the composite of stroke, bleeding, and death over almost 7 years.21 A model-based health economic analysis of the STROKESTOP trial recently reported a small lifetime gain in QALYs (≈0.065 QALYs per person), as did we, and projected net cost savings, yielding an economically dominant result.23

Additionally, Chen and colleagues performed a model-based study assessing the potential cost-effectiveness of several different AF screening strategies with a focus on wearable (watch or wristband) devices.24 Their study, which also concluded that AF screening was likely to be cost-effective, was different from ours in many ways, including importantly that the health benefits of screening and subsequent therapy were assumed and the downstream costs of care were modeled rather than assessed empirically. It is noteworthy that each of the 6 “contemporary” screening strategies used in that analysis involved the use of patch monitors to confirm an AF diagnosis.

We found moderate uncertainty in our cost-effectiveness projections. Our bootstrap resampling results indicate a high (>90%) probability that the monitoring group in mSToPS had a small but measurable gain in QALYs and life years compared with control. We observed more uncertainty in projected incremental costs, which were more variable among these older individuals, the majority of whom did not have AF. Although our point estimate showed higher lifetime costs with screening, given the high costs of medical care in an elderly population, the relative increase in overall costs was small. Importantly, we did not find evidence that the mSToPS screening approach resulted in substantial induced costs for AF care.

Our findings were minimally influenced by plausible variations in our analytic assumptions or the external parameters used in our calculations. We did observe some potential differences in the cost-effectiveness estimates in a few key subgroups, however. For example, we found a potentially larger benefit from screening in older (≥75 years) than younger individuals. This seems plausible given that AF incidence and stroke risk are known to rise with age. Other possible differences (eg, by sex) were mainly related to instability in the estimates of incremental cost and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Limitations

Our study should be considered in light of several limitations. The main limitation of this study was that the source data for our projections came from nonrandomized group comparisons. The participants in mSToPS were, in essence, self-selected. Unmeasured differences may have been present in monitored participants’ health, health behaviors, and usage of the health care system, relative to control. Although we used robust methods to address potential imbalances in the cohort’s measured characteristics (including adjustment for observed health care utilization patterns), we cannot exclude the possibility that the observed health benefits of AF screening were influenced by unmeasured confounding. Second, our lifetime cost-effectiveness analysis was based on projections and cannot be verified by empirical data. To avoid overly optimistic extrapolation, we assumed no further health benefits of AF screening beyond those observed during the 3-year follow-up period and their projected late consequences.

A further disadvantage of the pragmatic approach to determining outcomes from insurance claims is the occurrence of administrative censoring due to disenrollment from the insurance plan. The potential for differential censoring to affect outcomes ascertainment is not clear but may have tended to underestimate the potential benefit of screening by more often failing to observe events in the control group, which had a higher censoring rate.

Third, both the screened population and control group for the mSToPs study were selected for characteristics associated with increased risk of AF or increased risk of stroke with AF compared with the general population. As such, we cannot assume that our findings would apply to younger or healthier individuals. Finally, our findings pertain specifically to the AF screening strategy used in mSToPs—continuous monitoring with two 2-week patch monitors, roughly 3 months apart—and should not be extrapolated to other screening strategies that may differ in cost, sensitivity, or specificity.

Conclusions

Based on patient-level data and lifetime projections derived from the mSToPS study, AF screening using 2-week Zio patch monitors in older Americans at high risk for AF or its consequences may provide high value from a health economic perspective. These findings are associated with both stochastic uncertainty and inferential limitations stemming from nonrandomized comparisons. If replicated in future randomized trials, these findings have important implications for payment policy and support continued, confirmatory research in this field.
