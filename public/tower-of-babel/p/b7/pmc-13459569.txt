Clinical implications of zolbetuximab-associated hypoalbuminemia in CLDN18.2-positive gastric or gastroesophageal junction cancer
Authors: A Ooki, K Shimozaki, S Fukuoka, S Udagawa, K Yoshino, K Yoshikawa, T Wakatsuki, M Ogura, E Shinozaki, K Chin, K Yamaguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundZolbetuximab enhances outcomes in human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative, claudin 18 isoform 2-positive advanced gastric cancer; nevertheless, the clinical implications of zolbetuximab-related hypoalbuminemia are not fully understood.Materials and methodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective study of 70 patients treated with zolbetuximab plus chemotherapy. We evaluated serial changes in albumin and their clinical implications, including associated on-target toxicities, changes in ascites, and antitumor activity.ResultsAll patients exhibited some level of albumin decrease. The lowest hypoalbuminemia was grades 0-1 in 28 patients (40%), grade 2 in 27 (39%), and grade 3 in 15 (21%). The occurrence of grade ≥2 hypoalbuminemia peaked between cycles 1 and 3 (36%-49%) and then decreased. The median time to grade 3 hypoalbuminemia was 28.0 days, with recovery to grade ≤2 occurring after a median of 16.5 days. Grade 3 hypoalbuminemia correlated with advanced age, poorer performance status, and lower baseline albumin levels; it did not manifest in patients who had previously undergone gastrectomy. It frequently coincided with limb edema (100%), anorexia (93%), endoscopic gastritis (47%), and new or worsening ascites (60%). Most patients who temporarily paused zolbetuximab treatment resumed without recurrent grade 3 hypoalbuminemia. The antitumor activity did not significantly differ in patients with grade 3 events compared with those with grades 0-2 events, with an objective response rate of 71.4% versus 51.7% and a median overall survival of not reached versus 18.0 months (hazard ratio 1.16, P = 0.77).ConclusionsZolbetuximab-associated hypoalbuminemia typically manifests early, is frequently reversible, and is accompanied by transient ascites.Key words: CLDN18.2, gastric or gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma, gastritis, hypoalbuminemia, zolbetuximab

Introduction

Gastric or gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma (GEA) remains a substantial global health concern, with nearly 1 million new cases and over 650 000 deaths annually worldwide.1 In advanced stages, treatment guided by biomarkers has broadened from human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) to include claudin 18 isoform 2 (CLDN18.2). This expansion highlights the growing importance of biologically targeted therapy for GEA.2, 3, 4

CLDN18.2 is a stomach-lineage tight-junction molecule with a highly restricted physiological expression in gastric epithelial cells.5,6 During malignant transformation and the loss of apical-basolateral polarity, the extracellular domain of CLDN18.2 becomes accessible to circulating antibodies, creating a therapeutically actionable target.6 Zolbetuximab, a chimeric IgG1 monoclonal antibody directed against CLDN18.2, exerts antitumor activity through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, complement-dependent cytotoxicity, and related immune-effector mechanisms.6,7 The clinical value of zolbetuximab has been established in the phase III SPOTLIGHT and GLOW trials. Zolbetuximab plus chemotherapy improved progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) compared with chemotherapy alone in patients with HER2-negative, CLDN18.2-positive advanced GEA.8, 9, 10

Clinical deployment has highlighted a toxicity profile consistent with a biologic ‘antigen dilemma’: the target is enriched on tumor cells yet retained in normal tissue of origin, creating the risk of on-target, off-tumor toxicity.11 Initially, the toxic effects of zolbetuximab were primarily associated with nausea and vomiting, particularly during the first infusion.8,9,12,13 However, a consistent observation was a decrease in albumin levels. According to US prescribing information, reduced albumin levels were reported as a laboratory abnormality in 78% of patients receiving zolbetuximab plus mFOLFOX6 in the SPOTLIGHT trial and 66% of those receiving zolbetuximab plus CAPOX in the GLOW trial (https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2025/761365s003lbl.pdf); grade 3 or 4 events occurred in 4.4% and 3.8% of patients, respectively. Hypoalbuminemia is a common adverse reaction according to the European Medicines Agency product information (https://www.ema.europa.eu). Real-world experiences have drawn attention to hypoalbuminemia.14, 15, 16, 17, 18 This toxicity is mechanistically associated with gastric mucosal injury. Zolbetuximab frequently induces early, transient gastritis, which is linked to a more significant decline in serum albumin levels, especially in cases of diffuse gastritis rather than focal.19,20 Scintigraphy in case reports demonstrated actual protein leakage from the stomach in patients treated with zolbetuximab,21, 22, 23 supporting protein-losing gastroenteropathy or protein-losing gastropathy as the cause of severe hypoalbuminemia. Preclinical studies also support direct, on-target, off-tumor injury to the gastric mucosa with CLDN18.2-directed therapy,11,24,25 leading to mucosal inflammation, impaired barrier integrity, and clinically significant albumin loss.

However, the temporal profile of serum albumin levels and their clinicopathological correlates are not well characterized. It is unclear whether hypoalbuminemia is merely a laboratory abnormality or a clinically significant finding associated with ascites, treatment efficacy, or treatment tolerance. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective study of 70 patients treated at our institution to characterize the longitudinal course of hypoalbuminemia, its association with other potential on-target and off-tumor toxicities, its correlation with changes in ascites, and its implications for antitumor activity.

Materials and Methods

Study design and population

This single-center, registry-based observational study was conducted at the Cancer Institute Hospital of the Japanese Foundation for Cancer Research in Tokyo, Japan. The study included patients with unresectable or metastatic, histologically confirmed HER2-negative, and CLDN18.2-positive GEA who were treated with zolbetuximab in combination with fluoropyrimidine–oxaliplatin chemotherapy between July 2024 and December 2025. Data pertaining to clinical, pathological, laboratory, radiographic findings, and outcomes were analyzed on 10 April 2026.

Eligible patients had unresectable or metastatic adenocarcinomas confirmed histologically as HER2-negative and CLDN18.2-positive. Patients with another active primary malignancy requiring systemic therapy were excluded from the study. Zolbetuximab, in combination with chemotherapy, was administered to all patients following our institutional protocol, initially described12 and subsequently revised (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100383). Zolbetuximab was prepared in a total volume of 250 ml at a final concentration of 2.0-5.0 mg/ml. At treatment initiation (cycle 1), the infusion commenced at 35 ml/h for the first 60 min, with a subsequent increase to 125 ml/h if tolerated. If emesis during cycle 1 was manageable, subsequent infusions lasted >2 h. Treatment continued until radiographic disease progression, unacceptable adverse events, withdrawal of consent, or a physician’s decision. Dose reduction, treatment delay, and treatment discontinuation were at the treating oncologist’s discretion, following routine practice.

Assessments

Baseline characteristics, laboratory data, adverse events, and clinical outcomes were retrieved from electronic medical records. Performance status (PS) was evaluated utilizing the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) scale, and adverse events were categorized based on the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0. Serum albumin levels were measured at baseline, on day 1 of each treatment cycle through cycle 6, and at additional time points as clinically indicated. Nadir albumin was defined as the lowest recorded value during treatment. For patients with grade 3 hypoalbuminemia, the time to recovery was the duration from the albumin nadir to grade ≤2. For patients with grades 0-2 hypoalbuminemia, the interval from the nadir to the initial documented increase in serum albumin was described. Additionally, concomitant limb edema, anorexia, endoscopic gastritis findings, and ascites changes were evaluated using computed tomography at the time of nadir albumin measurement. CLDN18.2 positivity was determined as per the criteria established in pivotal trials, indicating moderate-to-strong membranous staining in at least 75% of tumor cells, and was evaluated using the VENTANA CLDN18 (43-14A) RxDx Assay in the institutional pathology laboratory.8, 9, 10

Tumor response was evaluated following the RECIST, version 1.1, in patients having at least one target lesion. The objective response rate (ORR) was the proportion of patients achieving complete response (CR) or partial response (PR), while the disease control rate included patients with CR, PR, or stable disease (SD). The depth of response was determined as the maximum percentage reduction from baseline in the sum of the diameters of the target lesions. For patients lacking target lesions, the best overall response was categorized as CR, non-CR/non-PD, or PD based on radiological findings.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate patient characteristics, we generated summary statistics by presenting frequencies and proportions for categorical variables, and medians and ranges for continuous variables. Continuous variables were compared using the two-sample t test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate, while categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Exploratory univariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to investigate the relationship between clinicopathological factors and grade 3 hypoalbuminemia. PFS was defined as the duration from treatment initiation to confirmed disease progression or all-cause mortality, while OS was defined as the period from treatment commencement to death from any cause. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Patients who did not experience an event at the data cut-off were censored on the date of their last contact or tumor assessment. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the Cox proportional hazards model. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value of <0.05. The statistical analyses were conducted using JMP software, version 14.0.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), or EZR statistical software (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), which is a user-friendly interface for R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). EZR is a customized version of the R Commander tailored to include specific statistical functions commonly utilized in biostatistics.26

Results

Patients and baseline characteristics

Seventy patients were included in this analysis (Table 1). The median age of the patients was 61 years (range 30-84 years). ECOG PS was 0 in 31 patients (44.3%) and ≥1 in 39 patients (55.7%). Peritoneal metastases were present in 54 patients (77.1%), and baseline ascites were observed in 39 patients (55.7%). Diffuse-type histology according to the Lauren classification was observed in 55 patients (78.6%). Nineteen patients (27.1%) had undergone a prior gastrectomy, including 13 (18.6%) who had undergone a total gastrectomy.

Incidence and time course of hypoalbuminemia

All 70 patients experienced some degree of albumin decline during the treatment (Figure 1). The lowest grade of the albumin decrease was grade 0 or 1 in 28 patients (40%), grade 2 in 27 patients (39%), and grade 3 in 15 patients (21%). The frequency of grade ≥2 hypoalbuminemia increased rapidly after treatment initiation. The proportion of patients with grade ≥2 hypoalbuminemia was 13% at baseline, increased to 44% during cycle 1, peaked at 49% on day 1 of cycle 2, and then decreased to 36%, 29%, 30%, and 25% on day 1 of cycles 3, 4, 5, and 6, respectively. None of the patients developed grade 3 hypoalbuminemia after cycle 7. Overall, both the frequency and severity of hypoalbuminemia tended to decrease as the treatment continued. Among the 55 patients with nadir hypoalbuminemia of grade ≤2, 53 continued zolbetuximab treatment without dose interruption or discontinuation, and their serum albumin levels subsequently improved during the course of treatment (Figure 1 and Table 2). Among the 15 patients with grade 3 hypoalbuminemia, 4 continued zolbetuximab without interruption, 9 temporarily interrupted treatment, and 2 permanently discontinued zolbetuximab; serum albumin levels subsequently improved in all patients, regardless of the management strategy (Figure 1 and Table 2). The median time to albumin nadir was 16.0 days in patients with grade 2 hypoalbuminemia and 28.0 days in those with grade 3 hypoalbuminemia, as compared with 23.0 days in patients with grade 0 or 1 hypoalbuminemia (Table 2). The corresponding median times to recovery were 19.5 days, 18.0 days, and 16.5 days, respectively. Among patients with grade 3 hypoalbuminemia, the median time from albumin nadir to recovery was 17.5 days (range 3.0-56.0 days) in those with dose interruption or discontinuation and 16.0 days (range 7.0-19.0 days) in those without dose interruption or discontinuation. Albumin supplementation was not routinely administered for hypoalbuminemia alone; only one patient received albumin, in the setting of severe infection.

Clinicopathological factors associated with grade 3 hypoalbuminemia

Patients with grade 3 hypoalbuminemia were older than those with grades 0-2 hypoalbuminemia (median age, 73 versus 59 years; P = 0.03) and were more likely to have an ECOG PS >0 (80.0% versus 49.1%; P = 0.03; Table 1). They also had a higher neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio at baseline (median, 3.4 versus 2.3; P < 0.01) and lower baseline albumin values, with grade 2 baseline hypoalbuminemia being overrepresented in the grade 3 group (33.3% versus 7.3%; P < 0.01). Grade 3 hypoalbuminemia occurred only in patients without prior gastrectomy; no patient with prior gastrectomy, including no patients with total gastrectomy, developed grade 3 hypoalbuminemia. The Lauren distribution also differed between the groups, with intestinal-type tumors occurring more frequently in the grade 3 group (40.0% versus 16.4%; P = 0.04). In the exploratory univariate logistic regression analysis (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100383), older age and worse ECOG PS were significantly associated with grade 3 hypoalbuminemia. Patients with a history of gastrectomy had substantially lower odds of grade 3 hypoalbuminemia than those without a history of gastrectomy. Patients with baseline grade 1 or 2 hypoalbuminemia had higher odds of developing grade 3 than those with baseline grade 0 hypoalbuminemia. Because no patient with prior gastrectomy or grade 0 baseline albumin decrease developed grade 3 hypoalbuminemia, the ORs for these variables could not be reliably estimated.

The median baseline albumin level was 4.0 g/dl in patients with grade 0 or 1, 3.7 g/dl in those with grade 2 nadirs, and 3.2 g/dl in patients with grade 3 nadirs (Table 2). The median decreases from baseline to nadir were 0.4, 1.1, and 1.7 g/dl, respectively (Table 2 and Supplementary Figure S3A, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100383). Therefore, patients who developed grade 3 hypoalbuminemia had lower baseline albumin levels and experienced a greater treatment-associated decline (Supplementary Figure S3B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100383). Exploratory analyses indicated that prior gastrectomy was associated with a smaller decline from baseline to the nadir, whereas ECOG PS 1-2 and a higher neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio were associated with a larger decline (Supplementary Figure S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100383). Older age and ECOG PS of 1-2 were associated with lower baseline albumin levels (Supplementary Figure S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100383).

Relationship with other on-target, off-tumor toxicities, and ascites

Grade 3 hypoalbuminemia was associated with other toxic effects consistent with on-target, off-tumor gastric injury (Figure 2A). Limb edema was present in all patients with grade 3 hypoalbuminemia, in contrast to 33% of those with grades 0-2 hypoalbuminemia. Anorexia occurred in 93% and 53% of patients, respectively. Endoscopic findings of gastritis were documented in 47% and 17% of the patients, respectively. Endoscopy was not mandated and was carried out at the discretion of the treating physician, mainly in patients with suspected zolbetuximab-related gastritis. Among eight patients with grade 3 hypoalbuminemia who underwent endoscopy, gastritis was documented in seven patients (87.5%). Increased ascites or new ascites around the nadir albumin were observed in 60% of patients with grade 3 hypoalbuminemia compared with 13% of those with grades 0-2 events. In case 1, after two cycles of zolbetuximab, the patient’s albumin level declined from baseline grade 1 to grade 3, accompanied by worsening anorexia (from grade 0 to grade 2), new-onset limb edema (from grade 0 to grade 2), and deterioration in ECOG PS (from PS 0 to PS 2; Figure 2B). Endoscopic examination revealed diffuse gastritis. Among the nine patients who underwent temporary interruption, all resumed zolbetuximab without dose reduction, and eight did not experience recurrent grade 3 hypoalbuminemia after reintroduction (Supplementary Figure S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100383). Among the nine patients with grade 3 hypoalbuminemia who had increased ascites and evaluable imaging at both the albumin nadir and recovery, five (56%) had a subsequent decrease in ascites as the serum albumin level improved (Supplementary Figures S6 and S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100383). The remaining four patients had no improvement in ascites: one had PD on computed tomography at the time hypoalbuminemia improved, two were subsequently confirmed to have PD at the next imaging assessment, and one had insufficient follow-up for repeat imaging. These observations suggest that at least a portion of ascites worsening after zolbetuximab initiation may reflect a hypoalbuminemia-related fluid shift rather than unequivocal peritoneal progression. ECOG PS worsened more frequently in patients with grade 3 hypoalbuminemia than in those with grades 0-2 events (Supplementary Figure S8, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100383).

Antitumor activity

Among the 36 patients with target lesions, the median depth of response was −30.1% in the grades 0-2 group and −39.1% in the grade 3 group (Figure 3A). The ORR was 51.7% in the grades 0-2 group and 71.4% in the grade 3 group (Figure 3B). For the 34 patients without target lesions, the proportions achieving non-CR/non-PD were 84.6% in the grades 0-2 group and 75.0% in the grade 3 group. Similar findings were observed when hypoalbuminemia was stratified into three severity categories: grade 0 or 1, grade 2, and grade 3 (Supplementary Figure S9, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100383). After a median follow-up of 10.9 months, the median PFS was 8.5 months (95% CI 6.0-10.2) in the grades 0-2 group and 5.1 months (95% CI 3.0-7.9) in the grade 3 group (HR 1.76, P = 0.11; Figure 3C). Most treatment discontinuations in patients with grade 3 hypoalbuminemia were due to PD rather than toxicity, with only one patient stopping treatment primarily because of an adverse event (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100383). Among patients who discontinued zolbetuximab-based treatment, 69% with grade 3 hypoalbuminemia received subsequent therapy, with most subsequent regimens being taxane-based. The median OS was 18.0 months (95% CI 12.0 to not reached) in the grades 0-2 group and not reached (95% CI 5.8 to not reached) in the grade 3 group (HR 1.16, P = 0.77; Figure 3C).

Discussion

In this retrospective study of 70 patients with HER2-negative CLDN18.2-positive advanced GEA treated with zolbetuximab plus chemotherapy, hypoalbuminemia emerged as a common, early, and clinically sequential toxicity. This study is the first to provide a detailed characterization of the longitudinal course of zolbetuximab-associated hypoalbuminemia, its co-occurrence with other on-target, off-tumor manifestations, its association with ascites dynamics, and its implications for response assessment in routine practice.

Several findings warrant further emphasis. Firstly, grade 3 hypoalbuminemia occurred in 21% of the patients, indicating that this was not a minor laboratory abnormality but a toxicity with clear clinical relevance. This proportion exceeds that reported in the pivotal phase III SPOTLIGHT and GLOW trials, in which grade 3-4 hypoalbuminemia occurred in 3.1%-3.9% of the patients.8, 9, 10 Severe hypoalbuminemia may be more apparent in routine practice than in trial populations enriched for physiological reserves and lower baseline nutritional vulnerability, and it may also be influenced by differences in ascertainment and attribution when it is not prespecified and centrally captured as an adverse event of special interest. In our cohort, clinically actionable correlates included lower baseline albumin levels, older age, poorer ECOG PS, and the absence of prior gastrectomy. Notably, no patient with prior gastrectomy (none with total gastrectomy) developed grade 3 hypoalbuminemia, a biologically plausible observation that is concordant with smaller reports suggesting the attenuation of stomach-specific toxicity after total gastrectomy.15 The grade of hypoalbuminemia is determined by both the pretreatment albumin level and the magnitude of the treatment-associated decline. Exploratory analyses suggested that among older patients, the risk was largely explained by a lower baseline albumin reserve rather than an exaggerated treatment-related decline, whereas patients with poor ECOG PS appeared to have both lower baseline levels and a larger on-treatment decrement. Collectively, surgical history, baseline nutritional status, and ECOG PS may help identify patients at increased risk of clinically significant hypoalbuminemia before treatment initiation.

Secondly, zolbetuximab-associated hypoalbuminemia exhibited a clear temporal pattern, typically manifesting early and diminishing over time, mirroring the cyclical nature of other zolbetuximab-induced adverse effects such as nausea, vomiting, and gastritis.13,19 Severe hypoalbuminemia seldom occurred in isolation, with most patients experiencing grade 3 hypoalbuminemia also presenting with limb edema, anorexia, and gastritis, putting them at risk of deteriorating ECOG PS. This clustering of symptoms supports the hypothesis that zolbetuximab binds to CLDN18.2 expressed in normal gastric mucosa, resulting in on-target, off-tumor mucosal injury that, in some cases, leads to clinically significant protein loss and subsequent edema. This mechanism offers a biologically plausible rationale for the observed protective association with prior gastrectomy. Although albumin levels improved after temporary interruption of zolbetuximab in most patients, this retrospective analysis with a small sample size cannot establish that treatment interruption itself accelerated recovery from hypoalbuminemia. However, zolbetuximab was usually interrupted in patients who had not only severe hypoalbuminemia but also concurrent clinically meaningful symptoms, including edema, anorexia, epigastric symptoms, fatigue, abdominal distension, or worsening ECOG PS. Therefore, temporary interruption may be a reasonable pragmatic option for patients with hypoalbuminemia accompanied by clinically significant symptoms, rather than a universally required intervention for all patients with grade 3 hypoalbuminemia. Furthermore, the majority of patients who resumed zolbetuximab treatment after a temporary interruption did not experience a recurrence of grade 3 hypoalbuminemia, suggesting that treatment interruption during severe symptomatic toxicity may allow for recovery without necessarily precluding successful rechallenge.

Thirdly, grade 3 hypoalbuminemia was not clearly linked to antitumor activity. However, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence of the absence of a prognostic effect, given the small number of grade 3 events, the limited number of survival events, the post-treatment definition of hypoalbuminemia, and baseline imbalances in age, ECOG PS, and serum albumin levels. Therefore, the present dataset is insufficient to determine whether severe hypoalbuminemia independently affects PFS or OS. This question should be addressed in larger, adequately powered cohorts. The implications for response evaluation are significant as CLDN18.2-positive GEA often presents with diffuse-type characteristics and peritoneal dissemination.27, 28, 29, 30 Given the challenges in assessing peritoneal disease radiographically, changes in ascites can heavily influence clinical judgments on disease progression.31 In this cohort, diffuse-type histology, a characteristic feature of CLDN18.2-positive GEA, was common (78.6%), as were radiographic peritoneal metastases (77.1%) and baseline ascites (55.7%). All nine patients with increased ascites had ascites and/or peritoneal metastases at baseline. Because malignant progression and treatment-related fluid retention could not be reliably distinguished in this context, ascitic fluid analysis, including cytologic examination, was not carried out. Our results suggest that the emergence or worsening of ascites shortly after zolbetuximab initiation should not be automatically construed as tumor progression. When ascites elevation coincides with hypoalbuminemia, along with symptoms such as edema, anorexia, or gastritis, potential treatment-related factors should be considered. Since ascites often resolves as albumin levels improve, repeat imaging post-biochemical recovery may aid in distinguishing temporary fluid accumulation from genuine progression in patients who can safely be observed. This differentiation is crucial as prematurely halting an effective regimen based solely on early ascites changes could deprive certain patients of treatment benefits. A decision-flow algorithm for monitoring and managing zolbetuximab-associated hypoalbuminemia, developed based on the findings of this study, may be useful in routine clinical practice (Figure 4).

Practical decision-flow algorithm for monitoring and management of zolbetuximab-associated hypoalbuminemia. This algorithm outlines a pragmatic approach to the early recognition and management of hypoalbuminemia during zolbetuximab-containing therapy. Baseline assessment should include serum albumin, ECOG PS, body weight, oral intake, edema, and ascites or peritoneal metastasis. Patients with low baseline albumin, ECOG PS ≥1, older age, or no prior gastrectomy may require closer surveillance. Because clinically relevant hypoalbuminemia occurs mainly during the first three treatment cycles, serum albumin should be measured on day 1 of each cycle. Repeat testing within 7-14 days may be considered for patients at higher risk or for those with a rapid decline in albumin. Clinical monitoring should include body weight, oral intake, anorexia, peripheral edema, abdominal distension, gastrointestinal symptoms, and ECOG PS. Management should be guided by both the severity of hypoalbuminemia and the associated symptom burden. Grades 0-1 hypoalbuminemia, or asymptomatic hypoalbuminemia, can generally be managed with routine monitoring. Grades 2-3 hypoalbuminemia without clinically meaningful symptoms should prompt closer monitoring and supportive care, including optimization of antiemetic therapy, nutritional support, and treatment of gastritis-like symptoms. When grades 2-3 hypoalbuminemia is accompanied by clinically meaningful symptoms or grade 4 hypoalbuminemia, temporary interruption of zolbetuximab should be considered, together with supportive care and at least weekly albumin monitoring until recovery. New or worsening ascites during the albumin nadir should be interpreted cautiously, particularly when measurable tumor lesions are stable or shrinking and ascites is accompanied by edema, anorexia, or other features of hypoalbuminemia. In this setting, repeat CT after albumin recovery may help distinguish transient hypoalbuminemia-related fluid accumulation from true peritoneal progression. Zolbetuximab may be resumed after recovery to grade ≤2 and clinical stabilization. Recurrent or refractory severe toxicity, or persistent inability to maintain oral intake, should prompt consideration of prolonged treatment interruption, including interruption of chemotherapy when clinically appropriate. Treatment decisions should ultimately be individualized according to the patient’s psychological and physical condition, treatment goals, and overall risk–benefit profile. CT, computed tomography; ECOG PS, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status.

Case reports describing severe gastritis with prolonged gastrointestinal symptoms and hypertrophic or foveolar mucosal changes further support mucosal barrier disruption as a plausible route for albumin loss.20,32 A recent report described delayed-onset gastritis with protein-losing gastroenteropathy, severe hypogammaglobulinemia, and pathological findings suggestive of an IgA-vasculitis–like process, raising the possibility that inflammation, vascular permeability, or immune-mediated contributions may occur in selected cases.22 Exposure-related effects may also contribute to population pharmacokinetics, and exposure–response analyses have associated higher zolbetuximab exposure with more frequent gastrointestinal toxicity, including nausea and vomiting.33 Although these data do not establish a specific mechanism of hypoalbuminemia, they are consistent with the broader concept that early gastric toxicities are, at least in part, exposure-dependent. A more precise mechanistic understanding, particularly regarding gastric mucosal injury, protein leakage, and host susceptibility, may enable the development of rational strategies for the prevention, early diagnosis, and treatment of this on-target off-tumor toxicity.

This study has several limitations. It was a retrospective study conducted at a single high-volume cancer center, so the findings may not be fully generalizable to other practice settings. Treatment interruption and resumption were not mandated by the protocol, leading to variations in supportive care and clinical decision-making. Radiographic assessments were not conducted at prespecified intervals, resulting in varied timing of ascites evaluation, particularly around the albumin nadir, potentially introducing variability in the apparent association between albumin decline and ascites progression. Moreover, the association between hypoalbuminemia and ascites should be interpreted cautiously due to the common occurrence of peritoneal metastasis and baseline ascites in this cohort, which could confound radiographic assessments. Central radiologic review and standardized quantitative ascites measurements were not carried out, and the distinction between hypoalbuminemia-related fluid accumulation and actual peritoneal progression remains partly inferential. Endoscopic assessments are not standard practice, and the observed incidence of gastritis may have been influenced by selection bias. Finally, the efficacy analyses were exploratory and might have been underpowered, especially for survival endpoints. However, the consistent temporal pattern, clinical clustering, ascites association, and biological coherence with emerging literature support these central conclusions.

Conclusion

Zolbetuximab-associated hypoalbuminemia was prevalent, manifested early, and escalated to grade 3 severity in about one-fifth of the patients in this real-world cohort. It frequently presents alongside edema, anorexia, and gastritis, highlighting the importance of early albumin monitoring and comprehensive evaluation of other on-target, off-tumor toxicities. The onset of new or worsening ascites following treatment initiation should not be immediately construed as disease progression but rather as a potential consequence of treatment-related albumin depletion. Hypoalbuminemia often shows improvement over time, is typically manageable with supportive care and temporary treatment interruptions, and is not associated with an apparent antitumor efficacy. In cases of clinically significant toxicity, a temporary pause in zolbetuximab therapy should be contemplated before considering permanent discontinuation, particularly given the feasibility of rechallenge in this cohort. Proactive identification and management of these adverse events can optimize the clinical outcomes associated with zolbetuximab.
