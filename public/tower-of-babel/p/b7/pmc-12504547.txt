Growth period of Kinosternon scorpioides changes the ovary morphology by follicular differentiation and structural reorganization
Authors: Brenda Stefany dos Santos Braga, Maria Luiza Cunha e Souza-Ferreira, Verônica Oliveira-Bahia, Maria Auxiliadora Ferreira, José Ribamar Marques, Diva Guimarães
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Knowledge of gonadal morphophysiology is essential for implementing techniques that can improve reproduction, production, and conservation. This study described the ovarian morphology and oocyte maturation of Kinosternon scorpioides during three growth stages: hatchlings, juveniles, and adults. Ovaries from 20 females were analyzed across these stages. The ovarian follicles were classified into four stages: hatchlings, juveniles and adults. The ovarian follicles were classified into four stages: stage I, characterized by the presence of the granulosa layer; stage II, which showed vesicles in the cytoplasm, in addition to the zona pellucida; stage III, where yolk platelets were observed at the periphery of the oocyte; and stage IV, in which the quantity of yolk platelets increased. In hatchlings, small follicles, covered by a flattened layer of follicular cells (stage I), as well as oogonia, were observed. In juveniles, pre-vitellogenic follicles (stages I and II) were identified, suggesting the onset of puberty, alongside the presence of oogonia. In adult females, larger follicles (stages III and IV) were found, containing yolk, oogonia, corpus luteum, and evidence of follicular atresia. This study revealed distinct morphological and functional changes from hatchlings to adults, with follicular maturation involving significant alterations in structures related to nutrition, protection, and steroidogenesis. These findings provide valuable insights into reproductive mechanisms, supporting improved management strategies and conservation efforts for this species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-91220-8.

Introduction

Chelonians play essential ecological roles in terrestrial, freshwater, and marine ecosystems due to their involvement in nutrient cycling, seed dispersal, and food webs, and the Amazon is a major habitat for these animals1. Furthermore, they hold socio-economic significance for various human populations in the region2–4. Despite this, habitat loss, overhunting, and climate change threaten with extinction more than half of the species within this taxon5,6.

Concerning chelonian conservation, both in situ and ex situ management strategies have been implemented to maintain populations. Among the techniques used, germplasm banks make it possible to preserve genetic resources, such as germ cells7. However, the application of this tool is hindered by a lack of information on the reproductive biology of wildlife6. In particular, there is insufficient knowledge regarding oocyte and follicle maturation during the growth period. These and other techniques enhance the efficiency of production systems and the recovery of endangered species8,9.

The reproductive system of female chelonians consists of a pair of ovaries and oviducts10. The ovaries are located dorsally to the kidneys, in the caudal part of the coelomic cavity11. The oviducts are contorted and elongated, similar in size and equally functional, extending from the vicinity of each ovary to the lateral wall of the cloaca10,12,13. The ovarian cortex is covered by a simple epithelium and contains follicles at various stages of development, atretic follicles, and germinal beds. The latter are defined regions near the ovarian epithelium, containing oogonia and early oocytes.

At the onset of puberty, immature ovarian follicles are observed in the process of maturation or vitellogenesis, gradually increasing in size until they reach maturity14,15. Based on this mechanism, maturation involves the gradual incorporation of yolk into the ovarian follicle, with the yolk providing proteins and lipids essential for embryo nutrition16,17. The liver plays a crucial role in yolk formation by producing vitellogenin (VGT), a yolk precursor glycoprotein18. VGT is secreted into the bloodstream in response to hormonal stimulation19. These substances are transported to the oocytes, where they are incorporated into the yolk20. Additionally, two pathways for yolk incorporation have been described in chelonian oocytes: the extrinsic route (via the bloodstream)21 and the intrinsic route (synthesis of yolk by organelles using precursor molecules)22.

Kinosternon scorpioides, the scorpion mud turtle, represents an important food resource in the Amazon and is being overexploited3. The incubation period of its eggs lasts, on average, 129 days23,24. The Kinosternidae family, to which this species belongs, exhibits temperature-dependent sex determination, with the pivotal temperature for species of the genus Kinosternon established at 27.5 °C25. However, it remains unclear at what point in the incubation process the thermosensitive period occurs, or what specific mechanisms determine sex.

Among its biological aspects, it is important to note that research on the morphology of the reproductive system is scarce, particularly studies that describe the microscopic and ultrastructural characterization of the ovary12,13. Understanding the morphology of the K. scorpioides ovary allows for a better understanding of the mechanisms involved in oocyte production in this species, which could facilitate advancements in reproductive technologies. Therefore, the aim of this study was to identify and describe the ovarian morphology (structural and ultrastructural) during the growth period in K. scorpioides, and to enhance the understanding of the cellular features of histology and cytology in developing follicles of Testudines, particularly within the Kinosternidae family.

Results

Ovarian anatomy

The ovaries of K. scorpioides showed similar syntopy across the different growth periods (hatchlings, juveniles, and adults; (Fig. 1a,e,i). The gonadal pairs were located near the ventromedial region of the kidneys, adjacent to the renal hilum. They were in medial contact with the colon and rectum, lateral to the upper portion of the oviducts, and dorsally in contact with the renal cortex (Supplementary Fig. S1 online). The ovaries were attached to the dorsal part of the coelomic cavity, ventrally to the urinary vesicle, and cranially to the lungs, small intestine, liver, and stomach, adhering to the abdominal wall. Ovarian weight values were as follows: < 0.001 in hatchlings, an average of 0.03 ± 0.02 g in juveniles and an average of 4.85 ± 1.65 g in adults. A significant difference in ovarian weight was observed between juveniles and adults (U = 0.00; df = 2; p = 0.003).

Small follicles of uniform size were observed on the ovarian surface of hatchlings and juveniles (Fig. 1b,f). In adult females, the ovaries appeared yellow and had an irregular outline, resembling a “bunch of grapes” due to the surface projection of the follicles, which varied in size (Fig. 1j).

Ovarian morphology

Two distinct regions were observed in the hatchlings: the ovarian cortex, predominantly filled with oogonia, and the medulla (Fig. 1c). In juvenile and adult females, there was little difference in the delineation of the cortical and medullary regions (Fig. 1g,k). The ovaries of adult females were more heterogeneous (Fig. 1k), as they contained follicles at various stages of development (the largest follicles had an average diameter of 13.02 mm; Fig. 1h), germinal beds corpus luteum, atretic follicles and lacunae (Fig. 1k). The lacunae were irregular in shape and scattered throughout the stroma surrounding the follicles, containing an amorphous substance. The ovaries of K. scorpioides were covered by a simple cuboidal epithelium, and the stroma was composed of loose connective tissue that was highly vascularized (Fig. 1d,h,l).

Follicular stages

Diagrams of the follicular stages were designed (Figs. 2a and 3a). The K. scorpioides had germinal beds in the cortical region, which contained oogonia and non-germ cells, organized in small groups surrounded by a thin layer of connective tissue (Fig. 2b,c). In K. scorpioides hatchlings, oogonia predominated and were characterized as spherical with a large, densely stained nucleus compared to the homogeneous cytoplasm. Oogonia with condensed nuclei were grouped inside the germinal beds (Fig. 2c), followed by oogonia with less condensed chromatin and apparent division (Fig. 2d) (Median = 8.12 μm; IQR: 6.95–9.72). In some compartments, pre-vitellogenic follicles at stage were also observed.

The adults exhibited all stages, with oogonia and stage I follicles inside the germinal beds. Additionally, corpus luteum and follicular atresia were observed.

Pre-vitellogenic follicles

Stage I (Median = 17.52 μm; IQR: 13.0–54.1): The nucleus was spherical and centralized, with dense chromatin and more than one nucleolus, which were peripheral and varied in size. The cytoplasm was homogeneous (Fig. 2e), and in some samples, the vitelline nucleus was observed adjacent to the cell nucleus. Flattened to cuboidal cells were noted in the granulosa layer (Fig. 2f,g).

Stage II (Median = 229.11 μm; IQR: 162.0–384.0): The nucleus contained more than one nucleolus (Fig. 2h). A basal membrane was observed adjacent to the granulosa layer (Fig. 2i), and zona pellucida (Fig. 2j) was also visible, later showing two distinct regions: one internally striated and the other externally hyaline (Fig. 2k). The cytoplasm appeared granular, and a narrow band of vesicles was observed in the peripheral region, which then moved towards the central region (Fig. 2l). The external surface of the pre-vitellogenic ovarian follicle was irregular, and the theca forms a capsule around the follicle and was composed of collagenous fibres (Fig. 2m,n).

Vitellogenic follicles

Stage III (Median = 1391.67 μm; IQR: 1246.0–1518.0): The vesicles increased in size as they approached the central region, which appeared homogeneous (Fig. 3b). In the peripheral cytoplasm, yolk platelets were observed inside the vesicles (Fig. 3c). Subsequently, these yolk platelets spread toward the central region of the cell.

Analysis of the ultrastructures revealed electrodense yolk particles with well-defined borders in the peripheral region of the ooplasm, which increased in size as they advanced toward the center (Fig. 3f). Concomitantly, vesicles containing electrodense material were observed, characterized as yolk platelets with irregular borders and appearance, some vesicles were observed to be in the process of fusion (Fig. 3f). On the periphery of the oocyte, membrane projections adjacent to the microvilli of the zona pellucida were visible, along with a large number of pinocytic vesicles (Fig. 3g). The zona pellucida was observed in its first region, the radiata, filled with microvilli (Fig. 3g). Between these structures and the membrane projections of the oocyte, there were spaces, some containing laminae that appeared to cluster together to integrate the vesicles (Fig. 3g). The second region of the zona pellucida was hyaline, located closer to the granulosa layer, and filled with filamentous material and occasional vesicles containing low-density particles (Fig. 3h).

The granulosa cells exhibited projections toward the zona pellucida (Fig. 3h). They were interconnected by desmosomes, and intercellular spaces were observed between them. Mitochondria were the most common organelles, and were typically elongated or circular in shape, well condensed (Fig. 3i). These cells contained irregular nuclei with dense and dispersed chromatin, and 1 or 2 nucleoli (Fig. 3j).

Below the granulosa, there was a thick basal membrane separating it from the theca (Fig. 3k). The theca, in turn, had two subdivisions: the inner theca, which contained a few layers of collagen fibers, microfilaments, and flattened cells (Fig. 3c,l); and the outer theca, which was composed of fibers, microfilaments, rare cells, and an irregular connective tissue capsule surrounding the entire structure.

Stage IV (Median = 1700.38 μm; IQR: 1454.0–1972.0): The yolk platelets filled most of the ooplasm (Fig. 3d). These yolk platelets were round (Fig. 3e). The external theca contained numerous lacunae and fibers (Fig. 3e).

The corpora lutea (Fig. 4a) contained a thick external theca and abundant luteinized tissue, with two distinct cell types: (1) larger cells with an eosinophilic cytoplasm and a spherical nucleus; (2) smaller cells with a slightly basophilic cytoplasm and more elongated nuclei (Fig. 4b). Atresic follicles (Fig. 4c) were observed in both pre-vitellogenic and vitellogenic follicles, particularly in adult animals. These follicles initially showed an irregular zona pellucida, which folded towards the interior of the oocyte. This was followed by degeneration of the zona pellucida, hypertrophy of the theca, and degeneration of both granulosa cells and oocyte.

Discussion

In this study, the structure, ultrastructure, morphometry, and ovarian follicular maturation were characterized during the growth periods of the animals (hatchling, juvenile, and adult). The results obtained in this study demonstrated morphological similarities between the ovaries of K. scorpioides hatchlings and those of the turtles Trachemys dorbignyi, Podocnemis expansa, Podocnemis unifilis26–28, as observed, the ovaries of this group are located under the ventromedial surface of the mesonephric kidneys, in the posterior portion of the coelomic cavity26–28. They exhibit an irregular surface and the presence of small follicles. This characteristic can be used to differentiate females from males, since chelonian hatchlings do not show distinct sexual dimorphism29,30, despite efforts to diagnose them31.

Adult individuals of K. scorpioides had heavier ovaries than juveniles. The increased weight observed in the ovaries of adults is attributed to the prominent presence of vitellogenic follicles, which are absent in juveniles. Given the presence of pre-vitellogenic follicles in stage II, it can be inferred that the gonads of the juvenile animals analyzed in this study represent individuals entering puberty. Previous studies have suggested that sexual maturity in K. scorpioides under captivity occurs between 2.8 and 4 years of age, with a carapace length of approximately 14 cm32. Therefore, the results observed in this study support this finding, indicating that puberty occurs around 36 months of age. It is important to note that the optimal conditions established in captivity may contribute to the earlier onset of puberty in these animals33.

The ovarian morphology of adult K. scorpioides observed in this study was consistent with the pattern found in other reptiles18. The gonads were observed as paired organs, located near the ventromedial region of the kidneys, and were filled with yellow structures12,13,34. In this species, it has been shown that the ovarian masses are asymmetrical, with the right ovary containing a greater number of follicles compared to the left13.

In juvenile and adult individuals, the stroma was reduced, and there was little demarcation between the cortical and medullary regions. These observations are consistent with those reported in Eretmochelys imbricata, where ovarian follicles, during their growth and maturation, begin to occupy the entire ovary, not being confined to the cortical region, which leads to a redistribution of ovarian componentes20,35. As described in the literature, the ovarian stroma contains follicles at various stages of development, as well as lacunae and collagen fibers13,20.

The cortex contains germinal beds, which are clusters of germ cells, including oogonia and stage I oocytes. Germinal beds have also been observed in the lizard Hemidactylus mabouia36, in limited quantities that vary between specimens37,38. In turtles, the number of these beds also differs39. These structures are not proportional to the size of the clutches20 and have a function closely linked to oogenesis and the development of follicles during the reproductive seasons22.

The oogonia in K. scorpioides, as in the turtles Pseudemys scripta elegans and P. sinensis, were observed in the cortical region throughout the growth period (hatchlings, juveniles and adults), being restricted to the germinal beds22,40. During oogenesis, oocyte nuclei were observed with fibrillar chromatin and few peripheral nucleoli. This arrangement suggests that RNA transcription is related to the synthesis of ribonucleoproteins essential for the growth and maturation of the oocyte40.

The structure of the oocyte shows a similar pattern across other tetrapod groups, where it is associated with the adjacent layers: zona pellucida, granulosa, basal lamina, and thecae22,41. Few variations have been identified that deviate from this general pattern42,43. These variations, although rare, are often related to specific adaptations for distinct reproductive strategies, such as variations in the thickness of the layers, the presence of subdivisions in the granulosa cells36,44,45, or specializations of the zona pellucida that facilitate interactions with the oocyte46,47. Despite these differences, the basic structural pattern reflects the evolutionary conservation of fundamental reproductive mechanisms in tetrapods.

The results showed that oocytes in vitellogenesis contained yolk platelets48–50. It can be inferred that these substances originate from vesicles present in the hyaline layer of the zona pellucida. These findings suggest that the substances migrate extrinsically to the follicle. The migration of percussive particles via external vesicles has also been reported in amphibians and fish22,41,50,51. In the turtle Pseudemys sinensis, the presence of yolk platelets from both extrinsic and intrinsic routes to the follicle has been reported22.

The zona pellucida consists of an extracellular matrix that surrounds the oocyte and is composed of glycoproteins. This structure has two layers in Testudines, as observed in this study (radiata and hyaline layers), which are common among tetrapods and responsible for protecting the oocyte and controlling fertilization. In organisms with eggs protected by a shell, the zona pellucida may be thinner, as seen in the turtle P. sinensis at 7.47 μm22, and in K. scorpioides. However, it may be thicker due to the reproductive specificities required by the environment in which these eggs are laid. For example, in mammals, the oocyte remains surrounded by the zona pellucida until the blastocyst implantation phase46, while in pelagic fish, it is necessary to ensure abrasion to the substrates52.

This study observed that the cells of the granulosa layer were adjacent to the zona pellucida during its growth, had a flattened to cubic shape, and formed a single layer with an irregular nucleus. This arrangement is shared among birds, crocodilians, and Testudines14,22. These cells perform vitellogenic, steroidogenic, and nutritional functions36,49,53. However, in some squamates, a different morphology was observed, with three distinct layers containing the following types of cells: (1) small, (2) intermediate and (3) pyriform53. In Scincus mitranus lizards, after the onset of vitellogenesis, these layers merged into a single layer53. In this species, the pyriform layer not only exhibited characteristics of steroid-producing cells, typical of granulosa cells, but also showed a high metabolic rate, nuclear activity, and extensive development of microvilli or protoplasmic prolongations, these structures penetrate the zona pellucida and connect to the oocyte via intercellular bridges36,53, and were not seen in K. scorpioides, suggesting that the reproductive metabolism of this species may not require mechanisms so specialized for the direct transport of nutrients, due to a longer reproductive cycle and eggs with more robust yolk reserves54, allowing a more gradual development process.

The thecae were observed in two distinct layers: the inner layer consisting of flattened cells and the outer layer containing many fibers, along with an irregular capsule surrounding the entire follicle. This morphology is associated with follicular growth, where the observed cells are likely the producers of steroid hormones (estrogen and progesterone)14,55. In contrast, some squamate lizards may possess an undifferentiated capsule, the function of which remains nuclear36,44,56. This suggests that both the differentiated theca and the undifferentiated capsule play essential roles in supporting follicular growth and hormone production, highlighting the evolutionary diversity of reproductive strategies across species.

In chelonians and crocodilians, the following functions have been associated with the internal thecae: hormone production, marked by the presence of enlarged cells with smooth endoplasmic reticulum, and support, due to the higher presence of fibers14,18,57. Their secretory activity is well known in mammals and fish, and this characteristic is well conserved across tetrapods18,58. However, further studies are needed to clarify the endocrine functions potentially present in the granulosa and inner theca layers of K. scorpioides ovarian follicles.

The corpora lutea observed here are the result of the ovulation process and are responsible for the synthesis of progesterone in response to luteinizing hormone59–61. The increase in progesterone levels decreases muscle contractility in the oviduct, thereby retaining the eggs for the proper production of albumin, deposition of membranes, and the eggshell62–64.

Follicular atresia was evident in adult animals and observed in both pre-vitellogenic and vitellogenic follicles. This process is related to follicular dominance, limiting litter size, and the regression of ovarian structures65–67. In this study, atresia was more evident in vitellogenic follicles, observed at both early and late stages, possibly related to the reproductive cycle of the animals and the process of follicular hierarchization14,22,68,69.

Morphometric analysis showed that variations in the morphology of the structures followed follicular growth, with no significant differences across the animals’ growth periods. The diameter of the oocytes showed significant differences between the stages analyzed, being greatest at stage IV. A similar growth pattern was observed for the layers of the thecae, which were thicker during the final stage studied. This is related to follicular development, with the inner theca likely responsible for hormone synthesis14,43.

The nuclear diameter showed a reduction between stages III and IV. This pattern may be associated with the accelerated growth of the follicle, which accumulates large amounts of yolk, leading to the condensation of the nucleus in the animal pole region of telolecithal eggs20,35. The granulosa layers and zona pellucida also exhibited similar growth, followed by a decrease in the final stage. This phenomenon can be attributed to oocyte growth, which likely impacts the analyzed layers, causing their thinning22,36.

Conclusion

This study investigated the structure, ultrastructure, morphometry, and follicular maturation of the ovaries in Kinosternon scorpioides across different growth stages (hatchling, juvenile, and adult). The ovaries, located ventromedially to the kidneys, exhibited distinct morphological and functional characteristics depending on the developmental stage. In hatchlings individuals, the ovaries primarily consisted of oogonia and Stage I follicles. In juveniles, structural redistribution led to a reduced distinction between cortex and medulla, while pre-vitellogenic Stage II follicles became evident. In adults, the ovaries displayed significant heterogeneity, containing follicles at various maturation stages, corpora lutea, and signs of follicular atresia.

Follicular maturation was accompanied by notable changes in the zona pellucida, granulosa, and thecae—structures integral to nutrition, protection, and steroidogenesis. Follicular atresia was more prominent in adults, reflecting the hierarchy of follicles and the regulation of litter size. Morphometric analyses revealed that oocyte growth and the development of surrounding layers were most pronounced during the final stages of maturation, highlighting conserved and adaptive evolutionary patterns in the species’ reproductive mechanisms. Insights into ovarian follicle maturation and their association with reproductive events can inform management strategies for this chelonian. These findings are especially relevant for optimizing the formation of breeding groups and improving conservation efforts.

Materials and methods

Scientific breeding

Kinosternon scorpioides belonged to the scientific breeding facility of the Brazilian Agricultural Research Corporation (LO 7310/2014-SEMAS-PA), located on the island of Marajó-Salvaterra, Pará, Brazil (48° 33′ 23.32″ W and 0° 42′ 25.26″ S).

The breeding facility (200 m2) was divided into the following sectors: nursery, recreate and reproduction24. The young animals (up to 12 months old, 50 g) were kept in the nursery area, the juveniles (up to 36 months old, 51–250 g) in the rearing area, and the adult animals (from 36 months old, over 250 g) in the reproduction area24. The animals were fed commercial carnivorous fish feed (32% crude protein) three times a week, based on 1% of their live weight.

Sample analysis

The ovaries of 20 animals (seven hatchlings, six juveniles, and seven adults) were dissected, weighed, and analyzed. They were then fixed in Bouin’s solution, preserved in 70% alcohol, and subjected to routine histological techniques, including dehydration in increasing concentrations of ethanol, infiltration, and paraffin embedding. Subsequently, they were sectioned to a thickness of 5 μm, stained with Hematoxylin-Eosin, and analyzed under a light microscope.

The ovarian follicles were classified according to the characteristics adopted for the Pelodiscus sinensis species as follows22: stage I and II pre-vitellogenic follicles; stage III and IV vitellogenic follicles. For each follicular type, the nucleus, cytoplasm, zona pellucida, granulosa layer, and theca layers (internal and external) were observed.

To analyze the follicles using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), gonad fragments were fixed in Karnovsky’s solution (4% paraformaldehyde, 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer, pH 7.2) for 24 h. The fragments were then washed in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.2) and post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.2) for 2 h. For SEM, the fragments were dehydrated in an increasing ethanol series, critical point dried using CO2, coated with 10 nm thick gold, and examined using a LEO 1430 SEM. For TEM, the samples were dehydrated in an increasing acetone series, infiltrated, and embedded in Epon 812. Ultra-thin sections were made with an ultramicrotome, contrasted with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and analyzed using a CLARA high-resolution SEM (TESCAN, 2021).

Morphometric analysis

A total of 200 follicles from each stage (I, II, III, IV) were analyzed, resulting in a total of 800 structures examined throughout the animals’ growth period. Regarding oocytes, only cells with an apparent nucleus were measured. The average length of two perpendicular axes was considered for these measurements. Additionally, the thickness of the zona pellucida, granulosa layer, and theca layers (internal and external) were observed in the same follicles. Each of these layers was measured four times.

All histological sections were analyzed, measured, and photographed using a light microscope (NIKON Eclipse Ci) coupled with a digital camera (NIKON DS-Ri1), equipped with NIS-Elements BR4.00.07 software. Follicles with the largest diameters were measured using a manual caliper for macroscopic analysis (0.02 mm precision).

Statistical analysis

The data were organized and plotted in electronic spreadsheets using Microsoft Excel software. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to analyze differences in mean ovarian weights according to growth period (juveniles and adults), as the data did not meet normality assumptions. The descriptive values related to oocyte diameter were represented by the median and the interquartile range (IQR). To analyze oocyte development during the animal’s growth period, the data were submitted to Permutation Multivariate Analysis of Variance—PERMANOVA70, with the distribution matrix created using the Euclidean method and 9999 permutations. For this analysis, oocyte and nucleus diameters, zona pellucida thickness, granulosa layer, and theca layers were considered as response variables, while follicle stage and growth period were treated as predictor variables. The adonis2 function implemented in the Vegan package71 was used for this procedure. All statistical analyses were carried out in the R 4.3.2 environment72.
