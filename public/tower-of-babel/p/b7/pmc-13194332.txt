An exploratory multi-biomarker panel including fecal calprotectin, Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor, Fibroblast-Growth Factor-21 and irisin shows poor diagnostic accuracy for detecting probable sarcopenia in community-dwelling older persons
Authors: Laurence Lapauw, Lode Vermeiren, Laura Vercauteren, Nadjia Amini, Lisa Peeters, Sebastiaan Dalle, Katrien Koppo, Muriel Derrien, Jolan Dupont, Jeroen Raes, Evelien Gielen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsSerum and fecal biological markers that reflect underlying drivers of sarcopenia, may aid in novel predictive or diagnostic tools for sarcopenia.AimsTo explore the potential of five specific myokines and fecal calprotectin (fCPT) as biomarkers for diagnosing the presence of at least probable sarcopenia in community-dwelling individuals aged ≥ 65 years.MethodsThis cross-sectional, exploratory study included 156 participants (median age 73y), of whom 59 (20♂, 39♀) had at least probable sarcopenia per EWGSOP2 criteria, and 97 (33♂, 64♀) controls. Myostatin, irisin, Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF), Insulin-like Growth Factor-1 (IGF-1), Fibroblast Growth Factor-21 (FGF-21), and fCPT were measured using Enzyme-Linked ImmunoSorbent Assay (ELISA). Muscle mass and function were assessed via appendicular lean mass (ALM), hand grip strength (HGS), 5-time chair stand test (CST), gait speed, and the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB). Linear and logistic regressions analyses were performed. Diagnostic accuracy was evaluated using Area’s Under the Curve (AUC).ResultsParticipants with fCPT < 50 µg/g walked faster than those with fCPT ≥ 200 µg/g. Higher fCPT associated with slower gait speed (β: -0.003; 95% CI [-0.001; -0.00002]), while IGF-1 associated with higher HGS (β: 0.077; 95% CI [0.039; 0.116]). BDNF alone diagnosed at least having probable sarcopenia (AUC: 0.63; sensitivity 53%, specificity 71%). A multi-biomarker panel comprising BDNF, irisin, FGF-21 and fCPT modestly improved accuracy (AUC: 0.71; sensitivity 64%, specificity 77%), but this improvement was not statistically significant compared with the single biomarkers BDNF (p = 0.330) and fCPT (p = 0.180).DiscussionThe multi-biomarker panel significantly diagnosed at least having probable sarcopenia, but with high false negative (36%) and positive (23%) rates, respectively.ConclusionThe clinical relevance of this specific multi-biomarker panel may be limited due to moderate diagnostic accuracy. Validation in larger cohorts is needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-026-03368-6.Keywords: Sarcopenia, Older adults, Fecal calprotectin, Myokines, Biomarkers

Introduction

Sarcopenia, a progressive skeletal muscle disorder, increases the risk of falls, fractures, and mortality in older adults [1–3]. Timely and accurate diagnosis is essential to prevent these outcomes. Clinically, diagnosis relies on assessing muscle strength, quantity or quality, and physical performance. However, limited access to tools - like Dual X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA) for muscle mass estimation - in office-based settings has prompted interest in blood-based biomarkers for staging. Prior studies have shown that biochemical markers (e.g. albumin) can discriminate stages of sarcopenia secondary to malignancy [4], and may help monitor treatment response [5].

Myokines - proteins released by contracting myocytes - act via autocrine, paracrine or endocrine pathways and may reflect sarcopenia’s multifactorial pathophysiology. Cross-sectional studies have priorly linked myostatin and irisin to muscle mass, strength and sarcopenia [6, 7]. Moreover, prior research combining myokines with non-myokine biomarkers (e.g., mitochondrial or NMJ markers) has shown to improve diagnostic accuracy in Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) and Parkinson’s disease [8, 9].

Yet, a first remaining research gap is that previous multi-biomarker studies insufficiently selected biomarkers reflective of sarcopenia’s multiple etiological processes [10, 11], such as chronic inflammation, anabolic resistance, mitochondrial stress, and NMJ alterations. Moreover, previous research has focused on (non-)myokines in sarcopenia secondary to comorbidities (e.g., Parkinson’s, COPD) [8, 9], rather than primary sarcopenia, diagnosed when aging is the sole factor. Although implication of a perturbed gut microbiota (GM) composition is increasingly evidenced in sarcopenia pathogenesis [12], gut-associated biomarkers for primary sarcopenia remain unexplored. Fecal calprotectin (fCPT), a validated intestinal inflammatory marker for Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD), correlates with perturbed GM. Given the overlap between GM disruption and high sarcopenia prevalence (24.4%–68.3%) in IBD, fCPT may serve as a surrogate marker for gut dysbiosis in sarcopenia. However, its value in primary sarcopenia has not been investigated. Additionally, studies on sarcopenia biomarkers rarely report diagnostic performance below ‘moderate’ (≥ 70% sensitivity/specificity) [13], raising the possibility of publication bias due to underreporting of negative findings.

This paper aimed to address aforementioned gaps by investigating a novel (multi-)biomarker in community-dwelling older adults with probable, confirmed or severe sarcopenia. We selected our multi-biomarker panel carefully to represent complementary biological axes that may drive development of (primary) sarcopenia. Specifically, we assessed fCPT - as a surrogate marker of GM dysbiosis, which is one of the hallmarks of aging [14] - alongside five serum myokines spanning anabolic, catabolic, stress-adaptive, and neurotrophic pathways. Irisin and IGF-1 reflect anabolic drive and muscle protein synthesis, which may be blunted in sarcopenia through anabolic resistance [15]. Myostatin is a canonical negative regulator of myogenesis linked to muscle atrophy. FGF-21 is a stress-responsive myokine upregulated with mitochondrial dysfunction and bioenergetic insufficiency [15–17]. BDNF, produced in muscle and the nervous system, supports motor-unit integrity via neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity and shows altered systemic levels in sarcopenia [18, 19]. Together, these markers capture intrinsic metabolic capacity and endocrine signaling from several axes contributing to sarcopenia and that converge on muscle mass, strength, and performance.

Given growing evidence that diagnostic multi-biomarkers may outperform individual markers, we conducted a retrospective, exploratory, cross-sectional analysis to determine the diagnostic accuracy of a novel multi-biomarker (irisin, myostatin, BDNF, FGF-21, IGF-1, and fCPT) for primary sarcopenia [1]. We hypothesized that the multi-biomarker would demonstrate superior diagnostic performance for at least suffering from probable sarcopenia as compared to any single biomarker.

Methods

Study design and population

In this cross-sectional, explorative study all participants were aged ≥ 65 years, were of Western European descent, and resided either in the community or in service flats in Belgium. Recruitment occurred between February 2021 and March 2024. Participants with sarcopenia were recruited as part of the “Exercise and Nutrition for Healthy AgeiNg” (ENHANce) trial (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03649698), a five-arm, triple-blinded randomized controlled trial investigating the effects of single or combined anabolic interventions (physical exercise, proteins, and omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids) for sarcopenia over a 12-week period [20]. In this analysis, we included 59 participants (39 women and 20 men, aged 65–96 years) with probable, confirmed, or severe sarcopenia (defined by the criteria of the European Working Group for Sarcopenia in Older Persons (EWGSOP2)) [1], who completed the baseline assessment. Eligibility criteria for inclusion were: absence of diabetes mellitus; Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD); active malignancy or receiving treatment for malignancy within the past five years; no use of antibiotics or systemic corticosteroids in the past three months; no known allergy to peanut or soy; estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≥ 30 mL/min; Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score ≥ 21; and absence of neurological conditions likely to impair muscle function, including neurodegenerative diseases (e.g. Parkinson’s disease) and non-neurodegenerative disorders with residual deficits (e.g. spinal cord compression, peripheral neuropathies). In addition, participants with any acute illness at enrollment (e.g. acute decompensated heart failure, acute viral illness) were excluded. Participants without sarcopenia were recruited from the “Trial in Elderly for Musculoskeletal problems due to underlying Sarcopenia – Faeces to Unravel the Gut and Inflammation Translationally” (TEMPUS-FUGIT) study (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT05008770) [21]. All control participants from the TEMPUS-FUGIT study met the same above-mentioned eligibility criteria for ENHANce, with the exception that they were not diagnosed with probable, confirmed, or severe EWGSOP2 sarcopenia. A total of 97 participants were included from TEMPUS-FUGIT: 64 women and 33 men. Both trials were approved by the Ethics Committee at University Hospitals Leuven and the Catholic University of Leuven (s60763 & s65127), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [22] and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. This study was reported in accordance with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Appendix I).

Assessment of muscle mass, strength, and physical performance

Upper limb muscle strength was evaluated through Hand Grip Strength (HGS, kg), measured with a Jamar-1 handheld dynamometer (TEC Inc., Clifton, NJ, USA). Measurements were performed six times, alternating hands at a 90° angle in a seated position, with the maximum value of the dominant hand recorded, as previously described [20]. Lower limb strength was assessed with the 5-time Chair Stand Test (CST, s), requiring participants to rise five successive times from a chair as fast as possible, with arms folded in front of the chest. Fat mass, appendicular lean mass (ALM), and Skeletal Muscle Index (SMI) were measured through DXA scans (Horizon A scanner, Hologic Inc., Bedford, MA, USA), the latter calculated as ALM (kg)/height (m)². Physical performance was evaluated by gait speed (m/s) and the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), a composite test comprising a balance test, the above-mentioned 5-time CST, and gait speed, with higher scores reflecting better physical performance (maximum score of 12 points). Participants wore inertial movement monitors (MoveMonitor+, McRoberts, The Netherlands) to track steps, total energy expenditure (TEE), basal metabolic rate (BMR), and physical activity level (PAL = TEE/BMR) [23]. Medication use and comorbidities were surveyed using a medical intake questionnaire.

Sarcopenia diagnosis

Sarcopenia was diagnosed according to the EWGSOP2, which defines three stages [1]. Low muscle strength (HGS < 16 kg in women and < 27 kg in men, or 5-time CST > 15 s for both sexes) indicated probable sarcopenia. If muscle mass was also low (SMI < 5.5 kg/m² in women and < 7.0 kg/m² in men, or ALM < 15 kg in women and < 20 kg in men), sarcopenia was confirmed. Sarcopenia was considered severe if, in addition to low muscle strength and mass, physical performance was low (gait speed ≤ 0.8 m/s). Sarcopenia was categorized as a binary variable if a person was at least ‘probably sarcopenic’, since muscle strength has been shown to be a more reliable predictor of adverse health outcomes [1]. By including individuals from the earliest stage of sarcopenia, we aimed to evaluate the biomarker’s potential for early—thus probable—sarcopenia detection, ensuring timely intervention.

Assessment of serum myokines and other biomarkers

Fasted serum samples were collected from participants at rest, after abstaining from food or liquid intake for at least 8 h overnight. Samples were centrifuged immediately after venipuncture at 1,200 g for 10 min at 24 °C. Serum was transferred to 2 ml cryotubes (Sarstedt SC Micro Tube Protein LB) and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Using 50 µl of each participant’s serum, levels of myostatin, BDNF, FGF-21 (Quantikine ELISA, R&D Systems Inc., Minneapolis, USA) and irisin (AdipoGen Life Sciences, San Diego, USA) were assessed using Enzyme-Linked ImmunoSorbent Assays (ELISA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol, in duplicate using validated multiplex assays. Inter- and intra-assay variabilities were < 10% for irisin, myostatin, BDNF, and FGF-21, indicating < 10% variation in measurements within a single run and between assay runs, respectively. This ensures high precision and reproducibility both within and across runs. Mean minimally detectable levels for irisin, myostatin, BDNF, FGF-21, and IGF-1 were 1 ng/ml, 2.25 pg/ml, 20 pg/ml, 4.67 pg/ml, and 7 ng/ml, respectively. Plasma high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP, mg/l), albumin (g/l), creatine kinase (CK, U/l), and IGF-1 (µg/l) were measured using Roche Cobas systems. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR, mm/h) was measured using capillary photometry (Test1 SDL Alifax, Polverara, Italy), and creatinine was measured using the CKD-EPI formula [24], according to the manufacturers’ instructions at University Hospitals Leuven, Belgium.

Assessment of fecal calprotectin and intestinal transit time

Immediately after collection, participants stored fecal samples temporarily for up to a maximum of 7 days at − 20 °C before transfer to the research facility in a cooling bag to avoid thawing, where they were stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Samples were weighed, vortexed, and analyzed for fCPT using ELISA (Bühlmann Laboratories fCAL ELISA, Schönenbuch, Switzerland). Detection levels for fCPT ranged between 30 and 1800 µg/g feces, with 1/10 or 1/50 dilutions performed to determine levels in samples below or above this range. Intestinal transit times were assessed using the Bristol Stool Scale (BSS) chart.

Statistical analysis

Distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous data was presented as means ± standard deviations (SD) or as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) in case of skewed distribution. Differences between two groups were assessed using the unpaired student’s t-test for normally distributed data, or Mann-Whitney U test for skewed data. Differences in normally distributed data across > 2 groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA, or Kruskall-Wallis for skewed data. Categorical data were compared using the Chi-squared test. Cohen’s d effect size (ES) was reported, with values ≤ 0.15 considered as small, > 0.15 ≤ 0.40 as medium and ≥ 0.75 as large ES, respectively [25]. Correlations (poor (< 0.3), fair (≥ 0.3; <0.6), moderate (≥ 0.6; <0.8) or strong (≥ 0.8)) were assessed using unadjusted Pearson’s (r), Spearman’s (ρ), or biserial correlation (ρbs) coefficients [26] and were adjusted for False Discovery Rate (FDR) multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg test (reporting q-values as FDR-adjusted p-value). Multivariate linear and logistic regression analyses, the latter reporting odds ratio (OR), were adjusted for age in model 1 and additionally for sex, PAL and nutritional status, based on MNA-score, in model 2. Sensitivity and specificity analyses were performed using Area Under the Curve (AUC) for Receiver Operator Characteristics, which were considered significant if the 95% confidence intervals (CI) excluded 0.50. For the multi-biomarker, logistic regression comprising biomarkers of interest was performed, followed by generation of predicted probabilities, prior to AUC analysis. We assessed all two to six analyte combinations of the six candidates and, noting a plateau in AUC gains beyond four biomarkers, selected the highest-AUC four-marker panel (BDNF, irisin, FGF-21, fCPT). This model was internally validated using bootstrap analysis to mitigate potential overfitting. We conducted post hoc, exploratory analyses comprising: (i) a sensitivity analysis using EWGSOP2 low lean mass (ALM; sex-specific cut-offs) as the outcome, and (ii) assessment of sex- and age-related heterogeneity through interaction terms and stratified ROC/AUC, with age examined both continuously and categorically (≤ 75 vs. > 75 years). All data was analyzed using Stata, Version 17.0 (Stata Corp LLC, TX, USA) and significance of results was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline population characteristics

Data from 156 older adults (66% women, 34% men) were cross-sectionally analyzed, with 59 participants (20 men and 39 women) suffering from at least EWGSOP2-defined probable sarcopenia (Figure SI), and 97 controls (33 men and 64 women). Regarding sarcopenia staging, 24 persons (40.7%; 9 men; 15 women) had confirmed sarcopenia, and 4 (6.8%; 1 man; 3 women) had severe sarcopenia. Seven participants had missing data for BDNF and irisin, all but one of whom were controls, and six participants had missing data for FGF-21 and myostatin, again all but one were controls. fCPT data were missing for 37 participants, all but one of whom had probable sarcopenia. High fCPT data missingness was largely due to operational and timing constraints: fecal sampling for fCPT was initiated after the ENHANce cohort had already started recruitment. Furthermore, within ENHANce, several participants considered an additional baseline fecal sample too burdensome on top of a 24-h urine collection and multi-day food diaries, particularly given the demands of the forthcoming 12-week intervention (nutritional supplementation and resistance exercises). Participants with missing fCPT were omitted only from analyses involving this biomarker.

Participants with at least probable sarcopenia were older (p = 0.002, ES: −0.58), had lower maximal HGS (p < 0.001, ES: 0.71), higher fat mass (p = 0.014, ES: −0.34), longer 5-time CST (p < 0.001, ES: −1.84), slower gait speed (p < 0.001, ES: 0.87), and lower SPPB scores (p < 0.001, ES: 2.06). Men with at least probable sarcopenia had significantly lower ALM and SMI compared to controls, which was not the case in women (Table 1). This sex contrast plausibly reflects men’s higher baseline ALM and steeper age-related loss, which yield greater between-group separation [27]. Persons with at least probable sarcopenia had significantly more arthrosis (p = 0.001; ES: −0.59)). Additionally, these individuals were less physically active (p < 0.001, ES: 0.76), took fewer steps per day (p < 0.001, ES: 0.57), performed worse on cognitive testing (p = 0.009, ES: 0.44), and had lower total cholesterol (p = 0.027, ES: 0.29) and lower low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels (p = 0.008, ES: 0.31) compared to controls, whereas high-density cholesterol (HDL) did not differ significantly (Table SI).

Levels of serum myokines and fCPT in persons without and with at least probable sarcopenia

Levels of BDNF were lower in participants with at least probable sarcopenia (p = 0.008, ES: 0.50) (Table 1). Levels of BDNF (p = 0.017, ES: 0.52) and IGF-1 (p = 0.000, ES: 0.71) were lower in individuals over 75 years (n = 61) compared to those aged between 65 and 75 years (n = 95) (Table SII). Participants in the highest 5-time CST tertile, indicating worse lower limb strength, had higher fCPT levels compared to those in the first (p = 0.008, ES: −0.62) and second (p = 0.019, ES: −0.31) 5-time CST tertiles (Figure SIIA). When further assessing sarcopenia-defining parameters among groups with clinically relevant differences in fCPT (fCPT < 50 µg/g, 50 ≤ fCPT < 200 µg/g, ≥ 200 µg/g) [28], participants with fCPT ≥ 200 µg/g had slower gait speed compared to those with fCPT < 50 µg/g (p = 0.006) (Figure SIIB). This finding was confirmed by comparing fCPT levels across gait speed tertiles, where in the lowest tertile—indicating the slowest gait speed—fCPT levels were higher compared to the second (p = 0.009, ES: 0.88) and third (p = 0.002, ES: 0.40) tertiles (Figure SIIC). Comparing between tertiles of HGS, IGF-1 was significantly higher in the third tertile compared to the first (p = 0.004, ES: −0.68) and second (p = 0.010, ES: −0.54) (Figure SIID). Likewise, IGF-1 was significantly higher in the third and second gait speed tertiles compared to the first (p = 0.030, ES: −0.43 and p = 0.017, ES: −0.44, respectively) (Figure SIIE).

Correlations between myokines, fCPT, the Bristol Stool Scale (BSS), sarcopenia-defining parameters or at least having probable sarcopenia

To minimize false-positive findings, the correlations were FDR-adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg test. The following correlations between sarcopenia-defining parameters and myokines or fCPT remained significant after FDR adjustment: the weak negative correlation between gait speed and fCPT (ρ = −0.246; q = 0.038), the weak positive correlation between HGS and IGF-1 (ρ = 0.248; q = 0.011), and the weak negative correlation between BDNF and having at least probable sarcopenia (ρ = −0.218; q = 0.039). Figure 1 shows a complete overview of the correlations maintained between sarcopenia-defining parameters and myokines, fCPT, and BSS after adjustment for multiple testing. Additionally, a significant weak positive correlation was observed between FGF-21 and both fat mass (ρ = 0.215; q = 0.048) and Body Mass Index (BMI) (ρ = 0.212; q = 0.049).

Correlations between myokines, fCPT and systemic inflammatory biomarkers

Correlations were assessed between systemic inflammatory markers of interest and myokines, fCPT, and BSS. None of the initially identified significant correlations (Figure SIII) remained significant after FDR-adjustment. However, some significant correlations persisted within inflammatory markers and within myokines, such as the weak negative correlations between FGF-21 and myostatin (ρ = −0.275; q = 0.036), between creatinine and albumin (ρ = −0.253; q = 0.039), and between fCPT and BSS (ρ = −0.282; q = 0.013). The latter finding suggest that higher intestinal inflammation correlates with looser stools.

Uni- and multivariate regression analyses of myokines, fCPT and sarcopenia (-defining parameters)

Given the exploratory nature of the study, we further assessed all significant correlations—not only those that remained significant after FDR adjustment—using univariate and multivariate linear and logistic regression analyses. Fecal calprotectin (fCPT) was associated, although weakly, with 5-time CST in age-adjusted analyses, a result which remained unchanged after further adjustment according to model 2 (β = 0.008; 95% CI [0.002, 0.012]; p = 0.037, Figure SIV). This suggests that participants with higher intestinal inflammation performed the 5-time CST more slowly, although the clinical relevance is very limited. fCPT was also significantly, though weakly, negatively associated with gait speed, but only in the fully adjusted model and with very limited clinical effect (Table SIII). IGF-1 was significantly associated with ALM in the age-adjusted model (β = 0.026; 95% CI [0.004, 0.049]), but the association became non-significant in the fully adjusted model (Figure SV). The myokine BDNF significantly reduced the odds of having at least probable sarcopenia, although the clinical relevance was limited. Moreover, in the fully adjusted model, the association between BDNF and the prevalence of at least probable sarcopenia was no longer significant (Figure SVI). No significant associations were found between fCPT and physical performance estimates, between FGF-21 and respectively BMI or fat mass, or between BSS and 5-time CST in the regression analyses (Table SIII).

Uni- and multivariate regression analyses of systemic inflammatory markers, myokines and fCPT

We proceeded with linear regression analyses for correlations that were significant prior to FDR adjustment, due to the exploratory nature of this study. The significant negative association between BDNF and creatinine in univariate analysis was no longer observed after further adjustment according to models 1 and 2. Myostatin was significantly associated with hs-CRP in all models (fully adjusted model: β = 0.052; 95% CI [0.044, 0.059]; p < 0.001) (Figure SVII). Likewise, a significant positive association between FGF-21 and creatinine was reported in both univariate and multivariate analyses. However, the effect was close to zero and therefore clinically very limited (Figure SVIII). A complete overview of associations from univariate and multivariate analyses is provided in Table SIV.

Diagnostic significance of single or combined myokines or fCPT as biomarker for at least suffering from probable sarcopenia

This study aimed to assess whether a combined panel of myokines and fCPT was superior to diagnosing at least probable sarcopenia compared to myokines or fCPT individually. Only BDNF significantly identified individuals with at least probable sarcopenia as a single biomarker, with 53% sensitivity and 71% specificity (95% CI [0.548, 0.718]), thus still missing nearly half of the cases and incorrectly classifying 29% of individuals without sarcopenia (Table 2). No single biomarker was significantly superior to the others in diagnosing at least probable sarcopenia, except for BDNF, which outperformed FGF-21 (p = 0.014) (Table SV). The most optimal multi-biomarker panel included BDNF, irisin, FGF-21, and fCPT, and significantly identified individuals with at least probable sarcopenia in the entire cohort, with a sensitivity of 64% and a specificity of 77% (AUC: 0.71; 95% CI [0.58, 0.83]; Table 2). For internal validation, bootstrapping the diagnostic accuracy of the multi-biomarker panel in the full cohort yielded a similar result (AUC: 0.71; BC 95% CI [0.57–0.83]). Moreover, the multi-biomarker panel significantly outperformed the single myokines myostatin (p = 0.011), irisin (p = 0.035), FGF-21 (p = 0.030), and IGF-1 (p = 0.020), but not BDNF (p = 0.330) or fCPT (p = 0.180) (Figure SIX).

Discussion

In this exploratory, cross-sectional analysis, we investigated if the diagnostic potential of a carefully selected multi-biomarker panel (BDNF, irisin, FGF-21 and fCPT) was superior to individual biomarkers in diagnosing community-dwelling older persons with at least probable sarcopenia. BDNF concentrations were significantly lower in participants with at least probable sarcopenia, and BDNF alone significantly diagnosed the condition (with an overall accuracy of 63%). The multi-biomarker panel also significantly diagnosed at least having probable sarcopenia and showed higher overall accuracy (71%) compared to BDNF alone. However, this improvement was not statistically significant compared to BDNF (p = 0.330).

Differences in myokines, fCPT, and systemic inflammatory markers

Lower levels of BDNF reported in those with at least probable sarcopenia, aligned with findings from prior studies in persons with sarcopenia secondary to chronic kidney disease and COPD [29, 30]. No significant differences in other biomarkers were observed. Conflicting results for irisin and myostatin refer to discrepancies between priorly reported findings - noting significantly increased myostatin levels in persons with sarcopenia [31], whereas other studies report no difference in myostatin levels between persons with and without sarcopenia [32]. Also, decreased levels of irisin have been reported to predict the odds of sarcopenia [31], whereas our study did not confirm such a finding. These discrepancies may reflect differences in population characteristics (e.g. comorbidities), diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia, or varying levels of physical activity prior to blood sampling.

Levels of FGF-21 did not differ significantly between individuals with and without at least probable sarcopenia in our study, consistent with previous findings [33]. The absence of significant differences in fCPT between controls and participants with at least probable sarcopenia contrasts with reports of higher fCPT in poorer skeletal muscle status, although this was reported in populations including persons with IBD [34]. As a marker of gut dysbiosis and a hallmark of aging, an fCPT between-group difference might have been expected. Notably, fCPT levels were not uniformly low or moderate but spanned a rather wide range, warranting further investigation.

Associations between sarcopenia (-defining parameters), fCPT, myokines and other systemic markers

BDNF showed a small inverse association with the odds of at least having probable sarcopenia, but with an ES near to unity and change with higher BDNF was < 0.01%. This is not clinically meaningful as compared to prior studies investigating this myokine in another geriatric syndrome, frailty, indicating an OR of 0.51 as clinically meaningful [35]. Moreover, the association was not maintained in fully adjusted models, nor were associations with sarcopenia-defining parameters reported, which contrasts some prior reports (e.g. in hemodialysis cohorts) [29, 30]. A plausible explanation is that BDNF’s primary role lies in neuroplasticity with transient fluctuations, whereas effects on skeletal muscle may require more stable, activity-induced elevations [36]. After FDR-adjustment, IGF-1 showed a weak positive association with muscle strength, consistent with findings in Italian [37] and Taiwanese [38] older adults, and remained significant in the fully adjusted model. According to gerontology specific benchmarks, ES of correlation between 0.2 and 0.3 reflects a modest effect. However, despite being statistically significant, it is not clinically relevant, since prior reports considered correlations in the range of |0.2–0.4 [39] and for HGS specifically a correlation coefficient reaching 0.5 has linked to be clinically meaningful [40].

The correlation between higher fCPT and slower gait speed persisted after FDR-adjustment and in the fully adjusted regression model, but according to gerontology-benchmarks, with medium ES and without clinical relevance. Comparison with previous findings is difficult, as no prior research has investigated these associations in a well-characterized population with at least probable sarcopenia as defined per EWGSOP2-criteria. Nevertheless, one study in young athletes found serum calprotectin to correlate negatively with physical fitness [41]. Finally, maintained positive correlations of FGF-21 with BMI and fat mass, respectively, align with previous findings [42], reflecting hepatically induced systemic metabolic stress [43]. However, magnitudes of the association were modest and somewhat conflict with the priorly reported insulin-sensitizing properties of FGF-21. Due to this ‘FGF-21 paradox’, it may be plausible that this myokine is more suitable to reflect underlying metabolic pathways [44].

No significant correlations between systemic inflammatory markers and myokines, fCPT or BSS were maintained after FDR-adjustment. Nevertheless, myostatin and hs-CRP were positively associated in both univariate and multivariate linear regression models, consistent with findings in Japanese community-dwelling middle-aged and older adults [45]. This finding is not unexpected, as myostatin negatively regulates skeletal muscle and is thus upregulated in catabolic states, similar to hs-CRP. Additionally, the positive associations between FGF-21 and creatinine in all regression models align with previous research [46].

Diagnostic accuracy and clinical utility of biomarkers

In the entire cohort, the multi-biomarker panel comprising BDNF, fCPT, irisin, and FGF-21 most accurately diagnosed probable sarcopenia, but with modest discriminative ability of 71%, and sensitivity and specificity of 64% and 77%, respectively. Consistent with previous research, our multi-biomarker panel equaled the discriminative ability of a single marker for diagnosing probable sarcopenia [8]. The discriminative ability of our multi-biomarker panel aligns with prior findings, such as the study by Shin et al., who reported a multi-biomarker panel comprising seven serum compounds (GDF-15, cystatin, blood urea nitrogen, aspartate aminotransferase/alanine transaminase, DHEA, myostatin and eGFR), with a sensitivity and specificity of 60.8% and 70.2%, respectively, for diagnosing sarcopenia according to the Asian Working Group on Sarcopenia [10]. Despite the higher sensitivity and specificity achieved by our multi-biomarker panel using fewer markers (including one fecal marker) compared to Shin et al. [10], our multi-biomarker panel would still result in substantial misclassification: 36% of individuals with at least probable sarcopenia would be missed and 23% of controls would be incorrectly identified as cases. Although there is no universal cut-off and clinical utility depends on context and the biomarker’s intended purpose (e.g. screening vs. diagnosis), prior literature often considers AUC values of ~ 0.80–0.90 as indicative of ‘good’ to ‘excellent’ discrimination and potentially clinically relevant. Since our multi-biomarker panel did not reach that range, the current clinical utility of the panel remains limited, and it would not be recommended to imply at this stage in clinical practice. The ‘ideal’ sensitivity and specificity for a biomarker depends on clinical setting and the objective of the test (e.g., screening, staging, treatment response monitoring), although it is generally accepted that sensitivity should reach 0.80 to 0.85 [47]. Modest performance of our multi-biomarker panel may at least partially be clarified by population heterogeneity. As such, exploratory interaction analyses did not detect significant interaction effects for sex or age (evaluated as both a continuous variable and as ≤ 75 vs. > 75 years). However, stratification according to sex and age categories suggested that performance of the multi-biomarker panel depended on population heterogeneity as it performed better in men than in women, and somewhat better in participants > 75 years than ≤ 75 years. Nonetheless between-group AUC differences were not statistically significant. These findings illustrate how a ‘one-size fits all’ approach may obscure subgroup-specific signals [48]. This is somewhat confirmed in our sensitivity analyses based on ‘having low muscle mass or not’, where this multi-biomarker panel was not able to distinguish low muscle mass better than chance. An additional source of heterogeneity attenuating the multi-biomarker performance may be diurnal and pre-analytical variability. For example FGF-21 shows diurnal/state-dependent fluctuations [49] and circulating BDNF is highly sensitive to pre-analytical handling (serum vs. plasma, platelet release) [50]. We measured BDNF in serum and although prior studies quantify BDNF in both serum and plasma, serum values are typically higher because platelets also release BDNF into circulation (and during clotting), inflating serum levels. Consequently, we cannot distinguish the level of BDNF attributable to skeletal muscle release. Thus, one of the strategies worth implementing in future research is an inclusion of pre-specified subgroup analyses (particularly by sex, as this is being more frequently implemented as standard [51]), and potentially data-driven subtype discovery to test whether distinct biomarker constellations improve diagnostic performance. Additional strategies to improve diagnostic biomarker performance may include proteomics and metabolomics (small-molecule protein or metabolites as functional readouts of cellular processes). As priorly demonstrated, peptides may reflect diverse bioactive functions and several have indeed been linked to sarcopenia-defining parameters [52]. Third, serial sampling could mitigate time-dependent variability and improve biomarker reliability [53, 54]. While fCPT in our multi-biomarker panel appears to improve diagnostic accuracy compared to panels composed solely of serum biomarkers, it may introduce additional clinical and financial burdens. Implementation of our multi-biomarker panel in clinical practice would require participants to provide both blood and fecal samples. Moreover, the development of customized ELISA assays is costly, with commercially available multiplex kits exceeding €1,000 per kit, or at least €25 per sample.

Currently, no (multi-)biomarkers based on biological samples have been implemented in clinical practice for the diagnosis, staging, or treatment monitoring of sarcopenia. First, this would require validation in large, external, and diverse cohorts. Cost-effectiveness studies should also evaluate the utility of such (multi-)biomarkers for sarcopenia, which, to the best of our knowledge, has not yet been conducted. Indeed, cost-effectiveness studies have compared several expert criteria for sarcopenia screening, with the EWGSOP1 criteria resulting in the most cost-effective increase in quality-adjusted life years [55]. However, because sarcopenia definitions are evolving, cost-effectiveness estimates derived under EWGSOP1 may not generalize to the more recent EWGSOP2 definition or to the forthcoming GLIS diagnostic criteria. Second, the development of a multi-biomarker panel for sarcopenia firstly requires a globally accepted operational definition, as the diagnostic performance of the same biomarker vary depending on the definition of sarcopenia, (as priorly demonstrated for the Cr/CysC ratio [56]).

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study lies in the careful selection of multiple myokines and the gut-dysbiosis biomarker fCPT to collectively reflect the multifactorial nature of primary sarcopenia, rather than focusing on individual markers [9]. Furthermore, this study was the first to include an emerging driving factor of sarcopenia—gut dysbiosis—reflected by fCPT levels. Because fCPT indexes intestinal mucosal inflammation and gut dysbiosis with a major application to monitor IBD disease activity, it is not specific to sarcopenia. Therefore, we applied stringent eligibility criteria to minimize confounding and better isolate a gut dysbiosis signal as a specific hallmark of aging due to the focus on ‘primary’ sarcopenia in this study [14]. Consistent with this approach, population data in older adults show that higher fCPT associates with gut dysbiosis outside the IBD context [57]. A third strength is the specific focus on a well-characterized population with ‘primary sarcopenia’. While some may argue that this does not reflect the ‘real-world’ population with sarcopenia, it allows for greater confidence that the observed associations are specific to primary (age-related) sarcopenia. For example, prior research has shown that impaired kidney function—highly prevalent in older populations—can inflate reported associations of blood biomarkers for Alzheimer’s disease [58]. We also standardized venipuncture without prior exercise, minimizing confounding from acute ‘exerkines’ release and thereby reducing bias in myokine measurements [12].

Additionally, this study adjusted significant correlations for multiple testing using the FDR method, which is not always consistently done in retrospective, exploratory cross-sectional studies. Not correcting for multiple testing would increase the risk of type I errors (false positives) [59]. Another strength is the transparent, pre-specified multi-biomarker panel derivation and its internal validation: from six pre-specified candidates (five myokines and fCPT) we systematically evaluated all two to six marker combinations, observed an AUC plateau beyond four, and selected the highest-AUC four-marker panel. The systematic evaluation of marker combinations with subsequent application of bootstrap optimism in the final panel is appropriate for small, pre-specified sets is in exploratory biomarker studies [60, 61]. A final strength of this study was its transparent and critical reporting of a negative finding: the rather poor clinical utility of our multi-biomarker panel.

However, some limitations should be addressed. First, participants were recruited from two parallel trials that were prospectively designed to use identical eligibility criteria, assessments, and procedures. Although groups differed by EWGSOP2-defined sarcopenia status (TEMPUS-FUGIT controls vs. ENHANce cases) and ENHANce participants subsequently underwent a 12-week intervention, harmonization at baseline supports comparability for the analyses presented. This reduces - but cannot fully eliminate - the risk of selection bias and trial-related heterogeneity, though residual confounding cannot be excluded. Moreover, the cohort consisted mainly of Western-European older adults and was relatively small, with skewed sarcopenia severity distribution (only including 4 with severe sarcopenia) and limited statistical power. These factors constrain external validity, generalizability and hamper to draw causal conclusions. Additionally, adopting ‘at least having probable sarcopenia’ heightens cohort heterogeneity, potentially diluting biomarker signal and restricting generalizability to other definitions (such as for example only having lower muscle mass), as demonstrated by our exploratory subgroup analyses. Given that sarcopenia severity likely entails stage-specific biology, different biomarker combinations (and cut-offs) may be necessary across stages. Collapsing its stages in a ‘one-size fits all’ biomarker risks overlooking stage-dependent associations.

Second, GM composition is known to vary by geographical region and dietary intakes. Consequently, surrogate markers of gut dysbiosis (e.g. fCPT) may exhibit population-specific distributions, limiting generalizability beyond Western-European settings (or their associated habitual diets) and introducing potential residual confounding related to geography. As such, fCPT has been shown to differ according to geographical location in Chinese infants (rural versus urban environments). Moreover, dietary patterns enriched in total and soluble fiber have been inversely associated with fCPT and positively with abundance of health beneficial GM taxa [62, 63].

Third, due to the exploratory nature of this study, no sample size or power calculations were conducted, as these should be performed a priori. However, to mitigate this limitation, we [1] reported effect sizes and confidence intervals [11], and [2] conducted post-hoc power calculations to assess the relevance of reported associations. These calculations revealed that the study had 80% power to detect correlations of ≥ 0.223 with 156 included persons. Additionally, because bootstrap optimism correction was applied only to the final selected panel (and not embedded within the selection step), some selection-stage optimism may remain and therefore warrants external validation in independent cohorts [64].

Fourth, despite maintaining statistical significance upon FDR-adjustment, identified correlations were often only ‘weak’. Furthermore, although we adjusted associations for several covariates (e.g., age, sex, PAL, and nutritional status), additional unmeasured covariates may have influenced the results. Nonetheless, it has previously been reported that adding too many covariates may lead to overfitting the regression model [13]. Fifth, no cut-off or weighted values for the combined multi-biomarker panel were determined. However, establishing cut-off values for any single or multi-biomarker panel would require strong discriminative ability—rather than the modest performance observed in this study. Moreover, prior research suggests that determining cut-off values would only be useful if standardization across platforms, assay techniques, and sample storage conditions is achieved [14]. Additionally, although we performed exploratory sex-stratified analyses of the multi-biomarker panel, not being able to match sex and age likely limited statistical power. Larger, balanced samples are needed to robustly evaluate sex-specific performance. Finally, the high and differential fCPT missingness (n = 37; mostly among participants with probable sarcopenia) might give a potential risk of selection bias, which could distort associations and the apparent performance of fCPT-containing panels. Accordingly, we analyzed fCPT using complete cases only and did not perform multiple imputation to avoid imposing unverifiable missingness assumptions that might exacerbate bias [65].

Conclusions and future perspectives

We identified associations between fCPT and gait speed and between HGS and IGF-1 which maintained significance upon adjustment for multiple testing and in multivariate linear regression. Despite the ability of the multi-biomarker panel comprising BDNF, FGF-21, irisin, and fCPT to significantly diagnose probable sarcopenia, the sensitivity and specificity of 64% and 77%, respectively, indicated a 36% false-negative and 23% false-positive rate. Therefore, the clinical potential of this multi-biomarker panel is currently still low. Although internal bootstrap validation was performed, the diagnostic accuracy of the multi-biomarker panel should be validated in external cohorts and might be improved by further exploring biomarker combinations. Moreover, the ‘one-size fits all’ approach may not be suitable for this multi-biomarker panel in heterogeneous sarcopenic populations and urges focus on specific subgroups (e.g. according to sex or sarcopenia severity). Therefore future research should continue the search for the ‘most optimal (multi-)biomarker’ but for specific sarcopenia “subtypes”, with improved sensitivity and specificity. Therefore, larger and more diverse cohorts are warranted with an a priori stratification according to sex, including proteomic and metabolomic markers and serial sampling.
