Suppression of 6-pyruvoyl-tetrahydropterin synthase promotes remyelination in multiple sclerosis
Authors: Hui Xiao, Ying Long, Yong-shun Guo, Yan-yan Zhu, Yue Wu, Yan-qing Yin, En-wei Ji, Gang Hu, Jun Gao, Jia-wei Zhou
Published: 2025

Background

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune disease, which is characterized by inflammation, demyelination, and axonal damage or neuronal loss [1]. The treatment of MS has been transformed by the successful application of chemotherapies and immunotherapies that efficiently alleviate disease severity and relief lineal symptoms. Despite these successes, the challenges of demyelination, axonal damage, and neuronal loss, that lead to permanent disability, remain unresolved [2, 3]. One strategy to offset neurodegeneration is to promote remyelination.

CNS remyelination is the multifaceted regenerative process that necessitates the intricate orchestration of oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), apoptosis, immune responses, and neuronal cells. Consequently, new myelin ensheathes demyelinated axons. It has been shown that during MS, surviving oligodendrocytes extend processes and ensheath demyelinated axons [4]. Although remyelination occurs in MS, it usually fails, leaving axons unprotected and vulnerable to degeneration [5]. Surviving oligodendrocytes are found to exhibit lower efficiency compared to those generated after demyelination in terms of generating new myelin sheaths, and they frequently misdirect new myelin towards neuronal cell bodies, as demonstrated in both zebrafish models and MS patients [6]. These findings suggest that oligodendrocytes generated after demyelination have a higher regenerative potential during MS. Normally, in response to the injury or death of oligodendrocytes, adjacent OPCs proliferate and migrate to the lesion site, followed by the differentiation into mature oligodendrocytes, which form new myelin and replace the damaged myelin sheath [7]. Enhancing remyelination, which focuses on the differentiation of OPCs, has been proposed as a novel therapeutic strategy for MS. However, clinical trials have revealed limited efficacy and poor tolerability in enhancing OPC differentiation, due to exhaustion of OPC pool and impaired functionality [6, 8, 9]. OPC proliferation and maintaining their density are a key to support oligodendrocyte formation in the adult brain [10, 11]. The limited proliferation of OPCs in MS lesion areas contributes to recurrent episodes of demyelination and remyelination [12]. These findings highlight the necessity of preserving the OPC proliferation capacity or elevating OPC pool as a fundamental step to promote OPC differentiation and remyelination in MS.

It has been hypothesized that the impaired OPC proliferation in MS may be attributed to inflammation [13–16]. Despite immunomodulatory treatment-mediated inhibition of pro-inflammatory mediators, the restoration of OPC proliferation remains limited, suggesting the involvement of yet unidentified mechanisms. In the last few decades, human genetic studies have provided vital clues that will unravel the mystery of brain diseases. In line with the strategy, we searched for a gene mutation that is associated with imaging-derived phenotypes of white matter, from human genetic data available in public domains, to decode neuroprotection and remyelination. It turned out that patients with 6-pyruvoyl-tetrahydropterin synthase (PTS) deficiency exhibit abnormal high-intensity signals in cerebral white matter and widespread delayed myelination, pointing to a potential link between PTS and myelination [17–19]. PTS is an enzyme for the de novo biosynthesis of tetrahydrobiopterin [20] and expressed in multiple cell types, including OPCs and oligodendrocytes [21, 22]. Expression of PTS is upregulated in the white matter of patients with MS [23]. These data also indicate a potential link between PTS and oligodendrocyte lineage cells (OLCs). However, the precise role of PTS in these cells during MS remains elusive and warrants further investigation.

In the present study, we demonstrated that the expression of PTS in OLCs is elevated in patients with MS. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data indicated that expression levels of PTS in OPCs are negatively correlated with their proliferation potential. Preferential ablation of Pts in OPCs, but not mature oligodendrocytes, significantly alleviated disease severity, which was accompanied by an increase in the OPC population and a reduction in demyelination within the spinal cord, but not neuroinflammation or immune cells infiltration. Pts deficiency in OPCs increased the activity of cholesterol biosynthesis pathway leading to augmentation in cell proliferation. Our study identified Pts as a new negative regulator of OPC proliferation during EAE and suggests that PTS in OPCs is a potential novel therapeutic target for MS, highlighting the importance of enhancing OPC proliferation for promoting remyelination in diseases, such as MS, as an effective approach to neuroprotection.

Methods

Human tissue collection

Tissue sections were obtained from the Netherlands Brain Banks, Netherlands Institute for Neuroscience, Amsterdam, Netherlands. All the materials have been collected from donors for or from whom written informed consent for a brain autopsy and the use of the material and clinical information for research purposes had been obtained by the Netherlands Brain Banks. For immunofluorescent staining, paraffin sections from a cohort comprising seven MS patients and five appropriately gender, age, and post-mortem interval-matched controls were used. All the MS patients were clinically and neuropathologically diagnosed, and the health control was described previously (Additional file 1: Table S1) [1]. This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Bioethics Committee, Institute of Neuroscience, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China, under ethical approval number ION-2017–001.

Animals

Adult or neonatal C57BL/6 mice were from the Shanghai Laboratory Animal Center, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha (Pdgfra)-CreERT2 mice (Tg(Pdgfra-cre/ERT)467Dbe/J, #018280; JAX stock) [24] and myelin proteolipid protein (Plp1)-CreERT2 mice (Tg(Plp1-cre/ERT)3Pop/J, #005975; JAX stock) [25] were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. Ptsflox/flox mouse line (C57BL/6JSmoc-Ptsem1(flox)Smoc) was generated by Shanghai Model Organisms Center. Characterization and genotyping of these mice were described previously [26]. All mice were maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle at 23 °C with food and water available and libitum.

Western blot analysis and quantification

Western blotting was performed as described previously [27]. The used primary antibodies are listed in Table S2 (Additional file 1). The membrane was washed and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the corresponding secondary antibodies. The list of primary and secondary antibodies is provided in Table S2 (Additional file 1). Peroxidase activity was detected with SuperSignal WestPico chemiluminescent substrate (Pierce Biotechnology) and visualized and digitized with ImageQuant (LAS-4000; Fujifilm). Optical densities of bands were analyzed by ImageJ (version 1.49 k, National Institutes of Health).

EAE

EAE was induced as previously described [28]. In brief, mice were subcutaneously injected with myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG35-55, 200 μg/animal) emulsified in CFA containing 5 mg/ml killed Mycobacterium tuberculosis (DIFCO), and i.p. injected with pertussis toxin (350 ng/animal; Calbiochem) on day 0 and day 2. Mice were scored daily by using a five-point scale with half-point gradations [29]. Prior to the onset of the experiment, all mice were renumbered to ensure that during the subsequent scoring process, the scorer was unaware of the specific experimental groups to which each mouse belonged. This renumbering was performed to reduce experimental bias and ensure uniform treatment and scoring of all experimental subjects.

Immunofluorescence, histopathology, and image analysis

Mouse spinal cord was removed and fixed in 4% PFA overnight. Paraffin-embedded tissue was sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm and then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for the assessment of inflammation and LFB for the detection of demyelination. To analyze the inflammatory infiltration, H&E stained sections were scored as follows: 0, none; 1, a few inflammatory cells; 2, organization of perivascular infiltrates; and 3, perivascular cuffing with extension into the adjacent tissue [30]. Immunofluorescent histochemical analysis of human brain sections was performed as previously described [23, 31]. The list of primary antibodies and secondary antibodies is provided in Table S3 (Additional file 1). Sections were imaged using VS120 (Olympus, Japan) or a laser confocal microscope (Nikon A1 or A1R, Japan). Data were obtained and processed using ImageJ.

Cell culture and transfection

Oli-neu cells, an immortalized mouse oligodendroglia precursor cell line [32], were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM)/F-12 (Invitrogen, USA) with 1% horse serum and 5% fetal bovine serum, N2 supplement (Invitrogen), B-27 supplement (Invitrogen), GlutaMAX (Invitrogen, USA), and 1% PS (penicillin and streptomycin). Cells were allowed to reach 90% confluence. All cells were transfected by Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Kit (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. siRNA targeting mouse and rat Pts were designed (Pts−223 sense, 5′-CCGACCUCAAAGAAUACAUTT-3′; antisense, 5′-AUGUAUUCUUUGAGGUCGGTT-3′; Hmgcs1 sense, 5′-GCAAGTTTATGTGACCTTATT-3′; antisense, 5′-TAAGGTCACATAAACTTGCTT-3′; non-silencing control sense, 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′; antisense, 5′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′). As previously described, the preparation of miR-30-based shRNA according to the siRNA sequence was performed [33].

Primary OPC culture

Sprague–Dawley rats at P0 were used for OPC culture, as described previously [34]. Briefly, the neonatal brains were dissociated, and cells were plated at a density of 1 ~ 2 × 106 cells/75-cm2 flask (Corning) with poly-L-lysine pre-treated in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum, and 1% PS. Ten days after plating, culture flasks were shaken to remove microglia, followed by shaking at 200 rpm for 18 ~ 20 h for the collection of OPCs. Collecting the cell suspension and incubating Petri dishes (Corning) for 45 min remove microglia and astrocytes. Resuspending OPCs with OPC culture medium, which contained Neurobasal medium (Invitrogen), B-27 supplement (Invitrogen), GlutaMAX (Invitrogen), 10 ng/ml PDGF-AA (Proteintech), 10 ng/ml FGF-2 (Proteintech), and 1% PS, OPCs were seeded to a 24-well plate at a density of 1.2 × 105 cells per well.

RNA isolation, RNA sequencing analysis, and quantitative PCR

Isolation of RNA, RNA-sequencing analysis, and quantitative PCR were performed as described previously [35]. Briefly, the spinal cord of PtsPdgfra cKO mice and littermate control were homogenized in TRIzol reagent on day 17 after EAE induction. Total RNA was purified, and cDNA was sequenced. Sequencing libraries were generated using NEB Next® Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA) following the manufacturer’s recommendations, and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. RNA-seq was performed on Novaseq 6000 (illumine, Inc.). qPCR was performed as previously described [23]. The list of primer sequences for amplifying mouse and rat cDNA fragments is provided in Table S4 (Additional file 1) and Table S5 (Additional file 1).

EdU detection

Detection of EdU-labeled cells was performed using the BeyoClick™ EdU-555 Kit (Beyotime, C0075S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After knockdown of Pts in Oli-neu cells, the preheated 2 × EdU working solution (20 μM) was added to cell cultures at an equal volume. The duration of EdU labeling was 2 h for Oli-neu cells in vitro. After EdU labeling, cells were fixed with 2% PFA followed by incubation with Click reaction buffer respectively.

Wound healing assay

The wound healing assay was performed following protocol previously published [36]. Cells were transfected with Lipo3000 for 24 h and then plated into 96-well dishes overnight to create a confluent monolayer. After scratching with IncuCyte® WoundMaker 96-pin tool, the wells were rinsed twice with D-Hanks to remove non-adherent cells and then treated with compounds. Cells were allowed to migrate until the first group reached 100% confluence. The scratches were recorded by IncuCyte with an interval of 2 h. The locations of the cells and the width of the gap were auto-analyzed by IncuCyte software.

Stereotaxic intracranial injection of LPC

Demyelination was induced by LPC stereotaxic intracranial injection of LPC as previously described with minor modifications [37]. In brief, mice were deeply anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (30 mg/kg, i.p.) and placed in a stereotaxic frame. The mice were injected with 2 µl of 1% LPC (9008–30 4; Sigma-Aldrich) into the corpus callosum with Hamilton syringe at the coordinates: Bregma: AP + 1.0 mm; ML ± 1.0 mm; DV − 2.3 mm. The rate of injection was 0.5 µl/min. Mice in control group were injected with 2 µl saline at each point. After injection, the needle was kept for 10 min and then slowly retracted.

Statistical analysis

Experiments were performed in a collaborative manner among multiple members of the team, which makes single-blind experiments feasible. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad software. Data presented as mean ± SEM. The statistics were submitted to a two-tailed unpaired t test, Holm-Sidak’s multiple t test, or two-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons test. P < 0.05 was considered as significant in statistics. P values are *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001; and ****, P < 0.0001.

Results

Expression of PTS in OLCs is upregulated in patients with MS

To examine the expression of PTS in brain tissue of patients with MS, PTS immunofluorescent staining was performed on the paraffin-embedded brain sections of age, gender, post-mortem interval matched MS patients, and controls. There was a marked increase in PTS immunoreactivity in Olig1+ OLCs in the normal-appearing white matter of MS brains; the abnormalities in the brain area are known to be associated with subtle axonal degeneration and subsequently form MS lesions [38], compared to controls (Fig. 1A and B). This result was consistent with the scRNA-seq data showing a notable elevation in the expression levels of PTS in OPCs of patents with MS (GSE118257) (Additional file 2: Fig. S1A) [39]. Notably, the Olig1+ cells with aberrantly high expression of PTS showed punctate staining pattern in the white matter of MS patients instead of uniform distribution in the cytoplasm as shown in normal cells (Fig. 1A), indicating that altered PTS expression is highly associated with oligodendrocyte pathology in MS.

It has been shown that altered oligodendrocyte heterogeneity is linked to MS [39]. To understand the impact of PTS expression on the OLC heterogeneity, we analyzed scRNA-seq data of OLCs that were obtained from publicly available repositories (Human, GSE118257; Mouse, GSE75330) [39, 40]. It was revealed that PTS was expressed in various stages during OLC differentiation, including committed oligodendrocyte progenitors (COPs), newly formed oligodendrocyte (NFOLs), myelin forming oligodendrocyte (MFOLs), and some subclusters of mature oligodendrocyte (MOLs), whereas its abundance in OPCs was very low under normal conditions. However, there was a steady increase in its expression levels during OLC differentiation, reaching its peaks at immune oligodendroglia (ImOIGs) in human or MOLs in mouse (Fig. 1C–F), indicating that PTS plays a role in the differentiation of OLCs.

Further analysis of differentially expressed genes between PTS-positive and PTS-negative OLCs based on the scRNA-seq data revealed a significant enrichment in pathways related to cell proliferation and differentiation, such as gliogenesis, glial cell differentiation, and axonal ensheathment (Additional file 2: Fig. S1B, C, D, and E). Given these findings, we hypothesized that PTS facilitates the transition of OPCs from a proliferative state to a differentiating state. To determine which cell types, either OPCs or oligodendrocytes, are influenced by Pts, the enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes on the scRNA-seq data (GSE180759) was carried out [41]. It was shown that PTS expression was associated with downregulated pathways related to cell proliferation in OPCs and upregulated pathways related to the differentiation and myelination of oligodendrocytes (Additional file 2: Fig. S1F and G), suggesting that PTS acts to inhibit OPC proliferation and promote the differentiation and maturation in OLCs. Consistent with this, we found that there was a significant elevation of PTS levels in PDGFRα+ cells in the white matter (Fig. 1G and H), as revealed by immunofluorescence staining on the paraffin-embedded brain sections of age, gender, post-mortem interval matched MS patients, and controls.

Selective ablation of Pts in OPCs attenuates EAE severity

To investigate the role of PTS in the regulation of oligodendrogliosis in the context of MS, experimental autoimmune encephalitis (EAE), an animal model of MS, was induced by active immunization with MOG35-55. Selective ablation of Pts in OPCs or oligodendrocytes was achieved by crossing Ptsflox/flox mice with Pdgfra-CreERT2 transgenic mouse line [42], to generate Ptsflox/flox; Pdgfra-CreERT2 (hereafter referred to as PtsPdgfra cKO) mice or with Plp1-CreERT2 transgenic mouse line to generate Ptsflox/flox; Plp1-CreERT2 (PtsPlp1 cKO) mice, respectively. PtsPdgfra cKO mice displayed ameliorated disease severity, as manifested by a significant decrease in clinical scores from disease onset (day 12 after immunization) to the peak of the disease (day 17) when compared to control (Fig. 2A). In contrast, oligodendrocyte-specific Pts-null mice showed no significant difference in disease progression as compared with wild-type (WT) EAE mice (Fig. 2B; Additional file 2: Fig. S2). These data suggest that Pts in OPCs plays a key role in disease progression during EAE.

Alleviated demyelination but not inflammatory response in the CNS of PtsPdgfracKO mice

In agreement with the reduced EAE clinical scores, PtsPdgfra cKO mice exhibited attenuated demyelination in the white matter of the spinal cord, as assessed by Luxol fast blue (LFB) staining (Fig. 2C and D). Likewise, immunofluorescence staining also showed that selective Pts deficiency in OPCs significantly elevated expression of myelin basic protein (MBP), a protein required for myelin formation and long-term maintenance, in the spinal cords of EAE mice (Fig. 2E and F), indicating that Pts in OPCs inhibits myelination during EAE. Consistent with this, the transcriptional levels of Mbp in the spinal cords of PtsPdgfra cKO mice were significantly increased (Fig. 2G). Together, these data suggest that Pts in OPCs, but not in oligodendrocytes, contributes to EAE progression and selective ablation of Pts ameliorates EAE through alleviating demyelination.

Additionally, we examined the changes in myelin and OLCs in aged PtsPdgfra cKO mice. Immunofluorescence staining of 15-month-old mice showed that selective deletion of Pts in OPCs did not affect either the expression of myelin basic protein (MBP) in the corpus callosum or the number of OLCs in the cerebral cortex under basal conditions (Additional file 2: Fig. S3A), indicating that Pts deletion in OPCs does not impair normal physiological functions in mice.

Besides the EAE model, we also investigated the role of Pts deletion in OPCs in lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC)-induced demyelination and remyelination model, which effectively distinguishes between demyelination and remyelination [43]. We found that LPC injection induced significant myelin damage in both WT and PtsPdgfra cKO mice. However, during the subsequent remyelination phase, the deletion of Pts in OPCs significantly accelerated myelin regeneration. By day 21, PtsPdgfra cKO mice displayed nearly complete remyelination, whereas control mice were still in the repair phase (Additional file 2: Fig. S3B). These findings further confirm that Pts deletion in OPCs markedly promotes myelin repair after injury, thereby alleviating disease progression.

Peripheral immune infiltration and neuroinflammation are crucial pathogenic processes involved in MS. We found that there was no alteration in the infiltration of immune cells into the spinal cord between genotypes on day 17 after EAE induction, as assessed by H&E staining (Fig. 3A and B). Consistent with this, mRNA expression levels of Cd3, Cd4, Cd8, and Cd45, markers of infiltrated immune cells, were also not altered in the spinal cord of PtsPdgfra cKO mice following EAE induction (Fig. 3C). Accordingly, there were no significant changes in the activation of immune cells and the production of inflammatory mediators in the inguinal lymph nodes and spleens of PtsPdgfra cKO mice as compared to control (Additional file 2: Fig. S4). These results collectively indicate that Pts deficiency has no impact on immune cell-mediated inflammatory response in the CNS during EAE.

Astrocytes and microglia are two major types of innate immune cells in the CNS that are known to be key players in progressive inflammatory demyelination in MS [44]. Indeed, our previous study has shown that astrocytic Pts promotes neuroinflammation during EAE [23]. To determine whether Pts in OPCs influences EAE disease course through the regulation of glial cell-mediated neuroinflammation, we analyzed the activation status of astrocytes and microglia in the CNS on day 17 after EAE induction. No significant differences in expression of GFAP and IBA1, markers of astrocytes or microglia, respectively, were detected in the spinal cord between genotypes (Fig. 3D–G). These results suggest that Pts regulates myelination during MS, in an excessive activation of astrocytes and microglia-independent manner. In agreement with this, there was no difference in the levels of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, such as interleukin (Il)−6, Il-1β, tumor necrosis factor (Tnf)-α, Il-17a, and Il-10, in the spinal cord between genotypes (Fig. 3H). Together, these data suggest that the influence of Pts in OPCs on the progression of EAE is independent of regulating inflammatory response.

Selective deletion of Pts promotes OPC proliferation

To investigate how Pts-deficiency contributes to amelioration of EAE severity, we performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis on the spinal cord tissue at the peak of the disease (day 17) following EAE induction. It was revealed that there were 496 downregulated genes and 386 upregulated genes (P < 0.05, fold change > 1.5) among all the detected genes between PtsPdgfra cKO mice and control during EAE (Fig. 4A). PtsPdgfra cKO mice displayed upregulated expression in genes that are involved in the regulation of OPC proliferation and remyelination or related to cell proliferation in general [45–47], such as Olig2, Olig1, telomeric repeat-binding factor 1 (Trf), translocator protein (Tspo), gap junction gamma-2 protein (Gjc2), myelin-associated glycoprotein (Mag), sex-determining region Y box 10 (Sox10), proliferating cell nuclear antigen (Pcna), and Lck/Yes-related novel protein tyrosine kinase (Lyn) (Fig. 4B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed that Pts deficiency induced elevation in expression of genes associated with cell cycle in EAE (Fig. 4C and D). This was unlikely attributed to neuroinflammation, as neuroinflammatory response was not significantly altered in EAE progression between genotypes (Fig. 3).

Augmented oligodendrogliosis was validated using immunostaining. It was shown that there was a 1.5-fold increase in the number of Olig1+ cells in Pts-deficient EAE mice as compared to control EAE mice (Fig. 5A and B). This alteration may be attributed to the expansion of OPC pool, as there was a significant increase in the number of OPCs (CC1—/Olig1+ cells) in the white matter of PtsPdgfra cKO mice after EAE induction (Fig. 5C). The number of oligodendrocytes (CC1+/Olig1+ cells) in PtsPdgfra cKO mice showed a tendency towards upregulation as compared to control (Fig. 5D). Notably, the augmentation in oligodendrogliosis was only observed after EAE induction. Under basal (non-EAE) conditions, depletion of Pts in OPCs did not alter the expression of MBP protein, gene expression related to myelin and OPC proliferation or the number of Olig1+ cells in the spinal cord (Additional file 2: Fig. S5). Collectively, these data indicate that ablation of Pts in OPCs is crucial for expansion of OPC populations during EAE.

Next, we sought to characterize the expanded OPC pool induced by Pts deficiency at the cellular level. Immunohistochemical analysis showed that the proportion of Ki67+/Olig1+ cells in PtsPdgfra cKO mice was twofold higher than that in control mice (Fig. 5E and F). Ki67 serves as a marker for cellular proliferation. These results indicate that Pts deficiency evokes OPC proliferation. In support of this, the number of 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU)-labeled cells were doubled in Oli-neu cells upon shRNA-mediated knockdown of Pts as compared to control (Fig. 5G and H). Consistently, mRNA levels of Pdgfra and Cspg4 (encoding NG2) were also significantly upregulated by Pts knockdown in Oli-neu cells (Fig. 5I). Pdgfra is known as a highly expressed cell marker for OPCs [42], while Cspg4 has been reported to promote the proliferation of OPCs [48]. Moreover, we found that the expanded OPC pool induced by Pts deficiency did not result from reduced apoptosis of OPCs (Additional file 2: Fig. S6). Together, these results support the notion that PTS exerts inhibitory effect on the proliferation of OPCs both in vivo and in vitro.

Pts-deficiency-induced proliferation of OPCs leads to enhanced remyelination in EAE

Previous studies have demonstrated that following OPC proliferation, OPC migration to the lesion site is one of essential steps in the process of OPC differentiation and remyelination in MS [7, 49]. We thus investigated whether Pts affects cell migration. Wound healing assays showed that overexpression of Pts significantly inhibited cell migration (Additional file 2: Fig. S7A and B) in both Oli-neu cells and human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 T cells. This was accompanied by suppressed expression of Cxcl12 (Additional file 2: Fig. S7C), which is a potent chemoattractant mediating cell mobilization [50]. Interestingly, the knockdown of Pts showed no significant effect on cell migration in both Oli-neu cells and HEK293T cells (Additional file 2: Fig. S7D and E), indicating that PTS is not directly involved in cell migration, and the remyelination induced by the knockout of Pts through promoting cell proliferation. These data suggest that in the context of demyelinating diseases, elevated expression of Pts contributes to inhibition of the proliferation and migration of OPCs.

Next, we determined whether increased proliferation of OPCs leads to mitigated remyelination in EAE. qPCR analysis showed that there was a marked increase in expression of Mbp in Pts-depleted EAE mice as compared to control (Fig. 2G). This was corroborated by the observations in primary cultured OPCs, where mRNA levels of Mbp, Plp1, 2’,3’-cyclic nucleotide 3’ phosphodiesterase (Cnp), and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (Mog), which are known to be expressed during OPC differentiation and oligodendrocyte maturation [51], were doubled following Pts-knockdown as compared to control (Fig. 5J). Taken together, these data suggest that increased Pts expression contributes to inhibited proliferation and migration of OPCs leading to compromised remyelination in EAE.

Cholesterol biosynthesis pathway mediates Pts-deficiency-induced proliferation of OPCs

To obtain molecular insights into how Pts negatively regulates proliferation of OPCs, the bulk RNA-seq was further analyzed. Besides the cell cycle, the most prominent changes in KEGG analysis were those associated with inflammation and steroid biosynthesis (Fig. 4C, Additional file 2: Fig. S8A). Further investigation into the changed genes within inflammation-related pathways revealed that only chemokines were altered, while inflammatory cytokines remained unchanged (Additional file 2: Fig. S8B), indicating that the protective effects of Pts knockout in EAE do not rely on the modulation of inflammation and suggesting a potential role for steroid biosynthesis related pathways in OPC proliferation. GSEA also demonstrated that two of these pathways, steroid biosynthesis and terpenoid backbone biosynthesis, were simultaneously enriched in PtsPdgfra cKO mice (Fig. 6A), suggesting the activation of cholesterol biosynthesis. Interestingly, the upregulated genes in steroid biosynthesis and terpenoid backbone biosynthesis were mainly associated with cholesterol synthesis (Additional file 2: Fig. S8C), with less impact on the other two branches of the steroid synthesis pathway, suggesting a preferential influence on the cholesterol pathway by PTS.

Consistent with this, PtsPdgfra cKO mice exhibited higher expressions of genes related to cholesterol biosynthesis pathway, such as 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase 1 (Hmgcs1) and cytochrome P450 family 51 (Cyp51), compared with control EAE mice (Fig. 6B). Hmgcs1 is known to be involved in de novo cholesterol synthesis [52, 53], whereas Cyp51 is an important enzyme for de novo cholesterol synthesis and associated with T cell proliferation [54, 55]. Upregulation of these two genes was validated using both Western blotting and qPCR analysis (Fig. 6C and D). Immunohistochemical analysis further confirmed the increase in expression of Hmgcs1 and Cyp51 in the white matter of the spinal cord of PtsPdgfra cKO mice compared to control after EAE induction (Fig. 6E–G), suggesting that Pts negatively controls cholesterol biosynthesis pathway activity. This was supported by data showing that siRNA-mediated Pts knockdown in OPCs resulted in elevated expression of Hmgcs1 and Cyp51 in vitro (Fig. 6H).

Cholesterol is an essential component of myelin and an important regulator for OPC proliferation, differentiation, and myelination [52, 56–58]. Inhibition of early enzymes involved in the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway leads to hypomyelination in vivo [59–61]. We thus speculated that Pts-regulated cholesterol biosynthesis contributes to the regulation of OPC proliferation. To test this, we conducted overexpression of Hmgcs1 in Oli-neu cells. This caused a profound increase in OPC proliferation, as assessed by quantification of EdU+ cells (Fig. 7A) and expression of Cspg4, a marker of OPCs (Fig. 7B). In contrast, Hmgcs1 knockdown significantly inhibited cell proliferation (Fig. 7C and D). Moreover, the promoting effect of Pts knockdown on cell proliferation was completely abolished when Hmgcs1 was also deleted (Fig. 7C and D), indicating that the promotion of cell proliferation induced by Pts depletion is in a Hmgcs1-dependent manner. To further assess the impact of the inhibition of cholesterol synthesis pathway on Pts-regulated cell proliferation, Oli-neu cells were exposed to atorvastatin, an inhibitor of HMGCR. This treatment significantly reduced expression of OPC proliferation-related genes, such as Pdgfra, Sox10, and Cdk4, as compared to control (Fig. 7E). Taken together, these data suggest that Pts suppress OPC proliferation via the suppression of cholesterol biosynthesis pathway.

Discussion

Remyelination therapies have emerged as a potential therapeutic approach for MS, due to their ability to promote axonal survival and integrity in both clinical settings and animal models [3, 62, 63]. Despite recent progress, remyelination therapies have achieved limited success so far. This is partially attributed to restricted availability of OPC pool which is compromised during the progression of MS. However, the underlying mechanisms remain elusive, and it is crucial to unlock the restricted capacity of OPC proliferation during MS. In the present study, we found that the aberrant expression of PTS in OLCs induced the inhibition of proliferation and migration of OPCs in the context of MS or EAE. Selective depletion of Pts in OPCs, rather than in oligodendrocytes, resulted in relief of the suppression of OPC proliferation and remyelination through augmentation of Hmgcs1–mediated cholesterol synthesis pathway both in vivo and in vitro. This led to a significant reduction of disease severity in EAE. Our findings suggested that PTS represents a critical machinery for demyelination in MS and removal of PTS selectively in OPCs is a key to establishing a novel remyelination therapy for MS.

Our study indicates that the poor OPC proliferation and subsequent migration/differentiation is a significant impediment to remyelination during MS. The prevailing scientific consensus posits that the compromised differentiation capabilities of OPCs play a critical role in impeding remyelination during MS. However, clinical interventions targeting this aspect have limited efficacy and poor tolerability [64, 65], indicating that some other factors may play critical role in remyelination. Hence, mounting evidence points to impaired OPC proliferation and recruitment as the root cause of remyelination failure in MS [12, 66]. Nevertheless, previous studies have shown that OPC recruitment does not act as a rate-limiting factor in remyelination, as boosting OPC recruitment in rodent models did not significantly improve remyelination efficiency [67, 68]. OPCs exhibit higher susceptibility than oligodendrocytes within the MS lesion, resulting in a reduction of OPCs, despite that oligodendrocytes exist in active MS lesions [39, 69–72]. In addition, promoting OPC proliferation not only can compensate for the reduced OPC population, but also facilitate their differentiation into mature oligodendrocytes for remyelination in EAE, which was consistent with the previous in vitro studies showing that high OPC densities stimulate their maturation [11]. Thus, our findings elevating OPC proliferation via suppression of PTS, instead of solely focusing on promoting OPC differentiation, represent a novel strategy of enhancing the efficacy of OPC-based interventions on MS and warrant further investigation.

We found in this study that Hmgcs1-mediated activation of cholesterol biosynthesis pathway is required for OPC proliferation. This molecular mechanism also functions in other types of cells, such as stem cells where driving endogenous cholesterol biosynthesis promotes cell proliferation [73–75]. However, aberrant PTS expression is unique in OPCs of MS brain, representing a mechanistically distinct molecular pathways used in the regulation of OPC proliferation in the context of MS. In addition to cell proliferation, previous studies have reported that the cholesterol synthesis pathway plays a significant role in promoting OPC differentiation and myelin formation [52, 56–58]. However, the specific proteins involved vary at different stages, suggesting their importance in determining the fate of OLCs. The importance of the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway for OLCs may stem from cholesterol, a major component of myelin, that plays a critical role in the myelin formation process, which is the primary responsibility of OLCs throughout their lifecycle. Yet, the precise mechanisms by which the cholesterol synthesis pathway influences the OLCs remain unclear and require further investigation.

Peripheral immune infiltration and neuroinflammation are two crucial pathogenic processes involved in the development of MS. Previous studies have predominantly focused on modulating the immune system. However, immunomodulatory treatments have demonstrated limited and short-lasting efficacy for MS [6, 8, 9]. Our data in the present study showed that Pts deficiency-induced expansion of OPC pools can effectively ameliorate demyelination and disease severity during EAE but with no significant impact on inflammatory response. Our findings highlight the pivotal role of OPCs in MS pathogenesis and emphasize that MS arises from a complex interplay between the CNS and peripheral immune system. Therefore, interventions solely targeting the peripheral immune system may not effectively address MS pathology, necessitating a novel treatment strategy involving simultaneous modulation of both CNS and peripheral immune system. Indeed, our previous findings suggest that astrocytes play a pivotal role in MS pathogenesis [23, 29]. Specifically, astrocytic dopamine D2 receptor plays a pivotal role in the process of astrocyte activation in MS pathogenesis [23]. Future therapeutic approaches for MS may target multiple key biological processes, including OPC proliferation/differentiation and astrocyte activation, combined with immunomodulatory therapy, potentially offering a more comprehensive and effective treatment strategy.

In our previous study, we demonstrated that astrocytic PTS promotes the progression of EAE by enhancing NF-κB activity [23]. However, in the present study, we found that Pts in OPCs increases susceptibility to EAE by suppressing OPC proliferation. One plausible explanation for the differential role of Pts between astrocytes and OPCs is that Pts activates distinct downstream effectors in different types of cells. For example, astrocytic Pts regulates the neuroinflammatory response by interacting with cleaved protein kinase C (PKC)-δ. Conversely, PKC-δ is known to be barely expressed in OPCs [21]. The proteomic disparities of PTS interaction across diverse cellular contexts remain elusive, constituting a pivotal area for further investigation that will enhance our understanding of the role of diverse glial cells in the pathogenesis of neurological diseases.

Analysis of existing single-cell sequencing data revealed that PTS expression significantly increases as OPCs begin to differentiate. Further enrichment analysis indicated that PTS plays a critical role in OPC differentiation, while its high expression suppresses cell migration. Combined with our findings that elevated PTS inhibits the OPC proliferation, we propose that when new oligodendrocytes are needed, OPCs first proliferate and migrate to the injury site. Upon receiving signals from the damaged area, the expression of PTS is upregulated, inhibiting OPC proliferation and migration while driving differentiation into mature oligodendrocytes. However, under the pathological conditions, extensive damage triggers widespread PTS upregulation in OPCs, accelerating their differentiation to rapidly repair the injury. Due to persistent damage, this rapid response fails to sustain long-term repair.

There are some limitations of this study. We showed that the expression of PTS was significantly increased in OLCs of MS patients and the differentiation of OPCs in the brain of MS patients was promoted in response to demyelination. However, it is unclear which factors drive the expression of PTS. A previous study demonstrated that in MS, dopamine receptor D2 (DRD2) activation enhances PTS expression in astrocytes, promoting neuroinflammation independently of BH4 synthesis [23]. Since OPCs also express DRD2, further investigation is needed to determine whether dopamine similarly induces PTS expression in OLCs as they do in astrocytes. Moreover, we found that inhibition of the aberrant expression of PTS in OPCs can promote the proliferation of OPCs through activating the cholesterol synthesis pathway. However, how the cholesterol synthesis pathway affects OPC proliferation still needs further exploration. We need to determine whether it is attributed to cholesterol itself, intermediate metabolites, or certain enzymes in the cholesterol synthesis pathway.

Conclusions

In summary, our study demonstrates that PTS is a previously uncharted inhibitory factor for OPC proliferation. In the context of EAE and perhaps MS as well, PTS inhibits the proliferation of OPCs and remyelination through suppressing the cholesterol synthesis pathway. Attenuation of aberrantly upregulated PTS expression in OPCs leads to a significant augmentation of OPC proliferation, contributing to amelioration of EAE. Thus, re-establishing OPC proliferative potential represents a promising therapeutic strategy for patients with demyelinating diseases, such as MS and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder.
