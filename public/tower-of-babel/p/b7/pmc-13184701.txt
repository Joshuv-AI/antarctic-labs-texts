Immune checkpoints in lung cancer
Authors: Eva María Garrido-Martín, Milica Kontic, Claire Rooney, Nahal Mansouri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has revolutionised the management of lung cancer, transforming it from a historically immune-resistant malignancy into a paradigm for durable immunotherapy response. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the biological and clinical foundations of checkpoint regulation in the lung and their implications for patient care. We first explore the unique pulmonary immune environment, where continuous exposure to environmental antigens necessitates a delicate balance between tolerance and defence. Within this context, immune checkpoints, such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and its ligand PD-L1 as well as cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4, maintain physiological self-tolerance but can be subverted by tumour cells to evade immune surveillance. Emerging inhibitory receptors, including LAG-3, TIGIT and TIM-3, contribute additional layers of immune regulation and resistance, highlighting opportunities for combinatorial therapeutic strategies. The clinical section summarises pivotal trials establishing ICIs as the standard of care across metastatic, locally advanced and early-stage lung cancer. These agents now span the full disease continuum, from neoadjuvant and adjuvant to perioperative and consolidation settings. We also address the management of immune-related adverse events and the need for precision in patient selection through biomarkers, such as PD-L1 expression, tumour mutational burden and circulating immune signatures. Finally, we discuss ongoing challenges, including mechanisms of primary and acquired resistance, and emerging approaches integrating spatial multi-omics, dynamic immune monitoring and microbiome profiling. Together, these advances are reshaping lung cancer immunotherapy towards a more precise, adaptive and durable model of care.

Introduction

By fine-tuning immune activation, checkpoint pathways maintain tolerance and prevent tissue injury while enabling effective immune surveillance. Physiological pathways, such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and its ligand PD-L1 as well as cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), are essential for maintaining self-tolerance. However, tumour cells frequently exploit these inhibitory circuits to escape immune destruction, fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment that supports tumour persistence and growth [1].

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting PD-1, PD-L1 and CTLA-4 have transformed the treatment landscape of lung cancer, delivering durable responses and prolonged survival across multiple disease stages. They are now the standard of care in metastatic nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC), as consolidation after chemoradiation in stage III disease, and in perioperative settings, as well as in small cell lung cancer in combination with chemotherapy.

In this review, we examine the fundamental principles of immune checkpoint biology as they relate to lung cancer and their translation into clinical practice. We explore the molecular mechanisms of key checkpoint pathways (PD-1/PD-L1, CTLA-4) and emerging regulators, describe how their dysregulation enables immune evasion, and summarise the biomarkers that have guided patient selection and therapeutic strategies.

Immune surveillance and the role of immune checkpoints in tumorigenesis

In lung cancer, immune checkpoint pathways are frequently subverted to silence anti-tumour immunity and promote immune escape. This section outlines the key biological underpinnings of checkpoint regulation in the lung, beginning with the organisation of the pulmonary immune landscape, followed by the physiological roles of PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4, emerging inhibitory pathways, and the mechanisms by which lung tumours exploit these circuits. Finally, it reviews historical milestones and translational biomarkers that link basic checkpoint biology to therapeutic innovation in lung cancer.

The pulmonary immune landscape

The lung's constant exposure to airborne particles, microbes and antigens requires tightly regulated immune defences that preserve tissue integrity while limiting excessive inflammation [2]. Epithelial, neural and immune cells form an integrated signalling network that maintains local immune tone.

Alveolar macrophages act as sentinels, clearing debris and releasing interleukin (IL)-10 and transforming growth factor (TGF)-β to sustain homeostasis, while epithelial-derived mediators, such as CD200 and integrin-bound TGF-β, reinforce macrophage quiescence. Dendritic cells sample antigens and prime adaptive immunity, and tissue-resident lymphocytes provide rapid defence without compromising gas exchange. Chronic exposure to smoke or pathogens disrupts this equilibrium, driving immune exhaustion and promoting tumour progression [3].

This immune-tolerant baseline provides the biological context in which checkpoint pathways operate and helps explain why lung tumours are particularly adept at exploiting inhibitory circuits such as PD-1/PD-L1, thereby shaping response and resistance to immune checkpoint blockade.

Physiological roles of immune checkpoints

T-lymphocytes, including CD4+ and CD8+ subsets, mediate tumour surveillance through cytotoxic activity and cytokine production. T-cell activation requires antigen recognition through the T-cell receptor (TCR) and co-stimulation via CD28–B7 interactions. Immune checkpoints, such as CTLA-4 and PD-1, function as inhibitory receptors that fine-tune T-cell activation to prevent hyperactivation and autoimmunity [4, 5].

CTLA-4 acts early in lymphoid tissue, competing with CD28 for B7-1 and B7-2, thereby limiting IL-2 production and clonal expansion. Its absence causes fatal autoimmunity, while clinical blockade enhances anti-tumour immunity but increases immune-related toxicity [6]. By contrast, PD-1 regulates effector responses in peripheral tissues. Binding to PD-L1 or PD-L2 recruits SHP-2 phosphatase, which inhibits TCR signalling and cytokine release. PD-1 loss results in mild autoimmunity, reflecting its more targeted role [7]. Together, PD-1 and CTLA-4 sustain immune homeostasis and tolerance, but in the tumour microenvironment these same pathways are hijacked to suppress cytotoxic immunity [4].

Emerging checkpoint pathways

Beyond PD-1 and CTLA-4, additional inhibitory receptors regulate T-cell exhaustion and immune tolerance through partially overlapping mechanisms [8].

LAG-3 (lymphocyte activation gene-3) binds to major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II molecules and limits T-cell proliferation, and dual blockade of PD-1 and LAG-3 has enhanced anti-tumour activity in preclinical models, although clinical efficacy has been variable. TIGIT (T-cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains) suppresses both T- and natural killer cell function by competing with the co-stimulatory receptor CD226 for binding to CD155 and CD112, thereby reducing interferon (IFN)-γ production and cytotoxicity. TIM-3 (T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3) engages ligands such as galectin-9 and CEACAM1, delivering inhibitory signals that blunt T-helper cell (Th)1 and cytotoxic T-cell responses while promoting regulatory macrophage polarisation.

These overlapping pathways maintain tissue protection but also create redundant mechanisms that tumours can exploit for immune escape.

Mechanisms of immune evasion in lung cancer

Lung tumours exploit checkpoint signalling through diverse mechanisms that foster an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Pre-invasive lesions often display loss of antigen presentation, human leukocyte antigen (HLA) heterozygosity or neoantigen silencing. PD-L1 expression may be induced by IFN-γ or driven intrinsically by epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) or PI3K/AKT activation. Downregulation of MHC I and β2-microglobulin further impairs cytotoxic recognition, while regulatory T-cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells and tumour-associated macrophages release IL-10, TGF-β and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) to reinforce tolerance [9, 10].

Metabolic reprogramming (i.e. adenosine accumulation, hypoxia, and nutrient depletion) drives T-cell exhaustion. Three major immune phenotypes have been defined (figure 1) [11]. These phenotypes not only differ in T-cell localisation but also in their molecular profiles and response to immune checkpoint blockade: 1) Immune-inflamed tumours (hot tumours) show abundant CD8+ infiltration and PD-L1 expression but often harbour exhausted T-cells driven by chronic antigen exposure. They may respond to novel checkpoint or cell-based therapies.2) Immune-desert tumours (cold tumours) lack immune infiltration due to defective antigen presentation and insufficient chemokine signalling. They may benefit from vaccines or adoptive T-cell transfer.3) Immune-excluded tumours display high PD-L1 and tumour mutational burden (TMB) yet are dominated by stromal barriers and immunosuppressive cytokines such as TGF-β and IL-10 that prevent T-cell access to tumour nests. Historical perspective

Checkpoint biology has evolved from basic immunology into a therapeutic revolution. CTLA-4 was discovered in the late 1980s as a negative regulator of T-cell activation, and PD-1 in 1992 as a mediator of peripheral tolerance. Preclinical models established that blocking these receptors could restore tumour rejection, culminating in the approval of ipilimumab in 2011.

Checkpoint blockade soon extended to lung cancer: nivolumab, pembrolizumab, atezolizumab, and durvalumab significantly improved survival in NSCLC, redefining standards of care [12]. Combination strategies now dominate, integrating chemotherapy, dual checkpoint blockade and anti-angiogenic therapy.

Biomarkers and translational insights

With the advent of checkpoint inhibitors, understanding the biological basis of predictive biomarkers has become critical.

PD-L1 expression reflects an adaptive resistance mechanism, typically induced by IFN-γ signalling within the tumour microenvironment. By engaging PD-1 on T-cells, PD-L1 attenuates cytotoxic function and maintains local tolerance. Expression is dynamic and context dependent, and can be influenced by inflammatory cues and oncogenic pathways. TMB quantifies somatic mutations that may generate neoantigens capable of eliciting T-cell recognition. High TMB, particularly in smoking-related NSCLC, increases the likelihood of immune visibility and underpins its use as a mechanistic biomarker of immunogenicity. Other emerging indicators, including IFN-γ gene signatures, CD8+ T-cell density, and tertiary lymphoid structures, reflect the degree of immune activation within the tumour [13, 14].

Together, these parameters define the biological framework for checkpoint responsiveness and provide a foundation for translational biomarker development.

Future directions

Future research should aim to refine our understanding of checkpoint regulation, resistance and immune-related adverse events (irAEs). Integrating multi-omic profiling, spatial mapping and dynamic immune monitoring will clarify how distinct checkpoints interact and adapt under therapeutic pressure.

Conclusion

The biology of immune checkpoints in lung cancer reveals that tumour progression reflects both malignant evolution and immune dysregulation. In the lung's antigen-rich microenvironment, PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 act as essential guardians of tolerance that, when subverted, enable immune escape. The discovery and therapeutic targeting of these pathways have transformed lung cancer care, while emerging checkpoints (e.g. LAG-3, TIGIT, TIM-3) highlight the layered complexity of immune control.

Clinical application of ICIs in lung cancer

ICIs targeting PD-1, PD-L1 and CTLA-4 have transformed lung cancer management, offering durable responses and prolonged survival across multiple disease stages. Randomised clinical trials in both NSCLC and small cell lung cancer (SCLC) have established ICIs as standards of care in metastatic, locally advanced and, more recently, resectable disease. Metastatic NSCLC

The first major advance came from the KEYNOTE-024 trial, where pembrolizumab monotherapy improved overall survival versus chemotherapy in treatment-naïve patients with PD-L1 ≥50%. This led to routine PD-L1 testing as a prerequisite for first-line therapy. KEYNOTE-042 expanded indications to PD-L1 ≥1%, although most benefit remained in the ≥50% subgroup [15].

For patients with lower PD-L1 expression or high disease burden, chemo-immunotherapy became standard. KEYNOTE-189 (non-squamous) and KEYNOTE-407 (squamous) demonstrated superior overall survival with pembrolizumab+platinum doublet chemotherapy across all PD-L1 strata.

Dual-checkpoint blockade has also shown efficacy. In the CheckMate 227 trial, nivolumab+ipilimumab improved overall survival versus chemotherapy, particularly in PD-L1 ≥1% or high TMB subgroups [16]. The CheckMate 9LA regimen added two cycles of chemotherapy to nivolumab+ipilimumab, improving early disease control with durable benefit across PD-L1 groups.

Additional approved immunotherapy-based regimens have further expanded the first-line treatment options in metastatic NSCLC. The POSEIDON trial demonstrated that durvalumab plus tremelimumab in combination with platinum-based chemotherapy significantly improved overall survival compared with chemotherapy alone, supporting a chemotherapy–dual immunotherapy approach in selected patients. Similarly, the EMPOWER-Lung 1 trial established cemiplimab monotherapy as an effective first-line option in patients with high PD-L1 expression (≥50%), showing superior overall survival compared with chemotherapy. Furthermore, the EMPOWER-Lung 3 trial evaluated cemiplimab in combination with platinum-doublet chemotherapy regardless of PD-L1 status, demonstrating a significant survival benefit and reinforcing the role of chemo-immunotherapy combinations as a standard of care in advanced NSCLC [17].

PD-L1-directed antibodies have further broadened options. IMpower110 confirmed atezolizumab monotherapy has benefit in PD-L1-high NSCLC, while IMpower150 (atezolizumab+bevacizumab+chemotherapy) improved overall survival even in patients with EGFR mutations or liver metastases [17].

SCLC

SCLC, where therapeutic options have long been dominated by chemotherapy, has gained modest but meaningful survival improvements through checkpoint blockade: The IMpower133 trial demonstrated that adding atezolizumab to carboplatin and etoposide significantly improved overall survival and 1-year survival rates in patients with extensive-stage SCLC [18].The CASPIAN trial subsequently confirmed these findings, showing that durvalumab combined with platinum–etoposide also improved overall survival compared with chemotherapy alone in extensive-stage SCLC [19].Both regimens are now considered first-line standards of care and are followed by maintenance PD-L1 inhibition. Ongoing research is focused on optimal treatment sequence, maintenance duration and identification of predictive biomarkers of benefit.

More recently, the role of immunotherapy has expanded into limited-stage SCLC. The ADRIATIC trial evaluated durvalumab as consolidation therapy after concurrent chemoradiotherapy in patients with limited-stage disease and demonstrated significant improvements in both progression-free and overall survival compared with placebo. These findings suggest that consolidation immunotherapy may represent a new standard of care in limited-stage SCLC, extending the benefit of ICIs beyond the extensive-stage SCLC setting [20].

An emerging immunotherapeutic strategy in lung cancer is bispecific T-cell engager (TCE) therapy. These agents bind CD3 on T-lymphocytes and a tumour-associated surface antigen, thereby redirecting endogenous T-cells to tumour cells independently of MHC antigen presentation. The DLL3-targeted bispecific antibody tarlatamab has shown clinically meaningful and durable responses in relapsed SCLC [21]. Because TCEs do not require pre-existing anti-tumour immunity, they may overcome resistance mechanisms to ICI, including impaired antigen presentation and T-cell exclusion. Similar approaches are therefore being explored in NSCLC, where they may represent a future therapeutic option alone or in combination with checkpoint inhibitors.

TCEs have a toxicity profile distinct from both checkpoint inhibitors and cellular therapies. Adverse events are most commonly cytokine release syndrome and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome, which typically occur early and are manageable with step-up dosing, corticosteroids and IL-6 blockade. Delayed autoimmune toxicities are uncommon, and treatment does not require individualised manufacturing or lymphodepleting chemotherapy, supporting the feasibility of broader clinical use.

Perioperative immunotherapy

Recent phase III trials have further expanded the benefit of immunotherapy in resectable NSCLC to combined neoadjuvant and adjuvant (“perioperative”) strategies [23]. The AEGEAN trial evaluated perioperative durvalumab administered with neoadjuvant platinum-based chemotherapy followed by adjuvant durvalumab after surgery and demonstrated significant improvements in event-free survival and pathological complete response without increasing surgical complications. Similarly, the CheckMate-77T trial showed that the addition of perioperative nivolumab to chemotherapy significantly improved event-free survival and increased pathological complete response rates compared with chemotherapy alone. The KEYNOTE-671 trial reported comparable benefits with perioperative pembrolizumab, showing marked improvements in both event-free survival and pathological response. Consistent findings were also observed in the NEOTORCH trial, in which toripalimab combined with chemotherapy in the perioperative setting significantly improved pathological response and event-free survival without adversely affecting resectability or perioperative safety.

Collectively, perioperative studies consistently demonstrate substantial improvements in pathological complete response (∼17–25% compared with 1–5% with chemotherapy alone), as well as significant reductions in the risk of recurrence or progression, with event-free survival hazard ratios generally ranging from about 0.40 to 0.68. These results provide strong clinical evidence supporting the integration of perioperative ICI-based strategies into the management of resectable NSCLC, although overall survival data remain immature for several regimens.

Locally advanced unresectable disease

The PACIFIC trial defined a new standard with durvalumab consolidation after concurrent chemoradiation, extending overall survival and progression-free survival in stage III unresectable NSCLC.

The clinical takeaway is that ICIs now play a role at every curative-intent stage, from neoadjuvant priming to adjuvant maintenance, demonstrating deeper responses and prolonged survival.

Safety and management of irAEs

irAEs stem from nonspecific immune activation and may involve any organ. Commonly affected sites include the skin, gastrointestinal tract, liver, endocrine glands and lungs.

Pneumonitis warrants particular attention in lung cancer, as it may mimic infection, tumour progression, radiation pneumonitis or heart failure, and is a major cause of treatment interruption, chronic respiratory impairment and treatment-related mortality. The clinical spectrum ranges from mild, subacute pneumonitis to fulminant respiratory failure, with long-term consequences including pulmonary fibrosis and oxygen dependence [27].

The risk of immune-related pneumonitis is increased with combination ICI regimens, particularly anti-PD-(L)1 plus anti-CTLA-4 therapy, and is associated with higher all-cause mortality in patients with NSCLC receiving ICIs. Diagnosis is challenging owing to nonspecific symptoms and the absence of validated biomarkers; therefore, high clinical suspicion, temporal association with therapy, exclusion of alternative causes and characteristic high-resolution computed tomography patterns are required.

Management requires prompt interruption of immunotherapy and early initiation of systemic corticosteroids. In steroid-refractory cases, escalation to additional immunosuppressive agents is required. Although current guidance from American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO), European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) and Society for Immunotherapy of Cancer (SITC) provides grade-based algorithms, substantial variability remains, and long-term pulmonary surveillance and multidisciplinary management are increasingly advocated to prevent irreversible lung injury.

Endocrinopathies, including hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency and hypophysitis, may develop insidiously and often require lifelong hormone replacement; regular monitoring of thyroid and cortisol levels is recommended. Gastrointestinal and hepatic toxicities are managed with grade-based steroid protocols, with escalation to anti-tumour necrosis factor therapy or vedolizumab for refractory colitis.

For grade 1 irAEs, ICIs may be continued with close monitoring; grade 2 events typically require temporary interruption and corticosteroids, whereas grade 3–4 toxicities necessitate permanent discontinuation and aggressive immunosuppression. Re-initiation of ICIs may be considered in selected patients following resolution of moderate irAEs, depending on organ system and severity.

Future directions

ICIs are now integral to lung cancer therapy across the disease continuum. Future gains will arise from stage migration (earlier use), rational combinations (e.g. radiotherapy, anti-angiogenic or targeted agents), and precision integration using dynamic biomarkers and circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) monitoring.

Efforts to refine treatment duration, re-challenge strategies and real-time immune profiling aim to tailor therapy while minimising toxicity. As perioperative and adaptive immunotherapy trials mature, lung cancer care is shifting decisively towards long-term disease control – and, for a growing subset of patients, potential cure.

Conclusion

ICIs have fundamentally reshaped the management of lung cancer, extending survival and in selected settings, achieving durable remission. Their integration across metastatic, locally advanced and resectable disease reflects a true paradigm shift – from palliative control to potential cure. Optimal application now depends on harmonising clinical trial evidence with individual patient characteristics, biomarker profiles and comorbidity considerations. As our understanding of immune resistance and tumour–host interplay deepens, the next frontier lies in precision immunotherapy, i.e. delivering the right agent, in the right combination, at the right disease stage, to maximise efficacy while minimising toxicity.

Challenges, biomarkers and future directions

ICIs targeting PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 have revolutionised oncology, yielding durable responses across multiple malignancies including melanoma, lung and renal cancers. Yet, despite these successes, only a minority of patients achieve long-term benefit. Tumour-intrinsic and microenvironmental mechanisms frequently drive both primary and acquired resistance, while irAEs remain a clinical challenge. This section focuses on mechanisms of ICI resistance, the evolution of biomarkers, and the expanding landscape of novel targets and therapeutic strategies designed to overcome resistance.

Limitations of current checkpoint strategies

Checkpoint blockade monotherapy has clear limitations due to the complexity of tumour–immune interactions. Resistance may arise de novo (primary resistance) or following an initial response (acquired resistance).

Primary resistance occurs when immune activation fails to develop despite therapy. This is often observed in “immune-desert” or “immune-excluded” tumours, where effector T-cells are absent or excluded from tumour nests. These immune phenotypes can result from defective antigen presentation (e.g. β2-microglobulin loss, MHC downregulation), oncogenic signalling (WNT/β-catenin activation, PTEN loss), or enrichment of immunosuppressive myeloid and stromal cells that secrete TGF-β, VEGF and CXCL12 to block T-cell trafficking [28, 29].

Acquired (adaptive) resistance develops after an initial response, reflecting the selective pressure imposed by immune attack. Mechanisms include loss of neoantigen expression, disruption of IFN-γ signalling, and compensatory upregulation of inhibitory checkpoints such as TIM-3, LAG-3 and VISTA [30]. Tumour cells may also undergo metabolic adaptation, for instance through increased oxidative phosphorylation or production of immunosuppressive metabolites, such as kynurenine via IDO1 and CD39/CD73 pathways, thereby impairing effector T-cell function [31, 32].

Collectively, these findings illustrate why single-agent checkpoint blockade rarely achieves durable control in the face of dynamic and multifaceted resistance mechanisms within the tumour microenvironment.

Emerging biomarkers and immune profiling

Accurate biomarkers are essential to predict response to ICIs and guide therapy, yet conventional markers (PD-L1 expression, TMB and microsatellite instability) have limited predictive value when used in isolation. PD-L1 immunohistochemistry is confounded by intratumoural heterogeneity and assay variability; high TMB does not always equate to neoantigen immunogenicity; and high microsatellite instability status applies to only a small subset of cancers [33].

Recent advances are shifting the focus towards integrated, multidimensional profiling, combining spatial, liquid and transcriptomic analyses. Challenges in personalising immune checkpoint therapy

Personalised immunotherapy requires integration of tumour genomics, host immunity and environmental influences. Variations in HLA genotype, germline immunogenetics, prior therapy and microbiome composition all modulate the response to ICIs.

Emerging computational frameworks and machine learning models now integrate genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic and microbiomic datasets to predict treatment response and resistance. These systems aim to generate adaptive, data-driven algorithms that enable real-time therapy adjustment based on evolving immune signatures [44]. Such approaches will underpin the transition from empirical to precision-guided immunotherapy.

Future directions

Recent neoadjuvant and adjuvant ICI trials demonstrate that early immune engagement can eradicate micro-metastatic disease and induce durable systemic immunity. Short-course perioperative PD-1 blockade reprogrammes the tumour microenvironment, enhances antigen presentation and expands effector T-cell populations, translating into improved relapse-free survival.

Looking forward, precision immunotherapy will increasingly rely on spatial multi-omics, liquid biopsy analytics and digital pathology to define evolving immune states. This integration will allow clinicians to deliver the right checkpoint inhibitor, or combination, tailored to the right patient at the right time.

Conclusion

While PD-1 blockade remains the cornerstone of immune checkpoint therapy, its limitations highlight the need for more nuanced, biologically informed strategies. The convergence of immunology, systems biology and computational modelling promises to reshape immunotherapy into a truly personalised, adaptive discipline that aligns dynamic tumour–immune interactions with precision treatment to achieve durable control.

Discussion

The biology of immune checkpoints in lung cancer underscores that tumour progression represents both malignant evolution and the collapse of immune surveillance. In the lung's antigen-rich environment, regulatory pathways such as PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4, which ordinarily protect against tissue-damaging inflammation, are hijacked by tumour cells to suppress anti-tumour immunity. The consequence is an immunologically restrained tumour microenvironment characterised by impaired antigen presentation, exhausted cytotoxic T-cells, and the dominance of inhibitory signalling networks.

The discovery of these pathways and their therapeutic blockade transformed lung cancer from an archetype of immune resistance into one of immunotherapy's landmark successes. Yet, immune evasion in lung cancer is multifactorial and extends beyond PD-1 and CTLA-4 to additional regulators including LAG-3, TIGIT and TIM-3. These overlapping inhibitory circuits emphasise that durable immune control will require rational combination or sequential strategies that address both primary and acquired resistance. Ongoing work is therefore essential to delineate the molecular mechanisms driving resistance, to identify biomarkers that predict responsiveness, and to fine-tune therapeutic interventions that maximise efficacy while minimising toxicity.

At the same time, the biology underlying checkpoint activation also explains the emergence of irAEs. These toxicities, reflecting the same immune reinvigoration responsible for tumour control, highlight the narrow therapeutic window between restoring immunity and provoking autoimmunity. Deeper mechanistic understanding of tissue-specific immune tolerance and checkpoint signalling will be crucial to achieving the optimal balance between anti-tumour efficacy and immune safety.

Together, these insights form the foundation upon which modern immunotherapy is built. They bridge molecular immunology with clinical practice, informing the design of more selective and adaptable checkpoint strategies. Ultimately, the challenge ahead lies in translating this biological complexity into precise, durable and safe therapeutic outcomes – an endeavour that will continue to shape the next generation of immunotherapy for lung cancer.
