Management of pneumonia by the emergency radiologist
Authors: Juana María Plasencia-Martínez, Joaquín Bravo-Urbieta, Ana Blanco-Barrio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSusceptibility to pulmonary infection depends not only on the patient’s clinical condition but also on the characteristics of the microorganism, such as its virulence and transmissibility, which are influenced by environmental factors, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and the emergence of pandemics. Understanding these factors, together with the patterns of pulmonary dissemination and their radiological appearances, helps narrow the differential diagnosis. In the emergency setting, however, the radiologist’s role extends beyond suggesting the aetiology of pneumonia. Recognition of potential complications, guidance on treatment implications, and assessment of prognosis are key contributions. This paper illustrates these concepts through a literature review and real clinical cases.Keywords: Pneumonia, Emergencies, Diagnosis, Infections

Introduction

Pneumonia is the infectious disease associated with the highest rates of hospitalization and mortality among adults in developed countries [1], with these rates increasing in the presence of comorbidities and advancing age. Establishing a definitive microbiological diagnosis in pneumonia is uncommon. Antimicrobial treatment is initiated empirically depending on the clinical suspicion and should subsequently be adjusted based on microbiological test results or evolution. Imaging studies play a key role in the diagnosis of pneumonia, assessment of severity, follow-up and interventional management in selected cases. Understanding each patient’s individual risk factors, severity assessment and conditions that influence the choice of treatment is essential for radiologists in the emergency context.

The aim of this study is to review the keys for the management of pneumonia particularly in the emergency radiology setting by a brief literature review and showing real cases.

Diagnostic and prognostic value of imaging tests

The diagnosis of CAP is based on the presence of compatible clinical symptoms (cough, sputum, chest pain or shortness of breath), together with confirmation by an imaging modality, including chest X-ray (CXR), computed tomography (CT), or ultrasound [2], [3], [4].

The approach for management in immunocompetent patients presenting with symptoms suggestive of pneumonia is represented as a flowchart in Fig. 1[2], [3], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]. CXR (posteroanterior and lateral views) is indicated as initial test when the physical examination is abnormal, vital signs are altered, there are poor prognostic factors, or in cases of exacerbated COPD; otherwise, CXR indication is not clear. CXR may be normal at symptom onset, with abnormalities appearing 12–48 h later [10]. Contrast-enhanced CT (CT) should be performed when complications such as lung abscess or complicated parapneumonic effusion is suspected and when clinical suspicions persist despite a normal or indeterminate CXR. The indication for ultrasound in complicated pleural effusions is uncertain, although it is more sensitive than CT for detecting septa and can guide diagnostic aspiration or drainage. As regards radiological follow-up with CXR, the evidence on this is limited, but it is not recommended when clinical improvement occurs within 5–7 days after the onset of pneumonia [6]. In fact, it can lead to confusion because clinical manifestations typically precede radiological changes and a clinical improvement may coincide with a radiological worsening. Follow-up CXR or further imaging with CT is recommended when unfavourable clinical evolution, clinical-radiological discordance or when imaging findings raise suspicion of alternative diagnoses such as neoplasia (e.g., nodular opacity or associated hilar mass) [10]. Finally, in elderly and underlying lung disease (e.g emphysema), radiological opacities may take up to 2–3 months to resolve, therefore.

In immunocompromised patients with suspected pneumonia, a CXR is recommended as the initial test [11], [12]. However, CXR may remain normal for up to 72 h after the onset of symptoms [11]. When the result does not match the clinical suspicion, urgent CT should be performed because pneumonia may be detected at an early stage. For example, invasive fungal infection can be suspected up to 5 days earlier by CT than microbiological tests [12], [13], [14]

Regarding non-radiological complementary tests, PCR testing for viruses in nasopharyngeal swab and urinary antigen testing for both Legionella pneumophila and Streptococcus pneumoniae are usually available in the emergency setting. Serum antigen testing for Aspergillus and Cryptococcus, and Beta-D-glucan testing for Mucor [11], [15] can be useful when clinical suspicion exists.

In order to narrow down the cause and severity of the infection, the main factors to investigate in an emergency setting are the timing of the onset of symptoms, reached temperature, oxygen saturation, extrathoracic symptoms, lack of response to antibiotics, hemogram and microbiological tests data when available, age, recent hospital admissions, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), any form of immunosuppression or risk behaviour and appropriate administration of specific immunisations and prophylaxis.

Pathophysiology and radiological semiology

To facilitate interpretation of radiological findings, pulmonary infections have been structured according to the predisposing routes of dissemination and pulmonary localization of the pathogen.

Alveolar space involvement

This is the most common pattern in bacterial CAP. Streptococcus pneumoniae is the most frequent pathogen. Pathogens may reach the lung through inhalation or, according to more recent theories, may originate from the pulmonary or tracheobronchial microbiota, which can overgrow when local host–pathogen balance is disrupted [2]. Infection of the alveolar space leads to damage of the alveolar wall with haemorrhagic oedema, followed by rapid microbial proliferation and infiltration by polymorphonuclear leukocytes. The inflammatory process extends from the alveoli to the alveolar ducts and terminal bronchioles, and spreads between adjacent alveoli through the pores of Kohn. This rapid interalveolar spread results in segmental consolidation or, less commonly in the current antibiotic era, lobar consolidation [10].

On CXR this pattern corresponds to lobar pneumonia, characterized by homogeneous, confluent opacities with subpleural onset and centripetal spread, often with air bronchograms [10]. On CT, consolidations (Fig. 2a) and ground-glass opacities (GGO, - Fig. 2b-) can be detected. Cavitation (Fig. 2c) is a sign of severity (see severity section).

Distal or peripheral airway involvement

This pattern is most frequently observed in HAP by nosocomial bacteria, such as MRSA or Pseudomonas aeruginosa, but it can also occur due to virulent bacteria from the community environment, such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, or viruses. Aspiration of colonized tracheal secretions results in pathogen deposition within distal bronchioles, leading to peribronchiolar exudate formation. Inflammation spreads along the respiratory and terminal bronchioles, producing multifocal infiltrates across multiple secondary pulmonary lobules, with potential extension between lobes. The resulting imaging pattern is known as bronchopneumonia or lobular pneumonia, characterized by a patchy distribution without lobar predilection [10] with centrolobulillar (Fig. 2d) and tree in bud nodules (Fig. 2e).

Interstitial involvement

Interstitial pneumonia is commonly caused by viral infections and by some community-acquired bacteria such as Mycoplasma spp., Chlamydia spp., and Rickettsia spp. Some authors also include infections caused by Pneumocystis jirovecii [10] and Legionella pneumophila [2]. This pattern is characterized by oedema and mononuclear cell infiltration of the peribronchial interstitium and bronchial walls, with subsequent spread through the interstitium of the alveolar walls. The alveoli are relatively spared, distinguishing this process from typical lobar pneumonia and giving rise to the controversial term “atypical pneumonia” [16].

On imaging tests, this pattern manifests as thickening of the distal bronchial walls and a reticular or reticulonodular pattern extending from the hilar regions towards the lung periphery. On CT, typical findings include GGO, thickening of the interlobular and intralobular septa, both (crazy paving pattern, Fig. 2f) and centrilobular or tree-in-bud nodules when the small airways are involved. Areas of consolidation may also be present.

Vascular space involvement

This pattern includes conditions such as septic emboli, angioinvasive fungal pneumonias, direct vascular involvement (e.g., COVID−19), or hematogenous dissemination of certain parasites. Septic emboli are caused pulmonary veins infectious occlusion, leading to venous infarction (Fig. 2g). Fungal invasion more typically occurs at pulmonary arteries, resulting in arterial infarction (Fig. 2h). In both, a central hypodensity or cavitation can be seen, and surrounding GGO correspond to haemorrhagic component. In mucor infection, in addition to the inverted halo sign caused by an arterial infarction (Fig. 2i), direct signs of arterial invasion (thrombosis, pseudoaneurysms) may be apparent. Local vascular inflammation (endarteritis) and/or thrombosis has been described in areas of the lung affected by SARS-CoV−2 pneumonia, manifested on CT as enlarged distal arteries (Fig. 2j). Echinococcus granulosus can embolize through the systemic venous circulation to the pulmonary arteries (Fig. 2k). When a haematogenous spread of infection finally reaches the parenchyma, a miliary nodular pattern develops (Fig. 2l), which is typical of mycobacterial infections.

Immunological status

Immunocompetent patient

CAP

CAP means pneumonia acquired outside the hospital or after recent hospitalization but more than 48 h ago. The previously classified healthcare-associated pneumonia is currently included as CAP [16], [17]. A pathogen is identified in only 38% of cases [2], [9]. Streptococcus pneumoniae accounts for just 15%, and up to 40% are viral [5]. Viral and bacterial co-infection is common, and no diagnostic test can reliably distinguish between viral and bacterial etiologist at the onset [3]. Between 10% and 40% of patients with CAP require hospitalisation, and among these, 5–20% intensive care unit (ICU) admission [18]. Thirty-day mortality in adults after hospitalization for CAP ranges from 3% under 60 years to 27% aged 60 and older [2], [19] (Fig. 1).

Pneumonia caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, and Gram-negative bacteria typically presents acutely, with purulent sputum, fever, chills, and pleuritic chest pain. Atypical pneumonia usually has a subacute presentation, characterized by nonproductive cough, low-grade fever, myalgia, and dyspnea, and therefore requires a different antibiotic regimen.

Bacteria

Streptococcus pneumoniae is the most frequent bacterial cause of CAP. The most common and characteristic radiological presentation is a lobar or sublobar pneumonia due to alveolar space involvement. Indicators of severity include the presence of multifocal consolidations at diagnosis (Fig. 1), pleural effusion, necrosis, cavitation and/or abscess formation in the consolidation(s) (see the severity section). Streptococcus pneumoniae is the most common pathogen associated with necrotizing pneumonia, resulting in cavitary pneumonia in 7% (Fig. 3).

Virus

According to some references, viruses represent the most identified pathogens in CAP [5]. Multifocal patchy GGO, crazy paving pattern, consolidations, and distal airway nodules with centrolobulillar nodules < 1 cm are typical CT findings. Notable exceptions include adenovirus infection, which may mimic pneumococcal pneumonia, and varicella, characterized by diffuse poorly defined nodules (4–6 mm), sometimes surrounded by a ground-glass halo [20] (Fig. 5a). Varicella pneumonia usually develops after the onset of cutaneous lesions and is accompanied by respiratory symptoms such as cough, dyspnea, and fever. The disease can be severe, with radiological resolution taking several weeks and, in some cases, leaving residual pulmonary calcifications as sequelae [21] (Fig. 5b) with no predilection for upper lobes, unlike post-tuberculous granulomas. Pleural effusion is rare in viral infections.

Parasites

Echinococcus granulosus

Infection with Echinococcus granulosus causes cystic echinococcosis or hydatid disease. Humans are accidental intermediate hosts, typically infected through contact with livestock or dogs carrying the parasite, by ingestion of eggs that penetrate the intestinal wall and enter the systemic circulation. Although rare and often asymptomatic in its early stages, the disease frequently involves multiple organs and may be mistaken for neoplastic pathology. The liver is most commonly affected (60–70% of cases), with subsequent spread to the lungs (20–30%), either by contiguous extension or hematogenous dissemination [22]. Positive serology plays a crucial role in diagnosis. From a radiological perspective, the presence of single or multiple pulmonary cysts with thin, non-calcified walls that do not collapse after drainage into the airways, containing fluid or air, and occasionally demonstrating floating membranes, in association with extrapulmonary cysts that may show wall calcification, is highly suggestive of hydatid disease [22]. HAP and VAP

The distinctive feature of hospital-acquired pneumonia is that it is caused by more virulent pathogens and that are more likely to be resistant to antibiotics than those causing community-acquired pneumonia. HAP is defined as pneumonia developed more than 48 h after admission and accounts for 15–22% of hospital-acquired infections [23]. Among HAP cases, 20–40% fail to respond to empirical antibiotic therapy and require ICU care. VAP is defined as pneumonia occurring in patients receiving mechanical ventilation [16]. It affects up to 20% of patients on mechanical ventilation for more than 48 h and accounts for over 80% of pneumonia cases in ICU [24]. Studies in the United States and Europe indicate that over 40% of cases of hospital-acquired pneumonia are attributed to Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. In Eastern Europe and the US, the proportion of multidrug-resistant pathogens is higher than in Western Europe [24]. The main risk factor of infection with multidrug-resistant pathogens is a previous hospital admission (Fig. 1), in whom is a priority to perform an antibiogram to treatment adjusting. The mortality rate reported for both HAP and VAP is 30–70% [23]. In patients requiring mechanical ventilation, multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa pneumonia is common and mortality rates exceeding 60% [25].

Radiological manifestations can be highly variable (Fig. 7), although a pattern of bronchopneumonia is frequently observed for airway involvement, frequently with cavitary and/or abscess-forming lung lesions (Fig. 8). The process sometimes progresses to fulminant acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), most commonly in the setting of bacteraemia.

Immunosuppressed patient

Across the European Union, 14.5 million estimated immunocompromised people, including patients living with an organ transplant, affected by cancer or on immunosuppressants, and about 765 000 people are living with HIV [26].

Annual immunization against influenza, pneumococcus, and Haemophilus influenzae is recommended for all patients with any form of immunosuppression [11]. Prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) is recommended in PLWH with CD4 T-cell count < 200 cells/µL, Citomegalovirus (CMV) prophylaxis, in seropositive SOT recipients and Herpesvirus prophylaxis in patients with hematological neoplasms [11].

The difficulty of establishing the etiological diagnosis of pneumonia in an immunocompromised patient is multifactorial and is influenced by:

•Difficulty in obtaining samples for etiological diagnosis due to the patients' clinical condition.

Specific immunological susceptibility profiles

Due to their frequency, severity, and potential improvement with early detection and treatment, the following specific conditions of immunosuppression deserve particular attention: transplant recipients, patients receiving immunosuppressive pharmacological therapy, patients with immunosuppressive diseases and PLWH.

Transplant recipients

Bacterial infections

Susceptibility is increased both during the HSCT conditioning period—including pneumococcal and more aggressive bacterial infections—and after initiation of immunosuppressive therapy beyond day 100, not only in HSCT recipients but also in SOT recipients, including infections caused by Legionella and Nocardia.

Nocardia is an opportunistic aerobic bacterium that primarily affects the lungs and has a predilection for the central nervous system (CNS), which is involved in approximately 33% of cases. Nodules or consolidations are the typical radiological manifestations, showing ring enhancement and a hypodense center on contrast-enhanced CT, and may cavitate (Fig. 9). Although uncommon, it may invade extrathoracic structures and cause subcortical brain abscesses (Fig. 9c) [20]. A subacute clinical course combined with the characteristic imaging findings should raise suspicion for Nocardia spp. pneumonia. Failure to suspect this pathogen during microbiological processing is a common cause of false-negative results.

Viral infections

Viral pneumonia classically occurs within 2–3 months following HSCT. Its radiological manifestations typically consist of GGO pattern or crazy-paving pattern, as well as poorly defined ground-glass nodules smaller than 1 cm (Fig. 10a). When well-defined pulmonary nodules larger than 1 cm coexist with typical features of viral pneumonia, or when imaging findings are discordant with the overall disease pattern or follow-up evolution, alternative or additional diagnoses should be considered [27]. In this setting, prolonged neutropenia and progressive enlargement of nodules strongly suggest invasive fungal infection, particularly angioinvasive aspergillosis The absence of an early ground-glass halo does not exclude this diagnosis, as it is present in only 20% of cases and is typically transient, lasting less than one week [27] (Fig. 10 b-c).

Fungal Infections

Assessment of severe neutropenia is crucial, as is expected in patients undergoing chemotherapy conditioning before HSCT. The most common and well-recognized forms of pulmonary fungal infection are angioinvasive aspergillosis (80%, Fig. 12a) and airway-invasive aspergillosis (20%, Fig. 12b), which typically occur in patients with hematologic malignancies and severe neutropenia during conditioning therapy and the early post-HSCT period.

(a) Lung nodules in contact with pleural surfaces which suggested interstitial location, with halo sign (arrowheads), due to angioinvasive aspergillosis. 12b. Centrilobular nodules, not in contact with the pleural surfaces (arrowheads), due to airway-invasive aspergillosis.

Pulmonary mucormycosis deserves particular attention as an increasingly frequent, highly aggressive invasive fungal infection associated with poor prognosis. Susceptibility to mucormycosis may result from several immunosuppressive conditions [27]. Early initiation of targeted antifungal therapy and, in selected cases, prompt surgical lung resection are decisive for patient outcomes. Early diagnosis requires a high index of suspicion and is critical, as there are no specific laboratory biomarkers, unlike aspergillosis, for which galactomannan testing may be useful. Moreover, microbiological confirmation is frequently not achieved. On imaging studies, mucormycosis should be suspected in the presence of nodular consolidations, nodules, or mass-like lesions displaying one or more of the following characteristics: (a) poorly visible or absent air bronchograms; (b) a complete or incomplete thick peripheral consolidative rim [27]; (c) a central area of lower attenuation, which may appear as hypodense consolidation (hypodense center sign), ground-glass opacity (reverse halo sign) or a crazy-paving pattern (bird’s nest sign) [27]; (d) Extensive adjacent ground-glass opacity. Signs of invasion are often subtle and should be actively sought, including amputated pulmonary arteries within lesions, pleural invasion, extrapleural extension, or mediastinal invasion, all of which occur more frequently than in angioinvasive aspergillosis [27] (Fig. 13).

Patients receiving immunosuppressive pharmacological therapy

Anti-B-cell therapies (anti-CD20 agents)

Anti-CD20 agents, such as rituximab, are widely used as maintenance therapy for follicular lymphoma and for some rheumatologic diseases. Antibodies generated by B lymphocytes are essential for antiviral immunity. Anti-CD20 drugs block B lymphocytes and impair antibody production both against infections and in response to vaccination, thereby increasing the risk of severe infections. When administered to patients with follicular lymphoma, anti-CD20 therapy promotes SARS-CoV−2 pneumonia progression from organizing pneumonia (OP) (Fig. 14 a–f) to severe with diffuse alveolar damage (Fig. 14 g-i) [20], [28], depending on the alveolo-capillary membrane damage degree within the spectrum of acute lung injury [20], [28]. In patients with rheumatologic diseases, rituximab increases susceptibility to severe pulmonary viral infections [20].

Corticosteroid therapy

Due to impairing both cellular and humoral Immunity, increasing susceptibility to bacterial, viral, and fungal infections. Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) deserves special attention particularly when prolonged treatment with high doses, such as those treated for primary or secondary brain neoplasms. In contrast to PJP in PLWH, which usually presents a subacute course, corticosteroid-associated PJP often manifests as acute and rapidly progressive pneumonia, and a diffuse GGO radiological pattern. Decisions regarding prophylaxis are guided by corticosteroid dose and duration, the presence of underlying structural lung disease, and the use of additional immunosuppressive agents.

Drugs associated with agranulocytosis

Drugs associated with agranulocytosis, such as metamizole, may induce neutropenia, increasing susceptibility to virulent bacterial infections (Staphylococcus aureus, Gram-negative bacilli, Klebsiella spp.) and mucormycosis (Fig. 16).

Patients with immunosuppressive diseases

Diabetes mellitus

Patients with diabetes mellitus exhibit increased susceptibility to infections caused by virulent bacteria, including Staphylococcus aureus, Gram-negative bacilli, and Klebsiella spp., as well as mucormycosis.

Attention should be paid to invasive Klebsiella pneumoniae liver abscess syndrome (IKLAS) [29], which occurs more frequently in diabetic patients and corresponds to multiorgan (lung and liver) septic emboli. In septic patients, the presence of multiple pulmonary nodules with imaging features suggestive of infarction—typically peripheral, poorly defined, and associated with peripheral ground-glass opacities and/or cavitation—should raise suspicion for septic pulmonary emboli. The infectious source should include the endocardium but also the gastrointestinal tract, skin and oral cavity (Fig. 17)

Hematologic neoplasms

In patients with hematologic neoplasms, both humoral and cellular immunity are impaired. As a result, these patients have increased susceptibility to bacterial (Fig. 19), viral and fungal infections. The specific pattern of infectious susceptibility depends on the underlying hematologic disorder, disease stage, and treatment regimen. Regarding viral infections, these patients are highly susceptible to late-onset viral pneumonia and antiviral prophylaxis, particularly against herpesviruses, are frequently administered. In this population, more than 50% of cases require hospitalization, and mortality exceeds 10%. Risk and disease severity increase in the presence of profound lymphopenia (absolute lymphocyte count <200 cells/mL), and in patients receiving active oncologic treatment. It is usually suspected clinically when opacities fail to respond to antibiotic therapy. Imaging findings in viral pneumonia are highly variable and often overlaps with those of other infectious agents. Multifocal patchy GGO with small (<1 cm) poorly defined centrilobular nodules or tree-in-bud nodules, crazy-paving pattern and consolidations are common manifestations [21] (Fig. 20).

A 71-year-old woman under follow-up for non-Hodgkin lymphoma underwent PET-CT, which demonstrated lymphomatous relapse and pulmonary metabolically activity, with a standardized uptake value of 5.3. Subsequent CECT identified a consolidation with air bronchogram and ground-glass opacities in the left upper lobe. Microbiological testing detected Legionella pneumophila. The patient showed clinical improvement four days after initiation of targeted antibiotic therapy.

An 82-year-old man with relapsed non-Hodgkin lymphoma presented with bronchopneumonia that was unresponsive to antibiotic therapy. Non-CECT demonstrated extensive patchy crazy-paving pattern, non-dominant consolidations (black arrowhead), peribronchovascular interstitial thickening (black arrow), and bilateral pleural effusions. A diagnosis of CMV pneumonia was established.

PLWH

In PLWH, both susceptibility to infection and the pattern of pulmonary involvement are closely related to degree of CD4 T-cell depletion [30]. The most common cause of pneumonia PLWH is bacterial infection, predominantly due to Streptococcus pneumoniae, followed by Pneumocystis jirovecii, with tuberculosis ranking third [30]. Other pathogens are Nocardia, Rhodococcus, viruses, Cryptococcus and parasites (Table 1).

Key elements in diagnostic orientation include CD4 T-cell count, the temporal evolution of symptoms (acute versus subacute) and the presence of extrapulmonary manifestations, such as CNS involvement [30].

Imaging features of bacterial pneumonia generally do not differ substantially from those observed in immunocompetent patients, although multifocal involvement is more frequent.

When radiological findings consist predominantly of diffuse GGO, PJP should be the primary consideration in both known and previously undiagnosed HIV infection (Fig. 21 a-d). This diagnosis is further supported by a subacute clinical course associated with severe hypoxemia. Although pulmonary cysts are characteristic of PJP in PLWH, they typically appear late in the disease course and are therefore of limited value for early diagnosis. The additional presence of numerous poorly defined distal airway nodules smaller than 1 cm, particularly in patients with very low CD4 T-cell count (<50 cells/mL), should raise suspicion for viral infection, most notably CMV [21]. CMV infection is rare in PLWH, and it is usually associated with PJP (Fig. 21 e-f).

Severity: complications

The findings on imaging tests suggestive of complicated or highly virulent pneumonia include multilobar involvement, necrosis, cavitation or abscess formation, and the presence of pleural effusion, particularly when exudative features such as loculation, septations, or pleural enhancement are present [10].

Necrosis

Necrosis of lung parenchyma occurs in approximately 7% of bacterial pneumonia cases [31]. It is more frequently associated with highly virulent microorganisms such as Staphylococcus aureus, Gram-negative bacilli, and anaerobic bacteria; however, Streptococcus pneumoniae remains the most common [32]. Related entities include:

Necrotizing pneumonia

is a severe inflammatory pulmonary process, characterized by arteritis, vascular thrombosis, ischemic necrosis, and subsequent destruction of lung parenchyma. On CECT, it manifests as areas of pulmonary consolidation with reduced enhancement. Cavitation may be present if necrotic material communicates with the airway, with or without an air–fluid level [33] (Fig. 23a). Regarding differential diagnosis, it should not be confused with consolidations superimposed on emphysematous lung, where radiolucent areas are due to pre-existing parenchymal destruction, not to true cavitation.

Pneumatocele

is commonly associated with Staphylococcus aureus. It is caused by parenchymal necrosis combined with a valvular airway obstruction mechanism [34], rather than from alveolar rupture. On CT, this entity presents as a single or multiple thin-walled, regular cystic lesions, often located subpleurally, which may enlarge rapidly (Fig. 8 a-b).

Lung abscess

represents a localized collection of purulent material possibly resulting from infection of a necrotic lung area. On CECT, it appears as a rounded lesion with homogeneously hypodense contents and a thick, hyperenhancing wall (Fig. 8 g-h). If the abscess communicates with the airway, gas enters the cavity, producing an air–fluid level whose size remains consistent across different CXR projections due to the spherical shape of the lesion. Differential diagnosis includes necrotizing pneumonia and cavitary neoplasm. Differentiation from necrotizing pneumonia can be challenging, although both are associated with virulent pathogens and treated similarly. Regarding cavitary neoplasm, the cavity walls may be thick and irregular in both, but lung abscess is commonly surrounded by consolidation. The presence of an air–fluid level is not pathognomonic of an abscess and may occur in these neoplasms [34].

Bronchopleural fistula

occurs when necrotic parenchyma communicates with the pleural space. On CECT, it may present as focal peripheral area of low-attenuation consolidation that directly communicate with an exudative pleural effusion through a small disruption of the visceral pleura, not always visible. Gas presence within the pleural cavity is variable.

Complicated pleural effusion

Pleural effusion occurs in approximately 40% of bacterial pneumonias. Most are uncomplicated parapneumonic effusions, consisting of sterile pleural exudate adjacent to pulmonary consolidation (exudative phase), which may resolve spontaneously. Approximately 10% of effusions become complicated when bacteria invade the pleural space, leading to an inflammatory response with polymorphonuclear leukocytes, fibrin deposition, and septation (fibrinopurulent phase), progressing to empyema and possible loculation. In the organization phase, fibroblast proliferation in the visceral and parietal pleura results in a fibrous pleural peel, which may extend through the chest wall, producing empyema necessitatis.

Complicated pleural effusion is diagnosed by positive Gram stain and/or culture, pleural fluid pH < 7.2, and fibrin deposition with septa and locules. On imaging tests, it may be suspected in the presence of loculations, intrapleural gas, septations, echogenic fluid on ultrasound, and thickening and enhancement of the parietal (90% of the cases) and/or visceral pleura (less frequent) on CECT (split pleura sign [35] -Fig. 3b-). Additional findings on CT may include increased density and thickening of the extrapleural fat and adjacent soft tissues. In patients with possible pneumonia and a pleural effusion > 2 cm thick, urgent diagnostic thoracocentesis with aspiration is indicated. An empyema requires urgent drainage, needing image guidance when they are small or difficult to access [35] (Fig. 3c-d). Surgical decortication is sometimes needed.

ARDS

Some rapidly progressive, fulminant viral or bacterial pneumonias may quickly lead to sepsis and ARDS. It is particularly common in herpes simplex virus infection (detected in 71% of samples from patients with ARDS - [21]-), and in 17–29% of COVID−19 pneumonia [36], usually associated with immunosuppressive factors (Table 1). And it is particularly severe in H1N1 infection [21]. The clinical onset usually precedes the radiological findings, as treatment-resistant hypoxia and early mechanical ventilation requirements. Pathophysiologically, parenchymal damage impairs the three layers of the alveolar-capillary membrane and leads to diffuse alveolar damage, the most severe entity within the spectrum of acute lung injury (recommended reading [28]). On imaging, it manifests as pulmonary opacities distributed diffusely or patchily, extending to the pulmonary periphery (Fig. 14 h). Pleural effusion is variable.

Conclusion

The radiological features of pneumonia on a CXR in an emergency setting, combined with key findings such as the patient’s immune status, are powerful tools for determining the etiology and severity of an infection. Certain radiological signs should prompt an urgent search for virulent pathogens or complications. CT plays a role when a CXR is inconclusive in immunocompromised or critically ill patients.
