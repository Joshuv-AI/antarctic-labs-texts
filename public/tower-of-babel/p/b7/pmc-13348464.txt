Complete pulmonary and renal recovery following severe paraquat poisoning: a six‑month radiological follow‑up and dynamic hematological observation
Authors: Salameh Soleymani, Arezoo Dehghanpour, Ehsan Ramezanian Nik, Aref Arminfar, Soheil Shahramirad, Roya Jahanbazi, Mohammad Reza Vataniman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParaquat poisoning is one of the most lethal toxicological emergencies, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Despite several proposed treatment regimens, no single approach has conclusively demonstrated a reduction in mortality. Early clinical manifestations may be nonspecific, and prompt recognition in the emergency department is critical. We report the unexpected survival of a young woman who ingested a potentially fatal dose of paraquat, with emphasis on long‑term radiological follow‑up and dynamic hematological changes.Case presentationA 22‑year‑old Iranian woman presented to the emergency department approximately 48 h after reportedly ingesting 20 mL of a 20% paraquat solution in a suicide attempt. She had initially been evaluated at another facility shortly after ingestion but had refused admission against medical advice. She developed oropharyngeal mucosal ulcerations, odynophagia, hemoptysis, and acute kidney injury (peak serum creatinine 3.9 mg/dL). Laboratory data revealed a triphasic white blood cell pattern, including profound leukopenia followed by a marked rebound after granulocyte colony‑stimulating factor (G‑CSF) administration. A chest CT obtained at admission showed no pulmonary involvement; however, a follow-up CT performed on Day 14 demonstrated diffuse bilateral ground‑glass opacities and patchy consolidations consistent with acute alveolar injury. The patient received early multidisciplinary management including high‑dose corticosteroids, cyclophosphamide, antioxidant therapy, broad‑spectrum antibiotics, antimicrobial prophylaxis against opportunistic infections, nutritional support, and electrolyte correction. Clinical improvement was observed within 72 h, and she recovered without requiring dialysis or mechanical ventilation. A six‑month follow‑up chest CT showed complete radiological resolution with no evidence of pulmonary fibrosis.ConclusionsThis case suggests that complete pulmonary and renal recovery may be achievable in clinically severe paraquat poisoning. The six‑month imaging follow‑up documents the complete resolution of acute lung injury, while the dynamic leukocyte pattern illustrates the severity of bone marrow suppression and subsequent recovery. These findings highlight the potential value of early recognition, intensive multidisciplinary management, and structured follow‑up, particularly in resource‑limited settings.

Introduction

Paraquat (1,1’-dimethyl-4,4’-bipyridinium dichloride) is a highly toxic, non-selective herbicide widely used in agricultural settings, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [1]. Both intentional and accidental ingestion are associated with exceptionally high mortality rates, exceeding 60% in some reports. As little as 10 to 30 milliliters of a 20% solution can result in fatal outcomes due to rapidly progressive multi-organ failure [2]. The lungs and kidneys are the primary target organs, and common clinical manifestations include irreversible pulmonary fibrosis, acute tubular necrosis, and severe gastrointestinal ulceration. The principal mechanism of toxicity involves redox cycling and the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which induce oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, apoptosis, and eventually widespread cellular necrosis [3]. In the absence of a specific antidote, early diagnosis and prompt initiation of targeted supportive therapy are critical for improving survival. Current treatment approaches, although based on limited evidence, typically include high-dose corticosteroids, cyclophosphamide, and antioxidants such as N-acetylcysteine (NAC), vitamin C, and vitamin E, which aim to suppress systemic inflammation and mitigate pulmonary injury. Nevertheless, prognosis remains poor and is closely associated with the ingested dose, treatment delay, and the extent of organ involvement [4, 5].

In this report, we present the case of a young woman with severe paraquat poisoning who, despite multiple poor prognostic factors including radiologic evidence of acute lung injury, achieved complete clinical and radiological recovery following early, aggressive, and multidisciplinary intervention. This case underscores the potential value of combined immunosuppressive and antioxidant therapy and highlights the importance of long-term follow-up to assess therapeutic efficacy, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Case presentation

A 22-year-old woman with no prior medical history ingested approximately 20 mL of a 20% paraquat solution in a suicide attempt. She was evaluated at a primary care facility a few hours after ingestion. Despite medical recommendations for close observation and psychiatric evaluation, she voluntarily refused admission and left the facility against medical advice (AMA). This discharge against medical advice highlights a critical challenge in emergency care of poisoned patients and underscores the potential consequences of delayed definitive management. Two days later, she presented to our emergency department with hemoptysis, odynophagia, and progressive dysphagia, prompting immediate evaluation for life-threatening toxic ingestion. Following this initial discharge, the patient developed progressively worsening cough with blood-streaked sputum, burning epigastric pain, nausea, and oral intolerance. Additional symptoms included odynophagia, dysphagia to both solids and liquids, hoarseness, generalized weakness, and severe dyspnea at rest. Due to clinical deterioration, she was admitted for further evaluation and management. Initial vital signs were as follows: blood pressure 100/50 mmHg, heart rate 85 bpm, respiratory rate 14 breaths/min, body temperature 36.8 °C, and oxygen saturation of 97% on room air. Throughout the hospitalization, vital signs remained stable and the patient remained afebrile. Blood pressure normalized within the first 48 h of fluid resuscitation and supportive care. Oxygen saturation was maintained above 95% on room air for the majority of the hospital courses; supplemental oxygen was provided transiently on days of increased respiratory demand but continuous oxygen therapy was never required, and the patient did not experience severe hypoxemia at any point during the 25-day admission.

Given the history of high dose paraquat ingestion and progressive respiratory and systemic symptoms, the patient was considered high-risk in the emergency department and admitted for close monitoring and aggressive management. The day of ingestion is designated as Day 0. Methylprednisolone (1 g IV daily) and broad-spectrum antibiotics (vancomycin and meropenem) were initiated on Day 2 post-ingestion (approximately 48 h after ingestion), the day of admission to our center. Cyclophosphamide (750 mg IV daily for two consecutive days) was administered on Days 3 and 4 post-ingestions, corresponding to approximately 72 and 96 h after ingestion respectively. Physical examination revealed cold and clammy extremities and widespread erythematous and ulcerative lesions throughout the oral mucosa and oropharynx, consistent with chemical burns caused by paraquat ingestion. Mild tenderness was noted in the epigastric region. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was not performed, as hemoptysis was attributed to diffuse mucosal injury from paraquat and was not hemodynamically significant. Laboratory findings demonstrated progressive normocytic anemia, with hemoglobin levels dropping from 14.9 to 7.3 g/dL (Fig. 1). The reticulocyte count increased to 8.7%, along with 6.7 nucleated red blood cells (NRBCs) per 100 white blood cells and 1% blasts, indicating severe bone marrow stress. Marked leukocytosis with neutrophilic predominance (> 90%) was observed initially, consistent with acute systemic inflammation. Platelet count decreased from 388,000 to 157,000 per microliter (refer to Table 1 for trends and interpretations). A dynamic triphasic pattern in white blood cell (WBC) count was observed (Fig. 2). The patient initially exhibited neutrophilic leukocytosis (peak WBC: 21.6 × 10³/µL on day 1), in line with a systemic inflammatory response to paraquat toxicity. This was followed by a steep decline in WBC count to profound leukopenic levels (< 0.5 × 10³/µL between days 9 and 14), likely to reflect bone marrow suppression due to oxidative stress and potential immunosuppressive effects of early therapy. Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) was initiated on day 14 (hospital day 12) due to profound neutropenia (absolute neutrophil count < 0.5 × 10³/µL), leading to a robust leukocyte rebound (peak WBC: 68.9 × 10³/µL on day 17), indicative of preserved hematopoietic responsiveness. White blood cell trends and their clinical relevance are illustrated in Fig. 2. Acute kidney injury was evident early, with a peak serum creatinine of 3.9 mg/dL and a urea level of 174 mg/dL (Fig. 3), both of which improved with supportive care. Electrolyte disturbances included hypokalemia (as low as 2.8 mmol/L) and hypomagnesemia (1.2 mg/dL), which were corrected during hospitalization. Ferritin peaked at 1124 ng/mL, and inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP; 32.3 mg/L) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH; 1939 U/L) were significantly elevated (Table 1 for details). Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis on two occasions showed respiratory alkalosis (pH > 7.5, pCO₂ ~32 mmHg, HCO₃ ~25 mmol/L) with preserved oxygenation (pO₂ >130 mmHg), likely attributable to hyperventilation driven by pain, anxiety, or systemic stress.

In the absence of confirmatory plasma paraquat assays, urinary paraquat measurement, or urine dithionite testing none of which were available at our center emergency management decisions were based on clinical presentation, laboratory trends, and early imaging findings.

A chest CT scan obtained at admission (Day 2 post-ingestion) showed no evidence of air-space disease, interstitial abnormality, nodules, or pleural effusion, confirming the absence of pulmonary involvement at the time of presentation. A follow-up chest CT performed on Day 14 post-ingestion (hospital day 12) revealed significant bilateral pulmonary involvement. The imaging demonstrated diffuse ground-glass opacities (GGOs) and patchy consolidations predominantly in the posterior and basal regions of both lungs (Fig. 4), findings consistent with acute alveolar injury secondary to paraquat toxicity. These radiologic features suggested an evolving pattern of diffuse alveolar damage (DAD) and were highly compatible with an acute respiratory distress syndrome-like (ARDS-like) process, despite preserving oxygenation at the time. Notably, a follow-up CT scan obtained six months later demonstrated complete radiological resolution, with no evidence of parenchymal distortion, fibrosis, or traction bronchiectasis (Fig. 5), indicating full pulmonary recovery and supporting the potential benefit of early aggressive immunosuppressive and antioxidant therapy. The patient was initially kept nothing by mouth (NPO) and treated with intravenous pantoprazole (40 mg twice daily). A pre-prepared mucosal-protective cocktail containing lidocaine 2% (4 mL), tetracycline suspension 500 mg, aluminum-magnesium hydroxide syrup (5 mL), and dexamethasone 4 mg was administered orally every 8 h; each dose consisted of 5 mL of the combined formulation. High-dose corticosteroid therapy started with methylprednisolone (1 g IV daily for 3 days), followed by dexamethasone (8 mg IV three times daily). Antioxidant therapy included N-acetylcysteine (NAC) at a total daily dose of 6 g IV in two divided doses, along with vitamin C (1.5 g IV every 8 h) and oral vitamin E (400 IU daily). Due to neutrophilic leukocytosis and a high risk of infection, broad-spectrum antibiotics were administered: vancomycin (1 g IV every 12 h) and meropenem (1 g IV every 8 h). To prevent pulmonary fibrosis, cyclophosphamide was administered on Days 3 and 4 post-ingestion (750 mg IV daily for two consecutive days). Supportive care included total parenteral nutrition (TPN), intravenous potassium chloride (20 mEq in 100 mL half-normal saline over 2 h), magnesium sulfate (2 g IV daily), and transfusion of one unit of packed red blood cells (PRBCs) due to symptomatic anemia. Oral supplements including iron, zinc, and multivitamins were also provided. Given the profound immunosuppression resulting from high-dose corticosteroids and cyclophosphamide, antimicrobial prophylaxis was administered to reduce the risk of opportunistic infections, including fluconazole (100 mg orally twice daily), cotrimoxazole (400/80 mg two tablets orally twice daily), and acyclovir (200 mg orally twice daily), each continued for the duration of immunosuppressive therapy. Dextromethorphan was administered as an antitussive agent, and metoclopramide was used for nausea control throughout the hospital course. During hospitalization, psychiatric evaluation was performed, and the patient was closely monitored for suicidality, with plans for referral to psychiatric services following clinical stabilization. Despite the severity of the initial presentation, the patient’s condition gradually improved. Her vital signs stabilized, renal function recovered, and mucosal lesions regressed. Follow-up laboratory tests showed a decrease in inflammatory markers and improvement in hematologic and renal indices. Although the patient initially developed acute kidney injury with elevated creatinine (peak 3.9 mg/dL on hospital day 2) and urea levels, her renal function improved with supportive care alone, and neither hemodialysis nor hemoperfusion was required. Hemoperfusion was considered but was not pursued due to unavailability at our center and clinical instability during the first 72 h. The patient was managed on the general medical ward throughout the entire 25-day hospital stay and did not require admission to the intensive care unit at any point during the clinical course. Total duration of hospitalization was 25 days. A summary timeline of clinical events, interventions, and key laboratory findings is provided in Table 2. This recovery coincided with normalization of several key paraclinical indices (Table 1).

Discussion

PQ is a highly lethal bipyridilium herbicide widely recognized for its role in fatal poisonings, particularly in the context of intentional self-harm [6]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 20% of all global suicides are attributed to pesticide ingestion, and among these, paraquat exhibits the highest case fatality rate, ranging from 50% to 90% [7, 8]. Its widespread availability and rapid systemic toxicity underscore its significance as a major public health threat.

From an emergency medicine perspective, this case underscores the importance of early risk stratification in patients presenting with suspected paraquat poisoning. Even in the absence of severe hypoxemia or hemodynamic instability at presentation, progressive symptoms and early radiologic lung injury should prompt hospital admission and aggressive management. Delayed presentation following discharge against medical advice, as observed in this case, represents a critical risk factor that may significantly worsen outcomes.

This report presents a 22-year-old woman who ingested approximately 20 mL of 20% paraquat in a suicide attempt and developed multiple systemic complications yet achieved full clinical and radiologic recovery. Her case offers a valuable opportunity to review the classical pathophysiology and natural history of paraquat poisoning while also highlighting a potential association between timely, multidimensional therapy and favorable clinical outcomes [9]. Clinically, the patient exhibited hallmark features of paraquat toxicity, including mucosal ulceration, burning epigastric pain, odynophagia, dysphagia, hemoptysis, and systemic inflammation. Laboratory findings revealed leukocytosis with neutrophilic predominance, normocytic anemia, elevated CRP and LDH, and a reticulocyte response with blasts and NRBCs, reflecting bone marrow stress [4, 10]. Renal involvement was prominent, with acute kidney injury (creatinine peak of 3.9 mg/dL, urea 174 mg/dL), consistent with known tubular toxicity of paraquat, particularly affecting the proximal tubules, and occurred despite hemodynamic stability. Notably, the patient did not require dialysis, in line with reports of favorable outcomes in young patients without comorbidities further emphasizing the potential importance of early supportive measures in selected patients [11]. She received a comprehensive regimen including high-dose corticosteroids (methylprednisolone followed by dexamethasone), cyclophosphamide, NAC, vitamins C and E, along with TPN, broad-spectrum antibiotics, electrolyte correction, and psychiatric support. A triphasic pattern of WBC response initial neutrophilic leukocytosis, followed by profound leukopenia, and then rapid rebound after G‑CSF has been previously reported in paraquat poisoning, reflecting inflammation, marrow suppression, and hematopoietic recovery. Similar hematologic dynamics were observed in our patient, aligning with earlier case reports; the temporal relationship between WBC trends, clinical deterioration, and therapeutic recovery is detailed in Table 2. Moreover, elevated serum G‑CSF levels in patients with poor outcomes, as shown in recent studies, suggest innate immune activation and support a potential role for cytokine-based interventions in selected cases with adequate marrow reserve [12].

Before attributing the Day 14 CT findings to paraquat-induced alveolar injury, several alternative etiologies must be systematically considered. First, drug-induced pneumonitis related to vancomycin or meropenem remains a theoretical possibility; however, the pulmonary lesions resolved without discontinuation of either agent, which would be atypical for drug-induced lung injury. Second, opportunistic pulmonary infection including Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia must be considered given the high-dose corticosteroid and cyclophosphamide therapy. The patient remained afebrile throughout the hospital course, inflammatory markers progressively declined, and recovery occurred without specific antimicrobial therapy for opportunistic pathogens, making this diagnosis less likely. Third, aspiration pneumonia is clinically plausible in the setting of severe odynophagia and dysphagia; however, the imaging features diffuse, symmetric ground-glass opacities with a posterior and basal predominance are more consistent with diffuse alveolar damage than with the typical tree-in-bud pattern of aspiration pneumonitis. Fourth, without confirmatory plasma paraquat concentrations, the degree of systemic exposure remains uncertain. Nonetheless, the combination of oropharyngeal burns, acute kidney injury, profound bone marrow suppression, and typical pulmonary imaging in a patient with a reliable history of ingestion strongly supports the diagnosis of clinically severe paraquat poisoning. Acknowledging these uncertainties, we interpret the pulmonary findings as most consistent with paraquat-induced alveolar injury, while recognizing that the contribution of other factors cannot be fully excluded. The development of pulmonary infiltrates on Day 14, despite initiation of corticosteroids and cyclophosphamide within 48–72 h of ingestion, indicates that early immunosuppressive therapy did not prevent the onset of alveolar injury. This observation represents an important logical limitation of this case: the favorable outcome cannot be attributed to prevention of lung injury but may instead reflect modification of the fibrotic response after injury was already established. Complete radiological resolution at six months, in the absence of progressive fibrosis, raises the hypothesis that early therapy may have limited fibrotic progression rather than preventing initial alveolar damage though whether this reflects a treatment effect or the natural recovery capacity of a young, previously healthy patient remains uncertain.

One of the most clinically significant findings in this case was the preservation of respiratory function despite clear evidence of early lung injury. A chest CT scan performed on Day 14 post-ingestion (hospital day 12) revealed bilateral diffuse ground-glass opacities (GGOs) and patchy consolidations predominantly in the posterior and basal lung zones hallmarks of acute alveolar damage (DAD) and consistent with an evolving ARDS-like pattern (Fig. 4) [13]. These findings highlight that although the patient maintained adequate oxygenation, radiologically she had already developed paraquat-induced lung injury. Importantly, a follow-up high-resolution CT performed six months later showed complete radiological resolution with no signs of fibrosis, architectural distortion, or traction bronchiectasis (Fig. 5) [14], While some reports have proposed a potential role for combined corticosteroid and antioxidant therapy in limiting fibrotic progression [15], it remains uncertain whether this favorable outcome reflects a treatment effect or the patient’s innate recovery, as discussed above. This contrasts with previous reports describing treatment-refractory respiratory failure and fibrotic progression in similar settings. The overall clinical course of this patient resembles previously reported survivors: young age, no comorbidities, multisystem involvement, and rapid implementation of aggressive, protocol-based care [16, 17].

Although the patient received early immunosuppressive and antioxidant therapy, we cannot definitively attribute the favorable outcome to these interventions alone. The complete radiological resolution at six months may, at least in part, reflect the natural course of recovery in a young, previously healthy individual without comorbidities. Moreover, the concurrent administration of multiple therapies, corticosteroids, cyclophosphamide, antioxidants, G‑CSF, and broad‑spectrum antibiotics precludes identification of a single effective agent. Therefore, while this case supports the potential value of early aggressive management, it does not prove the efficacy of any specific treatment regimen, and the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis‑generating rather than definitive.

Clinical lessons

Several clinically relevant observations can be drawn from this case. First, the triphasic leukocyte pattern underscores the importance of diligent hematological monitoring in paraquat poisoning: an initial leukocytosis may mask impending bone marrow suppression, and profound neutropenia can develop rapidly. The robust response to G-CSF in this patient suggests that cytokine support may be effective when hematopoietic reserve is preserved. Second, the discrepancy between preserved oxygenation and extensive CT findings on Day 14 highlights that significant lung injury can exist without hypoxemia; waiting for oxygen desaturation before imaging may delay recognition of evolving pulmonary toxicity. Third, the decision to forgo haemoperfusion, although driven by unavailability and clinical instability rather than choice, did not preclude a favorable outcome. This observation, while not generalizable, raises the question of whether aggressive immunosuppression and supportive care alone can suffice in settings where extracorporeal elimination is inaccessible. Finally, the complete radiological resolution at six months reinforces the importance of structured long-term follow-up, as early fibrotic changes may be reversible in young patients without comorbidities. The temporal relationship between key interventions and clinical responses warrants explicit description. Following initiation of methylprednisolone on Day 2, the patient’s hemodynamic parameters stabilized within 48 h and dyspnea showed gradual improvement by Days 5–7. Renal function, which peaked at creatinine 3.9 mg/dL on Day 3, began declining steadily thereafter with supportive care alone, reaching near-normal levels by Day 14 (creatinine 0.8 mg/dL), suggesting that early fluid resuscitation and electrolyte correction played a critical role in renal recovery. The most striking clinical turning point was the response to G-CSF initiated on Day 14: within 72 h, WBC rebounded from a nadir of 0.3 × 10³/µL to 68.9 × 10³/µL, demonstrating preserved hematopoietic reserve despite profound marrow suppression. Inflammatory markers followed a parallel trajectory, with CRP declining from 32.3 to 3.1 mg/L over the course of hospitalization, temporally correlating with clinical improvement. These correlations, while not establishing causality, provide a more granular understanding of the clinical course and may inform monitoring strategies in future cases.

Limitations

The authors acknowledge several limitations inherent to this case report, many of which reflect real-world constraints in resource-limited healthcare settings. First, confirmatory toxicological tests including plasma paraquat concentration, urinary paraquat measurement, and the urine dithionite test were not available at our center. Consequently, the diagnosis of severe paraquat poisoning rests on clinical grounds: a reliable history of ingestion of approximately 20 mL of a 20% solution, typical oropharyngeal mucosal burns, acute kidney injury (peak creatinine 3.9 mg/dL), and early pulmonary involvement on chest CT. While biochemical confirmation would have strengthened causal attribution, the constellation of clinical findings is highly consistent with clinically severe paraquat toxicity. Second, a baseline chest CT at the time of initial presentation was not obtained. As a result, we cannot definitively determine whether pulmonary injury predated or developed after the initiation of immunosuppressive therapy. This limitation precludes a precise assessment of whether early treatment prevented the onset of lung injury or merely accelerated recovery from established injury. Third, hemoperfusion, a widely recommended extracorporeal elimination method for paraquat poisoning, was not performed because this modality was unavailable at our institution. Interhospital transfer to a facility offering hemoperfusion was considered but deemed unsafe due to the patient’s clinical instability, ongoing need for intensive monitoring, and risk of deterioration during transport. Fourth, the concurrent administration of multiple therapeutic agents (corticosteroids, cyclophosphamide, antioxidants, broad-spectrum antibiotics, G-CSF, and supportive care) precludes attribution of the favorable outcome to any single intervention. The observed complete radiological resolution at six months may reflect the natural history of recovery in a young, previously healthy patient rather than a direct effect of the therapies provided. Similarly, the pulmonary infiltrates could have been contributed to by drug-induced pneumonitis, opportunistic infection, or aspiration, as discussed. Fifth, the profound leukopenia observed during hospitalization could be explained by paraquat-induced bone marrow suppression, cyclophosphamide-associated myelosuppression, or a combination of both; the concurrent use of cotrimoxazole as antimicrobial prophylaxis may have further contributed to leukopenia, and its relative contribution cannot be excluded. Although the rapid leukocyte rebound following G-CSF administration suggests preserved hematopoietic reserve, the relative contribution of each factor cannot be determined. Finally, as a single case report, these findings are not generalizable. The association between early aggressive multidisciplinary management and favorable outcome should be interpreted with caution, and no definitive therapeutic recommendations can be drawn from this experience alone.

Conclusion

This case illustrates that survival with complete pulmonary recovery from severe paraquat poisoning may be achievable, even when early acute lung injury is evident. Despite radiologic evidence of acute lung injury characterized by bilateral ground-glass opacities and patchy consolidations, the patient achieved complete radiological resolution at six-month follow-up. Whether this recovery reflects a disease-modifying effect of early immunosuppressive and antioxidant therapy, or the innate resilience of a young patient without comorbidities, remains uncertain; nevertheless, the complete resolution of acute lung injury without fibrosis is a noteworthy observation that warrants further investigation. Furthermore, the absence of dialysis, despite initial acute kidney injury, underscores the importance of timely supportive care. The observed triphasic leukocyte pattern, including profound leukopenia, followed by a rapid rebound after G-CSF administration highlighting the importance of close hematologic monitoring and the potential role of cytokine support as an adjunct in selected cases of paraquat-induced marrow suppression. Structured, multidisciplinary management remains essential, particularly in resource-limited settings.
