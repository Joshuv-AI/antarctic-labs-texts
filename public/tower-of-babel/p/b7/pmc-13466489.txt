Overweight and obesity in Jordanian adults before and after COVID-19: a multi-index comparison
Authors: Buthaina Alkhatib, Lana M. Agraib, Islam Al-Shami, Ahmad Suleiman Aslaih, Asem Shawabkeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prevalence of overweight and obesity has increased globally after COVID-19, including in Jordan. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of overweight and obesity among Jordanian adults using multiple anthropometric indicators.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 2,633 adults aged 18–65 (1,027 males; 1,606 females). Trained nutritionists collected anthropometric and body composition measurements, including body weight, height, waist circumference (WC), body mass index (BMI), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) using a structured survey. Obesity was calculated using both global and Jordan-specific cut-offs.ResultsThe prevalence of overweight and obesity varied by indicator, ranging from 41.7% (WHtR using Jordan-specific cut-offs) to 72.9% (body fat percentage). BMI-defined obesity affected 70–73% of both sexes. Central obesity was more prevalent in females (46.9–73.2%) compared to males (38.8–65.3%). Females had significantly higher odds of central obesity (OR = 2.23–2.48), whereas males showed higher odds when obesity was assessed using WHR, WHtR, and body-fat measures. Trends from 2009 to 2024 indicate persistently high prevalence rates, with slight declines in BMI- and WHtR-based obesity, while central obesity remained elevated, particularly among females.ConclusionObesity remains highly prevalent among Jordanian adults, with notable sex-specific differences, especially in central adiposity. Public health interventions should target high-risk groups and incorporate multiple anthropometric indicators along with sex- specific prevention and surveillance strategies.

Background

Obesity is a chronic, complex disease characterized by excessive fat accumulation that presents a significant health risk [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines overweight and obesity based on Body Mass Index (BMI), with overweight defined as a BMI greater than 25 kg/m² and obesity as a BMI greater than 30 kg/m² [2]. High BMI is associated with numerous chronic conditions, including non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes (T2D), various malignancies, musculoskeletal disorders, and mental health issues. These conditions diminish quality of life and increase healthcare costs [3].

The prevalence of obesity has reached pandemic proportions globally. There are 374 million males and 504 million women who are classified as living with obesity, with obesity surpassing underweight in 89% and 73% of countries for women and men, respectively. Compared to 1990, there had been an increase of 377 million in women and 307 million in males [4]. Almost half of the world’s adult population is predicted to be overweight or obese by 2030 [5].

Obesity has become a significant public health issue in Jordan, with its prevalence reaching alarming levels. A national survey conducted by Ajlouni et al. (2020) documented that 60.4% of men and 75.6% of women were living with obesity according to waist circumference criteria [6]. Additionally, the significant rise in obesity prevalence is linked to adopting a Western lifestyle and reduced physical activity [7]. Furthermore, Chong and colleagues emphasize that the highest prevalence of obesity was observed in regions such as Southeast Asia, where economic growth and urbanization have led to unhealthy diets and sedentary lifestyles [8].

Obesity can be assessed using multiple methods; BMI is a widely used measure for identifying overall obesity, but, although inexpensive and easy to use, it does not distinguish between lean and fat mass [9]. However, waist circumference (WC) offers insights into body fat distribution and is closely associated with central fat accumulation [7]. However, a recent study revealed that WC had a higher relative integrated discrimination index than BMI in both men (6.9% vs. 3.2%) and women (9.6% vs. 9.2%) [10]. However, several alternative methods to measure adiposity include skinfold caliper measurements, waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), body fat percentage (BF%), fat-free mass index (FFMI), and fat mass index (FMI) [11]. In addition to bio-electrical impedance analysis (BIA), dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA), underwater weighing (densitometry), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). However, the last-mentioned methods offer the most accurate results; they are highly unsuitable for conducting studies due to their high costs and specialized training and equipment needs [9].

The COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted the increased risk faced by individualsliving with obesity, who are more likely to experience severe illness and even death from the virus [12]. Lockdowns have led to reduced physical activity, unhealthy eating habits, and stress-induced emotional eating, contributing to the rise in obesity during the pandemic. Disruptions in food supply chains and limited access to healthy foods have also exacerbated the issue. Furthermore, psychological stress and sociodemographic factors have contributed to the worsening obesity rates [13, 14]. Therefore, this study aimed to:

i)Assess the prevalence of overweight and obesity among Jordanian adults in the post-COVID-19 period using multiple anthropometric and body composition indices;ii)Compare current obesity prevalence estimates with data reported in previous national studies conducted before the COVID-19 pandemic;iii)Examine variations in obesity prevalence according to sex, age group, and selected sociodemographic characteristics.

It was hypothesized that the prevalence of obesity among Jordanian adults increased significantly following the COVID-19 pandemic compared with previous years, and that this increase differed according to demographic and socioeconomic factors. Additionally, it was hypothesized that body composition indices would identify a higher prevalence of obesity than BMI alone in the post-COVID-19 period, reflecting the potential impact of pandemic-related lifestyle changes, including reduced physical activity, unhealthy dietary patterns, and increased psychological stress.

Methods

Study design and sampling

A cross-sectional study was conducted among Jordanian adults in the middle governorates of Jordan between January and June 2024. A convenience sample of both sexes was recruited. Individuals aged 18 or older were eligible for inclusion in the study. Pregnant and lactating women, subjects taking any immunosuppressive medication or corticosteroids, and those with thyroid gland problems were excluded. Participants were recruited using a non-probability snowball sampling technique. Initial eligible participants were identified and invited to participate, and each was asked to refer additional individuals who met the inclusion criteria. This recruitment strategy enabled efficient outreach within the target population. The response rate was 55%, and the final sample of 2633 was recruited.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

The research protocol underwent a thorough review and received Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval from the ethics committees of the Jordanian Ministry of Health (MBA, IRB, 20219-1/12/2022) and the Hashemite University Institutional Review Board (No. 19/1/2022/2023). Before data collection, informed written consent was obtained from each participant.

Data collection

Participants’ data were obtained through face-to-face interviews with trained reviewers (nutritionists). Data collection was completed using a structured survey that collected sociodemographic data (including age, sex, marital status, educational level, employment status, number of family members, monthly income (JD), smoking status, medications, medical history, and physical activity). The trained nutritionists completed anthropometric measurements, including body composition analysis, weight (kg), height (cm), WC (cm), and hip circumference (HC) (cm).

Anthropometric measurement and indices

Body height was measured using Seca stadiometers (Seca, USA), while weight (kg), fat mass%, and muscle mass% were measured using the bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) (electrical InBody H20N body fat Scale, FSA, US). Steps were taken to ensure the subjects wore light clothing and were barefoot. The measurement was recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm and 0.1 kg. Also, BMI (kg/m2) was calculated. Individuals were categorized according to the WHO [15]: overweight participants were defined as having a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 and < 30 kg/m2, while obesity was defined as a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2. WC and HC were measured to the nearest 0.1 cm by RIEDER Body Measure tape, Lock Pin, and Push-Button Retract (Inct. Bonus Kit, REIDHK, China). Based on WC, obesity was defined according to the Adult Treatment Panel III criteria (WC ≥ 88 cm for women and ≥ 102 cm for men) [6]. Moreover, the WHR was calculated. The classification of the subjects based on WHR was < 0.90 (normal weight), 0.90 to 0.99 (overweight), and ≥ 1 (obesity) for males; and < 0.80 (normal), 0.80 to 0.84 (overweight), and ≥ 0.85 (obesity) for females [15]. According to WHO, fat mass%≥25% and ≥ 35% indicate obesity for males and females, respectively [16]. Moreover, WHtR was calculated as WC (cm) divided by height (cm), and globally categorized as follows: ‘no increased risk’ (WHtR < 0.5), ‘increased risk’ (WHtR ≥ 0.5) [17]. However, Khader and colleagues (2019) suggested a Jordanian-specific cutoff point for WHtR: 0.6 for women and 0.57 for men [18]. Fat mass index (FMI)= (fat mass (kg)/(height (m)2); however, fat mass% * weight (kg) (Liu et al., 2013). FMI was categorized into quartiles as follows: Q1:< 7.1569, Q2: 7.157–10.773, Q3: 10.773–14.777, Q4:1 > 14.777.

Statistical analysis

The IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0) was used to analyze the data. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to verify the normality of variables. Normally distributed continuous variables are typically expressed as means and standard deviations. However, categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages (%). Bivariate analyses were conducted using the chi-square (χ2) test to assess differences between the two sexes, and independent-samples t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare the means of continuous variables between the two groups, as appropriate. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to examine the independent associations between sex and obesity indicators, adjusting for age, education, income, smoking, marital status, and physical activity. Temporal comparisons of obesity prevalence across survey years (2009, 2017, and 2024) were primarily descriptive, based on available published data. Formal statistical testing of trends was limited due to the absence of individual-level data for earlier survey years. Findings with a p-value of < 0.05 were statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 2,633 Jordanian adults (1,027 males and 1,606 females) were included in this study. As illustrated in Table 1, the largest age group was 18–35 years, representing 48.3% of males and 35.3% of females. The participants aged 36–50 years accounted for 25.6% of males and 37.4% of females, while those aged 51–65 years old represented approximately one quarter of both sexes. Educational attainment by sex: high school or below was most common among females (62.4%), whereas post-secondary education (students with ≥ B.Sc.) predominated among males (55.3%). Half of the males and two-thirds of the females were married. The highest proportion had income levels of 300–600 JD/month (males: 36.3%; females: 30.9%). The rate of female participants diagnosed with hypertension (20.6%) and diabetes (17.7%) was higher than that of males (9.9% and 9.2%, respectively). The total number of non-smokers in the study population was 448 (45.8%) males and 1176 (86.5%) females. The highest proportion of participants was physically inactive (61.2% of males and 68.2% of females).

Prevalence of overweight and obesity based on anthropometric measurements

The overall prevalence of overweight and obesity varied by indices (Table 2). Based on BMI, the total prevalence of overweight and obesity was 71.6%, while 72.9% based on BFP, 50.5% based on WC, 67.5% based on WC (IDF), 53.3% based on WHR, 72.3% based on WHtR (globally), and 41.7% based on WHtR (Jordanian specific cut points ). According to FMI, 25.5% of participants were at the highest level, while 22.8% were at the lowest level.

The sex-specific prevalence of overweight and obesity using the different indices is summarized in Figs. 1 and 2. By BMI, the prevalence of overweight and obesity was 70.2% in males and 72.6% in females. Using BFP, corresponding values were 68.3% and 75.1%, respectively. Central overweight and obesity prevalence, according to WC, was 38.8% in males and 58.1% in females, while it was 58.8% in males and 73.2% in females according to WC (IDF criteria). Concerning WHR, prevalence was 65.3% in males and 46.9% in females. Using WHtR (globally), prevalence estimates were 72.8% in males and 71.9% in females based on global cut-off points, and 46.0% and 38.9%, respectively, using Jordanian-specific cut-off points (Fig. 1). Regarding the FMI, the percentage of male participants decreased as the index value increased from Q1 (lowest) to Q4 (highest). Conversely, the rate of female participants increased from Q1 to Q4 (Fig. 2).

Table 3 presents the multivariable-adjusted associations between sex and obesity using different anthropometric and body composition indicators, with males as the reference group. After adjustment, no significant association was observed between sex and obesity defined by BMI (OR = 0.90; 95% CI: 0.62–1.31; p = 0.647), indicating comparable odds of BMI-defined obesity between females and males. In contrast, significant sex-specific differences arose when alternative obesity indicators were applied. In comparesion to males, females had significantly lower odds of obesity when assessed using body fat percentage (OR = 0.31; 95% CI: 0.17–0.54; p < 0.001), WHR based on global cut-off points (OR = 0.45; 95% CI: 0.27–0.75; p = 0.007), Jordanian-specific waist-to-height ratio cut-offs (OR = 0.35; 95% CI: 0.26–0.48; p < 0.001), and WHtR (OR = 0.14; 95% CI: 0.09–0.22; p < 0.001). On the other hand, females revealed significantly higher odds of central obesity when defined using WC criteria, wheres more than twofold higher among females compared with males using both WHO criteria (OR = 2.48; 95% CI: 1.83–3.37; p < 0.001) and IDF criteria (OR = 2.23; 95% CI: 1.50–3.31; p < 0.001). Analysis of FMI categories revealed a sorted association across quartiles. Compared with the lowest FMI quartile (Q1), females had higher odds of being classified in Q2 (OR = 1.76; 95% CI: 1.00–3.12; p = 0.015) and Q4 (OR = 2.24; 95% CI: 1.27–3.96), indicating a greater likelihood of higher adiposity levels among females. Thus, the findings demonstrate that sex differences in obesity persist after controlling for major sociodemographic and lifestyle confounders.

Time-based trends: prevalence of overweight and obesity

The prevalence of overweight and obesity by sex across survey years (2009, 2017, and 2024) is summarized in Figs. 3 and 4, based on BMI, WC, WHR, and WHtR. The prevalence of abnormal WHtR based on Jordanian cut-off points among males was 44.2% in 2017 (before COVID-19) and 46.0% in 2024 (the current result after COVID-19), while corresponding values among females were 49.4% and 38.9%, respectively (Fig. 3). According to the global WHtR cut-off points, the prevalence estimates were 83.1% in 2018 [19] and 72.8% in 2024 among males, while they were 72.9% and 71.9% among females during the same period (Fig. 4). According to WHO-defined WHR criteria, the prevalence of overweight and obesity among males were 63.4% in 2017 and 65.3% in 2024. In females, these percentages were 48.5% and 46.9% during the same period.

Discussion

The current study examined the prevalence of overweight and obesity among Jordanian adults following the COVID-19 pandemic, utilizing various obesity parameters and indices. The prevalence of overweight and obesity varies depending on the measurement method used. According to BMI, 71.6% of participants were overweight or obese, while BFP showed 72.9%. WC indicated 50.5%, and different WHtR classifications yielded 72.3% globally and 41.7% for Jordan-specific cut points. 25.5% of participants were at the highest level of FMI, while 22.8% were at the lowest. Regarding sex differences, using BMI, the prevalence was 70.2% in males and 72.6% in females. For BFP, it was 68.3% for males and 75.1% for females. Central obesity, measured by WC, was 38.8% in males and 58.1% in females. This pattern persisted across other measurements: males had higher obesity rates under some methods, while females had significantly lower odds under BFP and WHtR. After adjustment for various factors, BMI showed no significant difference between the sexes, whereas other obesity indicators did. Females had lower odds for obesity as per BFP and WHtR criteria, while they had higher odds of central obesity measured by WC. When examining FMI, females were more likely to have higher adiposity levels than males. Trend data over survey years (2009, 2017, and 2024) shows changes in obesity prevalence by sex. Results reveal that males had a slight increase in abnormal WHtR after COVID-19, while females showed a decline. Different measurements over the years yielded varied results, including a decrease in BMI among males from 77.2% in 2017 to 70.2% in 2024.

In line with the present findings, Yang and colleagues found that the prevalence of elevated WHtR has increased from 74.8% in 1999–2000 to 82.7% in 2017–2018; moreover, compared to females, males had a significantly higher prevalence of elevated WHtR (83.1% versus 72.9%, p < 0.001) [19]. These statistics showed a significant upward trend from 1999 to 2018, with consistent patterns observed across different socio-demographic groups, lifestyle factors, and health conditions. Women, older adults, former smokers, individuals with lower education levels, and those with comorbidities were more likely to have elevated WHtR [19]. Temporal comparisons across survey years suggest that obesity prevalence remains alarmingly high in the post-COVID-19 period, with only modest declines or stabilization observed for BMI- and WHR-defined obesity, alongside persistently elevated central obesity levels, particularly among females. Collectively, these findings highlight the limitations of relying on a single obesity metric, demonstrate persistent sex-specific disparities after adjustment for confounders, and suggest that the COVID-19 pandemic may have contributed to maintaining, rather than reversing, the already high obesity burden among adults in Jordan.

While the current finding suggests a modest slight decline or stabilization in obesity prevalence among Jordanian adults post-COVID-19, much recent research across different regions has reported the opposite. Using the Massachusetts Department of Public Health’s disease surveillance system (MDPHnet), Wu and colleagues found that Obesity prevalence increased from 2019 to 2020 to a greater extent than expected based on the increase in pre–COVID–19 years, from 15.1% in 2018 to 15.7% in 2019 and 17.3% in 2020 (differences in trends, 1.1%; 95% CI 0.3%- 1.9%) [20]. Also, in 2020, the prevalence of obesity and severe obesity in the Korean adult population aged 19 and over, compared with the normal weight group, showed significant increases of 2.5% and 1.4%, respectively, compared with 2019 rates (p < 0.05) [21]. In May 2022, the WHO released a report on the obesity epidemic in Europe, revealing that 60% of the adult population in the region is either overweight or obese, and almost 1 in 3 children are suffering from overweight or obesity [22]. The report also emphasizes the serious implications of this obesity epidemic, particularly as it intersects with the COVID pandemic to create a “twin pandemic,” resulting in increased morbidity and mortality [12].

In Jordan, as in other countries worldwide, Physical inactivity, increased sedentary behavior (e.g., screen time), poor eating patterns, and increased consumption of calorie-dense foods were linked to rising obesity rates during COVID-19 lockdowns [23]. Some studies also reported higher consumption of foods rich in sugar, salt, and fat, especially among adults living with overweight and obesity, suggesting emotional or stress-related eating patterns during lockdown [24]. Other research documented increases in the number of meals per day and suboptimal hydration (with many reporting drinking less than the recommended amount of water) [14]. Moreover, research indicates that female and male populations may respond differently to stress and lockdowns, though data specific to Jordan are limited. For example, globally, many studies report sex differences in activity levels and stress eating, which can influence obesity risk [23]. While detailed Jordan-specific longitudinal studies covering the post-COVID period remain limited in the published literature, regional and global trends suggest some persistence of sedentary habits and altered eating patterns even after lockdown restrictions eased, contributing to sustained obesity risk.

Cho and colleagues investigated how the prevalence of obesity among U.S. adults changed after the COVID-19 pandemic, considering the impact of stay-at-home orders and sociodemographic factors. Their findings revealed that adults living in states or territories with mandatory stay-at-home orders experienced a larger increase in obesity prevalence than those in areas without such orders. Additionally, younger adults (compared to those aged 65 and older) and individuals with less than a high school education (versus those with at least a four-year college degree) showed larger increases in obesity prevalence [25]. A study assessing the combined prevalence of underweight and obesity across 200 countries from 1990 to 2022 revealed significant findings [26]. The data indicated that the prevalence of these conditions increased in 162 countries (81%) among women and in 140 countries (70%) among men, with posterior probabilities of at least 0.80. Notably, obesity rates were higher than those of underweight, with a posterior probability of at least 0.80 observed in 177 countries (89%) for women and 145 countries (73%) for men in 2022 [26]. In 2019, obesity prevalence rates varied significantly across countries, ranging from 3.5% in Bangladesh to 32% in the United States. Of the 30 nations studied, only 13 had an obesity prevalence rate of 10% or less. Notably, nine countries reported obesity rates exceeding 20%: South Africa (23.3%), Turkey (26%), Egypt (30%), Iran (20.1%), Iraq (21%), Brazil (21.4%), Mexico (25%), and the United States (32%), specifically, in the Eastern Mediterranean region, the prevalence of obesity increased from 6.4% to 17.4% in 2019 [12]. The age-standardized global trend in obesity prevalence increased from 4.6% in 1980 to 14.0% in 2019, with a constant predominance of women in the prevalence rates of obesity compared to men [12].

Daniels and colleagues investigated the indirect effects of the first year post-COVID-19 on obesity. They found that factors such as increased food and alcohol consumption, longer periods of sedentary behavior, worsening depressive symptoms, and heightened financial stress contributed to the issue. These findings suggest that COVID-19 has intensified existing risk factors for obesity and will likely lead to higher obesity rates shortly [27].

In line with the findings of Ajlouni et al. (2020), this study also found that the prevalence of obesity—measured using various indicators, such as BMI, BFP, and WC—was higher among females than among males. This higher prevalence among females may be attributed to traditional and cultural restrictions that limit their lifestyle choices and access to exercise [6]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 42 studies (2013–2023) involving 29,137 Ghanaian adults found a similar trend, with obesity prevalence notably higher in women. The study revealed that women had a higher average BMI (25.3 vs. 23.1 kg/m²), higher rates of participants living with overweight (25.9% vs. 16.5%), and obesity (17.4% vs. 5.5%) than men [28]. Additionally, a secondary data analysis of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS) rounds in India indicated an increase in the overall obesity prevalence, with women disproportionately affected. The study suggested that sex differences may be due to a combination of socio-cultural, biological, and lifestyle factors, such as metabolic changes, reproductive health, and lifestyle habits [29].

The current data on body composition measurements used to assess excess weight indicates that the prevalence of females living with overweight and obesity is double that of males when using FMI. When evaluating BFP, differences between sexes were less pronounced but still showed a higher prevalence among females, with rates of 68.3% in males and 75.1% in females. This is not surprising, considering that the average body fat percentage is significantly higher in females (40.22%) than in males (31.80%) (both were abnormal according to WHO). Based on sex, the prevalence of obesity rose significantly between 1980 and 2019, increasing from 3.2% to 12.2% in men and from 6% to 15.7% in women. Women are expected to consistently have higher obesity rates due to the biologically driven differences, resulting in greater body fat [30, 31].

Multiple studies have examined the relationships among BMI, BFP, and FMI, showing that BMI alone may not be a comprehensive measure of obesity across populations. These studies emphasize the importance of incorporating BFP and FMI measurements, along with BMI, to achieve a more accurate assessment of obesity. Additionally, they report higher obesity prevalence when measured using BFP and FMI, findings that align with the current study’s results [32–35]. Furthermore, BMI has been reported to have a strong curvilinear correlation with BFP. This relationship is significantly influenced by age and sex; in most populations, BFP is higher in women than in men [32, 33, 36]. Wong et al. (2021) compared obesity using FMI and BMI and found that BFP identified more individuals as obese than either FMI or BMI (p < 0.001), overclassifying obesity in more than 35% of normal-weight individuals and 95% of those classified as overweight. BMI demonstrated a sensitivity of 78.9% and a specificity of 98.3% for obesity when using FMI as the reference, but under-classified obesity in the participants living in the overweight category by 14.9%. The discrepancy with BFP became more pronounced from ages 50 to 59, likely due to age-related loss of lean mass. The findings suggest that fat distribution, rather than total fat mass, may be a more critical factor in metabolic risk [35].

Inconsistent with the current result, a study conducted among healthy Saudi adults showed that 60% of participants met the BF% criteria for obesity, with a higher prevalence among females (30%) than males (25%) [37]. In addition, Li and colleagues’ study on Chinese adults demonstrated that the mean BFP was significantly lower in men than in women (23.9 ± 6.1% vs. 33.5 ± 7.1%, respectively; p < 0.01). Moreover, the optimal BFP cut-off values for predicting metabolic syndrome (MetS) and T2D were lower in men (25.45% and 26.65%, respectively) than in women (34.95% and 36.55%, respectively) [38]. Similarly, a population-based study in Singapore found that applying the BFP obesity cut-offs (> 25% for men and > 35% for women) resulted in notably high obesity prevalence—80.2% among men and 83.8% among women [39].

On the other hand, using WHtR global cut-off points, Jordanian cut-off points, and WHR, the prevalence of participants living with overweight and obesity was inversely proportional to other obesity indicators. It was higher among males than among females. The mean WHtR was 0.56 for males and 0.57 for females. Zhou and colleagues (2024) reported that these values were higher than the global median for both sexes in Central Asia, the Middle East, and North Africa. The Global median WHtR was 0.52 for females and 0.5 for males, while the median for Central Asia, the Middle East, and North Africa was 0.59 for females and 0.54 for males [40]. As for BMI, the current mean was 28 for males and 29.7 for females; this was higher than the median mentioned by Zhou and colleagues; the Global median BMI (females: 24.04, males: 23.53), and Central Asia, Middle East, North Africa (females: 27.59, males: 25.56) [40]. Yang and colleagues found that the prevalence of elevated WC increased from 46.9% in 1999–2000 to 60.3% in 2017–2018. Hence, WC prevalence was lower among males than among females (51.4% vs. 68.8%, p < 0.001) [19]. Although the mean WC of males (97.41 cm) was higher than that of females (91.78 cm) in the current study, it remained within the normal cut-off value (less than 102 cm). The prevalence of male WC abnormality was 38.8% compared to 58.1% in females. This was consistent with Yand and colleagues’ findings, who concluded that males had a lower prevalence of elevated WC than females (51.4% versus 68.8%, p < 0.001) in 2017–2018 [19].

The primary causes of the obesity epidemic are changes in the global food system and the spread of sedentary lifestyles. Furthermore, socioeconomic status disparities are closely linked to regional variations [41]. Moreover, it has been noted that obesity is more common among the poor in high-income nations and the wealthy in low-income ones [42]. This is due to several factors. People can buy more food when their income increases. Their eating patterns also shift, becoming increasingly “Westernized” in the process. Rich individuals also tend to lead more sedentary lives and hold sedentary occupations [43]. Nevertheless, in higher-income societies where obesity is socially stigmatized, this tendency shifts. Furthermore, high-income nations have better access to weight-loss programs, healthcare, education, and nutritious meals [12].

Strengths and limitations

One of the major strengths of this study is its large sample size of 2,633 participants, which enhances the reliability and statistical power of the findings and provides a solid basis for estimating the prevalence of obesity in Jordan. Additionally, using multiple anthropometric indices, including BMI, WHR, WHtR, and BFP, provides a comprehensive approach to assessing obesity from multiple perspectives, thereby strengthening the validity of the results. The study’s inclusion of both males and females enables a detailed analysis of sex differences in obesity prevalence, a crucial consideration for developing targeted public health interventions. Moreover, the study is highly relevant given its post-COVID-19 data collection, which provides insights into the pandemic’s impact on obesity trends in Jordan.

Despite the study’s strengths, several limitations should be considered. The study’s cross-sectional design limits its ability to establish causal relationships or observe changes over time, as it provides only a snapshot of obesity prevalence at a single point in time. The study was also conducted in the middle governorates of Jordan, which may not capture regional variations in obesity rates, thus limiting the generalizability of the findings to other areas of the country. In addition, some self-reported data, such as physical activity, were used, which may introduce information bias. Certain populations, such as pregnant women, individuals on immunosuppressive medications, and those with thyroid disorders, were excluded from the study, which reduces the applicability of the results to these groups. Additionally, although trained nutritionists performed the anthropometric measurements, small measurement errors could have occurred, potentially introducing inaccuracies in the obesity classifications and affecting the reliability of the findings. Moreover, snowball sampling may introduce selection bias, as individuals with similar characteristics or social connections are more likely to be recruited. The validity and calibration characteristics of the specific BIA device used were not fully described, potentially affecting measurement accuracy. In addition, BIA estimates are sensitive to hydration status, fasting status, and recent physical activity, and residual measurement error related to these factors cannot be ruled out despite standardized assessment procedures. Furthermore, BIA-derived estimates of body fat percentage and FMI are indirect measures and may differ from values obtained using gold-standard techniques such as dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA); therefore, the body composition results should be interpreted with caution. Temporal comparisons across 2009, 2017, and 2024 were based on aggregated prevalence estimates rather than individual-level longitudinal data, which limited the ability to test changes over time formally or to attribute observed trends to the COVID-19 pandemic. Finally, residual confounding may remain despite adjustment for key sociodemographic and lifestyle variables.

Conclusions

In conclusion, adults living with overweight and obesity remain highly prevalent in Jordan, with rates ranging from 41.7% to 72.9%, depending on the anthropometric or body composition index used. Sex differences are notable, with varying rates across indicators such as WHtR, WC, and WHR. Multivariable analyses demonstrated that sex differences in obesity persisted after adjustment for age, education, income, smoking status, and physical activity, although the direction and magnitude varied by obesity indicator. Females exhibited a greater adiposity burden when assessed using body fat–based measures, whereas males showed a higher prevalence of central obesity when WHR was used to define it. Additionally, the obesity pattern by FMI shows a decrease in males and an increase in females as obesity severity rises. While no significant sex difference was observed in BMI-defined obesity, females had higher odds of central obesity when assessed by waist circumference criteria. They were more likely to fall into higher categories of fat mass index, indicating greater adiposity severity. Conversely, females showed lower odds of obesity when evaluated using WHtR, WHR, and body fat percentage, underscoring the limitations of relying on a single indicator to assess obesity burden.

Over the past decade, the prevalence of overweight and obesity among adults in Jordan has remained persistently high, with only modest declines observed for certain indicators since 2017, particularly among females, while central obesity levels in 2024 remain markedly higher than those reported in 2009. These findings highlight the sustained public health burden of obesity and emphasize the importance of using multiple anthropometric and body composition indices to capture sex-specific patterns of adiposity. The results also have important implications for obesity surveillance and prevention in Jordan. Longitudinal and nationally representative studies are needed to understand recent trends better and assess the effectiveness of public health interventions. Additionally, incorporating multiple measures, particularly waist circumference and fat mass–based indices, into national monitoring systems and routine primary healthcare practice could improve the early detection of high-risk individuals. Such tactics may also support the development of targeted, sex-sensitive strategies to address modifiable risk factors such as physical inactivity and socioeconomic disparities.
