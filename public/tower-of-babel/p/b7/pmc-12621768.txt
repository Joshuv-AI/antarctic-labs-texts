Comparison of single-incision intragastric and conventional multiport laparoscopic wedge resections for endophytic gastric subepithelial tumors: a retrospective single-center cohort study
Authors: Jin Ah Kwon, Jin Sung Kim, In Kyu Park, Song Soo Yang, Dong Jin Park, Gyu Yeol Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeEndophytic gastric subepithelial tumors (SETs), especially those near the gastroesophageal junction (GEJ), present challenges in localization and resection. This study aimed to compare the outcomes of single-incision intragastric wedge resection (SIWR) and conventional multiport laparoscopic wedge resection (CWR) for endophytic gastric SETs, including a subgroup analysis of tumors located near the GEJ.MethodsThis retrospective, single-center study included 124 patients who underwent laparoscopic wedge resection for endophytic gastric SETs between January 2016 and December 2024. Operative and postoperative outcomes were compared between the SIWR (n = 23) and CWR (n = 101) groups. Subgroup analysis was performed for tumors located within 2 cm of the GEJ.ResultsThe mean tumor size was smaller in the SIWR group than in the CWR group (2.03 ± 0.97 cm vs. 2.57 ± 1.14 cm, P = 0.038). The SIWR group showed a longer postoperative hospital stay (6.30 ± 6.55 days vs. 4.89 ± 1.29 days, P = 0.045) and a higher complication rate (21.7% vs. 4%, P = 0.003), but a shorter time to oral intake (1.43 ± 0.95 days vs. 1.98 ± 0.98 days, P = 0.016). In the GEJ subgroup, SIWR was more commonly performed and showed a shorter time to oral intake without increasing early complications. Late complications were observed only in the CWR group.ConclusionBoth CWR and SIWR are feasible approaches for endophytic gastric SETs. SIWR may be preferable in selecting GEJ-proximal cases but requires cautious application due to its higher complication rate.

INTRODUCTION

Gastric subepithelial tumor (SET) has malignant potential, and surgical resection is generally recommended for tumors ≥2 cm in size. Minimally invasive surgical techniques, such as laparoscopy, are widely used for the removal of gastric SETs [123]. Gastric SETs can be categorized based on their growth patterns into exophytic, endophytic, and dumbbell-shaped types. Endophytic SETs, which grow inwardly within the gastric wall, pose a unique surgical challenge due to their concealed nature, making intraoperative localization more difficult. The surgical difficulty is further amplified when these tumors are located in the upper third of the stomach, especially near the gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) [456].

Intragastric wedge resection has been introduced as a technique for the management of endophytic gastric SETs, and numerous surgeons have attempted this approach [7891011121314]. Although promising, intragastric wedge resection remains technically demanding, and comparative studies with standard laparoscopic approaches are still scarce. To our knowledge, no previous study has directly compared SIWR and CWR in the management of endophytic SETs near the GEJ, which represents a significant clinical challenge.

This study aimed to address this gap by directly comparing the outcomes of single-incision intragastric wedge resection (SIWR) and conventional multiport laparoscopic wedge resection (CWR) for endophytic gastric SETs. Additionally, a subgroup analysis was conducted to specifically assess the outcomes for tumors located near the GEJ.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Ulsan University Hospital (No. 2025-01-009). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. The study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients

This was a single-center, retrospective study. From January 2016 to December 2024, a total of 256 patients underwent wedge resection for gastric SETs at Ulsan University Hospital. All operations were performed by 2 surgeons. Of these, 124 patients were confirmed to have endophytic gastric SETs and were included in the present a nalysis. Patient data were collected through a detailed review of medical records. The choice between SIWR and CWR was based on tumor characteristics and anatomical considerations. SIWR was generally preferred for deeply embedded endophytic tumors that were technically difficult to visualize and resect externally, particularly those located near the GEJ. In contrast, CWR was selected for tumors that appeared more accessible from the serosal surface on preoperative imaging or exhibited an exophytic growth pattern.

Operative technique

Conventional multiport laparoscopic wedge resection

Under general anesthesia, patients were placed supine on a table in the reverse Trendelenburg position. The surgeon and scopist stood on the patient’s right side. A 12-mm trocar was first inserted at the umbilicus using the Hasson open technique to establish pneumoperitoneum. Two additional trocars were then placed: one 5-mm trocar at the right subcostal margin and a 12-mm trocar in the right mid-abdomen under laparoscopic guidance. Additional trocars were inserted in the left abdomen as needed, depending on the operator’s judgment.

The stomach was systematically explored to identify the gastric SETs. In the case of an endophytic tumor that could not be identified by direct visualization or palpation, a gastrostomy was performed to locate the mass. Once the tumor was identified, it was either resected manually or removed using a laparoscopic linear stapler, depending on the situation.

Single-incision intragastric wedge resection

Under general anesthesia, patients were positioned in the lithotomy position. The surgeon and scopist stood between the patient’s legs. An incision of about 2.5 cm was made at the umbilicus. A glove port (Nelis Corp.) was inserted through the incision (Fig. 1A). Once pneumoperitoneum was established, a 3-dimensional flexible scope (Olympus) was introduced. The greater curvature of the lower body of the stomach was exteriorized through the incision.

A 2.5-cm incision was made on the stomach wall, and a second wound protector was inserted (Fig. 1B, C). The cap of the glove port was then secured to the inner wound protector, allowing for intragastric surgery with CO2 insufflation (Fig. 1D). During the extragastric phase, CO2 insufflation pressure was maintained at 12 mmHg. For the intragastric phase, it was reduced to 8–10 mmHg to minimize mucosal tension and ensure safe handling within the gastric lumen. The scope was inserted into the stomach with caution to avoid mucosal injury. After identifying the SET, a traction suture was applied to the normal mucosa near the tumor, as needed, to facilitate manipulation (Fig. 2A).

A powered articulating laparoscopic linear stapler (Signia, Medtronic) was used for tumor resection (Fig. 2A). The resected specimen was extracted through the wound protector, and the gastric incision was closed using barbed sutures (V-loc, Medtronic) (Fig. 2B).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using R software (ver. 4.3.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Continuous variables were compared using the Student t-test, while categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Among the 124 patients with endophytic gastric SETs, 101 patients underwent CWR, while 23 patients underwent SIWR. The mean follow-up period was 60.6 months.

Table 1 presents the clinicopathological characteristics of the patients who underwent laparoscopic wedge resection for endophytic SETs. The mean age of the patients was 56.5 ± 12.7 years. Among the total of 124 patients, 56 (45.2%) were male, and 68 (54.8%) were female. The mean body mass index (BMI) was 24.6 ± 3.25 kg/m2. In terms of tumor location, 84 tumors (67.7%) were located in the upper third of the stomach, 25 (20.2%) in the middle third, and 15 (12.1%) in the lower third. Gastrointestinal stromal tumors were the most common pathological finding, identified in 85 patients (68.6%), followed by leiomyoma in 19 patients (15.4%). The mean tumor size was 2.47 ± 1.13 cm. All cases achieved R0 resection, confirmed by histopathological analysis.

The operative outcomes are summarized in Table 2. The mean operation time was 78.8 ± 39.7 minutes, and the mean estimated blood loss (EBL) was 14.9 ± 16.7 mL. The mean time to first flatus after surgery was 1.90 ± 0.99 days. Sips of water after surgery started at an average of 1.88 ± 0.98 days. The mean postoperative hospital stay was 5.15 ± 3.06 days. A total of 9 patients (7.2%) experienced postoperative complications, and 3 cases (2.4%) showed complications of Clavien-Dindo (CD) grade IIIa or higher. One patient in the CWR group had a CD grade of IIIb, wherein the SET was located at the GEJ and CWR was attempted. During the procedure, the tumors could not be identified by inspection or palpation, necessitating gastrostomy. Significant bleeding occurred during gastrostomy, making visibility challenging. Postoperative biopsy revealed that the resected mass was a hematoma, and subsequent endoscopy confirmed that the mass remained at the same location. As a result, this patient ultimately required reoperation for definitive tumor removal, highlighting the limitations of CWR in challenging tumor localization cases. Two patients in the SIWR group developed CD grade IIIa complications, both due to postoperative bleeding. One patient had bleeding from the resection line, which was controlled by endoscopic coagulation, while the other experienced bleeding at the gastrostomy site, managed with endoscopic epinephrine injection.

A comparison of the operative findings between the CWR and SIWR groups is presented in Table 3. The mean resected tumor size was significantly larger in the CWR group (2.57 ± 1.14 cm) than in the SIWR group (2.03 ± 0.97 cm, P = 0.038). The mean postoperative hospital stay was significantly shorter in the CWR group (4.89 ± 1.29 days) compared to the SIWR group (6.30 ± 6.55 days, P = 0.045). Additionally, the incidence of complications was significantly higher in the SIWR group (21.7%) compared to the CWR group (4%, P = 0.003). The distribution of complications based on the CD classification was also different between the 2 groups. In terms of recovery parameters, the mean time to sips of water was significantly shorter in the SIWR group (1.43 ± 0.95 days) compared to the CWR group (1.98 ± 0.98 days, P = 0.016). However, there were no significant differences between the 2 groups in terms of operation time, EBL, or time to first flatus.

Wedge resection of SETs located near the GEJ is particularly challenging. Among the patients with SETs within 2 cm of the GEJ, 17 cases (16.8%) were in the CWR group, while 11 cases (47.8%) were in the SIWR group, showing a statistically significant difference (P < 0.001). Table 4 presents a detailed comparison of the operative findings between the 2 groups. In terms of operative outcomes, the mean time to sips of water was significantly shorter in the SIWR group (1.46 ± 0.82 days) compared to the CWR group (2.56 ± 1.38 days, P = 0.024). However, no statistically significant differences were observed between the 2 groups in other parameters, including operation time, EBL, tumor size, time to first flatus, or postoperative hospital stay. Regarding complications, 1 patient (5.6%) in the CWR group and 1 patient (9.1%) in the SIWR group experienced early complications (P = 0.715). In the CWR group, 1 patient experienced a CD grade IIIb complication, while 2 patients (11.1%) developed late complications. Among these late complications, 1 patient developed gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) that was managed with medication, and another patient had a recurrent mass at the site of the resected leiomyoma. In the SIWR group, 1 patient experienced a CD grade IIIa complication due to postoperative bleeding, which was managed endoscopically.

DISCUSSION

In Korea, esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) is an essential part of the national health screening program and is therefore widely performed. As a result, small asymptomatic gastric SETs are frequently detected incidentally, and many patients opt for surgical removal even when the lesions are small [1516]. Although several guidelines recommend surveillance for gastric SETs smaller than 2 cm in the absence of high-risk features, surgical resection is often pursued in Korea due to local clinical practice patterns. Patients commonly request surgical removal because of anxiety over potential malignancy and a preference for definitive treatment. The increasing safety and accessibility of minimally invasive surgical techniques further support this trend in clinical decision-making.

The difficulty of SET resection depends on the size, location, and growth pattern of the tumor. Exophytic lesions of sufficient size, particularly those located in accessible areas such as the anterior wall of the gastric body, are relatively easy to identify and resect. In contrast, small endophytic tumors are more difficult to localize intraoperatively, which complicates the resection process, especially when the tumor is situated near the GEJ [456].

Tumor morphology plays a key role in determining the surgical approach. CWR is generally suitable for accessible, exophytic lesions and offers technical simplicity and shorter operative times. However, it can be suboptimal for tumors on the posterior wall or near the GEJ due to limited visualization. In such cases, intragastric wedge resection offers direct intraluminal access, enabling precise resection of deeply embedded lesions. Despite its technical demands and potential for higher early complication rates, SIWR is especially advantageous for tumors in anatomically challenging locations such as the GEJ or in areas not easily accessible from the serosal side.

Intragastric wedge resection has emerged as a valuable technique for managing endophytic gastric SETs. Although this approach is promising, it is technically demanding, particularly when performed through a single-incision technique. While the feasibility, safety, and clinical utility of intragastric resection for tumors near the GEJ have been reported [512131417], many surgeons continue to favor extragastric or transgastric approaches due to their relative simplicity and familiarity [7181920]. In this study, we present our institutional experience with laparoscopic wedge resection of endophytic gastric SETs and compare the outcomes of SIWR and CWR, including a subgroup analysis of tumors located near the GEJ (Fig. 3).

In our cohort, 2 patients in the SIWR group experienced postoperative bleeding. One case involved bleeding from the resection line, while the other involved bleeding at the gastrostomy site. Both were successfully managed with endoscopic intervention without further complications. As the stomach is a highly vascular organ, particularly in the mucosal layer, postoperative bleeding is a known risk. Following these events, oversewing sutures using barbed suture material was applied to the resection line in subsequent cases, which effectively prevented further bleeding. Another bleeding event occurred in a young, obese male patient whose stomach could not be properly exteriorized through the umbilical incision. Excessive tension during the procedure led to intraoperative bleeding. Postoperatively, edema developed around the gastrostomy site, making it difficult to achieve primary closure. As the edema subsided, the pressure on the exposed vessels at the site decreased, resulting in delayed postoperative bleeding. This complication was resolved after adopting the “tunnel method” in subsequent cases [8]. In this technique, a tunnel is created by connecting a small wound retractor placed at the gastrotomy site to the transumbilical port. This configuration allows for stable and direct access to the stomach cavity, enhancing visualization and improving instrument control. By reducing mucosal tension and providing a more secure operating field, the tunnel method may help lower the risk of bleeding-related complications, particularly in patients with thick abdominal walls, high BMI, or cases where the stomach cannot be easily exteriorized.

In one case of the CWR group, a tumor located at the GEJ could not be identified intraoperatively, leading to unnecessary gastrostomy and bleeding that required further intervention. Although the lesion itself was not resected, we classified this event as a postoperative complication (CD grade IIIb) because it resulted in procedure-related morbidity, whereas the incomplete resection should be regarded as an outcome failure rather than a complication. The gastric SET was located on the posterior wall of the GEJ, and CWR was attempted due to difficulty in localizing the tumor intraoperatively. Opening the anterior gastric wall was complicated by unexpected bleeding, which significantly impaired visibility. A hematoma was subsequently mistaken for the SET and resected. Pathologic examination confirmed the specimen to be a hematoma, and follow-up EGD revealed that the tumor remained. The patient required reoperation, which was successfully performed using SIWR. This case illustrates that although CWR is widely used for endophytic gastric lesions, it may be suboptimal when localization is uncertain, particularly in the presence of bleeding or poor visualization. Moreover, the extragastric approach carries a risk of contamination due to gastric content spillage. In such situations, an intragastric approach such as SIWR may provide a safer and more effective alternative.

Both surgical techniques were feasible. However, the higher complication rate observed in the SIWR group, particularly during our initial experience, suggests a learning curve effect and the need for technical refinement. All CD grade IIIa complications occurred in the SIWR group. These findings suggest that while SIWR is technically viable, particularly for tumors located in anatomically challenging areas such as the GEJ, it carries a higher risk of postoperative morbidity and thus requires careful patient selection and surgical expertise.

SIWR was associated with a significantly shorter time to oral intake. However, other early recovery indicators, including time to first flatus and hospital stay, were comparable or less favorable than those of the CWR group. Thus, although SIWR may facilitate earlier oral feeding, its overall benefit in early postoperative recovery should be interpreted with caution.

SETs near the GEJ pose specific risks, including postoperative stricture or GERD, due to the potential for sphincter injury. The extragastric approach presents additional challenges: access may be obstructed by the liver, localization of endophytic tumors is difficult, and posterior wall access via gastrostomy often limits visibility. In this study, we compared outcomes in patients with SETs located within 2 cm of the GEJ. Although we hypothesized that SIWR would shorten operative time due to more direct access, the difference was not statistically significant, likely due to the longer-than-expected time required to transition into the intragastric phase.

Late complications were observed only in the CWR group. Follow-up EGD more than 1 year postoperatively revealed 1 case of newly developed GERD that required medical management and 1 case of local recurrence at the original resection site. The ability to visualize the GEJ directly during SIWR may help reduce the risk of sphincter injury and incomplete resection, potentially lowering the incidence of GERD and recurrence.

Taken together, these findings support the role of SIWR as a tailored approach for the resection of endophytic gastric SETs, particularly those located near the GEJ, where conventional methods may increase the risk of anatomical disruption or incomplete resection. However, the relatively high complication rate associated with SIWR underscores the need for technical refinements, improved intraoperative visualization, and the development of adjunctive measures to minimize bleeding and wound-related complications during intragastric surgery.

This study has several limitations. It was a retrospective, single-center, and non-randomized analysis with a relatively small sample size, particularly in the SIWR and GEJ subgroups, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, selection bias may have influenced the choice of surgical technique, as more technically complex cases were more likely to be treated with SIWR. Furthermore, SIWR is a technically demanding procedure and has not yet been widely adopted, which may have influenced the observed complication rates.

Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to directly compare SIWR and CWR for the treatment of endophytic gastric SETs, including tumors located near the GEJ. As such, it provides meaningful insights into the relative advantages and limitations of each technique and highlights the potential role of SIWR as an alternative approach in anatomically challenging cases. Future multicenter, prospective, randomized studies with larger sample sizes and long-term follow-up are needed to confirm and expand upon these findings.

In conclusion, both CWR and SIWR are effective minimally invasive options for the resection of endophytic gastric SETs. SIWR provides direct intraluminal access, which may be particularly advantageous for tumors located near the GEJ. However, its complication profile necessitates careful patient selection and procedural expertise, particularly during the early phase of adoption. CWR remains a well-established and reliable standard approach, though caution is warranted when operating near the GEJ because of potential long-term functional complications. Continued refinement of surgical techniques and evidence from larger-scale studies will be essential to guide optimal treatment strategies for this patient population.
