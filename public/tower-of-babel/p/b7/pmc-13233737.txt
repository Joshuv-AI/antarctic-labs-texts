Appendiceal tie syndrome causing small bowel obstruction in 30 year old woman
Authors: Anduamlak Bazezew, Thaddee Ngoy, Tesfahunegn Feleke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Appendicitis is a common cause of acute abdominal emergencies, but mechanical small bowel obstruction due to appendicealtie syndrome is rare. We present a 30-year-old healthy female patient with a typical presentation of febrile intestinal obstruction with multiple central air-fluid levels on an erect abdominal X-ray. During laparotomy, a phlegmonous appendix was found encircling and strangulating the terminal ileum with no signs of bowel ischemia. A simple appendectomy was performed, relieving the obstruction. Postoperative recovery was uneventful aside from a superficial wound infection. Appendiceal tie syndrome is a rare entity that can present as febrile small bowel obstruction in patients of any age group. The diagnostic value of advanced imaging remains uncertain and clinical suspicion is of paramount importance. Timely surgical intervention leads to improved outcomes. Simple appendectomy may result in resolution of obstruction and clinical symptoms if affected bowel segments are viable despite delayed presentation.

Introduction

Appendicitis is a common cause of abdominal surgical emergencies, occurring with an incidence of 7%-14% [1]. It has many complications, including functional intestinal obstruction by paralytic ileus or mechanical obstruction because of adhesions from periappendicular inflammation. However, mechanical occlusion due to strangulation of the terminal ileum by an inflammatory appendiceal knot is rare, and the clinical picture remains that of acute febrile intestinal obstruction [1].

This entity is difficult to diagnose preoperatively, even with cross-sectional imaging, and requires urgent medical and surgical management. We report on the management of a case of an inflammatory appendicular knot on the ileum in a patient with delayed presentation [2].

Case report

We present a case of a 30-year-old female patient with no significant past medical history presenting to the emergency department (ED) with complaints of non-localized abdominal pain associated with abdominal distension, bilious vomiting, and failure to pass faeces and flatus of 7 days duration. The patient had high-grade fever, shaking chills, malaise and loss of appetite for 1 day, prompting ED visit. She was taking over-the-counter acetaminophen, which partially controlled the pain and achieved defervescence. No gynecologic complaints were offered. On physical exam, she was acutely sick looking, febrile, with a maximum temperature of 39.2 °C, tachypneic, tachycardic, and significantly volume depleted but normotensive. Abdominal examination was remarkable for a distended abdomen with diffuse tenderness, right lower quadrant rebound tenderness, and reduced bowel sounds. Hernial sites were free. Laboratory work showed mild leukocytosis of 11,000 cells/microliter, no bandemia, normal creatinine, bilirubin and INR, and borderline low potassium 3.5 mEq/l, but no other electrolyte abnormalities.

Abdominal X-ray showed signs of small intestinal obstruction with multiple central air-fluid level (Fig. 1). An abdominal ultrasound was not done, as a clinical decision was made to perform an emergency laparotomy. The patient was kept nil per oral with active nasogastric aspiration. A midline laparotomy incision was made, and the peritoneal cavity was entered. On intraoperative examination, distended loops of small bowel segments from the jejunum to the distal ileum were observed. These loops were followed distally to reveal a constricting ring around the terminal ileum (Fig. 2) created by a phlegmonous appendix with its tip adherent to the root of mesentery (Fig. 3), obstructing an edematous loop of terminal ileum without signs of ischemia (Fig. 4). As the bowel was viable, a simple appendectomy was done, releasing the strangulation. No other lesions were found on the bowels on meticulous examination. The postoperative period was marked by superficial wound infection on the fifth day, which was managed with local wound care. She was discharged home after an 18-day of hospital stay. Discussion

Small bowel obstruction is a rare complication of acute appendicitis. The first case of intestinal obstruction due to acute appendicitis was described by Hotchkiss [3]. The etiologies were later divided into mechanical and septic appendicitis or a combination of both [3,4]. The appendix is a mobile organ and can be in a variable position. Hence, when inflamed, it tends to adhere to surrounding structures, resulting in mechanical small bowel obstruction. Thus, increased length of appendix has been proposed to be a predisposing factor for such an event [2].

The underlying pathology in intestinal obstruction caused by appendicitis is previously classified into: adynamic, mechanical (without strangulation), Strangulation of intestine, and intestinal obstruction due to mesenteric ischemia. Strangulation can be due to the appendix wrapping around the base of a bowel loop, or when an inflamed appendix adheres to the caecum, small intestine or posterior peritoneum and a part of the bowel herniates through the gap. Obstruction caused by the loop of the appendix attaching to the mesentery and causing obstruction by torsion or direct compression, which is similar to the one in our case, is uncommon [1,[4], [5], [6]].

Preoperative diagnosis is difficult to make and previous authors hardly report ultrasound or CT scan findings even in adult populations [7,8]. In our case, a clinical decision was made to perform an emergency laparotomy, considering the long turnaround time for further imaging. Although our patient presented abdominal complaints that stretched over 7 days, there were no findings of appendiceal perforation or abscess formation. The affected small bowel segments were also distended but viable despite closed-loop obstruction in sharp contrast to previous studies [6,7]. Olijira et al. reported a case of viable appendicoileal knotting in a 30-year-old woman successfully treated with appendectomy alone; however, their patient presented within 24 hours of symptom onset [9]. A recent article from Nepal reported a simple appendectomy in a 43-year-old male who presented with 5 days of abdominal complaints who intraoperatively was found to have ileoappendiceal knotting with viable bowel loops [8]. The intraoperative picture in the above articles seems to show a similar scenario where the inflamed appendix is adherent to the base of the mesentery, obstructing and strangulating the terminal ileum. We are not certain whether these cases are simple report biases or if there are unforeseen differences in mechanism/progression of obstruction.

Conclusion

Ileoappendiceal knotting is a rare entity that can present as febrile small bowel obstruction in patients of any age group. The diagnostic value of advanced imaging remains uncertain and clinical suspicion is of paramount importance. Timely surgical intervention leads to improved outcomes. Simple appendectomy may result in resolution of obstruction and clinical symptoms if affected bowel segments are viable despite delayed presentation.
