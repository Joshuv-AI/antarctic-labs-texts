Does brain-computer interface-based mind reading threaten mental privacy? ethical reflections from interviews with Chinese experts
Authors: Fangxu Han, Haidan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rapid development of brain-computer interface (BCI) technology has sparked profound debates about the right to privacy, particularly concerning its potential to enable mind reading. While scholars have proposed the establishment of neurorights to safeguard mental privacy, questions remain about whether BCIs can genuinely decode inner thoughts and what makes their ethical implications distinctive.MethodsThis study conducted semi-structured interviews with 20 Chinese experts in the BCI and neuroscience fields to explore their perspectives on the concept, feasibility, and limitations of BCI-based mind reading (BMR). The transcriptions of the interviews were analyzed through reflexive thematic analysis to identify key themes and insights.ResultsThe findings reveal a range of expert perspectives on the interpretations and feasibility of BMR. Most participants believe that current BCI technology cannot decode inner thoughts, although they acknowledge the potential for future advancements. Key technical challenges, such as signal quality and reliance on background information, are highlighted.ConclusionWe summarize the interpretations, feasibility, and limitations of BMR and introduce a distinction between “strong BMR” and “weak BMR” to clarify their technical and ethical implications. Based on our analysis, we argue that current BMR does not pose unique ethical challenges compared with other forms of mind reading, and therefore does not yet justify the establishment of a distinct right to mental privacy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-025-01229-x.

Background

Neurotechnology has advanced rapidly in this century, particularly in the field of brain-computer interfaces (BCIs), which enable direct communication between the brain and external devices, such as computers or robotic arms [1–6]. This technology has already been applied in medicine, especially for treating mental disorders [1], and holds the potential to transform how people live and communicate [7]. However, the vast amounts of brain data collected by BCIs raise concerns about the potential exposure of individuals’ privacy. With the integration of artificial intelligence (AI), BCI systems have significantly improved in performance and accuracy [8]. Does this advancement make the human brain—often considered the “last fortress” of privacy—more vulnerable? Some scholars have proposed the establishment of neurorights to address these challenges [9, 10], and Chile has already integrated these rights into its legal framework [11]. However, this development invites critical questions: do BCIs truly pose a threat to privacy, or are these concerns overstated? To address this question, it is essential to first clarify the following key issues: What constitutes mind reading? Can BCIs achieve true mind reading? Furthermore, should BCI-based mind reading (BMR) be treated as a unique ethical concern?

BCIs and their challenges to privacy

The BCI is a system designed to record central nervous system (CNS) activity and translate it into artificial outputs. Its primary purpose is to replace, restore, enhance, supplement, or improve the CNS’s natural outputs, thereby transforming how it interacts with the body and the external world [12]. BCIs are being used across various fields, including healthcare, education, entertainment, marketing, security, and the military [7]. BCIs can be categorized based on their level of physical intrusion into the brain: (1) invasive BCIs, which involve the direct implantation of intracortical microelectrodes into the brain, offering the highest signal quality but posing significant risks; (2) non-invasive BCIs, which analyze brain activity from the surface of the scalp; and (3) semi-invasive BCIs, which place electrodes between the cerebral cortex and the skull [13]. Additionally, BCIs can also be classified by the type of brain activity they utilize: (1) active BCIs, which require users to voluntarily perform mental tasks (e.g., imagining limb movements); (2) reactive BCIs, which rely on users’ responses to external stimuli; and (3) passive BCIs, which capture brain activity generated involuntarily by the users [2, 4, 6, 14].

While BCIs present transformative possibilities, they also give rise to ethical concerns, particularly in relation to privacy. Scholars argue that “use of BCI is the greatest ethical challenge that neuroscience faces today” [5]. In 2024, the Artificial Intelligence Ethics Subcommittee of China’s National Science and Technology Ethics Commission issued Ethics Guidelines for Brain-Computer Interface Research, marking the first localized framework in China to address privacy and ethical challenges associated with BCIs [15]. BCIs’ ability to decode brain activity and potentially infer thoughts has sparked intense ethical debates [16]. The intrinsic link between the brain and mental states endows BCI-derived brain data with profound philosophical significance and moral weight [17]. As a result, many bioethicists and scientists have adopted the term “mind reading” to describe the potential capabilities of BCIs.

Current research on BMR

In the natural sciences, the metaphor of “readability” dates back to Galileo, when scientists attempted to interpret the “book of the universe” through rational discourse. This concept extended to medicine, where discussions on “brain reading” began with 17th-century studies of brain anatomy [18]. Modern neuroimaging technologies further support this idea, suggesting that the mind might be “read” from the brain. Recently, the term “mind reading” has become prevalent in neuroscience, raising concerns about potential uses of neurodevices by companies and governments for surveillance. Such devices could bypass human rationality and consciousness, decoding thoughts without consent [7, 10].

Mind reading broadly refers to guessing or inferring others’ intentions, beliefs, desires, and emotions [19]. In daily human interactions, this ability is known as “natural mind reading” [20]. Recommendation algorithms in applications analyze browsing histories and interaction patterns to predict user preferences and deliver targeted content. Similarly, political preferences can be inferred from users’ social media likes [21], a practice often referred to as “digital mind reading” [22]. With the advancement of neurotechnology, a new form of mind reading emerged: “neurotechnological mind reading.” This article focuses on one specific subset of this emerging field—BMR.

Current BMR research focuses on literature reviews and philosophical discussions [16, 22–26]. Some key distinctions have been made: Tong and Pratte differentiate brain reading from mind reading, suggesting that decoding private, subjective information qualifies as mind reading, whereas decoding current visual information is brain reading [26]. Rathkopf et al. propose that neurotechnological mind reading involves algorithms interpreting mental content solely from neural data, excluding conventional symbols produced by the subject. While this definition distinguishes neurotechnological mind reading from conventional methods, it does not clarify why it has distinctive ethical implications [25]; Gilbert and Russo analyze claims related to AI and neurotechnological mind reading, highlighting inconsistencies in terminology and hype in the field. They argue that neurotechnological mind reading requires prior brain reading [16]; Brown adopts a more moderate definition of neurotechnological mind reading, suggesting that it reveals sensitive mental content without decoding thoughts syntactically [22]; and Ryberg compares neurotechnological and natural mind reading in terms of direct access, reliability, loss of control, and amount of information, finding no fundamental difference [24]. Gilead differentiates “mind privacy” from “brain privacy,” arguing that neuroimaging may breach brain privacy, but mind privacy remains protected. He argues that the irreducibility of individual mental differences prevents the mind from being reduced to the body, which is much less accessible externally [23]. These discussions highlight key issues but show a lack of empirical research on BMR, particularly involving interviews with BCI experts.

A scoping review of social research on BCIs reveals that only two out of 73 articles focus exclusively on BCI expert interviews [4]. Most empirical studies target BCI users, such as Kögel et al.’s exploration of user experiences [6]. However, as key participants in BCI practice, experts not only shape the technology itself but also directly influence related decision-making processes [27]. Compared with ethicists, these experts offer deeper technical insight, providing valuable perspectives on ethical issues, case evaluations, and future challenges. Interviewing experts can foster objective ethical reflections [28], and mitigate potential exaggerations in ethical debates.

BMR and neurorights

Debates surrounding BMR often arise in discussions about neurorights [29, 30]. As neurotechnology advances, particularly through BCIs, these technologies are increasingly capable of measuring and even influencing brain activity. While their primary applications remain within the realms of medicine and research, direct-to-consumer neurodevices are increasingly integrated into daily life [31]. Some scholars argue that existing human rights frameworks fail to adequately address the threats and risks associated with these technologies, advocating for the establishment of new rights, often termed “neurorights” [32]. The interpretation of these rights varies: Yuste et al. propose that they encompass four ethical priorities: privacy, enhancement, algorithmic bias, and identity [33]. Ienca, on the other hand, identifies four key domains: freedom of thought, privacy, mental integrity, and personal identity [9]. Notably, despite these differences, mental privacy is universally acknowledged as a fundamental aspect of neurorights [34].

For a long time, mental privacy has been regarded as a core characteristic of human individuality and freedom. As the “right to be let alone,” privacy is foundational to human liberty and dignity. The decoding of brain data could reveal sensitive personal information, such as sexual orientation or political preferences, potentially leading to discrimination and bias [34]. The right to mental privacy aims to protect individuals from unwarranted intrusions and data collection of their mental states. The issue of mind reading lies at the heart of debates surrounding the right to mental privacy [22, 24, 25, 30, 35–37]. Proponents argue that the potential of BMR underscores the importance of mental privacy, framing it as a critical safeguard against unauthorized access to the mind [9, 34]; however, opponents contend that current BCIs have not yet achieved true mind reading or that BMR does not introduce unique ethical concerns [22, 24, 25, 36, 37].

In this context, an important question is the distinction between mental privacy and neuroprivacy, which also involves the differentiation between mental data and neural or brain data. Mental privacy is concerned with protecting an individual’s mental information regardless of its origin, whereas neuroprivacy focuses specifically on neural data [9, 22]. This distinction raises critical questions: Is all mental data derived entirely from brain data? Does all brain data contain mental information? The Neurorights Initiative defines mental privacy as the protection of neural data, yet critics label this view as “neuroessentialism” or “neuroexceptionalism,” arguing that neural data are not inherently different from other sensitive data types, such as search histories or health records. Consequently, they assert that mental privacy is not uniquely distinct from general privacy [38].

Research questions

On the basis of the current state of research, we identify three critical issues in the ongoing debates about neurorights, particularly the right to mental privacy. These issues have significant implications for the ethical evaluation of BMR but remain underexplored.

The first issue is determining what constitutes mind reading. Divergent interpretations of this normative question could lead to biased interpretations of empirical data [39]. Establishing a basic consensus on its definition is crucial before delving into specific experimental details. While a precise definition may be elusive, discussions with experts can expand the understanding of the concept’s scope and implications.

The second issue is the need for a deeper understanding of the technical details of BMR. Are ethical papers and media reports claiming achievements in mind reading based on rigorous analysis? Do they misinterpret or exaggerate scientific findings? Understanding scientists’ actual perspectives on the feasibility of mind reading is essential. Detailed technical insights, obtained through expert interviews, are vital for addressing these questions.

The third issue concerns whether BMR is ethically distinct from traditional methods of mind reading. If BMR lacks fundamental differences from other techniques, justifying the right to mental privacy becomes challenging. After acquiring a detailed understanding of the technical specifics, we explore the conditions under which BMR might present unique ethical concerns.

This study seeks to address these questions through interviews with Chinese experts. The findings are categorized into three themes: interpretations of BMR, progress in BMR, and limitations of BMR. The discussion section is organized into three parts: the characteristics of BMR, distinctiveness of BMR, and the necessary conditions for achieving strong BMR. We review major English-language literature on these issues and incorporate insights from experts to provide a Chinese perspective. This study demonstrates that current BMR is not yet sufficient to necessitate the distinct right to mental privacy and that different types of BCI may have varying ethical implications.

Methods

Recruitment and participants

Participants were recruited through a combination of literature review, conference materials, and expert referrals. Initially, we identified potential interviewees by reviewing relevant academic literature and conference proceedings. Following this, we sent email invitations to individuals whose expertise aligned with the focus of our study. Additionally, some participants were invited during academic conferences we attended, while others were referred by colleagues or experts in the field. For those referred, we followed up with invitations via WeChat. In total, we recruited 20 experts in the fields of BCIs and neuroscience. Data collection

This study employed expert interviews to gather insights on BCI technology and its ethical implications. Interviews were chosen as the primary method due to their ability to provide in-depth exploration of emerging technologies and complex ethical issues that are difficult to address through surveys. The semi-structured, face-to-face format offered flexibility, allowing experts to express their views while ensuring focus on key topics [40]. These topics included recent developments in BCI technology, future trends, ethical and social implications, governance challenges, and, in particular, mind reading—specifically, its potential realization and the privacy-sensitive information it may reveal (see supplementary file). Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the institutional review board of Peking University. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

We conducted interviews with these 20 experts between June 2021 and October 2021. Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, most interviews were held online via Tencent Meeting. Each interview lasted approximately 50–80 minutes, with an average duration of over 60 minutes. The discussions explored experts’ perspectives on the latest advancements in BCI technology, its future development trends, and the associated ethical and governance issues. All interviews were conducted in Mandarin and transcribed verbatim by two other members of the research team and subsequently reviewed and verified by HC. To protect participants’ confidentiality, all experts were anonymized, except for S2, Professor Yi Zeng, who requested the use of his real name.

Data analysis

The transcripts were analyzed using NVivo 10 software, which facilitates qualitative data analysis through coding. We did not use the auto-coding function of NVivo; instead, all coding was done manually to ensure careful consideration and accurate categorization of each text segment. The analysis employed reflexive thematic analysis to identify, analyze, and report patterns (themes) within the data [41]. Each transcript was read multiple times to gain a comprehensive understanding of the content. All annotations and classifications of the transcripts were performed in Chinese to preserve the linguistic nuances of participants’ responses. The interview excerpts cited in this paper were translated into English by the authors.

The interview transcripts were independently coded by FH and HC. In the initial coding phase, both researchers marked relevant text segments and assigned descriptive labels, following an iterative process in which new codes were added as insights emerged. During this phase, special attention was paid to content related to mind reading—not only direct responses to questions on the topic but also relevant views expressed in the context of other discussion topics. After completing their independent coding, FH and HC compared and discussed their initial codes, and collaboratively developed themes through reflexive engagement with the data. Both authors brought distinct yet complementary perspectives informed by their disciplinary training and personal backgrounds. FH is a male graduate student specializing in the philosophy of science and technology, with an interdisciplinary background in Medical English and bioethics. His current research focuses on mind-reading in neurotechnologies. HC is a female associate professor with a background in science and technology studies and bioethics. She has extensive experience conducting qualitative research on the ethical, legal, and social implications (ELSI) of emerging medical technologies. Both authors are based in China and share the same cultural and linguistic background as the participants, which facilitated contextual sensitivity and reflexive engagement throughout the thematic analysis.

Results

Interpretations of BMR

Fourteen experts shared their interpretations of mind reading during the interviews, revealing diverse perspectives. Analyzing this varied understanding is crucial, as it directly influences our perception of current BCI capabilities. Differing opinions among experts on the scope and standards of mind reading, even when evaluating the same technological landscape, may lead to vastly different conclusions.

The most common interpretation, mentioned by 12 experts, involves the ability to read “inner thoughts.” Based on the context of the interview texts, this relatively vague term generally refers to whatever the user is currently thinking or the information present in their consciousness. This includes inner speech, awareness, logic, and decision-making.

“Now, it’s already possible to detect distinct brainwave patterns when a subject thinks of different languages or texts. Since they’re different, mind reading can indeed be achieved.” (E1).

“Now, it’s already possible to detect distinct brainwave patterns when a subject thinks of different languages or texts. Since they’re different, mind reading can indeed be achieved.” (E1).

“BCIs could observe and stimulate the brain in the future, decoding information similar to so-called mind reading. […] It can completely interpret what a patient wants to say.” (S3).

“BCIs could observe and stimulate the brain in the future, decoding information similar to so-called mind reading. […] It can completely interpret what a patient wants to say.” (S3).

“What is currently referred to as mind reading is all a scam. Right now, if someone doesn’t share their thoughts, no one else can know them.” (S4).

“What is currently referred to as mind reading is all a scam. Right now, if someone doesn’t share their thoughts, no one else can know them.” (S4).

“The idea of knowing inner thoughts purely by detecting neural activity is science fiction. […] I don’t believe mind reading can be achieved.” (S5).

“The idea of knowing inner thoughts purely by detecting neural activity is science fiction. […] I don’t believe mind reading can be achieved.” (S5).

“If, one day, human technology reaches a stage where I can pick up your brain signals from dozens of meters away and decode your thoughts, reasoning, and logic, then humanity will have no privacy left.” (S16).

“If, one day, human technology reaches a stage where I can pick up your brain signals from dozens of meters away and decode your thoughts, reasoning, and logic, then humanity will have no privacy left.” (S16).

Among those who interpreted mind reading as inner thoughts, some expanded the concept further. S1 suggested that identifying brain disorders could also be considered a form of mind reading:

“Some aspects can be read, such as determining epilepsy or depression. However, as of now, it’s impossible to discern what a user is thinking.” (S1).

“Some aspects can be read, such as determining epilepsy or depression. However, as of now, it’s impossible to discern what a user is thinking.” (S1).

S8 emphasized that retrieving visual images does not qualify as mind reading:

“It’s possible to retrieve an image, but that’s not mind reading.” (S8).

“It’s possible to retrieve an image, but that’s not mind reading.” (S8).

Two experts (S2 and S15) did not mention inner thoughts. S2 defined mind reading as the ability to access “imagined images” and “situational knowledge”:

“In 1997, researchers could show participants several images and then ask them to imagine one. With current technology, it’s even possible to reverse engineer and identify the exact image they imagine. […] It also enables access to situational knowledge, such as past experiences or familiarity with certain individuals.” (S2).

“In 1997, researchers could show participants several images and then ask them to imagine one. With current technology, it’s even possible to reverse engineer and identify the exact image they imagine. […] It also enables access to situational knowledge, such as past experiences or familiarity with certain individuals.” (S2).

S15 described mind reading as the ability to assess “emotions and feelings”:

“Mind reading is essentially about evaluating emotions, feelings, and mental fluctuations. […] similar to a polygraph or an electrocardiogram (ECG).” (S15).

“Mind reading is essentially about evaluating emotions, feelings, and mental fluctuations. […] similar to a polygraph or an electrocardiogram (ECG).” (S15).

The experts provided diverse interpretations of mind reading, which are overlapping and sometimes ambiguous. These perspectives can be categorized into following levels based on the complexity of brain decoding: (1) Brain disorders: primarily focused on clinical applications and disease diagnosis, which represent some of the most common applications of BCIs today [42]; (2) Emotions and feelings: relates to basic psychological states, capturing surface-level mental activities; (3) Visual images and inner speech: involves the encoding and decoding of specific perceptual information, such as an event-related potential (ERP), P300 wave [7]; (4) Consciousness, logical reasoning, decisions, and inner thoughts: encompasses higher cognitive functions, involving abstract mental processes that may require the coordination of multiple brain regions.

All the experts agreed that mind reading involves decoding or interpreting brain activity rather than merely accessing raw brain data. While specific interpretations varied, the most frequently mentioned concept was “inner thoughts,” indicating a generally stricter standard among the experts. This variation highlights that mind reading is still more of a colloquial term than a precise scientific definition. The similarities and differences in the experts’ views reflect diverse interpretations shaped by their respective backgrounds and understandings. These findings underscore the importance of establishing a unified definition to accurately evaluate advancements in BCI technology.

Progress in BMR

Eighteen experts provided insights into the feasibility of mind reading, with varied views on its current applications and challenges. Most agreed that mind reading has not yet been fully realized, whereas a few believed it was partially achieved or considered unfeasible.

It is important to note that, since a clear definition of mind reading has yet to be established, we did not attempt to create a unified standard and then search for evidence in the interview texts to determine whether mind reading had been achieved based on that standard. Even if an expert had a broad or loose definition of mind reading, we classified their opinion as an affirmation of its achievement, provided they believed it had been realized.

Partially achieved

Four experts (S1, S2, S9, and S15) believed that partial mind reading is currently achieved. However, they emphasized its limitations and did not claim that mind reading at the level of inner thoughts had been accomplished:

S1 noted that while some progress has been made, such as detecting brain diseases, determining what a person is thinking remains unattainable:

“Some aspects can be read, such as detecting epilepsy or depression. However, determining what a user is thinking about is still not possible. With tools such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), it might be feasible to some extent, but the results are inaccurate and incomplete.” (S1).

“Some aspects can be read, such as detecting epilepsy or depression. However, determining what a user is thinking about is still not possible. With tools such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), it might be feasible to some extent, but the results are inaccurate and incomplete.” (S1).

S2 argued that it is already possible to decode imagined images:

“So-called mind reading has been partially achieved. […] Access to situational knowledge, such as past experiences or familiarity with certain individuals, may only be a matter of time and engineering.” (S2).

“So-called mind reading has been partially achieved. […] Access to situational knowledge, such as past experiences or familiarity with certain individuals, may only be a matter of time and engineering.” (S2).

S9 suggested that simple forms of mind reading are achievable:

“Some relatively simple mind-reading effects can be achieved.” (S9).

“Some relatively simple mind-reading effects can be achieved.” (S9).

S15 stated that emotions and lies can be detected and identified:

“By matching a user’s brain activity with average characteristics, emotions can be detected and used to detect lies.” (S15).

“By matching a user’s brain activity with average characteristics, emotions can be detected and used to detect lies.” (S15).

Not yet achieved

Thirteen experts expressed that mind reading has not been achieved. Most of them interpretated mind reading as decoding “inner thoughts” and concluded that current technology remains far from this level. Two experts criticized the exaggerated claims in public discourse. S3 argued that true mind reading is still a distant goal:

“If brain activity could be decoded non-invasively, it would be unsettling. However, in reality, this is still hype; true mind reading is far off. […] EEG data cannot achieve this. Even with fMRI decoding, you might infer whether someone is thinking of an animal or a landscape, but it doesn’t truly access privacy.” (S3).

“If brain activity could be decoded non-invasively, it would be unsettling. However, in reality, this is still hype; true mind reading is far off. […] EEG data cannot achieve this. Even with fMRI decoding, you might infer whether someone is thinking of an animal or a landscape, but it doesn’t truly access privacy.” (S3).

S4 dismissed current claims of mind reading as fraudulent and suggested that, at best, indirect inference is possible:

“Theoretically, it’s possible, but it hasn’t been achieved. What is currently referred to as mind reading is all a scam. Currently, if someone does not share their thoughts, no one else can know them. At best, you can infer on the basis of scenarios or experience. […] In the future, it might be like measuring blood pressure with machines.” (S4).

“Theoretically, it’s possible, but it hasn’t been achieved. What is currently referred to as mind reading is all a scam. Currently, if someone does not share their thoughts, no one else can know them. At best, you can infer on the basis of scenarios or experience. […] In the future, it might be like measuring blood pressure with machines.” (S4).

Several experts highlighted technical and practical challenges. S7 noted the necessity of significant breakthroughs in neuroscience to enable mind reading:

“If BCIs could unravel how neural circuits regulate consciousness and cognition, then mind reading would be possible.” (S7).

“If BCIs could unravel how neural circuits regulate consciousness and cognition, then mind reading would be possible.” (S7).

S8 remarked that current applications do not constitute true mind reading and stressed the complexity of data interpretation:

“What’s currently called dream decoding is still in its infancy. It can show an image but isn’t mind reading. […] The data are incomprehensible to humans without machine processing.” (S8).

“What’s currently called dream decoding is still in its infancy. It can show an image but isn’t mind reading. […] The data are incomprehensible to humans without machine processing.” (S8).

S13 highlighted the need for multimodal approaches, as single methods are insufficient for mind reading:

“It’s unachievable at this stage. Evaluating positive or negative emotions typically requires multimodal techniques. EEG is just one component; you also need data from electromyograms (EMGs), galvanic skin responses, ECGs, eye movement signals, lip readings, and more. It’s not something that can be accurately assessed with a single method.” (S13).

“It’s unachievable at this stage. Evaluating positive or negative emotions typically requires multimodal techniques. EEG is just one component; you also need data from electromyograms (EMGs), galvanic skin responses, ECGs, eye movement signals, lip readings, and more. It’s not something that can be accurately assessed with a single method.” (S13).

When addressing this question, some experts also shared their expectations for the realization of mind reading. Two experts believed that mind reading could be achieved in the near future. E1 argued that the ability to distinguish different brainwave patterns has already been realized, suggesting rapid progress toward true mind reading:

“It could happen soon. Neuralink and Facebook are also working on mind reading. […] When you think of two different things, Facebook or Neuralink can already detect distinct brainwave patterns. If they are distinguishable, then mind reading can truly be achieved.” (E1).

“It could happen soon. Neuralink and Facebook are also working on mind reading. […] When you think of two different things, Facebook or Neuralink can already detect distinct brainwave patterns. If they are distinguishable, then mind reading can truly be achieved.” (E1).

S4 highlighted the rapid pace of technological advancements, which necessitates addressing associated ethical concerns:

“At this stage, BCI-related issues are not particularly prominent because brain control hasn’t yet been achieved. However, within our lifetime, it’s likely to happen—the technology is advancing incredibly fast. […] In the future, it could be as simple as using a machine to measure blood pressure—what you measure is what you get.” (S4).

“At this stage, BCI-related issues are not particularly prominent because brain control hasn’t yet been achieved. However, within our lifetime, it’s likely to happen—the technology is advancing incredibly fast. […] In the future, it could be as simple as using a machine to measure blood pressure—what you measure is what you get.” (S4).

In contrast, four experts (C1, E2, S3, and S6) considered mind reading to be far from realization. They emphasized various technical challenges. C1 noted the insufficient detail in current methods of extracting and decoding brain signals:

“The EEG signals we collect are too rough and do not meet the requirements for decoding, research, or practical use. We are still far from extracting true thoughts.” (C1).

“The EEG signals we collect are too rough and do not meet the requirements for decoding, research, or practical use. We are still far from extracting true thoughts.” (C1).

S3 and S6 highlighted the difficulty of acquiring high-quality signals, particularly through non-invasive (wearable) methods:

“Using non-invasive methods to decode the brain is still hype. It’s far from true mind reading.” (S3).

“Using non-invasive methods to decode the brain is still hype. It’s far from true mind reading.” (S3).

“It’s ultimately possible but not as quickly as some claim. By 2040 or 2045, this will not happen. On the basis of current research, wearable devices cannot achieve this because the signal quality is inadequate.” (S6).

“It’s ultimately possible but not as quickly as some claim. By 2040 or 2045, this will not happen. On the basis of current research, wearable devices cannot achieve this because the signal quality is inadequate.” (S6).

E2 identified challenges in data interpretation, asserting that current mathematical tools are insufficient for mind reading:

“EEG signals exhibit very typical nonstationary randomness. In the field of mathematical analysis of nonstationary random signals, even the latest techniques date back 60–70 years. […] Therefore, I can responsibly say there’s no need to worry about privacy issues right now.” (E2).

Fundamentally impossible

S5 went a step further, asserting that mind reading through brain reading is fundamentally impossible:

“It’s science fiction to think that detecting neural activity alone can reveal inner thoughts. The brain is a vast memory system, evolving in time and space, containing layers of past experiences. […] I don’t believe that mind reading can ever be achieved. Currently, the data we gather reflect a collective field potential and cannot access privacy or translate into anything meaningful.” (S5).

Most experts agreed that true mind reading is not achievable at present. However, their perspectives cannot be reduced to a binary “possible” or “impossible.”

First, S5 argued that relying solely on neural devices cannot achieve mind reading. Second, differences in how experts define mind reading complicate the issue. For example, S1 stated that it was possible to “determine epilepsy or depression” and suggested that with fMRI, one might infer a user’s thoughts. Meanwhile, S3, while acknowledging that fMRI could already distinguish between different user thoughts, asserted that “it’s not truly accessing privacy.” Despite similar evidence, S1 considered mind reading partially achievable, whereas S3 did not.

This disagreement about the feasibility of mind reading leads to varying opinions on when it might become possible. Experts offer significantly different projections regarding technological advancements and the overcoming of current challenges. While some experts, such as E1 and S4, pointed to the rapid pace of technological advancements and suggested that mind reading could be realized in the near future, others, such as S3 and S6, expressed skepticism, emphasizing the substantial technical challenges that remain, particularly concerning signal quality and non-invasive methods. This divergence underscores the uncertainty and variability in expert projections about the future of BMR.

Therefore, assessments should consider each expert’s definition of mind reading and the context of their statements. This emphasizes the need for clarity in discussions—whether “mind reading” refers to a full decoding of inner thoughts or more limited forms of mental inference. A more precise approach would involve situating expert opinions on a spectrum, with one end claiming that “complete mind reading has already been achieved” and the other asserting that “mind reading is fundamentally impossible.” This spectrum offers a more nuanced understanding of the range of expert perspectives, with most opinions falling somewhere in between these extremes.

Limitations of the BMR

Beyond the content of mind reading, technical processes involved play a crucial role in shaping judgments about BMR. By engaging experts in discussions about how mind reading experiments are conducted, we gain insights into the technological limitations of BCIs in this field. Several experts highlighted challenges specific to different BCI types. S3 pointed out issues with both invasive and non-invasive approaches, noting that while non-invasive BCIs are currently lack efficiency, invasive devices face challenges related to biocompatibility and durability:

“Current non-invasive BCIs are highly inefficient due to various factors such as temporal and spatial resolution, requiring extensive training over a long period. If environmental noise increases or the context changes, retraining is necessary, which limits their potential for development. As for invasive BCIs, they require the implantation of numerous electrodes into the cortex and multiple openings in the skull. Their effective lifespan is typically only two to three years, which means repeated drilling into the skull would be necessary.” (S3).

This approach significantly diverges from the “mind reading” described in science fiction, which bypasses consciousness entirely.

Discussion

Characteristics of BMR

BCIs involve a series of complex processes, which are typically categorized into three main stages: signal acquisition, signal processing, and application. Signal acquisition captures physiological signals that correspond to specific brain activities, such as motor, linguistic, auditory, and visual functions. The most commonly used method is EEG. Signal processing can be further divided into three subphases: feature extraction, feature classification, and feature translation. These steps involve sophisticated data processing, ultimately linking brain activity to external device operations for practical applications [1]. A detailed analysis of each stage of the BCI process reveals numerous limitations in applying this technology to mind reading.

Wexler offers an illustrative analogy for BMR experiments. Suppose that researchers instruct a participant to wear an eye tracker while displaying numbers representing calendar months on a computer screen. The participant is then asked to focus on their birth month. By analyzing eye-tracking data, researchers deduce the participant’s birth month [29]. Clearly, this conclusion relies on background information—specifically, the content displayed on the screen. This background information acts as an intermediary between the experimental data and the conclusion. Similarly, claims about decoding private information through BCIs often disregard the critical role of background information [29].

However, this kind of background information may not always be apparent. As S12 noted, training BCI algorithms requires long-term tracking of individuals to establish a “baseline for their neural activity,” which inherently incorporates significant amounts of personal background information. Research indicates that BCIs are not devices capable of reading thoughts or directly translating arbitrary cognitive activity. Instead, they detect “predefined and well-characterized brain activity patterns” and rely heavily on supervised machine learning and pattern recognition methods [43].

Beyond questioning the processes involved in BMR, some research denies the feasibility of BMR from philosophical or neuroscientific perspectives. Gilead outlines three key points: (1) our body and behavior do not allow any epistemic access to the mind; (2) individual brain structures and thinking patterns vary, making it impossible to establish universal laws governing the brain-mind bridge; and (3) psychophysical unity or inseparability does not entail psychophysical identity or reductionism [23]. Slaby and Choudhury argue that the brain is a dynamic, plastic entity. From birth, or even earlier, neural connections evolve continuously. Thus, “(a neural device) needs to have been coupled up to my brain and body from conception—or at least from birth, to be able to record my entire neural and hormonal life history. Then, and only then, might it be possible for it to decode the neural information” [44].

Chinese experts have also emphasized the technical challenges of BMR. For example, S2 noted that images decoded from BMR experiments are selected from a predefined list and represented as matching data patterns. However, research indicates that under uncontrolled conditions, these devices cannot reconstruct an individual’s specific visual experiences [45]. S3 highlighted the poor quality of data obtained from non-invasive BMR, making it difficult to extract meaningful information, whereas invasive BMR raises concerns such as physical harm and long-term compatibility. S12 stated that analyzing user preferences requires long-term tracking and large-scale data collection, which is typically user-specific and limited in validity. Therefore, S12 argued that such practices do not equate to true mind reading. S15, while acknowledging the possibility of BMR, emphasized that analyzing user preferences often relies more heavily on web browsing data and behavioral patterns than on EEG data.

Some experts distinguished between different operational principles of BMR. S11 explained that current mind-reading technologies do not achieve “think it and it happens” capabilities but require users to perform specific actions. For example, to control a wheelchair to move right, the user must imagine moving their right hand—this is considered active BCIs. In BCI typing systems, users must focus on a flashing block, with the system recognizing the brainwave frequency triggered by the flashing rather than directly decoding intended letters. These systems are categorized as reactive BCIs. These devices cannot extract meaningful information without user cooperation. Claims of mind reading in such experiments are typically achieved under specific, additional conditions.

Most experts agreed that while mind reading is not currently achievable, it is theoretically possible because different mental states generate distinct brain data. As long as these differences exist, BMR remains a possibility. However, the differentiation of brain data only implies the ability to distinguish different thoughts, not to decode them. S5 argued that the brain evolves over time and space, encompassing a wealth of past experiences. This spatiotemporal complexity led him to describe mind reading through neuronal activity alone as “science fiction,” aligning with Slaby and Choudhury’s perspective.

On the basis of the above discussion, the limitations of BMR can be summarized as follows: (1) signal quality issues: BMR suffers from insufficient signal quality, and improving this quality often requires more invasive devices; (2) dependence on background information: BMR relies on contextual information, which can stem from experimental design, data collection, or algorithm training; (3) low transferability: background information and algorithm models have limited applicability across individuals; (4) user cooperation: both active and reactive BMR require user collaboration, making silent privacy breaches impossible; and (5) technological limitations: some argue that mind reading cannot be achieved solely through neural devices.

Distinctiveness of BMR

While debates surrounding BMR are intensifying, the concept of mind reading predates the advent of neurotechnology. Both natural and digital forms of mind reading already exist, demonstrating that BMR is merely one subset of a broader phenomenon. Therefore, discussions about BMR as a potential violation of mental privacy must assume that BMR has unique attributes that are absent in other forms. Without such an assumption, these discussions risk encompassing, and potentially restricting, everyday forms of communication. More specifically, “a coherent account of such a right (mental privacy) should not turn behavior which we regard as morally unproblematic into right violations” [24]. The critical question is as follows: does BMR hold any ethical distinctions that differentiate it from natural and digital mind reading?

Brown categorizes claims about neurotechnologies infringing on mental privacy into several aspects: (1) directness: neurotechnologies directly access thoughts, distinguishing them from the indirect modes of natural and digital mind reading; (2) sensitivity: compared with other personal information, brain data are more private, precise, or reveal more psychological details; (3) control: neurotechnologies might bypass consciousness to directly observe mental states, making it difficult for individuals to prevent the disclosure of thoughts, emotions, and tendencies [22]. In addition to directness and control, Ryberg discusses two other possibilities: (1) reliability: neurotechnologies might access mental privacy more reliably than natural mind reading; (2) amount of information: neurotechnologies might be able to obtain more information than natural mind reading [24]. However, both articles ultimately refute all these claims, demonstrating that BMR lacks distinctiveness.

Empirical insights from interviews with Chinese experts further validate these conclusions. In addition to the obvious limitation of requiring physical wear, current BCI devices demand that users perform specific actions, engage in mental imagery, and undergo long-term dataset training. Any significant insights derived from neurotechnologies typically rely on complex systems of digital networks, programs, and auxiliary devices [22]. This intricate workflow effectively refutes claims that BCIs can directly access thoughts.

The claims of reliability and amount of information are also challenged by expert opinions. Brain data inherently contain uncertainty and significant noise, leading to low discernibility. S12 highlighted that the volume of information obtained by BCIs is vastly smaller than the total potential output of the brain. Similarly, C1, E2, and S6 argued that the quality of the signals captured to date is insufficient for decoding private information. For example, in identifying user preferences, the performance of BCIs often lags behind that of widely used smartphone applications. In emotional detection, brain signals are merely one of many data types considered. As S15 stated, “EEG is not the main tool for this purpose (mind reading).” Thus, current BCI devices lack significant advantages in terms of reliability or amount of information.

From a technical perspective, claims of control are also flawed. Active and reactive BCIs require users to either actively imagine or respond to specific stimuli, allowing individuals to simply refuse to cooperate to disrupt the BCIs’ function. Consequently, BMR cannot currently bypass human consciousness or compromise users’ control over their mental privacy. However, passive BCIs present a different scenario. S4 suggested that future BMR technologies might function like a “blood pressure monitor,” directly revealing a user’s thoughts. While most experts agreed that current technology falls far short of such capabilities, they acknowledged the possibility of achieving this in the future.

Among the various claims suggesting the uniqueness of BMR, the argument based on control is the most compelling. With advancements in technology, passive BCIs may eventually achieve mind reading that bypasses consciousness. Nevertheless, given the current state of neurotechnology, BMR does not exhibit unique characteristics and has yet to raise distinct ethical concerns.

Necessary conditions for achieving strong BMR

While current BMR lacks ethical distinctiveness, challenges related to control and privacy are likely to emerge as technology progresses. To better understand the future ethical implications of BMR, it is essential to consider not only its current limitations but also its potential evolution. Similar to the distinction between “strong AI” and “weak AI” in AI ethics, we propose categorizing BMR into “strong BMR” and “weak BMR.” Brown has previously suggested distinguishing between strong and weak forms of mind reading to alleviate public anxiety and unrealistic expectations about neurotechnology, although his analysis was not exhaustive [22]. This study builds on Brown’s suggestion by proposing a framework that addresses the technical requirements for achieving strong BMR. The goal is not to define BMR per se, but to create a framework for discussions on mental privacy and the ethical governance of BCIs, helping to identify technologies that may pose distinctive ethical challenges.

According to most experts, current BMR is limited to decoding simpler aspects of mental states, such as emotions or basic cognitive states. Experts like S3 and E1 acknowledged that while progress has been made in decoding mental states, true mind reading—especially decoding complex cognitive states or inner thoughts—remains speculative. However, many of the experts interviewed predicted that BCIs will eventually be able to decode more complex mental states, possibly including aspects of inner thoughts. As S16 suggested, if BCIs advance to the point where they can bypass conscious control, privacy concerns will become more pressing. This would mark a crucial distinction between strong and weak BMR.

Strong BMR represents a significant departure from both natural and digital mind reading, as it has the potential to bypass user control. This shift would transform the ethical concerns from questions of feasibility to issues of privacy, as strong BMR could access mental states without the individual’s awareness or consent. Passive BCIs, which can operate without the user’s knowledge or cooperation, are considered the most plausible pathway to achieving strong BMR. However, as BCIs advance, drawing a clear line between permissible and impermissible applications may become increasingly difficult. BCIs can serve multiple functions simultaneously, meaning users may be misled into thinking that brain data is being collected for one specific purpose, while in reality, it could reveal multiple private mental states [46].

Strictly speaking, users of active or reactive BCIs retain significant control over their thoughts, as they can withhold or manipulate the responses expected by the device, thereby preserving mental privacy. However, this does not mean that these devices are free from ethical concerns. For example, if users are coerced into using reactive BCIs, such as in an interrogation employing a P300-ERP-based lie detector, they might struggle to suppress involuntary reactive signals when exposed to specific stimuli [47]. Thus, while reactive BCIs do not qualify as strong BMR devices, they may still pose greater privacy risks than active BCIs under coercive conditions.

The realization of strong BMR requires more than just advanced BCI technology—it also depends on the integration of contextual and background information. If a so-called “BMR device” relies primarily on natural or digital mind-reading techniques, its ethical issues would fall outside the scope of BMR. Some argue that true BMR must operate entirely on the basis of brain data, independent of external background information [25]. While this standard may be overly stringent, at a minimum, BCI devices in practical use must focus on analyzing the brain data they collect. For example, as S15 noted, when a user’s preferences are analyzed, EEG data are combined with web browsing history and preference data. Such techniques fail to meet this condition. A very similar example is the “cold reading” technique used in fortune-telling fraud. In this case, a skilled “fortune teller” can quickly gather a large amount of information by analyzing the other person’s nonverbal cues—such as body language, age, clothing, mannerisms, and speech—creating the illusion that they are reading the other person’s mind.

Decoding brain data is inherently algorithm dependent, particularly for AI models [8], which require extensive datasets for training. This process often involves integrating background information, raising privacy concerns. We argue that BCI algorithms need to demonstrate transferability, meaning that algorithms trained on data from one individual or group remain applicable to others. As long as the process of collecting background information is unrelated to the target of BMR, concerns about mental privacy remain valid. There is a philosophical assumption that individuals or brains possess irreducible uniqueness. Since each brain’s physical composition is unique, there is no guarantee that signals from one brain’s image correspond to the same meanings in another brain. Hence, the ways in which brains and minds operate may well be one-of-a-kind [23]. Transferable algorithms might provide an effective rebuttal to this assumption.

The type of information decoded by strong BMR is another critical consideration, as it may differ fundamentally from that of weak BMR. For example, detecting whether a user is fatigued versus extracting their payment password clearly raises different ethical concerns. The former, in fact, is one of the key applications of current passive BCIs [48]. This distinction often shifts attention to the content of the information being read. Chinese experts described mind reading with terms such as inner thoughts, inner speech, imagined images, decisions, and emotions. However, these terms are inherently ambiguous. For example, the concept of “emotion” has been debated since Aristotle’s time and remains unresolved [49]. More importantly, the choice of such definitions is arbitrary. From detecting fatigue to uncovering sexual orientation, political preferences, or deeply personal memories never shared with others, it is difficult to identify a clear philosophical or ethical boundary. While such a boundary might be drawn in practical or legal contexts, this does not imply that it holds ethical significance.

From the above discussion, we propose the following necessary conditions for strong BMR: (1) brain data must be collected via passive BCI devices; (2) the analysis of data must focus on brain data; and (3) the algorithm used must exhibit universality, allowing for transferability across individuals or groups. The specific content of information decoded by the BCI does not determine the distinction between strong and weak BMR. However, on the basis of expert interviews, one particularly sensitive and ethically concerning category of mind reading content is inner thoughts.

Limitations and implications for future research

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size may not fully capture the perspectives of all relevant experts, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Future research should include a larger, more diverse sample to enhance data representativeness. Second, the study relies on qualitative interviews without quantitative data. Including questionnaires and accompanying statistical comparisons could offer a broader view of trends and expert consensus, thereby strengthening the validation of the results. Potential comparisons could examine differences in perspectives across disciplinary backgrounds (e.g., philosophers vs. neuroscientists, scientists vs. clinicians) or years of professional experience. Statistical analysis could further assess how these factors shape experts’ views on the feasibility of mind-reading technologies—whether they consider it partially achieved, not yet achieved, or fundamentally impossible. Third, the focus on general BCI ethics rather than mind-reading technologies limits insight into experts’ views on the specific ethical implications of BMR. Future studies should address mind-reading technologies directly to explore both technical feasibility and ethical concerns in greater detail. Finally, this study provides descriptive insights into BCI technology, but questions regarding new rights, such as mental privacy, require normative evaluation. Future work should encourage interdisciplinary collaboration between BCI experts, ethicists, policymakers, and legal scholars to bridge the gap between technical knowledge with ethical frameworks, ensuring that descriptive insights are effectively integrated with normative considerations.

Despite these limitations, this study highlights the importance of neuroethics research involving expert interviews to deepen our understanding of technical advancements and their ethical dimensions. For example, a recent interpretive content analysis on neurotechnological mind reading revealed that 46% of the included studies suggested that mind reading is already feasible [16]. This finding contrasts sharply with the conclusion of our study. While methodological differences preclude direct comparison, the discrepancy underscores the need for cross-cultural and interdisciplinary research. Cultural variations in the definition and interpretation of mind reading, as well as differences in ethical awareness and acceptance of neurotechnological devices, may significantly shape perspectives on this topic. Therefore, global ethical discussions—particularly those concerning the protection of neurorights—must account for these cultural nuances. Respecting and integrating these differences will help foster an international consensus that balances technological progress with the safeguarding of individual rights.

Conclusion

Our findings reveal a lack of consensus among experts on the interpretations of mind reading. While some view it as achievable, others remain skeptical. This divergence highlights the challenge of defining mind reading within the context of BMR, a debate that remains unresolved in the literature. The study also finds that current BMR technologies are not yet advanced enough to justify the establishment of a distinct right to mental privacy. There is considerable uncertainty about the feasibility of BMR, with experts expressing differing opinions on when, or if, it will be achieved. Understanding the technical limitations of BMR is crucial for any ethical evaluation.

Furthermore, this study examines whether BMR presents unique ethical challenges compared to traditional mind reading methods. Our findings indicate that different types of BCIs—active, reactive, and passive—pose distinct ethical risks. Notably, passive BCIs carry risks that are significantly different from those of active or reactive systems, a distinction with important ethical implications. Based on these insights, we recommend distinguishing between strong and weak BMR and propose a framework for defining strong BMR. This distinction will lay the groundwork for future ethical analysis, regulatory discussions, and governance structures that align with technological realities while safeguarding human rights.
