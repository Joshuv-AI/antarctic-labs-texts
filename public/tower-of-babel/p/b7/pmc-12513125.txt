The combined prognostic effect of the triglyceride-glucose index and estimated glucose disposal rate on long-term mortality among individuals with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome stages 0–3
Authors: Kexin Wang, Wenjie Wang, Jialong Niu, Yixuan Liu, Zhongke Wang, Zhijian Wang, Hongya Han, Yan Sun, Xiaoli Liu, Hailong Ge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have demonstrated that the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index in combination with the estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) could predict mortality risks in the normal population. Our studies have focused on their additive effects on patients with cardiovascular-kidney metabolic syndrome (CKM) syndrome stages 0–3.MethodsParticipants were sourced from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 1999 to 2018. The restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves modeled the associations between the TyG index, eGDR, and mortality. Cox proportional regression models, Kaplan–Meier (KM) curve and subgroup analysis were performed to investigate the combined association. Machine learning was used in the development of predictive models.ResultsA total of 11,206 participants with CKM syndrome stages 0–3 were involved. The median follow-up time was 104 days, in which 1,079 cases of all-cause death and 299 cases of cardiovascular death were recorded. The RCS curve proved that the associations of the TyG index and eGDR with mortality followed a J-shape and an L-shape, respectively. Compared with the low TyG/high eGDR group, the all-cause mortality rate in TyG ≥ 8.72 and eGDR < 10.8 group was 14.9%, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 3.338 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.448–7.696), while the cardiovascular mortality rate was 4.8%, with an HR of 1.786 (95% CI: 1.023–3.119).ConclusionsIn individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3, it is more advisable to integrate the TyG index and eGDR when assessing insulin resistance and predicting the risks of long-term mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05161-1.Keywords: Triglyceride-glucose index, Estimated glucose disposal rate, Insulin resistance, Cardiovascular-renal-metabolic syndrome, Machine learning

Introduction

It is well-recognized that overweight/obesity often serves as a starting point, with hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia frequently co-existing and interacting to drive atherosclerosis and cardiovascular diseases (CVD) [1]. Recently, the American Heart Association (AHA) released a presidential recommendation introducing the concept of cardiovascular-kidney metabolic syndrome (CKM) [2], which is a systemic health disorder including pathophysiological interactions among obesity, diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and CVD (including heart failure, atrial fibrillation, coronary heart disease, stroke, and peripheral artery disease). Between 2015 and 2020, over 25% of Americans were diagnosed with CKM syndrome [3]. Moreover, more than 75% of healthcare expenditures were allocated to treating the related conditions [4]. Increasing epidemiological data shows that as CKM advances from stage 0 to stage 4, the risks of both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality rise sharply [5]. Therefore, the AHA recommends that management strategies for CKM syndrome stages 0–3 ought to focus on the prevention of cardiovascular events [2].

In the state of overweight/obesity, insulin sensitivity decreases as insulin resistance (IR) markedly intensifies [6]. This progression plays a crucial role in the development of coronary microcirculation disorder and a decline in coronary blood flow reserve [7, 8]. The gold standard for detecting IR is the glucose disposal rate derived from the hyperinsulinemia-euglycemic clamp test [9]. However, this test is invasive and costly, thereby impeding its widespread use in routine clinical settings. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has been established as a non-invasive and straightforward marker of IR [10]. Research has shown that the TyG index is correlated with the incidence of cardiovascular diseases and their prognoses [11]. However, even after adjusting for traditional CVD risk factors, some patients with elevated TyG index levels still face a high risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes. This indicates that only a single indicator to assess IR status is insufficient. Previous studies have demonstrated that the estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) in combination with the TyG index could predict mortality risks more effectively [12]. And few studies have focused on patients with CKM syndrome stages 0–3 and their prognoses. Therefore, the objective of our study is to explore the combined effects of the TyG index and eGDR on long-term mortality in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. And the research is grounded in a large-scale, nationwide prospective cohort in America.

Method

Study design and population

Our research participants were sourced from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), which is carried out by the National Center for Health Statistics of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. This survey is dedicated to examining the health, nutrition, and health behaviors of the civilian non-institutionalized population in the United States. Our study used the datasets from 9 cycles of the NHANES from 1999 to 2018, which was available at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm. A total of 33,321 patients were excluded due to being younger than 20 years old. Additionally, the following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) a diagnosis of cancer (n = 4,506); (2) severe renal or hepatic insufficiency (n = 1,305); (3) pregnant women (n = 1,006); (4) leukopenia or Thrombocytopenia (n = 478); (5) missing data necessary for calculating TyG index or eGDR (n = 27,007); (6) ineligible for CKM syndrome stages assessment (n = 19,069); (7) CKM syndrome stage 4 (n = 3,405); (8) ineligible follow-up data (n = 13). Ultimately, 11,206 participants with CKM syndrome stages 0–3 were selected for the analysis. The detailed selection process is presented in the flowchart shown in Fig. 1.

Diagnostic criteria

In accordance with the most recent guideline issued by the AHA in 2023 [2], CKM has been categorized into four stages as follows: stage 0: individuals with no evidence of CKD, subclinical CVD, or clinical CVD; stage 1: individuals with obesity or pre-diabetes; stage 2: individuals with metabolic risk factors (hypertriglyceridemia [≥ 135 mg/dL], hypertension, diabetes), or CKD; stage 3: subclinical atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) or subclinical heart failure; stage 4: individuals with CKD who have developed clinical CVD, which includes coronary heart disease, heart failure (HF), stroke, peripheral artery disease, and atrial fibrillation. In addition, hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg, previously diagnosed hypertension or self-report, and patients who are taking any antihypertensive drugs. Furthermore, alcohol consumption was categorized by whether ≥ 4 drinks/day, and smoking was classified by whether ≥ 100 cigarettes in their lifetime.

Data collection and endpoints

Demographic and lifestyle variables were acquired through a standardized questionnaire during the home interview: age, gender, race, education, marital status, family poverty-to-income ratio, smoking and drinking status, medical treatment history, and disease status (history of coronary heart disease, diabetes, hypertension). In terms of anthropometric measurements, parameters such as body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and systolic blood pressure (SBP) were carefully recorded. As an integral part of the laboratory assessment, a diverse range of blood chemistry indicators was also collected, which included total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), etc. Meanwhile, the TyG index was calculated according to the formula: [ln(fasting TG [mg/dL]×FBG [mg/dL]/2) [13]. The eGDR was determined using the following formula: 21.158 - (0.09 × WC [cm])− (3.407 × hypertension [yes 1 or no 0]) − (0.551 ×HbA1c [%]) [14]. And the baseline eGFR was calculated as: 175×SCr (mg/dl) − 1.234×Age − 0.179 (×0.79 for women) [15].

The causes of death were ascertained based on the codes from the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10). And the endpoints of our study were all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality (ICD-10 codes I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I51, and I60-I69).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as the median and interquartile range (Q25, Q75), while categorical variables were depicted as numbers (percentages). The Kruskal–Wallis test was utilized to compare continuous variables, and the χ² test or Fisher’s exact probability test was used for categorical variables. The restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves modeled the associations between the TyG index, eGDR, and mortality, respectively. In addition, we calculated the median of the TyG index (8.72) and eGDR (10.8 mg/kg/min) to categorize all participants into four groups according to higher or lower than the value. Then we used the Cox proportional regression models to investigate the association between the TyG index, eGDR and mortality (model 1 adjusted for age, gender, race, and body mass index; model 2 adjusted for model 1 plus smoking status, alcohol consumption, educational level, family poverty-income ratio, antidiabetic agents, antihypertensive agents, antihyperlipidemic agents, eGFR, HDL-C, and TG). Kaplan–Meier (KM) curve was performed to examine the associations of insulin resistance indices and any all-cause or cardiovascular death. We also conducted the subgroup analysis to explore the relationship between the TyG index, eGDR and mortality across various levels of age, renal function, and diabetes status.

Furthermore, in order to confirm the prognostic prediction model, the data were randomly divided into two subsets: a training set and a test set according to a ratio of 7:3. Before applying machine learning (ML) algorithms, the Boruta algorithm was utilized to identify key features. To refine the variable-selection process, we further employed the variance inflation factor (VIF) to evaluate multicollinearity among variables. Once variable selection was completed, the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) was adopted to gauge the discriminatory power of the model. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was then used to assess the clinical validity of the model.

All statistical analyses were performed with R software version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Chicago, IL). All statistical tests were two-sided, and P values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of all participants

A total of 11,206 participants with CKM syndrome stages 0–3 were involved in our research and were divided into 4 groups. The baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. As the results showed, 5,865 (52.3%) were male with a middle age of 49 (36,62) years and BMI of 28.3 (24.7,32.5) kg/m2, 17.8% (1,995) and 45.9% (5,145) of whom had diabetes and hypertension, respectively. Most of the participants presented CKM syndrome stage 2 (49.2%), while a small subset of patients had CKM syndrome stage 0 (4.8%), 1 (17.6%), and 3 (28.4%). The median follow-up time was 104 days. The baseline characteristics were also compared across the four groups according to low or high categories for the TyG index and eGDR. It showed that patients with a higher TyG index and lower eGDR were more likely to have a higher BMI, FBG and HbA1c, as well as higher rates of diabetes.

In our study, 1,079 cases of all-cause death and 299 cases of cardiovascular death were recorded. The association between the TyG index, eGDR and mortality

Based on the RCS curve, the association between the TyG index and mortality exhibited a J-shape in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. As the findings indicated, all-cause and cardiovascular mortality increased significantly when the TyG index exceeded 9.93 and 10.15, respectively (Fig. 2). However, the results in patients with CKM syndrome stage 3 were not statistically significant (Fig. 2C and F). Furthermore, the relationship between eGDR and mortality followed an L-shape in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3 (Fig. 3). It was demonstrated that when eGDR dropped below 6.64, both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality increased substantially. We also used the multivariate Cox analysis to assess the association between the TyG index, eGDR and mortality, respectively. The results were shown in Supplementary Table 1. And the p for interaction of TyG* eGDR in both group were less than 0.001.

The combined effects of the TyG index and eGDR on mortality

When compared to the low TyG/high eGDR group, CKM syndrome stages 0–3 patients with a high TyG/low eGDR (TyG ≥ 8.72 and eGDR < 10.8) faced the greatest risks of both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality. The all-cause mortality rates were 14.9% and 3.9% respectively, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 3.338 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.448–7.696), while the cardiovascular mortality rates were 4.8% and 0.7%, with an HR of 1.786 (95% CI: 1.023–3.119) (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 2). Similar results were also observed in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–2 (HR = 4.630, 95% CI 1.563–13.720) and stage 3 (HR = 2.400, 95% CI 1.043–5.522) in all-cause mortality. Furthermore, in the low TyG/low eGDR (TyG < 8.72 and eGDR < 10.8) group, patients with CKM syndrome stages 0–3 and stages 0–2 had higher risks of all-cause mortality (mortality rate: 9.1% vs. 3.9%; HR = 2.944, 95% CI 1.037–8.362 and mortality rate: 15.0% vs. 5.7%; HR = 3.502, 95% CI 1.008–12.169) after adjustment.

Subgroup analysis

In our study, 1,995 (17.8%) patients were diagnosed with diabetes, and 5,275 (47.1%) patients had pre-diabetes status. Consequently, we conducted a subgroup analysis to investigate the additive effects of the TyG index and eGDR on mortality within different diabetes status categories. The results are presented in Table 3. Among patients diagnosed with diabetes, the high TyG index with low eGDR was associated with an elevated all-cause mortality rate (HR 3.107, 95%CI 1.058–9.123). And among patients with pre-diabetes status, both the all-cause mortality (HR 2.207, 95%CI 1.504–3.425) and cardiovascular mortality (HR 2.399, 95%CI 1.002–5.746) rates increased significantly.

Furthermore, in individuals of different ages and renal function ranges, the combined effect of the TyG index and eGDR on both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality was also analyzed. In the 45 < age ≤ 60 years and age ≤ 45 years group, after adjusting for confounders, the high TyG index in combination with low eGDR was consistently associated with an elevated risk of all-cause death (HR 2.085, 95%CI 1.175-3.700 and HR 2.737, 95%CI 1.006–5.599) (Supplementary Table 3). A similar result of all-cause mortality rate was also found in 90 > eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min per 1.73 m2 group (HR 2.336, 95%CI 1.024–5.329) (Supplementary Table 4). Moreover, among patients with normal renal function (eGFR ≥ 90 ml/min/1.73 m2), the group characterized by a high TyG index and a low eGDR exhibited increased risks of cardiovascular death (HR 2.969, 95%CI 1.087–6.687).

Machine learning and the development of predictive models

Supplementary Fig. 2 shows the results of Boruta algorithms. Variables identified in the green Area, including TyG and eGDR, were confirmed as essential factors in the model. The variables selected in model were as followed: age, race, gender, smoke, diabetes, BMI, FBG, HbA1c, TyG, eGDR, TC and eGFR. And there was no multicollinearity among these predictor variables with all VIF values < 10 (Supplementary Table 5).

The training set constituted 70% (n = 7844) of the overall sample, while the test set represented 30% (n = 3362). Baseline characteristics are detailed in Supplementary Table 6. Regarding the prediction validity of the model for all-cause mortality, the AUC in the training set was 0.852 (95% CI: 0.825–0.880). In the validation set, the AUC value was 0.844 (95% CI: 0.800-0.888) (Figure 5A). For cardiovascular mortality, the predictive model demonstrated an AUC of 0.833 (95% CI: 0.840–0.925) in the training set, and was 0.845 (95% CI: 0.788–0.902) in the validation set (Figure 5B). These results indicate that the nomogram has great discriminatory ability and predictive value to identify the high risks of mortality correctly. The results of DCA method is displayed in Fig. 6. Within the internal validation set, the net benefits of the predictive model far surpassed those of the two extreme assumptions. This finding indicated that the nomogram model held remarkable advantages in terms of both net benefits and predictive accuracy, making it a reliable and effective tool in the relevant context.

Discussion

Main findings

In this population-based multicenter study, we explored the additive impacts of the TyG index and eGDR on long-term mortality in individuals with CKM syndrome at stages 0–3. Significantly, according to the nonlinear correlations, both the TyG index and eGDR were independently associated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among those with CKM syndrome at stages 0–3. Moreover, we detected a potential additive effect of the TyG index and eGDR on long-term mortality. The combination of TyG ≥ 8.72 and eGDR < 10.8 was capable of effectively identifying patients at the greatest risk of mortality. Finally, following the adjustment for traditional risk factors, the potential additive effect mentioned above was most pronounced in pre-diabetes patients, patients under 60 years old, and patients with normal or minimally impaired renal function (eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min per 1.73 m2).

The TyG index, eGDR and mortality

Multiple studies have shown that the TyG index and TyG-related indices serve as independent risk factors for all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality among patients with or without diabetes. As a marker of vascular endothelial inflammation and functional disorder [16, 17], the TyG index has a strong correlation with the risk of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular diseases [18]. Our previous research has shown the efficiency of the TyG index in predicting major adverse cardiac events (MACEs) in hospitalized acute coronary syndrome (ACS) patients [19]. Compared with Quartile 1 (TyG ≤ 9.161), Quartile 4 (TyG > 10.013) had the highest risks of developing MACEs (HR 1.604, 95%CI 1.437–1.791). The result was even more pronounced in patients with mild to moderate renal insufficiency, suggesting a cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic synergy in the development of malignant cardiovascular outcomes. Moreover, in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3, a study from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) showed that TyG combined with WC (TyG-WC) or waist-to-height ratio (TyG-WHtR) were independent predictors of CVD incidence [20].

As a novel indicator for insulin resistance, eGDR integrates HbA1c and WC, which could reflect both abdominal obesity and a history of hyperglycemia. Tian et al. found that participants with eGDR levels ≥ 11.16 mg/kg/min had a 29% lower risk of new-onset stroke [21]. And eGDR is also correlated with decreased risks of CVD in the general population or non-diabetic adults with CKD [22, 23]. In addition, a meta-analysis of five studies, which included 19,960 individuals, demonstrated that in type 1 diabetes patients, the elevated eGDR was associated with the decreased incidence of CVD and all-cause mortality [24]. Some studies have also used the combination of eGDR and the TyG index to guide the clinical risk assessment. He et al. have proved that TyG > 9.04 and eGDR < 4 could effectively identify individuals at the highest risk of mortality in the diabetic population. However, little studies have concentrated on the combination of eGDR and the TyG index in CKM syndrome patients [12].

Therefore, we carried out this research specifically within the CKM syndrome population. The RCS curve proved that the associations of the TyG index and eGDR with mortality followed a J-shape and an L-shape, respectively. Moreover, we utilized the median values of the TyG index and eGDR as the basis for grouping. This method enables a more accurate reflection of the IR status in the majority of individuals. As the results showed, some patients with a high TyG index level did not show significant elevated risks of mortality. So it was necessary to combine multiple IR indicators to assess mortality risks in individuals with CKM syndrome 0–3. The combination of TyG ≥ 8.72 and eGDR < 10.8 was capable of effectively identifying patients at the greatest risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Furthermore, the relationship was more pronounced in young patients with a pre-diabetes status and normal or minimally impaired renal function (eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min per 1.73 m²). This could potentially be attributed to heightened oxidative stress response and activation of chronic inflammation, which in turn exacerbate insulin resistance in CKD patients [25, 26].

Mechanisms and implications

Numerous clinical and basic research studies have demonstrated that hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and overweight/obesity are traditional risk factors for both CVD and CKD [3, 27]. From the pathophysiological perspective, studies have established that IR can trigger abnormal fat cell function [28]. Subsequently, the systemic inflammatory condition gives rise to disruptions in cardiac mitochondrial calcium homeostasis, oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and autophagy/mitochondrial damage [29, 30]. These cumulative effects ultimately contribute to myocardial fibrosis, which in turn lead to the development of coronary microvascular disease and a decline in coronary blood flow reserve [31]. Moreover, systemic inflammation could aggravate insulin resistance status, resulting in hyperglycemia, blood glucose level fluctuations, and further promoting cardiac and renal dysfunction [32, 33]. Advanced glycation end products (AGEs) activate signaling pathways via their receptors, thereby further promoting oxidative stress, inflammation, and fibrosis [34, 35]. This leads to endothelial dysfunction and serves as a driving force behind the microvascular and macrovascular complications associated with diabetes mellitus [36]. Additionally, hyperglycemia, by activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), causes vasoconstriction and further induces cardiac and renal fibrosis [37, 38].

Thus, the most important treatment concept for CKM is the management of cardiometabolic and renal comorbidity. Hypoglycemic drugs with beneficial effects on the heart and kidneys should be selected at an early stage [2, 39]. This approach aims to comprehensively benefit the cardiovascular system, renal system, and metabolism, thereby enabling more effective prevention and treatment of arteriosclerotic cardiovascular disease and CKD. For example, results from the Canagliflozin and Renal Events in Diabetes With Established Nephropathy Clinical Evaluation trial (CREDENCE) showed that canagliflozin significantly reduced the risk of both renal composite endpoints (HR: 0.70; P < 0.001) and cardiovascular death or hospitalization for heart failure (HR: 0.69; P < 0.001) [40]. A similar result was also obtained in the EMPA-Kidney trial, illustrating that empagliflozin improved renal and cardiovascular outcomes in patients with CKD with or without diabetes [41]. Based on the above results, clinical guidelines unanimously recommend that sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors be selected for the treatment of CVD patients with CKD, regardless of whether they have diabetes mellitus. In addition, some trials also recommended the use of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RA) and the nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (ns-MRA), which could also gain cardiovascular and kidney outcome benefits in patients with CKM syndrome [42, 43].

Strengths and limitations

The merits of this research have been distinctly presented. As a large multicenter cohort study, our research is the first to investigate the combined prognostic effect of the TyG index and eGDR on long-term mortality in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3, which was rarely studied before. And we used machine learning to confirm our findings. In addition, the study was based on a large population including the individuals from 1999 to 2018 in America, which made our results more reliable and solid. Furthermore, we also conducted a subgroup analysis to reconfirm the association in different CKM syndrome stages, age, diabetes status, and renal function.

Certainly, the study also has some limitations. Firstly, as the NHANES is an observational cohort, it is unable to control for biases in patient selection. Therefore, in the future, more randomized controlled trials are required to be carried out. Secondly, the mortality records in the database account for only a small proportion of the total number of participants. Hence, the follow-up period should be lengthened to record more outcome events. Finally, given that the NHANES solely represents the United States population, additional multicenter studies should be conducted to strengthen the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

In individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3, it is more advisable to integrate the TyG index and eGDR when assessing insulin resistance status and predicting the risks of long-term mortality. Young patients with pre-diabetes status and normal or minimally impaired renal functions ought to have more stringent blood glucose control. Hypoglycemic drugs with beneficial effects on the heart and kidneys should be selected at an early stage.
