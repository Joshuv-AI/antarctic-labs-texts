Individual differences in anthropomorphism help explain social connection to AI companions
Authors: Dunigan Folk, Steven J Heine, Elizabeth Dunn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeople increasingly use conversational AI for support and companionship. Yet, current discourse on AI companions reveals a stark divide: some scholars argue that feeling connected to AI is impossible due to AI’s inability to experience emotions, while others contend that AI’s ability to provide the illusion of emotions is enough. Across two experiments (one pre-registered; Total N = 1274), we investigated whether accounting for individual differences in anthropomorphism could help bridge these two perspectives. Participants completed a measure of anthropomorphism and were then randomly assigned to discuss their past month through a conversation with a chatbot (chatbot condition) or by journaling (control condition) and then completed a measure of social connection. Our results suggest that for some individuals, AI’s artificial nature may pose an insurmountable barrier to meaningful connection; for others, however, this artificiality may be a minor obstacle, easily overcome by a tendency to anthropomorphize.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19212-2.Subject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour

Introduction

People increasingly use conversational AI for support and companionship1. Indeed, one popular chatbot, Replika, is marketed as an “AI companion who cares.” Yet, the notion that AI can care is misleading, because conversational AI cannot feel anything. Nevertheless, studies show that people feel more socially connected immediately after interacting with chatbots2,3; for example, participants felt less lonely after texting with AI compared to watching YouTube or doing nothing3. These average effects, however, may conceal important individual variation. Indeed, current discourse on AI companions reveals a stark divide: some people readily embrace these relationships, while others recoil at the notion of bonding with mindless machines4–6. Scholars are similarly split, with some arguing that connecting with AI is impossible due to its inability to experience emotions7,8 while others contend that the illusion of emotions is enough9. What could explain these divergent perspectives on AI companionship?

Anthropomorphism—attributing human qualities to nonhuman agents10—may be a critical individual difference that accounts for these contrasting perspectives. People who anthropomorphize AI may be more likely to emotionally connect with AI because they do not feel like they are interacting with a mindless machine. Crucially, there is evidence that people vary substantially in their tendency to anthropomorphize10,11 meaning that such differences in anthropomorphism may help to explain why some people readily connect with AI while others do not. As an initial investigation of this idea, Folk et al.12 developed a measure that assessed how much people anthropomorphized technology. The authors found that people high in anthropomorphism of technology expected to enjoy a hypothetical conversation with a chatbot more, and this helped to explain cultural differences in attitudes towards AI.

However, people struggle to accurately predict how experiences will actually make them feel13,14. As such, it is unclear whether anthropomorphizing technology can explain people’s emotional reactions to real interactions with chatbots.

Present research

To investigate the relationship between anthropomorphism and connecting with AI, we conducted exploratory (Study 1) and preregistered (Study 2) experiments. Participants first completed a measure of anthropomorphism of technology and were then randomly assigned to discuss their past month through a conversation with a chatbot (chatbot condition) or by engaging in a journaling exercise (control condition). Participants then completed a measure of social connection. We included the journaling control because it also required participants to discuss their past month but lacked the social element of having a conversation with a chatbot. Our primary hypothesis was that those who scored higher in anthropomorphism of technology would feel more connected after interacting with the chatbot, but that anthropomorphism would be less related to feelings of connection in the journal condition. Additionally, we investigated whether people garnered more feelings of social connection from the interaction with the chatbot compared to journaling.

Study 1

Methods

Experimental design

Participants were assigned to either interact with a chatbot for 6 minutes (chatbot condition) or engage in a journaling exercise for 6 minutes (journal condition). In the chatbot condition, participants interacted with a chatbot built utilizing the API for ChatGPT 3.5 (https://openai.com/api/). To mirror the behavior of social chatbots designed to provide companionship to their users, we prompted the chatbot to respond in a warm manner (participants did not see this prompt). Specifically, we asked it to “Respond in a very happy and positive manner and try to keep the conversation flowing by asking follow-up questions. The topic of the conversation should be what the person did over the past month.”

All participants in Study 1 (and Study 2) provided informed consent, and the study procedure was approved by the University of British Columbia’s Institutional Research Ethics Board (application #H20-02433); our methods and procedures also complied with all relevant guidelines and regulations.

Sample

A total of 242 American participants from Prolific Academic completed a survey in December 2023 in return for $1.34. Two participants were excluded for failing to follow instructions (see Exclusion Criteria below), resulting in a final sample of 240 participants (Mean Age: 38.94; SD = 13.28; 55% female; nchatbot = 124, njournal= 116). This gave us 80% power to detect differences between the chatbot and journal condition of d = 0.36 or greater.

Procedure

During the first portion of the online survey, participants completed demographic questions and a measure of anthropomorphism of technology (see https://tinyurl.com/3y9kme29 for list of measures included in the survey). Participants were then asked to “Please take a moment to think about what you did over the past month, and make a mental note of anything interesting or eventful that has happened to you over this time.” They were then asked to journal about the events or discuss them with a chatbot (see SOM for full prompts). Participants could not move on from the chatbot/journal task until at least six minutes had passed. They then completed the key measure of social connection, among other variables.

Measures

Anthropomorphism of Technology. We used a previously validated 8-item measure that assesses people’s beliefs about the human-like potential of artificial intelligence12 (α = 0.88). An example item is, “AI’s have the capacity to become conscious beings” with response options ranging from 1 = “Strongly disagree” to 6 = “Strongly agree.” The scale is related to, but distinct from, more general measures of anthropomorphism (e.g., correlations of approximately r = .4012).

Social connection. We used a 1-item measure of social connection that asked people “Compared to how socially connected you normally feel, how socially connected do you feel right now?” with response options ranging from − 5 = “Much less connected than normal” to + 5= “Much more connected than normal,” with 0 = “No different than normal.” We focused on this measure over existing measures of state social connection (e.g.,15) because it does not refer to any target for the feelings of connection (e.g., “I felt close to people”15). Measures that reference humans may have been interpreted differently across the journal and chatbot conditions.

Additional variables. Because Study 1 was an initial exploratory study, we included additional outcome and predictor variables that we explored but are not discussed here. Details for all of these measures are available on the OSF at https://tinyurl.com/3y9kme29.

Exclusion criteria

At the end of the survey, participants were asked whether they were able to successfully engage in their assigned task (i.e., interacting with the chatbot or completing the writing exercise). We excluded the two participants who indicated they were not able to engage in the task.

Results

Because this study was not pre-registered, all the results should be treated as exploratory. We fit a multiple regression model with social connection as the outcome variable and condition (1 = “Chatbot”, 0 = “Journal”), anthropomorphism of technology and a Condition X Anthropomorphism of technology interaction effect as the predictor variables. We centered the anthropomorphism of technology variable to reduce multicollinearity between the main effect and interaction term.

There was a main effect of Condition, B = 0.154, p = .014, indicating that people felt more socially connected on average after interacting with the chatbot (M = 1.48, SD = 2.12) compared to journaling (M = 0.79, SD = 2.15; Cohen’s d = 0.32), when controlling for anthropomorphism. There was no main effect of anthropomorphism, B = 0.024, p = .785. However, there was a significant Condition X Anthropomorphism interaction effect, B = 0.218, p = .013 (see Table 1 for detailed results); people who scored higher in anthropomorphism of technology felt more connected after interacting with the chatbot (r = .33, p < .001) whereas anthropomorphism and connection were unrelated in the journal condition (r = .02, p = .792; see Fig. 1). It is worth noting the significant interaction effect remained when we controlled for age and gender (p = .021; see SOM for full model details).

To further disentangle the nature of the interaction, we conducted a Johnson-Neyman spotlight analysis16 which identifies the score on the anthropomorphism scale at which people in the chatbot condition reported significantly more social connection than the journal condition. This analysis revealed that participants who scored higher than 3.13 (out of 6) on the anthropomorphism scale garnered significantly more feelings of social connection from a chatbot than a journal (p < .05), but that there were no significant differences between the chatbot and journal for people who scored below this threshold. 58% of our sample scored above this threshold, suggesting that most of the participants would feel more connected after texting with the chatbot vs. journaling.

Study 1 discussion

In Study 1, we found evidence that anthropomorphism is associated with feeling socially connected after interacting with a chatbot. In addition, spotlight analyses revealed that 58% of participants scored high enough on our anthropomorphism measure to reap more feelings of connection from interacting with a chatbot compared to journaling. We also found evidence that participants who interacted with a chatbot felt more socially connected than those who journaled. Nevertheless, Study 1 was a relatively small, exploratory study. As such, we conducted a high-powered, preregistered replication in Study 2.

Study 2

Methods

Sample

A total of 1041 American and Canadian participants from Prolific academic completed the survey in return for $1.60 in May 2024. Seven participants were excluded for failing to follow instructions (see Exclusion Criteria below), leaving a total of 1034 participants (Mean Age: 36.61; SD = 12.36; 53% female; 20% Canadian; nchatbot = 514, njournal = 520). This sample gave us 99% power to detect differences between the chatbot and journal condition of at least the same size as in Study 1 (d = 0.32) with a one-tailed t-test (as pre-registered). It also gave us approximately 99% power to detect Condition X Anthropomorphism interaction effects of at least the same size as those uncovered in Study 1 (B = 0.218).

We had initially pre-registered a sample size target of N = 580. However, prior to beginning data collection, we pre-registered a new target sample size of N = 1000 (original pre-registration: https://tinyurl.com/3dhxmhpu and the addendum: https://tinyurl.com/2rhyj89j). We pre-registered the larger sample because we gained access to additional financial resources and wanted to account for the possibility that the effect sizes from Study 1 were overestimates. Our final sample is slightly larger than the target of 1000 because we oversampled in anticipation of greater exclusion rates.

Procedure

The procedure for Study 2 was identical to Study 1, except that the surveys included slightly different sets of exploratory measures. While most of these measures are not relevant to the present analyses, we did include two questions assessing how frequently participants used chatbots for social and productivity related reasons. Please see the SOM for the full details on these measures.

We also updated the chatbot application in Study 2 to enable the ability to save the text from each conversation. Technical difficulties prevented 46 conversations from being saved, meaning that we had the text data from 468 conversations in the chatbot condition. All journal entries were saved directly through Qualtrics. People wrote slightly more words in the journal condition (M = 173.64, SD = 121.86) compared to the chatbot condition (M = 159.69, SD = 80.34), t(908.21) = 2.14, p = .032. This difference may be due to the extra time required to wait for and read the chatbot’s responses.

Exclusion criteria

As in Study 1, we asked participants whether they were able to successfully engage in the writing task they were assigned. We excluded 7 participants who did not engage in the task (as pre-registered).

Pre-registered analysis plan

To test for the Condition X Anthropomorphism interaction effect, we pre-registered the same regression model we used in Study 1. We also pre-registered a one-tailed t-test because we wanted to confirm our finding from Study 1 that people in the chatbot condition reported higher levels of social connection than those in the journal condition.

Results

In line with our preregistered prediction, our regression model revealed a significant Condition X Anthropomorphism interaction effect (B = 0.100, p = .013; see Table 1); people high (vs. low) in anthropomorphism were more likely to feel socially connected after interacting with a chatbot (r = .427, p < .001), and this relationship was smaller in the journal condition (see Fig. 1). Unlike Study 1, however, people who scored higher in anthropomorphism still felt more socially connected from journaling (r = .310, p < .001).

According to our pre-registered t-test, participants who interacted with a chatbot felt significantly more socially connected (M = 1.46, SD = 2.20) than those who journaled (M = 1.14, SD = 2.08), t(1027.8) = 2.39, p = .008 (one-tailed). The effect size (d = 0.15), however, was considerably smaller than in Study 1, largely because the participants in our journaling condition reported considerably more social connection than those in the previous study (MStudy1 = 0.79).

Exploratory analysis

Controlling for age, gender, and previous chatbot use. We conducted an additional regression analysis controlling for age, gender, and our measures of how participants reported using chatbots (see SOM for the description of these measures). In this model, the Anthropomorphism X Condition interaction effect remained significant (p = .021; see SOM for full model details).

Spotlight analysis. As in Study 1, we conducted a Johnson-Neyman spotlight analysis16. This analysis revealed that participants who scored above 2.87 (out of 6) on the anthropomorphism scale reported significantly higher levels of social connection in the chatbot condition compared to the journal condition (p < .05). This represents a large portion of our sample, as 72% of the participants scored above 2.87 on the measure of anthropomorphism.

Text analysis. To explore whether the content of what participants wrote differed across the chatbot and journal conditions, we used a pipeline outlined by Rathje et al.17 to conduct exploratory text analysis with ChatGPT 4.0 mini (https://openai.com/api/). Specifically, we had ChatGPT rate the levels of warmth/positivity and self-disclosure in the participants’ messages/journal entries on a scale from 1 to 5 (see the SOM for the full details about the rating procedure). We assessed warmth because we reasoned that writing in a warm and positive manner may be related to feeling more positive and socially connected after our assigned tasks. Likewise, we assessed self-disclosure because of the well-documented link between disclosure and feelings of connection in interpersonal relationships (e.g., 18–20). We did not assess any other constructs through text analysis. As shown in Table 2, participants who journaled wrote more warmly and disclosed more than participants who texted with the chatbot, p’s < 0.001.

Across both conditions, warmth was positively associated with social connection (r = .26, p < .001), but disclosure had no relationship (r = .02, p = .500). We also assessed whether the relationship between these constructs and social connection differed across conditions, but there were no significant interaction effects (p’s > 0.14).

Examining whether anthropomorphism predicted warmth and disclosure, we found that anthropomorphism was positively associated with warmth (r = .17, p < .001) and disclosure (r = .12, p < .001) across both conditions. Once again, these relationships did not differ by condition, as there were no significant interaction effects (p’s > 0.07).

General discussion

Our results suggest that individual differences in anthropomorphism help to explain why some people feel more connected to AI than others. Specifically, we found that anthropomorphizing technology was strongly related to feeling connected after texting with a chatbot, but that this relationship was significantly smaller when participants simply journaled. By examining individual variation in connecting with AI, our research adds nuance to previous work that focused solely on the average emotional response to AI companions2,3,21.

Why might anthropomorphism be related to emotionally bonding with AI? One possibility is that anthropomorphism influences how people engage with AI. Surprisingly, however, anthropomorphism was only weakly related to how much people disclosed (r = .12) and how warmly people interacted (r = .17) with their AI partner in Study 2. Even more telling, the relationship between anthropomorphism and disclosure and warmth was similar when people were simply journaling. Thus, if seeing human-like qualities in AI does foster connection, it likely is not through these two aspects of how people engage with chatbots.

A remaining possibility, however, is that anthropomorphism fosters connection because it changes how people interpret the chatbot’s responses, rather than how they themselves interact. When people perceive a mind in their AI partner, they may feel more genuinely understood and validated, and in turn, reap greater feelings of connection from the interaction. Future research should test whether feeling understood mediates the relationship between anthropomorphism and feeling socially connected to AI.

In Study 2 (but not Study 1), anthropomorphism was associated with social connection even when participants journaled. What could explain this unexpected relationship? One possibility is that people who anthropomorphize feel more socially connected in general; however, Folk et al.12 found no correlation between anthropomorphism and trait loneliness. Instead, it seems likely that anthropomorphism predicted connection while journaling because people who anthropomorphize have more active imaginations and form richer mental imagery22,23. As a result, when writing about their past month, participants high in anthropomorphism may have imagined moments with friends and family more vividly, increasing their sense of connection.

This unexpected finding points to the value of our journaling control condition. By ensuring that participants in both conditions engaged in written reflection about their past week, we were able to isolate the unique effect of interacting with a chatbot, above and beyond thinking about one’s own life. That said, it would be interesting to extend this work by including a condition in which participants shared details of their past week with another human. We would expect that anthropomorphism of technology should play little to no role in whether people feel connection with another human.

Our exploratory spotlight analyses suggested that most participants in our studies could feel social connection from a chatbot: over half scored above the threshold of anthropomorphism at which they would feel more connected after interacting with a chatbot compared to journaling. Of course, our sample of American and Canadian Prolific workers may be especially comfortable with technology, which could make them particularly amenable to benefitting from chatbots. Nevertheless, there was still substantial variation in participants’ feelings towards AI. Indeed, at the end of each survey we asked participants if they had any comments on the study, and the responses reflected a range of attitudes towards AI companions. One participant who scored in the top 25% of anthropomorphism expressed positivity about their interaction with the chatbot, saying that the chatbot “knew exactly what to say during our conversation. It felt like I was talking to a real-life person.” In contrast, one participant who scored in the bottom 25% of anthropomorphism had a much more negative impression, describing AI as a “hideous puppet feigning humanity.”

Despite these divergent responses, we found that, on average, people felt more connected from interacting with the chatbot compared to journaling. One plausible explanation for this effect is that people might communicate more warmly and disclose more when texting with a chatbot (vs. journaling), leading to more feelings of connection. While warmth was positively associated with feelings of connection in Study 2, people were less warm when interacting with the chatbot compared to journaling. Participants also disclosed less to the chatbot, although it is notable that there was no overall relationship with disclosure and connection in our study. As such, differences in disclosure and warmth across the two conditions cannot explain why people who texted with an AI partner felt more connected.

Our findings extend prior work showing that chatbots provide emotional benefits (e.g., social connection, positive affect) comparable to interacting with humans and to a greater extent than other activities like watching YouTube2,3,21,24. It is possible that the observed benefits of interacting with chatbots in these prior studies stemmed from the opportunity to express one’s feelings rather than from specifically interacting with a chatbot25,26. Indeed, the positive impact of expressive writing is well-documented. The findings here, however, show that the social connection benefits of texting with chatbots go above and beyond the effects of expressive writing alone. Nevertheless, it is worth noting that the difference between the two conditions was substantially smaller in Study 2 (d = 0.15) than in Study 1 (d = 0.32). Given the much larger sample compared to Study 1, the Study 2 estimate is less susceptible to effects of sampling variation and is therefore likely more accurate.

It is important to note that we focused exclusively on social connection. As such, our results do not speak to the relative benefits of AI (vs. journaling) for other emotional outcomes. For example, it is possible that journaling is more effective at reducing people’s stress compared to interacting with AI. Indeed, research shows expressive writing is particularly effective at helping people make sense of traumatic experiences25. Notably, we found that participants engaged in more self-disclosure when journaling than interacting with AI, suggesting that journaling may be a better avenue for working through deeply personal topics.

Although people reap immediate feelings of social connection from interacting with chatbots, some recent work suggests that repeatedly turning to AI for companionship may actually erode connection in the long-term27. Future work should examine whether an increased tendency to anthropomorphize makes people more or less vulnerable to the potential long-term costs of AI companionship. Anthropomorphism may buffer against the potential long-term costs because it makes interacting with AI more rewarding. However, people high in anthropomorphism could also be more likely to substitute AI interactions for human ones, potentially making them more susceptible to the long-term risks of AI companionship.

While we focused on individual differences in anthropomorphism, our findings dovetail with prior work examining the impact of designing robots and AI to be more human-like. Much of this prior work has focused on customer-service contexts and has found that people prefer more (vs. less) anthropomorphized AI28. However, this research typically only asks people to imagine hypothetical interactions with AI (e.g.,29,30). Our work shows that individual differences in anthropomorphism can help explain why people have vastly different emotional responses to interactions with the exact same AI.

Taken together, the present studies help illuminate an ongoing scholarly debate. On the one hand, scholars contend that the artificial nature of AI will prevent people from truly feeling connected with chatbots7,8. On the other hand, some scholars suggest that even artificial expressions from AI can provide genuine feelings of connection to users9. Our findings suggest that accounting for individual differences in anthropomorphism can help bridge these two perspectives. For some individuals, AI’s artificial nature may pose an insurmountable barrier to meaningful connection; for others, however, this artificiality may be a minor obstacle, easily overcome by a tendency to anthropomorphize.
