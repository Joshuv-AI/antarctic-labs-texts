Peptidyl Glycosyl Thiols and Disulfides for Enantioselective Hydrogen Atom Transfer (HAT) and Thiyl Radical Catalysis
Authors: Savannah M. Mason, Scott J. Miller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A novel class of peptidyl glycosyl disulfide and thiol catalysts is developed. The thiols are found to catalyze enantioselective olefin hydrosilylation up to 5:95 er. The disulfide catalysts are competent in an enantioselective thiyl-catalyzed cycloaddition reaction, yielding product with complementary selectivity to that previously reported with cystine-based catalysts. The modular nature of these catalysts provides new opportunities for asymmetric thiyl and thiol HAT catalysis.

Thiols and thiyl radicals catalyze diverse arrays of biological and chemical transformations. Thiyl radicals exhibit redox activity in biologically relevant processes, facilitating the reduction of nucleotides and metabolism of compounds such as glucose and toluene. In synthesis, thiyl radicals are often accessed through homolytic cleavage of disulfides, facilitated by the weak bond dissociation energy (BDE) of the S–S bond (Figure a). In both biological and synthetic transformations, the BDE of the S–H bond of thiols , (Figure a) renders them reactive species for hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) or abstraction (HAA) and polarity reversal catalysis. , A number of chiral thiols and thiyl radicals have been developed for use in asymmetric HAT reactions − and cycloadditions (Figure b), and the versatility of chiral thiols as ligands or chiral auxiliaries has also been demonstrated.

Carbohydrates are useful building blocks in the development of chiral catalysts due to several attractive features. Sugars are enantiopure compounds in high natural abundance, resulting in low costs and high availability. They are conformationally rigid structures with up to five contiguous stereocenters. Furthermore, the hydroxy groups can be used for the installation of functional groups, providing additional handles for catalysis. To this end, sugars have been equipped with a variety of functional groups, including thiols (Figure b), which have successfully catalyzed reactions for enantioselective olefin functionalization in high selectivity. ,,,

Inspired by the activity of enzymes in biochemical transformations, oligopeptides have also found their foothold for success in selective catalysis. These synthetic peptides mediate selective transformations benefiting from noncovalent interactions between catalyst and substrate. ,, A number of unique catalytic residues have been used in a myriad of selective transformations, , including cysteine (Cys) for thiol-catalyzed HAT − and thiyl-catalyzed cycloaddition (Figure b). , Similar to carbohydrate-based catalysts, these peptidic catalysts are rich in functional groups with many sites for noncovalent interactions between catalyst and substrate.

In this Note, we leverage the synthetic modularity and prior successes for both sugar-based and peptide-based organocatalysts to develop peptidyl glycosyl thiols and disulfidesa novel class of chiral thiol and disulfide catalysts (Figure c). We describe two modular syntheses to access both peptidyl glycosyl thiols and disulfides. The chiral thiol catalysts afforded products of olefin hydrosilylation with high levels of stereocontrol. Using the disulfides, products of thiyl-catalyzed cycloaddition were obtained with selectivity complementary to that previously observed with Cys-based catalysts.

We began our efforts with the synthesis of peptidyl glycosyl thiols (Scheme a). Using neat acetyl bromide and readily available glucosamine 1, the acetyl-protected glycosyl bromide 2 was synthesized in 94% yield, a step scalable to 50 mmol. The anomeric bromide was displaced to form the trityl-protected thioglycoside 3 in 43% yield, which was then subjected to amide coupling conditions using Cbz-d-Phe-OH. Following the trityl deprotection, peptidyl glycosyl thiol T4 was obtained in 49% yield over 2 steps. This route was amenable to the inclusion of both l- and d-amino acids.

Having established an effective route for peptidyl glycosyl thiols, we then sought to further revise the synthetic strategy, allowing modular access to both thiols and disulfides (Scheme b). In 2014, Megia-Fernandez and co-workers reported the synthesis of glycosyl disulfides from glycosyl halides using dithiocarbamate (DTC) salts as the sulfur source. Although this work included a number of different glycosyl halides as substrates, the only glucosamine-based substrate reported was the N-acetyl protected glucosamine chloride. Thus, following optimization using 2, we found that reaction between 2 and the benzyl DTC salt in the presence of excess Et3N afforded the desired disulfide 4 (Scheme b), as supported by UPLC/MS. Using HCTU as the amide coupling reagent, disulfide intermediate 4 was coupled directly to Cbz-l-Pro-OH to afford peptidyl glycosyl disulfide D2 in 43% yield over two steps. Intriguingly, we found the amide coupling with d-amino acids to be low yielding (10% yield over 2 steps with Cbz-d-Phe-OH). We observed that the products from coupling with l-amino acids exhibited conformational symmetry by 1H NMR spectroscopy, whereas those with d-amino acids were nonsymmetrical. These compounds appeared to be conformationally rigid, as no significant change to the 1H NMR spectrum was observed upon heating up to 75 °C. We hypothesize that the low yields observed in amide coupling with d-amino acids may be due to increased steric hindrance.

Upon subjecting D2 to TCEP·HCl, peptidyl glycosyl thiol T2 was obtained in 75% yield. Consistent with the challenges faced in the formation of the disulfide with d-amino acids, the reduction of d-Phe-derived D4 also proved to be low yielding, resulting in only trace product formation. This modular synthesis was amenable to the incorporation of different amino acids, providing access to chiral disulfides D1–D4 in only three steps and up to a 40% overall yield (Scheme c). The synthesis also afforded chiral thiols T1–T3 in four steps and up to 30% overall yield. Peptidyl glycosyl thiol T4, containing d-Phe, was obtained through the synthesis shown in Scheme a.

To the best of our knowledge, the earliest report for the use of glycosyl thiols as catalysts in stereoselective HAT originated from Roberts and co-workers in 1996 in the enantioselective hydrosilylation of olefins. , We first sought to evaluate peptidyl glycosyl thiols as chiral catalysts in olefin hydrosilylation under conditions identical to those previously reported by Roberts and co-workers. We selected the two substrates that exhibited the highest selectivities in the previous report, lactone-based substrates 5a and 5b (Table ). With the gem-dimethyl substrate 5a and commercially available thiol C1 (Figure b), the authors reported the formation of product 6a in 84% yield and 71.5:28.5 er. While we were able to reproduce the selectivity (observed 27.5:72.5 er, Table , entry 1), we observed a lower yield (57%) than that reported in the literature.

Satisfied with the reproducibility of the enantioselectivity in the reaction, we next evaluated peptidyl glycosyl thiols T1–T4 in the hydrosilylation of olefin 5a (Table ). The yields provided by T1–T4 were generally comparable to those provided by C1 (38–66% yield, Table , entries 2–5). The enantioselectivities afforded by peptidyl glycosyl thiols were similar to one another (38:62–34:66 er, entries 2–5), although lower than that afforded by C1.

We further evaluated the hydrosilylation of gem-diphenyl substrate 5b and found the observed yield and enantioselectivity with C1 (83% yield, 6.5:93.5 er, Table , entry 6) to be comparable to that in the literature (92% yield, 93:7 er). The yields afforded by peptidyl glycosyl thiols T2 (l-Pro-derived, 70% yield, entry 8) and T3 (l-Phe-derived, 72% yield, entry 9) were comparable to that afforded by C1. Interestingly, the lowest yield was afforded by T1, derived from l-Val (32% yield, 9:91 er, entry 7), which further proved to be challenging to reproduce and may result from a steric clash between the valine side chain and the diphenyl substituents on the substrate. Satisfyingly, the peptidyl glycosyl thiols tested afforded product in high enantioselectivity (entries 7–10), and product was provided in 6:94 er with catalyst T4, derived from d-Phe (entry 10).

Despite the success of the enantioselective hydrosilylation of olefins via classical thermal initiation by TBHN, we envisioned that mild reaction conditions could be identified under photoredox catalysis. Thus, radical initiation using photoinitiation might allow for the use of lower reaction temperatures. Indeed, we identified 4CzIPN as a competent photocatalyst under irradiation by blue LEDs, and when used with C1, product 6b was obtained in comparable yield and selectivity to that obtained by thermal initiation (Table , entry 1 compared to Table , entry 6).

As found in the reaction under thermal initiation, lower yield and selectivity were observed using catalyst T1, derived from l-Val, under photoinitiation conditions (Table , entry 2). The drop in selectivity, as compared to the selectivity observed under thermal initiation (60 °C, Table , entry 7), may be due to the limits of catalyst solubility in toluene at 35 °C. However, product 6b was obtained in good yield when using catalysts T2, T3, and T4 (70–73% yield, entries 3–5). Furthermore, the enantioselectivities afforded by T2, T3, and T4 were enhanced when compared to those obtained under thermal initiation (Table , entries 3–5 compared to Table , entries 8–10). Both T2 and T4, derived from l-Pro and d-Phe, respectively, yielded the product in 5:95 er, while T3, derived from l-Phe, yielded the product in 6.5:93.5 er. Gratifyingly, peptidyl glycosyl thiols proved to be competent chiral catalysts for enantioselective HAT reactions such as olefin hydrosilylation with high selectivity.

In 2018, the Miller group disclosed the use of Cys-based peptide catalysts as thiyl precursors in enantioselective cycloaddition with vinylcyclopropanes and vinyl tert-butyl ether. Thus, we sought to evaluate peptidyl glycosyl disulfides as chiral thiyl precatalysts in this cycloaddition reaction using vinylcyclopropane substrate 7 (Table ). Using Cys-based disulfide catalyst P1, we obtained cycloaddition product 8 in comparable yield and selectivity to that previously reported in the literature (Table , entry 1). Peptidyl glycosyl disulfides D1 and D2 provided products in good yields (77% and 76%, respectively, entries 2–3), indicating that the thiyl radicals from these catalysts can indeed be accessed by UV-promoted homolysis of the disulfide bond. Yields were lower with Phe-derived catalysts D3 and D4 (42% and 41%, respectively, entries 4–5), which may be the result of aggregation or solubility limitations in this solvent mixture. Consistent with previous findings, the diastereoselectivity was largely unaffected by the choice of chiral catalyst. In general, the enantioselectivities afforded by peptidyl glycosyl disulfides were lower than those afforded by P1. When using P1 and other Cys-based disulfide catalysts, the trans diastereomer was obtained in higher enantioselectivity than the cis diastereomer. Notably, the peptidyl glycosyl disulfide catalysts D1–D4 all afforded the cis diastereomer in higher enantioselectivity than the trans diastereomer. This significant finding indicates that the peptidyl glycosyl disulfides may afford selectivity complementary to that obtained with Cys-derived catalysts.

In this work, we disclose the synthesis of peptidyl glycosyl thiols and disulfides. We found the thiols to be highly selective HAT catalysts in enantioselective olefin hydrosilylation, which yielded products up to 5:95 er. We further demonstrated the competency of the disulfides as precatalysts in enantioselective cycloaddition by thiyl radical catalysis and discovered that these peptidyl glycosyl disulfides may provide complementary selectivity to that previously reported with Cys-based catalysts. The novel catalysts described herein may have broad applications in stereoselective thiol- and thiyl-catalyzed transformations in the future.
