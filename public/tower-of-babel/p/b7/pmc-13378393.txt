Dual BAFF and APRIL inhibition with TACI-Fc depletes IgA plasma cells and ameliorates lupus nephritis
Authors: Andrea Daamen, Claudia Rival, Mahua Mandal, Samantha St Jean, Amrie Grammer, Loren Erickson, Peter Lipsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryB cell activating factor (BAFF) and APRIL play an important role in driving systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) immunopathogenesis and kidney damage in lupus nephritis (LN); however, the underlying immune mechanisms contributing to disease pathology are poorly understood. To investigate molecular profiles during the progression of LN, we carried out gene expression analysis on kidneys and spleens from lupus-prone NZM2328 mice treated for 4 weeks from the time of disease onset with BAFF and BAFF/APRIL inhibitors. Inhibition of BAFF alone had a limited impact on gene expression profiles of diseased mice. However, targeting both BAFF and APRIL through competitive inhibition with a transmembrane activator and CAML interactor (TACI)-Fc fusion protein significantly altered immune populations in the spleen, depleted IgA plasma cells, and also decreased inflammation in the kidneys of diseased mice. The impact of targeting both members of the BAFF family of cytokines in lupus-prone NZM2328 mice provides support for its application in the treatment of human LN.Keywords: lupus, nephritis, transcriptomics, bioinformatics, B cells, mouse models

Introduction

Members of the B cell activating factor (BAFF) cytokine-receptor network play critical roles in regulating B cell homeostasis and maintaining immune tolerance.1 Elevated BAFF levels have been associated with the breakdown of B cell tolerance in both autoimmune-prone mice and humans.2,3,4,5 Belimumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting BAFF, is the first biologic drug approved for the treatment of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).6 By inhibiting BAFF, belimumab reduces B cell survival and activity, which helps control autoantibody production and inflammation. Clinical trials have shown moderate but clinically meaningful efficacy in SLE, particularly in reducing disease activity and preventing flares in patients with active, autoantibody-positive disease. Not all SLE patients respond, however, highlighting the heterogeneity of the disease and the need for biomarkers to predict responsiveness. Preclinical studies have suggested that broader immunosuppressive effects can be achieved with dual BAFF and APRIL targeting using transmembrane activator and CAML interactor (TACI) fusion protein therapy that binds to both BAFF and APRIL, blocking their interaction with all downstream receptors (BAFF-R and TACI and TACI and BCMA, respectively). These results suggest that APRIL contributes additional biologic effects on B cell biology that may contribute significantly to disease pathology in SLE, but its clinical use requires further refinement.

Lupus-prone NZM2328 mice spontaneously develop lupus-like features, including autoantibody production and glomerulonephritis, with females exhibiting more severe manifestations at an earlier age compared with males.7,8 Severe proteinuria (≥300 mg/dL) typically develops after 16 weeks of age, with approximately 30% and 70% of female NZM2328 mice developing lupus nephritis (LN) with proteinuria by 20 and 32 weeks, respectively.7 Moreover, about 12% of female mice produce anti-double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) IgG autoantibodies at 20 weeks, a proportion that increases rapidly to 40% after 24 weeks. In contrast, male NZM2328 mice do not develop proteinuria or progress to chronic disease.

The diverse clinical manifestations of LN and the variability in patient responses to anti-BAFF therapies underscore the importance of molecular profiling in refining the classification of lupus kidney pathology. In this study, we employed transcriptome analysis to understand the pathogenesis of LN more completely, with a particular focus on the relationship between anti-BAFF/APRIL treatment and chronic disease. Using NZM2328 mice, we aimed to identify pathological immune cell populations and processes modified following treatment with either anti-BAFF or TACI-Ig. To accomplish this, we employed a 4-week window of treatment in an attempt to identify the immediate pharmacodynamic impact of the agents separate from any influences of clinical improvement on gene expression.

Results

Splenic transcriptional profiling reveals reduced inflammation of spleens from lupus-prone mice following anti-BAFF/APRIL treatment

To investigate molecular changes following BAFF blockade, we examined the transcriptional profiles of the spleens of NZM2328 mice treated with either a BAFFR-Fc fusion protein, which neutralizes BAFF, or TACI-Fc fusion protein, which neutralizes both BAFF and APRIL. A group treated with cyclophosphamide, a BAFF-independent standard therapy, was included for comparison. Female NZM2328 mice were treated with a matched isotype control-Fc, BAFFR-Fc, TACI-Fc, or cyclophosphamide every other day for 4 weeks from the time of developing proteinuria. Then, gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was used to identify gene signatures representing immune cells and inflammatory molecules affected by therapy targeting the BAFF cytokine network (Figure 1A). Treatment with BAFFR-Fc only impacted myeloid cell populations, including a significant decrease in the monocyte/macrophage signature and a trending decrease in the neutrophil gene signature. Cyclophosphamide had a broader effect on both activated T cell and monocyte/macrophage gene signatures. In contrast, TACI-Fc had a robust, targeted effect on B cells, leading to decreased enrichment of the plasma cell (PC) and Ig chain gene signatures as well as a significant decrease in the Tfh cell signature.

In order to uncover additional treatment effects and compare the transcriptional impact of BAFFR-Fc and TACI-Fc treatment, we carried out differential expression (DE) analysis against control-Fc-treated mice. The spleens of TACI-Fc-treated mice exhibited 1,496 DE genes (465 upregulated and 1,031 downregulated), whereas BAFFR-Fc-treated mice only had 56 total DE genes (8 upregulated and 48 downregulated) (Figure 1B). Gene Ontology (GO) and pathway enrichment analysis revealed that genes upregulated by BAFFR-Fc treatment were related to mitochondrial metabolism and cell respiration, whereas downregulated genes were related to negative regulation of T cell activation and IL-12 production, as well as innate immune processes, including neutrophil degranulation (Figure 1C). TACI-Fc-upregulated genes were involved in many of the pathways downregulated by BAFFR-Fc therapy, including neutrophil degranulation, whereas downregulated genes were related to B cell activation and the unfolded protein response (Figure 1D).

Serum total IgA and IgA autoantibodies are depleted by BAFF- and APRIL-targeted therapy

Treatment of diseased NZM2328 mice with dual BAFF and APRIL blockade, but not targeting of BAFF alone, resulted in a significant decrease in PCs as compared to all other treatment groups. Thus, we examined the impact of therapy on serum immunoglobulins and anti-nuclear autoantibodies (ANAs) (Figure 2). Total serum IgG (Figure 2A) and autoantibodies against dsDNA, single-stranded DNA (ssDNA), histones, and chromatin (Figure 2B) were not significantly altered by the study’s end, reflecting the stable half-life of IgG, although they trended lower in the treatment groups compared to control mice.

Whereas IgG autoantibodies are the predominant class associated with LN, IgA autoantibodies have been increasingly recognized as important contributors to renal damage, particularly in certain patient subsets and lupus-prone animal models.9,10,11,12,13 To test whether IgA is affected by drug treatment, we measured total and ANA-specific IgA levels in the sera of mice (Figures 2C and 2D). Total IgA levels were higher in control mice as well as mice treated with BAFFR-Fc or cyclophosphamide. In contrast, total IgA levels were significantly reduced in mice treated with TACI-Fc (Figure 2C). Based on these results, we next tested whether the groups of mice had variable levels of IgA autoantibodies. Analysis of IgA levels against histones, dsDNA, ssDNA, and chromatin in mice following treatment confirmed that TACI-Fc significantly reduced IgA autoantibody levels compared to controls (Figure 2D). As expected, given the increased total IgA levels in mice treated with anti-BAFF or cyclophosphamide, IgA autoantibodies were not different compared to controls.

To investigate the relationship between splenic PC transcriptional profiles and the expression of immunoglobulin heavy chains in the serum of mice following anti-BAFF therapy, we carried out linear regression with PC GSVA scores. Both total serum IgG and IgA showed significant positive correlations with the PC GSVA score with similar correlation coefficients (r2 values of 0.21 and 0.16, respectively) (Figure 3A). In addition, the PC gene signature was significantly correlated with levels of histone IgG, histone IgA, dsDNA IgG, and dsDNA IgA ANAs, indicative of the relationship between splenic PC transcripts and serum autoantibodies (Figures 3B and 3C). As expected, the PC gene signature was also highly correlated with both Ighg1 (r2 = 0.752) and Igha (r2 = 0.565) immunoglobulin heavy-chain isotypes (Figure 3D). These results clearly demonstrate that the overall depletion of PCs is reflected in both the molecular and serum profiles of TACI-Fc treated mice.

Dual BAFF/APRIL-targeted therapy reduces splenic germinal centers and IgA+ PCs

In order to validate the significant reduction in the PC gene signature in the spleen and correlation with serum immunoglobulin levels, we investigated the impact of BAFF cytokine network-targeted therapy on germinal center (GC) development and PCs in the spleen. Immunofluorescent (IF) staining of peanut agglutinin (PNA)+ GCs revealed that 4-week, short-term treatment with BAFFR-Fc had no impact on splenic GC generation, whereas TACI-Fc and cyclophosphamide treatment significantly reduced the number of GCs (Figures 4A and 4B). In addition, in line with the total and ANA-specific levels of serum IgA, TACI-Fc, but not BAFFR-Fc, led to a significant decrease in IF staining of CD138+IgA+ PCs in the spleen (Figures 4C and 4D). Thus, this result aligns with our molecular analysis indicating that inhibition of both BAFF and APRIL is necessary to significantly reduce the production of autoreactive B cells in this model.

Kidney transcriptomes reflect reduced inflammation and tissue preservation with TACI-Fc treatment

The BAFF cytokine network is important for the survival and proliferation of autoreactive B cells that contribute to kidney pathology and the development of LN. Furthermore, we have previously characterized the stages of glomerulonephritis resulting in chronic renal disease in NZM2328 mice and confirmed that similar molecular profiles of kidney pathology are present in human lupus patients.8 Thus, we carried out gene expression analysis of kidneys from NZM2328 mice to examine the transcriptomic impact of BAFF- or BAFF/APRIL-targeted therapy. As in the spleen, GSVA of kidneys from mice treated with TACI-Fc revealed de-enrichment of the PC signature but also de-enrichment of the activated T cell, neutrophil, and monocyte/macrophage gene signatures as compared to control mice (Figure 5A). In particular, we observed a decrease in the pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage subset but not the M2 macrophage subset. In addition, multiple gene signatures indicative of the response to pro-inflammatory cytokines were decreased with TACI-Fc treatment, including IFN, TNF-induced genes, and the IL-21 complex (Figure 5A). In contrast, BAFFR-targeted therapy did not have an impact on the GSVA scores of any of the selected gene signatures, and cyclophosphamide only significantly decreased the TNF-induced gene signature as compared to control treatment.

To identify additional effects of anti-BAFF therapy that may not have been detected by GSVA with pre-selected gene modules, we carried out DE analysis (Figures 5B–5D). In the kidneys of diseased NZM2328 mice, both BAFFR-Fc and TACI-Fc treatment resulted in a greater total number of DE genes than observed in the spleen, and thus there was a greater overlap between them (Figure 5B). BAFFR-Fc treatment produced 1,027 total DE genes (222 upregulated and 805 downregulated), whereas TACI-Fc treatment produced a total of 5,822 DE genes (2,780 upregulated and 3,042 downregulated). GO and pathway analysis of BAFFR-Fc-treated mice revealed that upregulated genes were involved in glutathione metabolism and renal cell differentiation and downregulated genes were related to extracellular matrix processes and cell migration (Figure 5C). TACI-Fc-upregulated genes were focused on processes critical for maintaining aerobic metabolism through mitochondrial respiration, whereas downregulated genes were related to cell migration and immune cytokine signaling (Figure 5D).

In our previous analysis of the development of glomerulonephritis in NZM2328 mice, we found that reduction in transcriptional signatures of kidney tissue cells was indicative of disease progression and kidney damage.8 Thus, we examined the effect of treatment on the enrichment of kidney cells overall, kidney tubule cells, and podocytes by GSVA (Figure 5E). Notably, whereas DE genes upregulated with BAFFR-Fc therapy were related to kidney cell differentiation, there was no impact on the GSVA scores of kidney cell signatures. In contrast, TACI-Fc treatment resulted in significant increases in both the general kidney cell and kidney tubule cell gene signatures, indicative of a beneficial effect of TACI-Fc treatment on kidney pathology.

Anti-BAFF therapy decreases proteinuria but not immune complex deposition in the kidneys of lupus-prone mice

To explore the effect of BAFF-targeted therapy in the kidney in greater detail, we evaluated the deposition of immune complexes in the kidney using IF staining. Consistent with the increased levels of IgG autoantibodies observed for all groups of mice, C3 and IgG deposition was found at similar levels in glomeruli from mice, with the levels of IgG trending lower in mice after treatment with TACI-Fc and cyclophosphamide (Figures 6A–6C). IgA deposition is found in LN glomeruli,14 suggesting that IgA ANA might also contribute to SLE pathology. Therefore, we investigated the presence of IgA deposition in the kidneys. Kidneys from mice revealed IgA deposits in glomeruli from all groups of mice (Figures 6D and 6E). Notably, IgA deposits were increased in glomeruli from mice following BAFFR-Fc treatment compared to controls. In contrast, IgA deposits in glomeruli from mice treated with TACI-Fc were decreased compared to controls. Moreover, the majority of mice (>88%) showed colocalization of IgA/IgG in the glomeruli, except mice treated with TACI Ab, in which only 43% of mice showed colocalization (data not shown). These data suggest that IgA autoantibodies deposited in the glomeruli of NZM2328 mice are associated with immune complex formation. In addition, these data suggest that blocking BAFF may increase IgA deposition in the kidneys, whereas blocking BAFF and APRIL decreased IgA deposition.

Previous studies in NZM2328 mice demonstrate that anti-BAFF therapy reduces proteinuria but does not always lead to a complete resolution of glomerular IgG deposition, as IgG immune complexes already deposited in the kidneys may remain stable over the 4-week treatment period.15 Consistent with these prior findings, mice treated with BAFFR-Fc or TACI-Fc manifested reduced levels of proteinuria compared to control animals, similar to that noted in mice treated with cyclophosphamide (Figure 6F). In particular, severe proteinuria (≥300 mg/dL) was only observed in the control group (42%). Thus, while anti-BAFF and standard-of-care cyclophosphamide treatment reduce general outcomes of kidney damage, only TACI-Fc treatment improved the molecular profiles of treated mice through reduction of inflammation and preservation of kidney tissue.

Histologic examination reveals reduction in inflammatory populations and tissue injury with BAFF/APRIL inhibition

Histopathologic examination of a cross section from a single kidney of all mice, utilizing hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) stains, showed that 47% (8/17) of control-Fc mice had lesions consistent with glomerulonephritis, with severity ranging from minimal (1/8), mild (2/8), moderate (3/8), and marked (2/8) (Figures 7A and 7B). It was also notable that control-Fc mice exhibited pronounced tubulointerstitial inflammation not observed in the other treatment groups. Twenty-nine percent (2/7) of mice treated with BAFFR-Fc demonstrated glomerulonephritis, and in both cases, the lesions were minimal in severity (Figures 7C and 7D). None of the TACI-Fc-treated mice exhibited lesions consistent with glomerulonephritis (Figures 7E and 7F). Eighty-six percent (6/7) of cyclophosphamide-treated mice were histologically normal or exhibited only minimal multifocal glomerular hypercellularity, consistent with the expected effects of this treatment (Figures 7A–7H). Scoring of glomerular lesions demonstrated a significant benefit from TACI-Fc and cyclophosphamide treatment in H&E-stained sections but not from BAFFR-Fc, whereas PAS staining showed a trend toward improvement with TACI-Fc treatment (Figures 7I and 7J). Overall, these findings indicate that renal lesions consistent with LN were less frequent and less severe in mice treated with TACI-Fc compared with the control-Fc group, whereas the effect of BAFFR-Fc was not significant.

Molecular profiling of diseased NZM2328 mouse kidneys revealed broad de-enrichment of inflammatory immune cell populations and cytokines with dual BAFF/APRIL inhibition. To explore kidney tissue inflammation in situ, we carried out IF staining for total immune cells and selected myeloid populations identified through transcriptome analysis (Figure 8; Figure S1). Kidney glomeruli were identified by IgG immune complex deposition, and cellular infiltrates were assessed by the proportion of glomeruli colocalized with immune-specific markers. The percentage of glomeruli with CD45+ immune cells showed a trending decrease with TACI-Fc treatment (p = 0.0588), but BAFFR-Fc or cyclophosphamide therapy had no effect relative to control-Fc (Figures 8A and 8B). Similarly, glomeruli positive for CD68+ monocytes/macrophages were reduced with TACI-Fc (p = 0.0562) and in all but one mouse treated with cyclophosphamide but not BAFFR-Fc (Figures 8C and 8D). However, despite the decrease in the neutrophil gene signature enrichment we observed in kidneys with TACI-Fc treatment, there was no significant difference in the proportion of Ly6G+ glomeruli in any treatment group (Figures S1A and S1B). Fibrosis, identified by excessive extracellular matrix deposition and scarring, is an indicator of tissue injury in chronic kidney disease and IgA nephropathy.15,16 Thus, we also assessed the degree of fibrosis in glomeruli of diseased mice through staining for alpha smooth muscle actin (α-SMA)+ cells, and as with the immune cell infiltrate, the proportion of glomeruli with α-SMA+ cells showed a trending decrease only with TACI-Fc and cyclophosphamide therapy and not BAFFR-Fc (Figures S1C and S1D). Thus, blockade of BAFF alone was insufficient to prevent the development of glomerular inflammation and fibrosis in kidneys of diseased NZM2328 mice.

Discussion

The contribution of autoreactive B cells to the onset and progression of lupus has prompted interest in the development of therapies targeting the cytokines BAFF and APRIL, cytokines that provide critical support for the survival and differentiation of B cells. However, the finding that not all patients respond to BAFF-targeted therapies highlights that more work is needed to identify the best strategy to target the BAFF cytokine and receptor network and maximize clinical benefit on a per-patient basis. To address this, we carried out transcriptome analysis of the spleen and kidney from NZM2328 mice, an established model of human LN,8 following inhibition of BAFF with BAFFR-Fc or dual BAFF/APRIL inhibition with TACI-Fc. This analysis uncovered distinct molecular profiles for BAFF-targeted and BAFF/APRIL-targeted therapy, which indicated that blocking the activity of both cytokines with TACI-Fc was superior to inhibition of BAFF alone in reducing pathogenic B cells and mitigating kidney damage in lupus-prone mice. Histologic analysis of NZM2328 mouse kidneys confirmed the greater benefit of TACI-Fc in suppressing lupus glomerulonephritis.

Selective inhibition of BAFF is accomplished using antibodies against BAFF or inhibition or genetic ablation of BAFF-R, as APRIL does not bind to BAFF-R1. The majority of murine studies have utilized BAFF/BAFF-R knockout mice17,18,19 or BAFF-R inhibition applied for disease prevention or treatment.20,21,22 Overall, these studies reveal that BAFF blockade is more effective at preventing disease than in treating mice after disease onset, although there is substantial heterogeneity between strains.23 In addition, these studies were largely carried out using long-term BAFF inhibition, and those with shorter term anti-BAFF treatment were not carried out in an autoimmune-prone strain.24 Thus, we assessed the effect of short-term, 4-week treatment with BAFF-targeting BAFFR-Fc treatment on the molecular profiles of lupus-affected tissues. We chose this early time point of treatment in an effort to identify the initial pharmacodynamic impact of the agents as opposed to any alteration in gene expression profiles that might arise from subsequent clinical improvement.

BAFF inhibition had minimal impact on gene expression in the spleen but did decrease myeloid cell responses, including neutrophil degranulation, while supporting metabolic pathways and the T cell response. This result is in line with established pro-inflammatory roles for BAFF in supporting monocyte survival and differentiation as well as in aiding myeloid cell recruitment and activation of other immune cells.25 Interestingly, BAFFR-Fc treatment did not reduce B cell transcriptional profiles, GC formation, or splenic PCs in this model. One possible explanation for this is the function of BAFF and BAFF-R in promoting survival during B cell maturation, such that inhibition of BAFF alone may not significantly impact the expanded pool of autoreactive, class-switched B cells present in already diseased mice.26 The pro-inflammatory environment in lupus-prone mice could also promote therapeutic resistance as administration of IFNs has been shown to confer resistance to BAFF inhibition.25 In addition, 4 weeks of treatment may have been insufficient for BAFF inhibition alone to show significant effects on the molecular profiles of diseased NZM2328 mice, as B cell depletion in human clinical trials of BAFF inhibition was noted only after 8 weeks of treatment.27 Indeed, previous studies in lupus-prone mice indicated that 4 weeks of inhibition of BAFF did not have a significant effect on B cell populations, whereas BAFF inhibition may have a more acute effect in non-autoimmune mouse models.22,24 Additional studies with multiple preclinical mouse models and treatment regimens are needed to evaluate the pharmacokinetic impact of BAFF inhibition in greater detail.

In previous studies, ablated expression of APRIL had no impact on lupus disease progression and renal pathology beyond the effects of ablation of BAFF alone in NZM2328 mice.28 Thus, TACI-Fc treatment was used for dual inhibition of BAFF and APRIL as a way to broaden the impact on B cell biology and enhance the therapeutic impact on autoimmune pathology. In contrast to targeting BAFF alone, dual BAFF/APRIL inhibition resulted in a robust depletion of gene signatures related to B cells and, in particular PCs, a finding that aligns with the expression patterns of BAFF-R and TACI in peripheral B cell subsets. BAFF-R is highly expressed on immature B cells but is downregulated on GC B cells and absent on PCs, whereas TACI is expressed by all mature peripheral B cells including PCs.6 Previous studies utilizing TACI-Ig for BAFF/APRIL inhibition have also demonstrated the depletion of PCs in multiple lupus-prone mouse strains.20,29,30 However, this impact was largely restricted to short-lived, largely IgM-producing PCs and not long-lived IgG- or IgA-producing PCs.

As TACI-Fc but not BAFFR-Fc therapy was effective in depleting molecular signatures of PCs, we assessed the impact of these treatments on the production of individual class-switched immunoglobulin isotypes and anti-nuclear antibodies. It has been well established that BAFF-R inhibition as well as complete deficiency of BAFF or BAFF-R is insufficient to inhibit serum immunoglobulin and autoantibody production, and we found similar results with BAFFR-Fc treatment, which had no impact on serum IgG or IgA.17,18,19,20,21,22 Our results also confirm previous studies that found BAFF/APRIL-targeted therapy had little effect on IgG autoantibody production,28 but, notably, TACI-Fc treatment did significantly decrease total IgA and IgA autoantibody production in NZM2328 mice. This result suggests that dual BAFF/APRIL inhibition is required to target IgG and IgA PCs. The latter effect is supported by evidence that class switching to IgA is dependent on the interaction of APRIL with TACI.31 In addition, as aberrant IgA production is closely linked with the development of renal diseases, including IgA nephropathy,32 and may contribute to SLE tissue pathology, it is likely that TACI-Fc treatment would also have additional clinical benefit in LN patients.

Analysis of kidney gene expression and clinical phenotype revealed that while all treatments significantly decreased proteinuria, a standard indicator of kidney damage, only dual BAFF/APRIL inhibition decreased pro-inflammatory gene profiles in treated NZM2328 mice. TACI-Fc decreased B cell signatures as well as those of M1 macrophages and inflammatory cytokines, including IFN, although immune complex deposition in the kidney was not routinely reduced. Although BAFFR-Fc therapy did not alter inflammatory transcripts, it did preserve kidney function in other aspects. BAFF inhibition increased expression of kidney tissue cell differentiation pathways, and both BAFFR-Fc and TACI-Fc treatment supported metabolic gene signatures in the kidney. In addition, TACI-Fc treatment increased expression of kidney tubule cell gene signatures. Selective reduction in inflammatory cell populations and fibrosis with TACI-Fc but not BAFFR-Fc treatment was also confirmed by staining of the kidney tissue.

Based on our previous work in NZM2328 mice, in which de-enrichment of metabolism and kidney tissue cell gene signatures was found to be indicative of more severe disease,8 these results suggest that BAFF-targeted therapy may alleviate kidney pathology independently of its impact on inflammatory cell infiltration in the kidney. The clinical effect of BAFF blockade in the kidney appears to vary based on the murine model and disease stage nephritis.20,21,29 Therefore, additional studies comparing BAFFR-Fc and TACI-Fc therapy would provide further clarification of the exact contribution of BAFF and APRIL to LN pathogenesis.

The human anti-BAFF mAb belimumab has been shown to provide benefit to some patients with generalized lupus and LN when combined with standard-of-care therapy, but it has not undergone extensive preclinical testing.33,34 Our study provides evidence that the clinical benefit of selective BAFF inhibition is limited and that broader B cell-directed therapy through dual BAFF/APRIL inhibition is a promising strategy to maximize clinical efficacy for lupus patients. In fact, several drugs targeting BAFF and/or APRIL have been developed and show promise for the treatment of autoimmune and kidney diseases, including atacicept,35,36 telitacicept,37 and sibeprenlimab.38 This work highlights the necessity for further preclinical testing of BAFF/APRIL inhibitors in different murine models as a means to understand their method of action in greater detail and to identify human lupus patients who would benefit most from these therapeutic agents.

Limitations of the study

The limitations of this study include those related to the genetic mouse model and study design we utilized, which can be addressed by expanding this work in future studies. This study was carried out in the NZM2328 lupus-prone mouse strain, which is characterized by the development of severe LN; thus, the base phenotype of this model may not be representative for all lupus patients, including those without nephritis. Thus, repeating this study in additional genetic mouse strains could better encompass the effects of anti-BAFF therapy on heterogeneous presentations of lupus in human patients. Also, only female mice were used in this study, as, similar to human lupus patients, the majority of whom are female, female NZM2328 mice are predisposed to earlier and more severe disease. However, this could also lead to a female bias in interpreting the results of anti-BAFF treatment, and this should be considered when translating preclinical results to human patients. Furthermore, side-by-side comparison of short- and long-term treatment protocols in NZM2328 and other mouse strains is warranted to assess whether BAFF blockade alone would prove more efficacious over time, as well as whether dual BAFF/APRIL inhibition could revert the state of diseased mice to that of pre-diseased, healthy mice.

Experimental model and study participant details

NZM2328 mouse

This strain is a backcross of (NZB x NZW)F1 mice with NZW mice and develops progressive lupus-like disease including glomerulonephritis. Female mice, which are predisposed to earlier and more severe disease, that were 3–6 months of age with detectable proteinuria indicative of kidney pathology were used in this study. All mice were bred and maintained in the specific-pathogen free animal facilities at the University of Virginia and euthanized by CO2 inhalation, followed by cervical dislocation, with the approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee protocol #3506 and were used in compliance with the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animals Care policies.

Method details

Mice and treatments

NZM2328 mice were produced by extended backcrosses of (NZB x NZW)F1 mice with NZW mice, and provided by the University of Virginia.7 Female NZM2328 mice were injected intraperitoneally with 200 μg/dose of IgG2c isotype Control-Fc, BAFFR-Fc or TACI-Fc fusion proteins (Merck) 3 times per week, starting at 3–6 months of age, after confirmation of at least 15 mg/dL proteinuria based on duplicate semiquantitative urine dipstick measurements. BAFFR-Fc and TACI-Fc fusion proteins were generated by fusing the extracellular domains of mouse BAFFR or TACI to a mouse IgG2c Fc region engineered with Fc-silencing mutations that abrogate Fc receptor binding and complement activation, as previously described.41,42 The capacity of the reagents to competitively inhibit BAFF and BAFF/APRIL, respectively, has previous been reported.5,43,44 A fourth group was injected with cyclophosphamide monohydrate (25 mg/kg, Sigma C0768) once per week. Mice were assigned randomly to the different treatment groups. After 4 weeks of treatment, mice were anesthetized and perfused through their hearts with 30 mL of cold PBS. Kidneys and spleens were snap frozen for immunofluorescence and RNA isolation. Before (t = 0 weeks) and after (t = 4 weeks) treatments, a sample of blood and urine were analyzed for ELISA and proteinuria, respectively.

Proteinuria

Urine protein levels were assessed at baseline (t = 0 weeks) and after treatment cessation (t = 4 weeks) using proteinuria test strips (Uristix, Siemens, 2184). The test strips provide a semiquantitative readout corresponding to six color-coded categories: negative, 15, 30, 100, 300, and ≥2000 mg/dL. Proteinuria values were recorded as the mean of two consecutive measurements.

ELISA

IgG and IgA autoantibodies specific for chromatin, total histones, ssDNA, and dsDNA were measured by ELISA as previously described.45 Briefly, 96-well plates were coated overnight at 4°C with 10 μg/mL of purified histone, dsDNA, ssDNA or chromatin in 0.1M bicarbonate-carbonate buffer (pH 9.6). Plates were blocked with 1% BSA in PBS for 2h at room temperature, followed by incubation with serum samples diluted 1:100 and 1:300 for 2h at room temperature. After washing with 0.05% Tween 20 in PBS, plates were incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Southern Biotech, 1030-05) or anti-mouse IgA (Southern Biotech, 1040-05). Signal was developed using TMB substrate and the reaction was stopped with 2N H2SO4. Optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm. For total IgA ELISA, plates were coated with anti-mouse IgA capture antibody (BD 556969), and serum was diluted 1:3000 and 1:9000. Detection was performed using HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgA (Southern Biotech, 1040-05), with purified mouse IgA (BD 553476) used to generate a standard curve. For total IgG, plates were coated with anti-mouse IgG capture antibody (Southern Biotech, 1030-01), and serum was diluted1:50,000 and 1:150,000. Detection was performed using HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Southern Biotech, 1030-05), and a standard curve was generated using purified mouse IgG (Southern Biotech, 0107-01). Sera was tested before and after treatments.

Histology and glomerulonephritis scoring

Kidneys were fixed in AZF fixative (Newcomber supply, Part # 1009A) for 3 days, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin blocks were sectioned at 4–5 μm thickness using a microtome, mounted onto glass slides and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to assess overall renal architecture and inflammatory cell infiltration. Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining was performed to evaluate glomerular basement membrane integrity, mesangial matrix expansion, and capillary loop morphology according to standard protocols. Histopathologic evaluation was performed on a single kidney cross section per mouse. All slides were examined by a board-certified veterinary comparative anatomic pathologist. Glomerular lesions consistent with glomerulonephritis were identified based on features including glomerular hypercellularity, leukocyte infiltration, mesangial expansion, and structural distortion of capillary loops. Glomerulonephritis severity was graded semi-quantitatively on a scale of 1–4 as follows: 1 = minimal, 2 = mild, 3 = moderate, and 4 = marked. For each mouse, an overall score was assigned based on the predominant lesion severity and distribution across the examined sections.

Immunofluorescence

Frozen kidney sections (6-μm) were fixed with 100% ethanol:acetone (1:1) for 10 min at −20°C, and stained with the following reagents for the detection of immune complexes: goat anti-mouse C3-FITC (Cappel, 55510), goat anti-mouse IgG-FITC (Southern Biotech, 1036-02), or goat anti-mouse IgA (Southern Biotech, 1040-01), followed by biotinylated anti-goat IgG (Vector, BA9500) and Neutralite Avidin-Texas Red (Southern Biotech, 7200-07). For the evaluation of the cellular infiltrate and fibrosis, kidney sections were blocked using the Avidin/biotin kit (Vector, SP-2001) and incubated with the following antibodies: rat anti-mouse CD45 (biotinylated, clone 30-F11, ebioscience 13-0451-82), rat anti-mouse CD68 (clone FA-11, Biolegend 137001), rat anti-mouse Ly6G (biotinylated, clone RB6-8C5, ebioscience 13-5931-82), and rabbit anti-mouse alpha smooth muscle actin (a-SMA, Abcam, ab5694). For the detection of germinal centers, spleen sections (6-μm) were fixed and incubated with rat anti-mouse IgD (conjugated with FITC, clone 11-26c, eBioscience 11-5993-85), and Peanut agglutinin (PNA, biotinylated, Vector, B-1075). IgD signal was amplified using a goat anti-FITC antibody (Vector, SP-0601) followed by a chicken anti-goat IgG conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, A21467). For the co-localization of IgA and CD138, spleen sections were incubated with a goat anti-mouse IgA (Southern Biotech, 1040-01), and a rat anti-mouse CD138 (biotinylated, clone 281-2, BD biosciences 553713). Secondary antibodies included a chicken anti-goat IgG conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, A21467) and biotinylated F(ab’)2 mouse anti-rat IgG (Jackson Immunoresearch, 212-066-168), followed by Neutralite Avidin-Texas Red. All sections were counterstained with Hoechst (Invitrogen, 62249) and imaged using an Axio Imager 2 with Apotome (Zeiss) and a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM900). Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was determined in ImageJ using 2–4 fields per mouse. For a-SMA, fluorescence intensity was measured only in blood vessel-free areas. The percentage of glomeruli containing CD45+, CD68+, Ly6G+ cells, or α-SMA signal was determined by counting the number of glomeruli with staining over the total number of glomeruli observed in one kidney section. Glomeruli were identified with an anti-mouse IgG [immune complexes] conjugated with FITC as described above. All the glomeruli showed immune complex deposition as determined by co-localization with CD31 (data not shown). Germinal center numbers were determined in 2–3 spleen sections per mouse by counting the number of PNA+ clusters surrounded by IgD+ cells. IgA MFI was determined as described above. The percentage of IgA+CD138+ cells was determined by counting the number of double-positive cells over the total number of IgA+ cells in each field (2–4 images per spleen).

RNA-seq

Total RNA was isolated from freshly harvested mouse spleens and kidneys using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, including on-column DNase I digestion to remove genomic DNA. Tissue samples were homogenized in lysis buffer using a bead mill homogenizer prior to extraction. RNA quantity and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and RNA integrity was confirmed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer. Samples with an RNA integrity number ≥7.0 were included for sequencing. Qualified RNA samples were shipped on dry ice to Novogene (Sacramento, CA), where poly(A)-enriched mRNA libraries were prepared. Libraries were sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus Series platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads (PE150).

Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA)

The R/Bioconductor package GSVA46 (v2.2) was used as a non-parametric, unsupervised method to estimate variation in the enrichment of pre-defined gene sets (Table S1) in RNA-seq data from treated NZM2328 mice as previously described.8 Input for GSVA was a matrix of log2 expression values for all samples and curated gene signatures listed in Table S1. GSVA enrichment scores were calculated on a per sample basis for each gene signature and were scaled across all samples to values between −1 (negative enrichment) and +1 (positive enrichment). The browser-based gene set enrichment analysis tool EnrichR39 was used for GO term and REACTOME pathway enrichments.

Linear regression analysis

Linear regression analysis between plasma cell GSVA scores and clinical feature or log2 expression values was carried out using GraphPad Prism Software (v10) as previously described.8

Quantification and statistical analysis

Differential expression analysis from spleen and kidney RNA-seq data was carried out using the R/Bioconductor package DESeq2. p-values for GO term and REACTOME pathway enrichment were determined using a Fisher’s exact test through the gene enrichment analysis web tool Enrichr.39 All other statistics were determined using GraphPad Prism software (v10). Comparisons across treatment groups were calculated using Brown Forsythe and Welch’s ANOVA for GSVA and non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA for autoantibody and image quantification data followed by Dunnett’s T3 multiple comparisons test. Data shown are mean ± SEM. Significance was defined as p values ≤ 0.05. Details on statistical tests, number of animals and sections (n) for each experiment, and p-value thresholds can be found in the corresponding figure legends.
