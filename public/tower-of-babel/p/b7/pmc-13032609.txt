Color and translucency changes induced by try-in media in bleach-shade CAD/CAM ceramic discs: an in-vitro study
Authors: Alaa Al Haddad, Mikel Alrabadi, Othman Sa’deh, Noor H Ismail, Yazan Hassona
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study evaluated the optical influence of try-in media (distilled water, glycerin, and commercial try-in pastes) on ultrathin (0.5-mm) CAD/CAM bleach-shade ceramic discs, focusing on CIEDE2000 color difference (ΔE₀₀), translucency parameter (TP₀₀; ΔE₀₀ between white- and black-backed measurements), and device-reported shade outputs across different ceramic systems.MethodsForty-four 0.5-mm CAD/CAM ceramic discs were fabricated from Celtra Duo (BL2-LT, BL3-LT), VITA Suprinity (0M1-HT), and VITA Enamic (0M1-HT). Each specimen was measured over standardized white and black backgrounds using distilled water, glycerin, eight commercial try-in pastes, or no medium (control). Lab* values and VITA Classic/3D-Master shade outputs were recorded with a dental spectrophotometer under D65/2° conditions; a custom jig standardized specimen positioning and probe angulation. Repeated measurements (n = 5) were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models. Clinical interpretation used ΔE₀₀ perceptibility/acceptability (≈ 0.8/1.8) and translucency thresholds (TPT₀₀=0.62; TAT₀₀=2.62).ResultsRelative to the no-media condition, both water and glycerin produced large, clinically perceptible color shifts across all ceramics (mean ΔE₀₀ ≈ 3.2–5.6; p < 0.001), while direct water–glycerin differences were smaller (≈ 0.7–1.6). Translucency responses were material dependent: Celtra groups showed minimal ΔTP₀₀ relative to glycerin (typically |ΔTP₀₀| ≤ ~0.3), Suprinity showed a modest reduction ( ≈ − 1 to − 2), and Enamic exhibited pronounced translucency loss ( ≈ − 5 to − 7), frequently exceeding translucency acceptability (|ΔTP₀₀| ≥ 2.62). Only selected Celtra BL2/BL3 conditions with translucent/light try-in pastes approached ΔE₀₀ acceptability (≤ 1.8) on white backgrounds. Device-reported shade agreement sometimes masked clinically unacceptable differences: 11% of 3D-Master “matches” and 28% of Classic “matches” still corresponded to ΔE₀₀ > 1.8, particularly over black backgrounds.ConclusionsTry-in media are not optically neutral for ultrathin bleach-shade ceramics. Water and glycerin both produced clinically visible color changes, and translucency effects depended strongly on ceramic microstructure, with Enamic showing clinically unacceptable masking. Clinically, shade selection should not rely on device-reported shade matches alone; verification with appropriate try-in media and evaluation over both white and dark conditions using continuous metrics (ΔE₀₀, TP₀₀/ΔTP₀₀) is recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-07972-w.Keywords: Ceramic discs, Try-in pastes, CIEDE2000, Translucency, Bleach shades, CAD/CAM ceramics, Spectrophotometer, Color thresholds

Background

Porcelain laminate veneers have become a fundamental element of aesthetic rehabilitation due to their ability to conserve enamel, facilitate predictable bonding, and demonstrate high long-term survival rates when case selection and procedural protocols are appropriately followed [1–3]. Modern clinical practices increasingly favor minimally invasive and no-prep approaches, utilizing veneer thicknesses ranging from 0.2 to 0.5 mm, which preserve the integrity of the tooth structure while still providing durable aesthetic outcomes under routine functional conditions [2, 3]. The advent of chairside computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technology has further enhanced the planning and delivery process, enabling the fabrication of ultrathin restorations and same-day procedures, all while maintaining control over optical qualities and contouring [4].

At these ultrathin thicknesses, however, color control becomes challenging. The final appearance is not governed solely by the ceramic shade but by a complex interplay among ceramic thickness/translucency, the underlying substrate color, and the luting resin (shade and thickness) [4–6]. Experimental and clinical evidence demonstrates that darker substrates and higher translucency increase the influence of the background on the final color, whereas lowering translucency and optimizing veneer thickness enhance masking and value control—trade-offs that are especially pertinent when aiming for high-value (“bleach”) outcomes [4–6].

As the luting medium significantly influences the optical outcome in thin veneers, manufacturers offer resin cements in various shades along with try-in pastes designed to preview the post-cementation result. Recent research confirms that the shade of the try-in, the cement shade, and the restoration design each impact color difference calculated by the CIEDE2000 formula (ΔE₀₀), as well as value and chroma. Nonetheless, the concordance between try-in pastes and their corresponding cured cements is notably dependent on material and thickness, and is not universally one-to-one. Therefore, try-in pastes can assist in selection, though clinicians should anticipate variability depending on substrates, ceramic systems, and specific regions (incisal versus cervical) [7–10].

A related body of research has refined the visual thresholds used to interpret color differences. In dentistry, the 50:50% perceptibility threshold (PT) and acceptability threshold (AT) using CIEDE2000 typically center around ΔE₀₀ ≈ 0.8 (PT) and ≈ 1.8 (AT), with emerging data indicating that thresholds can vary with hue, chroma, and sample type [11–13]. Dedicated whiteness indices and their perceptibility thresholds have also been established to better quantify shifts in high-value, bleach-type shades [14, 15]. These thresholds serve as clinically meaningful benchmarks for determining whether try-in–cement differences or substrate-related changes will be noticed or accepted by patients and dentists.

Despite this progress, a practical gap persists in the area of ultrathin, high-value veneers: limited investigations have directly examined how various try-in pastes influence perceived value/whiteness and ΔE₀₀ in relation to the intended bleached ceramic shade across common ceramic types and clinically relevant background conditions. Furthermore, evidence remains scarce regarding whether “one-shade” jumps within a bleached series are maintained or diminished once try-in mediums and substrates are introduced, and whether these behaviors differ among various ceramics even when using the same shade [4, 7–10]. Addressing these questions possesses significant curricular and clinical implications for shade communication and chairside decision-making.

In clinical settings, practitioners frequently employ wetting agents such as distilled water or glycerin during the veneer try-in stage, assuming these media provide a neutral preview of the final bonded outcome. However, emerging evidence suggests that even small variations in intervening media can alter the perceived color and translucency of thin ceramics, potentially influencing shade selection and cement choice [7, 8, 10–12]. Despite their routine use, the optical neutrality of water and glycerin has not been systematically evaluated against reference conditions, raising questions about their reliability as clinical surrogates.

This study assessed the optical effects of try-in media on ultrathin CAD/CAM ceramic discs, with specific emphasis on comparing distilled water and glycerin to a no-media reference. Changes in color difference (ΔE₀₀), translucency parameter (TP₀₀; defined as the ΔE₀₀ between white- and black-background measurements), and device shade outputs were quantified to determine whether these wetting agents provide an optically neutral chairside preview. The study also examined whether responses differ between adjacent bleach shades and among ceramic systems of the same nominal shade. This work provides a comprehensive, directly benchmarked assessment of water and glycerin against a dry (no-media) baseline in ultrathin bleach-shade CAD/CAM ceramics, alongside commercial try-in pastes, using standardized multi-background spectrophotometric measurements.

The study hypothesized that try-in medium and background would significantly influence ΔE₀₀ and ΔTP₀₀, and that the magnitude of these effects would be ceramic-dependent. The null hypotheses are as follows: (H0₁) the try-in medium does not influence the final optical outcome (ΔE₀₀, TP₀₀, optical thresholds) in relation to the intended ceramic shade; (H0₂) the use of distilled water or glycerin as wetting agents does not produce significant differences in ΔE₀₀, ΔTP₀₀, or shade tab outputs when compared with the no-media reference, across ceramic groups and backgrounds; (H0₃) adjacent bleached shades show no difference in their susceptibility to try-in–induced shifts; and (H0₄) the ceramic material class does not modify the relationship between try-in selection and the final perceived color.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

This in-vitro investigation was conducted entirely on laboratory-fabricated specimens without the involvement of human participants or patient-identifiable data. Formal ethical approval and informed consent were not required. The study adhered to international standards for in-vitro dental research and reporting. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Study design

This in-vitro experimental study evaluated the effect of try-in media on the optical properties of ultrathin CAD/CAM ceramic discs seated on standardized zirconia backing. The primary outcome was the CIEDE2000 color difference (ΔE₀₀) [16], between each try-in condition and baseline (no media, water, or glycerin). Secondary outcomes included changes in L*, a*, b* coordinates, the translucency parameter (TP₀₀, ΔTP₀₀) [17], and spectrophotometer-reported shade outputs (Fig. 1). ΔE₀₀ and TP₀₀ are the primary metrics emphasized in this study, as these are the most extensively validated for comprehensive assessment of color and translucency in dental materials research. Special emphasis was placed on distilled water and glycerin as wetting agents commonly used for chairside preview, which were compared directly with the no-media baseline. Additional analyses evaluated whether shade differences within the same ceramic system or across different microstructure materials with the same nominal shade were maintained under try-in conditions.

Materials and specimens preparation

Four types of CAD/CAM ceramics, distinguished by varying bleach shades, translucency levels, and microstructures, were utilized to fabricate forty-four ultrathin standardized discs (Ø 10 mm × 0.50 ± 0.02 mm). A monolithic zirconia puck (IPS e.max ZirCAD LT, shade A2) was selected to fabricate forty-four baking discs (Ø 13 mm × 2.00 ± 0.02 mm), simulating a common, moderately discolored tooth foundation [5]. This provided a standardized and clinically relevant challenging backing for bleach-shade ceramics. All materials, along with their respective manufacturers, are comprehensively listed in Table 1.

Materials used in the study

For each ceramic group (Celtra Duo BL2-LT, BL3-LT; VITA Suprinity 0M1-HT; VITA Enamic 0M1-HT), 11 disc specimens were CAD/CAM-milled to a standardized thickness of 0.50 mm. VITA Suprinity discs subsequently underwent a crystallization firing cycle (Programat P310, Ivoclar Vivadent) according to the manufacturer’s protocol before final polishing. All specimens were inspected, ultrasonically cleaned, and stored dry. Thickness was verified with a digital caliper. Zirconia backing discs were milled from the same puck to ensure uniformity and sintered per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Testing protocol and data acquisition

Eleven testing assemblies (n = 11 per ceramic group) were prepared, each consisting of one ceramic disc specimen and one zirconia backing disc. A total of eight try-in pastes (3M: Trans, A1, A3, B0.5, WOT; Allcem: Trans, A1, WO) and two wetting media (distilled water, glycerin) were evaluated. A no-media condition served as the control.

For each test, a uniform film of the medium was applied to the ceramic disc’s intaglio surface using a microbrush. The disc was positioned centrally on the backing disc using a custom 3D-printed jig, with light finger pressure to remove excess. A pilot study confirmed the jig material did not interfere with measurements, showing no significant difference in L*a*b* values of a standard tile with and without the jig (p > 0.05).

Color measurements were performed using a VITA Easyshade Compact spectrophotometer (VITA Zahnfabrik; model DEASYCHP) operated in VITA Classic/3D-Master mode. Because this is a contact-type dental spectrophotometer, measurements were acquired with the Ø5-mm probe held perpendicular (≈ 90°) to the specimen surface and centered using a positioning jig to minimize edge-loss and placement variability. The Easyshade reports CIE L*a*b* values computed under CIE standard illuminant D65 with the 2° standard observer, consistent with ISO/TR 28642:2016 guidance for dental color measurement [18–20]. The device was calibrated to the manufacturer’s reference standard at the start of each measurement series and re-checked after every 10 readings, or whenever prompted by the device. Readings were obtained against calibrated white (L* = 95.7, a* = −0.1, b* = 2.4) and matte black (L* = 3.9, a* = 0.4, b* = −0.8) ceramic tiles placed beneath the assembly. Both tiles were matte (non-glossy) to reduce specular reflection; the measured surface faced upward, and the tiles were placed flush beneath the backing disc, centered under the measurement area.

For each assembly, five repeated readings were obtained with reapplication of the medium between measures. Specimens were cleaned with distilled water and 70% ethanol and air-dried between trials to prevent contamination or carryover. All measurements were taken in a light-controlled enclosure to reduce ambient light interference, utilizing the custom jig. A schematic representation of the custom jig employed in this study is provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was ΔE₀₀ analysis across baselines, evaluating color differences between each try-in medium and its reference condition under both standardized white and black backgrounds. Secondary outcomes included the translucency parameter (TP₀₀) and its change relative to baseline (ΔTP₀₀), enabling assessment of how try-ins influenced light transmission.

Color differences were calculated using the CIEDE2000 formula (ΔE00), which adjusts for human perceptual non-uniformity by weighting lightness, chroma, and hue. TP₀₀ was calculated as the CIEDE2000 color difference (ΔE₀₀) between the specimen measured over the white background and the same specimen measured over the black background [16, 17]. A threshold-oriented analysis was performed using established 50:50% perceptibility (PT ≈ 0.8) and acceptability (AT ≈ 1.8) thresholds for ΔE₀₀ [11, 13].

For translucency differences, 50:50% thresholds were applied using TPT₀₀ = 0.62 (perceptibility) and TAT₀₀ = 2.62 (acceptability) [17]. Because chairside evaluation depends on detecting small ‘vitality’ changes, primary benchmarking focused on TPT₀₀, while TAT₀₀ was included as a secondary benchmark in the Supplementary analyses. Spectrophotometer-reported shade outputs (VITA Classic and VITA 3D-Master) were also recorded to evaluate agreement with continuous color metrics and highlight potential discrepancies between categorical and numerical data.

Statistical analysis

Data were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Measurement repeatability was confirmed by an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) > 0.95 for five repeated readings per condition. A linear mixed-effects model (LMM) was applied, with try-in medium and background as fixed factors, ceramic as a blocking factor, and specimen as a random intercept. Planned contrasts included Celtra BL2 vs. BL3, Suprinity vs. Enamic, and pairwise try-in comparisons, with Tukey correction for multiplicity. To support interpretation beyond p-values, effect magnitude and uncertainty were summarized as mean differences referenced to glycerin with 95% confidence intervals (CI) (Supplementary Figures S2–S4). For the LMM, fixed-effect magnitude is reported as partial η².

Friedman tests with Conover post-hoc procedures served as non-parametric robustness checks, with Kendall’s W reported as an effect size where applicable. A threshold-oriented analysis was performed using established perceptibility/acceptability thresholds for ΔE₀₀ and a translucency perceptibility threshold (TPT) for ΔTP₀₀. All analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics v26.0 at α = 0.05.

The sample size of eleven specimens per ceramic group was chosen in alignment with prior veneer optical studies [5–8]. A post-hoc power analysis in G*Power 3.1 using observed ΔE₀₀ effect sizes indicated statistical power > 0.80 at α = 0.05, confirming adequacy.

Effect of ceramic shade step on color difference (Celtra BL2 vs. BL3)

A one-shade difference within the same ceramic system (Celtra Duo BL2-LT vs. BL3-LT) remained detectable under try-in conditions. Under the black background, the mean ΔE₀₀ between the two shades was approximately 0.46 units when compared across equivalent try-in media, confirming that subtle shade gradations are preserved even with interposed media.

Effect of ceramic material type on translucency

Absolute translucency (TP₀₀) was highest with glycerin and lowest with the no-media condition for all ceramics (Table 5 ). However, the change in translucency relative to glycerin (ΔTP₀₀) was strongly material-dependent (Table 6 A). Celtra Duo specimens showed minimal translucency change with most colored try-ins (|ΔTP₀₀| ≤ ~0.3). In stark contrast, VITA Enamic (a PICN hybrid ceramic) exhibited pronounced translucency loss (ΔTP₀₀ ≈ − 5 to − 7), while VITA Suprinity (a zirconia-reinforced glass ceramic) showed a more moderate reduction (ΔTP₀₀ ≈ − 1 to − 2). Statistical support and effect sizes

Measurement repeatability was high (ICC > 0.95). LMM effect sizes indicated a large ceramic-block contribution for ΔE₀₀ (η²p ≈ 0.45) and a medium-to-large try-in effect for TP₀₀ (η²p ≈ 0.25), consistent with the CI-based summaries (Supplementary Figures S2–S3) and threshold benchmarking (Fig. 3; Supplementary Figure S4).

Discussion

This study evaluated the optical impact of try-in media on ultrathin bleach-shade CAD/CAM ceramic discs. The findings reject the null hypotheses, showing that (1) try-in media modify optical outcomes; (2) distilled water and glycerin are not optically neutral relative to a dry baseline; (3) adjacent bleach shades remain perceptibly distinct under try-in conditions; and (4) ceramic type strongly governs translucency response. Collectively, these results have direct implications for chairside shade evaluation in ultrathin, highly translucent restorations.

A clinically important finding is the non-neutrality of wetting agents that are often used to “preview” shade. Although colored try-in pastes have been studied, the optical behavior of water and glycerin—particularly for bleach shades and ultrathin ceramics—has not been consistently benchmarked against a dry baseline. Here, both water and glycerin produced large color shifts versus no media (ΔE₀₀ ≈ 3.2–5.6), well above perceptibility and often beyond acceptability. These shifts refute the practical assumption that either medium provides a neutral preview. A plausible explanation is refractive-index (RI) matching at the ceramic–intaglio interface: replacing air (RI ~ 1.0) with water (RI ~ 1.33) or glycerin (RI ~ 1.47) reduces interfacial scattering and alters the contribution of the underlying substrate to the observed color and translucency [21, 22]. This mechanism is expected to be magnified at 0.5-mm thickness, where interfacial optics exert a large influence on appearance.

Although the water–glycerin difference was smaller (≈ 0.7–1.6 ΔE₀₀), the choice of baseline remains clinically consequential. If shade approval is performed under a dry condition, subsequent wetting with either agent is expected to yield perceptible—and not infrequently unacceptable—changes. From a practical standpoint, glycerin may be preferred as a standardized wet reference, because it produced a consistent wet interface against which colored try-in pastes can be compared, supporting more reproducible chairside evaluation. This aligns with prior work showing that interposed media can materially influence perceived outcomes in thin, translucent ceramic restorations [7, 8, 23].

Beyond wetting agents, the data clarify how ceramic properties modulate the effects of colored try-in media. Notably, a one-shade step within the same system (Celtra BL2 vs. BL3) remained perceptible under try-in conditions (ΔE₀₀ ≈ 0.46 under black backgrounds), indicating that engineered shade gradations are not fully masked by interposed media. However, translucency behavior was strongly material-dependent. When referenced to glycerin, Celtra showed minimal translucency change across try-ins (typically |ΔTP₀₀| ≤ ~0.3), Suprinity showed a modest reduction ( ≈ − 1 to − 2), whereas Enamic exhibited pronounced translucency loss ( ≈ − 5 to − 7). Interpreted against clinically anchored thresholds, many Celtra/Suprinity conditions remained within translucency perceptibility (TPT₀₀ = 0.62) and acceptability (TAT₀₀ = 2.62), while Enamic frequently exceeded translucency acceptability, consistent with marked masking. This pattern supports the view that the ceramic microstructure, particularly the polymer–infiltrated ceramic network (PICN), can amplify interactions with resin-based media, altering light transport and apparent vitality. Additionally, it indicates that a try-in paste which functions predictably with one type of ceramic may not do so with another, thereby affecting the perceived vitality and value of the final restoration [24–31].

The threshold analysis and shade outputs converge on a practical warning: categorical shade labels are insufficient. In 11–28% of cases, a device-reported “match” still corresponded to ΔE₀₀ > 1.8, particularly over black backgrounds. Thus, reliance on shade names alone risks overlooking clinically unacceptable differences. For chairside evaluation of ultrathin restorations, a refined approach is supported by the present data: (1) avoid assuming that water is neutral; (2) use a consistent wet reference (e.g., glycerin) when comparing media; (3) evaluate try-in conditions over both light and dark backgrounds to approximate variability in clinical substrates; and (4) interpret outcomes using continuous metrics (ΔE₀₀ with PT/AT and ΔTP₀₀ with TPT₀₀/TAT₀₀) rather than shade labels alone.

This investigation has strengths including standardized ultrathin geometry, use of a positioning jig for repeatable probe angulation and specimen centering, evaluation of multiple clinically available media, and interpretation using contemporary metrics and clinically grounded thresholds [11–13, 17]. Limitations include the in‑vitro design and the use of flat discs, which do not reproduce anatomical curvature or surface texture and may not capture edge‑loss effects encountered clinically. Because the positioning support was yellow and measurements were obtained with a contact-type spectrophotometer, surrounding colors could have influenced readings via edge‑loss/stray‑light effects despite probe centering; this should be considered when extrapolating to clinical veneers. The background tiles were matte; surface gloss was not quantified. In addition, only one substrate shade was evaluated; broader backing variability may further modulate the observed effects. Finally, while whiteness indices (e.g., WID) can be informative for bleach shades, they were not analyzed in the present work and may be incorporated in future studies for a more focused assessment of whiteness changes [14, 15].

Future research should investigate the correlation between try-in pastes and their corresponding cured resin cements using the same methodology [32–34]. Studies employing anatomically shaped veneers on extracted teeth with varying substrate shades would enhance clinical translatability [35]. Additionally, evaluating the efficacy of pre-heated composite as a try-in/luting agent for such ultrathin restorations is a promising clinical avenue [36–39].
