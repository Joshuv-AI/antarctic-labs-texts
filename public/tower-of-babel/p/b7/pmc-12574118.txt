Mental health literacy among populations in and from war and armed conflict zones: a narrative review
Authors: Fareeda Abo-Rass, Ora Nakash, Hanan AboJabel, Yoshitaka Nishikawa, Bizu Gelaye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMental health literacy (MHL) plays a key role in promoting mental health outcomes and help-seeking behaviors, making it a critical component of public mental health strategies. While MHL has been widely studied in general populations, little is known about the concept among individuals living in war and conflict zones and refugees displaced from these settings, despite their high burden of mental health challenges. This narrative review aims to collect, synthesize, and summarize the available literature on MHL among individuals residing in ongoing war or armed conflict zones and refugees originating from these regions.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, we conducted a systematic search of Medline, PsycINFO, CINAHL, and Web of Science for studies published between January 1997 and December 2024. We included studies that examined MHL using an established framework, yielding a final sample of 35 studies. Data were extracted and synthesized thematically using a narrative approach, focusing on five core areas: study characteristics, population characteristics, measures and frameworks, MHL levels and correlates, and discussions of war and conflict contexts. Findings were analyzed separately for populations residing in conflict zones and for refugees or immigrants from those zones.ResultsTwenty-six studies focused on populations residing in conflict zones and nine on refugees or immigrants from such areas. Most studies employed quantitative cross-sectional designs and used standard MHL tools. Findings indicated moderate-to-high MHL levels among populations in conflict zones and low-to-moderate levels among refugee groups. Common correlates of higher MHL included younger age, female gender, higher educational attainment, and prior use of mental health services. However, most studies did not integrate war-related experiences into their analytical frameworks.ConclusionsThis review highlights a critical gap in the literature: although conflict-affected populations face unique mental health challenges, existing studies often assess MHL using frameworks developed in the global north and non-conflict settings and without incorporating the context of war or displacement. There is an urgent need to reconceptualize MHL frameworks and tools to reflect the lived experiences, needs, and sociopolitical realities of individuals in crisis settings. Context-sensitive approaches are essential for informing relevant interventions and improving mental health outcomes in these vulnerable populations.

Background

Mental health literacy (MHL) was first defined by Jorm et al. [1] as “knowledge and beliefs about mental disorders that aid their recognition, management, or prevention” [1]. Based on this definition, Jorm [2] conceptualized the MHL framework as comprising six dimensions: the ability to recognize disorders; knowledge of risk factors and causes; knowledge of self-treatment; knowledge of available professional help; knowledge of where to seek information; and attitudes that promote recognition and appropriate help-seeking behaviors. Since Jorm et al.‘s seminal work, researchers have expanded upon the concept of MHL to include additional dimensions and perspectives. For example, Kutcher et al. [3] emphasized the importance of promoting positive mental health, reducing stigma, and enhancing help-seeking efficacy. Similarly, O’Connor and Casey [4], through the development of the widely used Mental Health Literacy Scale (MHLS), highlighted the significance of recognizing mental disorders, providing appropriate support, and accessing reliable information. Yet, despite these important contributions, the conceptualization and measurement of MHL remain rooted in Western psychiatric frameworks, which assume a universal understanding of mental health and its indicators of literacy.

Although the concept has evolved, most definitions continue to emphasize the integration of knowledge, attitudes, and help-seeking behaviors related to mental health, with increasing recognition of MHL’s significance for individual and community-level outcomes [5]. Reflecting this, MHL has attracted growing academic and practical attention and is now widely recognized as a core component of public health strategies, with an important role in prevention and in promoting mental well-being and improving mental health outcomes across diverse populations. Research has consistently demonstrated that individuals with high MHL are more likely to engage in positive help-seeking behaviors, participate in health-promoting activities, and utilize mental health services; MHL has also been linked to better quality of life and lower stigma and psychological distress [6–10]. While these associations are well documented across various populations, primarily in the Western world where most studies have been conducted, there remains limited knowledge about MHL among specific vulnerable groups, particularly individuals living in war or armed conflict zones, and those who have been displaced or migrated from such contexts.

The number of war zones and armed conflicts varies over time, but current data reveal a widespread and persistent pattern of global violence driven by both wars and political conflicts. In 2024, the Armed Conflict Location & Event Data Project (ACLED) [11] identified 24 regions experiencing war or politically motivated armed conflict, with rising political violence events by 15% compared to the previous year. In addition, the current global migration landscape highlights the profound impact of such conflicts on population displacement. Each year, millions of individuals are forced to flee their homes, either across international borders or within their own countries. In 2024, the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees [12] reported that at least 42.7 million people were forcibly displaced worldwide due to persecution, conflict, violence, or human rights violations. The majority of these refugees originate from countries experiencing ongoing armed conflict and political instability. Notably, over 6 million refugees have fled Syria, approximately 6 million from Afghanistan, and more than 5 million from Ukraine [12].

Multiple studies have underscored the profound impact of living in war zones or areas affected by armed conflict on mental health, particularly among vulnerable populations such as women, children, and the elderly. These populations consistently exhibit higher rates of mental health disorders, such as anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), compared to those living in non-conflict settings [13–15]. These studies highlight the widespread lack of access to mental health care in conflict-affected areas and underscore the urgent need for context-sensitive interventions, calling for greater attention to the psychological consequences of wars and conflicts [13, 16].

Importantly, research indicates that the psychological impact of armed conflict is not limited to the period of active violence but frequently persists into the post-displacement and resettlement phases. A systematic review by Bogic et al. [17], which analyzed 29 studies involving more than 16,000 refugees, found wide-ranging prevalence rates for mental health disorders: 2.3% to 80% for depression, 4.4% to 86% for PTSD, and 20.3% to 88% for anxiety disorders. While this wide range is due to differences in refugee populations, methodologies, tools, and the quality of studies, the findings nonetheless reflect the enduring mental health burden experienced by individuals displaced by conflict, even years after resettlement.

Acknowledging the high number of war zones and the millions of individuals displaced around the world, and the heavy mental health toll of such circumstances, many studies have sought to examine different aspects of mental health and to develop relevant interventions. However, it is surprising that a significant gap in understanding MHL remains among these populations, given the socio-psycho-political contexts outlined above that might shape and affect their MHL. This narrative review aims to collect, synthesize, and summarize the available literature on MHL among individuals residing in ongoing war or armed conflict zones and refugees/immigrants originating specifically from these regions, as these populations experience distinct environments, direct exposure to conflict on the one hand, and displacement and resettlement challenges on the other, that may shape their mental health literacy in different ways. To address these aims, the review explores five key areas: Study Characteristics; Population Characteristics; Measures and Frameworks; MHL Levels and Correlates; and War and Conflict-Related Discussions.

Methods

Search strategy: This narrative review adhered to a structured search methodology, guided by the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines as detailed by Page et al. [18], to maintain methodological integrity. We conducted systematic searches across four electronic databases: Medline, PsycINFO, CINAHL, and Web of Science in December 2024. The search was restricted to publications from January 1997, when the concept of MHL was originally introduced [1], through December 2024, the date of the search. No ethical approval or informed consent was necessary for this study, as it did not involve human participants or primary data collection.

Titles, abstracts, and keywords were searched using combinations of terms drawn from two key conceptual domains: Category 1: Mental Health Literacy, and Category 2: War, Conflict, and Disaster Zones. The search terms used in this review were as follows: (“Mental health literacy”) and (“Conflict zones” OR War OR “Political violence” OR Occupation* OR “Post trauma” OR “Humanitarian disaster*” OR “Armed conflict*” OR “Military conflict*” OR “War affected areas” OR “War torn regions” OR “intractable conflict” OR displaced*” OR “Refugee*” OR “forced migration” OR Sudan* OR Sahel* OR Yemen* OR Israel* OR Palestine* OR Gaza* OR Ukraine* OR Russia* OR Myanmar* OR Lebanon* OR Syria* OR Congo* OR Somalia* OR Mali* OR Burkina Faso* OR Niger* OR Ethiopia* OR Mozambique* OR Afghanistan* OR Kurd* Or West Bank* OR South Sudan* OR Horn Africa* OR “Central African Republic*”).

The specific search terms related to country names and geographical locations were selected based on the assumption that these areas are currently experiencing ongoing political conflict. The country names were sourced from the ACLED [11], an independent, impartial, international non-profit organization that collects data on violent conflicts worldwide. Additionally, information was obtained from the “List of Ongoing Armed Conflicts” on Wikipedia on the same day in December 2024.

In each database, a combination of synonymous keywords was used alongside Boolean operators, truncation, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), database-specific thesauri, and wildcard symbols where applicable. To enhance the comprehensiveness of the search, we also conducted a manual review of the reference lists of all included studies to identify any additional relevant publications that may not have been retrieved through the initial database search.

Screening and selection process: Inclusion criteria: (a) Studies that examined any population residing in an ongoing war or political armed conflict area or refugees or immigrants originating from these regions; (b) Studies that investigated MHL based on one of the established definitions of the concept or using validated tool; (c) Studies published in English or having abstract in English; (d) Peer-reviewed articles as well as grey literature.

Exclusion criteria

(a) Studies that examined attitudes, beliefs, or knowledge related to mental health but did not specifically address them within a framework of the MHL concept; (b) Studies that examined contexts unrelated to political conflicts, such as conflicts stemming from economic issues, crimes, or non-violent forms of disagreement. (c) protocols without results.

During the initial search stage, all retrieved results from the four databases were imported into Covidence, a systematic review management tool. This process yielded 512 articles, with one additional study manually identified through other sources. After removing duplicates, 252 articles remained. To ensure consistency in applying the eligibility criteria, the two lead authors independently screened the same set of ten titles and abstracts, discussing discrepancies to reach a consensus and calibrate their approach. Following this calibration, in the second stage, the first author screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining articles. A total of 205 articles were excluded because they did not meet one or more of the inclusion criteria, resulting in 47 articles selected for full-text review.

In the third stage, 12 studies were excluded after full-text review for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Reasons for exclusion included the absence of a theoretical framework underpinning the examination of mental health literacy (n = 7), the population not being from conflict-affected regions (n = 2), the article being a protocol without reported results (n = 1), or the use of measures that lacked evidence of validation (n = 2). Ultimately, 35 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the final review.

Data extraction and synthesis: We followed the guidance of Popay et al. [19] and Rodger et al. [20] in analyzing and presenting the findings of this narrative review. First, we categorized the included studies into two groups: (1) residents of war and conflict zones (n = 26), and (2) refugees and immigrants originating from these zones (n = 9). Subsequently, we identified five key topics that align with the objectives of our review: Study Characteristics; Population Characteristics; Measures and Frameworks; Mental Health Literacy Levels and Correlates; War and Conflict-Related Discussions. Within each topic, we systematically extracted and synthesized the data, identifying crucial patterns and summarizing the findings descriptively for each study group.

Study characteristics – limited geographical scope and predominantly quantitative methodsed geographical scope and predominantly quantitative methods The studies included in this review were conducted across three geographical locations experiencing ongoing political conflicts: Ethiopia and Nigeria [21–31], Lebanon [32–34], and Israel [6, 35–45]. A total of 26 studies were reviewed, all of which were peer-reviewed and published in English. Most studies employed quantitative cross-sectional designs, with only a few incorporating qualitative [36, 40, 41] and mixed-method approaches [21]. Sample sizes across the studies ranged from 21 participants in qualitative studies to 3,179 in quantitative surveys. Studies from sub-Saharan Africa consisted of 11 investigations conducted in Ethiopia and Nigeria. There were three studies from Lebanon, all quantitative. There were 12 studies from Israel, including both qualitative and quantitative research on diverse populations. These studies examined Palestinian citizens of Israel, mixed populations of Palestinian and Jewish Israelis, comparisons between older Jewish adults who immigrated from the Former Soviet Union (FSU) and native-born Israelis, and first- and second-generation FSU immigrants residing in Israel. Population characteristics – diverse samples with limited gender, educational, and clinical variations The reviewed studies involved diverse populations, including adolescents [23, 24, 26, 31, 35, 38], university students [25, 28, 30, 33, 34, 37, 40], teachers [27], traditional healers [21, 24], pharmacists [29], general public samples [32, 36], and specific groups such as older Jewish adults [44, 45], Bedouin community [36], Bedouin women [39] and FSU immigrants [42]. Reported mean ages ranged widely—from around 16 years in adolescent samples [22, 23, 31, 35, 38], to the early 20 s among university students [25, 30, 33, 34], the mid-30s in general public samples [6, 32], and into the late 60 s and early 70 s among older adult populations [44, 45].Most studies reported that the majority of their samples were female participants [21, 27, 28, 32–34, 36, 37, 39, 42]. Educational backgrounds varied significantly, from informal education among traditional healers [21] to higher education among students, teachers, and general public participants [6, 25, 28, 30, 32, 42]. Although all samples were non-clinical, several studies assessed clinical characteristics and found mild to moderate levels of psychological distress, primarily using the General Health Questionnaire-12 (GHQ-12) [6, 30, 35, 38, 39, 46, 47]. The majority of participants reported no current or past mental illness [21, 25, 37]. Measures and frameworks – narrow range of assessment instruments and framework Most studies utilized all or part of the Mental Health Literacy Scale (MHLS) [4], or adapted versions of it [21, 24, 35, 37–39, 44, 45]. Additional tools included the Mental Health Literacy Questionnaire (MHLQ) [22, 23, 30, 48], the Mental Health Knowledge Schedule (MAKS) [49], the Community Attitudes Toward the Mentally Ill (CAMI) [50], the Literacy of Suicide Scale (LOSS) [32, 33, 51], and vignette-based questionnaires initially developed by Jorm [1, 26–28, 31, 33, 42]. The qualitative studies were typically grounded in Jorm’s comprehensive mental health literacy framework [2, 36, 40, 41].The majority of studies assessed general MHL. However, several studies focused on specific disorders, particularly depression [26, 27, 31, 33, 42], schizophrenia [28, 42], and suicide literacy [33]. Among the studies using the MHLS or its adapted versions, some assessed individual dimensions of MHL separately—either all or a subset of them [6, 35, 44, 45], while others generated an overall index score for MHL [21, 30, 39]. Mental health literacy levels and correlates- surprising levels amid familiar patterns Most reviewed studies demonstrated moderate-to-high levels of MHL. However, Aluh et al. [26] reported low MHL among Nigerian teachers, with only 16.3% correctly identifying and labeling depression. Similarly, Aluh et al. [28] found poor MHL regarding schizophrenia among Nigerian undergraduate students.Among studies examining correlates of MHL, common factors associated with higher MHL included female gender, younger age, higher educational attainment, and personal experience with mental health issues or prior use of mental health services. Among immigrants from the FSU residing in Israel, higher levels of acculturation were associated with better MHL [42]. Several studies also found that higher MHL was positively associated with help-seeking behaviors and mental health service use. In particular, the MHL dimension concerning knowledge of where to seek information emerged as especially important [6, 35, 37, 38]. Qualitative analyses highlighted how cultural and social contexts—such as prevailing beliefs, treatment preferences, stigma, access to services, and language—play a key role in shaping understandings and responses to mental health, thereby informing mental health literacy [36, 40, 41]. War and conflict-related discussions – unexamined relationships Although all included studies were conducted in countries affected by political conflict or war, explicit discussions of how MHL might be related to this context were generally limited. Most studies did not integrate conflict-related contexts into their methodologies or analytical frameworks. A few studies acknowledged in their introductions that the broader socio-political environment could influence mental health and MHL [21, 24, 32, 37]. However, these references were typically broad and suggestive, rather than offering a direct or systematic examination of the relationship between conflict experiences and MHL assessment.

Findings from studies of refugees and immigrants originating from war and conflict zones

Study characteristics – few studies and only quantitative methods A total of nine studies focusing on immigrants from war or conflict zones were reviewed, covering populations in Australia [52–56], the United States [57], South Korea [58], and Israel [59]. Most of these studies were peer-reviewed, quantitative cross-sectional studies, with the exception of two grey literature dissertations [57, 60]. One study applied a pre-post intervention design [60]. Sample sizes ranged from 23 to 375 participants, with a more limited sample size for dissertation studies. Population characteristics – limited sociodemographic characteristics with moderate-severe clinical characteristics Reviewed studies involved varied adult immigrant populations, including refugees and immigrants originally from Iraq, Sudan, Afghanistan, Syria, and Lebanon residing in Australia, Middle Eastern migrant women and Ethiopian immigrants in the United States, North Korean refugees in South Korea, and Ethiopian immigrants in Israel. Participants’ ages ranged widely from 18 to 80 years, with mean ages typically in the mid-30s to mid-40s; however, Chimoriya et al. [52] and Morgamo [60] reported mean ages around the early 50 s and late 40 s, respectively. Female participants were predominant in five studies [52, 57–60], with balanced gender representation in the other four Australian studies. Education levels were mixed, ranging from no formal education [58] or high school education or less [57] to post-secondary education [59, 60]. While almost all samples were non-clinical, two exceptions were noted. In the study by May et al. [53], part of the sample was recruited from counseling centers, and in Noh et al. [58], participants were recruited with the assistance of professional counselors. In the latter, 59.75% of participants reported having ‘serious’ mental health issues. Among the non-clinical samples, several studies still assessed clinical characteristics and reported moderate-to-high levels of depression and PTSD symptoms among participants [52, 54, 56, 57]. Measures and frameworks – limited instruments with emphasis on PTSD and depression Most studies used established measures, including vignette-based questionnaires developed from Jorm’s MHL framework [1] in Australian studies, MHLS [57, 60], the MAKS [59], and a custom questionnaire based on McGill et al. [61] in Noh et al. [58]. All studies examined MHL dimensions separately, including symptom recognition, causal beliefs, treatment beliefs and preferences, and attitudes and stigma. While explicit analyses linking these war experiences directly to MHL outcomes were lacking, some studies did recognize war experiences as causes of PTSD [55] and measured war-related trauma exposure [56]. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this narrative review is the first to specifically examine MHL among individuals living in war and armed conflict zones and refugees or immigrants originating from these areas. Our review identified 26 studies conducted with populations residing in war and conflict-affected regions, and only 9 studies focusing on refugees. This limited body of literature underscores the relative scarcity of research on MHL among these highly vulnerable groups, particularly when compared to the broader and more extensively studied populations in the global MHL literature.

Regarding the characteristics of study samples, research among populations residing in war and armed conflict zones included diverse groups such as adolescents, students, older adults, and traditional healers. However, many studies featured predominantly female participants and showed limited variation in clinical characteristics. Similarly, studies conducted among refugee populations often included a narrow range of groups, although they tended to be more gender balanced. Hence, beyond the limited number of studies in this area, the narrow characteristics of the samples further constrain the generalizability of findings. Of note, it is important to examine diverse subgroups within the same populations, as MHL is often shaped by highly-specific experiential and sociocultural contexts [6]. Doing so would enable a more nuanced understanding of how mental health knowledge, beliefs, and help-seeking behaviors are distributed and expressed within war and conflict-affected communities.

The majority of studies included in this review employed quantitative cross-sectional designs across both populations, including refugees and individuals residing in conflict zones. While such methods are useful for identifying general patterns, they offer limited insight into lived experiences and the contextual factors that shape MHL. Understanding lived experience is particularly crucial when examining mental health issues among vulnerable populations, as it allows for a deeper exploration of the complex social, cultural, and structural forces that influence mental health beliefs, knowledge, and behaviors [62, 63].

In addition, the geographical distribution of studies limits the depth of contextual understanding of MHL among the reviewed populations. Research on individuals living in war and conflict zones was heavily concentrated in just a few countries, namely Ethiopia, Nigeria, Israel, and Lebanon, with a striking absence of studies from other major armed conflict-affected countries such as Sudan, Yemen, and Afghanistan. While this concentration is not unexpected given the significant challenges and limited resources for conducting research in active conflict settings, these omissions remain critical, as such areas not only reflect geopolitical instability but also represent growing populations at high risk for mental health challenges. Similarly, the studies focused on refugees from political armed conflict zones were conducted primarily in Australia, with only two in the United States, one in South Korea, and one in Israel. This pattern is consistent with the findings from another review on group-based peer support interventions among refugees and immigrants, which also reported that most research was concentrated in economically developed countries such as the United States, Canada, and Spain [64].

While our search strategy included both peer-reviewed and grey literature and did not restrict inclusion to English-language publications, only requiring the availability of an abstract in English, only two grey literature sources were ultimately included, and all studies reviewed were published in English. This outcome highlights the dominance of English-language academic publishing and suggests broader structural barriers to producing and disseminating mental health research within vulnerable populations. Ironically, these are the very populations who may benefit most from access to research outputs in diverse languages and formats beyond conventional academic writing.

Regarding the theoretical framework and tools of MHL, most studies in this review relied on Jorm’s MHL framework [2] to examine MHL. While this six-dimensional framework remains the most widely used in the field and offers a comprehensive structure for understanding various aspects of mental health knowledge and attitudes, it was originally developed in high-income, Western, and non-conflict settings. As such, its application to populations in the global south affected by political violence or displacement may not fully capture context-specific understandings of mental health. In addition, most studies used standardized tools, particularly the MHLS [4], yet very few attempted to contextually adapt these instruments to the unique cultural or conflict-related experiences of the studied populations. These concerns align with scholars in cultural psychiatry and medical anthropology, who have emphasized that applying Western psychiatric constructs universally risks overlooking local contexts, rendering them less valid or meaningful [65, 66]. In this context, it is worth noting that the studies conducted in Nigeria, Ethiopia, and Lebanon were led by local researchers affiliated with universities in these countries. Nonetheless, despite being locally led, these studies also relied heavily on Western-derived MHL frameworks and standardized tools, with limited contextual adaptation. This pattern illustrates how the dominance of Western conceptualizations of mental health extends beyond authorship or institutional affiliation [67].

In line with this, among studies conducted in conflict zones, most assessed general MHL, while the studies among refugee populations more often focused on specific disorders such as depression and PTSD. This emphasis is not surprising, given the high prevalence of these conditions among displaced populations, as demonstrated in previous reviews [17]. Notably, this mismatch between the research tools and the lived realities of the populations studied may partly explain the findings regarding MHL levels across groups. It is possible that the use of frameworks and measures that are not fully relevant to these populations, and the tendency to assess general MHL, contributed to the moderate-to-high MHL levels reported in many of the studies. Several of the studies included in this review acknowledged this as a potential limitation in interpreting their results [36, 41, 54].

Furthermore, the studies included in this review found similar correlates of higher MHL, such as being younger, female, having higher formal education, and prior experience with mental health services, to those reported in studies of general populations in non-conflict settings [66, 67]. This may suggest that, despite different contexts and challenges, certain sociodemographic and clinical characteristics associated with MHL remain consistent across diverse populations.

Finally, and most critically, one of the main goals of this review was to examine whether MHL is being assessed among these populations in the context of wars. Across all the studies included, an unexpected and important finding emerged: there was a general lack of integration of war and conflict-related experiences into the assessment and analysis of MHL. Although all studies involved populations affected by political violence, displacement, or armed conflict, very few explicitly examined knowledge and beliefs about individuals’ mental health and help-seeking behaviors while they were experiencing these situations, but rather focused on general mental health–related issues. The failure to incorporate the broader sociopolitical and historical context into their analytic frameworks, may be partly attributed to the dominance of a medicalized model of MHL, which tends to conceptualize mental health symptoms and conditions as individual pathologies, divorced from the surrounding social, cultural, and political environments. Such a decontextualized approach risks overlooking how exposure to war, displacement, systemic violence, and chronic instability shape both the experience and interpretation of psychological distress. In conflict-affected populations, where suffering is often collective and deeply embedded in structural inequalities, the absence of contextual analysis renders much of the existing research insufficiently responsive to the lived realities of those it seeks to understand.

Importantly, this omission reflects a critical gap in the literature, as MHL is inherently context-dependent and should be examined within the specific sociocultural and geopolitical environments in which it is situated [6, 68–70]. Indeed, even sub-contextual factors may significantly shape MHL, as emerging evidence suggests [70]. This raises a pressing question: to what extent are current studies on MHL among individuals in war zones and refugee contexts truly capturing forms of knowledge and understanding that are relevant, meaningful, and applicable to the lived experiences of these populations?

Limitations, conclusions, and implications

There are several limitations of this narrative review that should be noted. First, although the search strategy was comprehensive and systematic, some relevant studies may have been missed, especially those published in local or regional journals that are not indexed in the databases we searched. Second, we included only studies with abstracts available in English, which may have excluded important research published entirely in other languages. Future studies should try to include work written in other languages and from a broader range of sources, including local and community-based publications. Third, one limitation of our categorization approach is that specific populations, in this review, Ethiopian immigrants in Israel, do not fit necessarily fit into a single category. While we classified them as immigrants for the sake of consistency with the way the studies framed them, they are also residing in a country experiencing ongoing political conflict, which illustrates the complexity of applying rigid classifications. Finally, since this is a narrative review, we did not assess the quality of the included studies, which limits our ability to evaluate the rigor of the evidence or make statistical comparisons.

Despite these limitations, this review reveals important patterns and gaps in the literature. The frameworks and tools used to assess MHL were often not adapted to the specific realities of war, conflict, or displacement. Most studies focused on general MHL and did not examine knowledge, beliefs, or help-seeking behaviors related to mental health in the specific contexts experienced by these populations. In addition, although the studies included diverse samples, many lacked variations in key sociodemographic characteristics, and the geographic focus was narrow, missing several major conflict-affected regions.

Given these gaps, we argue that current MHL frameworks require reconceptualization. There is a need to develop or adapt frameworks and tools that assess what individuals in crisis settings know and believe about mental health, not just in general terms, but in ways that are relevant to their lived experiences. For example, tools should examine beliefs about the importance of caring for one’s mental health even when physical safety is threatened, knowledge of symptoms likely to appear in such extreme conditions, and contextually relevant coping strategies. General tools like the MHLS, which assess knowledge of disorders such as bipolar disorder, may not reflect the urgent mental health realities of people living through war or forced displacement. New frameworks should be developed with these considerations in mind. Only then can we move forward in meaningfully assessing MHL among conflict-affected and displaced populations and designing interventions that are truly responsive to their needs.
