“Mucopolysaccharidosis syndrome in a 9-Year-old boy: oral-dental management and diagnostic considerations”: a case report
Authors: Nosrat Nourbakhsh, Elahe Asnaashari Esfahani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMucopolysaccharidosis (MPS) comprises a group of metabolic diseases with a disorder in the function of lysosomal enzymes that lead to the accumulation of glycosaminoglycans (mucopolysaccharides) (Kubaski et al. in Diagnostics 10:161, 2020; Hampe et al. in Cells 9:1838, 2020; Tomatsu et al. Mol Genet Metab 110(1–2):42–53, 2013). At least seven variants of this disorder have been identified to date (Muenzer et al. in Pediatrics 124(6):e1228–e1239, 2009; Muenzer et al. in Eur J Pediatr 171:181–8, 2012). this study aims to report a case of mucopolysaccharidosis in a 9-year-old child. Also, the treatments and dental observations made for the child have been described. Also, a review of past articles has been done to report the types of diseases, medical and dental considerations, etc. of this disease.Case descriptionthe present case report describes the orofacial and systemic characteristics, diagnostic methods, and dental management of a 9-year-old boy with MPS with a one-year follow-up in association with a brief review of past articles.ConclusionSince MPS patients have many changes in their oral and dental structures, they pose many challenges for dentists. Also, these patients need considerations in dentistry due to the involvement of different body organs. Knowing the oral-dental and systemic problems of these patients can help their dentists to provide effective and safe treatment for them.

Background

mucopolysaccharidoses (MPS) is a rare disorder comprising a diverse group of chronic metabolic conditions due to a genetic mutation. This disease results from a deficiency of specific lysosomal enzymes that determine the MPS type. Such enzyme deficiency gives rise to defective catabolism of glycosaminoglycans (mucopolysaccharides), resulting in increased accumulation of mucopolysaccharides within intracellular lysosomes in different body tissues [1–3]. Seven types have been identified: Types I, II, III, IV, VI, VII, and IX [4–7]. The prevalence of all types of MPS is one case in 20,000 live births [2, 3]. The disease is caused by a mutation in the IDVA (alpha-L-Idosiduronose) gene, which has the necessary orders to produce the enzyme that hydrolyzes large sugars called glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) [8]. A mutation in the IDVA gene decreases the activity of the IDVA enzyme or eliminates it, resulting in the accumulation of GAGs within cells, especially within lysosomes [9].

MPS has diverse clinical manifestations based on the enzymatic defect type, the affected organ, and the glycoprotein involved [7, 10]. A diagnosis might be arrived at through clinical examinations and tests involving urine and blood. In addition, psychomotor activity tests, genetic assessments, skeletal surveys, and/or peripheral cell tests can be helpful [11, 12]. Enzyme activity tests are the gold standard for diagnosing MPS. enzyme therapies [13], Bone marrow transplants (BMTs) [14] have raised hopes to increase the life expectancy of patients with MPS disorders, as well as their quality of life [13].Children with MPS would have special healthcare needs in terms of the severity of the condition. MPS patients often undergo a multidisciplinary treatment modality by specialized pediatric teams, including cardiologists, neurologists, psychiatrists, orthopedists, physiotherapists, and ophthalmologists, because of the multiple effects of the condition [10]. Since different organs of the body are involved in this disease, it is important to consider this issue in the dental treatment of these children. In this disease, children’s mouths and teeth are affected, and knowing the oral and systemic characteristics of these children is important for the dentists treating them. In this study, we tried to discuss the disease and its considerations by reviewing past articles.

Case presentation

In September 2022, A 9-year-old boy was referred to the postgraduate Department of Pediatric Dentistry in the Faculty of Dentistry at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences. complaining of pain in the permanent first molar tooth on the right side of the lower jaw. The general and craniofacial conditions of the child raised a suspicion of a syndrome. The patient was a single child, and his parents had no consanguineous marriage. The parents, who were both healthy, reported that the child had been diagnosed with type 1 MPS at 15 months of age. The patient was a candidate for bone marrow transplantation but was on the waiting list because there was no proper donor.

Medical history

At 15 months of age, the child’s physician referred him to an endocrinologist for laboratory tests due to the abnormal size of his head circumference and suspicion about the presence of a congenital disorder. According to the order of the pediatric endocrinologist, a blood test was taken from the child in one of the government hospitals in Isfahan city, and it was sent to Germany for examination. As a result of the blood test, it has been shown that the child has mucopolysaccharidosis type 1. Since then, Aldurazyme enzyme injection was scheduled weekly in terms of the child’s weight. Captopril is taken daily (half a pill in the morning and half a pill at night) according to the prescription of the child’s cardiologist. Genetic tests showed that the parents were carriers for this problem, and the child’s family history revealed no other information (Fig. 1). The child’s medical record summary showed the presence of cardiac problems (a thickening of the mitral valve with normal pericardium), renal hydronephrosis, corneal opacity (without signs of cataract and glaucoma), and healthy liver, spleen, and optic nerve. According to the mother’s report, the child was frequently affected by the common cold and had sleep apnea up to 3‒4 years of age. The child also suffered from constipation. In addition, the child’s bilateral wrist radiography was in his records, with a diagnosis of carpal tunnel syndrome (Figs. 2 and 3).

Discussion

Mucopolysaccharidoses (MPS) is a rare, heterogeneous congenital metabolic disorder comprising a diverse group of chronic metabolic conditions due to a genetic mutation. It includes some rare inherited disorders resulting from a deficiency of specific lysosomal enzymes that determine the MPS type. Such enzyme deficiency gives rise to defective catabolism of Glycosaminoglycans (mucopolysaccharides), resulting in increased accumulation of mucopolysaccharides within intracellular lysosomes in different body tissues [1–3].

Some accumulations might be seen in the respiratory system, spleen, central nervous system, blood, and bone marrow, gradually leading to injuries to cells, tissues, and different body systems [15, 16]. They are responsible for degrading three mucopolysaccharide classes: heparan sulfate, dermatan sulfate, and keratan sulfate [4, 5]. MPS is associated with significant morbidity and mortality resulting from the progressive accumulation of GAGs in different body systems [6].

Types

Seven MPS types have been identified: Types I, II, III, IV, VI, VII, and IX. Further classification is based on the deficiency of eleven enzymes that break down GAGs (Table 1) [6, 7].

Recently, internationally accepted guidelines have recommended classifying MPS I patients into three clinical sub-types: Hurler, Hurler-Scheie, and Scheie syndromes, with Hurler and Scheie syndromes as the most and least severe forms, respectively [17].

Initiation and epidemiology

The prevalence of all types of MPS is one case in 20,000 live births [2, 3], with the severe type affecting 1 in 100,000 newborns. The mild type has a lower incidence, affecting 1 in every 500,000 newborns [18].

Pathophysiology

The disease is caused by a mutation in the IDVA (alpha-L-Idosiduronose) gene, which has the necessary orders to produce the enzyme that hydrolyzes large sugars called glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) [8]. A mutation in the IDVA gene decreases the activity of the IDVA enzyme or eliminates it completely, resulting in the accumulation of GAGs within cells, especially within lysosomes.

Lysosomes are cellular components that degrade different molecules and recycle them. Conditions such as disorders in lysosomal storage (MPSI) give rise to disruptions in the synthesis of molecules within lysosomes, called lysosomal storage disorders. The lysosomes enlarge due to the accumulation of GAGs, resulting in the enlargement of many tissues and organs. Researchers believe that GAGs might interfere with the function of other intra-lysosomal proteins and disrupt the movement and treatment of molecules in the cells [9].

The mechanism of heredity

MPS diseases exhibit a recessive gene inheritance except for MPS II or “Hunter syndrome”, which is X-linked [1].

Clinical manifestations

MPS has diverse clinical manifestations based on the enzymatic defect type, the affected organ, and the glycoprotein involved [7, 10].

The affected individuals might present severe physical and neurological developmental problems: abnormal upper airways, restrictive lung disease, skeletal abnormalities, cervical spine deformities, and behavioural problems [19].

The most common clinical manifestations are coarse facial features, corneal opacity (with severe visual impairment and a possible loss of visual ability), hepatosplenomegaly, cardiac valve disorders, skeletal deformity, lumbar kyphosis or a hump, and high urinary levels of mucopolysaccharides [20]. Many craniofacial and dental abnormalities associated with MPS, which may or may not result in a higher risk of dental conditions, a depressed nasal bridge, a protruded forehead, a snub nose, and introverted nostrils have been seen in many articles [21, 22].

The seven distinct types of MPS are associated with dental anomalies, malocclusions, tooth eruption deviations, TMJ pathoses, a high caries index, and periodontal diseases with different degrees of orthodontic and pediatric problems [23, 24].

Many physical signs were present in the present case, including macrocephaly, a rough face, skeletal deformity, corneal opacity, and cardiac valve abnormality. In addition, some dental abnormalities were observed, including malocclusion (open bite), short and broad mandible, dental caries, dental spacing, and gingivitis. Also, behavioural problems were observed.

Dental care is often more difficult in these patients, resulting in significant unmet restorative needs [25, 26].

Despite the patient’s poor cooperation, we managed to conduct comprehensive dental treatments through behaviour management.

The treatment of the child’s first permanent molar tooth was carried out by another dentist, and in the one-year follow-up, we noticed the presence of a lesion in the distal root of this tooth, and the possible cause of this lesion could be the lack of proper filling of the distal root. The child had no clinical symptoms such as pain or swelling in the tooth area. In consultation with an endodontist and because of the great importance of this tooth, the parents of the child have been strongly encouraged to refer for re-treatment. (Fig. 15).

The literature on MPS suggests alterations in the enamel and dentin structure, especially in the DEJ in MPS type I [27] and type IV [28]. However, their extent and significance are unknown.

It should be pointed out that, in this case, the structures of enamel and dentin of the existing teeth were clinically healthy without any changes.

Liu and Gardner have reported short and broad mandible in patients with Hunter syndrome, with a long intergonial distance, and a longer distance around the arch, giving rise to increased interdental spacing, consistent with the present case [29].

The main systemic manifestations of Hunter syndrome include macrocephaly, moderate-to-severe delays in development, a dysmorphic face, skeletal deformities, joint abnormalities, hepatosplenomegaly, Heart valve problems, hirsutism, hyperactivity, and uncoordinated behaviours. A prominent forehead, temporal bulges, and skull deformities can also be found. Secondary systemic manifestations include recurrent otitis media and hearing deficiency [29, 30].

The affected individual’s ability to speak and walk decreases over time with age. In addition, they exhibit progressive problems concerning the consumption of solid food, with weight loss. According to Hunter and Dorfman, tonsillar and adenoid problems lead to severe, recurrent respiratory infections. The patients’ hirsutism usually increases after 2.1‒3 years of age. Death is usually attributed to cardiac or respiratory arrest [30, 31]. According to Tyagi [29] and Scarpa et al. [31], the affected individuals might have some manifestations such as macrocephaly, hydrocephaly, heart valve abnormalities, short stature, mental retardation, dysostosis multiplex, cardiovascular anomalies, indigestion, skin thickening, large vocal cords that might be associated with a bass and hoarse voice. In addition, the airways might be narrow in some patients, leading to recurrent upper respiratory tract infections and short respiration pauses during sleep (sleep apnea) such as present case. The skin is usually curved inwards, and the feet are separated when the knees are touched. These patients fall repeatedly during walking and have an unusual gait. As mentioned previously, most of these signs were present in the present case.

Furthermore, the affected individuals might exhibit decreased hearing ability and recurrent ear infections [32]. Some patients have short stature and joint deformities that affect the joint movements. Most severe cases usually have dysostosis multiplex, which is considered a skeletal abnormality and can be seen on x-ray films [29, 33]. The carpal tunnel syndrome is seen in many affected children, characterized by numbness, tingling, and hand and finger weakness [34]. The cervical spinal canal stenosis might exert pressure on the spinal cord and cause injuries [35]. The above syndrome had already been reported in the patient’s medical record in the present study.

Children with this syndrome usually have a short life and sometimes survive only until the end of childhood. However, in the mild type of the condition, they reach puberty. These children might or might not have a short life. Those with the mild form of the condition possibly have a learning disability. Those with the severe type of the condition mostly survive up to 10 years of age [36, 37]. Cardiac diseases and airway obstruction are the main reasons for mortality in both cases [38]. The present case did not have a learning disability. The affected children usually have normal IQs [39].

Orofacial anomalies

Oral manifestations include short and broad mandible, localized, radiolucent lesions of the jaws, flattened TMJs, and macroglossia.

Similar to the present study, macroglossia is due to the precipitation of GAG in the tongue structure, which usually leads to anterior open bite and significant compression of the maxilla.

Conical, peg-shaped teeth with generalized spacing, a high-arched palate with flattened alveolar ridges, and hyperplastic, hypertrophic, and enlarged gingivae are common features [28–30], with a thickened and macroglossic tongue [29]. The TMJ usually exhibits a flat articular surface; however, the condyle might be flat or small, with a large coronoid process, exhibiting hypomobility [29]. There are large interdental spaces due to small teeth [29, 30] that might be conical and often hypoplastic. Anterior open bite in these patients has been attributed to a large protruding tongue [30]. Root formation is delayed, and the molar teeth usually assume a more posterior position and exhibit distoangular tipping [29]. The palate is high-arched in most cases, and the palatal rugae are prominent, with deep grooves in the midsagittal plane [29]. Although the hard palate was not high in the present study, it exhibited prominent rugae on the hard palate.

Delayed tooth eruption might be due to dentigerous cysts with well-defined borders, hyperplastic dental follicles due to the precipitation of collagen, and GAG around unerupted teeth [40].

Gingival bleeding might be seen in most patients, which might be due to poor oral hygiene and dexterity limitations [41]. Patients with MPS have many oral manifestations; in addition, they have behavioural problems and mental disabilities, requiring treatment in speciality centres [13]. Therefore, prevention is necessary in this high-risk group.

Diagnostic methods

A diagnosis might be arrived at through clinical examinations and tests involving urine and blood. In addition, psychomotor activity tests, genetic assessments, skeletal surveys, and peripheral cell tests can be helpful [11, 12]. A rapid diagnosis is of utmost importance since treatment is effective only in patients < 2.5 years of age [10, 42].

Fortunately, this case has been under treatment since 15 months of age.

Enzyme activity tests are the gold standard for diagnosing MPS, and determining the IDUA mutation site is an essential component of the puzzle in diagnosing MPS I [43].

The condition can be diagnosed through increased levels of keratin sulfate, GALNS activity testing, skin biopsy, leukocyte and fibroblast culturing, and radiography [44, 45].

Treatment options

Recent advances in enzyme therapies have raised hopes to increase the life expectancy of patients with MPS disorders, as well as their quality of life [13].

Bone marrow transplants (BMTs) are a possible treatment for these patients since it is a stem cell disorder [14]. BMT option should be considered based on age, donor compatibility, degree of cardiac and brain involvement, and parental consent [14].

This case is a candidate for bone marrow transplantation; however, at the time of this manuscript’s preparation, a suitable donor has not been found. On the other hand, since complications such as graft-versus-host disease and graft rejection are possible, we suggested alternatives, i.e., the transplantation of the stem cells of exfoliated primary teeth. The child’s sound maxillary primary teeth are present in the oral cavity. The advantage of the possible grafting of SHED from the primary canine, which is an available, easy, and autologous source, is decreased odds of graft rejection. In addition, it is not necessary to wait for a suitable donor.

Determining the prognosis is difficult due to inadequate transplant experience with Hunter syndrome patients. The most common failure causes are infection, the transplanted organ rejected by the host, and GvHD [46]. Oral complications include infections and mucositis. Infection is the most common etiologic factor for morbidity and mortality during and after transplantation [14]. As a result, it is imperative to eliminate possible oral infection sources before undertaking transplantation and to maintain good oral health.

Management

Children with MPS would have special healthcare needs in terms of the severity of the condition. MPS patients often undergo a multidisciplinary treatment modality by specialized pediatric teams, including cardiologists, neurologists, psychiatrists, orthopedists, physiotherapists, and ophthalmologists, because of the multiple effects of the condition. Patients with MPS I, II, and III usually manifest significant cognitive impairment and behavioural problems; however, MPS IV patients exhibit normal intelligence [10].

MPS Patients pose various challenges for dental practitioners since they have complicated medical and physical problems. As these children grow up, they exhibit moderate-to-severe delays in their development, hyperactivity, and disruptive and uncoordinated behaviour [30, 33, 35] According to Gardner [30], they resist discipline and exhibit stubbornness and fearlessness; finally, their difficult management generally leads to institutionalization.

Many MPS patients, especially those with MPS III [10], also exhibit progressive behavioural problems as the condition progresses. For general anaesthesia of these patients, essential healthcare surgical units are necessary with adequate facilities and pediatric anaesthetists since these patients have complex anaesthetic difficulties, especially during intubation [1]. Managing dental and periodontal problems in these patients might pose significant challenges while the patient is conscious. Therefore, poor oral health influences the quality of life [47] and necessitates high-risk anaesthetic procedures.

Behavioural problems are a characteristic feature of some types of MPS, complicating treatment under local anaesthesia. Medical comorbidities often necessitate treatment in specialist centers under a multidisciplinary approach [47].

Although the patient was at a high caries risk, no fluoride therapy was undertaken due to a renal problem, and the use of fluoridated toothpaste and tooth mousse, containing casein phosphopeptide-amorphous calcium phosphate, was recommended. CPP-ACP is a chemical compound that results in remineralization and prevents demineralization and dental caries by amassing calcium/phosphate on tooth surfaces [48].

Conclusion

Since MPS patients have many changes in their oral and dental structures, they pose many challenges for dentists. Knowing the systemic and dental characteristics of these patients is helpful for the dental management of these patients.
