The efficacy of intentional compressed placement of flow diverter stent for improvement of operative results against internal carotid artery and posterior communicating artery bifurcation aneurysms: patient series
Authors: Ryuzaburo Kanazawa, Noboru Kuniyoshi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDInternal carotid artery and posterior communicating artery bifurcation aneurysms (ICPC ANs) have a relatively high recurrence rate. The authors report 8 cases in which good results without coil compaction were confirmed during the follow-up period by using a small number of coils followed by flow diverter (FD) stent placement.OBSERVATIONSThe mean maximum aneurysm diameter in the 8 cases was 8.7 ± 4.1 mm, the mean number of coils used was 1.5 ± 0.9, and the average volume embolization rate was 14.7% ± 9%. In 6 of the 8 cases, no coil compaction was observed 1 year after treatment compared with the end of procedure, and in 2 cases, the coil shape changed in a way that preserved the origin of the fetal-type posterior communicating artery, and no compaction was observed within the aneurysm. Complete embolization was achieved in 7 of 8 cases; 1 case, in which direct oral anticoagulant was used, had a neck remnant, but no aneurysm enlargement or significant compaction was observed.LESSONSFD placement with intentional stent compression may be effective in the treatment of ICPC AN. An internal carotid artery and posterior communicating artery bifurcation aneurysm (ICPC AN) is known to be an aneurysm with a relatively high recurrence rate. In the International Subarachnoid Aneurysm Trial (ISAT), 1 ICPC AN was reported to have a significantly higher retreatment rate (47%, p = 0.03),1 and similar reports have been published.2–4 In recent years, there have been reports that placement of flow diverter (FD) stents is effective.5–10 Large aneurysms are more likely to rebleed than small ones.11 In a report by Aikawa et al., 4 of 6 cases of rebleeding were ICPC ANs, and all were large or giant.3 Kim et al. reported the treatment outcomes of 171 cases of ICPC AN, of which 43.6% were fetal posterior communicating artery (PcomA) aneurysms, but recanalization was seen in 30.9% (retreatment was seen in 7 cases [4%]); it was reported that recanalization was more common in cases with large-diameter aneurysms, with insufficient embolization, and requiring adjunctive techniques.4 Low wall shear stress (WSS) and high oscillatory shear index (OSI) have been reported at the rupture point of ruptured aneurysms,12 and a high OSI has also been reported to be significantly associated with aneurysm recanalization,2 suggesting that the use of FD stents may be effective in preventing recurrence. We report here cases in which an FD stent was used in combination with a small number of coils for ICPC ANs, with good outcomes and almost no coil compaction.

Study Description

The study included 8 cases of unruptured ICPC ANs treated between June 2021 and July 2023. All procedures were performed with the patient’s written consent. Pipeline embolization device (PED; Medtronic Co., Ltd.) was used in all cases, and stent mesh compression was performed as much as possible near the neck when placing the stent. As a rule, a small number of coils were used in combination. The coils used were left to the discretion of the surgeon. Antithrombotic drugs were started 2 weeks before surgery (aspirin 100 mg and clopidogrel 75 mg/day), and platelet aggregation was confirmed before surgery (using Hematotracer ZEN, DS Medical Co., Ltd.). In principle, dual antiplatelet treatment was performed for 6 months after the procedure, and reduction in dosage was determined at the surgeon’s discretion by referring to the results of platelet aggregation. Only case 8 was taking a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) due to atrial fibrillation, and only clopidogrel was added.

A PED was used in all procedures. The microcatheter for coil insertion was placed into the aneurysm using the jail technique. Basically, the stent was deployed from the terminal segment of the internal carotid artery rather than from the middle cerebral artery, the system was pushed at the neck of the aneurysm to close the struts and achieve neck coverage, and then a small number of coils were placed into the aneurysm. When the surgeon deemed it sufficient, the microcatheter was removed, and the stent was then deployed and removed. The reason for deploying the stent from the distal end of the internal carotid artery is to make the procedure of pushing the stent at the neck of the aneurysm safer and more effective.

Follow-up evaluation was performed the day after treatment and 3 months later by MRI, and cerebral angiography was performed 6 months and 1 year later. Cerebral angiography was performed with written consent from the patient. In case 8, where consent was not obtained from the patient for cerebral angiography 1 year after procedure, CT angiograms and head plain radiographic images were used for the 1-year examination.

The maximum diameter of the aneurysm, number of coils used, volume embolization rate (VER), follow-up results (according to the Raymond classification), presence or absence of coil compaction, relationship between PcomA and P1, whether PcomA was antegrade, type of PcomA, whether access from P1 was possible, and PcomA patency at follow-up were evaluated. The follow-up results were classified as complete obliteration, neck remnant, dome filling, and regrowth, and coil compaction was classified as present or absent. Coil compaction only at the origin of the PcomA and without compaction within the aneurysm was judged as absent (Table 1).

Results

Treatment was completed successfully in 8 cases, with no obvious complications. The results are shown in Table 1. The mean maximum diameter of the aneurysm was 8.7 ± 4.1 mm, the mean number of coils used was 1.5 ± 0.9, and the average VER was 14.7% ± 9%. Complete obliteration was confirmed in 7 cases (87.5%) by angiography 1 year later. One case of a neck remnant, for which the patient was taking a DOAC, was thought to have influenced the outcome. In cases in which PcomA flowed antegrade before surgery (6 cases), PcomA patency was confirmed in all cases. In addition, of the 8 cases in which coils were used, no coil compaction was observed in 6 cases despite low VER, and in the other 2 cases (cases 5 and 8), compaction was observed with blood flow remaining to the PcomA, but there was almost no compaction within the aneurysm.

Case 2: Female in Her 60s

Treatment was performed for an irregular left ICPC aneurysm. Two versatile filling coils (VFCs; Micro Vention Terumo) were placed, with a VER of 26.5%, and a 4.25 × 18–mm PED was used. As shown on a carotid artery angiogram obtained 1 year later, the coil shape had not changed and embolization was complete (Fig. 1).

Case 3: Female in Her 50s

Case 4: Female in Her 70s

A left ICPC aneurysm was discovered, with the main complaint of visual impairment. The PcomA was of the fetal type, and the VER was 6%. Normally, it would be clear that coil compaction would occur, but the coil shape only changed to preserve the origin of the PcomA, and no overall compaction was observed (Fig. 3).

Case 8: Female in Her 70s

This patient had a history of craniotomy for a right internal carotid artery aneurysm and a middle cerebral artery aneurysm. A left ICPC aneurysm had been previously identified, but because its growth was gradual, preventive treatment was selected. A 5 × 20–mm PED was used, along with two VFCs. Cerebral angiography 6 months later showed inflow of contrast medium near the neck, but no growth of the aneurysm or significant coil compaction was observed, and a contrast CT scan 1 year later showed similar findings (Fig. 4).

Discussion

ICPC aneurysms have traditionally been thought to have a high recurrence rate. In the ISAT study, only ICPC AN has been reported to have a significantly higher retreatment rate.1 Suzuki et al. retreated 11 of 35 ICA aneurysms (31%) 10 mm or larger in their own case study and concluded that aneurysms 10 mm or larger have a high risk of recurrence.13 Aikawa et al. reported that 6 cases of rebleeding occurred after coil embolization in 227 cases, 4 of which were ICPC ANs, all of which were large to giant.3 Kim et al. reported the results of 171 cases of ICPC AN embolization, and recanalization was achieved in 53 cases (31%) and retreatment was performed in 7 cases (4%), with maximum aneurysm diameter, insufficient embolization, and use of a balloon or stent being risk factors for recurrence.4 Toyota et al. reported 22 retreatments over a period of approximately 7 years, 10 of which were ICPC ANs.14

There are various reports on the treatment of ICPC AN using an FD stent. In a meta-analysis, Vivanco-Suarez et al. reported on the treatment outcomes of FD in 397 patients (403 aneurysms) with ICPC AN; complete obliteration was achieved in 42% of cases with fetal PcomA and 77% of cases with non-etal PcomA. Of the FDs used, 85% were PEDs.5 Kühn et al. reported the treatment outcomes of FD use in 56 cases of nonfetal PcomA aneurysms and noted that almost complete embolization or better was observed in 43 of 48 cases (89.6%) for which follow-up angiography was performed 3–9 months later.7 Ten Brinck et al. reported that of 21 cases of ICPC AN in which FDs were successfully placed, complete embolization was achieved in 14 of 18 cases (77.8%) with nonfetal PcomA. However, they also reported that effective obliteration was not achieved in 3 cases in the fetal PcomA group.8

There have also been reports comparing treatment with FD alone and treatment with coils in combination. Park et al. reported that retreatment was required in 8 of 68 cases (11.8%) in the FD-alone group and in 1 of 65 cases (1.5%) in the coil combination group, and that the combination with coils was significantly better.15 In another report, they compared 797 aneurysms in 689 patients treated with FD alone with 109 aneurysms in 104 patients treated with coils in combination and reported that there was no difference in treatment outcomes, despite the aneurysm size and neck diameter being significantly larger in the coil combination group.9 Akiyama et al. treated 46 cases of ICA aneurysms with coils in combination and reported that complete embolization was achieved in 70.5% of cases, with a mean VER of 14.8%, and concluded that light coil packing was effective in preventing aneurysm rupture and nerve compression.10

Experimentally, there are several reports that, after FD placement, systolic blood pressure decreases and diastolic blood pressure increases within the aneurysm, with the mean blood pressure remaining almost unchanged;16,17 that it decreases by about 10%;18 and that the mean blood pressure within the aneurysm decreases transiently after FD placement but returns to the original value after 6 minutes, while the systolic pressure decreases by about 20% and the pulse pressure inside the aneurysm drops by about half.19 In a report on fluid dynamic factors in clinical cases, Raychev et al. reported that an eclipse sign of three or more phases remaining on angiography was a factor related to aneurysm occlusion 12 months later in 65 cases of FD treatment.20 Kim et al. reported that the depth of the aneurysm and neck size were risk factors for recurrence in ICPC AN, and that recurrence was more frequent in cases with a high intra-aneurysm OSI, with no association with peak pressure or WSS.2 In addition, Kerl et al. reported that FD placement reduces inflow into the aneurysm in a porosity-dependent manner, and this effect is enhanced by stent compression, but the mean blood pressure within the aneurysm does not change.21

Observations

From the above, it can be inferred that by compressing the FD firmly when placing it, the OSI and pulse pressure inside the aneurysm might be effectively decreased, which contributes to the suppression of aneurysm recurrence, and this is reflected in the eclipse sign in the relatively long phase. In all 8 of our cases, treatment was performed with a relatively low VER; however, the shape of the coil mass immediately after treatment did not change, and so-called coil compaction did not occur in the follow-up after treatment. In cases 5 and 8, both the size and neck diameter were large, and coil compaction would normally occur, but only a change in the position of the coil that preserved the origin of the fetal PcomA was observed, and there was no change in coil shape inside the aneurysm. These are noteworthy results. In our experience, rather than the combined use of coils to effectively obliterate the aneurysm, we believe that reducing the OSI and pulse pressure within the aneurysm, by packing the struts of the stent and placing it so that its efficacy can be maximized, is most important. The combined use of coils was thought to have helped to visually confirm this fact. In case 2 (Fig. 2), these characteristics seem to be very clearly illustrated. Furthermore, there is no doubt that improvement in the visualization of various devices, resulting from advances in angiography equipment, has contributed to making treatment like this possible.22

However, because there are only a few cases and only short-term results within 1 year are shown, future follow-up is necessary to determine long-term results. In addition, because there are few rescue methods in the event of significant recurrence in fetal PcomA cases, it is important to carefully judge the suitability of the procedure and ensure that the patient’s understanding of the procedure is adequate.

Lessons

Good results were obtained by using an FD stent in combination with a small number of coils for ICPC AN, which is known to have a high recurrence rate. Despite the small number of coils, the lack of coil compaction may have allowed visualization of intra-aneurysm OSI reduction and a decrease in pulse pressure associated with FD placement. To obtain these results, it was considered effective to place the stent by compressing its mesh as much as possible within a safe range. In the future, accumulation of cases and long-term follow-up will be necessary.
