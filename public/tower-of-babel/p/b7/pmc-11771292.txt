Effort–Reward Imbalance and Its Association with Musculoskeletal Pain among Information Technology Professionals
Authors: Aruna Raju, Devi R. Nithiya, Arun Tipandjan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Context:Psychosocial factors at the workplace play a significant role in the development of work stress and are associated with cardiovascular diseases, diabetes mellitus, and musculoskeletal disorders. Imbalance due to high effort and low reward at the workplace among information technology (IT) professionals can lead to work stress.Aim:To assess the effort–reward imbalance (ERI) among IT professionals and its association with musculoskeletal pain.Settings and Design:A cross-sectional study was conducted among 200 IT professionals to assess their work-related stress and musculoskeletal pain.Methods and Material:A short version of the ERI questionnaire and Nordic musculoskeletal questionnaire was used. Along with the ERI questionnaire, the details on sociodemographic profile, chronic diseases, and substance abuses were ascertained.Result:Among the study population, the prevalence of musculoskeletal pain in any part of the body was 77% in the past one year. Effort–reward imbalance was present in 72% of the study population, and linear regression showed a significant correlation between the effort–reward component with the severity of musculoskeletal score (r = 0.725 P < 0.001). Odds ratio of ERI for musculoskeletal pain was 3.2, 95% CI (2.1-4.7), P < 0.01.Conclusions:Psychosocial factors at work assessed through ERI appeared to be an important component in the development of musculoskeletal pain among IT professionals.

INTRODUCTION

The IT sector in India is creating millions of employments and plays an important role in transforming our country’s economy.[1] Rapid technological development, increased worldwide competition, changing consumer demands, growth of the service sector, just‐in‐time management, minimum social contact, night shift, job insecurity, upgradation of new technology, time-constrained projects, work environment, and continuous visual focusing on the monitors have resulted in increasing stress among the employees.[2]

Psychosocial factors in the workplace are receiving increasing attention in the development of work stress. Occupational stress causes symptoms not only in the workplace but also tends to interfere with personal life leading to poor physical and emotional well-being.[3]

Effort–reward imbalance (ERI) model can be best used to assess the adverse health effects of stressful experiences at the workplace. An excessive effort has a significant positive relationship with psychosocial health problems and sickness absenteeism.[4] Rewards are best assessed in terms of monetary benefits, career opportunities, job security, and the experience of esteem and recognition for achievements. The ERI model also consists of an overcommitment component, which reflects an excessive personal dedication to work as a coping style and difficulty to withdraw from work.[5] Individual perception of an imbalance between the effort employed and the reward expected has a significant relationship with work associated musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs).

It is well established in occupational medicine that MSDs are strongly work-associated and one of the most prevalent occupational diseases in modern society. Research in occupational medicine has identified a number of biomechanical and psychosocial risk factors for the development of work-related MSDs. However, the potential role of psychosocial factors especially effort–reward imbalance and its relationship with MSD in IT professionals need to be explored.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

The current study is a cross-sectional study, where the questionnaire was sent to prospective participants via e-mail. IT professionals with more than 2-year experience, who have consented to take part in the study, were recruited from Elcot Sez, Sholinganallur, Chennai, through simple random sampling. This study was approved by the Institutional Research Committee and the Institutional Human Ethics Committee (IHEC No: 12/2020/10). Considering the prevalence of ERI as 70% from a study by Darboe et al.[6] with 10% precision and 95% confidence interval, using the formula 4Pq/d2, where P = 70, q = 30, d = 10% and with 15% nonresponse rate, the minimum sample size was 200.

Exclusion criteria

IT professionals involved in heavy physical work, those with a history of musculoskeletal injury due to any accident in the last 1 year.

Sociodemographic details including age, gender, work experience, and time spent in front of a computer per day were collected. The study included two dependent variables—effort–reward imbalance and Nordic musculoskeletal disorder.

ERI was measured by a standardized, psychometrically validated questionnaire by Siegrist et al.[7] It has three scales—effort scale composed of five items; the reward was measured by ten items. The effort reward ratio was calculated as (e/r) x c, where “c” is the correction factor for different numbers of items in the nominator and denominator. The correction factor in this study is 0.5 (5/10). A value of 1 indicates no imbalance, and values greater than 1.0 indicate a high amount of effort spent that is not met by the rewards received or expected in turn. Overcommitment questionnaires measuring personal coping characteristics are assessed by six questions measuring preoccupation with work and inability to switch off from work. The response format for all the questions was based on four-point Likert scale.

The Nordic musculoskeletal questionnaire (NMQ)[8] is a standardized, validated tool that consists of a general questionnaire for the analysis of point prevalence, period prevalence, and severity or disability (effect on pain/discomfort on normal activities over the last 12 months and last 7 days) of musculoskeletal pain in different body areas such as neck, shoulders, elbows, wrists/hands, upper back, lower back, hips/thighs, knees, and ankles/feet.

An index was created, based on the Nordic questionnaire, to represent the severity of symptoms in accordance with Cunha et al.[9] for each anatomic region, with scores between 0 and 4.

Score = 0: absence of symptoms.

Score = 1 means symptoms reported either in the last 7 days OR in the past 12 months.

Score = 2 means symptoms reported in the last 7 days AND in 12 months.

Score = 3 indicates that the person has had symptoms for 7 days OR 12 months and in addition had to be absent from work and

Score = 4 represents symptoms reported in the last 7 days AND in 12 months with absence at work.

The respondent is asked to report the occurrence of symptoms within the past 12 months and 7 days preceding the interview as well as any leave of absence over the previous year.

Statistical analysis was done using SPSS 17.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) software. Data were checked for normality using Shapiro–Wilk test. All normally distributed data were represented as mean and standard deviation. The prevalence for reported trouble in the last 12 months and within the last 7 days was calculated for all the nine anatomical locations. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was done to find the association between components of ERI with the sum of severity index of all nine sites and sickness absenteeism. Gender difference in ERI and the sum of severity index of musculoskeletal pain was assessed using an independent t-test. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed. P < 0.05 with two-tailed tests were considered significant.

DISCUSSION

The aim of this cross-sectional study was to examine the relationship between ERI and musculoskeletal symptoms. ERI implies that stress develops when there is an imbalance between the perceived effort at work and the rewards received. Employees with higher overcommitment in their work exhibit an inability to withdraw from work obligation and spend excessive time in work making them more vulnerable to develop stress. Long working hours tended to create stress. Tsutsumi et al.[10] showed a higher prevalence of ERI among people who had prolonged working hours, and the current study also shows a significant positive association between prolonged hours of computer use per day (long working hours) and ERI, indicating long hours of work contribute to work stress. The relationship between long working hours and work stress requires further investigation.

Sickness absenteeism determines the performance of the worker and could be a major cause of productivity loss.[11] According to WHO, musculoskeletal problem remains one of the main causes of sickness absenteeism that can have adverse effects for both individuals and organizations.[12] In this study, sickness absenteeism due to musculoskeletal pain in the past 1 year showed a positive association with effort, overcommitment, and ERI. We also observed sickness absenteeism to have a negative association with the reward component.

Epidemiological studies have shown that job stress is associated with increased risks of musculoskeletal injuries or symptoms.[13]

Working in computer stations leads to a set of musculoskeletal disorders. In the present study, neck, lower back, and wrist were the most common sites of musculoskeletal pain. About 77% of the study population have reported experiencing some form of pain in the past 12 months. Neck symptom is the most prevalent problem among IT professionals. Similar to present study, Hasanat et al[14] reported that in software engineers neck and lower back pain are the most common complaint.

Current study results suggest that there was an increased risk for musculoskeletal-related problems among those who reported high levels of both effort–reward imbalance (ERI ratio > 1) and overcommitment. Overcommitment related to an increased risk of musculoskeletal symptoms is in line with the previous study by Yu et al.[15] and Ruela et al.[16] but contrary to the findings by De Jonge et al.[17]

ERI, effort, reward, and overcommitment subscale were significantly associated with musculoskeletal symptoms. In line with our current findings, Herin et al.[18] also reported that ERI scores, effort, and reward subscales were significantly associated with musculoskeletal symptoms. In a study by Redivo et al.,[19] in visual display units users, showed MSD group had higher overcommitment than the controls who were free of MSD.

We observed an odds ratio of 3.2 for ERI on musculoskeletal indicating an increase in the risk of musculoskeletal-related problems at ERI ratio levels above 1. Similarly, Bernal et al. reported an odds ratio of 6.13 (95%CI 5.32–7.07) for ERI and musculoskeletal disorders.[20] Therefore, the present study indicates that high efforts along with low reward and higher inclination towards over commitment increase the risk of musculoskeletal pain among IT professionals.

In physiological terms, musculoskeletal pain produced by the work stress model of ERI and overcommitment can be due to repeated activation of low-threshold motor units in specific muscles after stress-induced systemic activity of the autonomic nervous system leading to increased muscle reactivity and delayed recovery.[21] Consistent with our current study, Strom et al. have shown central region of body like neck and shoulders are more susceptible for muscular tension and leading to detrimental musculoskeletal symptoms.[22] Hence, Psychosocial stressors within a workplace are stronger risk factors for the development of musculoskeletal-related problems.

This study indicates psychosocial factors in workplaces such as high effort, low reward, and high overcommitment that is the personal ways of coping with demands are potential contributors to musculoskeletal pain.

The limitations in the study are that it is a cross-sectional study that precludes making causal conclusions. Longitudinal studies are needed in the future to examine the causal relationships among the variables. A self-report questionnaire was used in our study rather than conducting face-to-face investigations.

CONCLUSION

ERI questionnaire can be used to identify work-related risk factors for musculoskeletal pain. Besides this study, we are not aware of any other study among IT professionals assessing psychosocial work environment and musculoskeletal pain. In this way, the results of this study will support the still lacking establishment of public health prevention policies and surveillance programs among IT professionals in India. So, addressing ERI and musculoskeletal disorders through health education and awareness programs among IT employees in workplace will reduce stress and improve their health.
