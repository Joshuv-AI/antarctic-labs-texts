Artificial intelligence in emergency mechanical ventilation: a prospective observational comparison with emergency physician ventilator settings
Authors: Gürkan Altuntaş, İsmail Ataş, Mümin Murat Yazıcı, Özlem Bilir, Bünyamin Onur Harmancı, Enes Hamdioğlu, Nurullah Parça
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) has the potential to support clinicians in high-risk and complex decision-making processes, such as mechanical ventilation. This prospective observational study aimed to compare mechanical ventilator settings determined by emergency physician (EP) with recommendations generated by three large language models (ChatGPT-5, Gemini, and Copilot) in the emergency department (ED).MethodsThis prospective, analytical, single-center study included 30 intubated patients managed in an ED over a three-month period. Clinical data, including diagnoses, vital signs, and initial arterial blood gas parameters, were presented to ChatGPT-5, Gemini, and Copilot. The AI models’ recommendations for ventilation mode, tidal volume, respiratory rate, PEEP, and FiO₂ were compared with the initial settings adjusted by EP. Agreement for ventilator mode selection was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistics, while agreement for continuous ventilator parameters was evaluated using Bland–Altman analysis.ResultsA total of 30 patients were included in the study. The median age was 73 years (IQR: 60–84), and 66.7% were male. When the ventilator setting preferences of the EP were analyzed, the most commonly used modes were VCV (46.7%) and SIMV (40.0%). Among the AI models, ChatGPT-5 primarily recommended VCV (76.7%) and, to a lesser extent, CPAP (10.0%); Gemini most frequently preferred VCV (56.7%) and PCV (43.3%); and Copilot predominantly recommended PCV (70.0%). Data on the compatibility of mechanical ventilator mode selection revealed that AI models showed ‘poor’ agreement with expert opinion (EP) based on diagnosis. ChatGPT showed 50.0% agreement (Cohen’s kappa: 0.199; 95% Confidence Interval (CI): −0.087 to 0.486), Google Gemini 43.3% agreement (Cohen’s kappa: 0.164; 95% CI: −0.098 to 0.426), and Microsoft Copilot 20.0% agreement (Cohen’s kappa: −0.043; 95% CI: −0.230 to 0.143).ConclusionAgreement between AI-generated ventilator settings and the EP was limited. Current AI models may offer supportive input; however, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary and exploratory, and further large-scale, multicenter studies are needed to validate these results.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-026-01627-3.Keywords: Mechanical ventilation, Artificial intelligence, Ventilator settings, Decision support systems, Emergency medicine

Introduction

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI)–based decision support systems have emerged as promising tools for mechanical ventilation management. Large language models (LLMs) and machine learning techniques have the potential to recommend personalized ventilator settings by analyzing patient data [1, 2]. In emergency medicine, where rapid and accurate decision-making is critical, such technologies are gaining increasing attention [3, 4].

Mechanical ventilation is a life-saving intervention for patients with acute respiratory failure, and appropriate adjustment of ventilator parameters directly affects patient outcomes, including the risk of ventilator-associated complications and mortality [5, 6]. However, optimizing ventilator settings in the emergency department remains challenging due to time constraints and the dynamic nature of critically ill patients [7].

Current literature on AI-assisted ventilator management is largely focused on intensive care settings, and studies in the emergency department are limited [8, 9]. In addition, the extent to which AI-generated recommendations align with clinician decisions and their applicability across different clinical scenarios remains unclear [10, 11].

Recent randomized controlled trials evaluating automated closed-loop ventilation systems have reported no significant differences in patient-centered outcomes compared with protocolized conventional ventilation strategies. However, these studies assess automated ventilator control systems rather than AI-based clinical decision support tools. Therefore, the role of LLM-based systems in supporting ventilator management decisions in emergency settings remains insufficiently explored [12].

This study aimed to compare ventilator settings determined by an emergency physician (EP) with recommendations from three AI models (ChatGPT-5, Gemini, and Copilot) in patients receiving mechanical ventilation in the emergency department.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This prospective, observational, single-center study was designed as an exploratory pilot study conducted in the emergency department of a tertiary care hospital. The study included patients who were intubated in the emergency department or brought to the emergency department intubated and connected to a mechanical ventilator within a three-month period. For each patient, the first arterial blood gas parameters obtained after intubation and the EP’s mechanical ventilator settings were recorded. These data were compared with ventilator settings recommended by three different AI models (ChatGPT-5, Gemini, and Copilot) by presenting the same parameters. Approval was obtained from the Recep Tayyip Erdoğan University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee prior to conducting the study (Decision No: 2025/363). The ethics approval included the use of fully anonymized clinical data for analysis and entry into artificial intelligence models; therefore, all data were entered into the AI systems in anonymized form. Informed consent for participation was obtained from patients’ legal representatives when required in accordance with institutional policies and ethical standards. We used the STROBE reporting guideline to draft this manuscript, and the STROBE reporting checklist when editing, included in Supplementary Material A.

Participant selection and data collection

During the study period, all patients who were intubated in the emergency department and connected to a mechanical ventilator were included in the study. Exclusion criteria were patients under the age of 18 years, patients whose relatives did not give consent for the study, and patients who were not connected to a mechanical ventilator because their procedures in the emergency department were short. All eligible patients during the study period were consecutively included to minimize selection bias.

Demographic data, vital signs, emergency department diagnoses, emergency department outcomes (hospitalization and exitus), and arterial blood gas parameters were obtained from patient files and the hospital information management system (HIS). Mechanical ventilator settings by EP and ventilator settings suggested by three different AI models (ChatGPT-5, Gemini, and Copilot) were simultaneously recorded in standard patient collection templates. All data were recorded using a preprepared standard data coding template and converted into a format suitable for statistical analysis.

Patient involvement

Patients or members of the public were not directly involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of this research. The study was based on prospective data obtained from hospital records, and no interventions were performed beyond standard clinical care. However, the outcomes of this study aim to contribute to the development of safer and more efficient AI-assisted mechanical ventilation strategies that could ultimately improve patient outcomes and enhance emergency care practices in the future.

Study protocol

The management of patients who are intubated in the emergency department or who arrive at the emergency department intubated is the responsibility of EPs. In the follow-up process, intubated patients were connected to a mechanical ventilator by an EP, and the initial parameter settings of the ventilator were meticulously adjusted in accordance with the patient’s physiology.

The first arterial blood gas analysis obtained immediately after endotracheal intubation in the emergency department was performed by an EP, and the results were evaluated. These parameters corresponded to the initial post-intubation stabilization period and were the values presented to the AI models. All measurements were obtained using standard hospital monitoring systems and laboratory analyzers used in routine clinical practice. Within the scope of our study, basic parameters, such as pH, partial carbon dioxide pressure (PaCO₂), partial oxygen pressure (PaO₂), oxygen saturation (SaO₂), lactate, and bicarbonate (HCO₃-) in arterial blood gas analysis, obtained from intubated patients in the emergency department were recorded in the patient collection template. These data were systematically archived for use in the evaluation of decision processes related to mechanical ventilation management.

Mechanical ventilator settings were adjusted by a single EP with five years of clinical experience who had received formal training in mechanical ventilation and routinely applied lung-protective ventilation strategies, considering patients’ blood gas parameters and overall clinical status. Mechanical ventilator settings are based on five basic parameters: ventilation mode, tidal volume, respiratory rate, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), and inspiratory oxygen fraction (FiO₂). Mechanical ventilator devices used in the study included Biyovent (Türkiye), Newport (USA), and Dräger Oxylog 3000 plus (Germany). Across these devices, commonly available ventilation modes included volume-controlled ventilation (VCV), pressure-controlled ventilation (PCV), synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation (SIMV), pressure support ventilation (PSV), continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), and, in some models, airway pressure release ventilation (APRV) or noninvasive positive pressure ventilation (NPPV) [13–15]. Ventilation parameters, including mode selection, tidal volume, PEEP, and FiO₂, were adjusted according to lung-protective strategies and tailored to each patient’s lung mechanics, oxygenation status, and overall clinical condition.

In the next stage of the study, each patient’s diagnosis, height-based ideal body weight (IBW), vital signs, and basic arterial blood gas parameters were presented to three different AI models: ChatGPT-5 (OpenAI, USA), Gemini 2.5 flash (Google DeepMind, USA/UK), and Copilot (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). Each case was queried individually on 7 December 2025 between 10:00 and 12:00 using the paid versions of the respective platforms. To prevent contextual carryover, every patient scenario was entered in a separate and independent session, and identical prompts and formatting were used for all models. No multi-case conversations were conducted. For each case, the models were asked to provide recommendations for five basic mechanical ventilator parameters: ventilation mode, tidal volume, respiratory rate, PEEP, and FiO₂ (Fig. 1). The prompt structure and representative AI responses are also illustrated in Fig. 1. The detailed AI query protocol, including prompt structure and output extraction rules, is provided in Supplementary Material B. The recommendations of the AIs were obtained through the ability of the respective models to analyze clinical data and provide decision support thanks to their natural language processing and LLM-based structures. ChatGPT-5 is an AI system built on a large language model architecture that can make recommendations for medical text analysis and clinical decision support applications [16]. Gemini, on the other hand, can be used in the analysis of clinical parameters, with its large-scale data processing and clinical decision recommendation capability [17]. Copilot’s potential lies in healthcare software development, simplifying tasks from analyzing medical records to developing health applications [18].

Responses of chatGPT-5, google gemini, and microsoft copilot to the same simulated clinical scenario

The recommendations generated by the AI models were compared with the ventilator settings determined by the EP. The primary outcome of the study was the level of agreement between AI-generated ventilator settings and those determined by the EP in the emergency department setting. Given the exploratory nature of the study, the analyses were intended to provide preliminary insights rather than definitive conclusions.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using Jamovi statistical software (The Jamovi Project, 2021, version 1.6, Sydney, Australia). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR); categorical variables were presented as number (percentage, %). Agreement for the categorical variable (ventilator mode) was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistics. Agreement for continuous ventilator parameters (tidal volume, respiratory rate, PEEP, and FiO₂) was evaluated using Bland–Altman analysis, with mean bias and limits of agreement reported. In all statistical analyses, p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

During the study period, all patients who were intubated in the emergency department and connected to a mechanical ventilator were included. Exclusion criteria included one patient under 18 years of age, two patients whose relatives did not give consent for the study, and two patients who underwent only manual ventilation with a self-inflating balloon and were not connected to a mechanical ventilator because their procedures in the emergency department were short. As a result, 30 patients were included in the study. The median age was 73 years (IQR: 60–84), and 66.7% were male. The most common comorbidities were hypertension (66.7%), congestive heart failure (26.7%), diabetes mellitus (23.3%), and atrial fibrillation (23.3%). The most common admission diagnoses were pneumonia (13.3%), hemorrhagic stroke (13.3%), and pulmonary edema (13.3%). The mortality rate in the study population was 16.7% (Table 1).

Discussion

AI has become an innovative tool in emergency medicine practice in recent years, supporting rapid decision-making processes, reducing the margin of error, and attracting attention with its capacity to analyze clinical data. In the emergency department environment, where time is essential for critically ill patients, the potential of AI systems to support clinicians in complex and dynamic decision processes, such as ventilator management, is increasing. In this study, we compared the performance of EP and three different AI models (ChatGPT-5, Gemini, and Copilot) in the mechanical ventilator settings of intubated patients in the emergency department. The findings revealed that the AI models provided feasible recommendations for some of the ventilator parameters but could not replace the clinical experience of EP.

Significant response differences were observed between the three AI models. Gemini adopted the most cautious approach, frequently emphasizing clinical limitations, whereas Copilot provided the most direct and explicit parameter recommendations. ChatGPT-5 balanced these approaches by offering both descriptive and practical guidance. Importantly, all models emphasized that clinician judgment remains indispensable, highlighting the supportive rather than substitutive role of AI. The particularly cautious behavior of models such as Gemini may reflect built-in safety filters and defensive algorithms designed to limit medico-legal risks. This was especially evident in high-risk conditions such as sepsis, where AI models tended to avoid providing definitive recommendations.

The use of AI-based clinical decision support systems raises several ethical concerns. Utilization of real-world clinical data requires strict protection of patient privacy, secure data management, and transparent governance. AI models may also reproduce biases present in training data, potentially leading to unequal recommendations across subgroups. Furthermore, the “black-box” nature of many algorithms limits transparency and explainability, complicating clinical interpretation. Integration of AI tools in emergency medical settings may additionally create a risk of over-reliance and reduced clinical autonomy [19]. Consequently, clinical adoption of AI must be supported by robust ethical frameworks, continuous auditing, regulatory oversight, and clearly defined human–machine responsibility.

Agreement analysis using Cohen’s kappa demonstrated very limited concordance between the AI models and the EP in ventilator mode selection. Although ChatGPT-5 showed the highest agreement among the evaluated models, the kappa values indicated only slight agreement beyond chance. Gemini demonstrated similarly low concordance, while Copilot showed no meaningful agreement, with a negative kappa value suggesting agreement no better than chance. These findings indicate that AI models frequently produced ventilator mode recommendations that suggesting agreement close to chance relative clinician decision-making in the emergency department setting.

Agreement between AI models and the clinician for continuous ventilator parameters showed relatively small mean differences for respiratory rate and PEEP. However, the wide limits of agreement indicated substantial variability at the individual patient level, particularly for tidal volume and FiO₂ recommendations. These findings suggest that although AI models may approximate clinician decisions in some parameters, their recommendations may diverge in specific clinical situations, underscoring the importance of clinician oversight when interpreting AI-generated ventilator settings. From a clinical perspective, some of the observed differences in ventilator parameters, such as tidal volume, respiratory rate, and PEEP, may fall within clinically acceptable ranges used in routine practice and may not necessarily translate into meaningful differences in patient outcomes. Therefore, statistical variability between AI models and clinician settings should be interpreted in the context of clinical relevance and established lung-protective ventilation strategies. For example, while some tidal volume recommendations may fall within the commonly accepted range of 6–8 mL/kg based on ideal body weight, the wide limits of agreement observed in this study indicate that variability at the individual patient level may still be clinically relevant in certain cases.

These findings likely reflect fundamental differences between clinician reasoning and AI-based decision generation. EPs integrate dynamic clinical information such as bedside examination findings, real-time treatment response, and evolving physiological trends, whereas large language models generate recommendations primarily from static data inputs such as isolated blood gas values. Consequently, AI systems may perform more consistently in structured or protocol-based tasks but may struggle to capture the full clinical complexity of ventilator management in emergency settings. Another important factor is the clinical timing of FiO₂ adjustment. In routine emergency practice, FiO₂ is often initiated at relatively high levels immediately after intubation to ensure adequate oxygenation during stabilization and is subsequently titrated according to oxygenation targets. Therefore, differences between AI-generated FiO₂ recommendations and clinician settings should be interpreted cautiously within the context of early post-intubation management rather than being considered inherently incorrect. Similar observations were reported by Gün, who emphasized that although artificial intelligence can provide reliable guidance in structured analytical tasks such as blood gas interpretation, comprehensive clinician judgment remains essential for individualized ventilator management [20]. Unlike automated or closed-loop ventilation systems that directly adjust ventilator parameters in real time, large language model–based systems provide recommendation-based decision support and do not autonomously control ventilator settings.

In the current literature, it has been reported that AI-based decision support systems are promising for ventilator management but have not yet been fully integrated into clinical practice [1, 8]. Reinforcement learning (RL)-based optimization systems in particular have shown a high success rate at the theoretical level but remain limited due to patient heterogeneity and clinical safety. In addition to the literature, our study makes a unique contribution in terms of the simultaneous comparison of different AI models in the same patient group. It should also be noted that the present analysis evaluates the level of agreement between AI-generated recommendations and clinician-selected ventilator settings rather than the clinical correctness or outcome superiority of either approach. Therefore, discrepancies between AI recommendations and clinician decisions should be interpreted as differences in decision-making approaches rather than indicators of clinical inferiority.

Artificial intelligence models may be a valuable decision support tool for ventilator management in the emergency department, saving clinicians time, particularly in determining the initial ventilator settings. However, the experience of EP and patient-specific assessments should always be prioritized in the clinical decision-making process. Future studies should include larger validation studies with multicenter patient populations, different AI versions, and clinical integration pilots. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary and exploratory and may not be directly generalizable to all emergency department settings.

Limitations

The strengths of this study include the comparison of three different AI models within the same patient population. Key limitations are the single-center design, the limited sample size, and the lack of transparency in AI decision-making mechanisms. Although blood gas parameters were used as inputs, they were available only as single time-point measurements. Serial blood gas analyses could potentially improve AI performance by capturing dynamic physiological changes, particularly in conditions such as sepsis or acute respiratory failure. Another limitation is that the recommendations were obtained from specific versions of large language models at a single point in time. Because LLM systems evolve rapidly and may behave differently across versions or deployment settings, the reproducibility of these results may vary in future implementations. Another limitation of this study is that the reference ventilator settings were determined by a single EP, which may introduce potential reference bias and reflect individual clinical practice rather than a standardized or consensus-based benchmark, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings. This may also influence the observed agreement levels between AI models and clinician decisions. The use of different ventilator devices in the emergency department may represent a potential source of variability, although the main ventilation modes analyzed in this study were available across all devices. However, this could not be assessed due to the absence of repeated measurements. The inclusion of only 30 patients is an important limitation; nevertheless, this study represents a preliminary exploratory pilot investigation that provides initial evidence on the feasibility of AI-supported ventilator management and establishes a foundation for future large-scale, multicenter studies. The relatively small sample size limits the statistical power of the study and may affect the stability and precision of agreement estimates, particularly for Cohen’s kappa and Bland–Altman analyses.

Conclusion

Agreement between AI-generated ventilator settings and the EP was generally limited, particularly for ventilator mode selection. These findings suggest that current AI systems do not consistently align with clinician decision-making in emergency department mechanical ventilation management. Although AI models may offer structured suggestions for ventilator parameters, their recommendations showed variability and should be interpreted cautiously. These findings are preliminary and exploratory; therefore, AI tools should be considered supportive clinical decision aids, while clinician judgment remains essential for safe and individualized ventilator management.
