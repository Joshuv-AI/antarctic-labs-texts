Eligibility versus appropriateness in allogeneic HCT
Authors: Mohamed L Sorror, Andrew S Artz, Rebecca L Olin, Reshma Ramlal, Sergio A Giralt
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR:

Allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (allo-HCT) has expanded into older age groups due to advances in conditioning, donor availability, and supportive care.1, 2, 3 Although chronological age is no longer an absolute contraindication, it continues to influence outcomes and decision-making in ways not fully captured by traditional risk models. This perspective was prompted by an audience question at a presentation at the 2026 Transplantation & Cellular Therapy Meeting at Salt Lake City, UT, arising from 2 clinical cases with adverse biology but divergent transplant recommendations. These cases exposed a deeper conceptual issue: age in transplantation is often treated as either irrelevant (“just a number”) or determinative, when in fact it operates along 2 distinct dimensions, prognostic and contextual.

As transplant candidacy expands into the eighth decade of life, clearer conceptual frameworks are required to avoid both reflexive age exclusion and indiscriminate therapeutic escalation.

Case 1 involved a 74-year-old man with TP53-mutated myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) (TP53 p.R280G, variant allele frequency 7.7%) and multiple comutations in addition to significant comorbidity burden (hematopoietic cell transplantation-comorbidity index [HCT-CI] 8; prior colon cancer, diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon corrected of 61%, and rheumatologic disease). Here, both transplant-related mortality and relapse risk are expected to be substantial. In biologic assignment studies comparing reduced-intensity allo-HCT to nontransplant therapy in patients aged 50 to 75 years with advanced MDS, adjusted 3-year overall survival (OS) for transplant recipients was ∼48%, with leukemia-free survival ∼36%, although these analyses were not stratified by TP53 mutation and underrepresented patients with very high comorbidity scores.4 In contrast, outcomes specifically for TP53-altered myeloid neoplasms after allo-HCT have been consistently inferior; registry and multicenter analyses report ∼20% to 30% 3-year OS, with relapse rates exceeding 50% in many series.5 When these disease-specific risks are combined with advanced age and an HCT-CI score of 8, parameters associated with markedly increased nonrelapse mortality (NRM) in comorbidity analyses,6 the expected probability of durable remission becomes limited.

Case 2 involved a 60-year-old man with inv(3) adverse-risk acute myeloid leukemia (AML) in first complete remission after azacitidine/venetoclax, HCT-CI of 4 (cardiac ejection fraction 35%, atrial fibrillation, diabetes, and obesity). Here, relapse risk without transplantation is extremely high. Contemporary series demonstrate 3-year OS rates of <10% for patients with inv(3) AML treated without allo-HCT.7 In transplant cohorts of adverse-risk AML, relapse remains substantial but is reduced compared with nontransplant strategies, with relapse risks commonly reported in the range of ∼40% to 50% and NRM near 20% to 30%, depending on conditioning intensity and comorbidity burden.7, 8, 9 Importantly, comorbidity-adjusted analyses suggest that patients with HCT-CI scores around 4 remain within ranges where transplant-related mortality is elevated but not prohibitive,8 particularly in the setting of otherwise potentially curable disease.

One presenter debated against transplantation for case 1 but agreed on transplantation for case 2.

The challenging question: why should transplant be discouraged in 1 patient and supported in the other when both TP53 alteration and inv(3) predict high relapse risk after allo-HCT? Is age truly relevant if it is often said to be “just a number”?

At first glance, the differing recommendations appear inconsistent if disease biology alone is considered. However, the intersection of age, comorbidity burden, and expected disease trajectory produces markedly different proportional benefits from allo-HCT.

Prognostic role of age and comorbidity: the prognostic impact of age on NRM after allo-HCT is measurable but often modest in multivariable models10,11; however, its effect becomes magnified when interacting with comorbidity burden and disease status.12 Comorbidity burden, quantified by the HCT-CI, further amplifies risk, particularly at extreme scores (≥7-8). Clinical experience and registry data suggest that physiological reserve differs meaningfully between a 60-year-old patient with HCT-CI score of 4 and a 74-year-old patient with HCT-CI score of 8, even when both meet formal eligibility criteria. Extreme HCT-CI scores (≥7-8) are associated with sharply increased early mortality,6 and such patients are underrepresented in registry-derived risk models. Importantly, age retains independent prognostic significance even when adjusting for HCT-CI, performance status, and conditioning intensity,12 underscoring that comorbidity does not fully account for age-related vulnerability.

Thus, age does not merely accompany comorbidity; it modifies how comorbidity is tolerated and how risk translates into early mortality.

For quality of life (QOL) and functional outcomes, the impact of allo-HCT on QOL and functional independence is central in older adult to decision-making. Prospective longitudinal data demonstrate that older allo-HCT recipients experience greater declines in physical function and QOL during the first 1 to 2 years compared with non-HCT counterparts, and recovery trajectories are heterogeneous.13 Patients in the highest third of scores per the Comprehensive Health Assessment Risk Model experience both increased NRM and decreased disability-free and frailty-free-survival,14 which are of great importance to the older population.

Contrary to the concern that transplant uniformly worsens QOL, a prospective donor vs no donor analysis demonstrated comparable longer-term QOL between groups.15 AML or MDS is associated with substantial and persistent decrements in global QOL, transfusion dependence, and psychological distress, particularly in relapsed or refractory states.16 Remission status is strongly associated with improved well-being.16 Thus, the potential of allo-HCT of improving short-term QOL is not inconsequential. In selected patients, allo-HCT may preserve or restore meaningful QOL by preventing relapse-driven decline.

Beyond its prognostic association with NRM, age provides essential context for treatment decision-making by defining remaining life expectancy, competing risks of death, and patient priorities. In the general population, individuals in their early 60s have an estimated remaining life expectancy exceeding 20 years, whereas those in their mid- to late 70s have a remaining expectancy closer to 8 to 10 years, even before accounting for comorbid illness.17,18 Time horizon matters. A 10% to 15% absolute improvement in 3-year survival has fundamentally different implications for a patient with an anticipated 25-year life expectancy than for one with an expected survival of less than a decade. Age does not merely modify transplant risk; it modifies the meaning of benefit.

This distinction is further reinforced by geriatric and oncology studies demonstrating that although younger patients are more willing to accept substantial treatment-related toxicity for potential long-term disease control, older adults are more likely to prioritize functional independence, symptom control, and QOL over marginal extensions in survival.19 In the context of allo-HCT, these age-associated differences in life expectancy and goals of care critically shape the proportionality of risk and benefit and should be explicitly incorporated into shared decision-making.13

As our field increasingly treats older adults, the question is no longer whether they can undergo transplantation, but whether they should. Age should neither be dismissed nor weaponized in transplant decision-making. It must be recognized as both a biologic modifier of risk and a contextual determinant of proportional benefit. What appears to be inconsistency in recommendation may instead reflect appropriate alignment of therapy intensity with disease biology, physiological reserve, remaining life expectancy, and patient-defined goals. Eligibility is a threshold determination; appropriateness is a clinical judgment. Conflating the 2, risks both overtreatment and undertreatment. A more explicit acknowledgment of age’s dual role may improve transparency, consistency, and patient centeredness in allo-HCT decision-making.
