Association of vitamin K intake with physical function, handgrip strength, and mortality among U.S. adults: NHANES 2003–2016
Authors: Hyunmi Han, Clara Yongjoo Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND/OBJECTIVESVitamin K (VK) plays a key role in regulating calcium homeostasis in bone and soft tissues and may be associated with physical function (PF), handgrip strength (HGS), and mortality; however, nationally-representative studies are scarce.SUBJECTS/METHODSIn this cross-sectional study, we assessed the association of VK intake with PF, HGS, and mortality in adults aged 50 and older using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2003–2016 (n = 4,950). VK intake was assessed using two 24-h dietary recalls and dietary supplement use questionnaires and categorized as < or ≥ the Adequate Intake (AI). Self-reported PF was categorized into 5 activities (activities of daily living, instrumental activities of daily living [IADL], lower extremity mobility [LEM], general physical activities, and leisure and social activities). HGS was measured using a grip strength dynamometer. Linear models, odds ratios, and Cox proportional hazards analyses were performed. All analytical models were adjusted for potential confounders.RESULTSVK intake meeting the AI was associated with a 0.60-fold (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.43–0.84) and 0.64-fold (95% CI, 0.47–0.89) decrease in the odds of IADL and LEM difficulty, respectively. When stratified by sex, these associations were significant only in males. However, VK intake was not associated with HGS or mortality.CONCLUSIONVK intake meeting the AI is associated with lower odds of impaired IADL and LEM, especially in males.Keywords: Vitamin K, physical functional performance, hand strength, mortality, NHANES

INTRODUCTION

The decline in physical function (PF) among older adults is linked to diminished quality of life, including physical, mental, and economic challenges, ultimately culminating in increased mortality [1,2,3]. As the aging population grows, this decline also imposes substantial socioeconomic burdens due to higher medical costs and care dependency [4,5]. Therefore, identifying modifiable nutritional factors to maintain and improve PF among older adults is a key public health priority.

Vitamin K (VK) intake may facilitate maintenance of PF. VK is predominantly present in green vegetables, meat, and fermented foods, and acts as a cofactor for the carboxylation of Gla-containing proteins such as osteocalcin and matrix Gla protein (MGP), regulating bone and muscle homeostasis. Additionally, VK directly affects skeletal muscle cell proliferation and migration [6,7]. VK deficiency may impair the activation of these proteins, leading to various dysfunctions such as osteoporosis, soft tissue and vascular calcification, cognitive decline, and deterioration in PF—effects that are particularly critical in older adults [8,9]. However, most prior studies have focused on serum VK concentrations as a marker of VK status rather than dietary intake [10,11]. Although serum VK levels incorporate intake and absorption efficiency which may have higher physiological relevance, circulating VK, such as phylloquinone (VK1), has a short half-life of approximately 6–10 h, which can result in inaccurate estimates of habitual VK intake and status [12,13]. One previous study showed different relationships of mobility limitation and disability with plasma VK1 and dephospho-uncarboxylated MGP, a metabolite of VK known to be more stable in plasma [11]. However, few studies have assessed plasma dephospho-uncarboxylated MGP as a marker of VK status. On the other hand, PF and handgrip strength (HGS) differ according to sex and gradually decline with age [14,15]. Likewise, the Dietary Reference Intakes (DRIs) are set according to sex and age due to the physiological and metabolic differences between sexes and age [16]. Therefore, when evaluating the relationship between VK intake and health outcomes, it is necessary to consider intakes and stratify participants by age and sex; however, this has seldom been accounted for in previous studies.

Previous studies on the association between serum VK status or VK intake and mortality has yielded conflicting results [17,18,19,20]. Among European populations, some studies reported a potential association between VK intake and all-cause mortality [19]. However, the relatively short follow-up duration of 4.8 yrs limits the ability to adequately account for deaths attributable to preexisting health conditions. In contrast, a long-term follow-up study in Danish populations confirmed a negative association between VK and mortality risk [20]. Participants in European studies where no association between mortality and VK intake or serum VK status was found were relatively young (baseline mean age 48.6 yrs), which may partially explain the absence of a detectable effect on mortality [17]. Similarly, studies in the United States (U.S.) have examined the association between VK and mortality; however, these studies primarily included Black participants or individuals with kidney disease, limiting the generalizability of the findings to the broader U.S. population [21,22,23].

Therefore, evaluating the relationship between VK intake, PF, HGS, and mortality according to sex and age can provide a better understanding of VK’s role in maintaining physical health and longevity. Accordingly, the objective of this study was to stratify adults aged 50 and older by sex and age using nationally representative data from the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2003–2016 to examine the relationship between VK intake and PF, HGS, and mortality.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Study population

Adults aged 50 and older who participated in dietary intake surveys twice (n = 15,330) of the U.S. NHANES 2003–2016 were included. Data from 2003 to 2016 were utilized, as this period provided a sufficient sample size and allowed for longitudinal follow-up of VK intake and mortality outcomes. Decline and thus differences in PF assessed by questionnaires utilized in the current study are rare in healthy young adults [24]; therefore, only adults 50 yrs and older were included. Individuals were excluded if they did not respond to at least one question on the self-reported PF (n = 7,307), were taking anticoagulants or neuromuscular blockers (n = 887), had a history of stroke (n = 247), fracture (n = 610) or cancer (n = 1,178), reported implausible total energy intake of < 500 or ≥ 5,000 kcal/day (n = 143), or were missing information on smoking or education status (n = 8) (Fig. 1). Excluded participants had a lower mean age (62.3 ± 0.2 vs. 65.7 ± 0.2 yrs), with a higher proportion of men compared to the included cohort. Body mass index, income level, smoking status, drinking status, energy intake, and Healthy Eating Index (HEI) scores were higher in the excluded cohort than included cohort, whereas protein intake and VK intake were comparable between the 2 cohorts (Supplementary Table 1). For the analysis of the association between VK intake and mortality, 4,399 individuals were analyzed (Fig. 1). HGS was measured from 2011–2014 due to data availability (n = 2,357; Supplementary Fig. 1). The protocol was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board (protocol numbers: 98-12, 2005-06, 2011-17, 2018-01, 2021-05).

Diet assessment

Dietary intake was assessed by averaging the data from 2 non-consecutive 24-h dietary recalls. For dietary VK supplements, information on the type of supplement, duration of use, number of intake days, and amount consumed over the past 30 days was collected. The reported product names were then matched to the NHANES Dietary Supplement Database to calculate the monthly VK supplement intake which was then divided by 30 to determine mean daily VK intake from supplements [25]. VK intake was categorized as < or ≥ 120 µg/day for males and < or ≥ 90 µg/day for females according to the Adequate Intake (AI) from the DRIs for North Americans [26]. Sensitivity analysis was conducted based on age and sex using various cutoff criteria, including intake percentiles 30, 50, and 70, and VK intake per body weight (kg). While data on dietary supplements were not available for participants from 2003–2006, 13.3% of the overall participants in 2007–2016 reported consuming VK containing supplements. When combining dietary and supplemental VK intake and categorizing them based on AI levels, the proportion of individuals whose group changed was minimal (1.8%, n = 89). Therefore, the 2003–2006 analysis used dietary intake only. The HEI-2015 was calculated using the codes provided by the National Cancer Institute for NHANES (2005–2016) [27]. The total HEI score (out of 100 points) and the HEI total score excluding total vegetables, greens, and beans (out of 90 points) were calculated.

PF and HGS assessment

PF measures from NHANES, whose validity has been previously established, were utilized in this study [28]. PF was assessed using 19 questionnaire items. Participants self-reported their level of difficulty for each item on a 4-point scale (1 = no difficulty, 2 = some difficulty, 3 = much difficulty, 4 = unable to do). Responses of “some difficulty” or higher were categorized as “having difficulty,” with a score of 1 assigned for “having difficulty” and 0 for “no difficulty.” Referring to previous studies, the 19 items were classified into 5 categories: activities of daily living (ADL), instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), lower extremity mobility (LEM), general physical activities, and leisure and social activities (LSA) [29,30].

HGS was measured using a digital grip strength dynamometer (Takei T.K.K.5401; Takei Scientific Instruments, Tokyo, Japan). Detailed procedures and protocols can be found in the examination manual [31]. HGS was measured 3 times for each hand, totaling 6 measurements, and the highest value was recorded.

Mortality data

Mortality data were acquired by linking NHANES data with mortality statistics from the National Center for Health Statistics up to December 31, 2019 [32]. Causes of death were classified according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9 and ICD-10, including deaths related to cardiovascular diseases, malignant neoplasms, and all other causes. However, due to the limited number of deaths, conducting cause-specific analysis was not feasible; thus, only all-cause mortality was examined.

Other variables

Participant characteristics, including sex, race, smoking status, drinking status, family income, education level, history of diseases, and physical activity, were investigated through the questionnaire, whereas height and weight were measured directly. Disease status was determined based on self-report for diabetes, kidney disease, osteoarthritis, congestive heart failure, coronary artery disease, angina, heart attack, and liver disease, as well as measurement-based confirmation of hypertension. Hypertension was classified as taking medication for hypertension or having a mean systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg or a mean diastolic blood pressure ≥ 80 mmHg [33]. Menopausal status was defined as either reporting the absence of menstrual bleeding in the preceding 12 mon or reporting a history of hysterectomy. Hormone use was assessed through the questionnaire.

Statistical analyses

Participants were stratified according to sex as both VK intake and PF differ by sex. Participants were also categorized by age (< 70 and ≥ 70 yrs). Given the marked decline in PF after age 70 and the age-specific categorization of DRIs, the results were stratified to reflect the applicability of the DRI criteria [34,35]. The interaction effects of age or sex on PF according to VK intake were also tested. A complex sample design analysis was performed after applying weights, stratification variables (strata), and primary sample units [36]. Participant characteristics were compared using χ2 and t-tests. Logistic regression and multiple regression analysis were performed to assess the association between PF and HGS, respectively, in relation to VK intake levels, stratified by sex and age. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was additionally performed to examine the potential non-linear association between VK and HGS. The association between VK intake and mortality was analyzed using Cox proportional hazards regression analysis, excluding deaths occurring within 2-yr of follow-up. Models were adjusted for sex, age, race, education, income level, disease, current smoker, alcohol consumption, survey year, physical activity, body mass index, total energy intake, protein intake, and HEI total score or HEI total score excluding total vegetables, greens, and beans. In stratified analyses by sex or age, the covariates sex or age were excluded, respectively. Further adjustments were performed for osteoarthritis status, menopausal status, and hormone use. Baseline PF limitation was additionally adjusted in the assessment of the association between VK intake and mortality. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, and all statistical analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

Over half of the participants had VK intakes below the AI (n = 3,083, 58.4%). Mean age was statistically comparable between the adults with VK intakes < and ≥ the AI (mean age of total population: 65.7 ± 0.2 yrs; Table 1). No significant differences were observed in disease prevalence or alcohol consumption between the 2 groups. These distributions remained consistent when stratified by sex.

Relationship of VK intake with PF and HGS

The odds ratio (OR) for VK intake and 5 categories of PF revealed that participants with VK intakes ≥ the AI had lower odds of IADL difficulty and LEM difficulty (adjusted OR, 0.60 [95% confidence interval {CI}, 0.43–0.84] and 0.64 [95% CI, 0.47–0.89], respectively) compared to those with intakes below the AI (Table 2). When stratified by sex, the association was maintained in men; in women, the association weakened although a trend for similar associations was observed (adjusted OR, 0.89 [95% CI, 0.54–1.46] for IADL difficulty and adjusted OR, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.62–1.46] for LEM difficulty). The associations were similar, especially for IADL, when analyzed according to the 30th, 50th, or 70th percentiles of VK intake, as well as based on tertiles of VK intake per kilogram body weight (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Adjustment for menopausal status and hormone use in women strengthened the relationship of VK with IADL and LSA, suggesting a potential role of female hormones in the relationship between VK and PF (Supplementary Table 4). Adjusting for the HEI score excluding total vegetables and greens and beans yielded similar results. Sample size was insufficient to assess the OR within age groups of each sex. However, no significant interaction effects of age and sex on PF according to VK were observed.

Relationship between VK intake and mortality

The mean follow-up period for mortality-related analyses was 9.8 ± 3.5 yrs. No association between VK intake and mortality risk was observed, regardless of adjustment for limitations of PF (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

Using data from NHANES 2003–2016, representative of the community-dwelling U.S. population, we assessed the association of VK intake with PF, HGS, and mortality in adults aged 50 and older, stratified by sex and age. Overall, VK intake meeting the current AI was associated with lower odds of IADL and LEM difficulty, particularly in men. VK intake was not associated with HGS or mortality.

Higher VK intake was associated with lower odds of IADL and LEM difficulty, with a stronger association observed in men than in women. It is known that VK absorption rates are similar between sexes or higher in men [37], while biological utilization is similar. Hence, the sex difference observed in this study may be related to VK metabolism mediated by sex hormones. Although VK intake was weakly associated with PF in women in this study, additional adjustments for menopause and hormone use resulted in a positive association between VK intake and PF as observed in men. Experiments in mice showed that estrogen therapy in ovariectomized female mice delayed the decrease in serum VK levels and increased VK absorption [38]. Therefore, in premenopausal women and postmenopausal women on hormone therapy, the physiological effects of dietary VK may be attenuated compared to men, due to increased absorption and longer plasma half-life of VK. Therefore, despite similar VK intake between sexes, difference in associations between VK and PF in men and women suggests a potential effect of sex hormones on this relationship.

The finding that VK intake is associated with self-reported LEM in adults 50-69 yrs of age is consistent with previous research. In previous studies, consuming VK-rich vegetables was associated with preventing declines in IADL and LEM in middle-aged women [30,39]. In a cohort study of 888 elderly residents in U.S. (335 men and 553 women; mean age 75.2 yrs), VK intake was not associated with bone density but was negatively correlated with hip fractures, suggesting a potential link of VK intake with balance and LEM [40]. In contrast, participants aged 70 and older of the present study showed fewer associations of VK intake with PF. Similarly, the associations between mobility limitation, disability, serum VK1 and uc-MGP levels were weak in adults aged 70 and older in previous studies [15,30]. However, in Australian women in their 70s (n = 1,347), VK intake was positively associated with PF [41]. The reason that the current study and others lack consistency with the Australian study may be due to the difference in the measurement of PF; in the former studies, PF was self-reported, whereas, in the latter study, muscle function was assessed by HGS and timed up-and-go. The results of our study are not completely contradictory as we showed that VK intake and HGS are positively correlated in men aged 50–69. Similarly, among UK Biobank participants, a positive association was observed between vegetable intake and HGS in middle-aged adults [42]. Self-reported assessments capture broad functional domains but are inherently subjective, whereas performance-based assessments provide objective evaluation yet cannot encompass all daily activities. Therefore, findings based on a single measure should be interpreted with caution [29]. The current study, which analyzed NHANES datasets, was unable to assess performance-based PF due to the lack of data available from NHANES. The present results point to the possibility that VK intake promotes IADL and LEM or prevents IADL and LEM decline in adults 50-69 yrs, but not older adults; however further intervention studies are required.

VK intake meeting the AI was not associated with a reduction in mortality risk. Consistent with our study, most data examining the relationship between dietary VK intake and mortality in healthy adults found no significant association in mortality risk [17,19,21]. However, some studies have reported a negative association between VK intake and the risk of all-cause mortality [19,20]. In a Danish cohort study, a threshold VK intake of 100 μg/day in relation to mortality was observed [20]. We did not observe any association in our study participants, whom had a higher mean VK intake (124 μg/day) than the Danish cohort. The different results are unlikely to be due to a threshold effect as no non-linear relationship between VK intake and mortality was found. As with the current study, most studies only measured VK intake at baseline, failing to reflect possible changes in VK intake during the follow-up period. Therefore, intervention studies on VK intake in healthy adults and their association with mortality are needed.

This study has several limitations. First, this is a cross-sectional study and most data are based on self-report, resulting in the possibility of reverse causality regarding the association between VK intake and PF, especially as diet was reported at a single time point. However, by longitudinally tracking a population representative of the US and analyzing the association of VK intake with mortality and excluding deaths within a 2-yr follow-up period, we aimed to minimize reverse causality. Second, due to the limitations of available data, we analyzed mortality outcomes over a relatively short follow-up period (9.8 yrs), and the small number of deaths did not allow us to assess cause-specific mortality. Nonetheless, this follow-up period is comparable to previous studies [21,22,23]. Third, in populations such as the US with low vegetable intake, a predominant source of VK, the use of two 24-h recalls may exhibit low reliability for VK intake. Furthermore, supplement intake was not consistently captured for all participants, and the inability to distinguish between VK1 and K2 is a limitation of this study [41,43]. However, as the dietary intake of VK2 is estimated to contribute to approximately 10% of total VK intake in US adults, we suspect that effects on the results are minimal [44]. In addition, owing to the lack of reliable biomarkers for VK intake, diet assessment may be the best option to assess VK intake. As VK intake may be associated with diet quality and diversity, we adjusted for HEI scores (both including and excluding vegetable intake) with similar results. Fourth, the lack of performance-based PF measures limits the comprehensive assessment of functional status. Fifth, the exclusion criteria resulted in a significantly different cohort compared to those excluded potentially introducing selection bias. Finally, the number of participants included in the analyses of VK intake with PF (n = 4,950), mortality (n = 4,399), and HGS (n = 2,357) differed across outcomes, which may reflect variations in participant characteristics and limit the representativeness of the study sample for the overall population aged 50 yrs and older. Still, this study has several notable strengths. First, while many studies rely on serum VK, which has a very short half-life, we used VK intake. For sensitivity analysis, several cutoffs were applied to ascertain appropriate VK intake levels and to ensure consistent results. Second, we categorized participants by sex and age using a sample representative of the U.S. population, providing insight into the association of VK intake with PF, HGS, and mortality with aging. Lastly, we aimed to control for various confounding factors and adjusted for the HEI to account for overall dietary quality and food diversity associated with VK intake.

Analyzing NHANES data from 2003–2016, VK intakes ≥ the AI was positively associated with IADL and LEM. This association was particularly evident in men. However, VK intake was not associated with HGS or all-cause mortality. Future longitudinal studies assessing performance-based PF stratified by sex and age are needed.
