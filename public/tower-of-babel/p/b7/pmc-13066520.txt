Design of benzo[1,2-b:4,3-b′]dithiophene-4,5-dione based donor-acceptor-donor small molecules for efficient near-infrared photothermal therapy
Authors: Yehui Kang, Yidong Deng, Huanda Ding, Liya Yu, Po Sun, Hua Sun, Yu Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of high-performance organic photothermal conversion agents (PTCAs) with strong near-infrared (NIR) absorption, high photothermal conversion efficiency (PCE), and excellent biocompatibility remains a critical challenge in photothermal therapy (PTT). In this study, we designed and synthesized a series of novel donor-acceptor-donor (D-A-D) small molecules based on a benzo[1,2-b:4,3-b′]dithiophene-4,5-dione (BDTD-4,5-dione) core as a strong electron-accepting unit, symmetrically functionalized with triphenylamine (TPA) derivatives as tunable electron donors. By focusing on the strong electron-donating N,N-dimethylaniline (NPA) unit—contrasted with a methoxy-substituted analog—we achieved effective modulation of the HOMO energy, bandgap, and supramolecular aggregation, underscoring the advantage of enhanced donor strength for photothermal applications. Spectroscopic and electrochemical analyses revealed that electron-rich substituents significantly redshifted the intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) absorption into the NIR region (650–900 nm) and enhanced molar extinction coefficients. Among the derivatives, the dimethylamino-functionalized compound (BDQ-NPA) exhibited the most redshifted absorption, a narrow bandgap (2.0 eV), and an outstanding PCE of 35.05% under 808 nm laser irradiation. Theoretical calculations confirmed strong D-A interactions and efficient charge separation in the excited state. BDQ-NPA self-assembled into stable nanoparticles with excellent photostability and biocompatibility, facilitating efficient cellular uptake and NIR-triggered photothermal ablation of cancer cells in vitro. In vivo studies demonstrated significant tumor growth inhibition in a murine model following a single dose of BDQ-NPA and 808 nm irradiation, with no observable systemic toxicity. This work establishes BDTD-4,5-dione as a promising acceptor core for NIR-absorbing organic PTCAs and highlights the efficacy of substituent engineering in optimizing photothermal performance for cancer theranostics.

Introduction

Photothermal therapy (PTT) has gained significant attention as a non-invasive strategy for cancer treatment1, utilizing photothermal conversion agents (PTCAs) to generate localized hyperthermia under near-infrared (NIR) light irradiation, leading to selective destruction of malignant tissues2. The effectiveness of PTT relies heavily on the development of advanced PTCAs that operate efficiently within the NIR biological window (650–900 nm), where light penetration is optimized due to minimal absorption by hemoglobin, water, and lipids3. Despite considerable progress in nanomaterial-based PTT, its clinical translation is hindered by challenges related to long-term biosafety, unpredictable biodistribution, and ambiguous metabolic pathways—issues particularly associated with inorganic or hybrid nanomaterials such as gold nanorods, carbon nanostructures, and metal chalcogenides4. In contrast, organic small-molecule PTCAs present a promising alternative owing to their well-defined structures, tunable optoelectronic properties, excellent biocompatibility, and potential for renal clearance5–7. However, the development of high-performance organic PTCAs faces three major limitations: insufficient NIR absorption beyond 800 nm, low photothermal conversion efficiency (PCE) often due to competing radiative decay or inefficient excited-state relaxation upon aggregation, and poor stability in the aggregated or solid state—a critical factor for in vivo applications where agents accumulate as nanoparticles or aggregates in tumor tissues8. Addressing these challenges requires rational molecular design strategies that simultaneously enhance NIR absorption, promote non-radiative decay, and maintain photothermal performance in the aggregated state.

One of the most effective molecular designs for NIR-absorbing organic chromophores is the donor–acceptor–donor (D–A–D) architecture9–11. This configuration facilitates intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) transitions, which are inherently redshifted compared to π–π* transitions and can be finely adjusted by modifying the electron-donating strength of terminal groups and the electron-accepting capability of the central unit12. The D–A–D structure also promotes a narrow HOMO–LUMO gap, essential for achieving NIR absorption13. Furthermore, the incorporation of sterically hindered or twisted donor units can effectively suppress detrimental π–π stacking and H-aggregation, thereby mitigating aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ) and enhancing non-radiative decay in the aggregate state—a phenomenon recently termed aggregation-induced photothermal emission (AIPE)14. Benzo[1,2‑b:4,3‑b′]dithiophene-4,5-dione (BDTD-4,5-dione) serves as an excellent electron-accepting core for constructing D–A–D type PTCAs15. Its rigid, planar structure facilitates extended π-conjugation and promotes efficient charge transfer, while the two carbonyl groups impart strong electron-withdrawing character, significantly lowering the LUMO energy level and strengthening donor–acceptor interactions16–18. Notably, the sulfur atoms within the dithiophene ring may enhance intersystem crossing (ISC) via spin–orbit coupling, increasing triplet state population and favoring non-radiative vibrational relaxation—a key mechanism for efficient photothermal conversion19,20. Additionally, the 2,7-positions of BDTD-4,5-dione are synthetically accessible and electronically favorable for functionalization with electron-donating groups, enabling facile construction of symmetric D–A–D systems. To optimize donor strength and fine-tune photophysical and photothermal properties, triphenylamine (TPA) and its derivatives were selected as donor units21,22. TPA exhibits a propeller-shaped three-dimensional geometry that disrupts excessive molecular planarity, suppressing π–π stacking and aggregation-induced quenching23. Its strong electron-donating ability, derived from the nitrogen lone pair, is further enhanced by introducing electron-donating substituents such as –OCH₃ and –N(CH₃)₂ on the peripheral phenyl rings24. These modifications not only modulate HOMO energy levels and overall electron-donating capacity but also improve solubility, tailor intermolecular interactions, and ultimately enhance photothermal performance in the solid state.

In this study, we report the rational design and synthesis of a novel series of D-A-D-type small molecules (BDQ-TPA, BDQ-MPA, and BDQ-NPA), wherein BDTD-4,5-dione serves as the central acceptor, and a series of structurally modified TPA derivatives function as terminal donors. Through systematic variation of the substituents on the TPA units, we investigate the structure–property relationships governing NIR absorption, bandgap engineering, ICT efficiency, and photothermal conversion performance. Comprehensive spectroscopic, electrochemical, and computational analyses are employed to elucidate the electronic effects of substituents on the HOMO/LUMO energy levels and charge transfer dynamics. Among the synthesized compounds, the optimal candidate (BDQ-NPA)—exhibiting the most redshifted absorption, highest molar extinction coefficient in the NIR region, and superior PCE—is identified and evaluated for its in vitro and in vivo photothermal therapeutic efficacy. Recently, Hu et al. designed D-A type molecules based on benzo[2,1-b:3,4-b’]dithiophene-4,5-dione. In this work, the isomer benzo[1,2-b:4,3-b’]dithiophene-4,5-dione can further enhance the molecule’s non-radiative transition through S···O non-covalent bond interactions, thereby enhancing photothermic performance. The introduction of N,N-dimethyl groups successfully extends the absorption of compound BDQ-NPA to 1000 nm, achieving efficient near-infrared photothermal therapy. This work not only realizes BDTD-4,5-dione-based D-A-D small molecules in NIR-driven tumor PTT but also offers a new molecular platform for the development of biocompatible, tunable, and highly efficient organic photothermal agents.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of BDQ-TPA, BDQ-MPA, and BDQ-NPA

The target D-A-D small molecules—BDQ-TPA, BDQ-MPA, and BDQ-NPA—were synthesized via a palladium-catalyzed double Suzuki–Miyaura cross-coupling reaction between the dibrominated benzo[1,2-b:4,3-b’]dithiophene-4,5-dione (BDTD-Br₂) and corresponding pinacol boronic ester-functionalized TPA derivatives (D-Bpin) bearing varying electron-donating substituents (Fig. 1a). This convergent strategy enables efficient construction of the symmetric D–A–D architecture under mild conditions, with high yields and excellent functional group tolerance. The successful formation of the C–C bonds at the 2,7-positions of the BDTD core was unambiguously confirmed by comprehensive spectroscopic characterization, including 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. In the 1H NMR spectra (Figs. S1–S3), a key diagnostic signal arises from the protons at the 2- and 7-positions of the BDTD core. In the precursor BDTD-Br₂, these protons resonate at δ = 8.05 ppm, characteristic of electron-deficient aromatic systems. Upon successful cross-coupling, these signals completely disappear in all three target molecules, and no residual peaks are observed in this region, confirming quantitative substitution. Furthermore, all aromatic proton signals in the final products appear below 8.0 ppm, consistent with the increased electron density at the BDTD core due to conjugation with the electron-rich TPA donors. This downfield shift attenuation reflects the effective delocalization across the D-A-D framework and confirms the formation of extended π-conjugated systems. Additional structural validation was achieved through characteristic signals arising from the peripheral substituents. In the 1H NMR spectrum of BDQ-MPA, a distinct singlet at δ = 3.82 ppm is assigned to the -OCH₃ protons. This chemical shift is slightly deshielded compared to typical aliphatic ethers (δ ≈ 3.3–3.5 ppm) due to the anisotropic effect and resonance donation of the adjacent aromatic ring, placing it within the expected range for aryl methyl ethers (δ ≈ 3.7–3.9 ppm). Similarly, in BDQ-NPA, a sharp singlet at δ = 2.95 ppm corresponds to the -N(CH₃)₂ protons of the N-methylaniline moiety. The observed chemical shift is elevated relative to aliphatic tertiary amines (δ ≈ 2.2–2.5 ppm) due to the combined effects of resonance donation and ring current anisotropy, consistent with literature values for N,N-dimethylaniline derivatives (δ ≈ 2.7–3.2 ppm)25. These substituent-specific resonances provide further evidence for the successful incorporation of the designed donor units. To corroborate the molecular connectivity and electronic structure, 13C NMR spectroscopy was performed (Figs. S4–S6). A particularly informative signal appears at δ ≈ 127 ppm, assigned to the para-carbon (C4) of the TPA aryl rings directly bonded to the BDTD acceptor. In unfunctionalized TPA, this carbon typically resonates near δ = 123 ppm. The observed downfield shift of ~4 ppm is attributed to the electron-withdrawing nature of the BDTD core, which reduces electron density at the adjacent aromatic carbon through conjugation, thereby deshielding the nucleus. To ensure the structural fidelity of the synthetic pathway toward the target D–A–D small molecules, we carried out comprehensive structural characterization of both the key intermediates using multinuclear NMR spectroscopy and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS). As a critical synthetic intermediate, NPA-Bpin—the bis(pinacolato)diboron-functionalized TPA derivative—was synthesized via a Pd-catalyzed borylation reaction. The successful formation of this boronic ester was confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Fig. S7), in which a distinct singlet resonance at δ = 1.31 ppm is assigned to the methyl protons of the pinacolato group (–O–C(CH₃)₂–O–). This signal is characteristic of boronic ester functionalities and is consistent with the expected electronic environment of the Bpin moiety in an arylboronate ester. The absence of residual protodeborylation or oxidative side products further supports the high purity and regioselectivity of the borylation step. Subsequent Suzuki–Miyaura cross-coupling reactions between the dibrominated BDTD acceptor core and the respective boronate ester donors (TPA-Bpin, MPA-Bpin, NPA-Bpin) afforded the target D–A–D architectures: BDQ-TPA, BDQ-MPA, and BDQ-NPA. Full structural confirmation of these conjugated molecules and their precursors was achieved through HRMS in positive ion mode. As shown in Fig. S8, the HRMS spectrum of BDQ-TPA shows a base peak at m/z = 707.18, assigned to the [M + H]⁺ ion (C₄₆H₃₁N₂O₂S₂⁺). This matches the theoretical monoisotopic mass of 707.17 (Δ = +6.8 ppm). Crucially, the observed isotopic pattern—including peaks at m/z 708.18 (12.1%), 709.17 (4.5%), and higher satellites—is in excellent agreement with the simulated distribution for this formula (Fig. S8 inset), confirming the molecular composition. Similarly, BDQ-MPA exhibits a dominant [M + H]⁺ signal at m/z = 827.22 (C₅₀H₃₉N₂O₆S₂⁺, calc. 827.22, Δ = +6.0 ppm), with an isotopic envelope that closely matches theoretical predictions (Fig. S9). For BDQ-NPA, the [M + H]⁺ peak appears at m/z = 879.35 (C₅₄H₅₁N₆O₂S₂⁺, calc. 879.35, Δ = +7.7 ppm), and its full isotopic distribution (M + 1: 58.4%; M + 2: 16.7%; etc.) aligns well with simulation (Fig. S10). The intermediate NPA-Bpin was also characterized by HRMS (Fig. S11): the [M + H]⁺ ion at m/z = 458.31 corresponds to C₂₈H₃₆BN₃O₂⁺ (calc. 458.29, Δ = −2.0 ppm), and the minor deviation falls within typical instrumental error (<5 ppm). Its isotopic pattern further corroborates the proposed structure. Collectively, the convergence of 1H NMR and HRMS data provides unambiguous evidence for the successful preparation of both the synthetic intermediates and final D–A–D chromophores. These rigorous analytical validations establish a solid foundation for subsequent structure–property investigations, including optical absorption, energy level alignment, and photothermal performance.

To further elucidate the structure–property relationships governing the optical and electronic behavior of the series, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed at the B3LYP/6-31 G(d) level (Fig. 1b, c). Geometry optimization in the ground electronic state (S₀) reveals that all three molecules adopt a highly planar conformation across the BDTD core and adjacent aryl rings, with dihedral angles <10°, facilitating extensive π-conjugation and efficient ICT. This extended planarity is a critical structural feature for minimizing the HOMO-LUMO energy gap and promoting NIR absorption. Frontier molecular orbital analysis confirms that the HOMO is predominantly localized on the TPA donor units and the connecting aryl rings, while the LUMO is largely confined to the electron-deficient BDTD-4,5-dione core (Fig. 1b). This spatial separation underscores the strong D–A character and supports an ICT-dominated lowest-energy transition. The calculated energy gaps (ΔE = ELUMO – EHOMO) are all below 2.3 eV, consistent with absorption in the visible to NIR region. Notably, BDQ-NPA exhibits the smallest bandgap (ΔE = 2.0 eV), which is attributed to a significant uplift of the HOMO energy level due to the strong electron-donating nature of the N-methylaniline substituent. In contrast, the LUMO energy remains largely unperturbed across the series, as it is primarily governed by the invariant BDTD acceptor. This donor-driven HOMO modulation results in a pronounced narrowing of the optical gap in BDQ-NPA, rationalizing the experimentally observed redshift in its absorption spectrum. These computational insights highlight the pivotal role of donor strength in bandgap engineering for NIR-absorbing organic photothermal agents.

The influence of different electron donating groups on the optical properties of molecules

To systematically elucidate the influence of electron-donating substituents on the optoelectronic and photophysical properties of the D-A-D chromophores, we first conducted a qualitative and quantitative assessment of the relative electron-donating strengths of the peripheral groups in the BDQ-TPA, BDQ-MPA, and BDQ-NPA series (Fig. 2a–c). The TPA-based donor in BDQ-TPA features unsubstituted phenyl rings, rendering it the weakest donor in this comparative study. In this case, the electron-donating capability arises primarily from hyperconjugative interactions involving the C–H σ-bonds adjacent to the nitrogen center, a relatively weak inductive and resonance effect. In contrast, BDQ-MPA incorporates methoxy (-OCH₃) substituents on the para-positions of the phenyl rings, which act as moderate π-donors through resonance donation of the oxygen lone pair into the aromatic system, thereby enhancing the overall electron density on the nitrogen center and increasing the HOMO energy level of the molecule. Most significantly, BDQ-NPA is functionalized with para-amino (-NH₂) groups, which are among the strongest organic σ- and π-donors due to the high-energy lone pair on nitrogen and efficient resonance delocalization into the phenyl ring. This aniline-type donor dramatically elevates the HOMO level, thereby reducing the HOMO–LUMO energy gap and promoting ICT character in the lowest-energy electronic transition. According to the energy gap law in non-radiative decay dynamics, a smaller energy gap between the S₁ and S₀ states generally correlates with an increased rate of non-radiative relaxation, which is favorable for photothermal conversion. This principle underpins the design strategy for high-efficiency photothermal agents: stronger donor–acceptor interactions lead to greater absorption redshift and enhanced non-radiative decay pathways, minimizing fluorescence emission and maximizing heat generation. As illustrated in Fig. 2d–f, the calculated reorganization energy in the low-frequency vibrational modes—associated with torsional and skeletal deformations—is significantly higher for BDQ-MPA and especially BDQ-NPA, compared to BDQ-TPA. The pronounced increase in reorganization energy for BDQ-NPA can be attributed to the enhanced charge transfer character upon photoexcitation, which induces a substantial geometric relaxation between the ground (S₀) and excited (S₁) states. This structural rearrangement is facilitated by the increased conformational flexibility of the aniline-substituted TPA donor, which is more prone to twisting around the N–C(aryl) bonds in the excited state, thereby amplifying the electron–phonon coupling. Consistently, the Huang–Rhys factor—which reflects the degree of vibrational excitation accompanying electronic transitions—exhibits a parallel trend across the series (Fig. 2g–i). BDQ-NPA displays the highest S value in the low-frequency region, indicating strong vibronic coupling and a large displacement along torsional coordinates in the excited state. This elevated S factor directly correlates with a faster non-radiative decay rate via internal conversion, in accordance with the energy gap law and the Marcus theory of electron transfer. Collectively, these computational and spectroscopic analyses demonstrate that the strategic incorporation of strong electron-donating substituents, particularly the aniline moiety in BDQ-NPA, not only induces a significant redshift in the ICT absorption band by narrowing the optical bandgap (ΔEg) but also promotes a large excited-state reorganization energy and enhanced vibronic coupling. These factors synergistically favor non-radiative decay over radiative pathways, thereby optimizing the molecular architecture for efficient photothermal conversion. This structure–property relationship provides a robust design principle for next-generation organic photothermal agents, where donor strength is leveraged not only to tune absorption wavelength but also to engineer the excited-state dynamics for maximal heat generation.

A quantitative analysis of the photophysical properties (Table 1) provides compelling evidence for the systematic modulation of optoelectronic behavior through rational donor engineering. As the electron-donating strength increases along the series BDQ-TPA → BDQ-MPA → BDQ-NPA, the lowest-energy absorption maximum exhibits a pronounced bathochromic shift—from 563 nm to 618 nm and ultimately to 685 nm—correlating with a progressive narrowing of the optical bandgap (Fig. S12). This trend is directly attributable to the enhanced ICT character facilitated by stronger donor–acceptor electronic coupling, as the elevated HOMO energy levels reduce the HOMO-LUMO gap in a predictable manner. Consistent with this evolution, the corresponding excitation energy (from S0 to S1) initially increases slightly from 1.80 eV (BDQ-TPA) to 1.91 eV (BDQ-MPA), likely due to subtle conformational or solvation effects, but then undergoes a significant decrease to 1.70 eV in BDQ-NPA. This pronounced red shift underscores the dominant role of the N-methylaniline donor in stabilizing the excited state and enhancing charge separation, aligning with time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) predictions of increased ICT character. Notably, the oscillator strength (f) remains robust across the series, fluctuating within a narrow range of 0.2073–0.2555, with BDQ-NPA exhibiting the highest value (0.2555). This indicates that the enhancement in donor strength does not compromise the radiative transition probability; rather, it maintains a strong electric dipole transition moment, ensuring efficient light harvesting despite the red-shifted absorption. Thus, BDQ-NPA achieves an optimal balance between extended NIR absorption and high molar extinction, a critical prerequisite for deep-tissue photonic applications. Most significantly, the reorganization energy (λ), particularly the dominant Gaussian component associated with geometric relaxation in the excited state, reveals a striking trend. It decreases from 0.83 eV in BDQ-TPA to 0.32 eV in BDQ-MPA, suggesting reduced structural distortion upon excitation for the intermediate donor. However, in BDQ-NPA, λ surges to 1.29 eV, indicating substantial nuclear relaxation following photoexcitation—likely driven by pronounced bond length alternation and conformational flexibility induced by the strong electron-donating N-methylaniline group. This large reorganization energy facilitates efficient non-radiative internal conversion by increasing the vibronic coupling between electronic states, thereby promoting rapid dissipation of electronic energy as heat. This dramatic increase in λ, combined with sustained oscillator strength and maximal ICT character, provides a clear mechanistic rationale for the exceptional PCE observed experimentally in BDQ-NPA. Characterization of BDQ-NPA NPs

Upon dissolution of BDQ-NPA in chloroform, we observed a distinct absorption peak at 685 nm using UV-visible spectroscopy (Fig. 3a). Upon formulation into NPs via nanoprecipitation, the absorption peak underwent a redshift from 685 nm to approximately 698 nm, suggesting an aggregation-induced bathochromic shift. While this spectral change is modest, it may reflect a degree of head-to-tail chromophore alignment reminiscent of J-aggregate formation, which can extend π-electron delocalization and lower the energy gap for electronic transitions. Such aggregation enhances NIR absorbance, thereby improving light-to-heat conversion efficiency under laser irradiation. Notably, although the primary absorption band of BDQ-NPA NPs lies in the 600–700 nm range, significant residual absorbance extends into the near-infrared region. To quantitatively assess the suitability of these nanoparticles for 808 nm laser-mediated PTT, we determined their molar extinction coefficient at this wavelength. Based on UV–vis–NIR spectroscopy and known nanoparticle concentration, the molar extinction coefficient (ε) at 808 nm was calculated to be 1.88 × 104 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹. This relatively high ε value confirms that BDQ-NPA NPs possess sufficient NIR absorption capacity to enable efficient light-to-heat conversion under 808 nm irradiation, supporting their practical applicability in PTT. The morphology and size distribution of the prepared BDQ-NPA NPs were thoroughly characterized. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements revealed an average hydrodynamic diameter of 130 nm with a low polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.19, indicating a narrow size distribution and high colloidal homogeneity (Fig. 3b). These results are consistent with transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging, which showed uniform spherical particles (Fig. 3c). The uniformity and monodispersity of these NPs are crucial for ensuring reproducible and predictable photothermal performance during PTT applications. To assess the stability of BDQ-NPA NPs under laser irradiation—a critical parameter for therapeutic efficacy—we subjected the NPs solution to continuous 808 nm laser irradiation for periods of 5, 10, and 15 min. Post-irradiation analyses, including UV-visible absorption spectroscopy, DLS, and zeta potential measurements, demonstrated that the NPs maintained their optical properties, particle size, and surface charge without significant changes (Fig. 3d–f). In addition, we performed high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to assess the photostability of BDQ-NPA nanoparticles. As shown in Fig. S13, the HPLC chromatograms reveal no detectable degradation peaks following light irradiation, indicating that the molecular structure remains intact under photothermal treatment conditions. These results confirm the robust photostability of BDQ-NPA NPs, suggesting minimal risk of degradation or aggregation under typical PTT conditions. Further stability studies were conducted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to evaluate the behavior of BDQ-NPA NPs in physiological environments. Visual inspection using a digital camera revealed that the PBS solution of BDQ-NPA NPs appeared uniformly transparent green (Fig. 3g), indicating homogeneous dispersion and absence of precipitation. Particle size analysis across various pH values (ranging from acidic to neutral) showed no significant variation in NPs size (Fig. 3h), underscoring the ability of BDQ-NPA NPs to maintain structural integrity even in acidic tumor microenvironments. This stability is essential for effective tumor targeting and sustained photothermal activity in vivo. Finally, the long-term stability of BDQ-NPA NPs was evaluated in different biological media, including deionized water (ddH₂O), PBS, fetal bovine serum (FBS), Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), and RPMI-1640. Solutions were monitored over a period of 14 days, with periodic visual inspections and UV-visible spectroscopy. No discernible changes in solution color or UV absorption spectra were observed in any of the tested media (Fig. 3i), indicating excellent NPs stability and resistance to degradation or aggregation. Optical performance of BDQ-NPA NPs

To evaluate the potential of BDQ-NPA NPs for PTT, we conducted a series of in vitro photothermal performance assessments under physiologically relevant conditions. As a critical first step, aqueous dispersions of BDQ-NPA NPs at varying concentrations (0–200 μg/mL) were irradiated with an 808 nm NIR laser at a fixed power density (1.0 W/cm²), and real-time temperature changes were monitored using an infrared thermal imaging camera. As shown in Fig. 4a, the resulting thermal maps clearly demonstrate a time- and concentration-dependent increase in temperature, with the highest concentration achieving a temperature rise exceeding 50 °C within 10 min of irradiation. These visual data were corroborated by quantitative photothermal heating curves recorded using a thermometer (Fig. 4b), which further confirmed the robust and reproducible heat generation capability of BDQ-NPA NPs. Additional experiments at fixed concentration but varying laser powers (0.5, 0.75, 1.0 W/cm²) revealed a power-dependent thermal response (Fig. 4c), underscoring precise controllability of the photothermal effect—essential for safe and effective therapeutic applications. To further evaluate the photostability of BDQ-NPA NPs under repeated thermal stress, we subjected the NPs dispersion to five consecutive laser-on (10 min) and laser-off (10 min) cycles. As illustrated in Fig. 4d, the maximum temperature achieved during each irradiation period remained nearly constant across all cycles, with no observable degradation in photothermal performance. This excellent cycling stability indicates minimal photobleaching or structural disintegration of the NPs during prolonged irradiation, a critical attribute for clinical translation where sustained heating may be required. The observed photothermal efficiency stems from the strong NIR absorption of BDQ-NPA NPs, facilitated by J-aggregate formation within the NPs, which enhances ICT and promotes non-radiative decay pathways. To quantitatively assess the PCE, we applied the standard protocol based on the Roper model, analyzing the transient temperature profile during both the heating and cooling phases. From the resulting time-dependent temperature decay curve (Fig. 4e), the PCE of BDQ-NPA NPs was calculated to be 35.05% (Fig. 4f), a value that ranks among the higher efficiencies reported for organic semiconducting polymer or small-molecule-based photothermal agents. This high efficiency is attributed to the extended π-conjugation, strong donor–acceptor character, and efficient exciton relaxation within the D-A-D framework. Beyond their photothermal capabilities, BDQ-NPA NPs exhibit strong photoacoustic (PA) signal generation, enabling their use as a dual-modal theranostic agent. We performed in vitro PA imaging of BDQ-NPA NPs at increasing concentrations under 808 nm pulsed laser excitation. As shown in Fig. 4g, the PA signal intensity increased progressively with NP concentration, manifested as a visibly brighter contrast in the PA images. Line-scan analysis across the sample cross-section further confirmed this trend, revealing a concomitant increase in the amplitude of the PA signal (Fig. 4h). Quantitative analysis yielded a strong linear correlation (R² > 0.99) between PA signal intensity and NP concentration (Fig. 4i), demonstrating the potential of BDQ-NPA NPs as a quantitative PA contrast agent for deep-tissue imaging and real-time monitoring of drug accumulation. Collectively, these results establish BDQ-NPA NPs as a highly efficient, stable, and multifunctional photothermal agent with excellent PA responsiveness. Cell uptake and in vitro therapy

Given that BDQ-NPA contains a quinone moiety, which is inherently susceptible to reduction, and considering the highly reducing intracellular environment rich in glutathione (GSH), we sought to evaluate whether BDQ-NPA nanoparticles (NPs) remain stable against GSH-mediated reduction in cells. To this end, we treated cells with various conditions—untreated control, control + laser irradiation (control+L), BDQ-NPA NPs alone, and BDQ-NPA NPs + L—and subsequently quantified intracellular GSH levels using a commercial assay kit. As shown in Fig. S14, cells treated with BDQ-NPA NPs exhibited GSH concentrations comparable to those of the untreated control group, indicating that the nanoparticles do not trigger significant GSH consumption during the experimental timeframe. This observation suggests that the quinone units within BDQ-NPA NPs remain largely unreduced under cellular conditions. This stability is further corroborated by our photothermal therapy experiments (e.g., Fig. 5c), which demonstrate potent, dose-dependent cancer cell ablation—implying that the optical and photothermal properties of BDQ-NPA NPs are preserved throughout the treatment period. We attribute this resilience to the nanoparticle architecture: upon self-assembly, the hydrophobic core effectively encapsulates the BDQ moieties, shielding them from direct contact with cytosolic GSH. This physical barrier likely limits reductant accessibility to the quinone groups, thereby suppressing potential reduction pathways. Such protective confinement may contribute significantly to the structural and functional integrity of BDQ-NPA NPs in the intracellular environment. Based on their stability, we evaluated the therapeutic effect of BDQ-NPA NPs on tumor cells. Firstly, we evaluated the uptake of BDQ-NPA NPs by A20 cells using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). As shown in Fig. 5a, as the incubation time increases, the red fluorescence signal also becomes stronger, with the brightest signal at 8 h and then darkening at 10 h. This phenomenon indicates that the uptake of BDQ-NPA NPs by A20 cells accumulates the most after 8 h and gradually metabolizes afterwards. The quantitative results of red fluorescence are also consistent with this phenomenon (Fig. 5b). After determining the optimal time for A20 cells to uptake BDQ-NPA NPs, we conducted laser PTT experiments. As shown in Fig. 5c, the cell viability of the non-irradiated group was above 80% at a concentration of 25 μg/mL, indicating that this concentration is safe and has no significant toxicity to cells. However, under laser irradiation, the viability of A20 cells decreased to below 45%, indicating that BDQ-NPA NPs at a concentration of 25 μg/mL had the best therapeutic effect on A20 cells. In addition, we evaluated the biosafety of BDQ-NPA NPs in HK-2 cells. As shown in Fig. 5d, the viability of HK-2 cells remained above 75% at concentrations ranging from 0 to 50 μg/mL. This indicates that BDQ-NPA NPs has high efficacy and safety for cells, with no significant toxicity, and can be used as a biomaterial for in vivo experiments. In order to observe the survival of cells after laser irradiation more intuitively, we used Calcein AM (live) and PI (dead) staining kits for detection. As shown in Fig. 5e, it was observed through CLSM that the cells in the control group (−L and +L) and BDQ-NPA NPs group (−L) were mostly alive, while the BDQ-NPA NPs group (+L) showed large-scale cell death. This indicates that BDQ-NPA NPs has a good photothermal therapeutic effect on tumor cells. Flow cytometry analysis further confirms this conclusion. As shown in Fig. 5f, we used the Annexin V-FITC/PI kit to detect apoptosis in each group. We found that the cells in the control group (−L and +L) and BDQ-NPA NPs group (−L) did not undergo apoptosis, while the apoptosis rate in the BDQ-NPA NPs group (+L) reached 54.78%. To benchmark the performance of BDQ-NPA NPs against established photothermal agents, we conducted a direct comparison with indocyanine green (ICG), a clinically used cyanine dye (Fig. S15). In cellular photothermal therapy assays, BDQ-NPA NPs achieved a lower IC50 value (25 μg mL⁻¹) compared to ICG (50 μg mL⁻¹), and induced a higher apoptosis rate (54.78% vs. 24%, Fig. S15g, h). These findings underscore the advantages of BDQ-NPA NPs in terms of both photophysical robustness and biological efficacy. While croconaine and other advanced dyes offer strong NIR absorption, their clinical translation remains limited. In contrast, the combination of high PCE, excellent stability, and potent therapeutic activity positions BDQ-NPA NPs as a promising candidate for further development in photothermal applications.

Conclusion

In summary, we synthesized different D-A-D structures based on BDTD-4,5-dione as the receptor and TPA derivatives as the donor. And it was characterized by nuclear magnetic hydrogen spectrum and carbon spectrum. The influence of optical properties of D-A-D structures with different substituent donors on infrared absorption was investigated through geometric configuration, HOMO and LUMO, recombination energy, and Huang Rhys factor. Select the photothermal molecule BDQ-NPA with the strongest absorption and narrowest optical bandgap for subsequent photothermal anti-tumor experiments. In vitro studies, we first prepared BDQ-NPA NPs and performed solution and photothermal characterization. Then, biosafety and tumor cell thermal ablation experiments were conducted. These experimental results indicate that BDQ-NPA NPs have good solution stability, photothermal stability, and PCE. In addition, in vivo anti-tumor experiments have shown significant anti-tumor effects in subcutaneous tumor models, demonstrating good biocompatibility and safety. Altogether, this work demonstrates that BDQ-NPA NPs represent a promising therapeutic nanoplatform for image-guided photothermal tumor therapy, offering a versatile design strategy that may be extended to the development of organic agents for other biomedical applications, including inflammatory disease modulation and targeted ablation of pathological tissues.

To further evaluate the biological toxicity of NPA on normal cells, we incubated HK-2 cells with NPA in a 96 well plate for 12 h. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS and a mixture of CCK-8 and DMEM medium (1:10) was added to each well. One hour later, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a PowerWave X enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay reader. The formula for calculating cell survival rate (%) is: [(OD (sample) − OD (blank))]/[(OD control) − OD]31.

Calcein-AM/PI staining assays

In order to observe more intuitively the toxic effects of BDQ-NPA NPs on tumor cells and the PTT effect. We added A20 cell suspension and BDQ-NPA NPs to each chamber of the four-chamber glass dish (8 × 104 cells/well). Incubate together at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 6 h, then irradiate the laser group cells with 808 nm laser (1.0 W/cm², 5 min). Subsequently, 10 μL of PI and 10 μL of Calcein AM were added to each chamber and incubated for 20 min. Finally, observe under the Leica DMI8 laser confocal microscope.

Cell apoptosis

To further investigate the photothermal effect of BDQ-NPA NPs on tumor cells, we used the Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit. Firstly, inoculate cells (2 × 105 cells/well) into a 6-well plate, and after adding BDQ-NPA NPs to the BDQ-NPA NPs group, incubate for 6 h. After incubation, the laser group was irradiated with 808 nm laser (1.0 W/cm², 5 min), and then incubated for another 2 h. Afterwards, the cells were collected by centrifugation and washed, stained with 5 µL of membrane associated protein V-FITC and 10 µL of PI (SONY, China) for 15 min, and finally analyzed using flow cytometry (CytoFLEX, Beckman, USA).

Establishment of in vivo tumor model

All animal procedures have been approved by the Zhejiang Provincial Hospital Animal Care and Use Committee (GB/T 35892-201820240306105017486843). All animal were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital. All animal experiments were conducted according to the guidelines evaluated and approved by Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital Animal Room Ethics Committee (Use License: 20241126407447). Prior to the experiment, male mice (4-6 weeks old) from the limited company were acclimated for one week. Animals are placed in a sterile environment and have free access to food and water. All experiments followed the guidelines established by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital. For xenograft tumor models, 5 × 106 A20 cells were subcutaneously injected into the right axilla area of nude mice. One week later, mice with tumor sizes (~80 mm³) were selected to establish a tumor model, followed by in vivo imaging and anti-tumor experiments. Record the tumor size and mouse weight every other day, and calculate the tumor volume using the formula: length × width² × 0.5.

In vivo fluorescence imaging

After establishing a tumor model in the subcutaneous tumor of A20 in the right axilla, mice were randomly divided into a control group and an BDQ-NPA NPs group. After the tumor volume reached 200 mm3, 200 μL of solution (BDQ-NPA NPs: 100 μg/mL, Ce6:10 μg/mL) was injected into the tail vein of each group. Fluorescence imaging (excitation: 650 nm, emission: 665 nm) was performed using an IVIS imaging system at 3, 6, 9, 12, and 24 h after injection. After 24 h of injection, dissect the main organs and tumors for ex vivo fluorescence imaging.

In vivo PAI

In vivo PAI was performed using a LOS-3D PAI device (Suzhou TOMOWAVE Medical Imaging Co., Ltd). Firstly, we establish a mouse tumor model and perform PAI when the tumor volume reaches 200 mm3. Inject 200 μL BDQ-NPA NPs (100 μg/mL) through the tail vein, anesthetize mice with isoflurane (RWD Life Sciences Co., Ltd, Shenzhen), and perform in vivo PAI on nude mice with A20 tumors under the Dutch skin to evaluate the distribution of BDQ-NPA NPs in the mice. Under 808 nm excitation, photoacoustic images were collected at 3, 6, 12, and 24 h after injection.

The in vivo anti-tumor effect of BDQ-NPA NPs

When the size of the A20 tumor reached ~80 mm³, the mice were divided into four groups: (i) Control group (PBS); (ii) Control group + laser; (iii) BDQ-NPA NP; (vi) BDQ-NPA NPs + laser. The laser group was irradiated with 808 nm laser every other day (1.0 W/cm², 5 min) for five consecutive times, and the temperature changes were detected by an infrared thermal imager. Measure the tumor size and weight every other day during treatment, and calculate the volume using V = length × width² × 0.5. After treatment, euthanize the mice and collect the main organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) for H&E staining to evaluate their biological safety. Perform H&E, Ki67, and TUNEL staining on tumors to analyze the damage caused by treatment. Perform liver (AST, ALT) and kidney function (CREA, BUN) analysis on the serum of tumor-bearing mice to evaluate the toxicity of NPA.
