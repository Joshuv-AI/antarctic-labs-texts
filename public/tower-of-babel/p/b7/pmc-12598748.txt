Local rigidity constraints for deformable image registration in cbct‐guided radiotherapy
Authors: Tom J W Draper, Cornel Zachiu, Bas W Raaymakers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeformable image registration (dir) is a critical component for planning and quality assurance in cbct‐guided adaptive radiotherapy treatment. Conventional dir methods apply a uniform regularization over the image domain, failing to account for the local biomechanical properties of different tissues. Recent advancements emphasize the need for spatially varying models to improve anatomical plausibility.PurposeThis study aims to introduce a novel, lightweight framework that incorporates local rigidity constraints in dir to improve anatomical consistency and motion estimation accuracy. The approach is designed to be general, computationally efficient, and compatible with existing dir pipelines.MethodsThe proposed framework jointly optimizes the rigid‐body transformation and the surrounding tissue deformations. The proposed framework was evaluated on 57 pelvic ct‐cbct image pairs with annotated landmarks. Local rigidity constraints were imposed on automatically segmented sacrum, hip, and femur bones. Four registration algorithms were tested, combining different regularization types, with and without rigidity constraints. Accuracy was measured using the target registration error (tre) and biomechanical plausibility was assessed via the Jacobian of the estimated motion. Secondary verification was performed on a 4D thoracic ct database. With automatic segmentation and rigidity constraints placed upon the ribs and thoracic vertebrae, accuracy and plausibility were analyzed in these structures and inside the lungs.ResultsAcross 57 cases, the constrained methods improved landmark alignment compared to baseline models, increasing the proportion of cases with mean tre below 3 mm. Local rigidity constraints significantly reduced unphysical deformations in rigid bones as indicated by the Jacobian determinant mappings and analysis on the residual energy of the Jacobian orthogonality. Analysis of thoracic ct images showed improved alignment of the ribs and vertebrae with marginal increase in tre of the landmarks in the lungs. The added constraints increased the runtime to a maximum of 40 s.ConclusionsThe proposed framework enforces local rigidity constraints during dir, increasing accuracy without significantly compromising speed. It removes anatomically implausible deformations in rigid structures. Its efficiency and anatomical reliability make it well‐suited for cbct‐guided adaptive radiotherapy.Keywords: deformable image registration, image‐guided radiotherapy, local rigidity constraints

INTRODUCTION

Accurate motion estimation is essential for planning and quality assurance for cone beam computed tomography (cbct)‐guided adaptive radiotherapy treatment (art). Deformable image registration (dir) is a proven, reliable technique for motion mitigation in art, respecting strict quality assurance criteria such as the recommendations outlined by aapm Task Group 132. 1

With recent developments in online art, tumor tracking and dose accumulation, there is a demand for dir methods that accurately measure image alignment, but also respect the local, biomechanical properties of the anatomy, that is, anatomical consistency. 2 , 3 , 4 Conventional dir methods aim to align images based on their image similarity and interpolate in regions with low contrast. However, these methods are agnostic of the underlying biomechanics. Local, physics‐based dir methods improve accuracy and ensure clinically reliable motion estimates by modeling motion based on biomechanical properties. Pioneering work in dir assumes the underlying anatomy behaves as a uniform material that stretches the same in all directions, where the elasticity of the material can be tuned by the regularization parameters. 5 Previous studies have shown that including local information in the form of control regions or imposing local elasticity properties improves accuracy, precision, and anatomical plausibility of the deformations. 6 , 7 These results have been demonstrated across multiple different target regions (abdomen, thorax, pelvis) and across multiple imaging modalities.

Aside better anatomical consistency, local regularization is one method to improve motion estimates from dir when image volumes are affected by noise or artifacts. Several strategies are given by the estro Physics Workshop 2021 on Commissioning and Quality Assurance for dir in Radiotherapy. 8 To obtain satisfactory results from dir, a registration algorithm can, for instance, be guided solely by the regularization in areas affected by image artifacts or use external information such as point‐ or contour‐guidance. The benefit of local regularization in this context is that it does not require annotated landmarks or delineated contours on both images used for registration. Instead, only a delineation on the reference image is required. This can be of great importance in a clinical setting, since a quick and precise method for landmarks annotation or region segmentation is not always readily available.

Variational methods for dir allow for a natural way to impose local biomechanical properties on the estimated motion by means of regularization. 9 Recent developments have shown that a spatially varying regularization can lead to improved accuracy and anatomical consistency in the thoracic, pelvic, and head‐and‐neck areas for ct/cbct image registration. 6 , 10 , 11 For instance, the anatomically constrained deformation algorithm (anaconda), 6 the finite element model for multi‐organ registration morfeus, 12 and spatially adaptive EVolution (aevo) 11 impose soft constraints to improve the smoothness, elasticity, and incompressibility, respectively, of the motion at a local level. In previous work, local rigidity has been enforced either by embedding rigid transformations directly into the deformation field through specific regularization assumptions, by direct penalization of the Jacobian, or by leveraging the properties of B‐spline basis functions under the assumption that the deformation field can be represented in this form. 10 , 13 , 14 , 15

We present a lightweight framework that can add local rigidity constraints to standard dir algorithms. This approach is analyzed for accuracy and anatomical consistency. Our framework does not rely on a specific parametrization of the deformation field, but can be used as an add‐on to any conventional registration algorithm. We use the methodology in the recently proposed bone rigidity error to efficiently compute optimal rigid transformations and jointly optimize them with the deformation field. 16 Concretely, the contributions of this article are as follows:

Implementation details

For the purpose of fast image registration, both the conventional and proposed models were implemented in c++/cuda (version 11.2) and executed on a gpu. 17 The deformation field and ADMM variables are computed entirely on a gpu. To this end, a 3D (I)DCT implementation was performed using the Fast Fourier Transform from the cufft library and appropriate index shuffling and coefficient scaling to emulate the DCT behavior (as cufft does not provide a native DCT implementation). The appropriate coefficient manipulation in three dimensions was derived from the work of Makhoul and the multidimensional DCT implementation (MIRT_DCTN) in Matlab. 24 , 25 The only part that was calculated on the host memory was the singular value decomposition of a 3×3 matrix in the Kabsch algorithm, which was done using the JacobiSVD function from the Eigen c++ template library (version 3.4.0). 26 The models were compiled as mex functions using the Matlab mex api, enabling integration with Matlab (version 2023a). Data pre‐ and post‐processing and visualization was done in Matlab. All experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an Intel(R) Xeon(R) W‐2133 CPU and an Nvidia Quadro RTX 5000 GPU.

EXPERIMENTAL METHODS

Experimental data

The primary dataset of this study is obtained from The Cancer Imaging Archive (tcia). It contains ct‐cbct pairs for 58 patients with prostate or anal cancer. The cbct images have an image resolution of 512×512×88 voxels, while the ct images range between 512×512×95 and 512×512×226 voxels. All images have an (1×1) mm2 in‐plane resolution and 3 mm slice thickness. tcia provides a list of annotated landmarks per ct‐cbct image pair. The number of landmarks ranges between 91and212 and was on average 154. The landmarks are annotated on rigid structures, that is, bones. Before registration, the ct and the corresponding landmarks were resampled on the grid of the cbct image. For a single case (case ID: “028”), we were unable to confirm the spatial correspondence between the landmarks on the ct and cbct images and was therefore removed from the data.

A secondary dataset was used to study the effect of rigidity constraints on soft tissues. The point‐validated, pixel‐based breathing thorax model (popi) contains six 4D ct imaging data, of which the max‐inhalation and max‐exhalation phase were used for registration. 28 These 3D image volumes have an image resolution of 512×512×139 to 512×512×187 voxels. The in‐plane resolution ranged from (0.78×0.78) to (1.17×1.17) mm2 with 2 mm slice thickness. Each image volume contained a list of 100–113 annotated landmarks inside the lungs.

The ct images from the tcia database were segmented using the TotalSegmentator tool, giving delineations of the femur and hip bones, the sacrum, and the body contour. 29 Each delineation was refined to remove small disconnected components. The proposed model imposed local rigidity constraints on the femurs, hip bones, and sacrum. The body contour was solely used during post‐processing, specifically for analyzing the Jacobian, where only values within the body contour were of interest. For the popi database, TotalSegmentator was used to yield segmentations of the ribs and thoracic vertebrae, covering 34–36 structures in total, which were likewise refined before registration.

Registration parameters and image preprocessing

This study compares the results between unconstrained dir methods and their locally rigid, constrained counterpart. Concretely, four different registration algorithms were evaluated within our framework, using either smoothness or curvature regularization, and using unconstrained or locally rigid, constrained optimization. An overview of the model parameters is given in Table 1. The value of κ/ρ=108 was arbitrarily chosen to ensure that the rigid component dominates in Equation (9). The smoothness regularization parameter was fixed to α=1.0 in all experiments. For curvature regularization, α=25.0 was used for the tcia dataset, and α=5.0 on the popi dataset. These values were empirically selected as a balance between motion field regularity and registration accuracy. The ADMM variable is fixed at ρ=1. The lcc takes two input parameters, the width of the Gaussian convolution kernel σ, and the size of the convolution kernel. These values were set to σ=2.5 and the kernel radius to 5, following previous implementation of the lcc metric. 20

All images were sampled at a resolution of 256×256×128 voxels prior to registration. This resolution was empirically chosen as a trade‐off between image detail and computational cost, proportional to the number of voxels. After registration, the deformation field was resampled to the original image resolution before subsequent analysis was done. During registration, a coarse‐to‐fine strategy was employed to estimate the motion at a lower resolution level, which serves as a prior estimate for subsequent levels in the scheme. 30 Four additional levels were used, that is, the lowest resolution was 16×16×8 voxels. For popi, three layers were used, as going any coarser could result in rigid structures vanishing from the image volume.

This benchmark is used only in the tcia experiment, as the landmarks in popi were not annotated on rigid structures, but inside the deforming lungs.

Landmark‐based accuracy analysis

Using image registration with both constrained and unconstrained registration model (smoothness and curvature regularization), the anatomical landmarks of each individual case of the tcia and popi datasets were aligned. Figure 4 shows the distribution of the mean tre using each of these models, together with the initial misalignment, as well as the point cloud registration. Using the constrained models improves the number of cases of the tcia experiment with mean tre below 3 mm from 80.7% to 82.5% for smoothness regularization, and from 73.7% to 82.5% for curvature regularization. This is on par with the results of the point cloud registration, which also reduced the number of cases below the 3 mm threshold by 82.5%.

Computational performance

The registration time was 7.70(0.02) s for tcia and 7.38(0.01) s for popi using the baseline model. Using the constrained model, the registration time increased to 15.25(0.38) s for tcia, and 35.71(1.16) s for popi. All registrations were executed below 20 s for tcia and 40 s for popi. The longer runtime on popi reflects the larger number of structures provided to the algorithm (5 for tcia, 34–36 for popi).

DISCUSSION

This study proposes a novel, light‐weighted framework to impose multiple local rigidity constraints on dir. Within this framework, two constrained registration algorithms were evaluated on a large dataset of pelvic cbct‐ct registration, with constraints imposed on the femur, hip, and sacrum. To analyze the effect of rigidity constraints on soft tissue deformations, we included six ct–ct thoracic image volumes to resolve respiratory motion, with rigidity constraints on the ribs and thoracic vertebrae. These registration algorithms used smoothness and curvature regularization, respectively. Results were compared with an unconstrained, conventional dir counterpart. Using the locally rigid framework, the mean tre of aligned landmarks improved, see Figure 4, and the residual rigidity error estimated by the Jacobian orthogonality decreased in all rigid structures, as shown in Figure 1 and Table 2. Imposing the rigidity constraints did increase the mean computation time from 7.7 to 15.25 s for pelvic cbct‐ct registration, and from 7.4 to 35.7 s for thoracic ct–ct registration. The longer computational runtime on the popi database is related to the increase in rigid structures given to the registration algorithm (5 for tcia, 34–36 for popi). These runtimes remain compatible for typical applications within current cbct‐guided art workflows. 3

The EOC distribution reduces both in mean and median when using local rigidity constrained dir, as shown in Table 2 and Figure 1, respectively. As illustrated in Figure 3, the EOC distributions are skewed due to effects that appear between interlocking regions, such as the hip–femur interface. Due to the sudden change in rigid motion and the discrete, finite difference approximation used to calculate the Jacobian of the deformation field, outliers appear in the distribution. We observed that these effects are localized near structural boundaries, as quantified by the number of voxels near the edge with more than 10% volumetric variation. As shown in Figure 2, some outliers also appear at the thinnest part of the hip structures. We hypothesize that the magnitude of this effect depends on the resolution at which registration is performed. At coarser resolutions, very thin structures may be under‐represented or even partially vanish, giving rise to numerical artifacts. A more systematic investigation of these resolution‐dependent effects will be pursued in future work.

Figure 4 shows a clear improvement of the mean tre using both a baseline and locally rigid registration algorithm on the tcia dataset. The maximum voxel dimension was chosen as threshold based on recommendations given by aapm Task Group 132 and to emphasize clinical sufficiency rather than marginal technical improvements. 1 Using locally rigid image registration increases the proportion of cases with low mean tre to 82.5%, the same as using the optimal point‐cloud registration. This is consistent with the recommended accuracy targets and shows a slight improvement over the unconstrained models. The largest outlier in Figure 4 is from a single case. The cause of the failed registration was that the left and right femur were almost completely out of the field‐of‐view of the cbct. Zooming in on an individual case further highlights the benefit of the locally rigid framework; Figure 6 shows a clear reduction in tre as the ratio κ/ρ increases, and Figure 2 shows that this reduction is achieved by removing the erroneous deformations inside of these rigid structures.

Additionally, we were also able to observe the interplay between rigidity constraints on the ribs and vertebrae, and soft tissue deformations of the lungs in the popi dataset. The mean tre remained within recommended limits for the same number of cases, despite a slight decrease in accuracy when rigidity constraints were applied, see Figure 4. As indicated in Figure 5, unconstrained dir could cause a misalignment in the ribs and thoracic vertebrae, which was resolved by enforcing rigid deformations in these structures. This misalignment was not reflected in the tre, since all anatomical landmarks are annotated inside the lungs. One may consider combining the proposed framework with a different regularization to resolve respiratory motion. In particular, the total variation model of Vishnevskiy et al. more effectively allows shearing motion. 20 It also employs an ADMM model in the numerical optimization, and this could be combined with the framework presented here.

In this study, the local rigidity constraints were implemented using the ADMM method for constrained optimization. As shown in Figure 6, this method is stable for input parameter κ/ρ. Moreover, in the absence of rigidity constraints, the method reduces to an implicit time‐stepping algorithm, that is, Equation (1), which is used in many state‐of‐the‐art registration models and therefore provides a formidable baseline model. 21 , 22 , 23 However, ADMM comes at additional computational cost by simultaneously optimizing the deformation field, but also the auxiliary and dual variables, which results in an increase of computation time for tcia and popi by roughly a factor of two and four, respectively. In previous work, it has been shown that local rigidity constraints can be enforced in the context of B‐spline registration algorithms without introducing these auxiliary variables. 13 , 35 , 36 These algorithms greatly benefit from the properties of B‐spline basis functions. Future work should establish whether local rigidity constraints can be enforced in our framework, maintaining its numerical stability and without the additional computational cost of the auxiliary variables.

A major benefit of this framework is that the delineations are only required on the reference (ct) image. It does not rely on potential delineations on the cbct image, which is usually of inferior quality. The ct delineations were easily obtained automatically using TotalSegmentator, and using gpu acceleration can significantly reduce the computation time of this step. Only minor changes were required on these delineations before using them within the proposed framework. However, it also suggests that the current framework only works for cbct‐to‐ct registration (as opposed to ct‐to‐cbct registration), unless the structure set can reliably be delineated on the cbct image. Recent advances in acquisition and reconstruction methods have greatly improved the quality of cbct, with image quality nearing a standard ct image, making this a feasible task in the near future. 37

Alternatively, there are two other approaches that could be employed to impose the constrained framework for cbct‐to‐ct registration, without delineating the cbct image. The first method would be a change in the numerical interpolation scheme, that is, pushing as opposed to pulling the pixel intensities as discussed in the context of dose accumulation. 38 Second, this could be achieved by calculating the forward and inverse transformation simultaneously via the inverse consistency metric, or combining this framework with diffeomorphic image registration algorithms to ensure an invertible transformation. These two options are within the scope of future work within our group.

A limitation of this study lies in the difficulty of validating the estimated motion specifically within soft tissue regions in cbct image volumes. This is primarily because most annotated landmarks of the tcia dataset are located in or near rigid anatomical structures, limiting our ability to assess soft tissue deformations directly using landmark‐based metrics. One contributing factor is the inherent challenge of cbct image registration, which is affected by limited field‐of‐view, relatively poor soft tissue contrast, and common imaging artifacts. 39 Again, improved cbct image quality will in turn enhance registration accuracy in future applications. Alternatively, a different approach would be to use a high‐quality ct image database to delineate regions of interest and annotate anatomical landmarks. Then, the quality of the ct images can be artificially degraded to create a synthetic cbct image. This way, landmarks and regions, obtained from the original ct images can be used to validate the results of image registration using the synthetic cbct's. 40

Finally, we expect that constrained optimization methods can be further developed in the context of dir to improve accuracy and anatomical consistency of the estimated motion. Applications such as dose accumulation or Hounsfield unit propagation require accurate, spatially adaptive dir methods. This framework could be extended to soft tissues by incorporating constraints derived from their biomechanics, or by integrating with generalized approaches like the generalized div‐curl formulation to unify different regularization strategies. 41 As such, our framework could be extended by integrating local rigidity constraints with the gdc methodology, enabling the exploration of a wider class of registration algorithms that go beyond the traditional regularization options.

CONCLUSION

This study introduces a novel framework to include physics‐based rigidity constraints for dir. This framework was evaluated on a cbct–ct dataset of patients diagnosed with prostate and anal cancer. The constraints were imposed on the sacrum, femur, and hip bones that were in the field‐of‐view. Secondary verification was performed on a ct–ct registration database with rigidity constraints on the ribs and thoracic vertebrae. The proposed model was compared to a baseline model without rigidity constraints, and was able to remove unphysical deformations in these rigid structures without compromising on the accuracy as measured by the target registration error of annotated landmarks. The proposed model also benefits that the structures only need to be delineated on the reference ct image. An efficient numerical model combined with gpu acceleration shows that the rigidity constraints can be imposed without significant additional computational overhead. This makes the framework particularly well‐suited for art, where accurate and anatomically plausible deformation fields are critical for daily treatment plan adaptation.
