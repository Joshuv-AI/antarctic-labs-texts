Mosaic chromosomal alterations and relation to haematological cancer in two large Danish cohorts

ABSTRACT
SummaryMosaic chromosomal alterations (mCAs) are large somatic gains, losses and copy‐neutral loss of heterozygosity identified in the peripheral blood, which represent a relatively unexplored spectrum of clonal haematopoiesis. We aimed to describe mCAs in two large Danish cohorts with extensive long‐term registry‐based follow‐up and examine associations with haematological malignancy. We analysed genotype data from 97 752 participants from the Danish Blood Donor Study and 86 301 patients from the Copenhagen Hospital Biobank using the MoChA software. Association to haematological cancers was modelled with competing‐risk methods and Cox regression, or logistic regression, via nationwide register linkage. Among 184 053 individuals, 15 459 (8%) carried autosomal mCAs and 14 322 females (17%) had loss of X, with prevalence increasing with age. In samples before or after diagnosis of haematological cancer, autosomal mCAs were detectable in 21%–62% of individuals depending on cancer subtype. Overall, the risk of haematological cancer was increased for individuals carrying autosomal mCA (hazard ratio [HR] 4.1 (95% confidence interval [CI] 3.1–5.5)), but not for individuals carrying loss of X chromosome (HR 1.1 [95% CI 0.7–1.6]). The level of malignancy risk was highly variable between different chromosomal aberrations, and cancer gene–related mCAs conveyed highest risk. Autosomal mCAs, especially those overlapping cancer gene loci, identify individuals at increased risk of haematological malignancy and may assist improvement of risk stratification and early detection.

INTRODUCTION

Mosaic chromosomal alterations (mCAs) of the blood refers to the presence of large somatic structural genetic changes—gains, losses and copy‐number neutral loss of heterozygosity (CN‐LOH)—in haematopoietic cells. 1 These alterations of autosomal and sex chromosomes, may arise at different stages of haematopoietic differentiation, and are considered a form of clonal haematopoiesis.

Current understanding of mCAs mostly comes from large population cohorts such as United Kingdom biobank, 1 , 2 Biobank Japan 3 and FinnGen 4 and from patients with specific diseases such as chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL) 5 and multiple myeloma (MM). 6 mCAs are not uncommon with a prevalence of ~5% at age 50 years, and risk factors include male sex, increasing age, tobacco smoking 7 as well as ethnicity and/or genetic constitution. 1 , 3 The studies also indicate that mCA carriers have an increased risk of haematological cancers, 1 , 3 , 8 , 9 lung cancer, 10 cardiovascular disease 11 and infections. 4

Given the limited number of previous investigations, studies are warranted to validate and extend their findings in contexts characterized by other healthcare, ancestry and exposure settings. We therefore assessed the prevalence and features of mCAs in two large Danish cohorts; healthy blood donors participating in the Danish Blood Donor Study (DBDS) 12 and hospitalized adults included in the Copenhagen Hospital Biobank (CHB), 13 and explored their association with haematological cancer.

METHODS

Study populations

Since 1968, all individuals living in Denmark have been assigned unique personal identification numbers (PIN), which are used by the Danish Central Population Register (CPR) to continuously monitor their vital status, that is, dates of birth, immigration, emigration and death, as relevant. 14 The PIN is also used by the Danish National Patient Registry (DNPR) and other nationwide registries, allowing their mutual linkages at the level of the individuals. 14 , 15

The DBDS 12 is a cohort and biobank that was initiated in 2010. Participants in DBDS are non‐renumerated blood donors who, following informed consent, fill out a questionnaire about health and lifestyle including anthropometrics and smoking, give permission for data retrieval from Danish registers and provide blood samples for genotyping and other analyses. 12

The CHB 13 has stored biological samples from individuals admitted to hospitals in the Capital Region of Denmark since 2009. The patients receive written information about sample storage and genotyping, and they can withdraw their sample and data from research use at any time.

Using information from DNPR, 15 we identified all individuals in DBDS and in CHB diagnosed with a haematological cancer. Myeloid cancers included myelodysplastic neoplasia (MDS), myeloproliferative neoplasia (MPN), including chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML) and acute myeloid leukaemia (AML). Lymphoid cancers included lymphomas of any kind, multiple myeloma (MM), including plasmacytoma and plasma cell leukaemia, chronic lymphoid leukaemia (CLL) and acute lymphocytic leukaemia (ALL). Additionally, we identified individuals registered with germline chromosomal aberrations by International Classification of Disease–Version 10 (ICD10) code and excluded these from the study populations. ICD10 codes are listed in Table S1.

Mosaic chromosomal aberrations in the study population

At time of the present investigation, a total of 200 658 individuals in DBDS and CHB had been genotyped using the Infinium® Global Screening Array (GSA) from Illumina® in collaboration with deCODE genetics, Iceland. We applied the MoChA software 1 , 2 , 16 to unprocessed Intensity Data (IDAT) files for these individuals to detect mCAs, using default parameters and filtration recommendations.

Identified mCAs were categorized as either autosomal or loss of X chromosome. Loss of Y chromosome (LOY) was not included, due to low single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) array coverage in the region (PAR1) used by MoChA to detect LOY. Autosomal mCAs were further classified as myeloid and lymphoid types based on a previous publication 8 and as provided from the MoChA software output. 16 In addition, the mCAs were also grouped according to overlaps with loci harbouring known oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes (TSG). Details can be found in Supporting Information: Methods.

Statistical analyses

Because of their different health profiles (blood donors vs. hospitalized individuals), the DBDS and CHB cohorts were analysed separately except for the Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. We assessed the prevalence of autosomal mCA and of loss of X chromosome. Individuals having both autosomal mCA and the loss of X chromosomes were included in analysis of both. We tested previously reported associations of mCA with male sex, age and smoking (in DBDS only) by binomial logistic regression analyses producing odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) adjusted for age, sex and GSA version. We also analysed the association between mCAs and SNPs 1 , 3 in logistic regression analyses adjusted for age, sex, GSA version, biobank origin and principal components (PCs) 1–10.

To further investigate the relationship between smoking and mCA, we conducted a MR analysis in individuals aged 50 years or older with genetic European ancestry from either of the two cohorts (N = 79 088). SNPs associated with smoking were derived from published genome‐wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics 17 and utilized as instrumental variables in the analysis. The instrument's effect on mCA was estimated using logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, GSA version, biobank and PC1–10.

We investigated associations between mCA and haematological cancers in DBDS and CHB. In DBDS, participants were followed from date of sampling until diagnosis of haematological cancer, death or end of study period 24 October 2024. The cumulative incidence of haematological cancer across the defined mCA strata was assessed using Aalen‐Johansen estimation with death as competing risk, and the association between mCA and risk of haematological cancer by means of cause‐specific Cox regression analyses adjusted for age, sex, GSA version and smoking status, yielding hazard ratios (HRs). For CHB, we used binomial logistic regression models to examine mCA's association with haematological cancer. Analyses were adjusted for age, sex and GSA version. Sensitivity analyses were performed to test robustness in unrelated individuals and potential effect of PCs (Supporting Information: Methods). All applied R packages are summarized in the Supporting Information: Methods.

Mosaic chromosomal aberrations

Analyses of samples from the 184 053 participants in the combined cohorts demonstrated autosomal mCA in 15 459 individuals (8% of all participants) and loss of X chromosome in 14 322 females (17% of female participants). In both cohorts, mCA frequency increased with age (Figure 2A).

Mosaic chromosomal alterations (mCA) demographic characteristics. (A) mCA prevalence and age; (B) median fraction of cells with autosomal mCA and time to diagnosis, red dotted line = median cell fraction for mCA overall (0.04); (C) boxplot of cell fraction split in time from sample to diagnosis.

Pile‐up plots were generated to evaluate and compare population level mCA signals to previous cohorts (Figure S1). The size of observed mCAs varied from 11 446 base pairs (bp) to gains/loss of entire chromosomes with a median autosomal mCA length of 78Mbp (IQR: 25–156). The median cell fraction of autosomal mCAs was 4% (IQR: 2%–12%) and, for females, loss of X chromosome was 4.0% (IQR: 1%–10%). Among individuals registered with haematological cancer, the median mCA cell fraction increased from 3% (IQR: 2%–9%) in samples taken more than 5 years before diagnosis, to 8% (IQR: 2%–22%) for samples taken 1–4 years before diagnosis, 11% in samples taken less than 1 year from diagnosis and 13% (IQR: 3–37) in samples >1 year after diagnosis. For comparison, the median mCA cell fraction was 3% (IQR: 1%–10%) in individuals who did not develop haematological cancer during follow‐up (Figure 3). Cell fraction in relation to length is visualized for all mCAs in Figure S2.

In DBDS, the most common mCAs were 14q CN‐LOH, 15q CN‐LOH, 1p CN‐LOH, 1q CN‐LOH and 11p CN‐LOH. Carriers of autosomal mCAs (median age 47 years, IQR: 34–57) and females with loss of X chromosome (median age 53 years, IQR: 46–59) were older than mCA naive individuals (median age 38 years, IQR: 27–49).

In CHB, the most common autosomal mCAs were 14q CN‐LOH, 13q loss, 15 gain, 20q loss and 11q CN‐LOH. Among the 7163 (8%) individuals in CHB who were registered with haematological cancer, analysed blood samples had been obtained more than 1 year before diagnosis in 1168 (16%), less than 1 year from diagnosis in 3734 (52%), and more than 1 year after diagnosis in 2247 (31%).

Risk factors for mCA

Analyses of sex‐specific differences in autosomal mCA prevalence yielded different results in DBDS and CHB. In DBDS, the prevalence of autosomal mCA was similar in men and women (adjusted OR 1.01 [0.94–1.09]), whereas it was more common in male patients in CHB (adjusted OR 1.27 [CI: 1.22–1.32]) (Table S2). Previously described associations to germline variants were broadly confirmed and ensured validity and comparability of the dataset (Table S2).

Among DBDS participants, autosomal mCA status was associated with neither current smoking (adjusted OR 0.99 [CI: 0.89–1.09]) nor ever smoking (adjusted OR 0.96 [CI: 0.88–1.03]). Meanwhile, among female DBDS participants current smoking (adjusted OR 1.13 [CI: 1.03–1.23]) was associated with loss of X chromosome (Table S2). The MR analysis did not indicate a causal effect of smoking on mCA (Figure S3A,B), for example, OR 1.05 [CI: 0.97–1.15, p‐value = 0.2] with the inverse variance weighted method.

mCA and haematological cancer

Carrying autosomal mCAs was significantly associated with an increased risk and prevalence of haematological cancers in analyses adjusted for age, sex, GSA version and smoking status if available (Table 2). Correspondingly, autosomal mCAs were associated with a higher cumulative incidence of haematological malignancies (Figure 3A). In DBDS, the relative risk of lymphoid cancer associated with mCAs was higher than the relative risk of myeloid cancer, whereas the opposite was observed in CHB, that is, a stronger association with myeloid cancer than with lymphoid cancer. Analyses restricted to individuals in CHB who were diagnosed with haematological cancers more than 12 months after blood sampling (henceforth referred to as the ‘filtered CHB cohort’) showed similar association patterns for autosomal mCAs (Table 2).

Loss of X chromosomes was not associated with risk of haematological cancer in DBDS (HR 1.1 [95% CI 0.7–1.6]) or the filtered CHB cohort (OR 1.1 [95% CI 0.9–1.4]), but with a slightly increased prevalence of haematological cancer in CHB (OR 1.3 [95% CI 1.2–1.4]) (Table 2).

Myeloid and lymphoid mCAs

Myeloid mCAs were strongly associated with an elevated risk of developing myeloid cancers in DBDS (HR 8.9 [95% CI 3.3–24.3]) and in CHB (OR 6.3 [95% CI 5.5–7.1]). Additionally, carriers of myeloid mCA had a moderately increased risk of lymphoid cancers in both DBDS and CHB. Similarly, lymphoid mCAs were found to be associated with a statistically significant increased risk of lymphoid cancers in DBDS (HR 9.1 [95% CI 6.2–13.6]) and CHB (OR 6.6 [95% CI 6.1–7.2]) and were also associated with a moderately increased risk of myeloid cancers in DBDS and CHB (Table 2; Figure 3B,C). All results, incl. the filtered CHB cohort are shown in Table 2.

mCA related to oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes

A total of 15 459 carriers of autosomal mCAs (incl. undetermined mCAs) were grouped into five categories: oncogene‐related gains (N = 3662 [24%]), TSG‐related losses (N = 3256 [21%]), oncogene‐ or TSG‐related CN‐LOH (N = 5731 [37%]), oncogene‐ or TSG‐related undetermined mCA (N = 1745 [11%]) and mCAs with no known relation to cancer genes (N = 1065 [6%]). The non‐cancer gene‐related mCAs had different characteristics by shorter length (median 0.7 Mbp [IQR: 0.2–1.7]) and higher cell fraction (median 22% [IQR: 12%–33%]) compared to the other cancer‐gene groups (median length between 29 and 83 Mbp and median cell fraction 2%–6%).

Among individuals from DBDS, the risk of haematological malignancy was high for oncogene‐related gains (HR 8.5 [95% CI 5.4–13.4]) and for TSG‐related losses (HR 6.7 [95% CI 4.0–11.1]), while no significant association was found for non‐cancer gene‐related mCA compared to mCA‐naive individuals (Table 2; Figure 3D). Among individuals from CHB, associations with haematological cancer were seen for all mCA strata, but especially for oncogene‐related gains (OR 7.5 [95% CI 6.9–8.2]) and TSG‐related losses (OR 6.1 [95% CI 5.6–6.7]) in comparison with individuals without mCAs (Table 2).

Chronology of mCA and haematological cancer

We assessed the interval from mCA detection to haematological cancer diagnosis in the DBDS cohort. A total of 59 individuals, who were mCA‐positive, were later diagnosed with a haematological cancer. The maximum time from blood sampling was 12 years. The mCA cell fraction tended to be higher close to diagnosis of a haematological malignancy (Figure 2B,C). The timing was visualized into intervals of 2 years (Figure S4).

Most common mCA in haematological cancers

We investigated the prevalence of autosomal mCA variants across haematological cancer subtypes, including myeloid (MDS, AML, MPN) and lymphoid (lymphoma, CLL, ALL, MM) malignancies. The frequency and types of the top 10 most prevalent mCA are presented for each disease group in Table 3, regardless of time from sample to diagnosis. Among the myeloid cancers, mCAs were observed in 45% of patients with MDS, 47% with AML and 41% with MPN. In the lymphoid cancers, the proportion of individuals with any mCA varied: CLL showed the highest prevalence with 62%, followed by ALL with 54%. Lymphomas and MM had lower mCA prevalences at 26% and 21% respectively.

DISCUSSION

This large population‐based study of 184 053 individuals from the DBDS and the CHB provides a comprehensive validation of mCAs and their association with haematological malignancies in a real‐world setting. Our findings support previous reports and extend understanding of mCAs associated with haematological disease in healthy and hospitalized individuals.

We detected autosomal mCAs in 3% of the younger and healthy DBDS cohort (median age 39 years) and 15% of the older, multi‐morbid and hospital‐based CHB cohort (median age 74 years). We observed a higher prevalence of mCAs in haematological patients across all haematological cancer subtypes (21%–62%) and confirmed that the occurrence of mCAs is increased in individuals of higher age and with haematological cancer. In both cohorts, individuals with autosomal mCAs had a significantly increased risk of developing haematological cancer. In DBDS, which included only individuals free of cancer at baseline, mCAs preceded haematological cancer diagnoses by up to 12 years, suggesting a potential role of mCAs as early markers of imminent disease. We demonstrate that mCAs overlapping the loci of known oncogenes or TSGs were more frequently associated with later malignancy than the mCAs not mapping to these areas. When examining the haematological malignancies individually, we found that specific mCAs are associated with specific cancer types, including well‐described associations, for example, 5q loss, 20q loss, 8 gain and 7q/7p loss in MDS 18 and 17p loss (TP53) in lymphoid cancers. 19 This underlines the potential of the detection of mCAs from SNP array data in identification of new disease‐specific chromosomal aberrations in large cohorts.

While lymphoid cancers (lymphoma, CLL, ALL and MM) are associated with a similar pattern of mCAs (e.g. 13q loss and 10p loss), the myeloid cancers (MPN, AML and MDS) differ more and display a complex pattern of mCA subtypes. Furthermore, we have confirmed myeloid‐ and lymphoid mCA types categorized by Nirula et al. 8 are associated with myeloid and lymphoid cancer, respectively, but also identified new candidate risk mCAs, for example, 7p and 9p loss. In addition, we found that myeloid mCAs also induce a small increase in lymphoid cancer risk and vice versa, indicating that mCAs may be a marker of haematopoietic instability not restricted to individual haematological cancer types. Overall, our findings show that mCAs are associated with haematological cancer development and may help distinguish low‐ and high‐risk mCAs, with potential relevance for early detection and risk stratification.

In addition to disease associations, we confirmed previously reported risk factors for developing mCAs, 2 , 7 including male sex, smoking and germline genetics. Except for smoking, these findings support the current understanding of causal pathways towards mCA acquisition, with many potential risk factors yet to be discovered.

The strengths of the study include the size and diversity of the study populations and linkage to high‐quality nationwide registries. The use of both a healthy and a hospital‐based cohort allowed for inter‐population comparison, mCA detection at various time points, and enhanced generalizability of the findings. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, despite the large sample size, rare mCAs and haematological cancer subtypes, especially in the DBDS cohort, may have been missed. Second, smoking data were only available in DBDS, limiting this risk factor analysis that most likely disproportionately affects the older population. Third, the majority of the haematological cancers in the CHB cohort were diagnosed before blood sampling. Although analyses of incident cancer cases in CHB mitigated this concern, this analysis may be biased by excluding a large proportion of cases. Lastly, the MoChA software employs a maximum likelihood model to estimate mCAs; events and aberrations of smaller sizes or cell fractions are filtered out, while other detected aberrations may represent other not yet described cellular events.

In conclusion, the study confirmed that mCAs are strongly associated with subsequent development of haematological malignancies, while specific mCA subtypes conferred variable risk with notable overlap to known cancer gene loci. Our work provides an overview of mCAs present in specific haematological cancer subtypes, which could assist improvement of risk stratification of precursor conditions, early cancer detection, and help develop preventive interventions. Future studies may focus on integrating mCA into clinical risk stratification models, exploring the risk factors of mCA development, and examining the role of mCAs in disease progression.
