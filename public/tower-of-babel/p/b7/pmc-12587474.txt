Vein bypass versus endovascular treatment for chronic limb ischaemia
Authors: Atalel F Awedew, Feron Getachew, Mihretu Jegnie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis is a protocol for a Cochrane Review (intervention). The objectives are as follows:To assess the benefits and harms of vein bypass compared with endovascular treatment for chronic limb ischaemia.

Background

Description of the condition

Peripheral arterial occlusive disease (PAOD) leads to reduced arterial blood flow, and may manifest as ischaemic limb pain at rest, non‐healing ischaemic ulceration, or gangrene. It is a leading cause of mortality, morbidity, disability, and limb loss globally [1] [2][3]. Approximately 113 million people worldwide over the age of 40 have PAOD [3]. In high‐income countries, PAOD is estimated to affect 500 to 1000 individuals per million [4], although reliable epidemiological data are scarce. Effective intervention for PAOD significantly mitigates the severe consequences of this debilitating condition. Management follows a stepwise approach, beginning with aggressive control of modifiable risk factors, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and smoking cessation [2] [5]. The overarching goals of treatment extend beyond limb salvage, aiming to reduce cardiovascular morbidity and mortality through comprehensive risk factor modification and optimal medical therapy. A meta‐analysis of 13 studies, encompassing 1527 participants with one‐year follow‐up, reported a 22% all‐cause mortality rate and 22% major amputation rate within one year [6]. When left untreated, PAOD predisposes the affected individual to significant cardiovascular mortality, disability, and limb loss. An analysis of salvage rates for people with non‐revascularisable chronic limb‐threatening ischaemia revealed that five years after the initial need for revascularisation, amputation‐free survival was 43%, all‐cause mortality was 35%, and the amputation rate was 33% [5][7].

The distinct anatomical and physiological characteristics of the affected vessel, along with the varying technical challenges associated with interventions at each site, may influence the efficacy and safety of vein bypass grafting versus endovascular approaches [8]. For instance, infrainguinal revascularisation often involves smaller‐calibre vessels and more complex lesions, which could lead to different long‐term patency rates and clinical outcomes compared to interventions for aortoiliac disease [8]. Given the significant morbidity and mortality associated with chronic limb ischaemia, identifying effective treatments with low complication rates remains a longstanding and critical area of research. However, despite the growing body of knowledge with respect to both vein bypass and endovascular interventions, the evidence comparing the effectiveness of these interventions remains limited.

Description of the intervention and how it might work

Revascularisation is recommended for individuals with PAOD to prevent morbidity and disability, improve quality of life, and preserve the limb. This can be achieved through vein bypass grafting or endovascular treatment. Autologous vein bypass grafting, particularly using the greater saphenous vein (GSV), is a highly effective revascularisation method due to its favourable characteristics [9]. When the ipsilateral GSV is unavailable (occurring in approximately 40% of patients), the contralateral GSV is the preferred alternative, provided the contralateral limb is healthy [9]. Intraoperative vein selection criteria include a minimum diameter (3.5 to 4 mm), good distensibility, and absence of sclerotic segments. Alternative venous conduits, such as small saphenous veins, arm veins, or composite vein grafts, may be used when necessary, prioritising optimal inflow and outflow sites due to conduit length limitations [9]. Endovascular treatment is an alternative revascularisation option. Over the past three decades, novel techniques, including drug‐coated stents, drug‐coated balloons, and atherectomy, have emerged alongside traditional endovascular options like plain balloon angioplasty and bare‐metal stents for treating peripheral arterial disease [10][11]. However, despite the growing body of knowledge with respect to both vein bypass and endovascular interventions, evidence comparing the effectiveness of these interventions remains limited.

Why it is important to do this review

Randomised controlled trials examining the effectiveness of vein bypass for the treatment of PAOD have produced inconsistent results, especially regarding amputation‐free survival, major adverse limb event (MALE), postoperative morbidity, and improvement in survival rates. Furthermore, the lack of robust, high‐level evidence demonstrating the superiority of vein bypass over endovascular interventions adds to the uncertainty regarding the optimal surgical approach for the treatment of PAOD. Recognising these limitations and uncertainties, this review seeks to synthesise high‐quality evidence to better understand the role of venous bypass grafts in the management of PAOD. By conducting a comprehensive review, we aim to address current gaps in the literature and offer valuable insights to inform evidence‐based decision‐making in the surgical management of PAOD.

Objectives

To assess the benefits and harms of vein bypass compared with endovascular treatment for chronic limb ischaemia.

Methods

This systematic review will adhere to the methodological standards outlined by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [12] [13] and the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [14].

Criteria for considering studies for this review

Types of studies

We will include all randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi‐RCTs that directly compare vein bypass grafting with endovascular treatment for the treatment of PAOD [15][13]. For the purposes of this review, quasi‐RCTs are defined as studies where participants are allocated to intervention or comparator groups (vein bypass grating versus endovascular) using a non‐random method. Examples of such methods include, but are not limited to, alternation (e.g. alternate days, weeks, or patients), use of case record numbers, date of birth, or any other systematic method that is not truly random (e.g. based on sequence of enrolment or presentation). While these methods aim to create comparable groups, they are susceptible to selection bias, as the allocation sequence may be predictable. Crucially, the identification of a study as quasi‐randomised will be based on a thorough examination of its methods section to ascertain the allocation sequence generation, rather than solely on the authors' explicit labelling of their study. We will consider abstracts for inclusion if they provide sufficient data, demonstrate a clear study design, and offer detailed information on participant characteristics.

We will exclude studies with a cross‐over or cluster‐RCT design from the review.

Types of participants

We will include all adult patients (over age 18 years) diagnosed with chronic limb‐threatening ischaemia (CLTI) (Rutherford category 4 or more), with no restrictions on sex, race, or geographic location. Rutherford categories 4, 5, and 6 represent CLTI characterised by ischaemic rest pain, minor tissue loss, and major tissue loss (gangrene), respectively. We have chosen to include participants with Rutherford categories 4 or higher in order to focus on the most severe manifestations of CLTI that typically necessitate revascularisation interventions, as opposed to claudication (Rutherford categories 1 to 3), which may be managed conservatively or with less aggressive interventions. We will exclude studies focused solely on a paediatric population (aged under 18 years). For studies that include both adult and paediatric participants, we will include the study only if data pertaining specifically to the adult population can be extracted. If adult and paediatric data are co‐mingled such that the outcomes for the adult population cannot be clearly differentiated or extracted, the study will be excluded from the review.

Types of interventions

Intervention

Vein bypass grafting is an effective revascularisation intervention for CLTI. Autologous vein bypass grafting, particularly using the GSV, and contralateral GSV are highly effective. Less often, the small saphenous vein, arm vein, or composite vein grafts are used. Intraoperative vein selection criteria include a minimum diameter (3.5 to 4 mm), good distensibility, and absence of sclerotic segments [9].

Control

Endovascular therapy offers a minimally invasive procedure for the treatment of CLTI. A wide range of established interventional techniques are permissible, contingent upon national standards of care. These include, but are not limited to, angioplasty [16].

Outcome measures

We will adopt outcome standards from the Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS) reporting guidelines for vascular surgical outcomes [16]. Reporting on these outcomes will not be an inclusion criterion; however, where these data are lacking, we will contact the authors of studies to request them. We will detail the characteristics of studies without the necessary outcome data in the 'Characteristics of included studies' section.

Data collection and analysis

We will perform data collection and analysis of this systematic review according to the recommendations and guidelines provided in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [12][21].

Selection of studies

Our selection and screening of studies will adhere to the PRISMA statement and recommendations in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [19]. Two review authors (AFA and MJ) will independently screen the titles and abstracts of identified studies using Covidence [22]. Subsequently, they will retrieve and assess the full texts of all records deemed potentially eligible. Any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion within the review author group.

We will document the study selection process in sufficient detail to facilitate a PRISMA flow diagram [14], and present the reasons for exclusion of excluded studies in a 'Characteristics of excluded studies' table.

Any disagreements arising during data extraction will be resolved through discussion and consensus among the review authors or in consultation with a third review author. The overall risk of bias will be determined based on the assessment of the five individual domains. We will classify a trial as having a high risk of bias if it is judged to be at high risk of bias in at least one domain for the result being considered. A trial will also be classified as high risk if there are some concerns across multiple domains such that our confidence in the result is substantially lowered. We will assign a classification of some concerns when the trial is judged to raise some concerns in at least one domain for the result, but is not judged to be at high risk of bias in any domain. Finally, we will classify a trial as low risk if it is judged to be at low risk of bias for all domains.

Measures of treatment effect

For dichotomous data, we will conduct the analysis using risk ratios (RRs) with accompanying 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For continuous data, we will use the mean difference (MD) along with its 95% CI if the studies utilise the same outcome measure. When studies use differing measures to assess the same outcome, we will calculate the standardised mean difference (SMD) and its 95% CI. For results reported as medians and interquartile ranges for skewed data, we will calculate the corresponding means and standard deviations (SDs) using the method described by Wan and colleagues [32], incorporating the reported median, interquartile range, and CI or range [32]. Where data cannot be transformed into a suitable format for inclusion in meta‐analysis, we will present the findings within a narrative synthesis.

Unit of analysis issues

We will include RCTs and quasi‐RCTs with a simple parallel design. All analyses will be conducted on an intention‐to‐treat basis. In the case of studies with multiple arms, we will include only the data from the relevant pair‐wise comparison and exclude data from the additional arms. For studies that report data for multiple eligible intervention or control groups, we will combine these data to align with our predefined intervention and control groups for statistical analysis, following the recommendations in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [33][34][26]. If a study also provides data for interventions that do not fit within our analysis (e.g. open surgery), we will not include these data in the analysis, but will list these excluded groups in the 'Characteristics of included studies' table.

Dealing with missing data

To address missing data, we will first attempt to contact the corresponding authors of the included studies to request unreported or unclear information (e.g. details on dropouts, or data from studies only published as abstracts). In order to assess the potential for bias introduced by missing data and to explore the robustness of our findings under different assumptions, we will perform sensitivity analyses. We will conduct a sensitivity analysis by excluding studies with more than 20% missing data. We will also conduct best‐case and worst‐case scenario sensitivity analyses for dichotomous outcomes, which are considered extreme‐case analyses to assess the impact of missing data on the overall effect estimate. In the best‐case scenario, participants with missing data in the intervention arm are assumed to have experienced desirable outcomes, while those with missing data in the control arm are assumed to have experienced undesirable outcomes. Conversely, in the worst‐case scenario, participants with missing data in the intervention arm are assumed to have experienced undesirable outcomes, while those with missing data in the control arm are assumed to have experienced desirable outcomes.

When means and SDs are not reported in the included studies, we will calculate these statistics, where possible, using the methods outlined in Chapter 6 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [33]. If data are presented graphically rather than numerically, we will extract data from figures using the PlotDigitizer online tool [35], wherever feasible. If we are unable to calculate the SD using the above method, we will impute the SD from other studies in the same meta‐analysis [36]. We will use an imputation method that will decrease the weight of the studies in the meta‐analysis of MD and shift the effect towards the null for SMD. We will clearly check and critically appraise the handling of missing data and the imputation methods employed in this systematic review using the RoB 2 tool.

Reporting bias assessment

We will make a diligent effort to investigate and address potential reporting biases according to the guidelines and recommendations in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [37]. If we identify more than 10 studies, we will assess the risk of reporting bias visually using a funnel plot [38]. Funnel plot asymmetry may indicate substantial publication bias and overestimation of the intervention [38]. This visual representation will illustrate the distribution of studies included in the meta‐analysis relative to the magnitude of effect or precision. Moreover, we will use statistical tests such as the Egger and Begg tests to quantify and detect reporting biases within the included studies.

Synthesis methods

Our data synthesis will adhere to the principles outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [39][21]. We will begin with a descriptive summary of the included studies, followed by a comprehensive narrative and quantitative synthesis. The narrative synthesis will involve an initial amalgamation of study findings, an exploration of relationships within and between studies, and an assessment of the overall strength of the evidence from each study. If clinical, methodological, and statistical heterogeneity is sufficiently low, we will pool dichotomous outcomes using meta‐analysis. We will perform meta‐analyses using RevMan [23] and Stata [40]. We will use a random‐effects model as we expect there will be clinical diversity. We will use the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator to estimate between‐trial variance. We will use the Hartung‐Knapp‐Sidik‐Jonkman method to calculate a CI for the meta‐analysis effect estimate when there are at least three studies and the estimate of heterogeneity is greater than zero. In other scenarios (i.e. in pooled analyses of two studies, or where the estimate of heterogeneity is equal to zero), we will use the Wald‐type method. All results will be presented with 95% CIs. If the I² statistic exceeds 75%, we will not present a pooled estimate of forest plots. When meta‐analysis is not feasible, we will employ non‐statistical methods to synthesise findings, visually presenting results and summarising effect estimates with ranges and distributions of observed effects.

Investigation of heterogeneity and subgroup analysis

In the case of clinical heterogeneity, we will consider differences in study settings, participant characteristics, interventions, comparators, and outcomes. Methodological heterogeneity, on the other hand, arises from differences in study designs and methodological quality (risk of bias) in studies [49] [31]. We will also assess statistical heterogeneity using Cochran's Q test, also known as the standard Chi² test, and the I² statistic [49]. The rough estimation of heterogeneity based on the I² statistic is:

Equity‐related assessment

We will stratify and descriptively report the included studies to examine whether the effects differ based on several key characteristics. These variables include the country in which the study was conducted, participant age group, sex, and Socio‐Demographic Index (a composite measure of income, education, and fertility) class of the country. This approach will identify any disparities and inform policies aimed at equitable access [41].

Sensitivity analysis

We will conduct sensitivity analysis according to the principles described in Chapter 10 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [39]. Sensitivity analysis will be performed by excluding studies with a low methodological quality (overall high risk of bias). We will conduct sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings by exploring the influence of key assumptions, imputed data, study risk of bias, alternative meta‐analysis models, and borderline decisions.

Two review authors (AFA and MJ) will independently assess the certainty of evidence for each outcome using the GRADE methodology [43], GRADEpro GDT guidance [42], and Chapter 14 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [44]. The GRADE approach facilitates clinical decision‐making, guideline development, and healthcare policy formulation by systematically appraising and summarising available evidence. The GRADE method is based on the evaluation of five key factors: (1) risk of bias, assessing the design and conduct of included studies for issues such as randomisation, blinding, and completeness of data; (2) inconsistency, addressing unexplained heterogeneity or variability in results across studies; (3) indirectness, evaluating the relevance of the evidence to the review question; (4) imprecision, examining the uncertainty around effect estimates, including CIs and sample size; and (5) publication bias in the available evidence [44].

The GRADE system categorises the certainty of evidence into four levels, as follows.

Consumer involvement

This review aims to foster active and ongoing consumer involvement throughout the review process, ensuring that their perspectives, priorities, and experiences influence key decisions and outcomes. Key stakeholders in consumer involvement include clinicians, guideline developers, policymakers, researchers, and patients. To achieve this, the review will employ a multifaceted approach to consumer engagement, utilising a combination of strategies such as direct engagement, collaborative partnerships, and effective dissemination of findings.
