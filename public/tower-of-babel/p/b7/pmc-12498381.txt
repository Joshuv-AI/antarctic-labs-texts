Co-designing a novel intervention for prison staff affected by moral injury: a protocol
Authors: Victoria Williamson, Radha Kothari, Neil Greenberg, Gladys C. M. Hui, Ashley Linehan, Dominic Murphy, Paul Moran, Julian Walker, Danielle Lamb
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Exposure to potentially morally injurious events (PMIEs) can contribute towards serious mental disorders. Prisons are uniquely challenging environments where staff must fulfil competing roles of security, care, and rehabilitation. Currently no manualised intervention for moral injury-related distress exists for prison staff. Here we describe the ‘Co-designing a program for Addressing staff wellbeing and Reducing moral injury Experiences’ (CARE) study protocol, which aims to co-develop an intervention to prevent and respond to moral injury-related ill health.Methods: Data will be collected from current and ex-prison staff. Current staff will be recruited from at least three prisons across the UK. We will survey a cross-section of prison staff exploring the prevalence of PMIEs and the impact of exposure on wellbeing, before carrying out qualitative interviews to explore the lived experiences staff moral injury. In addition, we will conduct interviews with ex-prison staff to understand the impact of PMIEs on wellbeing. These data will feed into the co-design of an intervention programme, designed to reduce distress among staff with PMIEs. Further feedback on the programme protocol will be sought from interview participants to ensure the co-developed programme is acceptable, and feasible to implement in future.Results: Quantitative staff survey data will be cross-sectional and will be analysed using multilevel regression models to explore associations between relevant factors. Qualitative data will be analysed using thematic analysis.Conclusions: Dissemination will include presentations at conferences, publication in academic journals, and free training events. By providing information about prison staff experiences, findings will not only inform the development of an evidence-based approach for staff in prison settings affected by moral injury but may also help inform broader approaches to providing support to other high-risk occupational groups.

Introduction

Moral injury refers to the intense psychological distress that can occur following events that violate ones deeply held moral beliefs (Bonson et al., 2023; Litz et al., 2009; Williamson, Murphy, Phelps, et al., 2021). Potentially morally injurious events (PMIEs) are often categorised as acts of commission (e.g. injuring a detainee with undue force); omission (e.g. being unable to prevent detainee suicide due to limited staffing); or betrayal by trusted others (e.g. provision of poor-quality equipment) (Williamson, Murphy, Phelps, et al., 2021). Following PMIEs, individuals can report experiencing intense feelings of shame, guilt, anger, worthlessness or disgust (Williamson, Murphy, Phelps, et al., 2021). Individuals can also experience negative changes in cognitions and beliefs (e.g. ‘I am an awful person,’ ‘my colleagues don’t care about me’) and use harmful coping strategies (e.g. substance misuse). While moral injury is not a diagnosable mental disorder, these negative changes in appraisals and responses following PMIEs can lead to the development of moral injury-related mental health conditions, such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression as well as suicidality, and substance misuse (Griffin et al., 2019; Williamson et al., 2018).

Prisons are uniquely challenging environments where staff attempt to fulfil multiple and often competing roles, including security, care, and rehabilitation. Research shows prison staff report feeling unable to carry out their roles or support prisoners effectively due to lack of resources, being party to, or witnessing events which contradict personal morals and feeling unsupported by leadership (Dennard et al., 2021). The combination of an under-resourced and deprived environment, along with the internal contradiction of care versus control and power imbalances, may elevate the risk and impact of PMIEs (Williamson et al., 2025).

The number of detainees in England and Wales is expected to rise by 24% from 2021 to 2026 (Prison population projections, 2021 to 2026 - GOV.UK). At the same time, prison staff vacancies and turnover are also increasing (Prison population projections:, 2021 to 2026 - GOV.UK; HM Prison and Probation Service workforce quarterly: June, 2022 - GOV.UK). Meanwhile, the prison estate has experienced reduced real-terms investment, increased overcrowding (61% of prisons in England/Wales are overcrowded [Sturge, 2022]), inadequate staffing, high workloads, increased incidents of detainee violence and self-harm (Justice Committee, 2022; Sturge, 2022) contributing to stress and distress amongst staff and prisoners (Sturge, 2022). It is common for prison staff to witness self-harm and suicide attempts, as well as witness or be involved in violent incidents (Bell et al., 2019; Spinaris et al., 2013). Exposure to such traumatic events is associated with work-related stress, burnout, and mental health difficulties among prison staff (Bell et al., 2019). Similar to other high-risk occupational groups (e.g. paramedics, police and fire-fighters), prison staff often report poor mental health and elevated rates of suicide (Lerman et al., 2022).

Currently, there is no provision of tailored support for moral injury-related distress for prison staff. Moreover, an effective, evidence-based moral injury prevention or mitigation intervention does not currently exist for any occupational group. Preventing moral injury-related mental health problems is an important consideration as international studies suggest that existing treatment approaches are insufficient for those with moral injury-related mental disorders (Steinmetz & Gray, 2015). For example, patients struggling with moral injury who have received standardised treatments, such as trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), report that these interventions do not fully address their symptoms (Bonson et al., 2024). While there is some early evidence that one-to-one psychological treatments for moral injury-related mental disorders such as Adaptive Disclosure (Litz et al., 2021) and Restore & Rebuild (R&R, [Williamson, Murphy, Bonson, et al., 2023]) may be beneficial, these treatments have largely been developed with and evaluated in military personnel/veterans. Furthermore, these treatments involve weekly sessions delivered over several months which may not be feasible to deliver in the resource-poor setting of prisons. A prevention intervention that includes appropriate psychoeducation and frank preparation, fosters adaptive coping strategies, peer support and signposting could be beneficial for prison staff (Leaman et al., 2017; Williamson et al., 2025; Woodall, 2024). However, the provision of any intervention for the prevention or mitigation of moral injury would need to be evidence-based and targeted, taking into account the needs of prison staff, their priorities and concerns, and competing demands on their time, in order to be acceptable and effective.

An approach increasingly used in healthcare service design and evaluation is co-design, where the lived experiences, concerns, perceptions and beliefs of service users are incorporated to improve the quality and delivery of care. A key component of co-design is to develop an in-depth, thorough understanding of how both stakeholders and service users experience and engage with a service, which is thought to result in both improved care and service performance by acknowledging and incorporating the individual’s lived experience across various stages of the care pathway (Dimopoulos-bick et al., 2018; Mulvale et al., 2019). This may, in turn, lead to better health outcomes and more efficient usage of limited healthcare resources (Dimopoulos-bick et al., 2018; Mulvale et al., 2019). A tailored intervention programme that effectively supports prison staff wellbeing has the potential to positively influence staff responses to interactions with detainees (e.g. potentially reducing rates of violence and reoffending), while improving staff retention and reducing rates of prison PMIEs caused by understaffing (e.g. being unable to prevent serious violence). This programme may prove to be beneficial to other occupations at risk of moral injury (e.g. Immigration Removal Centres staff, forensic mental health services). The CARE study (‘Co-designing a program for Addressing staff wellbeing and Reducing moral injury Experiences’) will use a mixed methods approach to explore moral injury in prison staff and co-develop an intervention programme that will mitigate the adverse psychological effects of morally injurious events. This article describes the CARE study co-design protocol. Data collection for this study will take place between May 2025 and March 2026.

Method

Study design

The purpose of this project will be to co-design a manualised intervention for prison staff with mental ill health characterised as ‘moral injury’ following exposure to PMIEs. The project will comprise three main stages (see Figure 1). The first of these is to conduct a comprehensive survey of PMIE exposure and wellbeing among staff who work in prisons across the UK, including male, female and juvenile settings, to understand the prevalence and impact of PMIEs in this context (Stage 1). The second stage is to conduct scoping interviews with prison staff, ex-prison staff and key stakeholders (e.g. clinicians, occupational health staff, chaplains, etc) who have been involved with supporting prison staff, to inform the development of the intervention (Stage 2). The data from Stages 1–2 will inform the initial core features of the intervention programme manual, which will be initially drafted by the research team. The final stage (Stage 3) will be to further develop and refine the intervention manual, with feedback sought from various Stage 2 participants, as well as those in prison leadership positions (e.g. governors, clinical leads) to explore perceived feasibility and acceptability of the new intervention, via qualitative interviews. We will use mixed methods to determine the content of the intervention programme, as well as key details of delivery. The over-arching aim is to optimise the accessibility of and engagement with the programme. Patient and public involvement (PPI)

Involvement from prison staff, including prison officers, clinicians working in prison healthcare roles, and wider stakeholders has informed the development of this protocol and will feature throughout the co-design process. At the protocol development stage, consultation was conducted with prison staff, leading experts, and clinicians who deliver treatment to patients with moral injury. Examples of the decisions made following this consultation included: broadening our inclusion criteria from prison officers to include a wider range of prison staff (e.g. prison healthcare, education, administration and probation staff), increasing the range of prison staff views to be collected, ensuring the generalisability of our findings across prison settings, as well as the likely acceptability and feasibility of our intervention programme.

Throughout the co-design process, we will consult with key stakeholders in several ways: (1) an expert advisory group made up of prison staff, ex-prison staff, and clinical psychologists/psychiatrists with experience of supporting prison staff and patients with moral injury, who are independent from the research team, will contribute towards intervention programme development decisions at a strategic level. (2) The expert advisory group will meet at regular intervals throughout the life course of the study to review study procedures, proposed intervention programme manual components, and data collected across Stages 1–3 to make collaborative decisions, address key issues and manage potentially conflicting perspectives that may have arisen throughout the co-design process.

Participants

Stage 1 survey participants will include prison staff, including officers of any grade, probation/resettlement, education, healthcare, administration, working at included male, female and juvenile prisons. We will include prisons across London, the Southwest, and North (e.g. Manchester) of UK, as these regions have amongst the highest rates of reported staff mental ill health, greatest HMP prison staff turnover, and greatest HMP prison staff demographic diversity (HM Prison and Probation Service workforce quarterly: June, 2022 - GOV.UK; Sturge, 2022; Justice Committee, 2022). We anticipate recruiting Stage 1 participants from at least three HMP prison services across these regions and have a target sample of approximately n = 200 Stage 1 participants. In Stage 2, participants will include 10 clinicians and other relevant stakeholder parties who provide a range of support to prison staff (e.g. occupational health, chaplains), 15 currently serving prison staff, and 10 ex-prison staff who left their HMP role within the last 5 years (total Stage 2 n = 35). In Stage 3, we will re-contact Stage 2 participants who provided consent to be re-approached (expected n = 25), for feedback on the draft intervention manual. We will also seek input via qualitative interview from up to 10 participants in positions of prison governance and management (total Stage 3 n = 35). Final participant numbers will be informed by assessing the range of perspectives represented in the study sample and the data participants provide.

Participant recruitment and inclusion/exclusion criteria

All participants in Stages 1–3 will be required to give written informed consent prior to participation. All staff who work in participating prisons will be eligible (i.e. prison officers of all grades, healthcare staff, forensic psychology, probation/resettlement, education). The Stage 1 survey will be required of all participants prior to taking part in Stages 2–3. Ex-prison staff (n = 10) taking part in Stage 2 must have left their HMP role within the last five years.

We will work closely with senior leaders at each site, to recruit a representative sample of staff working within prisons. Study adverts will be distributed via participating prison newsletters, staff noticeboards and mailing lists and researchers will regularly attend staff meetings to disseminate information about the study. Snowball sampling will also be used, and participants will be asked to share the study with colleagues. We will also post study advertisements on social media platforms (e.g. X, BlueSky) and relevant forums to recruit prison staff directly. In this case, a valid prison or NHS email address will be required to ensure participants are current employees and eligible for the study. The Stage 1 survey will be available to complete using a secure online platform. As prison staff have restricted phone and computer access while on duty, we will also include the option to participate in Stage 1 by distributing hard copies of the survey along with free return postal envelopes at participating prison sites. At the end of the Stage 1 survey, participants will be invited to provide their contact details (e.g. telephone number, email address) and will be provided with contact details for the research team if they wish to also take part in the Stage 2 qualitative interview.

Procedure

We will conduct data collection and analysis in line with the Medical Research Council’s (MRC) guidance on complex intervention development (Alicia et al., 2019; Cole, 2008). All study interviews will be conducted remotely via telephone or video conference call (e.g. MS Teams), audio-recorded with consent and transcribed verbatim.

Stage 1. During Stage 1, we will use the survey to collect data on prison staff’s exposure to PMIEs and moral-injury related responses via the MIOS (Litz et al., 2022). Self-reported symptoms of PTSD and Complex PTSD will be measured via the International Trauma Questionnaire (Cloitre et al., 2018). Measures of alcohol intake (Babor et al., 2001), general physical health (Ware et al., 1996), anxiety and depression symptoms (Kroenke et al., 2009), and functional impairment (Mundt et al., 2002) will also be administered. The Stage 1 survey will also include free text, open response questions to provide staff the opportunity to share their views about the support needed, including questions such as: ‘what kind of support would be helpful to cope well with morally challenging workplace events?; what should this support include?; when should this support be offered (e.g. annually, during induction, post-event, etc)?; how should this support be delivered (e.g. online, face to face)?; who should deliver this support?’ Basic demographics (e.g. age, sex, years in role) will be collected in all stages.

Stage 2. We will conduct one-to-one interviews with prison staff and ex-prison staff, as well as clinicians and key stakeholders who provide support to prison staff. These interviews will provide in-depth insights into the content, format, structure and delivery of the intervention programme. Prison staff and ex-prison staff will be interviewed about experiences of PMIEs, the impact of PMIEs on daily functioning, views of existing support available post-PMIEs, and their support needs. Ex-prison staff will also be asked about their reasons for leaving their prison role and the support they have been able to access since leaving. Clinicians/stakeholders will be interviewed about their views and experiences of supporting prison staff exposed to PMIEs, the key challenges faced when providing support or treatment to individuals with moral injury-related mental health problems, and gaps in existing support related to moral injury. In the interviews a summary of the existing literature about potentially effective pre-emptive and mitigation programmes for moral injury will be shared to explore participants responses to these stimulus materials. This is a common approach within co-design research, setting theoretical and evidential parameters (Williamson, Murphy, Bonson, et al., 2023).

Key priorities to be decided by participants will include: the nature and timing of the programme (e.g. a pre-emptive intervention approach delivered to new staff during induction; a mitigation approach delivered to staff following PMIEs); number of sessions; session format/content; presentation of content; session exercises; overall desired outcomes.

Stage 3. Data from Stages 1–2 will be used to inform the development of the manual. We will re-approach participants from Stage 2 who have given consent to be re-contacted, in order to conduct an additional one-to-one interview with them. The purpose of this additional interview will be to generate detailed feedback about the proposed format, content, and delivery of the intervention programme manual. We will also conduct interviews with staff in positions of prison governance and managers (n = 10) to explore the institutional factors that could impact the implementation and delivery of the programme.

Interview questions will be informed by the Critical Incident Approach (Butterfield et al., 2005) to examine participants views of the developed manual that may inhibit or facilitate a positive experience, or features that may have been overlooked by the research team. During the interview, participants in Stage 3 will be shown a visual representation of different components of the intervention manual’s proposed core aspects, developed from the findings of Stages 1–2. Participants will be asked to discuss their feelings, thoughts, and concerns, with questions including ‘What do you think would be the best way to do this?’, ‘What could be needed to support this part happening?’, and ‘Do you have any concerns about this part of the programme?’. Visual representations of the intervention programme manual will be shown to participants via screenshare (e.g. MS Teams) or sent via email/post for telephone interviews. Following an iterative process, these data will be used by the research team to further refine and optimise the initial programme manual prototype. The PPI expert advisory group will be consulted at key decision making points in the co-production process to create solutions to problems raised or manage inconsistent messages elicited from the Stage 3 interviews. These Stage 3 interviews will provide insight into the codesigned programme’s perceived acceptability and feasibility, allowing for revisions and improvements, ensuring any sub-optimal features are recognised and rectified prior to future pilot trials of the programme.

Data analysis

Quantitative data analysis

Stage 1, using descriptive statistics, we will examine the prevalence of likely mental disorders in prison staff who do/do not report exposure to PMIEs, whether there are any significant differences in mental disorder prevalence between groups, and whether there are any significant moderators of effects. We will use multilevel regression models to explore associations between relevant factors.

Qualitative data analysis

Interviews from Stages 2–3 will be analysed using two approaches: ‘fast and direct’ and ‘in-depth and detailed’ (Williamson, Larkin, Reardon, et al., 2021; Williamson et al., 2022). The ‘fast and direct’ analysis approach will involve making notes and written summaries of the key findings during study interviews, with notes collated and shared with the research team and PPI expert advisory group and, where necessary, used to rapidly alter the proposed intervention programme or procedures. This analytic approach will provide immediate, readily understandable feedback about the manual. The ‘slow and in-depth’ approach will use inductive, reflexive thematic analysis (Clarke & Braun, 2017). The aim of ‘in-depth and detailed’ analysis approach is to use an inductive approach to provide detailed feedback about the feasibility and acceptability of the manual, allowing for disliked/impractical manual elements to be modified prior to future testing. This analysis will capture areas of disagreement that may have been missed during the initial ‘fast and direct’ analysis. Peer-debriefing will be used, with regular reflective discussions with co-authors.

Ethics and dissemination

Approvals for this protocol and its associated procedures will be sought prior to study commencement from the University of Bath Research Ethics Committee, National Health Service (NHS) Research Ethics Committee, and the HM Prison and Probation Service (HMPPS) Research Ethics Committee. Approval has been secured from the King’s College London Research Ethics Committee (HR/DP-23/24-40349).

There are several ethical concerns that were considered when developing this study protocol. First, the potential for participants to disclose events during Stage 2–3 interviews that are illegal, or a criminal offence not already known to the authorities. Should this occur, the research team may be required to breach confidentiality and report events to the relevant authorities. All participants will be fully informed of the researchers’ need to disclose illegal activity prior to participating to mitigate against any potential harm and/or distress this could cause. The researcher will also have access to an experienced clinical team to discuss any possible disclosures (Williamson, Murphy, Castro, et al., 2021). It is also possible that the developed intervention programme manual may cause future recipients further psychological distress or worsen their moral injury-related symptoms. To address this concern, the manual will be developed collaboratively with experts and prison staff, taking into account previous research with morally injured samples. The manual will not include debriefing, which is contraindicated post-trauma (Kenardy, 2000), and any future evaluations will require close monitoring by the study team.

The results are expected to be of national and international interest to those working inside (e.g. researchers, clinicians) and outside (e.g. prison staff, policy makers) the academic sphere. The findings will be disseminated via a free training event made available to relevant stakeholders and clinical care teams in collaboration with the UK Psychological Trauma Society (UKPTS) and Society for Occupational Medicine (SOM). The results will also be shared at conferences, via creative mixed media (e.g. infographic booklet, animated video), and publication in academic journals. It is expected that the study may lead to further evaluation via pilot randomised control trial in the future.

Discussion

Moral injury represents a significant public health concern as it is significantly associated with the development of serious psychiatric comorbidities, including PTSD, depression, anxiety, alcohol misuse and elevated risk of suicidality (Battles et al., 2018; Griffin et al., 2019; Maguen et al., 2023; Williamson et al., 2018). The impact of moral injury-related mental ill health problems is not limited to an individual and it has been found to adversely impact families as well as wider social connections (Ferrajão & Oliveira, 2014; Kelley et al., 2019; Levi-Belz et al., 2022; Williamson, Murphy, Stevelink, et al., 2023). This research aims to co-design a novel intervention programme to address moral injury in prison settings. If future evaluations find our co-designed programme to be successful, the societal and economic impact of even marginally improving the current moral injury-related mental disorders treatment gap could be considerable, as mental illness is the largest single cause of disability in the UK, costing at least £117.9 billion per year (NHS England, 2022). If the intervention is effective, it may help to improve retention of staff in one of the most challenging and difficult sectors.

Studies with clinical care teams have found that clinicians report a lack of confidence and high levels of uncertainty about treating individuals presenting with moral injury-related mental health problems (Serfioti et al., 2023; Steinmetz & Gray, 2015; Williamson, Murphy, Stevelink, et al., 2021). Whether prison services currently feel adequately prepared to manage moral injury in prison staff is unknown. At present, there is no manualised treatment for moral injury-related mental health problems. One-to-one treatments that have been developed have been primarily tested with military personnel/veterans (Litz et al., 2021; Williamson, Murphy, Bonson, et al., 2023) and may not meet the needs of prison staff in their present format. These treatments are also often lengthy, requiring months of weekly sessions, and may not be feasible to offer in under-resourced prison contexts (Williamson et al., 2025). No effective prevention intervention for moral injury currently exists for any occupation either.

There is a paucity of moral injury research in the prison service globally (Williamson et al., 2025) and our study may therefore fill an important evidence gap about the needs and experiences of prison staff who regularly face traumatic and morally challenging situations. If the co-designed programme is later proven to be effective in future trials, it could have a substantial impact on the wellbeing of prison staff in the UK and, with further evaluation, internationally. The results of this study may shape how preventing moral injury, as well as recovery after PMIEs, is conceptualised and approached in the future, contributing towards a more holistic approach that considers the complex needs of staff working across the prison service. If the co-design process yields that a prevention style programme would be most preferable to staff, then the programme developed (if effective) stands to have a substantial impact on prison staff, the NHS and occupational health services by reducing the need for specialist interventions for current and subsequent moral injury-related disorders.

There are a number of limitations to this study which require consideration. While we will recruit participants from prison services across the UK, participants are required to ‘opt-in’ to participate, and therefore, the sample may not be representative of all prison staff who struggle with moral injury following PMIEs. This could potentially limit the generalisability of the findings. However, our inclusive approach to recruitment which incorporates the views of all prison staff across various disciplines and domains (e.g. healthcare, education, probation, governance, prison officers of all grades, and ex-prison staff) and various, inclusive ways staff can participate (e.g. paper or online Stage 1 questionnaire, telephone or video conference call interviews in Stages 2–3) may improve the validity of the sample. With these limitations in mind, it is our intention to collaboratively create a manualised intervention programme for moral injury, informed by the views of prison staff, clinicians, and other relevant parties, that will ultimately improve access to effective, evidence-based support.
