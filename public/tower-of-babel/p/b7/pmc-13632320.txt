Diverse non-vaccine high-risk HPV genotypes in rural Kerala: implications for cervical cancer prevention in an underserved population
Authors: Anusha M. Sharma, Sachin David, Parasmal Suresh, Sivadas Swathi Krishna, Neethu Puthalon Kunnath, Beena Karimbuvayalil Vasudevan, Lalitha Biswas, Keechilat Pavithran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe distribution of high-risk human papillomavirus (hrHPV) genotypes varies across populations and directly influences cervical cancer prevention strategies. Data from underserved rural populations remain limited.MethodsA community-based screening study was conducted among 358 women from rural and tribal regions of Kerala, India. High-risk HPV was detected using real-time PCR, and non-16/18 genotypes were identified through L1 gene sequencing and phylogenetic analysis.ResultsThe prevalence of hrHPV infection was 2.23% (8/358). Detected genotypes included HPV16, HPV31, HPV35, HPV45, HPV66, and HPV70. All detected infections were due to high-risk HPV genotypes, of which three were non-vaccine high-risk types. Phylogenetic analysis demonstrated close similarity with globally circulating strains.ConclusionsDespite low prevalence, the presence of diverse non-vaccine hrHPV genotypes highlights a potential gap between circulating strains and current vaccine coverage. These findings highlight the importance of continued region-specific HPV surveillance in underserved populations and provide baseline epidemiological data for future studies evaluating cervical cancer prevention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13027-026-00778-z.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer among women globally, with around 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths reported in 2022 [1]. Nearly 94% of these cancer deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries, highlighting disparities in access to HPV vaccination, early screening, and treatment. Persistent infection with hrHPV is the primary cause of cervical cancer [2]. Women living with HIV are at significantly higher risk, being six times more likely to develop cervical cancer compared to women without HIV. Prophylactic HPV vaccination, along with screening and treatment of precancerous lesions, are effective and cost-efficient strategies for prevention. In India, cervical cancer is the second most common cancer among women, with an estimated 127,526 new cases and 79,906 deaths annually (GLOBOCAN 2022) [3, 4]. It accounts for 6–29% of all cancers in Indian women, with wide regional variation. The burden is higher in rural areas due to limited access to screening and healthcare. Incidence rates range from 4.9 to 27.7 per 100,000 women, with northeastern and parts of southern and central India reporting the highest rates [5–7]. The general female population shows variable HPV infection rates, ranging from 10% in some western and southern areas to over 60% in parts of eastern India, emphasizing the need for region-specific epidemiological data [8]. The diversity of HPV genotypes also varies geographically, with HPV16 and HPV18 being predominant, but other high-risk types such as HPV33, 35, 45, and 58 were also detected across different regions. Multi-centre Indian studies reported the presence of HPV16 and/or HPV18 in 74–78% of cervical cancer cases, with notable contributions from other high-risk genotypes depending on the region [9]. Our earlier study analysed the prevalence of high-risk HPV and its genotypes in women from a rural region of Kerala, Vypeen Island, and reported a 2% hrHPV prevalence, with HPV16 and HPV33 as the predominant genotypes [10]. Despite increasing implementation of HPV vaccination programs, data on genotype distribution in rural and tribal populations remain limited. There is a significant lack of data on HPV prevalence and genotype distribution in underserved rural populations in India. These populations are often underrepresented in large epidemiological datasets. Our study specifically addresses this gap by providing region-specific insights, which are important for strengthening regional HPV surveillance and generating baseline epidemiological evidence for future public health planning. Understanding the circulating hrHPV types in these underserved settings is important for regional HPV surveillance and may contribute to future evaluations of vaccine coverage and cervical cancer prevention strategies.

Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence and genotype distribution of hrHPV in rural Kerala and assess its implications for vaccine coverage and cancer prevention.

Sample collection and DNA extraction

Cervical samples (n = 358) were collected by trained healthcare professionals using EziPrep cervical collection vials and cervical brushes from women who attended community-based medical camps conducted in six remote and tribal villages of the Idukki district, Kerala (Kumily, Vandiperiyar, Marayoor, Devikulam, Mankulam and Kanthalloor). These locations were selected as part of an institutional outreach program aimed at increasing cervical cancer screening coverage in underserved regions. These camps were conducted as hospital-led outreach programs targeting underserved and rural populations with limited access to routine healthcare services. Women from the local community voluntarily participated in screening activities. Such camps differ from routine clinical settings in that they are designed to enhance accessibility, promote early detection, and reach populations that may not otherwise seek hospital-based care. Eligible participants were women aged 30 years and above. Recruitment included both symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals attending the camps for general health check-ups or unrelated health concerns. Participation was voluntary and no financial incentives were provided; all women received free cervical screening and counselling. Samples were stored at 4 °C immediately after collection, transported to the laboratory on the same day and stored at -20 °C until processing. Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) within 3–5 days of collection. The β-globin internal control was used to assess DNA integrity and confirm suitability for HPV genotyping.

hrHPV detection PCR

DNA extracted from cervical samples was analysed using the QIAscreen HPV PCR assay on the Rotorgene Q Real-Time PCR Detection System (Qiagen, Germany). The assay targets the E7 gene of 15 high-risk HPV genotypes (HPV 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, 67, and 68). A human β-globin gene served as an internal control for sample adequacy and PCR inhibition. Each PCR reaction was performed in a total volume of 20 µL, comprising 15 µL of HPV-Risk master mix and 5 µL of extracted DNA template. For quality control, 5 µL of positive control and negative control were included in each run. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial enzyme activation step at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and combined annealing/extension with data acquisition at 60 °C for 30 s. Multiplex detection was achieved using four reporter dyes, with β-globin (Texas Red), HPV16 (FAM), HPV18 (Cy5), and the pool of 13 other high-risk HPV types (HPV Other) with VIC.

HPV16, HPV18 and the pooled hrHPV group (HPV Other) were interpreted according to the manufacturer’s criteria. Samples were considered HPV positive when HPV16 or HPV18 showed a Cq value below 36, or when HPV Other showed a Cq value below 33.5. Samples were classified as HPV negative when β-globin had a Cq value of 30 or less and HPV16, HPV18 and HPV Other had Cq values at or above their respective thresholds or showed no amplification. Samples with β-globin Cq values above 30 were considered invalid and were re-tested.

Genotyping by L1 region amplification and sequencing

Samples that tested positive for high-risk HPV types other than HPV16 or HPV18 by the hrHPV detection PCR assay were further analysed by amplifying a 450 bp fragment of the L1 gene using the consensus primers MY09 (5’-CGTCCMARRGGAWACTGATC-3’) and MY11 (5’-GCMCAGGGWCATAAYAATGG-3’). PCR amplification was carried out in a 25 µL reaction volume following conditions as described earlier [11, 12]. The amplicons were purified using ExoSAP (New England Biolabs) and sequenced bidirectionally using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit on an ABI 3500 Genetic Analyzer (Thermo, USA). Resulting sequences were analysed using the NCBI BLAST tool to determine HPV genotype. Verified sequences were submitted to the GenBank database. Each cervical sample analysed corresponded to a single participant. Mixed HPV infections were not detected; however, the sequencing approach used primarily identifies the dominant genotype, and co-infections cannot be completely excluded.

Phylogenetic analysis

Partial L1 gene sequences from HPV-positive samples were analysed to determine their genetic relatedness to globally circulating HPV strains. Sequence alignment was performed using ClustalW, implemented in MEGA version 11 software. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the maximum likelihood method, with the Kimura 2-parameter model (Mobini Kesheh et al., 2023). Bootstrap analysis was performed with 100 replicates to assess the robustness of the tree topology. The phylogenetic analysis supports sequence identity, similarity, and expected genetic relatedness to other global HPV strains, without indicating novel evolutionary divergence.

Results

Out of 358 cervical samples analysed by Real-Time PCR, 8 tested positive for hrHPV, corresponding to a prevalence rate of 2.23% (95% CI: 0.97%–4.34%) (Supplementary file 1).

Among the positive cases, two were identified as HPV16 by the real-time PCR genotype assay, which offers specific detection for HPV16 and HPV18. No HPV18 infections were detected in this study population. The remaining six were positive for other high-risk types pooled under the “HPV Other” category. These were subsequently genotyped, along with HPV16, through amplification of the L1 region followed by Sanger sequencing and BLAST analysis, revealing the presence of HPV31, HPV35 (two cases), HPV45, HPV66, and HPV70. No mixed infections were identified in this cohort. The genotype distribution highlights the presence of both vaccine-covered and non-vaccine hrHPV types in the study population (Supplementary file 2). Although the overall prevalence of hrHPV infection was low, the genotype distribution revealed a predominance of non-vaccine genotypes (high-risk HPV types not covered in currently available vaccines). The distribution of study participants by age group revealed that the highest number of cervical samples were collected from women aged 35–45 and 45–55 years. These two groups together accounted for 70% of the total participants enrolled in the study.

The resulting sequences were confirmed through BLAST analysis against reference HPV sequences. A total of six HPV isolates representing five high-risk genotypes (HPV31, 35, 45, 66, and 70) were analysed and submitted to GenBank with accession numbers PV413177 to PV413182. Phylogenetic analysis of the partial L1 gene sequences revealed close clustering with global reference strains, indicating genetic conservation and widespread distribution. HPV31 (PV413179) and HPV35 (PV413177, PV413178) grouped with sequences from the USA, China, Brazil, and Luxembourg, with strong bootstrap support (97% and 99%, respectively). HPV45 (PV413180) clustered with strains from China and Luxembourg (96% support), HPV66 (PV413181) aligned with globally distributed sequences (100% support), and HPV70 (PV413182) grouped with sequences from Brazil, Togo, and China (85% support) (Fig. 1).

Discussion

The observed low prevalence of high-risk HPV (hrHPV) in this rural population is consistent with our earlier findings but reveals notable differences in genotype distribution [10]. While HPV16 and HPV33 predominated in the earlier Vypeen Island study, the current data from Idukki district, Kerala, India identified a broader range of hrHPV genotypes, including HPV35 and HPV70, which were not previously observed. This regional variation highlights the need to strengthen genotype surveillance in underrepresented areas where social, economic, and health system differences may shape HPV epidemiology.

Of particular interest is the detection of HPV66 and HPV70, genotypes that are not included in current bivalent (targets HPV16 &18), quadrivalent (targets HPV6, 11, 16 &18), or nonavalent (targets HPV6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52 & 58) vaccines. HPV35, a non-vaccine type detected in this study, has also been associated with an increased risk of cervical cancer, particularly among women of African descent [1]. Although some cross-protection against HPV35 has been observed with the bivalent vaccine (Cervarix), such protection is incomplete and not long-lasting [13, 14]. HPV66, another genotype not covered by the vaccines, is classified as a “possibly carcinogenic” type, with a relatively low but documented association with precancerous and, in rare cases, cancerous cervical lesions, especially when compared to the established high-risk types HPV16 and HPV18 [15]. Current HPV vaccines do not directly target HPV66, and available data suggest limited or uncertain cross-protection [16]. Although traditionally considered a “probable high-risk” type, recent studies have linked HPV70 to high-grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN2/3) and invasive cervical cancer. Reports have also documented HPV70 as the sole genotype in cases of cervical squamous cell carcinoma, including in women living with HIV [17, 18]. Its emerging clinical relevance, especially in immunocompromised or unvaccinated populations, underscores the importance of including such genotypes in future vaccine development and public health surveillance frameworks. Cross-protection against HPV70 is even less likely, given its phylogenetic distance from vaccine-included types [19].

The detection of HPV45 in this Kerala cohort is important because this genotype is more often reported in sub-Saharan African populations, where it is one of the predominant high-risk types associated with cervical cancer. Its comparatively lower prevalence in Asian regions makes its presence in this study population epidemiologically meaningful [20]. Evidence from sub-Saharan Africa shows that HPV45 is frequently identified in HIV-positive women, where it contributes to both precancerous lesions and invasive cervical cancer. In contrast, all participants in this study were HIV-negative. Reports from South India, including Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh, have also identified HPV45 among high-risk genotypes in women with cervical lesions, though at lower frequencies than HPV16 and HPV18. As HPV45 is included in multivalent vaccines and is a recognized oncogenic genotype, its detection here underscores the need for continued molecular surveillance [21]. Tracking such genotypes will help ensure that vaccination and screening strategies remain aligned with the evolving HPV profile in the region [22–24].

This study adds region-specific data from Kerala to the epidemiology of human papillomavirus (HPV) infection in India, focusing on prevalence and genotype distribution. Consistent with existing literature, HPV prevalence varies widely depending on the study population, while genotype dominance remains relatively consistent. Notably, Indian studies report markedly lower prevalence in community-based populations compared to hospital-based settings. A population-based study from South Andaman reported a relatively low HPV prevalence of approximately 5.9%, reflecting the baseline burden of infection in the general population [25]. In contrast, hospital-based studies, such as those conducted in Bihar and Andhra Pradesh, have reported significantly higher prevalence rates ranging from 37.3% to 86.7%, attributable to the inclusion of symptomatic women and those with cervical lesions [21, 26]. Furthermore, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that HPV is present in approximately 85% of cervical cancer cases in India, underscoring its central etiological role in cervical carcinogenesis [27].

In terms of genotype distribution, the predominance of high-risk HPV-16 observed in this study is in strong agreement with findings reported across multiple regions of India. Studies from Bihar and Andhra Pradesh have consistently identified HPV-16 as the most prevalent genotype, often accounting for over 70–85% of HPV-positive cases, followed by HPV-18 as the second most common type [25, 27]. Both studies utilised PCR-based methods (TaqMan-based real-time PCR targeting & nested multiplex PCR) for HPV detection and genotyping.

This pattern is further supported by population-based data from South Andaman, where HPV-16 was also the leading high-risk genotype [25]. Cervical samples were collected and analyzed using PCR-based HPV DNA detection to determine HPV prevalence, genotype distribution, and associated risk factors. The consistency of HPV-16 dominance across geographically and demographically diverse populations suggests a relatively uniform oncogenic profile in the Indian context. However, the detection of additional high-risk genotypes such as HPV-33, 31, and 45, albeit at lower frequencies, highlights the importance of comprehensive genotyping in understanding the full spectrum of HPV infection and its potential implications for disease progression. The meta-analysis by Satapathy et al. revealed substantial heterogeneity across different regions of India, with prevalence estimates ranging widely between zones [27]. In this regard, the present study from Kerala fills an important gap in the literature by providing localized data from a region that has been relatively underrepresented in HPV epidemiological studies.

A key finding of this study is the predominance of hrHPV genotypes that are not fully covered by currently available vaccines. While the nonavalent vaccine provides broader protection against seven hrHPV genotypes (HPV16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58), it does not address HPV35, HPV66 or HPV70 genotypes, all of which were identified in this cohort. This highlights a potential gap between circulating HPV genotypes and vaccine coverage in specific populations, which may influence the long-term effectiveness of cervical cancer prevention programs. Region-specific surveillance data contribute to understanding the local genotype distribution and may serve as a foundation for future investigations incorporating detailed demographic, behavioural, and clinical variables to identify high-risk groups and evaluate targeted screening approaches [27]. The use of community-based medical camps proved essential in accessing women from rural and tribal settings with limited healthcare access. Such outreach strategies not only provide epidemiological insights but also serve as potential platforms for delivering education, screening, and vaccination services. The absence of HPV18 in this cohort is noteworthy, suggesting regional differences in transmission patterns or effective natural clearance, and merits further investigation.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. The sample size was relatively small, with only 358 participants, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of Kerala or other rural regions in India. The cross-sectional design captures only a snapshot of hrHPV prevalence and genotype distribution, without providing information on progression to cervical lesions. Additionally, the community-based recruitment strategy may introduce selection bias, as women attending screening camps may differ in health-seeking behaviour, symptom status, or perceived risk compared to the general population. Therefore, the observed HPV prevalence should be interpreted as representative of the screened participants rather than the wider population. Confidence intervals were calculated using the exact (Clopper–Pearson) method. This method is preferred here because of the small number of events (n = 8), to avoids underestimating uncertainty compared to normal approximation.

This study provides preliminary insights into the prevalence and distribution of circulating high-risk HPV genotypes. Sanger sequencing has limited sensitivity for the detection of mixed HPV infections, particularly when one genotype is present at low abundance. Therefore, minor co-infecting HPV types may not have been detected in this study.

In conclusion, the detection of non-vaccine high-risk HPV genotypes such as HPV35, HPV66, and HPV70 highlights the importance of continued surveillance of circulating HPV genotypes. These findings provide baseline epidemiological data from an underserved rural population and support the need for larger epidemiological studies to better characterize HPV genotype distribution and evaluate vaccine coverage in India. This study underscores the value of community-based screening programs for detecting hrHPV in rural regions and contributes to regional HPV surveillance and provide baseline data for future epidemiological investigations. While the findings are consistent with existing literature, this study provides region-specific data from a community-based cohort, highlighting the need for larger studies to further explore genotype distribution in such populations. Future community-based studies should incorporate participation metrics and information on non-participants to better assess selection bias and improve interpretation of screening data.
