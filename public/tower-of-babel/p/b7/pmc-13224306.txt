Development and Feasibility of CALORIE CARE© Programme in Head-and-Neck Cancer Survivors
Authors: K. Vijaya Kumar, Arun Varghese, Hritika D. Pai, Stephen Rajan Samuel, M. S. Athiyaaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Head-and-neck cancer (HNC) survivors undergoing concurrent chemoradiotherapy frequently experience treatment-related impairments such as trismus, cervical and shoulder dysfunction, and masticatory muscle weakness. This study aimed to develop and evaluate the feasibility of the CALORIE CARE programme, a tailored exercise intervention for HNC survivors undergoing concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT). The secondary objective was to assess changes in mouth opening measured by maximal interincisal opening (MIO).Materials and Methods:A feasibility study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital in Karnataka, India, from January 2024 to January 2025. Eligible participants were HNC survivors with TNM (Tumor, Node, and Metastasis) stages III–IVb and MIO <35 mm undergoing concurrent chemoradiotherapy. The CALORIE CARE programme was developed using the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDeR) checklist. The 8-week intervention included mobility, stretching, strengthening, swallowing exercises, and aerobic activity. Feasibility outcomes included recruitment rate, adherence rate, and withdrawal rate. Exercise adherence was assessed using the Exercise Adherence Rating Scale-Kannada (EARS-Kn). MIO was measured at baseline, week 4, and week 8.Results:Among 53 eligible patients, 37 consented to participate (recruitment rate 69.81%), and 35 completed the programme (withdrawal rate 5.4%). The adherence rate was 73.94%. The mean EARS score was 17.8, indicating good adherence. A statistically significant change in MIO was observed between baseline and week 4 (p < 0.001), whereas no significant difference was observed between week 4 and week 8 (p = 0.075).Conclusion:The CALORIE CARE programme appears feasible, well tolerated, and demonstrates good adherence among HNC survivors undergoing chemoradiotherapy. Early implementation of multidomain rehabilitation may help delay functional deterioration during treatment; however, its efficacy for improving clinically meaningful outcomes requires confirmation in controlled trials.

INTRODUCTION

In India, the cancer incidence in 2022 was 14,61,427, with females showing a slightly higher rate than males.[1] Among these, 198,438 cases were related to oral cavity and pharyngeal cancers, making them one of the top five cancer types in the country.[2] Notably, mouth-and-tongue cancers are highly prevalent among individuals aged 15–39 years.[2,3] A projected 30% increase in the incidence of head-and-neck cancer (HNC) is expected by 2030.[1] Major risk factors contributing to HNC in India include tobacco chewing, smoking and alcohol use, with minor contributors being poor oral hygiene, micronutrient deficiency and human papillomavirus (HPV) infection.[2,3]

The treatment for HNC depends on the cancer stage. Early-stage cases (I and II) typically undergo surgery followed by radiotherapy, while advanced stages (III, IVa and IVb) require a combination of surgery, radiotherapy and/or concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT).[4] Despite advances in treatment, survivors frequently experience long-term impairments. Studies have shown that HNC survivors often develop trismus, muscle spasms, radiation-induced fibrosis, muscle weakness, temporomandibular joint (TMJ) osteonecrosis, pain, cervical dystonia and peripheral nerve dysfunction.[5-8] These issues contribute to reduced range of motion, muscle strength, increased pain in the neck-and-shoulder regions and greater disability levels.[6-8]

The mechanisms underlying these impairments include weakening of torque-producing muscles (e.g. sternocleidomastoid), devascularisation, scar formation, trick movements and progressive fibrosis due to chronic inflammation.[6,7] Jaw mobility typically ranges from 40 to 70 mm, with trismus diagnosed when mouth opening is ≤35 mm.[9] Trismus is often linked to acute oral mucositis, which affects 40–66.7% of patients, leading to pain, reduced mandibular mobility, changes in diet consistency and enteral feeding dependency.[9,10] Long-term side effects such as TMJ osteonecrosis, fibrosis of the masticatory muscles and tissue adhesions further restrict mouth opening and impact chewing, swallowing, oral hygiene, pulmonary function and overall quality of life.[5,9-11]

Physical therapy plays a key role in managing these issues. Traditional approaches such as stretching, strengthening and resistance training are effective in reducing functional loss and improving quality of life.[6,7] Mouth-opening exercises have proven beneficial in preserving mandibular mobility and are recommended early during cancer treatment.[11,12]

A case report by Lee et al. demonstrated that impairments following submandibular gland tumour excisions, such as TMJ, neck and shoulder dysfunction, were successfully treated with passive stretching, progressive strengthening and aerobic training, improving functional outcomes.[13] Similarly, Ortiz et al. identified a correlation between mouth opening and cervical and shoulder function in HNC survivors.[14] Given this functional relationship, shoulder girdle exercises should be incorporated into jaw mobility rehabilitation, even in the absence of a direct anatomical connection.

Although prior research focused primarily on trismus, HNC survivors often face a combination of issues, including TMJ hypomobility and masticatory weakness, facial and tongue muscle tightness and cervical and shoulder girdle dysfunction. This underscores the need for a comprehensive exercise intervention targeting these diverse impairments. The primary aim of this study was to develop the CALORIE CARE programme and evaluate adherence among HNC survivors undergoing radiotherapy or CCRT in a tertiary care hospital. The secondary aim was to assess changes in mouth opening throughout the course of treatment.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

This was a pilot feasibility study to test the CALORIE CARE programme in HNC survivors undergoing CCRT. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethical Committee (IEC KMC MLR 12/2023/475). The study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital in Mangalore, Karnataka. For the feasibility study, patients were recruited from January 2024 to January 2025. The study inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) above 18 years of age, (2) histologically proven primary diagnosis of HNC, TNM stages – III - IVb, (3) HNC survivors presenting with maximal interincisal opening (MIO) <35 mm, (4) HNC survivors who underwent CCRT; (5) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group score of 0–2. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) HNC survivors with distant metastases, (2) HNC survivors with impaired cognitive function and (3) HNC survivors with other medical conditions that prevented the participants from enrolling in the programme.

As a pilot feasibility study, a formal sample size calculation for efficacy was not performed. The target sample size was considered adequate to provide reliable estimates of feasibility parameters, including recruitment, adherence and withdrawal rates, consistent with recommendations for pilot studies.[15]

Development and procedure of the calorie care programme

Development and validation

The CALORIE CARE programme was developed and described according to the template for intervention description and replication (TIDeR) checklist to ensure comprehensive reporting and replicability.[16] The programme was validated through a multi-step process: (1) It was grounded in a review of available clinical and scientific evidence for exercise therapy in HNC survivors;[5,9,11,17,18] (2) the initial version was collaboratively developed and refined by a multidisciplinary panel, including two senior physiotherapists (each with over 15 years of experience), an oncologist and a rehabilitation nurse to ensure clinical relevance, practicality, and safety; and (3) its description adhered to the TIDieR checklist to ensure completeness. The intervention was thus validated through its evidence-based foundation, expert consensus and adherence to a standardised reporting framework.[5,9,11,17,18]

Based on available evidence, the programme incorporated shoulder girdle and neck muscle strengthening, targeting muscles such as the deltoid, middle trapezius, scapular stabilisers and neck flexors and extensors.[5,17,18] The relevance of all exercises was discussed by the panel. Swallowing exercises, consisting of tongue movements and facial exercises, were included.[19] Given that many participants had a tracheostomy and depended on enteral feeds, isometric exercises for scapular muscles were designed to be performed in sitting or standing positions.[17,18] Chewing exercises were added to improve masticatory muscle function and strength.[9,19,20] Tongue depressors were selected as the mouth-opening device due to their cost-effectiveness, accessibility and suitability for the participants’ predominantly low socioeconomic background.[11]

The resulting CALORIE CARE programme was individualised and tailored to address the heterogeneous impairments HNC survivors’ experience. It targeted five key domains as follows: Mobility: TMJ, cervical and shoulder girdle range of motion exercisesStrengthening: Muscles of mastication, cervical and shoulder girdlesTightness: Stretching for muscles of mastication, cervical and shoulder girdleSwallowing: Tongue mobility and facial expression exercisesAerobic activity: Brisk walking.

Implementation procedure

After obtaining clearance from the attending physician, the primary investigator approached eligible patients. The study’s purpose was explained, and written informed consent was obtained. Participants were then assessed for their specific impairments, and the CALORIE CARE exercises were tailored based on this assessment.

All participants who consented were provided with an exercise logbook to record the number of sessions attended and the reasons for any missed sessions. As participants were admitted to the hospital for their 8-week anticancer treatment, all exercise sessions were conducted under the direct supervision of a physiotherapist. Baseline characteristics, details of the anticancer treatment plan (Including radiotherapy dosage, and details of chemotherapy drugs), and treatment-related side effects were documented at the end of the 8th week.

Exercise intervention

The CALORIE CARE programme was an 8-week, individualised, physiotherapist-supervised exercise intervention for HNC survivors, performed 6 days/week, with each session lasting 25–30 min. It included mobility, stretching, strengthening, swallowing exercises and 20 min of brisk walking daily [Table 1 provides an overview of the exercises].

Mobility and swallowing exercises began with 1 set of 10 repetitions, progressing to 2 sets based on tolerance. Stretching targeted the masticatory, cervical and shoulder muscles (30s hold × 3 reps). Strengthening included isometric and resistance exercises using TheraBands/weights, with intensity guided by the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) scale (11–15). Progression or reduction in intensity was guided by patient-reported exertion.

Brisk walking was performed 4–6 days/week at RPE 11–15, with sessions split into two or more bouts if needed. Physiotherapists provided motivation, corrected trick movements and ensured proper technique throughout.

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes - recruitment rate, adherence and withdrawal

The recruitment rate was defined as the number of participants who consented divided by the total subjects eligible, with ≥30% considered acceptable.[21] Adherence was calculated as sessions attended over total sessions, with ≥60% as the cutoff.[21,22] Withdrawal rate, defined as the percentage who did not complete the intervention programme, was acceptable if <25%.[15] Participants received exercise diaries to track session attendance and record reasons for missed sessions.

Exercise adherence rating scale (EARS)

Exercise adherence was assessed using the EARS, a validated self-reported tool that identifies adherence levels and reasons for adherence or non-adherence. Section B of the EARS, known as the ‘exercise adherence rating scale’, includes 6 items with a maximum score of 24; higher scores indicate greater adherence.[23,24] EARS-Kannada had an internal consistency of 0.93.[24] The Kannada-translated version of the scale was administered to participants at the end of the programme to assess their level of adherence.

Secondary outcome - MIO (maximal interincisal distance)

MIO is measured in millimetres using a ruler. In the sitting position, MIO was measured as the distance between the edge of the incisor of the mandible and the maxilla. In edentulous patients without dentures, the distance between the incisal edge of the most vertical maxillary central incisor and the corresponding mandibular alveolar ridge was measured.[9,13]

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using Jamovi software (version 2.3.28). Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the baseline characteristics and feasibility outcomes, which were presented as means, counts and percentages. Changes in MIO across baseline, 4th week and 8th week were analysed using the Friedman test. post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted when the Friedman test indicated statistical significance (p < 0.05).

DISCUSSION

This study provides a detailed description of an 8-week intervention programme designed for HNC survivors undergoing radiotherapy/CCRT in a tertiary hospital. The primary aim of the study was to assess the feasibility of the CALORIE CARE programme. To assess feasibility, we focused on recruitment, adherence and withdrawal rates and to measure the subjective response towards adherence to the programme, self-reported EARS-Kannada was used.

The overall recruitment rate of the programme was 69.81%, which exceeded our predefined feasibility threshold of ≥30%. These findings are consistent with previous literature, with recruitment rates varying from 36% to 76.9%.[21,22] Our study revealed an adherence rate of 73.94%. A total of 1274 sessions were attended out of 1723 sessions. The common reasons for missed sessions were receiving chemotherapy/ post-radiotherapy (13.5%), experiencing pain, fatigue and vomiting (18.9%), and having a combination of both (59.5%). A total of 5.4% of treatment session denials were due to other reasons, such as blood transfusion and pain post-tracheostomy change. The adherence rate observed was consistent with the findings of the systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Bye et al., which reported adherence rates ranging from 45% to 94%.[25] The CALORIE CARE programme was deemed feasible because of several factors. The intervention was tailored to each subject’s capacity and adjusted according to the acute toxicity of anticancer treatment.[26] All the sessions were supervised and supported by a physiotherapist.[27] In addition, patients are hospitalised during anticancer treatment, eliminating travel barriers.[27] A recent study by Van Aperen et al., on HNC survivors who underwent a tailored intervention programme reported an overall adherence rate of 72%.[27]

A total of 37 patients initially consented to participate in the CALORIE CARE programme, with two subsequently withdrawing, resulting in a withdrawal rate of 5.40% and a retention rate of 94.60%. One patient attended 36 sessions, comprising 14 attended and 22 sessions denied, whereas another attended 7 sessions in full. The retention rates reported in previous feasibility studies of exercise intervention programmes for HNC survivors have varied, ranging from 81% to 100%.[22,25,28] The higher retention rate may be attributed to the fact that participants attended an on-site intervention programme and received support from a physiotherapist.[21,26]

The subjective response regarding adherence to the CALORIE CARE programme was assessed using the EARS-Kannada, a self-report tool. The 35 patients who completed the intervention programme reported a mean score of 17.8. Pai et al. cross culturally adapted and validated the EARS scale in Kannada to assess exercise adherence in HNC survivors and reported that a score >17 indicated adherent. Our study result is consistent with these findings.[24]

Among the 35 patients, one participant experienced trapezius spasm, which was assessed as likely related to their prior anticancer treatments (surgery and radiation) rather than the exercise intervention itself. The subject underwent anticancer treatment, including a prior wide local excision, left supra-omohyoid neck dissection and marginal mandibulectomy. Based on the histopathological report, the subject was subsequently advised to undergo adjuvant radiotherapy (60 Gy in 30 fractions). Trapezius muscle spasm may have occurred because of anticancer treatment, making the muscle susceptible to spasm through mechanisms such as muscle weakness, fatigability and ectopic activity in the innervating motor nerve. The secondary aim of the study was to assess MIO during the intervention programme at baseline, at the 4th week and at the 8th week. A change in MIO was observed from baseline to week 4 (p < 0.001). However, no significant changes were observed between the 4th week and the 8th week (p = 0.075). The increase in mouth opening from baseline to the 4th week may be due to TMJ mobility exercises and jaw stretches, which improve the elasticity and flexibility of the connective tissues around the joint and help relax the muscle.[11,29] No statistically significant differences were observed between the 4th and 8th weeks, as acute toxicities, such as oral mucositis, peaked during this period. Liu et al. reported that oral mucositis peaked between the 4th and 6th weeks.[10] The study also identified risk factors for oral mucositis, including site, chemoradiotherapy, higher radiation dosage,[30] clinical staging, diabetes, poor oral hygiene and prior reconstructive surgery.[10] The severity of oral mucositis results in significant pain (OM-induced pain) in the oral cavity, which subsequently leads to a reduction in mouth opening among the subjects.[30]

Although a change in MIO was observed from baseline to week 4, the clinical meaningfulness of this change is uncertain. Gondivkar et al. (2021)[31] demonstrated that trismus (MIO ≤35 mm) has a severe negative impact on oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL), with functional limitations in eating, chewing and speaking. Their study found that a 1mm reduction in mouth opening was associated with a 0.47-point increase (worsening) on the OHIP-14 scale, indicating that even small changes can be functionally relevant for patients.[31] In our study, the median MIO remained well within the trismus range (≤35 mm) at all time points. The subsequent decline in MIO by week 8, likely due to peak treatment toxicity, underscores the challenging clinical course. Future studies should incorporate patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) related to mastication, swallowing and speech to better determine the true functional impact of rehabilitative interventions on OHRQoL.

In the present study, 78.37% of participants underwent chemoradiotherapy, with 83.78% subjects receiving radiation doses exceeding 63 Gy. The majority of participants (70.27%) had tumours located in the oral cavity, while 21.6% had diabetes as comorbidity. Cancer staging showed that 40.54%, 35.13% and 21.62% of the participants were classified as stages III, IVa and IVb, respectively. In addition, 56.8% of the participants in our study had a history of tobacco chewing, a known factor contributing to poor oral hygiene, and 54.05% of participants had undergone surgical intervention before chemoradiotherapy.

These clinical characteristics may have influenced the progression of trismus during the treatment period. Faravel et al. reported that trismus commonly develops by the 6th week of chemoradiotherapy, with contributing risk factors including tumour site, tumour size, prior surgery and reconstruction, and cumulative radiation dose.[12] These findings align with our study, in which a reduction in MIO was observed between the 4th and 8th weeks of treatment. The risk factors identified in our study are consistent with those reported in the literature, supporting the possibility of treatment-related trismus development in this population.

Although our study did not demonstrate a significant improvement in MIO between the 4th and 8th weeks, the findings suggest a potential delay in the progression of MIO reduction when compared to baseline values (baseline = 24 [IQR 18–39]; 8th week = 23 [IQR 17.5–38.5]). This observation indicates that early initiation of an exercise programme may help reduce the progression of trismus in HNC subjects.[11] Furthermore, intervention programmes such as the CALORIE CARE programme should be encouraged in survivors of HNC to prevent further decline in mouth opening during the on-going anticancer treatment.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of the study lies in the inclusion of 37 subjects in the pilot phase, which allowed for an assessment of the feasibility of the CALORIE CARE programme for HNC survivors undergoing chemoradiotherapy. The intervention was individualised, with all exercise sessions supervised by a physiotherapist. The CALORIE CARE programme specifically addressed the heterogeneous impairments experienced by participants because of various anticancer treatments. In addition, all participants had histopathologically confirmed TNM stages of III or higher, and none were diagnosed with HPV infection.

The present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, all participants were recruited from a single centre using convenience sampling, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. Second, the absence of a control group means that observed changes in MIO cannot be definitively attributed to the CALORIE CARE programme and may reflect natural recovery or other factors. Third, the study did not perform subgroup analyses based on key variables such as prior surgery, tumour site or radiation dose, which are known to influence trismus outcomes. This should be addressed in future larger trials. Finally, important factors in cancer rehabilitation such as fatigue and nutritional status were not assessed, which may have influenced both adherence to the programme and functional outcomes.

Future recommendations

Future research should aim to evaluate the impact of the CALORIE CARE programme on oro-motor function, cardiorespiratory fitness, muscle strength and quality of life among HNC survivors. To strengthen the evidence, upcoming studies should consider employing quasi-experimental or randomised controlled trial designs with larger sample sizes. Incorporating assessments of nutritional status and fatigue is essential for a comprehensive understanding of intervention outcomes. Furthermore, subgroup analyses based on factors such as prior surgical history or cancer stage may provide valuable insights into differential treatment responses.

CONCLUSION

The CALORIE CARE programme demonstrated good feasibility, with acceptable recruitment, high adherence, and low withdrawal rates among head-and-neck cancer survivors undergoing concurrent chemoradiotherapy. The intervention may help delay the progression of treatment-related trismus during anticancer therapy. These findings support the feasibility of implementing a multidomain rehabilitation programme early during treatment. Future studies with larger sample sizes and controlled designs are required to evaluate the effectiveness of the CALORIE CARE programme on functional outcomes and quality of life.
