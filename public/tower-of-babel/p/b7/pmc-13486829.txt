Nuclear condensates formed by truncated mutant NEK1s impede ribosomal RNA biogenesis and drive motor dysfunction
Authors: Yixin Wang, Wen Hu, Rui Huang, Fanmin Wu, Haopeng Su, Jianye Zang, Xiaolei Huang, Yanli Liu, Haigang Ren, Jian Li, Min Zhang, Yong Zhang, Guanghui Wang, Zongbing Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNIMA-related kinase 1 (NEK1), a serine/threonine kinase, is a risk variant for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). While the full-length NEK1 is involved in diverse cellular processes, such as DNA damage response and microtubule stability, the pathogenic mechanism of NEK1 nonsense mutations in ALS remains elusive. Here, we demonstrate that three truncated forms of NEK1 derived from ALS-related NEK1 nonsense mutations translocate from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, exhibit nucleolar localization, and simultaneously form liquid-like nucleoplasmic foci. In contrast to the diffuse cytoplasmic distribution of wild-type NEK1, these nuclear-localized truncated mutants are prone to undergo liquid-liquid phase separation both in cells and in vitro. Mechanistically, the truncated NEK1s interact with the nucleolar protein FBL, thereby impairing ribosomal RNA biogenesis and translation. Transgenic flies expressing truncated mutant NEK1s display motor dysfunction and reduced survival length, and a knock-in transgenic mouse model expressing ALS-related NEK1 mutant similarly exhibits motor deficits accompanied by ribosomal RNA dysregulation. These findings suggest that ALS-related NEK1 mutants expressing truncated forms of NEK1 cause cell toxicity by interfering with ribosomal RNA metabolism and reveal a gain-of-function mechanism in ALS pathogenesis involving NEK1.Subject terms: Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Intrinsically disordered proteins, Mechanisms of disease

Introduction

Mounting advances in genome sequencing have provided unprecedented opportunities to identify genetic mutations, uncover previously unrecognized functions of disease-associated genes, and dissect the common pathogenic pathways underlying diverse diseases. This is particularly relevant to amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), a fatal neurodegenerative disease characterized by the progressive loss of motor neurons in the motor cortex and spinal cord. To date, the identification of approximately 50 ALS-associated genes has transformed our understanding of ALS from a disorder driven by a single proteinopathy to a complex disease closely linked to dysregulation of RNA metabolism1. Most studies focused on the genes with high mutation frequencies in ALS patients, such as TARDBP and C9ORF72. In contrast, other genes, such as the NEK1, a risk factor accounting for up to 3% of ALS cases2–4, have received comparatively limited attention.

The human NEK1 (Nima-related kinase 1), a multifunctional serine/threonine kinase, consists of 1258 amino acids and is characterized by an N-terminal kinase domain, a basic domain, a coiled-coil (CC) domain, and a C-terminal acidic region containing two nuclear export signals (NES). NEK1 exerts pleiotropic effects on diverse biological processes, including ciliogenesis5,6, microtubule stability7, and the DNA damage response8,9. In reported ALS cases, in addition to missense variants, several nonsense mutations in NEK1, such as p.Arg550Ter, p.Arg812Ter, and p.Ser1036Ter, have been identified, which are predicted to form truncated NEK1s due to premature termination. Previous studies have reported that NEK1 is required for the microtubule homeostasis and nucleocytoplasmic transport in induced pluripotent stem cell-derived motor neurons7 and that loss of NEK1 cause DNA damage and impaired DNA repair in ALS-derived motor neurons10. However, the pathogenic mechanism underlying truncated forms of NEK1 derived from ALS-related NEK1 nonsense mutations remains unclear.

Liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) is a biophysical phenomenon that facilitates the formation of numerous biomolecular condensates enriched with proteins and nucleic acids11,12, accounting for the assembly of cellular membraneless organelles, such as nucleoli13–15 and stress granules12,16. Under pathological scenarios, genetic mutations of risk factors can disrupt the phase behaviors of these biomolecular condensates. For example, aberrant phase separation of mutant SHP2 leads to constitutive activation of MAPK signaling17, Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 2 (CMT2)–associated mutants disrupt stress granule dynamics18, and frameshift mutations in HMGB1 impair nucleolar function19. Notably, in ALS, encoded proteins of several pathogenic genes, such as TDP-43, FUS, and hnRNPA1, are components of membraneless organelles. Mutations in these proteins alter the phase behavior of TDP-43 droplets20,21, accelerate the liquid-to-gel transition of FUS condensates22, and promote the fibrilization of hnRNPA123. These aberrant phase transitions disrupt RNA metabolism21,24,25, highlighting dysregulated phase separation as a central mechanism in ALS pathogenesis.

In this study, we found that ALS-related truncated mutant NEK1s specifically localized to the nucleolus and formed discrete foci within the nucleoplasm, where they impaired rRNA biogenesis via interacting with and disrupting the phase behavior of the nucleolar protein FBL, thus causing obvious cell toxicity in mammalian cell lines, motor dysfunction in transgenic Drosophila melanogaster models, and decreased survival length in knock-in transgenic mouse models. Our findings suggest that truncated forms of NEK1 derived from ALS-related NEK1 nonsense mutations impair ribosomal RNA biogenesis.

Results

Truncated mutant NEK1s translocate to the nucleoli and form discrete nucleoplasmic foci

Four ALS-related NEK1 genetic mutations—one missense mutation, p. Arg261His (GFP-261), and three nonsense mutations, p. Arg550Ter (GFP-550), p. Arg812Ter (GFP-812), and p. Ser1036Ter (GFP-1036)—were indexed and corresponding plasmids were constructed in this study (Fig. 1a). The expression of full-length NEK1 and the four GFP-tagged NEK1 mutants was successfully detected at the corresponding molecular weights using antibodies against either GFP or N-terminal NEK1 epitopes. However, the C-terminal NEK1 antibody recognized only constructs containing the specific 975-1025 amino acid sequence (Fig. 1b). Similarly, the intermediate NEK1 antibody failed to detect the GFP-550 construct, potentially because this antibody recognizes an epitope within the 550-812 region, although the exact site remains commercially proprietary (Fig. 1b).

In HEK293T cells in which GFP-tagged NEK1 constructs were expressed, both wild-type NEK1 and mutant GFP-261 exhibited a diffuse cytoplasmic distribution (Fig. 1c). However, all truncated mutant NEK1s displayed obvious nuclear translocation (Fig. 1c). Moreover, GFP-550 and GFP-812 localized to the nucleoli, as indicated by nucleolar NPM1 staining, and simultaneously formed discrete nucleoplasmic foci. In contrast, GFP-1036 primarily displayed nucleoplasmic foci (Fig. 1c). Given that the nucleus contains a variety of membraneless organelles, such as Cajal bodies, nuclear speckles, paraspeckles, and DNA damage foci, we examined the co-localization of nucleoplasmic foci formed by truncated mutant NEK1s with these organelles. However, none of the tested components of these membraneless organelles co-localized with the nucleoplasmic foci formed by truncated mutant NEK1s (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Similarly, truncated mutant NEK1s showed no overlap with other structures such as nuclear lamina or lipid droplets (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). Notably, compared with GFP-550 and GFP-1036 groups, GFP-812 displayed a greater number and proportion of nucleoplasmic foci (Fig. 1d, e). Similar cellular distribution of truncated mutant NEK1s was observed in SH-SY5Y cells, a human neuron-like cell line (Fig. 1f). To rule out potential bias introduced by the GFP tag, we replaced it with an mCherry tag in all constructs or directly deleted the GFP tag, and observed a comparable distribution pattern to that detected in GFP-tagged cells (Supplementary Fig. 1e, f). Furthermore, we performed subcellular fractionation assay, and found that, consistent with the findings in immunofluorescence assay, truncated mutant NEK1s were enriched predominantly in the nucleus, whereas wild-type NEK1 and GFP-261 were primarily cytoplasmic (Supplementary Fig. 1g). Although truncated mutant NEK1s formed numerous nucleoplasmic foci resembling pathological inclusions, they were enriched predominantly in the soluble fractions (Supplementary Fig. 1h).

To exclude the artificial effect of overexpression on the formation of NEK1 nucleoplasmic foci, we generated doxycycline-inducible SLIK-GFP-WT, SLIK-GFP-550, and SLIK-GFP-812-expressing 293 T cell lines (Fig. 1g). When the expression level of SLIK-GFP-812 was comparable to that of endogenous NEK1 (Fig. 1h), we still observed enrichment of GFP-550 and GFP-812 in the nucleoli, as well as the formation of discrete nucleoplasmic foci (Fig. 1i). In contrast, the expression of GFP-WT at the concentration equivalent to endogenous NEK1 displayed cytoplasmic localization (Supplementary Fig. 1i, j), corroborating our results in overexpressing cultured cells (Fig. 1c–f). In summary, these findings demonstrate that truncated mutant NEK1s preferentially localize to the nucleoli and form discrete nucleoplasmic foci, in contrast to the predominant cytoplasmic distribution observed for wild-type NEK1 and the missense mutation.

Truncated mutant NEK1s de-mix into liquid-like nuclear condensates

The enrichment of truncated mutant NEK1s in membraneless organelles, such as nucleoli, is reminiscent of various biomolecular condensates formed via liquid-liquid phase separation11. To assess the material properties of the nucleoplasmic foci formed by truncated mutant NEK1s, we performed three classical experiments: a fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) assay, a 1,6-hexanediol (1,6-HD) assay, and a fusion test. Using the FRAP assay, we bleached the fluorescence of truncated mutant NEK1s in the nucleoli and nucleoplasmic foci, respectively. We observed that all truncated mutants in the nucleoli exhibited rapid recovery of fluorescence intensity within 20 s (Fig. 2a–c), indicating high fluidity. The nucleoplasmic foci formed by truncated mutants showcased comparable fluidity to that in the nucleoli (Fig. 2d–f). Similar recovery rates were also observed in doxycycline-inducible SLIK-GFP-550 and SLIK-GFP-812 cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). Additionally, the enrichment of truncated mutant NEK1s was disrupted by 3% 1,6-HD (Fig. 2g, h), an aliphatic alcohol commonly used to dissolve LLPS26. The fusion events of the condensates were also captured through live-cell imaging (Fig. 2i). We found that GFP-812 condensates fusion occurred in the majority of cells: approximately 40% of cells exhibited one fusion event during the 10-min imaging period, while ~20% displayed two fusion events, indicating that fusion of GFP-812 condensates is a frequent process in cells (Supplementary Fig. 2c). As suggested by biochemical analyses (Supplementary Fig. 1h), these results collectively indicate that nuclear condensates formed by truncated mutant NEK1s in both nucleoli and nucleoplasmic foci exhibit liquid-like material properties.

To provide clarity in terminology, nucleoplasmic foci observed in this study refer to nuclear condensates that are nucleoli-deficient, while the term ‘nucleolus’ is retained for condensates that are nucleoli-sufficient.

The coiled-coil domain is indispensable for the assembly of nucleoplasmic foci by NEK1 mutants

Given that GFP-812, relative to GFP-550, incorporates the complete CC domain, we investigated the domain responsible for the formation of nucleoplasmic foci using the GFP-812 construct (Fig. 3a). Deleting the basic domain from GFP-812 (GFP-812-ΔB) resulted in diffuse cytoplasmic localization in 293 T cells (Fig. 3b), possibly due to the loss of nuclear localization signals within the basic domain (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Remarkably, deletion of the CC domain (GFP-812-ΔCC) caused a substantial reduction in the percentage of nucleoplasmic foci, from approximately 60% to 5% (Fig. 3c), and in the number of nucleoplasmic foci, from average 10 to 2, per cell (Fig. 3d), whereas maintaining nucleolar enrichment (Fig. 3b–d). Interestingly, deletion of the kinase domain (GFP-812-ΔK) led to an increase in the formation of nucleoplasmic foci (Fig. 3b–d). Together, these findings indicate that the CC domain is necessary for the formation of nucleoplasmic foci.

To further confirm the roles of individual domain in condensate assembly, we overexpressed the kinase, basic, and CC domains, respectively, in cultured cells (Fig. 3e). GFP-Kinase exhibited diffuse cytoplasmic distribution, whereas GFP-Basic localized prominently to the nucleolus, suggesting that the basic domain acts as a nucleolar localization signal within GFP-812 (Fig. 3f, g). GFP-CC, on the other hand, formed diffuse nucleoplasmic foci in approximately 50% of cells (Fig. 3f, h), indicating that the CC domain alone is sufficient to drive nucleoplasmic foci formation. Interestingly, combining the basic and CC domains (GFP-BC) significantly increased the number of nucleoplasmic foci, accompanied by nucleolar localization (Fig. 3f–i). These results suggest that the basic domain increases the capability of forming nucleoplasmic foci based on the CC domain.

The basic and CC domains are predominantly composed of basic amino acids, such as lysine (K) and arginine (R) (Supplementary Fig. 3a), which are essential for LLPS as reported in previous studies27. Predictions using IUPred3 and PONDR analyses further indicated that these domains are prone to undergo phase separation (Supplementary Fig. 3b). To examine this prediction, we employed an optogenetic system in which the photolyase homology region (PHR) of Arabidopsis thaliana Cry2 undergoes self-oligomerization and promotes phase separation of coupled IDRs upon exposure to blue light28 (Supplementary Fig. 3c). The kinase, basic, and CC domains were fused to the N-terminus of an mCh-Cry2 protein, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3d). As anticipated, pHR-CC underwent phase separation following blue light activation, whereas the pHR-vector and pHR-Kinase did not form nucleoplasmic foci under the same conditions (Supplementary Fig. 3e). Surprisingly, in pHR-Basic-expressing cells, blue light stimulation induced the rapid formation of numerous nucleoplasmic foci, in addition to nucleolar enrichment (Supplementary Fig. 3e), similar to the nucleoplasmic foci formed by GFP-BC (Fig. 3f). These results suggest that both the basic and CC domains are capable of undergoing phase separation, however, phase separation of the basic domain depends on the scaffold provided by the Cry2 protein or by the CC domain of NEK1.

To directly visualize the droplets formed by CC domain in vitro, we expressed and purified the His-tagged CC domain of NEK1 in an E coli. system (Supplementary Fig. 3f). The CC protein solution exhibited a turbidity gradient in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3j), which was corroborated by measuring the absorbance at 600 nm wavelength (Fig. 3k). The turbidity exhibited by CC solution is reversible in a temperature-dependent manner, which was indicated by loss of a myriad of droplets at low temperature (Fig. 3l). Notably, the recombinant CC protein assembled spontaneously into regular spherical droplets up to 20 µm in size, and fused frequently into larger droplets on the coverslips (Fig. 3m), indicating liquid-like properties. Moreover, the formation of liquid droplets required specific protein concentrations and appropriate electrostatic interactions regulated by various NaCl concentrations (Fig. 3n). All these data suggested that the CC domain is necessary and sufficient for the formation of NEK1 biomolecular condensates.

The coiled-coil domain drives the multimerization of NEK1-related nucleoplasmic foci

Given the competence of the CC domain in full-length NEK1 to undergo dimerization in yeast systems, as previously reported29, we hypothesized that multimerization mediated by the CC domain may drive the formation of liquid droplets. To examine the capability of multimerization, we co-expressed GFP-CC and mCherry-CC in 293 T cells and observed that nucleoplasmic foci formed by GFP-CC co-localized with that formed by mCherry-CC (Fig. 4a, b). In contrast, no co-localization was found between GFP-CC condensates and mCherry alone (Fig. 4a, c). Besides, a fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) acceptor bleaching assay demonstrated significant energy transfer between GFP-CC and mCherry-CC within the condensates, compared with that between GFP-CC and mCherry, indicating intermolecular interactions between GFP-tagged and mCherry-tagged CC domains (Fig. 4d–f). To further confirm these interactions, an immunoprecipitation assay revealed that GFP-CC bound to mCherry-CC but not to mCherry alone (Fig. 4g). Next, we employed a crosslinking assay using disuccinimidyl suberate (DSS) to examine the oligomeric state of CC and BC domains. Notably, a smear band corresponding to high-order oligomerization was detected in DSS-induced HA-tagged CC and BC samples, respectively (Fig. 4h). This result indicates that the CC domain is capable of undergoing oligomerization. We extended the DSS-induced crosslinking assay to truncated mutant NEK1s by analyzing 550-HA and 812-HA groups. Both exhibited high-order oligomerization (Fig. 4i). However, deletion of the CC domain abrogated this oligomerization, illustrating that intermolecular interactions mediated by the CC domain are critical for the oligomerization of truncated mutant NEK1s (Fig. 4i).

To investigate whether the oligomerization of the CC domain promotes phase separation, we constructed a variety of chimeras in which the CC domain of GFP-BC was replaced with structural domains capable of oligomerization (Supplementary Fig. 4a). These domains included the N-terminal oligomeric domain of NPM1 protein (NPM11-122)30, the CC domain of NEK2 protein (NEK2-CC)31, and the CC domain of SynGAP protein (SynGAP-CC)32 (Fig. 4j). Remarkably, all three chimeras exhibited discrete nucleoplasmic foci in transfected cells (Fig. 4k). However, these oligomeric domains alone either failed to form condensates or produced significantly fewer condensates (Fig. 4l and Supplementary Fig. 4b–e). To exclude the possibility that the promiscuous interactions mediated by any CC-containing chimeras account for the formation of biomolecular condensates, we replaced the CC domain of GFP-BC with that of YAP, which domain confers YAP with the capability of phase separation33,34(Supplementary Fig. 4f). Surprisingly, no foci was detected in cells expressing GFP-Basic-CC(YAP) chimera (Supplementary Fig. 4g). Moreover, we synthesized a recombinant CC domain in which the amino acid sequence was randomly reordered while preserving its overall amino acid composition (Supplementary Fig. 4f). Interestingly, although the recombinant CC domain retain a coiled-coil-like structure predicted by AlphaFold35 (Supplementary Fig. 4h), the GFP-Basic-CC(Random) chimera failed to form nucleoplasmic foci in cells (Supplementary Fig. 4g). These data suggested that the specific CC conformation is required for biomolecular condensate assembly in vivo.

Next, we determined the necessity of CC oligomerization for phase separation. Because it’s difficult to identify the amino acid residue required for the oligomerization of NEK1 CC domain, we constructed an artificial chimera, GFP-Basic-CC(SynGAPMut), in which the Leu1202 and Lys1252 amino acids of SynGAP CC domain were mutated to Asp, respectively, termed as CC(SynGAPMut) (Fig. 4j), so as to convert the trimer formed by SynGAP CC into a monomer32. Importantly, the mutant chimera Basic-CC(SynGAPMut) showed a significant reduction in the number of nucleoplasmic foci compared with that of CC(SynGAPWT) (Fig. 4k and Supplementary Fig. 4e), suggesting that CC oligomerization is required for the assembly of NEK1 nucleoplasmic foci.

In summary, inspired by the “scaffold and client” model36 used to explain biomolecular condensate formation, we propose a model for truncated mutant NEK1s-mediated nucleoplasmic foci assembly. In this model, the CC domain acts as a scaffold mediated by oligomerization to assemble the nucleoplasmic foci, the capability of which are further enhanced by the basic domain, the predicted IDR, which probably incorporates other unknown factors (Fig. 4m).

Truncated mutant NEK1s bind to and co-localize with the nucleolar protein FBL

To explore the biological functions of truncated mutant NEK1s, we overexpressed GFP, GFP-550, GFP-812, and GFP-1036, respectively, in mammalian 293 T cells and conducted immunoprecipitation assays (Fig. 5a), followed by proteomic analyses to identify common binding proteins among the three mutant groups (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Data 1). Gene Ontology (GO) pathway analyses of the 234 overlapped proteins revealed enrichment in several biological pathways, including the “Cell cycle” pathway (Fig. 5c), which aligns with previous studies that demonstrated the role of NEK1 in mitosis37. Additional biological processes, such as “RNA metabolism”, “translational regulation”, and “rRNA processing” were also enriched (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 5a). Notably, among the cellular components identified (Supplementary Fig. 5b), “Nucleolus” and “Stress granule” were obvious items, while “DNA repair” was highlighted as a significant molecular function (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 5c). These findings suggest that truncated mutant NEK1s may be associated with membraneless organelles.

Seven representative proteins associated with membraneless organelles, including nucleolar proteins (RPA194, NPM1, FBL, DDX21), DNA damage foci markers (Ku70, Ku80), and a stress granule marker (DDX3X), were selected for co-localization analyses (Fig. 5e). Consistent with previous results (Fig. 1c), truncated mutant NEK1s co-localized with nucleolar markers but not with markers of stress granules and DNA damage foci (Fig. 5e). Interestingly, among these nucleolar markers, only FBL was recruited to the nucleoplasmic foci formed by truncated mutant NEK1s (Fig. 5f–h). To exclude any artifacts derived from overexpression, we induced the expression of truncated mutant NEK1s at physiological levels. Under this condition, truncated mutant NEK1s also co-localized with endogenous FBL (Supplementary Fig. 5d–f). Consistently, biochemical analyses confirmed that truncated mutant NEK1s interacted with both exogenous (Supplementary Fig. 5g) and endogenous FBL (Fig. 5i). Furthermore, FRET assays revealed in situ interactions between FBL and truncated mutant NEK1s (Supplementary Fig. 5h–k). Previous studies have shown that RNA-binding proteins, such as TDP-43, FUS, and hnRNPA2B1, are prone to form nuclear condensates, the aggregation of which are classical pathological features of ALS38. However, neither cytoplasmic aggregation nor abnormal nuclear condensates were detected in truncated mutant NEK1s expressing cells (Supplementary Fig. 5l, m). In summary, truncated mutant NEK1s specifically interact with RNA-processing proteins, particularly the nucleolar protein FBL. These findings suggest that truncated mutant NEK1s may interfere with nucleolar RNA processing.

FBL, a conserved nucleolar protein prone to undergo phase separation, is involved in multiple steps of ribosomal biogenesis, including rRNA synthesis, sorting, cleavage, and modification39–42. To determine whether truncated mutant NEK1s disrupt FBL function, we first performed Western blot assays and observed no changes in the expression levels of nucleolar proteins, such as UBF, FBL, and NPM1 (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Next, we used FRAP assays to assess the impact of truncated mutant NEK1s on FBL dynamics in living cells. In GFP-550 and GFP-812 expressing 293 T cells, the fluorescence recovery rate of FBL was significantly reduced (Fig. 6a, b), indicating that truncated mutant NEK1s might impair the normal nucleolar function of FBL.

Since phase separation is crucial for physiological functions of FBL, we expressed and purified the GFP-tagged FBL protein (Supplementary Fig. 6c) and performed in vitro phase separation assays. Consistent with previous studies15,42,43, the purified GFP-FBL protein underwent phase separation in a protein concentration- and salt concentration-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 6d). Notably, the addition of CC dramatically increased the size of droplets formed by GFP-FBL (Fig. 6c, d and Supplementary Fig. 6e), indicating a direct alteration on the phase behavior of FBL. To determine whether CC interferes with the rRNA sorting function of FBL, we added purified CC protein to the mixture containing the FBL and 5’ ETS, a 5’ snippet of pre-rRNA known to interact with FBL42. As reported previously42, FBL and 5’ ETS underwent phase separation and formed droplets in vitro. However, the addition of CC altered the distribution of 5’ ETS, forming dense foci around the edges of the droplets (Fig. 6e, f), indicating a disruption in rRNA sorting. These findings suggest that truncated mutant NEK1s might impair the function of FBL in rRNA processing by affecting its phase separation properties.

Truncated mutant NEK1s impair rRNA biogenesis, impede translation, and cause cell death

To investigate the effect of truncated mutant NEK1s on rRNA processing in cells, we first examined nucleolar morphology and found a significant increase in the number of nucleoli (Supplementary Fig. 6f) and a reduction in nucleolar area (Supplementary Fig. 6 g), indicating nucleolar dysfunction. Accordingly, the production of rRNAs, including 5.8S and 18S rRNA, was significantly reduced (Fig. 6g). Furthermore, RNA sequencing revealed numerous dysregulated genes, particularly those encoding ribosomal proteins (Fig. 6h and Supplementary Data 2), suggesting that truncated mutant NEK1s may impair rRNA biogenesis and disturb ribosomal homeostasis.

Given that rRNA processing is essential for cellular translation, we used the surface sensing of translation (SUnSET) assay to monitor global protein synthesis. After cells were treated with puromycin, a structural analogue of aminoacyl tRNAs44, the elongation of the polypeptide chain was blocked due to the incorporation of puromycin into the ribosome. The fluorescence intensity of the antibody against puromycin was then used to indicate the rates of protein synthesis in doxycycline-induced SLIK-GFP-812 cell line (Fig. 6i). Compared with control and GFP-negative cells, cells stably expressing GFP-812 induced by doxycycline treatment exhibited a significant decrease in the puromycin signal, indicating reduced protein synthesis in GFP-812 expressing cells (Fig. 6j, k). In addition, we performed a luciferase reporter assay to assess translational activity. Consistent with the results of the SUnSET assay, Renilla luciferase activity was decreased by approximately 40% in GFP-550- and GFP-812-expressing cells, compared with that in the GFP group (Fig. 6l), suggesting that truncated mutant NEK1s inhibit global translation. Moreover, increased propidium iodide (PI) staining signals (Supplementary Fig. 6h, i) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity (Supplementary Fig. 6j) were detected in truncated mutant NEK1s-expressing cells compared with the control cells (Supplementary Fig. 6h–j), indicating cellular toxicity. Taken together, these findings suggest that truncated mutant NEK1s impair rRNA biogenesis, thereby impeding translation and contributing to cell death.

Truncated mutant NEK1s cause motor dysfunction and reduce the lifespan of Drosophila

To determine whether overexpression of truncated mutant NEK1s induces neurotoxicity in vivo, we constructed transgenic fly models in which the expression of wild-type hNEK1 (hNEK1-WT) and truncated mutant NEK1s, including GFP-550 and GFP-812, was driven by the GAL4-UAS system45. By crossing with the DA-GAL4 fly line, the expression of hNEK1-WT and truncated mutant NEK1s was induced in all fly tissues (Fig. 7a). The expression of hNKE1-WT, GFP-550, and GFP-812 was successfully detected in fly brains using a GFP antibody (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Fig. 7a). Using an antibody that recognizes an intermediate epitope of NEK1 (Fig. 1a), specific bands of identical molecular weight to that recognized by the GFP antibody was detected in hNEK1-WT and GFP-812 groups but not in the GFP-550 group (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Fig. 7a). Consistent with our findings in mammalian cell lines (Fig. 1c), truncated mutant NEK1s displayed obvious nuclear distribution in neurons, as indicated by the localization of Elav, a neuronal-specific protein46 (Fig. 7c). Notably, nuclear condensates formed by truncated mutant NEK1s co-localized with the endogenous Fib protein, the Drosophila homolog of human FBL protein (Fig. 7d), compared to diffuse cytoplasmic distribution exhibited by neurons in hNEK1-WT flies (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c), suggesting that we have successfully constructed NEK1 transgenic fly models.

Consistent with our results in cultured cells (Fig. 6g), overexpression of truncated mutant NEK1s in flies significantly reduced rRNA levels, including 5.8S, 18S, and 28S rRNAs (Fig. 7e), compared with the group overexpressing hNEK1-WT (Supplementary Fig. 7d), indicating the disruption of rRNA biogenesis. To assess the effect of mutant NEK1s on motor behavior, we performed climbing tests. We found no behavioral differences in 1-day-old flies, however, at 10 and 20 days of age, flies overexpressing truncated mutant NEK1s showed a significant reduction in climbing ability (Fig. 7f). By contrast, hNEK1-WT flies displayed climbing performance comparable to that of control flies (Supplementary Fig. 7e). These data suggest that truncated NEK1 mutants causes progressive motor dysfunction (Fig. 7f). Furthermore, female flies expressing truncated mutant NEK1s, particularly in the GFP-812 group, exhibited shortened survival length compared with control and hNEK1-WT groups (Fig. 7g and Supplementary Fig. 7f). In summary, these findings indicate that overexpression of truncated mutant NEK1s disturbs rRNA biogenesis and is detrimental to neurons in vivo.

NEK1-812 knock-in transgenic mouse exhibits motor dysfunction and rRNA dysregulation

To investigate the function of truncated NEK1 mutants at physiological expression levels, we generated a transgenic mouse line in which the endogenous mouse Nek1 gene locus was replaced with a GFP tagged human NEK1-812 fragment, so as to drive the expression of GFP-hNEK1-812 under the control of endogenous mNek1 promoter (Fig. 8a). Western blot analysis showed a prevalent expression of wild-type mNEK1 in various organs, including the brain, heart, liver, spleen, and lung (Fig. 8b). Consistent with our results in cultured cells, GFP-hNEK1-812 displayed the bands at desired molecular weight. A single band representing GFP-hNEK1-812 was detected in homozygous transgenic mouse, whereas two bands, indicating wild-type mNEK1 and GFP-hNEK1-812, respectively, were found in the heterozygous group (Fig. 8b). Given the poor recognition of NEK1 antibodies in immunohistochemical assay, we determined the cellular distribution of mNEK1 and GFP-hNEK1-812 in primary cortical neurons isolated from wild-type, heterozygous, and homozygous embryos. Compared with the cytoplasmic distribution of wild-type mNEK1, neurons from heterozygous and homozygous mice displayed obvious nuclear enrichment of NEK1 signals and formed non-nucleolar nucleoplasmic foci, indicating the nuclear translocation of GFP-hNEK1-812 under physiological expression levels (Supplementary Fig. 8a). These results suggest that we have successfully constructed the hNEK1-812 knock-in transgenic mouse model.

Next, we performed a series of motor behavioral tests to determine the neurotoxicity of GFP-hNEK1-812 in vivo. No differences in body morphology and body weight were observed between wild-type control and heterozygous mice up to 12 months of age (Supplementary Fig. 8b, c). However, the heterozygous mice showed dramatically decreased survival length compared with wild-type controls (Supplementary Fig. 8d). Moreover, compared with wild-type mice, the heterozygous male mice displayed an age-dependent reduction in travel distance in the open field test, indicating motor dysfunction (Supplementary Fig. 8e). We further evaluated motor function using three additional assays ─ the rotarod test, balance beam test, and footprint test. Consistently, the heterozygous mice showed decreased latency on the rotarod, increased numbers of slips on the balance beam, and reduced stride length compared with wild-type controls (Fig. 8c–e and Supplementary Fig. 8f). These data suggest that the expression of GFP-hNEK1-812 at physiological level is sufficient to induce progressive motor deficits.

We then asked which brain regions contribute to the motor dysfunction in heterozygous mice. Immunostaining assays showed that, compared with wild-type control mice, heterozygous mice displayed reduced thickness of the motor cortex and the cerebellar molecular layer (Fig. 8f, g and Supplementary Fig. 9a, b), indicating injury to upper motor neurons. In contrast, no significant loss of motor neurons was detected in the lumbar spinal cord (Supplementary Fig. 9c, d). To investigate the origin of these motor deficits, we performed immunostaining analyses to evaluate the synaptic integrity of neuromuscular junctions (NMJs) in the gastrocnemius (GA) muscle. The degree of motor neuron innervation was determined by the colocalization between presynaptic axon terminals labeled by synaptophysin (SyPhy) staining and postsynaptic motor endplates labeled by fluorophore-conjugated α-bungarotoxin (α-BTX). Complete colocalization was defined as full innervation, whereas partial or absent colocalization indicated partial innervation or denervation, respectively. Compared with wild-type mice, 12-month-old heterozygous mice showed a significant reduction in fully innervated NMJs, accompanied by increases in partially innervated and denervated NMJs (Fig. 8h, i). Chronic muscle denervation can lead to muscle degeneration, commonly characterized by the presence of centrally localized nuclei derived from regenerating muscle precursor cells47. Consistently, heterozygous mice showed a significant increase in centralized nuclei within GA muscle fibers compared with wild-type controls (Supplementary Fig. 9e, f). Collectively, these data suggest that expression of hNEK1-812 disrupts NMJs integrity and contributes to lower motor neuron dysfunction.

Importantly, in line with our cell culture findings, rRNA levels were significantly decreased in the motor cortex of heterozygous mice compared with wild-type controls (Fig. 8j). In summary, these findings suggest that physiological expression of truncated NEK1 mutants leads to motor dysfunction and rRNA dysregulation in vivo.

Discussion

In this study, we identified that all three disease-related NEK1 mutants that express truncated NEK1 translocate from cytoplasm to the nucleus, localize in the nucleoli, and form nucleoplasmic foci, and we characterized the properties of these biomolecular condensates: (1) Physical properties - truncated mutant NEK1s in both the nucleoli and nucleoplasmic foci exhibit liquid-like property, undergoing LLPS in vitro; (2) Assembly - the CC domain serves as a scaffold via intermolecular oligomerization and is essential for the assembly of nucleoplasmic foci, whereas the basic domain, composed of intrinsic disorder sequences, amplifies these condensates; (3) Composition - truncated mutant NEK1s interact with various RNA-processing proteins, particularly the nucleolar proteins, such as FBL; and (4) Cellular functions - truncated mutant NEK1s impair rRNA biogenesis, impede cellular translation, and induce motor dysfunction in both transgenic Drosophila and knock-in truncated NEK1 mouse models (Fig. 9).

Schematic model illustrating the pathogenic mechanism underlying cell toxicity caused by truncated mutant NEK1s.

The wild-type NEK1 primarily exhibits a cytoplasmic distribution, whereas truncated mutant NEK1s aberrantly translocate to the nucleus, accumulate within the nucleolus and form discrete nucleocytoplasmic foci. This truncated mutant NEK1s recruit the nucleolar protein FBL and alter its phase separation properties, thereby suppressing FBL-mediated rRNA biogenesis. Finally, the expression of truncated mutant NEK1s impair cellular translation and lead to neuronal deficit.

Genetic analyses revealed that homozygous NEK1 nonsense mutations are fatal, causing developmental diseases, such as short-rib polydactyly syndrome (SRPS)48, whereas heterozygous mutations allow survival, suggesting a characteristic of haplosufficiency for organismal development. However, in ALS, an aging-related neurodegenerative disease, heterozygous nonsense mutations are detrimental to motor neurons3. Notably, transcripts synthesized by the truncated NEK1 mutants have been detected in lymphoblastoid cell lines from patients with both ALS49 and SRPS48, suggesting a hypothesis that truncated NEK1 mutants may be neurotoxic. Our results show that truncated mutant NEK1s impaired rRNA biogenesis and rendered motor dysfunction in transgenic Drosophila models. Moreover, truncated mutant NEK1s caused motor dysfunction and neuronal deficit in knock-in mice models at the physiological expression level. Thus, our findings suggest that the nuclear accumulation of truncated mutant NEK1s interferes with cellular RNA homeostasis. Due to the lack of clinical samples, we were unable to determine the expression levels and subcellular localization of truncated NEK1 mutants in brain tissues from ALS autopsy cases.

Disrupted RNA metabolism is a common mechanism in ALS pathogenesis50, and many ALS-related genetic mutations are essential for maintaining cellular RNA homeostasis50. Beyond mRNA metabolism, rRNA processing also represents a vulnerable target in ALS. The nucleolus, the largest membraneless organelle in the cell, plays a crucial role in rRNA biogenesis14. The nucleolar misposition of pathogenic proteins and nucleolar dysfunction have been observed in ALS patients with C9ORF72 mutations51,52. For the NEK1, ALS-related truncated mutants shifted NEK1 localization from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, forming biomolecular condensates in both the nucleolus and nucleoplasm. These condensates sequester multiple nucleolar proteins, thereby impairing ribosomal RNA biogenesis. Notably, aberrant nucleolar enrichment of pathogenic proteins caused by frameshift mutations can also be detected in other diseases, such as KRT10 in ichthyosis with confetti53 and HMGB1 in brachyphalangy, polydactyly and tibial aplasia/hypoplasia syndrome19. Collectively, these findings suggest that disrupted rRNA biogenesis may be a common mechanism underlying the pathogenesis of diverse diseases.

Weak interactions mediated by IDRs are essential for phase separation11, but in our case, the basic domain in NEK1, the predicted IDR, is not sufficient to exclusively undergo phase separation in cultured cells. Instead, the CC domain, a classical structure required for protein-protein interactions, is indispensable for phase separation both in cells and in vitro. Notably, the CC domain of NEK1 is prone to form dimerization in the yeast two-hybrid system29. In our DSS-induced system, the CC domain tends to form oligomers and is essential for the oligomerization of truncated mutant NEK1s. These findings support the theory that stoichiometric molecular interactions mediated by multimerization represent another type of weak strength that drives phase separation54. Interestingly, coupling the basic domain to the CC domain strongly enhanced the phase separation capability of the CC domain. This suggests a model in which the basic domain functions not only as a signal recognition particle but also as an amplifier to fine-tune condensates formed by the CC domain. It is plausible that the basic domain recruits acidic biomolecules, such as proteins or RNA, to enlarge the condensates, resembling the role of the RNA-binding domain in G3BP1 condensates55. Thus, we propose that stoichiometric molecular interactions serve as scaffolds for the assembly of biomolecular condensates, whereas IDRs, such as the basic domain, fine-tune the threshold for phase separation via promiscuous interactions with surrounding molecules.

Consistent with our observation, most recently, Georgiadou et al. also reported that truncated NEK1-R812X mutant is able to form nuclear puncta in both cultured cell lines and motor neurons from a transgenic mouse model56. Extending these observations, we systematically characterized the material properties and assembly mechanisms underlying nuclear condensates formed by truncated NEK1 mutants. Interestingly, they found that NEK1-R812X-induced nuclear condensates colocalize with PML bodies, where SUMO family members, including SUMO1, SUMO2, and SUMO3, are enriched56. Ablation of Pml triggers motor dysfunction in truncated NEK1 transgenic mouse model, a phenotype that is also observed in our transgenic fly and knock-in mouse models. Together, these two independent studies provide convergent evidence that truncated NEK1 mutants exert neurotoxic effects through a gain-of-function mechanism.

In summary, we uncover that truncated forms of NEK1 derived from ALS-related NEK1 nonsense mutations disrupt ribosomal RNA biogenesis. Our findings broaden the understanding of NEK1-related ALS pathogenesis and suggest that RNA metabolism dysregulation is a common pathophysiological pathway in ALS.

Methods

Cell culture

293 T cell line (GNHu17, National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures) were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco). SH-SY5Y cell line (SCSP-5014, National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures) were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco). The growth medium contained 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

The primary cortical neurons were isolated from the cerebral cortex of 16-17 days embryos. Cortical tissues were dissected and digested with 0.25% trypsin (Gibco) for 15 min at 37 °C, and then neutralized with suitable DMEM. DNase I was used to isolate neurons from adhesive tissues. Tissues were gently triturated using a fire-polished pipette to obtain a single-cell suspension. Neurons were subsequently plated on poly-D-lysine-coated glass coverslips and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The genotype of each embryo was determined by PCR and electrophoresis analyses.

Cell transfection and treatment

All transfections were performed with Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, 11668019) or Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000015) reagents following the manufacturer’s instructions. The medium was changed after 24 h of transfection, and all experiments were initiated at 24–48 h post-transfection.

To verify the liquid properties of cellular condensates formed by truncated mutant NEK1s, cell medium was replaced with 3% 1,6-hexanediol (1,6-HD) (Sangon Biotechnology, A601513) for 10 min. Then the cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by immunofluorescence and statistical analysis.

Generation of stable cell lines

To generate doxycycline-inducible GFP-WT, GFP-550 and GFP-812 expression cell lines, 293 T cells with 50% confluence were transfected with pSLIK-GFP-WT, pSLIK-GFP-550 and pSLIK-GFP-812 plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, 11668019) following the manufacturer’s instructions. After 36 h transfection, cells were kept under puromycin (Beyotime, ST-551) selection for 2 ~ 3 days until non-transfected control cells were died. The cells were recovered to reach proper confluence, followed by doxycycline (Sigma, D9891) treatment to induce appropriate expression of GFP-550 and GFP-812 and then perform Western blot and immunofluorescence analysis.

To generate a mCherry-FBL stably expressed 293 T cell line, lentiviral particles were first produced and then used to infect 293 T cells, cells were kept under puromycin (Beyotime, ST-551) selection for 2 ~ 3 days until non-transfected control cells were died.

Plasmids construction

The full-length NEK1 was amplified and cloned into the pcDNA3.1-3×FLAG vector by YouBio Inc. To generate pmEGFP-C1-NEK1-WT and pmCherry-C1-NEK1-WT, full-length NEK1 was amplified from pcDNA3.1-NEK1-WT-3×FLAG, and subcloned into the pmEGFP-C1 and PA-mCherry-C1 vector through Hind III/BamH I sites. Clones, including GFP- and mCherry-tagged NEK1-R261H, NEK1-R550X, NEK1-R812X, NEK1-R1036X, NEK1-R812X-ΔKinase, NEK1-R812X-ΔBasic, NEK1-R812X-ΔCC, NEK1-Basic, NEK1-Kinase, NEK1-CC, and NEK1-BC, were amplified or cleaved from pmEGFP-C1-NEK1-WT and cloned by site-directed mutagenesis kit (Takara, R401) or one-step clone kit (Vazyme, C112-02). To generate HA-tagged NEK1-R550X, R812X, CC, and BC clones, the R550X, R812X, CC, BC sequence were amplified from pmEGFP-C1-NEK1-WT by PCR and then inserted into the pKH3-3×HA vector through Hind III/Xba I sites. The plasmids NEK1-R550X-3×HA and NEK1-R812X-3×HA were used as templates to generate plasmids NEK1-R550X-ΔCC-3×HA and NEK1-R812X-ΔCC-3×HA.

To generate chimeric plasmids, fragments, including N-terminal oligomerization domain of NPM1 (NPM1-oligo), CC domain from NEK2, CC domain (SynGAP-CC-WT) and its mutation CC-L-D&K-D (SynGAP-CC-Mut), were amplified from pmEGFP-C1-NPM1, pmEGFP-C1-NEK2, mSynGAP-1147-1310-WT, and mSynGAP-1147-1310-Mut, respectively, by PCR and then subcloned into pmEGFP-C1-NEK1-Basic plasmid by one-step clone kit (Vazyme, C112-02).

To generate plasmids for membraneless organelles, RPA194, FBL, NPM1, DDX21, Ku70, Ku80, and DDX3X were amplified from template plasmids or 293 T cDNAs, and then inserted into PA-mCherry1-C1 or pmEGFP-C1 vector via one-step clone kit (Vazyme, C112-02).

To generate pET15b-NEK1-CC, NEK1-CC was amplified from pmEGFP-C1-NEK1-WT and cloned into pET15b vector through Nde I and BamH I sites. To generate pET21a-GFP-FBL, GFP-FBL was amplified from the template pET28a-GFP-FBL and cloned into pET21a vector through BamH I and Xho I sites.

To generate pHR-NEK1-Kinase-mCh-Cry2WT, pHR-NEK1-Basic-mCh-Cry2WT, and pHR-NEK1-CC-mCh-Cry2WT, structural domains containing kinase, basic, and CC were first amplified, assembled with mCh-Cry2WT fragments, and inserted into the pcDNA3.1+ vector. Then the fragments, including Basic-mCh-Cry2WT, Kinase-mCh-Cry2WT, and CC-mCh-Cry2WT in pcDNA3.1+ vector, were cleaved and inserted into the pHR-mCh-Cry2WT vector via BamH I and Not I sites.

To generate the pSLIK-GFP-WT, pSLIK-GFP-550 and pSLIK-GFP-812 clones, entry clone pEN-TRE containing GFP-tagged NEK1-WT, NEK1-550 and NEK1-812 sequences were transferred to the destination clone pSLIK via Gateway recombination according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen). To generate the pCDH-mCherry-FBL clones, mCherry-FBL was amplified from pmCherry-C1-FBL and then subcloned into the pCDH-EF1-FHC vector through Xba I and BamH I sites.

To generate plasmids for construction of transgenic Drosophila, GFP-tagged NEK1-WT, GFP-tagged NEK1-550 and NEK1-812 sequences were amplified and cloned into the pUAST-attB vector via one-step clone kit (Vazyme, C112-02).

All constructs used in this study were ligated by one-step clone method following the manufacturer’s instruction (Vazyme, C112-02) or restriction and insertion cloning. Primer sequences for plasmid construction used in this paper was listed in Supplementary Data 3.

Protein expression and purification

The N-terminal His-tagged CC domain in pET15b vector was expressed in E. Coli BL21 (DE3) strain (TIANGEN, CB105). A single colony was inoculated in lysogeny broth (LB) media with constant rotation overnight at 200 rpm, 37 °C, followed by dilution with LB media in a ratio of 1:100. After several hours of incubation at 37 °C until an OD (600 nm) of 0.6 ~ 0.8, isopropyl-β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) (MCE, HY-15921) was added to the media at the concentration of 0.4 mM to induce the expression of CC protein. Then the media was incubated at 160 rpm for 18 h, 16 °C, followed by centrifugation at 2348 x g for 5 min to collect the BL21 bacteria. The pellets were lysed in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 5% Glycerol) using sonication on ice, followed by centrifugation at 18192 x g for 15 min. The supernatant was loaded on to Ni-NTA column (Beyotime, P2233), and then the beads were washed with wash buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 5% Glycerol, 40 mM Imidazole), followed by elution with elution buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 5% Glycerol, 300 mM Imidazole). After equilibrated with balance buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl), the eluted protein was further purified by size exclusion chromatography on a gel filtration column (Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL, Cytiva). The concentration of purified protein was determined using BCA kit (Takara, T9300A). Then the purified proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE, stained with Coomassie blue reagent, and stored in small aliquots at -80 °C.

The C-terminal His-tagged GFP-FBL in pET21a vector was expressed in E. Coli BL21 (DE3) strain (TIANGEN, CB105). A single colony was inoculated in LB media with constant rotation overnight at 225 rpm, 37 °C, followed by dilution with LB media in a ratio of 1:100. After several hours of incubation at 37 °C until an OD (600 nm) of 0.6 ~ 0.8, IPTG was added to the media at the concentration of 0.6 mM to induce the expression of GFP-FBL protein. Then the media was incubated at 180 rpm for 20 h, 16 °C, followed by centrifugation at 2348 x g for 5 min to collect the BL21 bacteria. The pellets were lysed in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 1 M NaCl, 10% Glycerol, 0.5 mM PMSF, 20 mM Imidazole) using sonication on ice, followed by centrifugation at 18192 x g for 15 min. The supernatant was loaded on to Ni-NTA column (Beyotime, P2233), and then the beads were washed with wash buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 1 M NaCl, 10% Glycerol, 0.5 mM PMSF, 30 mM Imidazole), followed by elution with elution buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 1 M NaCl, 10% Glycerol, 0.5 mM PMSF, 250 mM Imidazole). After equilibrated with balance buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 1 M NaCl, 10% Glycerol, 0.1 mM PMSF), the eluted protein was further purified by size exclusion chromatography on a gel filtration column (Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL, Cytiva). The concentration of purified protein was determined using BCA kit (Takara). Then the purified proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE, stained with Coomassie blue reagent, and stored in small aliquots at -80 °C.

Protein prediction

Intrinsic disorder regions of basic and CC domain were predicted by PONDR and IUPred3 (https://iupred3.elte.hu/), and the cut-off value is set as default. The structure of NEK1-CC-Random was predicted by AlphaFold35.

The positions of nuclear localization signals within NEK1 were predicted by SeqNLS (http://mleg.cse.sc.edu/seqNLS), and the cut-off value is set as 0.86. We found four NLS sites, two located in the basic domain (aa 291-299, aa 355-369), two located in the CC domain (aa 540-548, aa 504-515).

Phase separation assay

To induce phase separation of CC domain, the purified CC protein was diluted to indicated concentrations in LLPS buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT). The samples were dripped on to 96-well glass plates and imaged 10 min after droplets assembly using A1R HD25 confocal microscope (Nikon).

To investigate the material properties of droplets formed by GFP-FBL, the purified GFP-FBL protein was diluted to indicated concentrations at different salt levels. For co-LLPS assay, the mixtures containing CC and GFP-FBL under indicated concentrations were dripped on to 96-well glass plates and imaged using A1R HD25 confocal microscope (Nikon).

To investigate the impact of CC protein on rRNA sorting ability of FBL (Figs. 6e), 5’ ETS fragments RNA were in vitro transcribed (Promega, P1300) and UTP were labeled with Cy3 (Beyotime, D7337), followed by Urea-PAGE separation and purification (TIANGEN, DP412). A final concentration of 10 ng/μL rRNA was mixed with droplets formed by GFP-FBL alone or mixtures containing CC and GFP-FBL. The samples were dripped on to 96-well glass plates and imaged 10 min after droplets assembly using A1R HD25 confocal microscope (Nikon).

Turbidity assay

The purified CC protein was diluted to indicated concentrations in LLPS buffer (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT). Samples were incubated at room temperature for 10 min and then pipetted to 384 well plates. The UV absorbances of samples were measured using a microplate reader at 600 nm wavelength (TECAN Infinite@ 200 PRO).

Immunoprecipitation

293 T cells were transfected with indicated plasmids for 48 h, followed by cell lysis in cell lysis buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP40, and 0.5% sodium deoxycholate) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 04693132001). After centrifugation at 15294 x g for 20 min, 4 °C, the supernatant was incubated with magnetic Dynabeads protein G (Invitrogen, 10004D) charged with anti-GFP antibody (lab generated). After overnight end-to-end rotation at 4°C, the beads were washed six times with cell lysis buffer to discard unbound proteins. Then the beads were resuspended in loading buffer and boiled at 95 °C for 10 min to elute bound proteins. The eluted proteins were subjected to Western blot analysis as described above.

Subcellular fractionation

Cytosolic and nuclear fractions were isolated using nuclear and cytoplasmic protein extraction kit (Beyotime, P0027). In brief, 293 T cell pellets were deposited via centrifugation, and then lysed with cytosolic extraction buffer supplemented with 1 mM PMSF. After incubation on ice for 30 min, then the supernatants denoting cytosolic fractions were harvested via centrifugation at 15294 x g for 15 min. The cell pellets were washed two times with cytosolic extraction buffer, and then resuspended with nuclear extraction buffer supplemented with 1 mM PMSF, followed by incubation for 30 min on ice. After centrifugation at 15294 x g for 10 min, the supernatants denoting nuclear fractions were harvested. The cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions were subjected to Western blot analysis. Histone H3 were used as nuclear marker, and GAPDH were used as cytosolic marker.

Extraction of soluble and insoluble protein fractions

293 T cells were transfected with indicated plasmids for 48 h, followed by centrifugation in 845 x g for 5 min to harvest cell pellets. The cell pellets were lysed in RIPA buffer (10 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 1% Triton X-100, and 0.1% SDS) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 04693132001) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 04906837001). The cell lysates were sonicated and centrifuged at 15294 x g for 15 min, then the supernatant denoting soluble fraction was harvested. The pellets denoting insoluble fraction was washed two times with RIPA buffer and dissolved in urea buffer (9 M urea, 50 mM Tris pH 8.0). The soluble and insoluble fractions were subjected to Western blot analysis.

Protein crosslinking assay

The cells were transfected with indicated plasmids for 36 h, then the cell pellets were harvested using centrifugation at 845 x g for 5 min. After washing the cell pellets with ice-cold PBS for three times, PBS solution supplemented with freshly prepared 1 mM disuccinimidyl suberate (DSS, Sangon Biotech, C100015) solution was added to resuspend the cell pellets. Following incubation at room temperature for 30 min, the reaction was quenched by adding quenching buffer to a final concentration of 20 mM Tris for 15 min at room temperature. The samples were subjected to Western blot analysis.

Silver staining and mass spectrometry analysis

The cells were transfected with plasmids including GFP, GFP-C1-550, GFP-C1-812, and GFP-C1-1036, respectively, for 36 h, followed by immunoprecipitation assay to elute bound proteins. Three biological replicates per condition were performed. The eluted samples were loaded on to SDS-PAGE, followed by silver staining assay according to manufacturer’s guidance (Beyotime, P0017S). The bands were excised, reduced, and cleaved by Trypsin solution at 37 °C overnight, and the resulting peptides were dried under vacuum. Liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) on Q-Exactive HF X (Thermo Fisher Scientific) mass spectrometer was performed by BGI Genomics.

All rawdata files were processed and transferred to mgf files, followed by peptides search using Mascot software (Mascot 2.3.02, Matrix Science)57 based on Uniport database (Human, 20305 sequences, 18 Aug 2021). The peptide identifications were restricted to tryptic cleavage, a peptide mass tolerance of 20 ppm, and a fragment mass tolerance of 0.05 Da. Dynamic modifications were set for methionine oxidation and glutamine deamidation on the N terminus. A maximum of one missed cleavage and a static modification was set for carbamidomethyl cysteine. The Percolator v2.0758 was used to filter the peptide spectral match false discovery rate to 1%, followed by protein identification based on the parsimony principle.

Proteins enriched in truncated mutant NEK1s groups, but not in GFP group, were selected for downstream bioinformatical analyses. Overlapping proteins among groups GFP-550, GFP-812, and GFP-1036 were visualized using a Venn diagram, followed by gene ontology (GO) pathway analyses, including biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components, using the DAVID v6.8 database59.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

The total RNA of each sample was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Vazyme, R401-01). Five hundred nanograms of the total RNA were reverse-transcribed into cDNA with a HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme, R323-01). Real-time PCR was performed with ChamQ Blue Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q312-02). qRT-PCR was run on an ABI 7500 apparatus (Applied Biosystems). The expression of mRNA was measured via the ΔΔCt method relative to that of an endogenous control gene.

For qRT-PCR analysis in Drosophila, approximately 50 heads from 20-day-old flies were collected. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol Reagent, followed by reverse transcription and Real-time PCR as described above. The primer sequences were listed in Supplementary Data 3. For qRT-PCR analysis in mice, the motor cortex was dissected from wild-type and heterozygous male mice. Total RNA was then extracted using TRIzol Reagent, reverse transcribed into cDNA, and subjected to real-time PCR as previously described.

RNA sequencing analysis

The total RNA with RNA integrity number (RIN) value above 7 was used for library preparation. The library for next-generation sequencing was constructed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (NEBNext®UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina®). The libraries with different indices were multiplexed and loaded on an Illumina HiSeq instrument according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequencing was carried out using a 2 × 150 bp paired-end (PE) configuration. Image analyses and base calling were conducted by the HiSeq Control Software (HCS) + OLB + GAPipeline-1.6 (Illumina) on the HiSeq instrument.

FASTQ files were trimmed with Cutadapt (v1.9.1) to remove quality of bases lower than 20, then clean data were aligned to reference genome (Ensembl, Homo Sapiens, GRCh38.p13) via software Hisat2 (v2.2.1). The FASTA format was converted from known GFF annotation file and indexed properly. HTSeq (v0.6.1) was used to estimate gene and isoform expression levels from the pair-end clean data. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) attached as Supplementary Data 2 were selected using EdgeR (v3.28.1) based on the negative binomial distribution, and then volcano plot was used to depict the DEGs based on the threshold with adjusted p values < 0.05 and |log2 (fold change)| > 1 using package ggplot2 (v3.1.1) in R studio (2023.06.1 + 524).

Drosophila stocks

The w1118/BDSC Drosophila strain were used. To generate transgenic Drosophila expressing GFP-WT, GFP-550 and GFP-812, the pUAST-attB constructs containing GFP-WT, GFP-550 and GFP-812, respectively, were inserted into the attP site of chromosome 2 (loci 25C6), which was performed by Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology (SIBCB). Transgenic flies with UAS-GFP-WT, UAS-GFP-550 or UAS-GFP-812, were crossed with DA-GAL4 flies to drive the ubiquitous expression of GFP-WT, GFP-550 and GFP-812. The DA-GAL4 stock was a gift from the Pan lab of Shanghai Institute of Immunity and Infection. All fly stocks were raised in a 25 °C 12 h light/dark cycle incubator on a standard cornmeal medium.

Drosophila climbing assay

Flies of the specified genotype were collected and transferred to a new vial, where they were gently tapped to the bottom to ensure uniform distribution. The flies were then allowed to acclimatize for 15 min. For each experimental group, three vials were used, with 10 flies per vial. The number of flies that reached the 10 cm mark within 12 s was recorded. Each experimental condition was tested in triplicate to ensure reproducibility and statistical reliability.

Drosophila survival curve analysis

In each vial, approximately 30 three-day-old female flies were raised at 25 °C under a 12 h light/12-hour dark cycle. Fly mortality was recorded every 2 days, and the survival curve was generated based on the survival data. Statistical analysis for significance was conducted using the log-rank test.

Animals

The EGFP-hNEK1-812 transgenic mice were constructed by Beijing Biocytogen Co., Ltd. The C57BL/6 J mouse strain were used. Briefly, the target locus in the mouse Nek1 gene — from the start codon to the intron3 — was substituted with the EGFP-hNEK1-812-CDS-WPRE-pA sequence through the CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knock-in system. This approach simultaneously resulted in the knockout of the endogenous mNek1 gene and the knock-in of the EGFP-hNEK1-812 DNA fragment. The primers used for genotyping in this study were listed in Supplementary Data 3.

All animals were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in a 12 h light/dark cycle, at 23 °C and 50% relative humidity. All animal experiments were approved by the Soochow University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, and were conducted in compliance with all relevant ethical regulations for animal testing and research. The protocol number: 202404A0833. All animal experiments were performed using male mice.

Rotarod test

Balance and motor coordination were evaluated over four days using an accelerating rotarod (Sansbio). Following acclimatization at 4 rpm, the fall latency during acceleration (4 to 40 rpm over 5 min) was automatically recorded each day. The daily average latency was used for analysis.

Open-field test

The open field test was performed in a 40 × 40 × 40 cm (width × length × height) square box (Xinruan Informatlon Technology Co). Each mouse was placed in the corner of the box and the path of movement was recorded by an overhead video camera for 10 min. Several measures including the total distance traveled (cm) and the travel path were recorded to assess locomotor activity.

Footprint test

The gait parameters of mice were assessed using the footprint test. The hindpaws and forepaws of the mice were stained with non-toxic blue or red ink, respectively. Mice were then allowed to walk freely along a narrow, enclosed runway (60 cm long, 5 cm wide, with walls 20 cm high) lined with white paper on the floor. The first and last few steps were excluded from analysis. A minimum of three consecutive, clear steps were analyzed for each mouse. Back stride length (the distance between consecutive footprints of the same paw) was measured from the footprints.

Balance beam test

Motor coordination and balance were assessed using the balance beam apparatus (SANS Biological Technology). The apparatus includes a 50 × 5 cm (length × width) beam that is 50 cm above the floor, and an enclosed escape box (10 × 10 × 10 cm) at the opposite end. Each mouse was trained several times until running through the beam without pause. During the formal test, the process of each mouse passing through the beam was recorded by a video camera. The number of foot slips for each hindlimb was calculated for further analyses.

Mice survival curve analysis

Survival was monitored and compared between the two genotypes. A cohort of 44 (wild-type) or 42 (heterozygous) age-matched mice per genotype was followed daily from weaning until natural death. The date of death was recorded for each animal. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and statistical significance was assessed using the Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantification of percentage of cells with nucleoplasmic foci

To measure the percentage of cells with nucleoplasmic foci, 293 T cells were transfected with indicated plasmids for 24 ~ 36 h, followed by immunofluorescence assay to label the nucleoli and nucleus. GFP-positive cells (Ctotal) in random selected fields and the numbers of cell with nucleoplasmic condensates (Cper) were counted, respectively, and four replicates were performed in each group. The percentage of nucleoplasmic foci per group was calculated as Percentage = Cper / Ctotal × 100%.

Quantification of numbers of nucleoplasmic foci per cell

To measure the numbers of nucleoplasmic foci per cell, 293 T cells were transfected with indicated plasmids for 24 ~ 36 h, followed by immunofluorescence assay to label the nucleoli and nucleus. The numbers of nucleoplasmic condensates per cell (Cnum) were counted, and four replicates were performed in each group.

Quantification of nucleolar numbers and area

To determine the effect of truncated mutant NEK1s on nucleolar morphology, 293 T cells were transfected with indicated plasmids for 24 ~ 36 h, followed by immunofluorescence assay to label the nucleoli with NPM1 antibody. The numbers of nucleoli per cell were counted based on the NPM1 signal. The area of single nucleolus was measured in Image J (1.53q).

Co-localization analysis

To determine whether two proteins exhibit co-localization in cells, 293 T cells were transfected with GFP- or mCherry-tagged plasmids for 24 ~ 36 h, followed by immunofluorescence assay to fix the cell. A short line was drawn across the region of interest, and the intensity profiles along the short lines in both channels were plotted. Co-localization was indicated by the enrichment of intensity along both channels.

Statistical analysis

These data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 (GraphPad) and three biological replicates per condition were performed. Significant differences were calculated using a two-tailed unpaired t test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s multiple-comparisons test. The criterion of significance was set at P < 0.05. The values are shown as the mean ± SEM.
