Impact of chemotherapy dose reduction during concurrent chemoradiotherapy on survival outcomes in limited-stage small-cell lung cancer
Authors: Jiahui Shan, Jiayi Yu, Lujun Zhao, Dan Yang, Xiao Chang, Leilei Jiang, Liuhua Long, Yue Teng, Xin Dong, Rong Yu, Huiming Yu, Shaohua Ma, Anhui Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDose reduction during concurrent chemoradiotherapy (cCRT) is frequently required in patients with limited-stage small-cell lung cancer (LS-SCLC) because of treatment-related toxicities or poor tolerance. However, the impact of chemotherapy dose reduction on long-term outcomes in LS-SCLC remains unclear. We aimed to evaluate whether chemotherapy dose reduction compromises survival in patients with LS-SCLC receiving definitive cCRT.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed patients with LS-SCLC treated with definitive cCRT across multiple centers. Patients were categorized into a dose-maintained group and a dose-reduced group according to whether chemotherapy dose reduction occurred during cCRT. To minimize baseline imbalances, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on propensity scores was applied using clinical covariates including age, sex, T stage, N stage, ECOG performance status, smoking status, weight loss, gross tumor volume, and chemotherapy regimens. Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis and Cox proportional hazards models in both unweighted and IPTW-weighted cohorts.Exploratory analyses were additionally performed within the dose-reduced group according to the timing of first dose reduction and the magnitude of dose reduction.ResultsA total of 1,013 patients were included, comprising 627 patients in the dose-maintained group and 386 patients in the dose-reduced group. After IPTW adjustment, baseline characteristics were well balanced between groups.For OS, the median OS after IPTW adjustment was 57.8 months (95% CI 42.9–70.1) in the dose-maintained group and 42.8 months (95% CI 37.5–67.5) in the dose-reduced group. Chemotherapy dose reduction was not associated with a statistically significant difference in OS (HR = 1.17, 95% CI 0.95–1.44, P = 0.132).For PFS, the median PFS after IPTW adjustment was 25.0 months (95% CI 20.7–31.0) in the dose-maintained group and 20.5 months (95% CI 17.1–26.9) in the dose-reduced group. Introduction

Small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) accounts for approximately 13–15% of all lung cancers and is characterized by rapid tumor growth, early metastatic dissemination, and a high initial sensitivity to chemotherapy and radiotherapy [1, 2]. Approximately one-third of patients present with limited-stage disease (LS-SCLC), for whom curative treatment remains feasible [3, 4]. Concurrent chemoradiotherapy (cCRT) combining platinum-based chemotherapy and thoracic radiotherapy has long been established as the standard treatment for LS-SCLC and has demonstrated superior survival compared with sequential treatment strategies [5, 6]. In a landmark randomized trial, Turrisi et al. demonstrated that twice-daily thoracic radiotherapy administered concurrently with cisplatin and etoposide significantly improved survival compared with once-daily radiotherapy [5]. More recently, the CONVERT trial further confirmed the role of concurrent chemoradiotherapy as the backbone of treatment for LS-SCLC, showing comparable survival outcomes between once-daily and twice-daily radiotherapy regimens [7]. Despite these advances, the prognosis of LS-SCLC remains suboptimal, with median overall survival typically ranging from 24 to 30 months and 5-year survival rates rarely exceeding 25–30% [1, 3].

Although concurrent chemoradiotherapy improves survival outcomes, it is frequently associated with substantial treatment-related toxicities, including hematologic suppression, radiation-induced esophagitis, and fatigue [8]. These toxicities often lead to chemotherapy dose reductions, treatment delays, or early discontinuation in routine clinical practice, particularly among elderly patients or those with poor performance status [9]. In real-world settings, clinicians frequently face the challenge of balancing treatment intensity with patient tolerance. While maintaining adequate chemotherapy dose intensity is traditionally considered important for maximizing treatment efficacy, the clinical consequences of chemotherapy dose reduction during concurrent chemoradiotherapy remain unclear.

Maintaining chemotherapy dose intensity has been associated with improved outcomes in several malignancies, and reductions in dose intensity have sometimes been linked to inferior survival outcomes. However, most evidence supporting this concept originates from studies of other solid tumors, and its applicability to SCLC remains uncertain. Given the distinctive biological characteristics of SCLC, including its rapid proliferation and high chemosensitivity, moderate reductions in chemotherapy dose intensity may not necessarily compromise treatment efficacy. Nevertheless, data evaluating the impact of chemotherapy dose reduction during concurrent chemoradiotherapy in LS-SCLC remain limited.

Furthermore, most evidence supporting current treatment strategies for LS-SCLC has been derived from clinical trials conducted under highly controlled conditions with strict eligibility criteria, which may not fully reflect real-world clinical practice [3]. Although the treatment landscape of LS-SCLC is evolving, chemotherapy remains a key component of curative-intent cCRT, and the impact of chemotherapy dose intensity during the concurrent phase remains clinically relevant.

In routine clinical practice, chemotherapy dose reduction during cCRT is common in patients with LS-SCLC, particularly among those with limited physiological reserve or treatment-related toxicities. However, whether such dose modification compromises long-term outcomes remains insufficiently defined. Therefore, we conducted a multicenter retrospective study of patients with LS-SCLC treated with definitive cCRT to evaluate the association between chemotherapy dose reduction during treatment and survival outcomes. In addition to the primary comparison between dose-maintained and dose-reduced patients, we further explored whether the timing and magnitude of dose reduction within the dose-reduced group were associated with differential survival outcomes.

Methods

Study design and patients

This multicenter retrospective study included patients diagnosed with limited-stage small-cell lung cancer (LS-SCLC) who received definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy between January 2015 and December 2025 at participating institutions. Eligible patients were required to have histologically confirmed SCLC and clinical stage consistent with limited-stage disease according to the Veterans Administration Lung Study Group (VALSG) staging system. All included patients received concurrent chemoradiotherapy (cCRT), defined as thoracic radiotherapy delivered concurrently with platinum-based chemotherapy. No patients treated with sequential chemoradiotherapy were included in this study. Patients with extensive-stage disease, those who did not receive concurrent chemoradiotherapy, or those with incomplete clinical or follow-up data were excluded. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review boards of the participating centers. Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Treatment and data collection

All patients received concurrent chemoradiotherapy consisting of thoracic radiotherapy combined with platinum-based chemotherapy regimens, most commonly cisplatin or carboplatin plus etoposide, according to institutional treatment protocols.Among the 1,013 included patients, 173 (17.1%) received durvalumab after cCRT during the study period.

Chemotherapy dose reduction was defined as any decrease in the dose of at least one cytotoxic agent during concurrent chemoradiotherapy relative to the patient-specific dose prescribed at treatment initiation. Patients were categorized into a dose-maintained group if all planned concurrent chemotherapy cycles were delivered without dose reduction, and into a dose-reduced group if a reduction in the dose of at least one chemotherapy agent occurred in any cycle during cCRT.

To further account for treatment timing during the concurrent phase, treatment delay was defined as any prolongation of a concurrent chemotherapy cycle by more than 7 days relative to the planned schedule. For an additional simplified treatment-intensity analysis, patients were categorized into two composite groups: (1) no dose reduction and no delay during cCRT (maintained-intensity group) and (2) any dose reduction and/or delay during cCRT (reduced-intensity group).

Baseline clinical data were extracted from medical records, including age, sex, smoking history, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, T stage, N stage, presence of weight loss, gross tumor volume (GTV), and chemotherapy regimen. These variables were considered potential confounding factors and were incorporated into subsequent propensity score analyses to account for baseline differences between the treatment groups. Patients were followed through outpatient visits or telephone contact, and survival status was confirmed through medical records or direct patient contact.

Among patients in the dose-reduced group, additional exploratory classifications were performed according to the timing of first dose reduction and the magnitude of dose reduction. The timing of first dose reduction was categorized as cycles 1–2 versus cycle ≥ 3. The magnitude of dose reduction was defined according to the percentage decrease at the first dose-reduction event relative to the initially prescribed dose at treatment initiation, and was categorized as < 20% versus ≥ 20%. It was not defined according to the cumulative administered chemotherapy dose relative to the total planned dose across all cycles.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint of this study was overall survival (OS), defined as the time from the initiation of treatment to death from any cause. Patients who were alive at the last follow-up were censored. The secondary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the time from treatment initiation to the first documented disease progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first.

Statistical analysis

To reduce potential selection bias and baseline imbalances between the dose-maintained and dose-reduced groups, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on propensity scores was applied. Propensity scores were estimated using generalized boosted models implemented through the twang package in R. Covariates included in the propensity score model were age, sex, T stage, N stage, ECOG performance status, smoking status, weight loss, gross tumor volume, and chemotherapy regimen. Covariate balance between groups before and after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with an SMD less than 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance.

Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Differences between groups were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Both unweighted and IPTW-weighted analyses were performed, and robust variance estimation was applied in weighted Cox models to account for the use of IPTW. As a supplementary analysis, G-CSF use was additionally included as a covariate in Cox regression models to assess whether the association between chemotherapy dose reduction and survival outcomes was materially altered. Although the primary exposure of interest remained chemotherapy dose reduction during cCRT, we additionally performed a simplified treatment-intensity analysis incorporating both dose reduction and treatment delay in response to the reviewer’s concern regarding RDI. OS and PFS were compared between the maintained-intensity and reduced-intensity groups using the same Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression framework as in the primary analysis. Median survival times with 95% confidence intervals were reported, and survival rates at 3, 4, 5, and 6 years were also estimated.

Exploratory survival analyses were additionally conducted within the dose-reduced group according to the timing of first dose reduction and the magnitude of dose reduction. Kaplan–Meier curves and univariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess associations with OS and PFS in these exploratory analyses. Because these variables were defined during treatment rather than at baseline, the corresponding results were considered hypothesis-generating and interpreted cautiously. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2), and a two-sided P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Overall survival

The median follow-up duration was 48.9 months (interquartile range, 40.1–57.7). During the follow-up period, deaths were observed in both treatment groups. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that the median overall survival (OS) in the unweighted cohort was 57.3 months (95% CI 42.9–70.0) in the dose-maintained group and 43.1 months (95% CI 40.0–62.1) in the dose-reduced group. After IPTW adjustment, the median OS was 57.8 months (95% CI 42.9–70.1) in the dose-maintained group and 42.8 months (95% CI 37.5–67.5) in the dose-reduced group. Cox proportional hazards analysis showed no statistically significant difference in OS between the two groups after IPTW adjustment (HR = 1.17, 95% CI 0.95–1.44, P = 0.132). Progression-free survival

Progression-free survival (PFS) was also analyzed. In the unweighted cohort, the median PFS was 24.7 months (95% CI 20.7–30.4) in the dose-maintained group and 20.5 months (95% CI 17.8–26.7) in the dose-reduced group. After IPTW adjustment, the median PFS was 25.0 months (95% CI 20.7–31.0) in the dose-maintained group and 20.5 months (95% CI 17.1–26.9) in the dose-reduced group. Compared with the dose-maintained group, chemotherapy dose reduction was associated with a modest but statistically significant decrease in PFS (HR = 1.19, 95% CI 1.00–1.40, P = 0.047). Additional simplified treatment-intensity analysis

To further account for treatment timing during cCRT, we performed a simplified treatment-intensity analysis comparing patients with no dose reduction and no delay during cCRT (maintained-intensity group) with those with any dose reduction and/or delay during cCRT (reduced-intensity group). The reduced-intensity group showed significantly worse OS and PFS in both the unweighted and IPTW-weighted analyses (OS unweighted: HR = 1.25, 95% CI 1.03–1.51, P = 0.023; OS IPTW-weighted: HR = 1.27, 95% CI 1.05–1.54, P = 0.014; PFS unweighted: HR = 1.26, 95% CI 1.07–1.48, P = 0.006; PFS IPTW-weighted: HR = 1.23, 95% CI 1.04–1.45, P = 0.017) (Supplementary Figure R2).

Subgroup analyses

Predefined subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the consistency of the association between chemotherapy dose reduction and overall survival across clinically relevant variables, including sex, age, T stage, N stage, ECOG performance status, smoking history, weight loss, gross tumor volume (GTV), and chemotherapy regimens (Fig. 4). A trend toward worse survival with dose reduction was observed in several subgroups, including patients aged ≥ 70 years (HR = 1.46, 95% CI 0.97–2.20), those with baseline weight loss (HR = 1.60, 95% CI 0.94–2.70), and patients receiving EP chemotherapy (HR = 1.26, 95% CI 1.00–1.59), although these associations did not reach statistical significance for interaction. Notably, patients with T4 disease showed a higher hazard associated with dose reduction (HR = 1.41, 95% CI 1.01–1.97), although no significant interaction was observed.

Exploratory analyses within the dose-reduced group

Among the 386 patients in the dose-reduced group, 183 underwent first dose reduction during cycles 1–2 and 203 at cycle ≥ 3; 248 had a reduction magnitude < 20% and 138 had a reduction magnitude ≥ 20%. Exploratory analyses were then performed according to the timing and magnitude of dose reduction. Compared with patients whose first dose reduction occurred during cycles 1–2, those whose first dose reduction occurred at cycle ≥ 3 had significantly better PFS (HR = 0.71, 95% CI 0.55–0.92, P = 0.011) and OS (HR = 0.68, 95% CI 0.51–0.92, P = 0.013) (Fig. 5). In contrast, when patients were categorized according to the magnitude of dose reduction (< 20% versus ≥ 20%), no statistically significant difference was observed in PFS (HR = 1.05, 95% CI 0.80–1.37, P = 0.751) or OS (HR = 1.19, 95% CI 0.87–1.62, P = 0.282) (Figure S1). Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective study including 1,013 patients with limited-stage small-cell lung cancer (LS-SCLC) treated with concurrent chemoradiotherapy, chemotherapy dose reduction was associated with inferior progression-free survival after IPTW adjustment, whereas overall survival was not significantly different between the dose-reduced and dose-maintained groups. These findings provide real-world evidence regarding the clinical impact of chemotherapy dose modification during definitive treatment of LS-SCLC.

Concurrent chemoradiotherapy remains the standard treatment for patients with limited-stage small-cell lung cancer. Previous meta-analyses have demonstrated that the addition of thoracic radiotherapy to chemotherapy significantly improves survival compared with chemotherapy alone, supporting the importance of combined-modality therapy in LS-SCLC [10]. Randomized clinical trials have further established platinum-etoposide–based concurrent chemoradiotherapy as the cornerstone of curative treatment in this disease [5, 11, 12]. The landmark study by Turrisi et al. showed that twice-daily thoracic radiotherapy improved survival compared with once-daily radiotherapy when delivered concurrently with cisplatin and etoposide, emphasizing the importance of treatment intensity and optimal scheduling in LS-SCLC management [5]. More recent randomized evidence has suggested that once-daily high-dose thoracic radiotherapy may achieve similar outcomes, although no clear survival superiority has been demonstrated [13].

Despite the well-established role of concurrent chemoradiotherapy, treatment-related toxicity frequently leads to chemotherapy dose reduction in routine clinical practice. Hematologic toxicity, including neutropenia and leukopenia, is particularly common during combined-modality therapy. Previous studies have suggested that chemotherapy dose intensity may influence survival outcomes in small-cell lung cancer. In a landmark randomized study, Arriagada et al. demonstrated that higher initial chemotherapy doses were associated with improved survival outcomes in patients with limited-stage disease [14]. Similarly, earlier clinical investigations evaluating intensified chemotherapy regimens suggested that systemic treatment intensity may play an important role in disease control in chemosensitive tumors such as SCLC [15].

In the present study, chemotherapy dose reduction was associated with shorter progression-free survival after IPTW adjustment. This finding may be explained by reduced systemic treatment intensity leading to inadequate eradication of micrometastatic disease, thereby increasing the risk of early recurrence. In LS-SCLC, occult metastatic dissemination is common even in patients with limited disease burden, which underscores the importance of effective systemic therapy as part of curative-intent treatment [3]. However, the observed difference in PFS did not translate into a statistically significant difference in overall survival. Several explanations may account for this finding. First, subsequent lines of therapy after disease progression may partially compensate for earlier relapse, thereby attenuating differences in OS. Second, dose reduction may occur more frequently in patients with poorer baseline health status, which may introduce residual confounding despite propensity score weighting. Third, OS is generally less sensitive than PFS for detecting early treatment effects, particularly in retrospective analyses.

Compared with the original dose-reduction-only grouping, the composite treatment-intensity indicator incorporating both dose reduction and delay showed a similarly directional and somewhat stronger association with both OS and PFS, suggesting that treatment timing provides additional prognostic information beyond dose reduction alone.

An additional exploratory finding of this study was that, within the dose-reduced group, the timing of dose reduction appeared to carry important prognostic information. Patients who underwent their first dose reduction during cycles 1–2 had significantly worse PFS and OS than those whose first dose reduction occurred later in treatment. By contrast, the magnitude of dose reduction (< 20% versus ≥ 20%) was not significantly associated with either endpoint. Although this association persisted after adjustment for measured baseline factors, it should still be interpreted cautiously because the timing of first dose reduction is a treatment-emergent variable and may reflect unmeasured frailty, early treatment intolerance, changes in clinical status, or time-dependent bias, rather than a direct effect of timing itself. Therefore, this finding should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Subgroup analyses demonstrated that the association between chemotherapy dose reduction and survival outcomes was generally consistent across most clinical subgroups. Although no statistically significant interaction was observed, a trend toward worse survival associated with dose reduction was observed in several subgroups, including older patients, those with baseline weight loss, and patients with T4 disease. These findings suggest that patients with more aggressive disease biology or poorer physiological reserve may be more sensitive to reductions in treatment intensity. However, given the exploratory nature of subgroup analyses, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Another important observation of this study is that chemotherapy dose reduction did not significantly reduce the incidence of treatment-related toxicities. Rates of hematologic and radiation-related adverse events were broadly comparable between the dose-maintained and dose-reduced groups. This finding suggests that dose reduction alone may not substantially mitigate treatment-related toxicity during concurrent chemoradiotherapy. Instead, optimized supportive care strategies, including proactive hematologic monitoring and growth-factor support when appropriate, may be required to improve treatment tolerability [16].

In our cohort, G-CSF use was more common in the dose-reduced group than in the dose-maintained group, and additional adjustment for G-CSF use did not materially alter the main results, suggesting that differences in G-CSF support were unlikely to account for the observed survival findings.

In addition to systemic therapy, several other treatment components are critical in the management of LS-SCLC. Prophylactic cranial irradiation has been shown to significantly reduce the risk of brain metastases and improve survival among patients achieving complete remission after initial therapy [17]. Furthermore, the timing of thoracic radiotherapy relative to chemotherapy has been demonstrated to influence treatment outcomes. Evidence suggests that earlier integration of thoracic radiotherapy within combined-modality therapy is associated with improved survival [18]. In addition, the interval between initiation of chemotherapy and completion of thoracic radiotherapy has been reported to be an important prognostic factor in LS-SCLC, highlighting the importance of treatment delivery and overall treatment intensity [19].

The treatment landscape of LS-SCLC is evolving. In particular, the phase III ADRIATIC trial demonstrated a significant overall survival benefit with durvalumab after concurrent chemoradiotherapy, establishing consolidation durvalumab as a new standard of care for LS-SCLC [20]. In our cohort, 173 of 1,013 patients (17.1%) received durvalumab after cCRT during the study period, which may have contributed to treatment-era heterogeneity and should be considered when interpreting survival outcomes.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective observational study, and residual confounding cannot be completely excluded despite the use of IPTW to balance baseline characteristics. Propensity score methods are widely used to address confounding in observational studies but cannot account for unmeasured variables [21]. Second, detailed information regarding the reasons for chemotherapy dose reduction was not available, which may have influenced treatment decisions and outcomes. In addition, the exploratory analyses according to timing and magnitude of dose reduction were based on treatment-emergent variables rather than baseline characteristics; therefore, these findings are subject to potential time-dependent bias and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Third, variations in treatment practice across participating institutions may have introduced additional heterogeneity. In addition, our supplementary treatment-intensity analysis represented a simplified approximation of RDI rather than a formal per-patient RDI calculation for each chemotherapy agent, and should therefore be interpreted accordingly. Another limitation is that radiotherapy relative dose intensity (RDI), including actual/planned dose and treatment interruptions, was not formally analyzed. In our cohort, only 23 of 1,013 patients (2.3%) did not complete the planned thoracic radiotherapy, indicating limited variability in radiotherapy delivery. Because of the very small number of patients with incomplete radiotherapy, the association of radiotherapy RDI with PFS, OS, and toxicity could not be reliably assessed. Finally, although standardized differences were used to assess covariate balance after weighting, which is recommended as a diagnostic tool in propensity score analyses [22], prospective studies are still needed to confirm these findings.

In conclusion, in this multicenter real-world cohort of patients with limited-stage small-cell lung cancer treated with concurrent chemoradiotherapy, chemotherapy dose reduction was associated with inferior progression-free survival but not with a statistically significant difference in overall survival after IPTW adjustment. These findings suggest that maintaining chemotherapy dose intensity during concurrent chemoradiotherapy may be important for optimal disease control when clinically feasible, while individualized treatment strategies remain necessary to balance treatment efficacy and tolerability. Among patients who required dose reduction, earlier dose modification was associated with poorer survival; however, this exploratory finding should be interpreted cautiously because it may reflect greater frailty or poorer treatment tolerance rather than a direct causal effect of dose-reduction timing.
