Sex- and Age-Stratified relative handgrip strength and risk of eight chronic diseases in Middle-Aged and older adults: evidence from a National aging cohort study in China
Authors: Xixi Nan, Shuotong Zhang, Jiyu Che, Junyao Yang, Zhongming Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIdentifying cost-effective biomarkers for chronic disease screening remains a global challenge. This study examined sex- and age-specific associations between relative handgrip strength (RHGS) and eight chronic diseases to inform targeted screening and resource allocation.MethodsLongitudinal data from 11,415 participants in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) aged ≥ 45 years were analyzed. Participants were stratified into sex-specific, body mass index-adjusted RHGS quartiles. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to examine associations between RHGS and risks of diabetes, dyslipidemia, low cognitive performance, depression, heart disease, hypertension, stroke, and arthritis, stratified by sex and age group.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 84 months, higher RHGS quartiles were linked to lower risks of diabetes, dyslipidemia, low cognitive performance, and stroke. In older males, the highest quartile was associated with a 53% lower risk of low cognitive performance and a nonlinear relationship with diabetes risk. In middle-aged males, the highest quartile meant a 64% lower risk of dyslipidemia. Among females, higher RHGS was associated with cardiovascular disease and reduced low cognitive risk in older females. Low baseline RHGS predicted higher risks, even if RHGS improved later.ConclusionsThis study supports incorporating RHGS assessment into routine midlife health screenings and adopting sex- and age-specific strategies to improve cost-effective disease prevention. Clinically, prioritize cognitive screening in older adults with low RHGS, focus on glucose and lipid disorders in middle-aged individuals with low RHGS, and emphasize cardiovascular risk assessment in middle-aged and older females with low RHGS.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03209-y.

Introduction​

The rapid aging of the global population has made chronic diseases a pressing public health concern for middle-aged and older adults [1]. Conventional methods for assessing chronic disease risk primarily rely on invasive biomarker assays and advanced imaging modalities [2]. Despite being highly accurate, these approaches are costly, time-consuming, and often impractical for large-scale implementation in community and primary healthcare settings. Thus, there is a critical need for simple, cost-effective, and scalable assessment tools that complement existing diagnostic frameworks while improving resource efficiency and enhancing population health management.

Relative handgrip strength (RHGS), calculated as absolute handgrip strength normalized to body mass index (BMI), has emerged as a promising non-invasive biomarker integrating musculoskeletal, metabolic, and neurological health domains [3, 4]. Lower RHGS is strongly associated with increased risks of all-cause mortality and multiple chronic diseases [5–13]. Although RHGS is increasingly recognized as a biomarker for chronic disease risk stratification, its potential to optimize screening strategies for population-level secondary prevention remains underexplored. Most existing studies are based on Western populations, limiting generalizability to Asian cohorts due to ethnic differences in body composition, lifestyle, and disease susceptibility [14, 15]. Moreover, prior research has primarily focused on single-disease outcomes, lacking a comprehensive assessment of RHGS in relation to multiple chronic conditions. Importantly, few longitudinal studies in Asian populations have systematically investigated how the associations between RHGS and multiple chronic diseases vary across sex and age subgroups. Given that middle-aged and older adults differ by sex and age in lifestyle habits, physical activity levels, and hormonal status, their risks of developing chronic diseases are heterogeneous [16–18]. Targeting the specific disease risks of sex- and age-stratified subpopulations can help optimize the cost-effectiveness of chronic disease screening strategies. Therefore, further research is necessary to advance the refinement and application of RHGS in chronic disease screening.

This study utilized large-scale population data to systematically investigate the association between RHGS and the incidence of eight high-burden chronic diseases: arthritis, low cognitive performance, depression, diabetes, dyslipidemia, heart disease, hypertension, and stroke. By examining potential moderating effects of sex and age, we aimed to validate RHGS as a health biomarker and refine its application in public health screening. Compared with traditional approaches, RHGS-guided screening demonstrates higher cost-effectiveness in identifying high risk subgroups. Our findings are expected to inform personalized intervention strategies and contribute to early prevention paradigms for chronic diseases in aging populations.

Methods

Study population

This study is a longitudinal follow-up analysis based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), designed to investigate the association between RHGS and the risk of multiple chronic diseases in middle-aged and older adults. CHARLS is a nationally representative, large-scale longitudinal survey of Chinese adults aged 45 years and older, providing comprehensive data on demographics, health status, lifestyle, socioeconomic factors, and biometric measurements. To date, the CHARLS database has completed five waves of follow-up surveys, with data collection occurring in 2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. The study methodology has been described in detail in previous publications [19].

For this study, we utilized follow-up data from 2011 to 2018. Considering data completeness and consistency, data from 2020 were not included. Initially, 17,708 participants were enrolled. A total of 6,293 individuals were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) age < 45 years old or missing age data (n = 777); (2) BMI or HGS of 0 or missing (n = 4,420); (3) BMI or HGS values outside the 1st-99th percentile range (n = 554) [20]; (4) Subjects who did not participate in any of the follow-up visits (n = 542). Ultimately, 11,415 participants were included in the final analysis and followed up until 2018 (Fig. 1). This study utilized publicly available data from the CHARLS database, with data access authorized by the database administrators. The CHARLS study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University (approval number: IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Data collection

CHARLS investigators collected data following predefined standardized protocols. Blood pressure (BP) was measured three times in a seated position after a 5-minute rest period, with the average value recorded. Body weight and height were measured while participants wore light clothing and no shoes. Peripheral venous blood samples were obtained by trained professionals after overnight fasting, stored at − 20 °C, and processed according to standard laboratory procedures. Biochemical analyses included fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), White Blood Cell (WBC), serum C-reactive protein (CRP) and serum cystatin C (Cys-C), and lipid profiles.

RHGS measurement

HGS was assessed by trained interviewers using a standardized dynamometer. Measurements were conducted with participants in a standing position, arms naturally hanging at their sides while holding the dynamometer at a standardized distance from the body. Each hand was tested twice, and the highest recorded value was used as the absolute HGS. RHGS was calculated as absolute HGS (kg) divided by BMI (kg/m2). To account for sex-related differences, RHGS quartiles were established separately for males and females. Participants were then classified into four RHGS groups based on sex-specific quartiles: Q4 (highest quartile), Q3, Q2, and Q1 (lowest quartile) [21].

Measurement and classification of metabolic equivalent of task (MET)

The physical activity (PA) of each participant was assessed using a structured questionnaire based on the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [21]. Across the four survey waves from 2011 to 2018, participants were asked whether they engaged in at least 10 min of vigorous physical activity (VPA), moderate physical activity (MPA), or low-intensity physical activity (LPA). If they responded affirmatively, they were further questioned about the frequency (days per week) and duration (minutes per day) of each PA level. Following established methodologies, the midpoint of each reported time range was used for data conversion. Weekly duration scores for each PA level were calculated as the product of weekly frequency and daily duration. The total physical activity volume (PAV) was expressed in metabolic equivalent of task (MET) units using the following formula: PAV = (8.0 × weekly VPA duration) + (4.0 × weekly MPA duration) + (3.3 × weekly LPA duration).

In accordance with IPAQ classification criteria, a total PAV score of < 600 MET-min/week was defined as insufficient physical activity. Therefore, participants were categorized into two groups: insufficiently active (0–599 MET-min/week) and sufficiently active (≥ 600 MET-min/week).

Definition of chronic diseases

This study focused on the following chronic diseases: low cognitive performance, depression, diabetes, dyslipidemia, arthritis, heart disease, hypertension, and stroke. The incidence of each disease during follow-up was recorded as the primary outcome variable. Follow-up time was defined as the period from baseline to the first occurrence of the target disease or the end of follow-up.

Chronic disease diagnoses were determined based on: (1) Self-reported physician diagnoses; (2) Objective physiological and biochemical measurements and (3) Standardized questionnaire assessments. Hypertension was defined as self-reported physician-diagnosed hypertension and/or BP ≥ 140/90 mmHg. Diabetes was defined as self-reported physician diagnosis and/or FBG ≥ 126 mg/dL and/or HbA1c ≥ 6.5% at baseline. Dyslipidemia was diagnosed based on any of the following criteria: (1) Self-reported physician diagnosis of dyslipidemia; (2) Total cholesterol ≥ 240 mg/dL (6.2 mmol/L); (3) Triglycerides ≥ 200 mg/dL (2.3 mmol/L); (4) Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) ≥ 160 mg/dL (4.1 mmol/L); and/or (5) High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) < 40 mg/dL (1.0 mmol/L). It is worth noting that, due to the lack of biochemical test results in 2013 and 2018, the diagnoses of DM and dyslipidemia in these years were based on self-reports from the participants. Low cognitive performance was defined as a cognitive score in the bottom 10% of the overall distribution based on cross-sectional data [22]. Depression was assessed using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression (CES-D) scale, with a score ≥ 10 indicating the presence of depressive symptoms and a score < 10 indicating the absence of depressive symptoms [23].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio (version 4.2.1). A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD), while non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Baseline characteristics across different HGS groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or nonparametric tests for continuous variables, and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was used to estimate survival probabilities, and differences in disease incidence over time were statistically evaluated using log-rank tests. Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between RHGS and the risk of incident chronic diseases. The restricted cubic spline (RCS) method was used to analyze whether there was a nonlinear relationship between RHGS and the risk of disease.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

A total of 11,415 participants were included in this study. Participants were stratified into sex-specific RHGS quartiles (Q1: lowest, Q2, Q3, Q4: highest) based on BMI-adjusted HGS values (distribution shown in Supplementary Fig. 1). Across all quartiles, males demonstrated consistently higher RHGS values than females, highlighting significant sex differences in HGS values (Supplementary Table 1).

The baseline characteristics showed a comparable distribution of males and females across the four RHGS quartiles. Increasing RHGS levels were associated with a progressive decline in age. Higher RHGS quartiles were also associated with a more favorable glycolipid and cardiometabolic profile, reflected by gradual decreases in BMI, systolic BP (SBP) and diastolic BP (DBP), FBG, TG, HbA1c, WBC, CRP and Cys-C levels (all P < 0.05 for trend). Conversely, HDL-C levels, physical activity (MET%), alcohol consumption (drink%), smoking prevalence (smoke%), and sleep duration increased progressively with higher RHGS quartiles (all P < 0.05 for trend) (Table 1). Normality tests for continuous variables, assessed using Q-Q plots, are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Stratified survival curves for disease across RHGS quartiles

Over a median follow-up of 84 months, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis combined with log-rank testing demonstrated significant differences in disease progression across RHGS quartiles for all eight chronic conditions examined (P < 0.001 for all log-rank tests). A consistent stratification pattern and clear dose-response relationship were evident, with higher RHGS levels associated with progressively improved survival trajectories from Q1 to Q4 (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Overall association between RHGS and chronic disease risk

Cox regression analyses revealed a nearly consistent inverse association between RHGS quartiles and chronic disease risk in both unadjusted models and models adjusted for demographic factors (age, sex, region, household registration, education level, and marital status), all with P < 0.05. Further adjustment for lifestyle and clinical confounders, including smoking status, alcohol consumption, sleep duration, physical activity, history of falls, WBC, CRP and Cys-C, further supported the protective role of higher RHGS. Compared with the lowest quartile (Q1), participants in the highest RHGS quartile (Q4) had a 37% lower risk of diabetes (P = 0.006); a 47% lower risk of dyslipidemia (P < 0.001); a 43% lower risk of low cognitive performance (P < 0.001) and a 50% lower risk of stroke (P = 0.007). These results indicate a dose-response relationship, where higher RHGS levels are consistently associated with lower risks of chronic diseases (Fig. 2).

Age- and Sex-Stratified risk profiles

To explore potential effect modification by sex and age, participants were further stratified into four demographic subgroups: older males (≥ 60 years), older females, middle-aged males (< 60 years), and middle-aged females. As shown in Fig. 3, among older males, those in the highest RHGS quartile (Q4) had a 53% lower risk of low cognitive performance compared to Q1 (HR = 0.47, P = 0.039). A significant protective effect against DM was also observed in the third quartile (Q3) compared to Q1 (64% risk reduction, P = 0.017). In middle-aged males, higher RHGS quartiles were associated with substantially lower risks of dyslipidemia (HR = 0.29 for Q2, HR = 0.36 for Q4, P = 0.021 for both). Although no significant association was found for diabetes incidence in this group, a borderline trend was noted. Among older females, significant risk reductions for low cognitive performance were observed in both the Q2 and Q4 groups, with reductions of 36% (HR = 0.64, P = 0.034) for Q2 and 43% (HR = 0.57, P = 0.043) for Q4, compared to Q1. Additionally, those in Q3 had a 49% lower risk of hypertension (HR = 0.51, P = 0.019) compared to Q1. For middle-aged females, those in the Q3 group had a 55% lower risk of developing heart disease (P = 0.041). A significant dose-response association for DM, dyslipidemia and stroke was observed in this group, with risk decreasing progressively from Q1 to Q4 (P for trend < 0.05) (Fig. 3).

Nonlinear assessment of risk heterogeneity

To further examine the heterogeneous risk patterns observed in the quartile-based analyses, where significant associations appeared in Q2 and Q4 but not Q3, or only in Q3, RCS analyses were conducted to assess potential nonlinear relationships between RHGS and disease outcomes across demographic subgroups. The RCS analyses revealed a significant nonlinear association for DM risk in older males (P-nonlinear = 0.035). In contrast, no significant nonlinear associations were found for dyslipidemia risk in middle-aged males (P-nonlinear = 0.871); low cognitive performance (P-nonlinear = 0.189) and hypertension risk (P-nonlinear = 0.558) in older females; or low cognitive performance (P-nonlinear = 0.552) and heart disease risk (P-nonlinear = 0.229) in middle-aged females (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Association between RHGS change trajectories and chronic disease incidence

To further examine the relationship between RHGS and the risk of developing chronic diseases, participants were categorized based on changes in RHGS from 2011 to 2015. Those whose RHGS changed by less than 15% were classified as the stable group (G1), those with an increase greater than 15% as the increased group (G2), and those with a decrease greater than 15% as the decreased group (G3). The results showed that participants in the stable group had a lower risk of developing various chronic diseases. In contrast, individuals in the increased group had a 2.24-fold higher risk of developing DM compared to the stable group (P = 0.001), and showed a trend toward increased risks of low cognitive performance (P = 0.076) and hypertension (P = 0.071). For stroke, the highest risk was observed in the decreased group, which had a 1.8-fold higher risk than the stable group (P = 0.002), followed by the increased group, which had a 1.68-fold higher risk (P = 0.041) (Fig. 4).

To further explore the underlying patterns, we compared baseline RHGS values among the three groups. At baseline in 2011, the increased group had the lowest mean RHGS (2.00 ± 0.79), while the decreased group had the highest (2.91 ± 0.92). By 2015, the mean RHGS in the increased group had risen to 2.73 ± 0.87, whereas it had declined to 2.00 ± 0.81 in the decreased group. In contrast, the stable group maintained a relatively consistent RHGS, ranging from 2.70 to 2.77 over the four-year period (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this study, age- and sex-stratified analyses of a nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older adults revealed heterogeneity in the association between RHGS and the risk of various chronic diseases across different subgroups. These findings highlight the clinical value of stratified risk assessment and support the cost-effectiveness of incorporating RHGS measurements into routine chronic disease screening for middle-aged and older adults. They also provide important evidence to inform targeted screening and prevention strategies.

In the overall population, RHGS showed a linear dose-response relationship with almost all conditions in unadjusted models, demonstrating a significant inverse association between higher RHGS levels and disease risk. However, after adjusting for confounding factors, significant associations remained only for DM, dyslipidemia, low cognitive performance, and stroke, with higher RHGS linked to reduced risk for these outcomes. Age- and sex-stratified analyses further revealed significant differences in risk patterns across different subgroups. Specifically, higher RHGS was associated with a significantly lower risk of DM in older males and middle-aged females, while a borderline significant association was observed in middle-aged males. No significant association was found between RHGS and DM risk in older females. This result aligns with previous studies that also found no significant association between RHGS and DM in older or postmenopausal females [24, 25]. This may be attributed to the marked decline in estrogen levels after menopause, which can affect energy expenditure, insulin secretion, and insulin sensitivity [26]. In contrast, studies by Zheng et al. and Zhao et al. found a significant association between relative grip strength and DM only in males, possibly because both studies defined relative grip strength as grip strength adjusted for body weight [27, 28]. Furthermore, previous research did not identify a nonlinear relationship between RHGS and DM risk in older males, which may be due to the use of sex-stratified analyses without accounting for age effects in the RCS models [28]. Meanwhile, such a non-linear relationship also needs to be verified in a larger sample.

For dyslipidemia, a negative association with RHGS was observed only among middle-aged individuals (both males and females), with a dose-dependent relationship particularly evident in middle-aged females. This finding is partially consistent with previous research, which suggests that RHGS is especially relevant to dyslipidemia risk in females [5, 11]. However, possibly due to differences in ethnicity and subgroup classification, our study did not find a significant association between RHGS and dyslipidemia in older adults. We speculate that this may be related to age-specific changes in lipid metabolism (such as decreased cholesterol synthesis rates), which may weaken the association between lipid profiles and health outcomes in older populations [29]. Additionally, the widespread use of statins among older adults may directly affect lipid levels, potentially masking any association between RHGS and dyslipidemia risk in this group [30]. These results suggest that special attention should be given to monitoring glucose and lipid disorders in middle-aged individuals and older males with low RHGS. Moreover, fasting blood glucose should also be monitored in older males with the highest grip strength, as our findings indicate there may be a nonlinear relationship.

Regarding low cognitive performance, although a dose-dependent protective effect of RHGS was not evident in the overall population and no nonlinear association was detected, subgroup analysis showed that higher RHGS was significantly associated with a lower risk of low cognitive performance among older adults. This result is consistent with previous studies demonstrating an inverse relationship between RHGS and the incidence of cognitive impairment in older populations [31, 32]. In contrast, no significant association was found in middle-aged individuals in our study, which may be explained by the greater age-related risk of cognitive decline, diminished physiological reserve, and the statistical properties of subgroup interactions. Notably, prior research suggests that a stable association between grip strength and cognitive function tends to emerge around age 65 [33]. Furthermore, our subgroup analysis showed that the protective effect of higher grip strength against the incidence of cardiovascular diseases (including heart disease, hypertension, and stroke) was mainly observed in middle-aged and older females. This aligns with previous research indicating that hormonal changes during menopause are closely associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular diseases in females [34, 35]. In contrast, a separate study using CHARLS data reported an association between higher grip strength and cardiovascular diseases in both genders, which may be attributed to its use of absolute grip strength as the baseline measure [36]. Taken together, these findings suggest that priority should be given to monitoring cognitive function in older adults with low RHGS, while particular attention should be paid to cardiovascular risk in middle-aged and older females with low RHGS.

To further examine the association between RHGS and disease incidence, we analyzed the relationship between longitudinal changes in RHGS and the risk of developing various diseases. The results showed that, compared with the stable group, individuals in the RHGS-increased group had a significantly higher risk of developing DM and stroke, with a similar trend observed for low cognitive performance and hypertension. Further analysis revealed that, at baseline in 2011, the RHGS-increased group had the lowest grip strength, whereas the RHGS-decreased group had the highest. Previous studies have indicated that early-life influences on muscle strength may have long-term effects on mortality risk, and that higher grip strength may provide a larger physiological and functional reserve [37]. Better muscle strength in midlife may offer a greater safety margin above disease thresholds, thereby exerting a protective effect [38]. These findings further highlight the importance of closely monitoring individuals with low grip strength for chronic disease risk, as their risk remains elevated even if their grip strength improves later. Therefore, more frequent screening and monitoring are essential to enable early detection and timely intervention. Stratifying the population by age and sex and tailoring screening efforts to the predominant diseases in each subgroup may help cost-effectively maximize screening efficiency.

This study has several limitations. The cohort was derived from a Chinese population, so caution is needed when generalizing these findings to other ethnic groups. Future research should validate the necessity and effectiveness of RHGS-based stratified interventions in diverse populations and geographic regions. Finally, many sociodemographic and health status variables were self-reported, which may have introduced measurement error, misclassification, or recall bias [39].

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study highlights the importance of sex- and age-stratified RHGS assessment in advancing precision public health efforts. We recommend incorporating RHGS measurement into routine health screenings for middle-aged and older adults, with risk stratification based on population-specific thresholds. Clinically, cognitive function screening should be prioritized for older adults with low RHGS, while screening for glucose and lipid metabolic disorders should be emphasized in middle-aged individuals with low RHGS. Additionally, particular attention should be given to cardiovascular disease risk in middle-aged and older females with low RHGS. Future research should validate this strategy using larger datasets and establish appropriate threshold values to guide its implementation.
