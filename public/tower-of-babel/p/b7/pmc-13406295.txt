Research status, hotspots, and frontiers in functional abdominal pain syndrome: A bibliometric and visual analysis
Authors: Ziqin Zhou, Liu Liu, Jia Fu, Huixin Yan, Yan Sheng, Zhaohui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Functional abdominal pain syndrome (FAPS) is a relatively uncommon functional gastrointestinal disorder. Recent years have witnessed substantial advancements in the study of FAPS; however, no bibliometric analyses have been conducted to date.Objective:This study systematically reviews current research and emerging issues in the field of FAPS using bibliometric methods to understand research trends and future development prospects.Methods:The foundational literature in the field of FAPS was sourced from the Web of Science database. Visual analysis was performed using CiteSpace and VOSviewer software, focusing on factors such as the number of published papers, countries (regions), research institutions, journals, authors, highly cited papers, and keywords.Results:From 1996 to 2025, 996 publications were analyzed, showing a phased growth in annual publication volume peaking in 2021. The United States led in publications and centrality. The “Journal of Pediatric Gastroenterology and Nutrition” had the highest publication count. Marc A. Benninga authored the most articles, notably “Functional Disorders: Children and Adolescents.” The prevalent keyword was “children.” Research on FAPS focuses on evolving disease definitions, the “brain–gut interaction disorder” paradigm, the microbiota–gut–brain axis, children with irritable bowel syndrome, functional abdominal pain, and nonpharmaceutical therapies like probiotics, dietary interventions, and cognitive behavioral therapy. Pediatric populations and non-pharmacological interventions were the most prominent themes, whereas adult-specific research and biomarker-related studies appeared less frequently.Conclusion:This bibliometric study identified key research areas including the microbiota–gut–brain axis, pediatric populations, and non-pharmacological interventions, alongside emerging topics such as gut–brain interaction disorders. These findings suggest that future research should prioritize large-scale trials validating non-pharmacological therapies and biomarkers for central sensitization. Clinically, the development of age-specific diagnostic protocols and interdisciplinary management teams integrating gastroenterology, neurology, and psychology is warranted to improve patient outcomes.

1. Introduction

Functional abdominal pain syndrome (FAPS) is a relatively uncommon functional gastrointestinal (GI) disorder that falls under the category of functional gastrointestinal disorders (FGIDs). It has since been reclassified as one of the disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI).[1,2] According to the diagnostic criteria outlined in Rome IV, it is categorized as centrally mediated abdominal pain syndrome (CAPS).[3,4] The primary characteristic of FAPS is chronic or persistent abdominal pain that endures for 6 months or longer. This pain is not associated with eating or excretion and cannot be entirely accounted for by other organic or metabolic diseases during clinical evaluation.[1,3,4]

FAPS is linked to functional abdominal pain disorders (FAPD), which encompasses subtypes such as irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), functional abdominal pain – not otherwise specified, abdominal migraine, and functional dyspepsia. While all these disorders primarily feature abdominal pain, FAPS emphasizes the “central-mediated” mechanism.[5-7] The prevalence of FAPS ranges from 0.5% to 2.1%, with women affected at rates 1.5 to 2 times higher than men.[8] Patients with FAPS frequently report chronic abdominal pain, often accompanied by neuropsychiatric symptoms such as anxiety, restricted and repetitive behaviors, sensory hyperresponsiveness, and abnormal eating patterns, including reduced emotional eating or slow eating. These symptoms are linked to dysregulation of the brain–gut axis, suggesting that FAPS involves abnormal processing within the central nervous system, which enhances pain perception.[6] In children and adolescents, FAPS and related FAPD are associated with high school absenteeism, psychological comorbidities such as anxiety and depression, and symptoms that persist into adulthood.[9-11] This condition results in significant functional impairment across various life domains for patients, including work, intimate relationships, social and leisure activities, family life, and the ability to care for oneself or others. Consequently, it increases the burden on patients and their families and adversely affects quality of life.[1,12]

Current research indicates that the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying FAPS involve several factors. Central nervous system sensitization is pivotal in the development of chronic visceral pain, leading to visceral hypersensitivity.[13-15] Abnormal signaling within the gut–brain axis constitutes a core mechanism, which includes alterations in afferent neural pathways.[2,15,16] Additionally, dysbiosis of the microbiota and disruptions in neurotransmitter systems, such as the serotonin system, are also implicated.[15,17] However, these mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated due to the lack of definitive organic abnormalities, which hinders targeted therapy development.[18] In the realms of diagnosis, treatment, and therapy, FAPS faces numerous challenges: significant diagnostic obstacles arise from the diversity of causes and the lack of specific biomarkers, resulting in repeated diagnostic tests that often yield inconclusive results, thereby increasing the medical burden.[12] Treatment options are limited and frequently ineffective, particularly concerning the core symptom of abdominal pain. Currently, antispasmodic agents are commonly employed, yet reliable analgesic strategies are lacking, and the quality of evidence is low, as exemplified by insufficient evidence for probiotic treatments.[1,5,12,19] Moreover, the absence of international guidelines and the exploration of psychotherapy, such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), which is supported by weak evidence, have negatively impacted patients’ quality of life, contributing to issues such as school absenteeism and anxiety.[10,11,20] These challenges are partly rooted in an incomplete understanding of the pathophysiological basis, which obstructs the advancement of targeted therapies.

The clinical definition and academic understanding of FAPS have evolved with successive iterations of the Rome diagnostic criteria. Rome IV made key revisions to FAPD diagnostics by increasing the required pain frequency from 2 to 3 days per month to ≥1 d/wk. It removed the vague term “abdominal discomfort” and retained abdominal pain as the core diagnostic feature, thereby markedly improving diagnostic specificity.[21] As a result, many patients who met Rome III criteria no longer satisfy Rome IV. Patients meeting the Rome IV definition exhibit greater pain intensity, more pronounced abdominal distension, increased somatization, and greater rectal hypersensitivity, while levels of anxiety and depression comorbidity remain similar.[22] In children, Rome IV reclassifies FAPDs as DGBI and subdivides them into 4 subtypes. It recommends differential diagnosis based on symptom patterns, which reduces unnecessary invasive testing.[23] A pooled analysis of 66 studies across 29 countries with 2,01,134 subjects found that the prevalence of pediatric FAPDs declined from 13.2% under Rome III to 9.0% under Rome IV and that incidence remains higher in females.[24]

The Rome V criteria released in 2022 further transformed the disease classification system by abandoning the traditional age-stratification model and introducing a framework centered on anatomical sites and symptom patterns. For the first time, this standard explicitly defines central-mediated abdominal pain syndrome, dividing it into 2 subtypes according to the presence or absence of physiological regulatory triggers, and designates central sensitization as the core pathogenesis of FAPS.[25] Factor analysis of a multinational cohort validated the Rome IV diagnostic framework: its 8 core symptom factors account for 45% of phenotypic variation, supplying robust methodological support for iterative refinement of the criteria.[26] In clinical practice, functional abdominal pain in children frequently lacks clear evidence of organic lesions. Alarm features such as weight loss, GI bleeding, and persistent fever signal an increased risk of organic disease but do not distinguish functional from organic abdominal pain.[27] Clinicians must also differentiate conditions that mimic functional pain, notably anterior cutaneous nerve entrapment syndrome; using Carnett’s sign, the abdominal pinch test, and diagnostic blockade of the rectus sheath improves diagnostic accuracy.[28]

Advances in companion diagnostics have prompted a major revision in understanding FAPS pathogenesis. The field has moved beyond single-cause models – whether isolated intestinal dysfunction or simple psychological explanations – and now embraces a multidimensional gut–brain–microbiome model that spans peripheral, central, developmental, and socio‑psychological domains.[29] At the peripheral level, dysbiosis, mucosal barrier disruption, neuroimmune dysregulation, and altered enteric glial cell function together promote visceral hypersensitivity. Activation of mast cells and eosinophils releases inflammatory mediators that directly stimulate intestinal nociceptive pathways and trigger abdominal pain.[30-32] At the central level, maladaptive visceral signal processing due to central sensitization is the principal driver of chronic abdominal pain. Disrupted functional connectivity within pain‑related brain networks, autonomic imbalance, and aberrant recruitment of specific visceral pain circuits collectively contribute to disease progression.[13,20,33,34] Early developmental insults and family stressors exacerbate pain trajectories in children, and autism comorbidity introduces distinct heterogeneity in gut‑brain interactions. Anxiety, depression, and sleep disturbances commonly co-occur with FAPS and substantially increase patient burden; however, these conditions are comorbid features and do not distinguish functional from organic abdominal pain.[6,24,35]

In conclusion, current research has clarified the standardized diagnostic framework and multilayered pathogenesis of FAPS, but notable limitations remain. Most studies have addressed single diagnostic criteria, localized pathological pathways, or clinical features within individual populations. Few have undertaken macroscopic, systematic investigations using bibliometric methods, and there is no comprehensive review of the global FAPS research landscape, international collaboration networks, hotspot evolution, or frontier research gaps. This gap hampers the field’s ability to apprehend overall development trends and core knowledge deficits, and it obstructs the formation of a broad theoretical foundation for deeper mechanistic studies, accurate clinical diagnosis and treatment, and optimized multidisciplinary intervention systems. These shortcomings motivate the bibliometric and visual analysis carried out in this study.

Bibliometrics employs mathematical and statistical methods to quantitatively describe and display relationships among published works, facilitating the analysis of published information and its associated metadata.[36,37] CiteSpace and VOSviewer are widely utilized software tools for visual analysis, which analyze bibliometric results during the scientific mapping process using various algorithms.[38] This research methodology emphasizes factors such as publication volume, country, author, institution, journal, highly cited literature, and keywords pertinent to specific fields of research. It provides insights into the changes and developmental trends of research hotspots and frontiers within these domains.[39] This study systematically reviews FAPS-related literature indexed in the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) from 1996 to 2025 to give clinicians and researchers a clearer view of the field’s research hotspots and frontier directions.

2. Material and methods

2.1. Data sources and search strategy

WoSCC is widely recognized by researchers as a significant bibliographic database that encompasses a diverse array of publications across multiple disciplines. It is deemed the most appropriate database for bibliometric and visual analysis.[40] Consequently, Web of Science was chosen as the data source for this study. Data were collected on December 15, 2025, encompassing literature published by WoSCC from 1996 to 2025. The search formula employed was TS = (Functional Abdominal Pain Syndrome OR Functional Abdominal Pain OR Chronic Idiopathic Abdominal Pain OR Functional Chronic Abdominal Pain OR Centrally Mediated Abdominal Pain Syndrome OR Centralized Abdominal Pain Syndrome OR Brain–gut Axis-related Abdominal Pain Syndrome). The language restriction was set to “English” and the document types were limited to “Articles” and “Review” (Fig. 1). This bibliometric analysis only extracted published literature from public databases without involving human subjects or animal experiments. Therefore, ethical approval and informed consent were not required in this study.

2.2. Data analysis

English publication data were collected from WoSCC and saved as plain text for subsequent analysis with multiple software tools. CiteSpace (version 6.3.R3), VOSviewer (version 1.6.20), and Microsoft Excel 2021 were used to perform quantitative, bibliometric, and visual analyses. Excel 2021 was used to produce a comprehensive chart showing the annual growth trend of publications. CiteSpace, developed by Chaomei Chen at Drexel University in Philadelphia, is bibliometric visualization software that detects hotspots and trends in specific research fields. VOSviewer, developed by Nees Jan van Eck and Ludo Waltman at Leiden University in the Netherlands, is a quantitative analysis tool that extracts key information from large publication sets and constructs bibliometric network graphs.[41,42] In this study, we combined these 2 tools, set appropriate parameters, and conducted bibliometric and visual analyses of FAPS research, focusing on publication counts, countries (regions), research institutions, journals, authors, highly cited papers, and keywords.

3.5. Citation burst analysis and emerging trends

The analysis of highly cited articles and citation bursts reflects both the authority of the research and the continuous expansion of the frontiers in the field of FAPS (Fig. 6A, B). As indicated in Table 6, nine of the 10 most-cited papers are published in Q1 journals, while 1 is in a Q2 journal; notably, the top 5 papers have each been cited over 30 times. The paper titled “Pediatric functional abdominal pain disorders” by Thapar has received 61 citations and is published in Nature Reviews Disease Primers (Q1, IF 60.6), highlighting the significance of high-impact journals in disseminating core findings and fostering therapeutic innovation. The 10 papers with the highest emergent time span from 2007 to 2025, with Thapar (2020) exhibiting the greatest emergent intensity (29.41). This work systematically elucidates the relationship among gut flora, neuroimmunity, and the brain–gut axis. The Rome IV criteria, proposed by Hyams et al (2016), transformed the diagnostic approach for FGIDs in children from “excluding organic diseases” to a “positive diagnosis based on symptoms.” Additionally, the meta-analysis by Korterink et al (2015) first reported the global prevalence of FAPDs in children as 13.5%, along with associated risk factors. Overall, the research hotspots in this domain encompass clarifying the epidemiological burden and risk factors, evolving toward innovative diagnostic criteria, and further investigating the core mechanisms of the microbial-gut–brain axis, thereby providing essential theoretical support for subsequent translational research.

The time-weighted analysis of keywords reveals the temporal evolution of research hotspots.[44] As illustrated in Figure 7C, this analysis indicates that the initial phase of the study concentrated on defining and classifying concepts such as “functional abdominal pain syndrome” and “functional gastrointestinal disorders.” During the subsequent phase, the emphasis shifted, resulting in an increased prominence of keywords such as “symptoms,” “diagnosis” and “management.” Additionally, terms related to “quality of life,” “epidemiology” and “risk factors” saw a significant rise in importance, highlighting the diseases’ effects on quality of life, their epidemiological characteristics, and associated risk factors.

To elucidate the thematic structure and evolutionary context of the FAPS research field, we employed CiteSpace to perform cluster analysis on high-frequency keywords.[45] Utilizing the log-likelihood ratio algorithm, we constructed a network for keyword clustering, revealing the formation of 11 distinct clusters within the FAPS domain (Fig. 7D), which are centered on the overarching theme of “brain–gut interaction - functional gastrointestinal disorders - clinical assessment and intervention.” The primary clusters encompass disease entities such as IBS and inflammatory bowel disease, neural regulatory mechanisms associated with heart rate variability, and research avenues including probiotic intervention and randomized controlled trials (RCTs). The timeline chart (Fig. 7E) illustrates a progressive deepening of research, evolving from basic symptoms, diagnosis, and trigger exploration to a focus on disease entities and neural mechanisms. In recent years, the emphasis has shifted toward intervention verification and evidence-based research.

Keyword citation burst analysis identified 25 highly emergent keywords spanning the years 1996 to 2025 (Fig. 7F). During the early stage (1996–2010), the emphasis was on symptom description and diagnostic criteria. In the midterm (2011–2019), the focus shifted to non-pharmacological treatments, including gut-directed hypnotherapy, CBT, and the mechanisms of diet, inflammation, and psychological comorbidity. Following 2020, keywords such as “functional abdominal pain disorders” and “disorders of gut-brain interaction” gained prominence. This new research paradigm of “brain–gut interaction disorder” reflects an overall evolutionary trajectory that transitions from the description of phenomena to the exploration of mechanisms and the establishment of a comprehensive bio-psycho-social management framework.

4. Discussion

4.1. General information

In this study, we performed bibliometric and visual analyses of 996 documents pertaining to FAPS research from 1996 to 2025. The overall trend shows a year-on-year increase in the literature on FAPS, suggesting that clinicians and researchers are devoting greater attention to in-depth investigations of this disease.

Based on the results of multidimensional analysis, the knowledge evolution of FAPS research can be categorized into 3 distinct stages. The period from 1996 to 2010 represents the early exploration stage, which primarily focused on defining symptoms and establishing diagnostic criteria. From 2011 to 2019, the field entered a phase of stable growth and expansion, emphasizing psychosocial factors, analyzing brain–gut axis mechanisms, and clinically validating non-pharmacologicaltherapies. Since 2020, targeted interventions and evidence-based research have emerged as the mainstream, driven by an in-depth exploration of the “microbe-gut-brain axis” and the establishment of a new paradigm centered on “brain–gut interaction disorder.” FAPS research has transitioned from a singular symptom description to an interdisciplinary framework characterized by “pediatrics as the core, with gastrointestinal and neurological support, alongside multidisciplinary collaborative integration.” This evolution has gradually led to the development of an integrated research model encompassing biological, psychological, and social dimensions, thereby providing a comprehensive theoretical framework for optimizing clinical diagnosis, treatment, and innovative mechanisms in this domain.

The plateau in publications after 2021, which coincides with the emergence of the DGBI paradigm, may indicate a transitional phase in the field. Moving from descriptive work to mechanistic and interventional studies typically requires longer study durations and can transiently suppress publication output. The sustained rate above 55 articles per year signals ongoing robust interest, yet the lack of a renewed upward surge implies that breakthrough innovations – such as validated biomarkers or large-scale intervention trials – are required to catalyze the next growth phase in FAPS research.

4.2. Global research landscape and collaborative patterns

Analysis of global research and collaboration patterns indicates a marked increase in findings within this field following 2011, which correlates with the widespread adoption of the “brain–gut interaction disorder” paradigm as a fundamental pathological mechanism of functional GI diseases.[25] The United States leads in both the volume of publications and the centrality of international cooperation networks, serving as the primary hub for global scientific research collaboration.[46] The Netherlands holds the second position in publication volume, yet its centrality is comparatively lower, underscoring the United States’ prominent and connective role. Fostering balanced cross-border and cross-regional cooperation is essential for integrating diverse advantages and enhancing the breadth of research and the potential for clinical translational potential.

However, the limited centrality of nations outside North America and Western Europe in the collaboration network reveals a marked geographic disparity in FAPS research capacity. This imbalance can produce underrepresentation of population-specific clinical features, genetic backgrounds, and healthcare system contexts, which constrains the generalizability of current evidence. Promoting balanced cross-border and cross-regional collaboration is therefore essential to integrate diverse strengths and to broaden research scope and clinical translational potential.

At the institutional level, top academic institutions in North America and Western Europe constitute the central output cluster, fostering a tightly knit, multicenter cooperative network.[47-49] The concentration of leading institutions in North America and Western Europe (8/10 in the United States, 2/10 in the Netherlands) indicates a tightly knit, Western-centric research ecosystem. This clustering facilitates high-quality multicenter collaboration but risks intellectual homogeneity and may overlook region-specific research questions. Expanding institutional partnerships to Asia, South America, and Africa would diversify methodological approaches and improve the external validity of FAPS research findings. This network’s primary research focus lies at the intersection of gastroenterology and pediatrics. For future research breakthroughs, it is necessary to establish a standardized pediatric clinical assessment cohort and deepen the integration of core disciplines in mechanism exploration and comprehensive intervention plan design.

4.3. Research hotspots: from association to mechanism

Keyword and co-citation analyses indicate that the focus of FAPS research has progressively shifted from initial symptom descriptions and disease definitions to the investigation of brain–gut interaction mechanisms. Early studies concentrated on defining clinical manifestations, including terms such as “dysfunction,” “diagnostic criteria” and “recurrent abdominal pain.” Recently, however, the emphasis has transitioned to elucidating mechanisms, particularly the role of brain–gut axis dysfunction in the onset and progression of diseases.

The co-citation network reveals 2 distinct scholarly clusters: 1 focused on diagnostic frameworks and classification (exemplified by Drossman and Hyams) and another centered on mechanistic investigation and interventional validation (exemplified by Walker and Saps). Cross-cluster integration is limited. Bridging these clusters by combining diagnostic standardization with mechanistic studies offers a critical opportunity to move the field beyond its current siloed structure.

Dysregulation of the brain–gut axis and central sensitization are crucial mechanisms underlying chronic visceral pain. This condition can lead to visceral hypersensitivity. An imbalance in intestinal flora and the dysfunction of neurotransmitters like serotonin contribute to abnormal bidirectional signaling between the brain and the intestine.[50] The increasing number of citations related to these concepts indicates a growing consensus on the significant role of neuro-immune-microbial networks in supporting the concept of FAPS as a disorder characterized by interactions between the brain and the gut.

The prominence of pediatric specialty journals (Journal of Pediatric Gastroenterology and Nutrition, Pediatrics) alongside adult-focused gastroenterology and pain journals indicates that FAPS research is predominantly pediatric-centric, while adult FAPS (CAPS) remains comparatively understudied. This age-based asymmetry may reflect diagnostic challenges in adults, where FAPS overlaps with IBS and other functional disorders, or a historical research bias toward childhood functional pain. Future bibliometric and clinical studies should explicitly address this adult research gap to support comprehensive, lifespan-spanning management strategies.

Psychosocial factors and non-pharmacological interventions represent crucial frontiers. Key terms like “cognitive behavioral therapy,” “gut-oriented hypnotherapy,” “depression” and “anxiety” are emphasized, signaling the broadening of investigations within the biological-psychological-social integrated model.[51] Psychological interventions have the potential to ameliorate symptoms and regulate central pain processing, underscoring the transition from a singular biomedical approach to an integrated treatment paradigm.[52,53] Presently, the emerging classification framework of “brain–gut interaction disorders” is propelling the field toward an innovative phase that amalgamates mechanistic exploration and multidimensional intervention.

4.4. Translational implications and therapeutic perspectives

In addition to exploring mechanisms, bibliometric analysis indicates that clinical translational research and targeted intervention strategies are becoming central to the field. The surge in citations and keyword clustering reveals that psychobehavioral interventions, including CBT and gut-directed hypnotherapy, dietary regulations such as the fructose malabsorption diet and the low FODMAP (fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols) diet, and microbiota-targeted interventions like probiotics and prebiotics are increasingly recognized as potential management strategies for FAPS/CAPS.[54,55] Although most intervention studies, particularly those related to the microbiome, provide limited evidence, they underscore a significant interest in modulating brain–gut interaction pathways to alleviate symptoms.

The prominence of review articles and consensus papers (e.g., Rome IV criteria, Thapar’s primer) among the most-cited works indicates that the field has emphasized framework development, while relatively few high-impact primary studies have produced practice-changing evidence. This pattern highlights an urgent need for prospective, hypothesis-driven research – especially RCTs and biomarker-discovery projects – that can produce the next generation of widely cited, practice-changing findings.

The heterogeneity in clinical presentations, comorbid psychological conditions, and variability in treatment responses in FAPS pose significant challenges for clinical practice while also presenting opportunities for precision medicine. Developing personalized multi-modal integrated management strategies based on symptom subtypes and central sensitization characteristics is considered a promising research avenue.[6] Analyzing the evolution path from “disease characterization-mechanism investigation-intervention validation” through bibliometric analysis, along with the absence of robust evidence like large RCTs, can offer objective insights to identify unexplored areas and enhance future clinical trial planning and prioritization.[56,57]

4.5. Emerging trends and future directions

The evolving research theme indicates the need to enhance the comprehensive understanding of disorders related to brain–gut interaction (DGBI) through a multidimensional and interdisciplinary framework in the future. The amalgamation of neuroimaging, psychophysiological markers, and diverse analytical techniques is crucial for examining central sensitization, visceral hypersensitivity, and the causal relationships within the microbiota–gut–brain axis, as well as for delineating subtypes or predictive biomarkers of treatment efficacy.[58-60]

The increasing presence of psychology and psychiatry, together with emerging links to geriatrics, signals expansion beyond traditional biomedical boundaries. This trend aligns with the DGBI paradigm and indicates that future FAPS research will increasingly require interdisciplinary teams able to integrate biological markers, psychological assessments, and social determinants of health. Funding agencies and academic institutions should proactively support cross-disciplinary training programs and collaborative grant mechanisms.

The post-2020 citation burst for “disorders of gut-brain interaction,” together with the persistent prominence of “children” and “irritable bowel syndrome,” highlights a gap between conceptual advances and the empirical literature. While classification has shifted toward mechanism-based models, the evidence base remains dominated by pediatric studies and research focused on symptom overlap. Closing this gap will require dedicated adult CAPS cohorts, validated biomarkers that distinguish FAPS from IBS, and mechanistic studies spanning age groups. The waning burst intensity for “hypnotherapy” and “cognitive behavioral therapy” after 2019, despite their clinical relevance, further emphasizes the need for large-scale efficacy trials to revitalize these non-pharmacological approaches.

Integrating the mentioned biological markers with digital tools and artificial intelligence for clinical data analysis can accelerate the investigation of the intrinsic heterogeneity of FAPS/CAPS.[61-63] This strategy can streamline the classification of disease subtypes based on their fundamental mechanisms, enhance the development of customized personalized interventions, and ultimately shift from a generalized treatment strategy to a precision medicine management model.[64,65]

4.6. Limitations

To our knowledge, this study is the first systematic bibliometric and visual analysis of FAPS/CAPS. It provides a concise resource for researchers and clinicians in gastroenterology, pediatrics, and psychosomatic medicine, allowing them to quickly grasp the field’s research trajectory, knowledge structure, and emerging hotspots.

This study has several limitations. First, the WoSCC, while authoritative for bibliometric analysis, predominantly indexes English-language journals and may therefore omit important work published in Chinese, French, Spanish, or Russian. This language bias is especially relevant for FAPS research because cultural and health-system differences shape symptom expression, diagnostic pathways, and treatment access. As a result, traditional medicine approaches to functional abdominal pain in East Asian contexts and community-based intervention models in low-resource settings may be underrepresented, which limits the generalizability of our conclusions about “global” research trends and may overemphasize Western biomedical frameworks. Second, bibliometric measures based on citation counts favor older publications and established topics, which can delay recognition of recent paradigm-shifting studies. In FAPS research, where the DGBI reconceptualization and microbiota–gut–brain axis studies are relatively recent, citation-based metrics may understate their impact and underrepresent emerging methodological advances such as multi-omics integration and machine learning–based phenotype clustering. Third, the algorithmic constraints of CiteSpace and VOSviewer can miss subtle semantic relationships and implicit drivers of collaboration, such as funding patterns, conference networks, or informal mentorship structures that shape research trajectories. Fourth, bibliometric analysis cannot evaluate the methodological quality or clinical relevance of included studies, and high citation counts may reflect accessibility or controversy rather than scientific rigor. Therefore, the research priorities identified in this study should be regarded as data-driven hypotheses that require validation through expert consensus and systematic evidence synthesis rather than as definitive directives. To increase practical relevance, future bibliometric studies in this field should incorporate structured feedback from clinical experts via Delphi surveys or focus groups to confirm that identified research priorities align with unmet clinical needs. Finally, this study is limited by its exclusive reliance on policy bibliometric mapping. Co-citation networks, keyword co-occurrence, and burst detection reveal structural features of the research landscape, but they cannot fully capture nuanced thematic evolution, paradigm shifts, or intellectual debates that qualitative content analysis would elucidate. For instance, the apparent shift from “exclusion diagnosis” to “positive symptom–based classification,” suggested by the citation dominance of Rome IV criteria and the burst emergence of “disorders of gut–brain interaction,” and the inferred underdevelopment of adult CAPS relative to pediatric FAPS, are interpretive patterns derived from metadata rather than findings verified through systematic qualitative review of article content. Future studies should combine qualitative methods (e.g., framework analysis of full texts, Delphi expert consensus) with bibliometric metrics to validate these emergent narratives and to identify subdirections within FAPS research (e.g., age-specific mechanisms, biomarker-driven subtyping) that policy mapping alone cannot resolve.

5. Conclusion

This bibliometric study analyzed research trends, the knowledge framework, and recent advances in FAPS by integrating publication and citation metrics. The analysis delineated key research areas – most notably the microbiota–gut–brain axis, pediatric populations, and nonpharmaceutical interventions – and identified emerging topics such as gut–brain interaction disorders. From these findings, we propose 3 priorities to propel the field forward: build international collaborative networks to harmonize CAPS diagnostic criteria across pediatric and adult populations; prioritize mechanistic research on the microbiota–gut–brain axis to reveal actionable therapeutic targets; and conduct large-scale RCTs that compare CBT, gut-directed hypnotherapy, and dietary interventions using rigorous designs with appropriate control arms and explicit stratification by age group and comorbidity profile. Incorporating these bibliometrically identified themes into clinical guidelines and continuing medical education will promote a shift from symptom-based management toward mechanism-driven, precision care for patients with FAPS.
