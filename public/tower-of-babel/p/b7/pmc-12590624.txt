Chronic health condition and risk of suicidal behaviours in two hospitals in Ghana
Authors: Rachel Onomah, Priscilla Osei-Bonsu, Winifred Asare-Doku, Mabel Oti-Boadi, Joseph Osafo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe study examined the lived experiences of patients with chronic kidney condition and their risk of suicidal behaviours.MethodsNine chronically ill patients from two hospitals in Ghana were interviewed. Data collected through interviews were thematically analysed.ResultsThe themes that emerged from the analysis highlighted participants’ perspectives on their personal conceptualisation of chronic illnesses, their experience of chronic illness, medication adherence, chronic conditions, and suicidality, as well as health services-related challenges related to their conditions and varying coping methods, including religion, information, and social support. The study revealed that chronically ill patients adopted coping strategies such as religiosity, health-seeking information, and social support to manage their condition. On the other hand, participants highlighted the negative impact of adherence-related experiences and poor attitudes among health workers on their well-being. Again, loss of meaning, feared loss of virility and chronicity and its related issues (chronic condition and suicidality) were found to increase suicide ideation in chronic kidney patients.Recommendation.The number of crisis intervention service providers in health centres should be expanded and equipped with adaptive skills to train families and religious bodies about proper coping skills, as they provide social support which is crucial in reducing the prevalence of both chronic conditions and patients’ risk of suicide. The National Health Insurance Scheme should be improved to cater for patients with chronic kidney conditions to ease their financial burden. Chronic kidney conditions place a heavy physical, emotional, and financial burden on those affected. Addressing these challenges requires coordinated and sustained efforts, including targeted policy interventions. A holistic approach is essential to improve the management of chronic kidney disease and reduce its incidence, as well as associated outcomes like suicide, especially in developing countries such as Ghana.Keywords: Chronically ill patients, Qualitative method, Coping methods, Holistic treatment

Background

Suicidal behaviours have been associated with psychological and psychiatric disorders such as bipolar, anxiety, and depression, among others [27, 53]. Socio-cultural factors such as unemployment and low-level education, unfavourable events before suicide and ongoing family violence have been closely linked to suicidal behaviours but at different levels in males and females [18]. In fact, from an extensive review of risks associated with suicidal behaviours in low and middle-income countries, suicidal behaviours have been linked to loss of job and financial difficulties [20, 22]. Also, several chronic physical diseases are associated with an increased risk of self-harm and suicide [54]. Since chronic diseases are expensive to treat, they sometimes lead to family abandonment and social isolation from family members who themselves may be constrained financially [21].

Chronically ill patients are often prescribed multiple or lifelong medications. Some suicide cases have been linked to intentional overdose or incorrect dosing of such medications [5]. Adherence is more likely among diabetes patients who believe they can influence the course of their illness through personal actions [47]. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a growing global health concern, affecting an estimated 10–15% of the population. Rising rates of obesity, diabetes, and hypertension across both high- and low-income countries are major contributors to the increasing prevalence of CKD [11, 16].

In Ghana, as in the rest of the world, CKD has been recognised as an established public health problem. In a study in Ghana, CKD is reported to be common among haemodialysis patients with consequential effects on their general health, well-being and quality of life [13]. Ghana has recently reported some 12,000 cases of kidney failure that are diagnosed annually, demanding immediate attention to solutions for management and treatment [3]. The increasing CKD burden in the country is largely due to the emergence of diabetes and hypertension, which are both risk factors for kidney disease [13, 40). What is more, a shortage of healthcare resources and poor awareness about kidney diseases add to the challenges encountered by CKD patients in Nigeria [40]. Similarly, reports from Ghana by Boateng et al. [12] attribute reduced sessions (associated with cost), limited dialysis machines as challenges faced by patients of End Stage Chronic Kidney Disease.

Suicide remains a significant public health concern in Ghana [32, 50]. Research indicates a growing burden of chronic illnesses in the country, often accompanied by financial strain, which contributes to family tension and conflict [21–23]. These pressures can lead patients to skip medications or treatments, increasing the risk of mental health conditions and suicidal ideation [8].

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients undergoing treatment are particularly vulnerable to psychological distress, with anxiety and depression being the most common. This is largely due to the invasive nature of dialysis and the extensive lifestyle changes required, which can lead to frustration and emotional exhaustion [41]. In cultural contexts like Ghana, where chronic illness may be viewed through fatalistic or isolating lenses, stigma can further deepen patients’ emotional burden exacerbating social withdrawal and depressive symptoms.

The intersection of chronic illness and mental health, if left unchecked, can have very serious consequences, including suicidal ideation. Individuals with chronic illnesses, such as those who have been diagnosed with CKD, are more likely to experience suicidal thoughts and behaviours when they become emotionally distressed [17]. Because of the limited mental health resources for most CKD patients in Ghana, suicidal ideation among this population is likely to increase. One other issue that can worsen the plight of CKD patients is the current economic meltdown, as frequent financial demands associated with continued care costs could become a significant stressor for patients and increase anxiety about what lies ahead [34].

Given the psychosocial challenges linked to suicide risk, religiosity and social support have been identified as important coping resources [44]. In Ghana, strong stigma surrounds suicide, with no one wanting to be associated with it [43]. As chronic conditions like Type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease continue to rise in Ghana [22, 23]), there is a growing need for suicide research to explore the connection between chronic illness and suicidal behaviour.

While significant research has been conducted on suicide and its risk factors in Ghana, there remains a gap in understanding how individuals with chronic illnesses perceive the causes of their condition and the health-related challenges they face. This study aims to fill that gap by exploring the lived experiences of patients at Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital and the Police Hospital. It also lays the groundwork for future psychological research on the intersection of chronic illness and suicide risk in Ghana and the broader African context.

Ghana: the study site

Ghana is a country in West Africa with a population of 33.79 million. It boasts a rich culture and diverse religious faiths, including Christianity, Islam, and African traditional religions. According to the 2021 government census, approximately 71 percent of the population are Christians, 20 percent are Muslims, 3 percent believe in indigenous or animistic religious beliefs, and 6 percent belong to other religious groups or have no religious affiliation.

In recent times, high levels of chronic illnesses have been reported in Ghana [23] with links to suicidal behaviours [5]: (Singhal, Seminog, Hawton & Goldacre, 2014). Suicide is a scary phenomenon that threatens our existence and has been reported as a burdensome reality in Ghana [31, 49]. There is a lack of national statistics on annual suicide rates. However, evidence from cross-sectional studies have indicated suicidal behaviour among some groups of persons: adolescents [9, 49], college students [48, 51], and older adults [36]. The studies have shown increasing vulnerability of the youth, particularly those in pre-university and university to suicidal ideation and attempts [48, 51]. Muruthi and colleagues [36] have also indicated significant risk of among older women who are suffering from a type of chronic illness. Research indicates that socioeconomic factors, such as financial instability and social isolation, significantly contribute to the risk of suicide among various demographic groups [1, 31].

Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital, which has evolved into a national referral centre and the third largest hospital in Africa [33], registers admissions for both males and females suffering from hypertension, diabetes, sickle cell disease and other conditions; as well as deaths for these conditions. It also has a department for chronic care set up in 2013 to take care of chronically ill patients, especially diabetic and hypertensive patients. The outpatient department of chronic conditions (various clinics) and both the male and female medical wards of kidney patients were the settings of the research. The rationale for choosing Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital is that it is a referral centre with a lot of reported cases from all parts of the country.

With the establishment of the Ghana Police Hospital in 1976 to provide quality healthcare to members of the Ghana Police Service, their families, and those involved in criminal cases, the hospital has since expanded its infrastructure and services to cover the general population. In recent times (April 2012), it established a dialysis centre for the treatment of kidney diseases in the country. Additionally, with the Police Hospital now handling more cases from the general public than its original mandate [25], it has also become one of the country's referral centres. Chronic illnesses like hypertension, rheumatism, and joint pains are among the top cases treated at the hospital [25]. It is appropriate that these two hospitals are selected for a study of this nature.

Methods

This study used the qualitative approach to explore factors that lead chronically ill patients to attempt suicide or ideate about it in July 2019.

Participants and procedure

All patients with CKD in Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital and the Police Hospital constituted the population of the study. Since the study was about CKD, all patients suffering from the condition qualified to be included in the study. Purposive and convenience sampling techniques were employed in the study. Purposive Sampling was employed in selecting the Ranal Units of the Korle-Bu teaching Hospital and the Police Hospital, while convenient sampling was utilised in selecting patients from these units for inclusion in the study. Sixteen (16) participants with chronic kidney conditions originally consented to participate in the study, but only nine (9) finally accepted to be interviewed. Informed consent was sought from participants before data collection. All participants could understand and speak the English Language, and so interviews were conducted in English. Participants sometimes used their local dialect (Twi) in conveying some exclamations and expressions. Responses in Akan (Twi) were transcribed and translated into the English language and subsequently incorporated into the study.

A semi-structured interview schedule was developed by the researchers and employed for the collection of data. This schedule was utilised for its ability to help the researcher acquire the in-depth individual experiences of participants. It included a brief description of the study, demographic information and questions on suicidal ideation, attempt and coping mechanisms. Interviews were recorded with the permission of participants in the study. The interviews were conducted in rooms provided by nurses in charge of both the Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital (Renal Clinic) and the Police Hospital (Male and Female wards). All interviews were conducted in English. Some participants, in limited instances, made some passing comments in the Akan Language when answering the interview questions. Responses in the Akan dialect were translated and subsequently analysed. Ethical approval was sought from the University of Ghana Ethics Committee for the Humanities (ECH 052/17–18). This study observed the ethical requirements of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Analysis

The qualitative analysis was completed using Thematic Analysis [15]. This procedure was used to identify patterns and themes from the interview to analyse the experiences of participants. The description of patterns makes it a distinctive feature from the other types of qualitative analysis. Coding was guided by key issues identified through a review of relevant literature and aligned with the study’s aims. The researchers interpreted the codes with flexibility, allowing the analysis to remain responsive to emerging insights from the data [15]. Again, thematic analysis helped in uncovering overt experiences of suicidal behaviour in patients and gave meaning to the experiences of chronically ill patients [55].

Conceptualisation of chronic condition

Patients’ first experiences shared were on the cause of their illness. During their narrations, the ‘conceptualisation of chronic condition’ was noticed as a major theme with three sub-themes emerging: namely, developmental changes, genetic conditions, and diabolical manipulations.

Developmental changes

Findings from the study show that patients attribute the cause of their condition to changes that occur in the biological development of a person. The narrative below explains how a participant thinks ageing automatically causes an individual to have some organ failure:

I will say it’s my age. Everybody has a threshold age. When you are about 15years old, your kidneys are perfect. At about 50, your kidney level drops and when you get to 60… it comes to stage 4 and becomes weak… (PM 2 Age 73, Renal Failure)

Genetic conditions

Another participant, on the other hand, attributed the cause of her condition to genetic predisposition resulting from having a sickle cell gene.

…. You know, as for me, they said it is the sickle cell condition that has caused it. You know, I am sickle cell positive, and they say it has made my kidneys weak. (PF 5, Age 56, Renal Failure)

Diabolical manipulations

From the data, some participants expressed fear of spiritual manipulation by other competitors in their field of work (electrical work and carpentry, respectively).

…sometimes I think “s333…obi na a y3 me saa’ (someone is spiritually causing my sickness). Someone can cause it ooooo. Because it is common in my kind of work. (PM 8, Age 46,)

A second participant (PM 3), who is also an artisan, similarly confirmed this practice in their trade.

I don’t know. You know, in our work, people can give you a sickness. Hmmm… I don’t know, I’m just praying I get well. (PM 3, Age 53, Diabetes)

There is a strong cultural anxiety about anti-life forces in Ghana and how someone’s life could be ‘destroyed’ by using black magic. This belief has been highlighted by Pentecostal churches in recent time in Ghana. This is what the participant is referring to in the text as someone “can give you sickness”. (PM 3, Age 53, Diabetes)

Experiences of chronic illness

This theme addresses the experience of patients with chronic conditions. Generally, the participants reported some unique stressful conditions which were associated with being chronically ill. These are analysed under three main sub-themes: cost-related stress, treatment-related stress and biographical disruption.

Cost-related stress

The findings from the study show that patients with chronic conditions are mostly stressed out from the cost of treating their medical conditions, from the fees they have to pay, to the transportation cost of reaching the hospital, as illustrated in the narratives below:

…when I worked for 33years continuously, when I came on pension, I was given four thousand Ghana cedis (USD 330). If I tell you how much I receive a month, you will be surprised. It cannot even pay my electricity bills, how much more dialysis? And the cost of dialysis is Errrrmm… 200…... Errrrmm, GH₵250 (USD 20) per session, and I have to come twice a week. My transportation, food, and add drugs, my pension money is all… gone. At this age, no elderly person in this country should pay for dialysis…So, GH₵10.00 (USD 0.95), GH₵100.00 (USD 9.52) benevolence from people is what is keeping us. Transportation too from where I stay to Korle-bu is GH₵110.00 (USD 10.47) because I can’t go by “trotro”, (i.e., public transport) but by taxi. Sometimes, if my condition is bad, the taxi has to wait for me and take all his daily sales from me. (PM2, Age73, Renal Failure)

… My problem now is how I will pay my bills when I’m discharged. All the money I had on me, about GH₵300.00 (USD 28.56) is finished. What I rely on are small monies people give me when they visit. Can you imagine I still have to provide for my family because my wife is unemployed? Yes …. but it doesn’t cover anything. A lot of the things I have to buy. Even what you just put down (pointing to a cotton wool), is GH₵ 20.00 (USD 1.90), a day. I have to buy, so I need help. (PM8, Male, Age 46, Diabetes)

Treatment related stress

Another source of stress was the challenges faced in accessing treatment, including faulty machines, lack of treatment kits, and occasional inexperience of healthcare staff.

…The difficulties come in assessing health care. Sometimes you come and some machines are not working, so you have to wait. Yes, the place is small and now a lot of people are coming. Hmmm… I just pray to God to help us. (PF 5, Age 56, Renal Failure)

…. You have asked a very pertinent question. I will say NO! I will explain why I told you that I had this illness in 2004. They fitted a Fistula for me at ‘37 Military Hospital’ and I have carried for 15 years plus, I’ve never had a problem. I came to ‘Korle- Bu Teaching Hospital’ and they have spoilt it. Sometimes I come, there are no ‘lines’. Look, for the past one month I have been coming up and down, and they say they have no’ lines’. Now my Fistula is faulty, I’m sitting here trying to get one to be fitted for me. So for that one … that question “deε” it’s not the best (frowned face). (PM2, Age73, Renal Failure)

Biographical disruption

This theme addresses the disruption of the life course of participants ranging from ability to work to inability to work. This is expressed in the quotes below:

Oh … I worry, I worry but what can I do? It has already happened. What pains me is that all the things I used to do when I wasn’t sick, now I can’t do them again. The challenge is that I can’t go to work as I used to. (PM 3, Age 53, stroke)

The only thing is that the pain is too much, and the doctor told me that’s how it is. It will go by itself. The problem now is the pain, my work, now. I was exporting fish, shrimps…. now the business has slowed down just because of my health (PM 4, Age 53, stroke, diabetes & hypertension).

Adherence to medication

Three patients admitted having difficulties in taking their medications and adhering to all other forms of treatments and diet regimes. Some reported the difficulties they faced in paying for their treatments, and others also spoke about how these regimes and treatments burden them. Consequently, they sometimes stopped treatment/medication to see if death would occur.

Participant 7 confirmed that some renal patients die due to non-attendance at the clinic for dialysis.

Cost of treatment

… When it is getting to the weekend, hmmm…. (Red eyes) I begin to cry, because I am thinking of how to get GH₵ 500.00 (USD 41) for dialysis next week. It is not easy ooo. Yes, I try to eat all they say I should eat, like coconut oil, kontomire , errrr, that green leaf, hmm…sister it’s not easy…sometimes you feel tired of all these things, one person, don’t do this, don’t do that. Sometimes you get tired…. A lot of us die because of this. You come to the clinic, and they will say this person is dead because he or she didn’t come for dialysis. (PM 7, Male, Age 44, renal disease)

Side Effects of Medication

Some patients reported non-adherence due to actual and perceived side effects. Participant 8 cited weakness, while Participant 7 expressed discomfort and concern over the permanency of medical devices.

It’s not easy ooo…. (takes the interviewer’s hand to touch his fistula on his arm). You see, this thing is always vibrating. You can’t sleep on your arm, and it’s supposed to be there for the rest of your life. It is so uncomfortable...Hmm. (PM7, Age44, Renal Disease)

…I was just worried I was going to be taking this medicine for life, and I hear it will affect me too…you understand? (Smiles)…I mean my thing (Pointing to his manhood). (PM 9, Age 45, 2 year, Hypertension)

Fatalism

Participant 9, in contrast, expressed a readiness to die, questioning the purpose of taking medication that merely manages rather than cures the condition. He viewed death as inevitable:

Oh yes! Now that I am here, everything they give me I take. But you see, if I’m working, I sometimes forget, and I even think that it’s not necessary when it will not cure the disease, why not stop and let whatever will happen, happen. After all as an officer, all die be die’. (PM 9, Age 45, Hypertension)

Chronic condition and suicidality

The theme addresses participants’ suicidal behaviour following their chronic conditions. It analyses some of the suicidogenic factors embedded in the chronic conditions of patients under three sub-themes: loss of meaning, feared loss of virility, and chronicity and related issues.

Loss of meaning

One patient reported that his suicidal behaviour appears to be driven by four main issues: first, the disclosure of a renal failure, second, the fear related to the suffering he would be exposed to, third, he is unable to take care of his children, and fourth, his wife has abandoned him. All of these show that the patient appears to have lost his sense of meaning in life. This is expressed in the narrative below:

Honestly (with a smile), my sister, you know, when they told me I have kidney failure, when I went home, (head bowed) I took poison to die. Oooooh….! Yes! If I die, then I will not suffer again. They talked to me and I stopped, but sometimes, I wish I had died” k3 k3”(just like that). Just look at how I look now. I’m weak now, no work. What is my dignity? I can’t even take care of my children. My wife is gone. Hmm… that day, it was God who let my father see me. Like, ooooo…by now, I would be gone, and all this suffering would not be there. (Tearing eyes) (P M7, Age 44years, renal disease)

Feared loss of virility

I tried jumping from a rooftop once, when I first saw that I had the BP, Hmmm…I was just worried I was going to be taking this medicine for life, and I hear it will affect me too…you understand? (Smiles)…I mean my thing (Pointing to his manhood). And at that time, my first wife too had divorced me who will marry me? (PM 9, Age 45, 2 year, Hypertension)

These same patients confirmed not attempting suicide when they did not have the conditions. Therefore, it may be explained that chronicity could be a reason for contemplating suicide:

(Smiling) I never thought of that, that time, I was fine (laughing). I have money because I work, so I was just chilling, so how should I think of killing myself? (PM 7, Age 44, renal disease)

Me? (Smiles and shakes head) “Na wiase y3 me d3 papa” (I used to enjoy life), all I was doing was chilling, chilling, even my wife’ koraa’, I didn’t mind her. Me, kill myself? Ah sister. (PM 8, Age 46, Diabetes)

The ‘Chronicity’ and related issues of the condition

Three patients confirmed through their narrations that their chronic conditions made them feel suicidal. One of them expressed the thought of dying following the belief that her condition is incurable.

Hmmm…sometimes. My sister, I have been thinking ooo…it’s just God. If I think of all the pain, money and the ups and downs, then I keep asking why I'm worrying myself when they say it is chronic. (PM 7, Age 44, Renal Disease)

Another patient felt suicidal following an incapacity resulting from his chronic condition:

(Smiles) Oh… yes...! Even three days ago, I was trying to push my walker to visit the gents. But on my way, I fell. Lying down for more than an hour because I can’t walk and it was late. Everyone was asleep. I called for help, help, help, but nobody helped, so I stopped. Then a lady came, but she couldn’t carry me, so she called a man to carry me to my bed. In fact, that night, I wished I had died and left this world. Hmm… (Smiles) They say it is not good to kill yourself. They say it is not good for Christians to do that. I know I don’t have to do that, and God will help me. (PM 8, Male, Age 46, Diabetes).

For another patient, he felt suicidal because he believed the distress experienced in his past marriage created the chronic condition. The complex interrelationship between marital distress and his health is a bother. He seems to draw an active link between unstable marriage and poor health. This patient seems to express suicidal thoughts from the combined burden of chronicity and marital distress:

When I think about divorce, I just feel like just dying. Hmm…you don’t understand, do you know that my first wife left me and is now married to another officer? How would you have felt if it were you? Hmmm… If this one also goes, hmmm…there will be trouble. And all this sickness started just when she left. (PM 9, Age 45, Hypertension)

Health services related challenges

Some participants reported problems with their health services, which were analysed to examine the systematic level of negative experiences that chronic patients faced. They included health insurance challenges and poor attitudes of health workers.

Health insurance challenges

Two patients (End Stage Diabetes and Renal) and another with a very high cost of treatment also complained about the ineffectiveness of the National Health Insurance Scheme. Patient 2 admonished administrators and the government to look at including the treatment of these chronic conditions in the scheme.

Since I came here, it’s friends who have been helping me. Some will say, have this or that. That’s what I use to pay my debt. My problem now is how I will pay my bills when I’m discharged (PM 8, Age 46, Diabetes)

(Smiles & shakes his head)…… Insurance, in Ghana? …At this age, no elderly person in this country should pay for dialysis…So you are the social scientist, these are the things that young ones should start pressing for. You may not think about it, but you get old and realise that the whole system … the politicians are just milking … (looks stressed) us! When they do four years of politicking, they just take ex gratia (stressed) because he is a politicians and you, as a common worker, suffer…(PM 2, Age 73, Renal Failure)

Poor health worker attitude

Some participants also reported poor attitudes of health workers, including misplaced folders, disrespect, and poor caring attitudes. Patients narrated the various challenges that occurred, especially when it came to their care during treatment by nurses and sometimes doctors. In a particular setting, patient care & satisfaction were not a source of stress but were in the other. The narratives from patients indicated that they sometimes get frustrated as a result of the kind of care they were given. Narratives from the following patients confirm this:

As for the Doctors, I will say yes, but the nurses… they don’t have patience. You know it, and you are asking me (eyebrows raise and pointing at the interviewer). Sometimes your file… that you are looking for, becomes a problem. But I will say yes &no, it’s not all the nurses that are difficult. Some of them, as soon as they see you, will say Oh… “Masa woaba? (sir, you are here)” Sit down and let me get your file. But those who are not good… hmm… So sometimes I don’t give my file to them again. Ah! I carry my file away. Previously, it was not allowed, but now they allow us to carry our files home. Today my file is here (open his bag)… It’s here, it’s in my bag. (PM 2, Age 73, Renal Failure)

Hmmm…., I’m an officer, so if my people are not doing well, I will say it. In fact, most of them are not professional. You will be in bed and calling, but they will pretend not to have heard you. Some of them, anytime they check my BP, it’s high. Just because they are very annoying. Not just professionals. (PM 9, Age 45 Hypertension)

Coping

Participants expressed three major ways of coping with their chronic conditions: first through religiosity, second, through information about the condition, and third, through social support. For instance, a patient coping through religiosity said:

I accept God’s design. The only thing I do is to pray for God to lift me out of where I am now to heal me. Why should I think of harming myself? For what? I can’t take my life, I didn’t bring myself to this world, somebody did, and He is the only person who can take my life, why should I think of killing myself? If I'm gone, I’m gone, I don’t think about that. (PM2, Age 73 years, Renal Failure)

I keep wondering, so when will this end? I cry most of the time, but I always say why am I crying. If I cry, it means that I am not strong. I always tell myself that tears are a sign of weakness, and I’m not weak. I think I should get back on my feet; God is in control. My tears will mean that God has abandoned me, and He has no power. I shouldn’t make God feel bad, so I stop (smiling with almost-tearing eyes). As I said, God, it’s God, hmmmm…. (PF 1, Age 18, Renal Disease)

Information about the condition

With the knowledge about the condition, this patient is well informed about its chronicity and has embraced it; which helped him cope:

… So if you ask me what caused it, I will say it’s my age. Everybody has a threshold age. When you are about 15years old, your kidneys are perfect. At about 50, your kidney level drops and when you get to 60… it comes to stage 4 and becomes weak. That’s the way I understood it. I believe in God, that’s all. (PM 2, Age 73, Renal Failure)

Social support

The findings from the study indicated various forms of social support systems that helped patients cope. These include family, friends, health insurance, and some benevolent organisations.

The narratives by patients indicated that there were several forms of support systems that played very important roles in their treatment and health. Most patients who had very strong support systems were very optimistic about good health and had no ideation/or attempts of suicide. The few who had support systems, but the nature of the condition was draining the source of their support system, were among those who were contemplating suicide. Participants 1, 3 and 4 narrated that they cared for themselves, but when the need arose, their families, specifically the children and fathers, helped. All these patients were outpatients with mild forms of conditions.

Oh! … I drive. So I take care of myself. No family member or friend. Now that I am here, one of my children is driving my car. (PM 3, Male, Age 53, Hypertension)

I work, so I always use the money I get from the job. I sell … I sell edible and inedible things. I work at err…. Market. I don’t get help from the church. My father helps me, but his salary is not enough for me and my siblings’ education. That is why I work. (PF 1, Age 18years, SHS, Renal Disease)

Patient 4 indicated he gets help from friends and his family, too, and so he doesn’t feel any pressure on finances.

I have a good family that they are supporting me, and my children support me. Because I was a good person when I was not sick…(PM 4, Age 53, stroke, diabetes& hypertension)

From the narration of patient 7, it can be deduced that when the source of support is overburdened and not forthcoming, frustration sets in, which can lead to thoughts of suicidal behaviours.

It is my father who has been taking care of me ooooo, with his pension money ooooo. As for the insurance, it does not cover the dialysis, you have to pay. Errrrmm. Not really. One ooooo…it is my pensioner father ooooo. Hmm…he has been helping me with that with his small pension money. Ahaa…you know, sometimes some groups come here and they help those who don’t have the money. But that is not always the case. Even that one, when you are lucky, you get. (PM 7, Age 44, Renal Disease)

Though as Ghanaians, we claim to be very religious with a large Christian population, very little support comes from churches, as explained by Patient 2 below:

…church!? Sometimes, some churches don’t do anything. They will ask you to pay your tithe. I’m afraid I’m being very blunt. Some churches insist that, you pay your tithes…none of my church members has ever visited me. That’s the country we are in. Everybody is thinking about himself and his pocket. (PM 2, Age 73, Renal Failure).

Discussion

The study found that patients with strong social support systems, particularly from family and friends, tended to exhibit lower levels of suicidal behaviour. Chronic illness often renders patients physically incapacitated, limiting their ability to engage in meaningful employment and support themselves financially. As personal savings diminish and treatment costs rise, the emotional and psychological burden intensifies. However, those with robust support networks are better able to cope, drawing on financial assistance or emotional encouragement from their social circles. Social support serves as an effective coping mechanism for preventing suicidal behaviour and managing negative thoughts and feelings associated with suicide [44]. On the other hand, adaptive behaviour refers to the ways individuals meet their personal needs as well as deal with the natural and social demands in their environment [39]. This behaviour has been found to partially mediate the relationship between social support and emotional well-being to positively help former patients of prostate cancer [57]. Maladaptive behaviour refers to actions that hinder an individual's ability to function or adapt to specific settings, ranging from mild forms like nail biting and irritability to more severe forms such as self-injury or hypersexual behaviour [26]. Teaching adaptive coping skills can help individuals with chronic illness and their support networks manage the condition more effectively and reduce maladaptive behaviours such as self-harm, suicidal ideation, or suicide.

Arguably, patients recovering at home often experience milder forms of illness and benefit from greater social support from family and friends compared to those hospitalised. Inpatients typically lack consistent access to loved ones, and when visits are allowed, they are brief or limited to assisting with care due to systemic healthcare constraints. Yet, social connectedness remains a powerful coping mechanism, especially for individuals in pain or experiencing hopelessness [30]. In this study, the reasons provided by participants for their attempts or ideation of suicide include the burden of taking care of their wives and children, and in some instances, divorce threats from spouses.

The study revealed that some participants experienced suicidal ideation, with a few attempting suicide without completing it. Strong social support and religiosity emerged as key coping mechanisms, helping individuals across varying levels of chronic illness maintain a higher quality of life. In the African context, religion plays a particularly influential role in curbing suicidal behaviour, as major faiths such as Christianity, Islam, Judaism, and Hinduism strongly condemn suicide [24, 31].

The analysis revealed that the cost of treatment, particularly medication, was a major factor contributing to non-adherence among patients. In some cases, patients deliberately skipped medications due to exhaustion from strict dietary and medication regimens, limited access to treatment, and the financial burden associated with their condition. This finding is consistent with existing literature, which highlights financial constraints as a key barrier to adherence in chronic illness management [10, 52]. Similarly, Marshall, Wolfe, and McKevitt [35] also link non-adherence to treatment costs, appointment fees, and diet-related expenses.

Some patients attributed their non-adherence to the side effects of medication. While some believed antihypertensive drugs weakened them and reduced their ability to work, others, particularly men, linked these medications to impotence, a belief often influenced by lay perceptions. This aligns with findings by Beune et al. [14], who reported that hypertensive patients in Ghana associated non-adherence with structural, cultural, gender, and psychological factors, including financial constraints, fear of addiction, and fear of impotence. Intentional non-adherence can be life-threatening and, in some cases, may be considered a form of suicidal behaviour. Such actions are particularly dangerous in chronic disease management, as they have been linked to suicidal tendencies [28].

Findings from the study confirm that individuals living with chronic conditions are at increased risk of suicidal ideation and attempts. The burden of chronicity, characterised by physical limitations, financial strain, and emotional distress, was found to significantly impact patients’ sense of meaning and purpose. Many participants expressed that their diagnosis led to feelings of hopelessness, as the long-term nature of their illness threatened their employment, financial stability, ability to care for their children, and even their marriages. For some, death was perceived as a form of escape from these overwhelming challenges. Concerns around masculinity and virility also emerged as significant themes. Some male participants feared that the side effects of antihypertensive medications, particularly sexual dysfunction, would diminish their sense of manhood.

The study also highlighted the psychological toll of physical incapacity. One participant described feeling deeply humiliated when he fell at the hospital and needed help from a woman to get up, challenging his identity and dignity as a man. This perceived loss of autonomy and strength contributed to his suicidal thoughts. Ultimately, incurability, incapacity, and a diminished sense of self were key factors associated with suicidal behaviour among participants with chronic illnesses.

The complex relationship between marital distress and health also emerged as a significant concern. This combination of chronic illness and marital strain contributed to suicidal thoughts, aligning with findings by Singhal et al. [54] and Ahmedani et al. [6]. This outcome also supports earlier studies by de-Graft Aikins [19] and [23], who identified chronic illness as associated with financial hardship, life disruptions, and reduced mobility and productivity.

In this study, four out of nine participants reported either suicidal ideation or attempts. Of these, three were male and one female, contrasting with prior findings that suicidal ideation, planning, and attempts are typically higher in females [29, 37, 38]. Despite this, men are known to complete suicide at rates nearly three times higher than women, particularly in high-income countries [37]. Additionally, while literature reports that 9.1% of fatal and non-fatal suicides in Ghana from 2006 to 2008 involved adolescents [2], this study found the opposite. The adolescent participant expressed hope and dismissed suicide as an option, which may be attributed to strong religious beliefs and future aspirations.

Systemic issues in society also play a critical role, particularly within the exosystem level of Bronfenbrenner’s ecological model. Participants highlighted the inadequacies of Ghana’s National Health Insurance Scheme, especially its failure to cover key treatments like those for renal failure. As a result, patients face significant financial burdens and must rely on family, NGOs, or charitable support [23]. These financial strains contribute to feelings of burdensomeness, a known factor in suicidality [45, 46]. This suggests that life circumstances must be considered in the treatment and care of chronic conditions. Participants also called on national leaders to strengthen the National Health Insurance Scheme, particularly to better support the elderly, who they felt deserved care in recognition of their contributions to national development.

Another major theme was the challenge of poor health service delivery, particularly the attitudes of health workers. Many participants reported that, instead of receiving compassionate care, they experienced mistreatment, particularly from nurses, which compounded their stress. This reflects systemic failures in the health sector, as identified by Agyemang et al. [4] and [23], who noted that health professionals are often not adequately trained in diagnosing and managing chronic health conditions. This aligns with Bronfenbrenner’s (1979) ecological model, which views behaviour as shaped by interactions within nested environmental systems—including hospitals. Thus, negative patient experiences within health institutions can directly influence suicidal behaviours.

In summary, poor health services and negative worker attitudes, combined with the burden of chronic illness, inadequate insurance coverage, and systemic inefficiencies, can heighten emotional distress and increase the risk of suicidal behaviour in patients with chronic conditions.

Recommendation and implications for practice and policy

Attribution can be made to chronic conditions as risk factors of suicide in Ghana. These risk factors are largely found in the ecosystem that is beyond the individual patient. The following recommendations are made for the prevention and mitigation of suicide behaviours.

Since most risk factors are institutional, the Ministry of Health (Ghana) should provide in-service training for health workers to raise awareness of the challenges faced by chronic patients. This would encourage more compassionate care, reduce additional stress and lower the risk of suicidal behaviours. Additionally, the Ministry should facilitate increased contact between hospitalised patients, their families, and religious groups to strengthen social support. Furthermore, programs should be implemented to equip chronic kidney patients with the skills and adaptive behaviours necessary for effective self-management of their conditions.

Intensifying public education on chronic health conditions is essential. Encouraging moderation and healthy lifestyle practices can help prevent many of these largely lifestyle-related diseases. Additionally, psychologists, social workers, and other mental health professionals should strengthen collaborative efforts within hospitals, establishing multidisciplinary teams where none exist, to improve the treatment and care of patients with chronic conditions. The high rate of suicide in the country demands intensified public education. Health sector journalists should be trained on suicide awareness and encouraged to increase coverage of the rising incidence of chronic conditions and their link to suicide. Media efforts should also emphasise the importance of reducing lifestyle factors that contribute to chronic diseases.

Strengths and limitations

This study has some strengths and limitations. Qualitative analysis thoroughly examined the factors and experiences of people with chronic illnesses and suicidality. The present study provides a foundation for future psychological research on suicide and chronicity in Ghana. The study's limitations include a small sample size, which could affect generalisation to a large population, and the lack of consideration of biological or psychological factors like anxiety, stress, or depression as risk factors for chronic patient suicide. The study's findings may be skewed due to the lack of representation of other religious faiths among the majority of participants. Future studies to explore religion as a key variable, purposively sampling participants by faith to better understand how different religious beliefs support coping with chronic kidney disease.

Conclusion

Suicide remains a critical public health issue in Ghana, particularly among individuals with chronic conditions such as chronic kidney disease. Stakeholders, including the Ministry of Health and Ghana Health Service, need to recognise the heightened suicide risk in this population. Expanding and regularly supporting crisis intervention services within health centres is vital to effectively reduce both the incidence of chronic illnesses and associated suicidal behaviours.
