Beyond the Bud: new cannabis products and modes of consumption in Ontario, Canada’s legal hybrid market
Authors: Chelsea Noël, Shawn Dookie, Deborah M. Scharf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCanada is home to a very active commercial legal cannabis market, offering insight into the opportunities and challenges posed by rapid cannabis commercialization under a hybrid regulatory model. This environmental scan provides a descriptive snapshot of available cannabis products, modes of consumption, and their advertised product potencies.MethodsResearchers extracted cannabis product type, mode(s) of consumption, and drug (THC, CBD) potency information from the online inventories of ten large Ontario, Canada cannabis retailers. Products and modes were categorized according to Canadian Cannabis Survey (CCS) categories where applicable.ResultsOntario retailers sell cannabis products including dried flower, hash/hashish, oils, vape pens, concentrates, edibles, beverages and topicals. Drug potencies varied within and across product types and were reported in non-standardized units. Modes of consumption included inhalation (smoking/vaping/dabbing of a range of flower and concentrate products), oral ingestion (eating, drinking), mucosal absorption (sublingual, vaginal) and topical, transdermal applications. We identified new products, terms, and features (e.g. multimodal products), not described in resources informed by the CCS.ConclusionsOntario’s hybrid cannabis retail environment demonstrates substantial product and labeling diversity. These findings underscore the importance of adaptable surveillance frameworks capable of capturing evolving products, modes, and potency reporting practices. Background

When Canada legalized recreational cannabis in 2018, it became the first G7 nation to regulate non-medical cannabis use at a national level [1, 2]. While the federal Cannabis Act established broad objectives to protect youth, reduce illicit supply, and promote public health, implementation details were delegated to provinces and territories [1, 3]. As a result, retail systems now vary widely across Canadian jurisdictions, from fully public to private and hybrid models, each with differing degrees of control, enforcement, and oversight over cannabis retail sales [4, 5]. In hybrid systems, where both government and private operators sell cannabis, the push for profit can blur the boundaries between regulation and commercialization, echoing challenges long seen in alcohol and tobacco markets with similar regulatory models [6, 7]. This competitive retail market has also incentivized cannabis companies to flood the market with diverse products to secure their market share. Products now widely available through government-approved retailers extend far beyond traditional smokable flower and leaf to include varied modes of consumption such as vaping, dabbing, edibles, topicals, oils and tinctures [8].

In this paper, we provide a snapshot of cannabis products and consumption methods available for legal purchase in Ontario – Canada’s most populous province. Ontario provides a particularly instructive case to governments and policymakers in earlier stages of cannabis policy decision-making because it operates under a hybrid system where the government manages online sales but allows private retailers to dominate storefront operations. Its relatively high population density, hybrid retail model, and concentration of licensed cannabis producers (LCPs) have created a competitive retail landscape with over 1,700 stores as of 2024 (roughly one per 8,800 residents), compared to one per 100,000 persons in the neighboring province of Québec [9–14]. This has enabled rapid product development, including marketing of potentially harmful cannabis products and modes targeting youth, such as high-potency cannabis concentrates, multi-route products, and youth-oriented formats such as flavored edibles and beverages [14, 15]. Despite federal rules restricting promotion, industry actors continue to test and exploit regulatory loopholes, mirroring early patterns of alcohol and tobacco commercialization [16, 17]. Ontario thus provides a case study of product and mode diversity within a hybrid regulatory structure, illustrating that even within a federally regulated environment, local conditions may accelerate commercialization at a pace that challenges public health systems to keep pace.

National cannabis surveillance tools (e.g., the Canadian Cannabis Survey [CCS]) [18] provide valuable insights into the trajectory of provincial cannabis markets, but surveys take time to create, update, administer, and analyze, making it difficult to track emerging trends in real time. This includes the nimble and responsive development and testing parameters to comprehensively assess novel cannabis product types and use modalities (e.g., edibles, capsules, tinctures, sprays, vapes, and concentrate “dab” rigs), potency information, and multimodal cannabis use as they are introduced to the commercial market. This data is needed to understand cannabis’ psychoactive effects, onset, duration of action, and risk for cannabis poisoning (e.g., from over-consumption of edibles and extracts in particular, whose effects occur more slowly over time) and how to minimize public health risks [19–21].

The purpose of this paper is to describe what is available for purchase through Ontario, Canada’s legal cannabis retail market focusing on the range of available product types, modes of consumption, and product potency profiles. We do this to identify implications for public health surveillance, local interest holders, and decision-makers in other jurisdictions considering a hybrid regulatory framework. Data are from a rapid environmental scan of 10 large Ontario cannabis retailers’ online inventories and include visual and descriptive profiles of the range of available cannabis product types, modalities, and their associated tetrahydrocannabidiol (THC) and cannabidiol (CBD) profiles. Findings can facilitate data-informed conversations about cannabis regulation, health, risk, treatment considerations, and safer cannabis use in a variety of care environments.

Methods

We scanned and extracted data from the online inventories of Ontario’s top ten cannabis retailers, by store count, as listed in the Ontario Cannabis Store (OCS) Insights report (March 2022) [12]. We used store count because detailed retail sales data are not publicly available [22], and because store count captured chains with the largest physical presence in the province at the time of data collection.

For each of the top ten retailers, we further selected store locations across six geographic regions of Ontario to capture variation in population density, urbanicity, and retail market context. The six regions included: the Greater Toronto Area (GTA), Toronto, Eastern Ontario, Western Ontario, Northern Ontario, and Northwestern Ontario (Thunder Bay and District). The goal was to capture heterogeneity in retail environments rather than to generate a statistically representative provincial sample.

Across the ten retailers and six regions, 60 potential store locations were identified (6 regions x 10 retailers). Of these, 49 (81.7%) brick-and-mortar locations maintained active online inventories which were included in the final scan. Where retailers operated multiple stores within a selected region, we chose the location situated in the area with the highest surrounding population density to maximize the likelihood of capturing comprehensive and diverse product inventories.

The three general categories of data that we extracted from online retail inventories were cannabis product type, mode(s) of consumption, and range of THC and CBD potencies. Where possible, products were classified according to the categories used in the CCS [18]. These included product types of 1) dried flower/leaf, 2) hashish/kief, 3) cannabis oil for oral use, 4) cannabis vape pens/cartridges, 5) cannabis concentrates/extracts, 6) cannabis edible products, 7) cannabis beverages, 8) topicals, and 9) other (not captured). Where product labeling indicated use under the tongue or buccally (e.g., sublingual sprays, strips, or packets), these were categorized separately from orally ingested oils to reflect differences in route of consumption and potential pharmacokinetic profile. Similarly, transdermal patches were categorized separately from topical preparations (e.g., creams, lotions, bath products) because transdermal products are designed for systemic absorption rather than localized application. These distinctions were retained to reflect functional differences in consumption and to highlight emerging formats that may not be clearly differentiated within existing surveillance categories.

The minimum and maximum THC and CBD potency values for each cannabis product type were documented across all retailers, except one whose online platform had no function to filter or sort products by THC and CBD potency. For each product listed within selected store inventories, we extracted THC and CBD potency information as displayed on retailer websites. Potency values were recorded in the units provided by retailers, including percent (%), milligrams (mg), milligrams per millilitre (mg/mL), miligrams per gram (mg/g) and grams (g). Where potency was reported per individual unit (e.g., mg per piece, mg per capsule), per package, or per volume, values were recorded as presented (i.e., without conversion to standardized units). No independent laboratory testing was conducted; potency values reflect retailer-reported data.

Modes of consumption were also classified according to the categories used in the CCS [18] including: 1) smoking, 2) edibles, 3) ingestible oils, 4) vaporizing using a vape pen or e-cigarette, 5) drinking, 6) dabbing, 7) applying to skin, plus 8) other (not captured).

Data was collected by two coders for each retailer location, and then double-screened to ensure inter-rater reliability. Any discrepancies or disagreements were discussed and resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. “Other” (not captured) products and modes of use were described according to packaging and labelling language used by manufacturers and/or retailers, and new categories of emerging products and modes of use are described. Data were collected between January and August 2025.

Results

In what follows, we present the cannabis product types, modes of use, and potencies of products identified on n = 49 (81.7%) retail location websites of 10 total Ontario retailers.

Cannabis product types

Across the 49 included retail locations, dried flower/leaf, vape pens/cartridges, edible products, and ingestible oils were available at all stores (100%). Hash/kief, beverages, and topicals were available at nearly all locations (≥97%). Concentrates/extracts were available at 93.9% of stores. Products categorized as “other” (not captured by existing CCS categories) were identified at 79.6% of locations, representing the least consistently available product type.

Within categories, dried flower and vape products were the most consistently stocked product types across retailers. Products classified as “other,” including inhalers, Rick Simpson Oil (RSO), multi-route dabbing syringes, sublingual packets/strips, toothpaste, and transdermal patches, were present in fewer stores. Products that did not clearly fall under existing CCS categories included inhalers (metered-dose devices delivering aerosolized cannabis), Rick Simpson Oil (RSO; highly concentrated oil often marketed for multi-route administration), multi-route dabbing syringes labeled for inhalation, vaporization, or infusion into food and beverages, sublingual packets and strips designed for mucosal absorption, pouch-style sublingual products intended to be held in the mouth for extended release (similar in format to nicotine pouches), cannabis-infused toothpaste, and transdermal patches designed for sustained systemic delivery through the skin. These formats differ in structure, route of administration, or intended pharmacokinetic profile from traditional flower, edible, oil, or topical categories and illustrate diversification of cannabis product types beyond those explicitly differentiated within current CCS classifications.

Potency

THC and CBD product potency values varied markedly across location, retailer, and product type (Appendix Tables 2–3). Among traditionally inhaled products, concentrate formats demonstrated the highest THC concentrations. Diamonds ranged from 30% to 96% THC, shatter from 38% to 91%, and live resin from 58% to 87%. Vape cartridges ranged from 1% to 100% THC, while disposable vape pens ranged from 0.17% to 98%. Hash products ranged from 22% to 83% THC, with rosin ranging from 0% to 86%. Infused pre-roll products ranged from 0% to 66% THC, whereas dried flower (dry, milled, ground) ranged from 0% to 90% THC. The upper values within the flower category likely reflect mislabeled infused products rather than non-infused dried flower.

In contrast, edible and beverage products largely reported THC in milligrams per unit. Edible baked goods, chocolates, and candies typically ranged from 0.00 mg to 10.00 mg THC per unit, consistent with federal per-unit limits, although total package milligram values varied. Cannabis beverages ranged from 0% to 12% THC in percent-based reporting, with milligram values generally between 0.00 mg and 10.00 mg per unit.

Topical products frequently reported low or zero THC content, with milligram values generally below 75 mg per container, although some topical gels reported higher total milligram values.

CBD potency also demonstrated substantial variation. Within flower products, CBD ranged from 0% to 60%. In oil-based products (e.g., droppers/syringes), CBD ranged from 0% to 4189.00 mg total per container, reflecting differences in reporting format (percent concentration versus total milligrams per bottle). Capsule, tablet, and soft gel formats ranged from 0 mg to 6000 mg total CBD per container. Concentrate products generally reported low CBD relative to THC, with diamonds ranging from 0% to 20% CBD and shatter from 0% to 10% CBD. Topical products showed particularly wide CBD ranges, with roll-on or lotion products ranging from 20 mg to 2000 mg per container, and topical gels reporting total CBD values up to 2000 mg.

Across categories, products were available in THC-dominant, CBD-dominant, and balanced formulations. THC-dominant products were most common among inhaled concentrates and vape formats, whereas CBD-dominant or CBD-only products were more frequently observed in oil, capsule, and topical formats.

Inconsistencies in labeling formats and unit presentation limited direct comparability across product types and retailers. Potency was also reported using multiple units, including percent (%), milligrams (mg), milligrams per millilitre (mg/mL), grams (g) and mg/g. Reporting formats also differed, with some products listing potency per individual unit (e.g., mg per piece, capsule, or drop), other per total package, and others per product volume. Values exceeding 100 reflect total milligram content per container rather than percent concentration. These variations highlight heterogeneity in how potency information is displayed with the legal retail market at the time of data collection.

Modes of consumption

All 49 retail locations (100%) offered products intended for smoking, ingestible oil use, edible consumption, and vaporizing via vape pen or e-cigarette. Drinking formats (e.g., beverages, drink mixes) were available at 98% of locations, while products intended for topical application were available at 88% of stores. Dabbing-related products were available at 78% of locations.

Modes categorized as “other” (not clearly differentiated within existing CCS categories) were observed at 65% of stores, representing the least consistently available grouping. These included inhalers, sublingual packets and strips, pouch-style mucosal products, toothpaste, and transdermal patches.

Traditional inhalation (smoking and vaporizing) and ingestion (edibles and oils) were the most consistently represented modes across retailers. However, the presence of mucosal (sublingual and pouch-style) products and transdermal formats illustrates diversification beyond the modes explicated in current national surveillance tools.

Discussion

This environmental scan of 49 retail locations across 10 of Ontario, Canada’s largest retailer chains provides a descriptive snapshot of the province’s legal market, capturing the breadth of both traditional and novel cannabis product types, modes of consumption, and drug potencies available at the time of data collection. Across all retailers, dried flower, vape pens/cartridges, edibles, and ingestible oils were widely available, alongside a substantial presence of concentrates, beverages, topicals, and emerging product types that were not yet captured by national surveillance tools used at the time of extraction. Modes of administration extended well beyond smoking to include vaping, ingestion, drinking, dabbing, and topical application, and included products marketed for more than one route (i.e., mode) of administration (e.g., products labeled for inhalation and oral infusion). THC and CBD potency ranged widely across products, but inconsistent labeling and data access precluded systematic product comparisons. These findings are consistent with previous calls for standard units of potency in the cannabis retail market [23]. These findings are especially relevant to regulators and healthcare providers who must make decisions about potency limits and guide the public in understanding safe cannabis use and dosing in Ontario’s increasingly complex and evolving cannabis landscape.

The scan also revealed a mismatch between current market realities and public health surveillance tools. Specifically, we identified the need for additional categories to document examples of newer concentrate products (e.g., diamonds, sugar, live resin, sauce) as well as examples of newer topical methods (e.g., bath products). Our findings suggest that additional categories may be needed to describe products for newer modes of consumption such as inhalers, sublingual packets, strips and spray, toothpaste, and transdermal patches. Multi-route dabbing products and RSO presented a particular classification challenge due to their hybrid functionality (i.e., they can be used in a variety of ways). Dabbing typically involves inhaling vapourized liquid cannabis concentrate. Some dabbing liquid is now being marketed as multi-route. For example, our scan identified a syringe of dabbing distillate that can be dabbed, vaped, infused in a beverage or mixed with other cannabis products [24]. This has potential regulatory implications, because while the bioavailability of inhaled cannabis may be consistent—35% approximately—whether vaping or dabbing, lab testing reveals the THC potency of dabbing liquid tends to be much stronger, around 76%, when compared to vaping liquid, which shows a THC potency of approximately 26% [25]. Differences in bioavailability and potency when the product is ingested further illustrate the complexity of classifying and communication risk for multi-route products.

Lastly, we identified inconsistencies between how retailers categorized and described available products and modes. For example, sublingual packets, strips and sprays were labelled as “edibles” or “edible oils”, and “concentrate vaporizers” were sometimes labelled as “dab rigs”. These findings highlight the need to be nimble and responsive in updating monitoring tools (e.g., CCS, National Cannabis Survey) and data collection methods (e.g., self-report surveys, market analysis) to better reflect current market realities that affect policymaker and healthcare provider decisions.

An important public health question is how the proliferation of diverse product types and modes of consumption may intersect with patterns of use among younger populations, including Ontario youth ages 19–25 who, according to Canadian Medical Association and the American Academy of Pediatrics, should not use any cannabis due to ongoing brain development [26, 27]. Ontario’s current commercial market shows a significant shift from traditional consumption methods (e.g., smoking dried flower) to a broad array of novel products, such as edibles—in youth-appealing flavours like strawberry and bubble-gum, concentrates consumed through youth-appealing devices and packaging such as colourful disposable and rechargeable vaporizers, beverages, topicals, and sublingually applied products (e.g., packets, strips, sprays), unlike those that have historically typified cannabis use. These findings align with recent U.S. studies from cannabis-legalized jurisdictions, which have documented a similar proliferation of product types and increasing popularity of high-potency, processed forms of cannabis among the youngest legal age users. [8, 15, 28] In comparison to other government-only retail models in Canada (e.g., Québec’s), which operate through centralized purchasing and publicly managed storefronts, Ontario’s hybrid retail structure may facilitate greater product diversification and retail density and may potentially accelerate these trends, raising questions for public health surveillance regarding exposure to youth-appealing, novel, high-potency products and potentially harmful modes of administration among high-risk groups. Although this study did not directly analyze product inventory in other jurisdictions, prior descriptions of government-only models suggest more constrained retail expansion and product assortment. The findings from Ontario may provide useful contextual insight for jurisdictions considering cannabis legalization, particularly those considering commercial or hybrid regulatory models. Empirical province-to-province comparisons of these regulatory models will be informative.

Data from our study suggest that the next steps for Ontario and other jurisdictions considering a hybrid commercial and regulatory approach are to expect and accept a diverse and rapidly evolving range of products and modes of consumption available for legal purchase, including minimum legal age youth (i.e., those just newly able to legally purchase cannabis). Decision-makers should also anticipate challenges in ensuring that surveillance and monitoring systems can effectively capture the diversity of product types, modes of administration, and potency reporting formats present in the legal market. We agree with previous calls [23] for regulators’ enforcement of standardized information on product labels, such as pharmacological features (e.g., standardized dose definitions, onset and duration of action), and effect (e.g., what happens when taken alone, or with other products, or by adjusting the route), which may support public health and safety through clearer consumer understanding. Jurisdictions should also plan for additional resources for public health and surveillance, as traditional surveys and other methods can benefit from supplementation with faster, more nimble approaches to understanding what, how much, and how people (including special populations, such as young adults) are using new-to-the-market cannabis products. The number of knowledge gaps that we identified in our data compared to Canada’s national monitoring strategy suggests that online environmental scans could be one such useful approach. Importantly, because we did not include direct comparison of Ontario’s retail market with government-only or fully private retail systems, we acknowledge that we cannot determine the extent to which observed product and mode diversity, sales volume, consumer purchasing patterns or demographic uptake is attributable to retail model structure versus broader national industry trends. Interprovincial comparisons, using similar approaches across different retail models are needed to attribute observed product diversity to Ontario’s hybrid structure, specifically.

Limitations

This scan focused on Ontario’s top ten cannabis retailers, by store count, with online inventories. While these retailers likely represent a large portion of the legal retail market, they may not reflect the full diversity of smaller retailers, retailers without online inventories, inter-provincial cannabis sales, or the illegal cannabis market.

This study documents product availability and labeling characteristics at a single point in time and does not assess product sales volume, consumer purchasing behaviour, marketing exposure, or demographic patterns of use. It is also limited to the Ontario context, which has a unique retail environment (i.e., high outlet density, hybrid sales model), and findings may not generalize to provinces (e.g., Québec or the Atlantic provinces) or other international jurisdictions with different retail or regulatory contexts. As such, conclusions about product popularity, consumer demand, or differential uptake across age groups cannot be drawn from these data. Integration of retail sales data and population-based survey data would be required to assess these outcomes.

Although locations and retailers were selected based on the last publicly available data provided through the Ontario Cannabis Store, this study represents a cross-sectional snapshot in time of available products and modes of consumption as categorized by the CCS and may not capture rapidly evolving product inventories, modes of consumption, seasonal product rotations, or shifts in retailer market share. This approach may not capture additional complexities of modes of consumption, products, or product features, such as multi-modal use of products. The top ten retailers, by store count, may have also shifted since the publication of the Ontario Cannabis Stores Insights report in 2022.

Moreover, while Ontario operates under a hybrid retail model, this study did not include direct comparison with provinces operating under government-only or fully private retail systems. Therefore, we cannot determine the extent to which observed product diversity is attributable to Ontario’s retail structure versus broader national industry trends. Comparative interprovincial research is necessary to examine how retail model structure may influence product availability and market complexity.

Lastly, potency values were recorded as reported on retailer websites and reflected multiple reporting formats, including percent concentration, milligrams per unit, milligrams per gram, milligrams per millilitre, and total milligrams per container. Because reporting formats were not standardized across product categories, full harmonization to a single comparable unit (e.g., milligrams per dose) was not consistently feasible without introducing assumptions beyond the available data. As such, potency findings reflect labeling variability rather than standardized cross-product comparison.

Conclusions

Ontario’s cannabis retail market illustrates the opportunities and risks that can emerge in a commercial, hybrid legalization model. The results of this rapid scan illustrate and describe the breadth and diversity of cannabis products, modes of consumption, and drug doses available to consumers through Ontario’s major cannabis retailers. Findings reveal a varied and evolving market including traditional and novel product types such as high-potency concentrates, infused edibles, and beverages. The widespread availability of high-potency, youth-appealing, multimodal products, combined with inconsistent potency labeling and classification, presents significant challenges for public health surveillance and clinical care. As legal cannabis markets continue to evolve globally, Ontario provides an early case study of how product innovation and commercialization can challenge existing regulatory and monitoring systems. We present a new and useful approach for quickly and efficiently assessing, updating and disseminating critical information about the full range of cannabis products, modes of administration, and product potencies available in the public marketplace. We also offer a forward-looking perspective for other jurisdictions, illustrating how cannabis market expansion may complicate efforts to monitor and regulate emerging products effectively. To respond, public health systems may benefit from developing and applying nimble, efficient and adaptive tools for surveillance, classification, and clinical education, particularly in rapidly evolving or hybrid cannabis markets.
