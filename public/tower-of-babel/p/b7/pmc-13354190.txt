Beyond fingerprint and voice print: cough sequence sound for identity verification
Authors: Haomiao Li, Siyan Wang, Linh He, Joseph Colonel, Robin Zhao, Anaïs Rameau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional speaker verification systems rely on speech-based features such as fundamental frequency, resonance, and spectral characteristics. In clinical and remote monitoring settings where patients may be unable or unwilling to speak, non-speech sounds like coughs could provide a passive biometric signal for identity verification. While prior research has shown that individual coughs carry identity-specific features, most studies rely on single, voluntary coughs collected in controlled settings, limiting generalizability. Here, we investigated whether cough sequences contain sufficient information for speaker verification. We used the Coswara dataset, which contains 2746 cough recordings. The ECAPA-TDNN (Emphasized Channel Attention, Propagation, and Aggregation in Time Delay Neural Network) model, pre-trained on VoxCeleb, was adapted for cough-specific features. Speaker embeddings were extracted using SpeechBrain. Rejection rates were calculated by comparing shallow and heavy coughs from the same user, while false acceptance rates were computed by comparing a user’s shallow cough with another’s heavy cough. Equal Error Rate (EER) balanced false acceptance and rejection rates, while the F1 score measured precision and recall. We achieved an EER of 13.39% with an F1 score of 0.8559, indicating strong performance. Cosine distance between model embeddings effectively distinguished between genuine and impostor cough samples, confirming the model’s ability to verify identity using cough sounds. We demonstrate the feasibility of using cough sequences for speaker verification, highlighting coughs as a viable signal for identity verification.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing, Neuroscience

Introduction

Speaker verification (SV) is the process of accepting or rejecting a speaker’s claimed identity based on their vocal characteristics1,2. Traditionally, SV systems rely on speech-based features, including but not limited to fundamental frequency, resonance, and spectral characteristics of human voices, to verify speaker identity3. However, when speech is unavailable or unreliable, such as for individuals who are unable to vocalize, non-speech biometric verification methods may be an alternative. Respiratory sounds such as cough represent one potential contactless biometric signal that could support emerging applications in remote patient monitoring, longitudinal symptom tracking, and infection-conscious clinical environments where conventional biometric approaches (e.g., fingerprint or facial recognition) may be limited4–7. Prior work has also demonstrated that cough acoustics encode demographic characteristics such as gender, suggesting that respiratory sounds may contain more identity-relevant structure than previously assumed8. In these settings, cough acoustics may provide an additional modality for secure digital health access and identity verification.

In parallel with these technical opportunities, the use of cough recordings in digital health applications and respiratory disease screening tools raises important privacy and ethical considerations. This question is particularly relevant in infectious disease screening contexts, where respiratory audio collected for public health monitoring could unintentionally enable re-identification of individuals within shared datasets6,9,10. Physiologic sounds such as cough are not typically treated as identifying biometric signals; however, if cough acoustics encode stable person-specific information, they may function as an unintended biometric modality. Understanding whether cough recordings contain identity-relevant information is therefore important not only for further developing verification tools but also for informing responsible collection, storage, and use of respiratory audio data in digital health systems.

Recent studies have explored the use of non-speech sounds, such as coughs, as alternative biometric signals for speaker recognition and verification, with reported performance generally falling within an Equal Error Rate (EER) range of 10%–15%11–15. EER is a standard evaluation metric that reflects the point where the rates of false acceptance and false rejection are equal; lower EER values correspond to higher overall accuracy. For context, state-of-the-art speech-based speaker verification systems typically achieve much lower EERs, around 2.87% to 3.22%, on standard benchmarks such as VoxCeleb11. While the performance of cough-based verification currently trails behind these traditional speech-based methods, these findings support the idea that cough sounds still encode individual-specific acoustic features that may be leveraged for identity verification.

Prior research on cough-based identity verification has primarily used voluntary single coughs collected under controlled conditions, such as those intentionally produced by participants upon instruction, typically in a structured or clinical setting2,4,13,16. While this approach enables reliable data collection, a single cough may not fully capture the acoustic and physiological variability inherent in natural coughing behavior, which often occurs in sequences (multiple coughs on one breath), suggesting that modeling sequential coughs may offer a more realistic basis for identity verification.

Additionally, current approaches to cough-based identity verification face major challenges related to the need for large, diverse datasets of cough sounds. Most datasets contain multiple cough sound samples from a small number of subjects (<100) due to challenges with data collection at scale, and many dataset quality are limited by the variability in data collection protocols. There are also regulatory constraints related to privacy and security15,17–21. The recording quality variability introduced by health status, face coverings during the COVID-19 pandemic, and background noise in various recording environments further complicates acoustic data quality and standardization, leading to datasets with limited potential for generalizable algorithms17–20.

In terms of modeling, many systems rely on traditional classification methods such as Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA). LDA is a statistical method used for dimensionality reduction and classification. It projects high-dimensional data onto a lower-dimensional space that best separates different classes, which requires retraining for each new user, making LDA-based systems impractical for large-scale or continuously expanding systems17. To address the existing limitations in using cough sounds for SV, recent studies have focused on improving feature extraction techniques to generate more effective embedding, which is a compact digital fingerprint that captures the unique features of that person’s cough sound as vectors for identity verification tasks21,22.

In this study, we hypothesized that using sequential cough sounds in SV would allow the capture of unique temporal and acoustic patterns that would lead to a robust and generalizable identity representation. We focused on sequential coughs rather than isolated single coughs due to the nature of our dataset. To test this, we trained the model using a binary classification objective on pairwise similarity scores. Specifically, the model learned to distinguish between genuine and impostor pairs of cough embeddings by minimizing a logistic loss. This approach allowed the model to distinguish between individuals by comparing similar and dissimilar examples, even when only limited data is available. Our model built on ECAPA-TDNN, an advanced neural network originally developed for SV3,12. It extended the Time-Delay Neural Network (TDNN) framework, which was first used in the 1980s to analyze time-varying sounds like speech for tasks including phoneme recognition and temporal pattern modeling23. Earlier studies have used TDNN-F, a lightweight model optimized for low-resource devices, to track temporal changes in cough sounds for disease detection tasks24–26. However, none of these efforts were directed at identifying the individual producing the cough. To our knowledge, this study is the first to apply ECAPA-TDNN to cough-based identity verification. Its ability to capture subtle, person-specific patterns in short audio sequences makes it well-suited for this task25.

Methods

Dataset

The Coswara dataset was developed during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 by researchers at the Indian Institute of Science (IISc), Bangalore. It comprises audio recordings collected using consumer-grade devices under real-world recording conditions from over 2100 individuals and includes various respiratory sounds such as breathing, coughing, and speech samples (e.g., counting numbers). The cough recordings are further categorized into shallow (gentle) and heavy (forceful) types. Each participant contributed both shallow and heavy coughs. Specifically, the shallow category comprises a sequence of voluntary coughs with limited effort, while the heavy cough includes a sequence of voluntary coughs involving greater effort27. This structured approach allows for a more nuanced analysis of cough patterns and improves speaker verification tasks beyond isolated cough events.

Data preprocessing

Before training, raw audio was standardized for consistency. All files were loaded using the torchaudio library and resampled to 16 kHz in preparation for input to deep learning systems. Multi-channel audio files were downmixed to mono by averaging across channels, which simplified the input while preserving speaker-relevant features28.

Model architecture and improvement

The core of the proposed speaker verification system was the spkrec-ecapa-voxceleb model, a pretrained neural network based on the ECAPA-TDNN architecture implemented in the SpeechBrain library29. SpeechBrain is an open-source, all-in-one speech processing toolkit designed to support tasks like speech recognition, speaker verification, diarization, enhancement, and separation. While this model was originally trained on spoken voice data and has not been specifically trained on cough sounds, prior work suggests that speech-trained networks can generalize to non-verbal sounds like coughs with additional adaptation30,31. Here, we adapted the model using the Coswara dataset to improve its ability to extract speaker-specific features from cough recordings.

To prevent data leakage and ensure valid evaluation, we divided the Coswara dataset into three speaker-disjoint subsets, such that no individual appeared in more than one set. We designated 799 participants (collected between 2021-08-16 and 2022-02-24) as the test set, selecting the most recent samples to more closely approximate real-world deployment, where models are evaluated on newly collected, previously unseen users. This set was strictly held out and only used during the final evaluation phase. From the remaining participants, we randomly sampled 1262 cough recordings and split them into training and validation sets using an 80/20 ratio. The training set was used to fine-tune the model, while the validation set was used to monitor performance, select the best model checkpoint, and determine the optimal decision threshold. This strategy avoided the risk of the model memorizing speaker-specific patterns from the training data and artificially inflating performance and leaking user information.

We fine-tuned the ECAPA-TDNN model using cough audio recordings to enhance its ability to extract speaker-specific embeddings from non-speech signals. To adapt the model for cough-based identification, we enabled gradient updates for the embedding and classifier modules of the pretrained model, allowing them to better learn cough-specific speaker characteristics while leveraging general acoustic knowledge learned from large-scale speech corpora.

To construct meaningful verification pairs, we used the shallow and heavy cough recordings from each participant in the Coswara dataset. A positive (genuine) pair consisted of a shallow cough and a heavy cough from the same speaker, while a negative (impostor) pair combined the shallow cough from one speaker with a heavy cough from a different individual. Each cough was encoded into an embedding vector, representing that individual’s cough “fingerprint.” The model then computed cosine similarity between pairs of embeddings: higher similarity scores indicated likely matches (same speaker), while lower scores suggested mismatched identities32,33. We specifically designed our evaluation protocol to focus exclusively on cross-type pairs (shallow–heavy). This design choice was primarily dictated by the structural constraints of the Coswara dataset, where each participant provided exactly one continuous recording of shallow coughs and one of heavy coughs. Forming within-type pairs by splitting a single recording would introduce channel bias (e.g., shared noise and microphone characteristics), artificially inflating performance. By utilizing cross-type pairs, which originate from two distinct recording prompts, we mitigate intra-session channel bias and provide a more rigorous test of whether the model has learned robust, effort-independent speaker-discriminative features.

The training objective followed a binary classification approach. Cosine similarity scores were computed for each pair, and a binary cross-entropy loss was applied to encourage higher scores for genuine pairs and lower scores for impostor pairs.

This pairwise verification framework allowed the model to learn discriminative embeddings by directly comparing positive and negative examples during training. This approach eliminated the need to retrain the model when new users are introduced, as it did not rely on fixed speaker classes but instead on direct comparison of embeddings.

We used a conservative learning rate (1e-5) during training to make only gentle adjustments to the model’s internal parameters. This helped preserve useful knowledge from its previous training on speech, while allowing it to learn the unique characteristics of cough sounds. We trained the model over 10 epochs. After each training epoch, we monitored performance on a held-out validation set using binary cross-entropy loss. The model checkpoint with the lowest validation loss across all epochs was saved and used for final testing. Once training was complete, the model was able to convert cough sounds into a unique speaker embedding and suggest whether two cough data came from the same person.

Evaluation measures

Model performance was evaluated using two standard speaker verification metrics: Equal Error Rate (EER) and F1 Score. Equal Error Rate (EER) is a standard performance metric in speaker verification that reflects the point at which the model makes an equal rate of two types of errors: the false acceptance rate (FAR), where different speakers are incorrectly accepted as the same, and the false rejection rate (FRR), where the same speaker is incorrectly rejected as different. EER is calculated by adjusting the similarity threshold until FAR and FRR are equal. A lower EER means better overall performance. Unlike traditional approaches that aim to maximize accuracy, EER finds the point where errors on both sides are balanced. On the ROC curve, which plots the True Acceptance Rate (TAR), how often the model correctly accepts trials from the same speaker, against the False Acceptance Rate (FAR) at different thresholds, the EER corresponds to the point where (1 − TAR) = FAR.

The F1 Score captures the balance between precision (how often predicted matches were correct) and recall (how many true matches were detected), providing a measure of the model’s ability to distinguish between same and different speakers.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study involved secondary analysis of publicly available, de-identified data from the Coswara dataset. The Coswara dataset was collected with human-participant ethics oversight and informed consent as described by the dataset creators, and in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The present study did not involve direct interaction with human participants and did not require additional institutional review board approval.

Results

Dataset characteristics

The evaluation was conducted on the test dataset comprising cough recordings from 799 participants. Metadata accompanying the dataset included demographics and health-related information such as COVID-19 status and symptoms. Both heavy and shallow coughs were used as input features for algorithmic training and evaluation, consistent with their emphasis in prior sections.

The gender distribution of participants was as follows: 1900 male, 844 female, and 2 others. This imbalance reflects the broader Coswara dataset itself, which contains a higher proportion of male participants, rather than a sampling artifact. The age distribution included 59 participants under 18 years, 1172 participants aged 18-30, 859 participants aged 31-45, 589 participants aged 46-70, and 67 participants over 70 years. Geographically, the dataset included participants from 50 countries, while with a majority from India (2515 participants), followed by the United States (87 participants), France (19 participants), and Canada (15 participants). Speaker verification performance

To assess the model’s ability to differentiate between individuals, we computed the EER based on similarity scores across all test pairs. The lowest EER the model achieved was 13.39%, which occurred at a cosine similarity threshold of 0.2917. Figure 1 shows the corresponding ROC curve. The steep rise near the origin indicates that the model achieved high true positive rates even at low false positive rates, which is desirable for identity verification.

Classification performance

Additionally, we evaluated how well the model performed when making binary classification decisions, predicting whether two cough samples are from the same individual, through F1 score. The highest F1 score the model reached was 0.8676 with a threshold of 0.2970. F1 scores across thresholds had a mean of 0.6909, variance of 0.0361, and SD of 0.1901. Subgroup analysis

To further evaluate the model’s robustness across different health conditions, we conducted a subgroup analysis stratified by COVID-19 status. Among the participants included in the test set, 459 individuals were COVID-positive, and 128 were COVID-negative, while the remaining participants did not report their test status or did not take the test. For the COVID-positive group, the model achieved an EER of 12.25% and a best F1 score of 0.8794 at a threshold of 0.31. For the COVID-negative group, the model achieved an EER of 10.48% and a best F1 score of 0.8907 at a threshold of 0.30. Discussion

This study introduces a scalable, pairwise verification approach for cough-based speaker verification. Unlike traditional classification-based approaches, which aim to directly assign samples to predefined speaker categories, our approach learned the unique sound patterns from both shallow and heavy coughs for each person and compares whether two cough sounds likely came from the same individual via cosine similarity17. Instead of putting each cough into a fixed category, our model learns by comparing pairs of cough recordings to see how similar they sound. This helps it recognize patterns in each person’s coughs and tell users apart. Because of this approach, the model can work with new users without needing to be retrained, making it more practical for real-world use. Compared to prior work, our model achieved an Equal Error Rate of 13.39% and an F1 score of 0.8676, where EER values often exceed 15–20% under constrained settings, demonstrating strong verification performance under wide-range and unconstrained conditions11–15. By leveraging cough sequences, the model extracted discriminative, speaker-specific patterns that improved generalization to unseen users. These findings support our hypothesis that sequential cough sounds contain identity-specific information sufficient for speaker verification. However, the performance gap relative to speech-based verification (around 2.87–3.22% EER in VoxCeleb benchmarks) indicates that cough acoustics are a weaker biometric signal, which may require further model and dataset improvements11.

There is growing interest in audio-based biomarkers within digital health, reflecting a broader shift toward non-invasive and scalable monitoring tools. Voice has been proposed as an “audiomic” marker for a range of conditions, and start-up activity shows significant momentum in applying voice analytics to health technology34,37. Respiratory sounds such as cough and breathing are being used to support the diagnosis of asthma, COPD, and COVID-19, and even for biometric verification17,34,38–42. In cardiovascular care, conversational AI and phonocardiography analytics are being applied to improve heart failure management33. Together, these examples illustrate how audio analysis is emerging as a promising, low-cost, and accessible approach to continuous health monitoring.

The advent of contemporary AI allows leveraging cough acoustics in diagnostics and cough frequency monitoring in ways that were not possible before9. Machine learning algorithms can detect early changes in respiratory status by analyzing subtle patterns in cough sounds, including variations in frequency, timing, and acoustic features6,35,36,40–44. Several studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of these techniques in different clinical contexts. For instance, deep learning models have been used to distinguish coughs from other sounds with high accuracy using both audio and motion signals40. Other work has shown that disease-specific acoustic signatures, such as spectral features in the cough power spectrum, can be used to differentiate conditions like COVID-19, asthma, and pneumonia45.

In addition to disease detection, our work builds on digital biometric developments by demonstrating that coughs also encode individual-specific characteristics that can support identity verification. Importantly, this dual-use potential of cough-based audio data, in both clinical monitoring and biometric identification, raises new questions about its classification as protected health information, especially as passive data collection through mobile apps and wearables becomes more common46–48.

These concerns are especially relevant as cough-based systems become increasingly feasible for real-world deployment through mobile health tools, such as smartphone apps and wearable sensors49–54. In practice, such systems could support patients through contactless authentication for remote monitoring platforms, assist clinicians by maintaining consistent identity linkage across longitudinal respiratory recordings, and provide system operators with an additional biometric verification layer for digital health applications. At the same time, regulators and data governance bodies will play an important role in determining how respiratory audio should be classified and protected as these technologies move toward clinical implementation. Our findings that cough sounds carry speaker-specific acoustic patterns suggest that even involuntary, non-speech sounds may be used for identity recognition. This highlights the need for updated privacy standards to guide how such data is classified, stored, and governed55–58. Without clear frameworks, the expansion of cough-based technologies, from research to clinical and commercial application, risks exposing users to unauthorized surveillance or misuse of their health and identity information59,60. As these systems continue to scale, privacy safeguards, informed consent practices, and transparent user communication must evolve in parallel to ensure ethical implementation58–61.

Despite recent progress, the real-world implementation of cough sounds for identity verification remains limited due to challenges such as restricted demographic diversity in available datasets and the lack of scalability in current models. Several limitations of our study should be noted. First, the Coswara dataset provides only two cough recordings per participant (one shallow and one heavy), which constrains the model’s ability to capture the full range of natural variability in coughing behavior. In addition, all cough recordings in the dataset were voluntary, which may differ acoustically from spontaneous or reflexive coughs occurring during natural respiratory events. This may limit generalizability to passive or continuous monitoring scenarios, although voluntary cough recordings may still be appropriate for structured identity verification tasks such as prompted authentication within digital health applications. The dataset also has imbalances in demographic representation, with a predominance of younger individuals, more men than women, and primarily Indian participants, limiting generalizability to broader populations. The quality and consistency of the recordings also vary, with potential confounding factors such as background noise and the use of face coverings during recording, which may alter acoustic properties and affect model performance. Because these variables were not explicitly annotated in the dataset, their individual effects on model performance could not be systematically evaluated in this study. Additionally, COVID-19 status in the dataset was based on self-reported information rather than clinically verified diagnoses, which may introduce potential reporting bias and limit the reliability of subgroup analysis. Furthermore, the dataset does not allow comparison between sequential coughs and other non-speech sounds, such as singing, restricting the scope of analysis. This study also primarily leveraged sequential cough patterns for identity verification without explicitly integrating cough-level acoustic features from individual events. Finally, our study does not delve into algorithmic interpretability, and it remains unclear which phases or acoustic features of a cough carry the strongest speaker-specific information. Future research should develop models capable of highlighting which parts of a cough are most informative for identity encoding.

This study demonstrated the feasibility of verifying speaker identity from sequences of voluntary coughs. By adapting the ECAPA-TDNN model to distinguish individuals based on similarities and differences in their cough patterns on a diverse, real-world dataset, we show that even short, non-speech vocalizations can encode individual-specific information. Our model achieved strong verification performance using compact speaker-specific audio profiles derived from shallow and heavy coughs, which presents a scalable solution that does not require retraining for new users. These findings expand the scope of biometric technologies beyond speech and highlight coughs as a viable avenue for passive identity recognition. As interest grows in audio-based diagnostics and security, our work provides an important step toward understanding the dual-purpose potential of cough sounds in both healthcare and biometric systems. Future research should also consider the ethical and regulatory implications of extracting identity from involuntary health sounds to ensure responsible and equitable adoption.
