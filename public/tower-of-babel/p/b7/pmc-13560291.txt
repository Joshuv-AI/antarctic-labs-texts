Knowledge and practice of healthcare ethics among doctors and nurses in two Ghanaian hospitals: a comparative cross-sectional study
Authors: Prince Owusu Adoma, Francis Acquah, Felicia Kobina, John Foster Atta-Doku, Emmanuel Manu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEthical practice is central to patient trust, safety, and quality healthcare delivery. This study compared healthcare ethics knowledge and practice among doctors and nurses in two Ghanaian hospitals and identified factors associated with ethical practice.MethodsAn analytical cross-sectional study was conducted among 382 doctors and nurses in two government hospitals in Ghana. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire assessing healthcare ethics knowledge and self-reported practice. Bivariate analysis was conducted using Chi-square tests and T-tests, while Random Forest analysis was applied to identify variables of relative importance to healthcare ethics (HCE) practice.ResultsNo statistically significant differences were observed between facilities in mean healthcare ethics knowledge (26.3 ± 2.83; p = 0.98) and practice (23.5 ± 2.94; p = 0.16) scores. Healthcare ethics knowledge was significantly positively associated with practice (p < 0.05). Random Forest analysis identified age, knowledge, education, and gender as the most important predictors of ethical practice based on variable importance rankings, with differences observed in their relative contribution across facilities.ConclusionHealthcare professionals demonstrated moderate-to-high knowledge and practice of healthcare ethics, with no significant institutional differences. Ethical practice was influenced by demographic and educational factors, highlighting the need for context-specific strategies. Strengthening ethical decision-making may require integrating structured and continuous ethics training into professional development, alongside mentorship approaches that reinforce ethical standards in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12910-026-01542-z.

Introduction

Healthcare delivery extends beyond clinical competence to include adherence to ethical principles that safeguard patient rights, safety, and trust [1]. Medical ethics provides a framework for professional conduct through principles such as autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice [2, 3]. Compliance with these principles is essential for maintaining dignity, fairness, and accountability in healthcare practice [4]. Failure to uphold ethical standards may result in compromised patient safety, erosion of public trust, and increased medico-legal consequences for healthcare professionals and institutions.

Despite the foundational role of ethics in healthcare, healthcare professionals frequently encounter complex ethical dilemmas influenced by institutional pressures, cultural expectations, resource constraints, workload demands, and advances in medical technology [5–8]. In many low- and middle-income settings, increasing legal scrutiny and public awareness of patient rights further heighten the importance of ethical knowledge and consistent ethical practice [9–11]. Healthcare professionals are therefore required not only to understand ethical principles conceptually but also to apply them consistently in dynamic and resource-constrained clinical environments.

Evidence from sub-Saharan Africa suggests persistent gaps in healthcare professionals’ understanding and application of ethical principles. Research from Nigeria indicates that many practitioners rely primarily on undergraduate exposure to ethics without structured reinforcement in practice settings [12]. In Ghana, studies report moderate levels of ethics knowledge and generally positive attitudes among healthcare professionals; however, inconsistencies between knowledge and practice have been observed [13, 14]. These inconsistencies suggest that while theoretical knowledge may be present, translating ethical principles into routine clinical decision-making remains challenging [15]. Such discrepancies are particularly concerning in healthcare systems where patient–provider interactions are frequent and ethical decision-making directly influences care quality and patient outcomes.

Ethical practice is influenced not only by knowledge but also by demographic and professional characteristics such as age, education, gender, and work experience [14, 16, 17]. Although knowledge has been identified as an important determinant of ethical behavior [18], ethical practice is also shaped by the institutional environment in which healthcare professionals operate, including organizational culture, supervision structures, and clinical workload pressures. Comparative evidence examining how institutional context may be associated with variations in healthcare ethics knowledge and practice within Ghana remains limited. Most existing Ghanaian studies have focused on single institutions or specific professional groups, thereby restricting broader institutional comparisons [13, 14].

Understanding whether healthcare ethics knowledge and practice differ across institutions serving similar populations is important for informing targeted ethics education, mentorship models, and professional development strategies within Ghana’s health system. Institutional comparison may also reveal whether observed variations are primarily individual or contextual, thereby guiding more precise interventions. Therefore, this study compared healthcare ethics knowledge and practice among medical doctors and nurses in two government hospitals in Ghana and examined demographic factors associated with ethical practice.

Materials and methods

Study settings

The study was conducted in two government-owned hospitals located within the same municipality in Ghana. The facilities are accredited under the National Health Insurance Scheme and provide 24-hour clinical services to similar patient populations within the region. The institutions are referred to as Facility A and Facility B to maintain institutional confidentiality. Facility A operates as a secondary-level referral hospital and has been in operation for over a decade within the regional health system. It provides multidisciplinary clinical services and manages complex cases referred from surrounding health centers and district facilities. The institutional role as a referral center exposes healthcare professionals to higher-acuity clinical scenarios and interprofessional decision-making processes, which may influence the ethical demands encountered in practice.

Facility B functions as a municipal-level hospital delivering general medical, surgical, and preventive healthcare services at the district level. It provides routine outpatient and inpatient care and manages emergency and primary-level cases within the municipality. The clinical environment is characterized by general practice service delivery and continuity-of-care interactions, which may present different ethical considerations compared to a referral setting. The differences in institutional maturity, level of care, service scope, and clinical workflow between the two facilities provided a structured basis for examining potential variation in healthcare ethics knowledge and practice while minimizing geographic and population-related confounding.

Study design

An analytical cross-sectional study design was employed to compare healthcare ethics knowledge and practice among medical doctors and nurses in two government hospitals. The design also allowed examination of associations between socio-demographic characteristics and healthcare ethics practice within the study population. Data were collected over a three-month period from March to May 2023.

Sampling procedure

The study population comprised all permanent medical doctors and nurses employed at the two selected government hospitals at the time of data collection. Eligibility criteria required at least one year of continuous service at the facility. Contract, rotational, and independent staff were excluded. Healthcare professionals who declined participation or were unavailable during data collection were also excluded. A list of eligible medical doctors and nurses was obtained from the human resource departments of both facilities and served as the sampling frame. The total number of eligible healthcare professionals was 419. Given the finite size of the target population, a census (complete enumeration) approach was employed, whereby all eligible professionals were invited to participate. A total of 382 participants were included in the study, yielding a response rate of 91.2%. Non-participation was primarily due to absence during the data collection period or refusal to participate.

Data collection tool

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire developed for this study and informed by previously validated instruments [13, 19]. The instrument comprised three sections: socio-demographic characteristics, healthcare ethics knowledge, and healthcare ethics practice. Socio-demographic variables included age, gender, marital status, educational level, and intention to change profession. Personality orientation was assessed using a single self-report item in which participants indicated whether they were predominantly introverted or extroverted. Content validity was assessed by a panel consisting of a public health researcher, a clinical ethicist, and a senior nursing professional. The instrument was pre-tested among healthcare professionals outside the study facilities, with minor revisions made to improve clarity and contextual relevance.

Healthcare ethics knowledge was measured using nine items rated on a four-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge. A composite knowledge score was obtained by summing item responses (Cronbach’s α = 0.83). Healthcare ethics practice was assessed using eight items rated on a four-point frequency scale ranging from “never” to “always,” with higher scores indicating greater adherence to ethical practice. A composite practice score was computed by summing the item responses (Cronbach’s α = 0.60). The questionnaire was self-administered, and measures were implemented to ensure anonymity, confidentiality, and voluntary participation. An English-language version of the instrument is provided as Supplementary File.

Data collection procedure

Data collection commenced after ethical approval was obtained from the Ghana Health Service (GHS) Ethics Review Committee (Ethics Review Number: GHS-ERC 034/06/23). The heads of the two facilities subsequently granted administrative permission. The questionnaire was distributed to eligible healthcare professionals and collected after completion. Although retrieval was initially scheduled within two weeks, full collection required approximately four to five weeks due to varying staff availability. Data collection was conducted between 4 March and 31 May 2023. A total of 382 questionnaires were returned, yielding a response rate of 91.2%.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive and inferential statistical analyses were conducted to examine differences in healthcare ethics knowledge and practice between the two facilities and to identify predictors of healthcare ethics practice. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3, and Random Forest models were implemented using the randomForest package. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations. Chi-square tests were used to assess differences in categorical variables between facilities, and independent-samples t-tests were used for continuous variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Random Forest regression models were constructed separately for each facility to examine predictors of healthcare ethics practice. The composite healthcare ethics practice score was treated as the outcome variable. Predictor variables included healthcare ethics knowledge score and socio-demographic characteristics. Random Forest is an ensemble machine learning method that combines multiple decision trees to improve predictive performance and reduce overfitting [20, 21]. It was selected due to its ability to model complex, non-linear relationships and potential interactions among predictors without reliance on parametric assumptions. Variable importance was evaluated using percentage increase in mean squared error (%IncMSE) and increase in node purity (IncNodePurity), with higher values indicating greater predictive contribution. Variable importance plots were generated to facilitate interpretation.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of participants

A total of 382 healthcare professionals participated in the study. Facility A accounted for 55% (n = 210) of participants, while Facility B represented 45% (n = 172). The majority of participants were female (58.4%). Most (63.4%, n = 242) held a diploma or lower qualification, and just over half identified as extroverts (55%). Most participants were unmarried (64%), and 65% reported no intention to change profession. The mean age of participants was 28.30 years (SD = 5.37). Statistically significant differences between facilities were observed for age (p = 0.02) and educational level (p = 0.03), while no significant differences were found for gender, personality, marital status, or intention to change profession (Table 1).

Healthcare ethics knowledge and practice

The mean healthcare ethics knowledge score for the overall sample was 26.30 (SD = 2.83) out of 36, indicating relatively high knowledge levels. The mean healthcare ethics practice score was 23.50 (SD = 2.94) on a 32-point scale, suggesting similarly high levels of ethical practice. There were no statistically significant differences between Facility A and Facility B in healthcare ethics knowledge (p = 0.98) or healthcare ethics practice (p = 0.16) (Table 2).

Key influencing factors of healthcare ethics practice

Table 3 presents the results of the Random Forest analysis for the full sample and by facility. Variable importance was assessed using percentage increase in mean squared error (%IncMSE) and increase in node purity (IncNodePurity), where higher values indicate a stronger contribution to predicting healthcare ethics practice. Age and healthcare ethics knowledge emerged as the most influential predictors, based on their consistently higher importance values across models. In Facility B, healthcare ethics knowledge showed the highest contribution (%IncMSE = 33.3), while in Facility A, age was the dominant factor (%IncMSE = 26.18). Gender also contributed notably, particularly in Facility A, and education showed a stronger influence in Facility B. Personality, marital status, and intention to change profession showed lower importance across models. Similar predictors were identified across facilities, although their relative contributions differed.

Differences between %IncMSE and IncNodePurity rankings were observed across models, reflecting the distinct ways these measures capture variable importance. %IncMSE represents the impact of a variable on prediction accuracy, whereas IncNodePurity reflects its contribution to reducing model error during tree construction. The two measures consistently identified similar key predictors across settings. Lower-ranking variables, including personality, marital status, and intention to change profession, showed limited influence across models. These findings indicate that although the same predictors are relevant across facilities, their relative importance differs by institutional context.

Discussion

This study examined healthcare ethics knowledge and practice and evaluated the relative importance of demographic and professional predictors across two institutional settings. Knowledge and practice levels were comparable between facilities; however, predictor rankings differed, indicating that similar outcomes may be shaped by distinct contextual influences. Age emerged as the strongest predictor of healthcare ethics practice in the full-sample model based on both %IncMSE and IncNodePurity. This finding highlights the role of professional maturity and accumulated clinical experience in shaping ethical conduct. Ethical practice often requires navigating uncertainty and complex decision-making, and greater experience may enhance contextual judgment, consistent with Kirilmaz, Akbolat, and Kahraman [16] and more recent evidence linking professional experience to stronger ethical competence [22]. The prominence of age in the general model suggests that experiential learning remains a foundational driver of ethical practice.

Healthcare ethics knowledge was consistently influential across models and emerged as the dominant predictor in Facility B, where it demonstrated the highest importance values. The absence of significant mean differences in knowledge between facilities, coupled with its strong predictive contribution, suggests that the impact of knowledge on practice may be conditioned by institutional context. Knowledge appears necessary but not uniformly sufficient; its translation into ethical action may depend on professional culture, workflow structure, and organizational reinforcement. This interpretation aligns with evidence demonstrating a persistent disconnect between knowing and doing in health professions education and practice [23]. Contemporary scholarship further emphasizes that ethical action reflects the interaction between individual competence and organizational or environmental supports [24–26].

Educational level also demonstrated substantial predictive importance, particularly in Facility B. Formal education may provide structured exposure to ethical reasoning frameworks and professional standards, enhancing clinicians’ ability to recognize and respond to ethical dilemmas. This finding reinforces Thomas et al. [27] and González-Blázquez et al. [28], who emphasize the role of professional training in shaping ethical competence. Empirical studies conducted after 2010 further confirm that higher educational attainment is associated with improved ethical reasoning and application in clinical practice [29]. The stronger role of education in Facility B suggests that formal academic preparation may compensate for differences in institutional complexity or experiential exposure.

Gender ranked prominently in Facility A but did not demonstrate uniform influence across settings. Prior research suggests that gender-related differences in communication, empathy, and relational engagement may shape ethical interactions in clinical contexts [30, 31]. Its facility-specific prominence suggests that interpersonal dynamics within certain institutional environments may amplify gender-related influences on ethical practice.

Importantly, facility itself contributed meaningfully in the full model, indicating that institutional context exerts independent influence on ethical practice. Mean practice scores were slightly higher in Facility B; however, this difference was not statistically significant. The divergence in predictor rankings across facilities therefore provides deeper insight than mean comparisons alone. Institutional structure, patient case mix, professional composition, and workflow organization may condition how demographic and professional characteristics translate into ethical conduct. This interpretation aligns with evidence indicating that organizational climate and institutional culture significantly influence ethical behavior and moral decision-making in healthcare settings [32–34].

Personality orientation and intention to change profession demonstrated comparatively lower predictive importance. Although dispositional traits have been linked to professional behavior [22], the present findings suggest that structural, experiential, and educational factors exert stronger influence on ethical practice within clinical settings. The analysis demonstrates the value of examining variable importance rather than relying solely on mean differences. Comparable average knowledge and practice scores across facilities might suggest uniformity in ethical performance; however, the differing hierarchy of predictors reveals context-specific mechanisms shaping ethical behavior.

Healthcare ethics practice appears to emerge from the interaction of knowledge, experience, education, demographic characteristics, and institutional environment rather than from a single dominant factor, consistent with multi-level conceptual models of ethical practice in healthcare [35]. The findings suggest that strategies to strengthen ethical practice should account for institutional context. Approaches emphasizing experiential reinforcement may be particularly relevant in settings where age and experience dominate, whereas structured educational reinforcement may be more impactful where formal training plays a stronger role. Ethical practice should therefore be understood as a context-sensitive outcome shaped by the interaction between individual competencies and organizational environment.

Conclusion

This study identified age, healthcare ethics knowledge, education, and gender as key factors influencing healthcare ethics practice across two institutional settings. Knowledge and practice levels were comparable between facilities; however, the relative importance of predictors differed by context, indicating that similar outcomes may arise from distinct underlying influences. The findings suggest that ethical practice is shaped not only by individual competence and professional characteristics but also by institutional dynamics that condition how these factors operate. The examination of predictor importance, rather than reliance solely on mean comparisons, offers a more nuanced understanding of the mechanisms through which ethical behavior is expressed in healthcare settings.

Strengths and limitations

This study achieved a high response rate through a census approach, reducing sampling bias within the target population. Inclusion of both medical doctors and nurses strengthened professional representation. The use of randomForest modeling enabled identification of the relative importance of predictors beyond simple mean comparisons, providing deeper insight into institutional variation in healthcare ethics practice.

The cross-sectional design limits causal inference. Ethical practice was self-reported and may be subject to social desirability bias. The study was conducted in two hospitals within a single region, which may limit generalizability. Although randomForest identified influential predictors, it does not estimate effect size or directionality in the same manner as traditional regression models.

Implications for practice

The findings indicate that strengthening healthcare ethics practice requires context-sensitive strategies rather than uniform training approaches. Although knowledge levels were comparable across facilities, the relative importance of predictors differed, suggesting that institutional context shapes how ethical competence translates into practice. Ethics reinforcement initiatives should therefore be integrated into continuous professional development and adapted to facility-specific needs. Mentorship models may be particularly relevant in settings where age and experience dominate, whereas structured refresher training may be more impactful where formal education plays a stronger role. Embedding ethics discussions into routine clinical activities may further enhance the practical application of ethical principles.
