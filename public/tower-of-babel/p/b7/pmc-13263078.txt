A novel lumen-apposing metal stent for EUS-guided drainage of pancreatic fluid collections: A multicenter prospective study
Authors: Huiyun Zhu, Yuting Liu, Kaixuan Wang, Danqi Zhao, Xun Li, Xiaoping Zou, Xiaofeng Zhang, Youxiang Chen, Ning Zhong, Jianying Bai, Liang Zhong, Peng Li, Bin Xiao, Wei Chen, Enqiang Linghu, Zhaoshen Li, Ningli Chai, Yiqi Du, Zhendong Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:Various lumen-apposing metal stents (LAMSs) were used for pancreatic fluid collection (PFC) drainage for many years. The structural design of LAMS needs to be improved to reduce the occurrence of adverse events. This trial assessed the efficacy and safety of a novel modified LAMS for the drainage of PFCs.Methods:This open-label, multicenter, prospective trial was done at 11 tertiary care hospitals. This study enrolled patients (18–75 years old) with confirmed diagnosis of PFC with cyst diameter no less than 6 cm. Novel LAMS (Micro-Tech Co, Ltd, Nanjing, China) was used. The primary end point was the 1-month postoperative drainage success rate. The secondary end points were technical success rate and adverse events. This study is registered with Chictr.org.cn, ChiCTR2000039955.Results:Between December 9, 2020, and December 27, 2021, 100 patients with PFC were assessed for eligibility, and 94 patients met the criteria and agreed to participate in the trial. The median size of PFC cyst was 11.23 ± 3.84 cm. The drainage success rate was 90.48% (95% CI, 83.6%–97.3%) and achieved the prespecified target value of 75% (P < 0.0001). In subgroup analysis, the clinical success rates of pancreatic pseudocyst (PPC) and walled-off necrosis (WON) were 95.45% and 85%, respectively (P = 0.143). The overall technical success rate was 98.94%. Postoperative early adverse events occurred in 57 (60.64%) of 94 patients, and late adverse events were encountered in 16 (17.02%) of 94 patients. The overall rate of serious adverse event (bleeding-related death) was 2.13% (2/94). Patients with WON had a significantly higher rate of early adverse events compared to those with PPC (77.78% vs. 44.90%, P = 0.001).Conclusions:The novel LAMS used in this trial was technically feasible, efficient, and safe for the treatment of PFCs. Comparable with WON, the usage of LAMS in PPC achieved a high drainage success rate and acceptable adverse events.

INTRODUCTION

Pancreatic fluid collections (PFCs) refer to the accumulation of fluid and necrotic material encapsulated by a complete, nonepithelial membrane around the pancreas, as a common complication of moderately severe acute pancreatitis (MSAP). PFCs can be classified into pancreatic pseudocyst (PPC) and walled-off necrosis (WON) based on the contents within the cyst.[1] Approximately 5%–10% of patients with acute pancreatitis develop PFCs.[2] Clinical intervention is required for patients with PFCs who present with clinical symptoms such as abdominal pain and fever or who have complications such as infection or bleeding.[3] The treatment strategies of PFCs include open surgery or laparoscopy,[4,5] ultrasound- or computed tomography (CT)-guided percutaneous catheter drainage,[6] and EUS-guided transmural drainage.[7]

Thanks to the advantages of real-time imaging, precise localization, and being safe and efficient,[7] EUS-guided transmural drainage has achieved remarkable technical and clinical success rates and has become the first-line treatment for PFCs. Compared with double-pigtail plastic stent[8] and fully covered self-expanding metal stent,[9] lumen-apposing metal stent (LAMS) has the advantages of larger diameter and resistance to displacement. LAMS is more user-friendly and useful in case of direct endoscopic necrosectomy (DEN). After more than a decade of clinical application, LAMS has been reported with issues such as stent-related bleeding, low drainage success rate, and cumbersome removal of buried stent (migration rates).[10–13]

Therefore, our team designed a novel LAMS (Micro-Tech Co, Ltd, Nanjing, China) and conducted this premarket clinical trial. The appearance of the stent, the components of the device, and the endoscopic procedure are shown in Figure 1. The maximum diameter of the stent can reach 16 mm, which is conducive to maintaining smooth drainage of the cyst and provides a large channel for debridement. The stent features a single-umbrella design to prevent dislodgement into the cyst cavity and to facilitate removal after cyst drainage is complete. Although a single-center retrospective clinical study on the novel modified LAMS has previously been published in China, the current multicenter prospective trial has been completed to further strengthen the evidence base.[14] This trial is a multicenter, prospective study, collaboratively completed by the leading 11 hospitals in China specializing in EUS. It aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of the novel stent in treating PFCs, thereby laying the foundation for its further widespread application.

METHODS

Study design and participants

The study was an open-label, multicenter, premarket clinical trial conducted at 11 tertiary care hospitals (Supplementary Table 1, https://links.lww.com/ENUS/A388). The trial protocol aligns with the ethical guidelines of the 1975 and 2024 Declaration of Helsinki. The purpose of this study was to report to the China National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) for registration and market launch. The current trial was implemented as a sequential study as previously reported,[15] with an improved design of stent. This clinical trial protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of Shanghai Changhai Hospital (CHEC2020-0124). The trial protocol was subsequently approved by the ethics committees of all other participating hospitals and registered with Chictr.org.cn, ChiCTR2000039955.

Before the initiation of the trial, investigators and endoscopists from the participating centers thoroughly discussed the study methodology to align it with the standards of endoscopic practice. Eligibility criteria were adult patients (18–75 years) with confirmed diagnosis of pancreatic fluid collection (PPC or WON) according to the revision of the Atlanta consensus[1] and the cyst diameter no less than 6 cm. Exclusion criteria were cyst close to large blood vessels, combined with aneurysm, intracystic hemorrhage, cyst rupture, cardia gastric varices, suspected infection or cancer, pregnancy, or contraindications for endoscopic procedures. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient included in the trial before the use of LAMS for cyst drainage. Participants were permitted to withdraw or terminate the trial at any time.

Procedures

Preoperative evaluation

Demographic data of patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria and agreed to enroll in the trial were collected from electronic health records at participating centers. All participants underwent contrast-enhanced CT scans. For those with a history of contrast agent allergy, noncontrast CT or MRCP (magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography) was performed instead. In this trial, a centralized reading method was adopted. The cyst diameter and necrotic content were assessed by two radiologists. In the case of disagreement, the final decision required the unanimous agreement of at least two expert radiologists. The classification of necrotic content percentage in this trial refers to our previous study.[16]

Operation procedure

After completing the preoperative evaluation, the patients underwent EUS-guided transluminal drainage. The puncture site was determined by the endoscopic operator through EUS examination. In this study, lumen-apposing metal stents (Micro-Tech Co, Ltd, Nanjing, China) [Figure 1] with a diameter of 16 mm and a saddle length of 20 or 30 mm were used. All procedures were performed under general anesthesia, using linear echoendoscope by experienced endosonographers. Under EUS guidance, the fine needle was advanced through the posterior gastric wall into the cavity. A guidewire was then coiled adequately within the cyst, and subsequently, the LAMS was deployed over the guidewire between the stomach and the PFC cavity. Correct stent positioning will be reconfirmed using real-time fluorescence imaging prior to procedure completion. The use of other stents (e.g., plastic stent) or combined drainage methods was not permitted. No concomitant treatments, such as debridement, were allowed from the time of stent placement until the assessment of the primary end point.

Follow-up

The follow-up was conducted via telephone at 14 ± 3 days postoperatively to inquire about the occurrence of complications. At 30 ± 7 days postoperatively, cyst drainage efficacy was assessed, and stent removal was performed. Abdominal noncontrast CT was conducted at 60 ± 7 days postoperatively to evaluate cyst recurrence. Another telephone follow-up was performed at 90 ± 7 days postoperatively to assess cyst recurrence and postoperative recovery. For patients who did not achieve drainage success within 30 days postoperatively, investigators at each center may independently develop the subsequent treatment plan based on clinical situations.

All data were recorded using the electronic data capture (EDC) system (http://47.107.145.115/nwylzjEDC/index.jsp). Researchers recorded electronic medical information after treating the patients to ensure that data records were timely, complete, accurate, and authentic. Clinical research coordinators (CRCs) or investigators who had been authorized and trained in the EDC system logged into the system to fill in the data. Subsequently, clinical research associates (CRAs) logged into the system to cross-check each data point against the source documents. If any discrepancies were found between the entered data and the source medical records, the CRAs would raise queries. The CRCs or investigators then reviewed the original data item by item in response to the query list and made the necessary corrections.

Outcomes

The primary end point was the 1-month postoperative drainage success rate. Drainage success defined as the maximum diameter of the cyst has decreased by ≥50% compared to before drainage, without any additional interventions. The definition of drainage success is similar to previously published series using LAMS in the management of PFC.[14,17–19] The secondary end points were technical success rate and adverse events. Technical success defined as a stent was successfully implanted into the cyst cavity and the gastrointestinal tract. Adverse events were categorized based on their occurrence time into early adverse events and late adverse events. Early adverse events were defined as those occurring within 2 weeks postoperatively, whereas late adverse events were those occurring after 2 weeks postoperatively. All end points were analyzed by intention-to-treat.

In this trial, neither the efficacy nor the safety evaluation indicators will impute missing data. A trial manager audited the data from all centers monthly, and any discrepancies were resolved timely. The primary end point was assessed by the principal investigator and the radiologist.

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculation

Combining the existing literature and clinical evidence, as well as the experience of clinical experts, the postoperative drainage success rate is assumed to be 88%. Meanwhile, the target value for the evaluation criterion is set at 75%. With a two-sided significance level of 0.05 for the statistical test and a power of 80%, considering a maximum dropout rate of 20% during the study, the calculated sample size based on statistical principles is 94 patients. The statistical analysis procedure was strictly conducted in accordance with the relevant standard operating procedures of the Statistics Department at the New Probe Health Development Research Center. The study summarized patient baseline characteristics, features of necrotic collections, procedure details, and outcome measures as means (with SD), and as frequencies and proportions for categorical data. Categorical data were compared using χ2 or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate, and continuous data were compared using the two-sample t test or the Wilcoxon rank sum test as indicated. Binary regression analysis (using an α value of 0.05) was conducted to identify factors associated with clinical drainage success. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM SPSS Software, Armonk, NY, USA) and SAS® 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). P values of <0.05 were considered as statistically significant.

RESULTS

Between December 9, 2020, and December 27, 2021, 100 patients with pancreatic fluid collection were assessed for eligibility, and 94 patients (27 [28.72%] women and 67 [71.28%] men) met the criteria and agreed to participant the trial [Table 1]. The median size of PFC along the transverse axis was 11.23 ± 3.84 cm (range, 6.07–23.17 cm). According to the Atlanta classification of PFC,[1] 49 patients (52.13%) with PPC and 45 patients (47.87%) with WON were included in this trial. All patients underwent EUS-guided pancreatic cyst drainage using LAMS. As 10 patients had a protocol violation, 84 patients were included in the intention-to-treat population and assessed for the primary end point [Figure 2]. Representative CT manifestations of failure and successful treatment cases of PPC and WON are shown in Figure 3.

Postoperative early adverse events occurred in 57 (60.64%) of 94 patients. Fever was the most frequent symptom, occurring in 40 (42.55%) patients, followed by abdominal pain (17, 18.09%), nausea (16, 17.02%), and bloating (5, 5.32%). Three (3.19%) patients experienced stent occlusion, two (2.13%) patients had stent migration, and two (2.13%) patients had bleeding complications. One bleeding event occurred for 3 days postoperatively and improved after symptomatic treatment. The other bleeding event occurred for 7 days postoperatively and successfully controlled after endoscopic hemostasis, and postoperative late adverse events were encountered in 16 (17.02%) of 94 patients. Fever remained the most common complication, occurring in 12 (12.77%) patients, followed by nausea (4, 4.26%), bleeding (2, 2.13%), and stent occlusion (2, 2.13%). One (1.06%) patient experienced stent migration, one (1.06%) patient had abdominal pain, and one (1.06%) patient had stent buried. Among the two patients with bleeding, one bleeding event occurred 18 days postoperatively; the patient did not seek medical attention in a timely manner and eventually died. Another one patient experienced hematemesis and subsequent shock on postoperative day 26. DSA indicated bleeding from the splenic artery’s pancreatic branch and intra-abdominal hemorrhage. Endoscopy revealed active bleeding from the necrotic cavity and sinus tract. Despite multiple resuscitation attempts, the patient eventually succumbed to the complications. The buried stent was successfully removed by endoscopically extracting the connecting guidewire of the LAMS.

Subgroup analysis of adverse events comparing pseudocyst with walled-off pancreatic necrosis is shown in Table 4. Patients with WON had a significantly higher rate of early adverse events compared to those with PPC (77.78% vs. 44.90%, P = 0.001). Within 2 weeks postoperatively, compared to patients with PPC, those with WON were more likely to experience fever (P = 0.043) and bloating (P = 0.022). There was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of late postoperative adverse events between patients with WON and those with PPC (P = 0.199). However, after 2 weeks postoperatively, patients with WON were more likely to experience fever compared to those with PPC (20.00% vs. 6.12%, P = 0.044).

DISCUSSION

This trial, conducted as a prospective, multicenter, open-label study, systematically evaluated the efficacy and safety of a novel LAMS for EUS-guided drainage of PFCs. The clinical success rate for draining PFCs using the novel LAMS was 90.48%, whereas the technical success rate was 98.94%. The incidence of adverse events within 2 weeks postoperatively was 60.64%, and the incidence of adverse events after 2 weeks postoperatively was 17.02%. The overall rate of serious adverse event (bleeding-related death) was 2.13%.

The novel LAMS demonstrated excellent clinical efficacy in treating PFCs, achieving the trial’s expectations. Compared to the existing LAMSs, the new stent also exhibited certain advantages. The AXIOS stent (Boston Scientific) is the most widely used LAMS for the drainage of PFCs to date. The technical success rate and clinical success rate were 97% and 88%, respectively.[20] However, the AXIOS stent is available in two lengths (1 and 1.5 cm), which may not be sufficient for cases where the distance between the cyst and the gastrointestinal lumen is large, or the cyst wall is thick. In such cases, the stent may experience significant internal counterforce, and the flanges at both ends of the stent may increase mechanical compression. This can lead to adverse events such as delayed bleeding and stent buried syndrome.[21] Additionally, the removal of the AXIOS stent might be complex.[12] The novel stent incorporates modified length specifications and structural design features, which may contribute to a reduction in associated adverse event rates. SPAXUS, a novel fully covered with silicone membrane stent, is known for its flexibility and can extend to 2 cm when the distance between the two cavity walls or the thickness of the cavity wall exceeds 1 cm. However, its release system cannot be precisely controlled by the endoscopist, and it is available in only one length specification, which limits its clinical use.[22] For PPCs, The technical success rate was 97.1% (33/34). The clinical success rate was 94.1% (32/34). The technical success rate of the novel LAMS for PPC was 97.96%, and the clinical success rate was 95.45%, both of which were noninferior to those of SPAXUS. The NAGI pseudocyst stent had a flared, double-flanged structure. For PFCs, the technical success rate was 53 of 54 patients (98.1%), and the initial clinical success rate was 36 of 47 (76.6%). The incidence rate of stent migration was as high as 11.1%, and migration was inevitable because of its relatively limited lumen-apposing function.[23] Other stents like HANARO (MI Tech, Seoul, Korea) and aixstent® PPS (Leufen Med, Germany) have not been widely applied, and clinical data on these stents remain relatively limited.[24]

The latest research indicated that the reintervention-free clinical success rate for endoscopic drainage of PFCs was relatively low.[25] The study enrolled a total of 92 patients with infected WON and necrotic content ranging from 10% to 50%. The reintervention-free clinical success rates were 67% in the metal stent group (n = 46) and 44% in the plastic stent group (n = 46) (P = 0.02). After interventions, the overall success rates reached 89% in both groups (P > 0.99). For WON with necrotic content >30%, multiple debridements were required to achieve clinical success.[26] In this trial, no additional stents were used after the placement of the novel LAMS, and no interventional procedures were performed prior to the assessment of the primary end point. The high drainage success rate in our study might be attributed to the fact that the necrotic content in most of WON cases was less than 20%. However, univariate regression analysis revealed that the drainage success rate with the novel LAMS was not affected by the necrotic content.

The incidence of postprocedural adverse events reported in this trial for the novel stent was relatively high. However, most of these were mild adverse events, such as transient fever, nausea, bloating, and abdominal pain. Subgroup analysis revealed that the occurrence of postprocedural adverse events was associated with the type of PFC. Specifically, postprocedural fever and bloating were significantly higher in WON than in PPC. This may be related to infection caused by necrotic material.[16] The novel LAMS has a wide diameter, and the incidence of stent occlusion is 2.13%, which is lower than that of other stents (36.3%–56%).[27,28] The novel stent also performs excellently in terms of antimigration, with a migration rate of 1.06%, which is lower than that of other LAMS (6.1%–8.7%).[26,27] Buried syndrome is also one of the refractory adverse events of LAMS. In the past, methods such as dismantling the stent[12] and shortening the stent dwell time were used to remove stent and avoid burial. However, the improved design of this new type of LAMS stent, which retains a proximal retrieval line, facilitates the removal of buried stents.

Bleeding-related death is the most severe adverse event associated with the new type of LAMS, with an incidence rate of 2.13%. One bleeding event occurred 18 days postoperatively; the patient did not seek medical attention in a timely manner and eventually died. Another bleeding-related death event occurred 26 days after the placement of stent. Previous studies have reported that the procedure-related bleeding of PFCs drainage was 9%–50%.[13,27–29] A comprehensive analysis of the potential causes of bleeding may include the following aspects: friction between the stent and the cyst wall leading to vascular rupture and bleeding; gastric acid flowing through the fistula and passing over pancreatic tissue, causing tissue ulceration and bleeding; excessive debridement; and pseudoaneurysm bleeding.[30] The following measures may reduce the incidence of bleeding: the stent should not be left in place for too long, preferably within 1 month; immediate debridement after stent placement, with a reduction in the number of debridement procedures;[31] prophylactic vascular embolization before the procedure; and using plastic stents in combination.[27]

There are some limitations in our trial. First, this study is a premarketing study, and drainage combination was not permitted. Despite this, it has demonstrated good drainage efficacy.[28] Second, this is a single-arm study that only observed the clinical performance of the metal stent without a control group. Follow-up research could include a series of clinical studies to explore the effects of the novel stent in combination with debridement or in comparison with plastic stents.[15,32]

In conclusion, the novel LAMS is technically feasible, efficient, and safe for the treatment of PFCs. Further adequately powered, well-designed, randomized controlled studies comparing the new LAMS with previous stents (LAMS or plastic stent) are required to validate its efficacy. The evidence from the study further supported our recently achieved consensus on pancreatic pseudocyst and walled-off necrosis from a Chinese multiple disciplinary team expert panel.[3] LAMS should be recommended as a first-line treatment for PFCs closely attached to the stomach wall.
