Comparative assessment of machine learning models for polymer solution viscosity prediction in enhanced oil recovery
Authors: Sami Abderraouf Belkhir, Altamish Ahmed Pakeer, Mariam Shakeel, Rizwan Muneer, Younes Alblooshi, Muhammad Rehan Hashmet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polymer flooding is among the most researched and utilized methods to improve oil recovery. Polymer solution viscosity is the primary governing parameter that aids in lowering the mobility of the injected fluid and augmenting the volumetric sweep efficiency. However, the design of a polymer solution is time-consuming and requires intensive resources because of the need to design several precursors, including makeup water salinity, temperature, polymer concentration, etc., to sustain the target solution viscosity. The objective of this study was to develop a fast and reliable approach to predict the viscosity of SAV10 polymer using the necessary input parameters, including polymer concentration, shear rate, temperature, and brine salinity. Four simple machine learning techniques, namely linear regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), decision tree (DT), and artificial neural network (ANN), were implemented with varying critical model parameters in each technique. Owing to the non-linear relationship between polymer viscosity and input parameters, a simple linear regression model was unable to capture them. The best machine learning model among the classical models used in this study was a Wide ANN model with one fully connected layer of 100 neurons. This model yielded an acceptable viscosity prediction with a coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.998 and a root mean square error (RMSE) of 0.31 for the test data. To further generalize the predictability and reduce the model error, advanced machine learning models, i.e. Gaussian Process Regressor and an ensemble machine learning stacking regressor was employed. However, the stacking regressor dramatically lowered the root mean square error by almost 40% while the model successfully generalized the unseen viscosity data and predicted the high viscosity values with considerable confidence. We developed an SAV10 viscosity predictor from routine inputs where a stacking-based ensemble resulted in an excellent match between the actual and predicted viscosities having R2 value of 0.998 with minimum RMSE of 0.208, and MAE of 0.223, while preserving accuracy on unseen high-viscosity data.

Introduction

Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR) has emerged as a critical technology in the petroleum industry, addressing the growing challenge of extracting oil from mature fields where conventional recovery methods have reached their limits. EOR encompasses a range of techniques designed to increase the amount of crude oil that can be extracted from an oil reservoir, typically by altering the properties of the oil or modifying reservoir conditions to improve flow characteristics1. These methods have become increasingly important as global oil demand continues to rise while new oil discoveries become less frequent and more challenging to exploit. EOR techniques can be broadly categorized into three main types: chemical injection, thermal recovery, and gas injection2. Thermal recovery involves the introduction of heat to reduce oil viscosity and improve flow. Gas injection, including carbon dioxide injection, aims to increase reservoir pressure and reduce oil viscosity. Chemical injection, which includes polymer flooding, surfactant flooding, and alkaline flooding, focuses on mobility control and modifying fluid properties to improve displacement efficiency3,4. The management of injection fluid viscosity is central to the success of many EOR techniques. Viscosity, defined as a fluid’s resistance to flow under applied stress, plays a crucial role in determining the efficiency of oil displacement and, consequently, the overall recovery factor5.

In oil reservoirs, the relationship between the viscosity of the displacing fluid (often water or a polymer solution) and the viscosity of the residual oil significantly impacts sweep efficiency – the effectiveness with which the injected fluid contacts the oil-bearing portions of the reservoir6. When the viscosity of the displacing fluid is substantially lower than that of the oil, a phenomenon known as viscous fingering can occur. This instability leads to the formation of finger-like intrusions of the less viscous fluid into the more viscous oil, resulting in premature breakthrough and poor sweep efficiency. Consequently, significant portions of oil may be bypassed and left unrecovered. Additionally, in heterogeneous reservoirs with high-permeability streaks, low-viscosity displacing fluids tend to channel through these paths of least resistance, further reducing overall recovery efficiency7. To address these challenges, the industry has developed and employed various viscosifying agents in EOR processes. These agents are designed to increase the viscosity of the injected fluid, thereby improving its ability to displace oil from the reservoir uniformly8,9. By bringing the viscosity of the injected fluid closer to that of the oil, these agents help to stabilize the displacement front, reduce fingering and channelling, and ultimately enhance sweep efficiency and oil recovery10. Polymers have emerged as one of the most effective and widely used viscosifying agents in EOR due to their ability to significantly increase fluid viscosity at low concentrations, as well as their cost-effectiveness and adaptability to various reservoir conditions.

The use of polymers in EOR, known as polymer flooding, has been implemented in numerous field applications worldwide, demonstrating its potential to increase oil recovery by 5–30% of original oil in place (OOIP) beyond conventional waterflooding11. Polymers used in EOR can be broadly categorized into two types: synthetic polymers and biopolymers. Synthetic polymers, particularly partially hydrolyzed polyacrylamide (HPAM) and its derivatives, dominate field applications due to their robust performance and economic viability. HPAM is a water-soluble polymer that can noticeably increase the viscosity of the aqueous phase at low concentrations12,13. Its effectiveness stems from its high molecular weight and the repulsion between negatively charged carboxylate groups along its backbone, which causes the polymer to expand in solution, increasing fluid viscosity14. Biopolymers, including xanthan gum, scleroglucan, and other microbial polysaccharides, offer certain advantages in specific reservoir conditions. These naturally derived polymers often exhibit better resistance to mechanical degradation and maintain their viscosifying properties at higher temperatures and salinities compared to some synthetic polymers15. However, their wider adoption has been limited by higher production costs, the potential for biodegradation inside the reservoirs, and sometimes lower viscosifying power compared to synthetic alternatives16. The behavior of polymers in reservoir conditions is complex and depends on multiple interrelated factors. Temperature, polymer concentration, salinity, and shear rate are among the key parameters that significantly influence polymer viscosity and, consequently, its performance in EOR applications17,18. Temperature affects polymer viscosity by influencing polymer chain flexibility and intermolecular interactions. Generally, as temperature increases, polymer viscosity decreases due to increased thermal motion of the polymer chains and weakened intermolecular forces. However, some polymers may exhibit more complex temperature-viscosity relationships, especially in the presence of salts or at high concentrations19. Polymer concentration directly influences solution viscosity, with higher concentrations typically resulting in higher viscosities. This relationship is often non-linear, with viscosity increasing more rapidly at higher concentrations as polymer chains begin to entangle and form transient networks. The critical overlap concentration (C*), which marks the transition from dilute to semi-dilute regimes, is an important parameter in understanding this behavior20. Salinity plays a crucial role in polymer behavior, particularly for polyelectrolytes like HPAM. Increased salinity generally leads to a reduction in viscosity due to charge screening effects that cause the polymer chains to coil more tightly. However, the relationship between salinity and viscosity can be complex, with some polymers showing improved performance in specific salinity ranges or the presence of certain ion types21. The shear rate affects polymer viscosity by influencing polymer chain alignment and potential degradation. Many polymer solutions exhibit shear-thinning behavior, where viscosity decreases with increasing shear rate as polymer chains align in the direction of flow22. Understanding this behavior is crucial for predicting polymer performance in different parts of the reservoir, from low-shear regions far from injection wells to high-shear areas near wellbores23. As the complexity of these relationships increases, traditional analytical methods often fall short of accurately predicting polymer behavior across the wide range of conditions encountered in oil reservoirs24. This limitation has led to the growing adoption of machine learning (ML) techniques in the field of EOR. Machine learning, a subset of artificial intelligence, involves the development of algorithms that can learn from data to make predictions or decisions without being explicitly programmed for the task25. In recent years, the application of machine learning in the oil and gas industry has grown exponentially, offering new ways to analyze complex datasets, optimize processes, and improve decision-making26. In the context of EOR, machine learning models are increasingly used to capture complex nonlinear relationships in experimental and field data that traditional correlations struggle with27. For instance, polymer solution viscosity which a key parameter controlling mobility ratio and sweep efficiency in polymer flooding often varies nonlinearly with concentration, temperature, salinity, and shear conditions. Instead of relying solely on time-consuming laboratory measurements, data-driven models can be trained to predict viscosity across a wider range of conditions with high reliability. Early work applied ANNs to estimate polymer solution viscosity from multiple influencing variables, demonstrating that trained networks could closely match measured viscosity values under varied environmental conditions28. More recent studies have extended this approach, applying support vector machines, regression trees, and neural network architectures to several commercial polymer systems, achieving strong correlation coefficients (R ≈ 0.99) for training and validation datasets29. Beyond viscosity prediction, machine learning has been employed to assess the feasibility and performance of polymer flooding itself by learning relationships between reservoir properties, polymer concentration, temperature, and predicted recovery outcomes effectively enabling rapid screening and decision support while reducing dependence on detailed core-flood experiments30. Parallel work in reservoir engineering uses ML frameworks to forecast cumulative oil recovery from time-series and simulation data, offering alternative proxies for traditional simulators in EOR assessments31. At the screening level, broader reviews show that machine learning classifiers and regression models can help select the most suitable EOR methods (including polymer-based techniques) based on reservoir and fluid characteristics, reflecting a trend toward data-centric decision tools in EOR planning32.

Different machine learning techniques offer unique strengths and certain limitations in predicting polymer viscosity. Linear regression provides a baseline and can reveal simple trends. SVM model excels at handling non-linear relationships and can perform effectively with relatively small datasets29. Decision trees offer interpretability and can capture complex variable interactions33. On the other hand, Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) can capture highly complex patterns but often require larger datasets for optimal performance34. Similar data-driven approaches have also been applied in polymer composite studies, where techniques such as response surface methodology and fuzzy logic have been used to model tribological behavior35.

Ensemble techniques such as Random Forests, Gradient Boosting, and stacking frameworks apply the “wisdom of crowds” principle to achieve higher predictive accuracy than a single base learner. By averaging or iteratively refining the outputs of multiple weak or diverse models, they reduce variance and bias, making them more robust to outliers and noisy rheology data and improving the reliability of polymer viscosity predictions. Their built-in feature subsampling and reweighting schemes also reduce the risk of overfitting non-linear interactions, offering performance on par with deep networks even when the dataset is only moderate in size. The trade-off is twofold. First, interpretability decreases as the ensemble becomes more complex; while global feature-importance rankings can be extracted, tracing an individual prediction through hundreds of trees or boosting stages is often impervious. Second, computational cost increases, as training and hyperparameter optimization (e.g., learning rate, tree depth, number of estimators) require substantially more time and processing resources than single-model approaches. Nevertheless, when predictive accuracy and generalization are the primary objectives, a well-optimized ensemble frequently offers a practical compromise between the transparency of simpler models and the data-intensive demands of deep neural networks.

This research presents a novel approach to predicting polymer viscosity in enhanced oil recovery (EOR) applications by integrating diverse machine learning models under harsh reservoir conditions, such as high salinity, high temperature, varying polymer concentrations, and shear rates. Through a comprehensive comparative analysis of these models, the study provides valuable insights into their performance while revealing complex relationships between key parameters influencing polymer viscosity. The multi-model framework not only enables cross-validation of results but also illuminates distinct aspects of the physical phenomena governing polymer behavior in reservoirs.

The novelty of this research lies in the comprehensive integration of all key factors influencing polymer viscosity, including polymer concentration, temperature, salinity, and shear rate, into a single, data-driven predictive framework. Unlike previous studies that analyzed these variables independently or in limited combinations, this work employs advanced machine learning algorithms capable of capturing their complex and nonlinear interdependencies with high accuracy. By combining polymer science with modern machine learning, the study overcomes the limitations of conventional statistical models that struggle with coupled relationships under harsh reservoir conditions. This approach enhances prediction reliability, reduces the need for extensive laboratory experimentation, and accelerates the design and optimization of polymer flooding operations. The findings also open pathways for developing customized polymer formulations suited to diverse reservoir environments, ultimately improving the efficiency, cost-effectiveness, and sustainability of enhanced oil recovery processes.

Brine and polymer

For this investigation, a brine solution with a pH of 6 and a total dissolved solids (TDS) of 43,036 parts per million (ppm) was prepared. The TDS level of 43,036 ppm ensures that the research undertaken is relevant to the Middle East region because it reflects the natural salinity range seen in the Arabian Sea. In our research, we utilized a polymer named SAV10, which is partially hydrolyzed polyacrylamide (HPAM) with 2-acrylamido-2-methylpropane sulfonic acid (ATBS) attached to the backbone of the branch. This polymer was sourced from SNF Floegar, which has been an integral part of this whole study. To perform this data analysis study, rheological experiments were performed to obtain SAV10 viscosity data as a function of polymer concentration, temperature, shear rate, and salinity. The following section presents the experimental setup used in the study.

Polymer solution preparation

Different polymer solutions were prepared utilizing the seawater having a salinity of 43,000 ppm as the base fluid. The required quantity of the polymer was accurately weighed using an electric weighing balance. The polymer particles were then uniformly dispersed into the curvature of the brine solution under stirring conditions at 650 rpm, ensuring the absence of fish-eye formations. Subsequently, the stirring speed was reduced to 350rpm, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 2 h to achieve thorough dispersion and homogenization. To prepare different salinity solutions, the seawater was diluted 2 times, 4 times, and 6 times.

Bulk rheological study

Using an Anton Paar MCR 302 rheometer, rheological analyses were carried out on different recipes of the polymer solution under varied parameters of the polymer to investigate the viscosity change under their influence. The shear rate was set in the range of 1 to 100 s− 1 to model the dependence of SAV10 viscosity on shear rate. To encompass a broader range of concentration, the polymer concentration was varied between 1000 and 4000 ppm. To model the thermal behaviour of SAV10, the range of temperature was set from 25 to 80 °C. Finally, the effect of salinity on SAV10 viscosity was captured by using 2-, 4-, and 6-times diluted seawater. Table 2 presents the dataset employed in this study. A total of 1079 data points were collected for the polymer.

Machine learning workflow

The machine learning workflow used in this study consisted of several sequential steps, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The process began with data collection and concluded with trained models capable of predicting the target parameters for new input combinations.

The data acquired from the rheological experiments was used to develop a model that can predict rheology under the given parameters. To identify the most effective predictive models for forecasting the target parameter of viscosity, a range of basic and advanced machine learning algorithms were tested and compared. These models vary in complexity, interpretability, and computational demands, offering different strengths depending on the nature of the dataset and the relationships between input features and target responses. Model performance was assessed using two statistical indicators: Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE). RMSE was considered the primary criterion for evaluating predictive accuracy, as it assigns greater weight to larger errors and thus provides a more rigorous assessment of model robustness. MAE was used as a complementary metric to represent the average magnitude of prediction errors in an interpretable manner. The combination of these two metrics ensures a comprehensive evaluation of both overall precision and consistency.

Data preprocessing

Before training any ML model, the raw data must be transformed into a clean, structured format suitable for statistical modelling. This process is known as data preprocessing, a critical step that directly impacts the accuracy and stability of predictive models. To prepare the data for analysis, preprocessing was conducted using Python and standard scientific libraries, including pandas, numpy, scikit-learn, seaborn, and matplotlib. The steps followed during the preprocessing stage are elaborated below.

Data Import and Inspection

The results were first loaded into a Pandas Data Frame, a tabular structure widely used in data analysis. This step allows previewing data, verifying its structure, and identifying any missing or malformed values.

Exploratory data analysis (EDA)

EDA involved visual and statistical techniques to understand the nature of the dataset, such as checking data types (numerical, categorical), plotting histograms and boxplots to view distributions and detect outliers, and using correlation matrices to assess relationships between variables. EDA helps identify trends, unusual patterns, or redundant variables that might distort the model. For instance, if two inputs are strongly correlated, they may introduce noise rather than value.

Data cleaning

Because the dataset was generated from laboratory rheological measurements, minor experimental errors may introduce noise or outliers. This step involved removing empty or irrelevant rows, standardizing units, and handling any errors from equipment output. Clean data reduces uncertainty in model training. Even a single incorrect value, such as a negative viscosity value, can skew the results and reduce confidence in predictions. Identifying true anomalous measurements without removing physically meaningful nonlinear behavior was a key challenge. Careful exploratory data analysis and statistical screening were therefore necessary to distinguish measurement artifacts from legitimate high-viscosity or extreme-condition responses.

Feature scaling

ML algorithms often perform better when inputs are on the same scale. Scaling was implemented using scikit-learn’s standard scaler, where the standard deviation was 1 and the mean was 0. Scaling prevents features with large numeric ranges, such as salinity, from overpowering others (e.g., Temperature) and speeds up model convergence.

The dataset was divided into training and testing subsets using the train_test_split function from Scikit-learn’s model_selection module. The function randomly shuffles the complete dataset and partitions it into two mutually exclusive subsets according to the specified proportion. The parameter random_state = 42 was used to fix the random seed, ensuring reproducibility of the split and consistency of results.

Because the split is performed through random sampling across the entire dataset, the testing subset contains samples distributed over the full range of input variables (shear rate, concentration, temperature, and salinity), rather than being extracted from a specific experimental condition or localized region of the dataset. This guarantees that the test data are representative of the overall data distribution and variability.

Machine learning techniques

This study employed four simple machine learning (ML) models and two advanced models to predict polymer viscosity based on the experimental data collected for the SAV10 synthetic polymer. A brief description of each ML technique is presented hereafter.

Simple ML models

Linear Regression (LR)

Linear Regression (LR)

Linear Regression (LR)

As one of the simplest and most widely used statistical methods, linear regression models the relationship between a dependent variable36 and one or more independent variables (such as temperature, concentration, salinity, and shear rate) as a linear equation. While it may not capture highly non-linear relationships, it serves as a useful baseline model and can provide insights into the relative importance of different factors37. Linear regression’s simplicity makes it a good starting point for analysis and can reveal general trends in the data. It can also be extended to polynomial regression to capture some non-linear relationships38.

2.Support Vector Machine (SVM)

2.Support Vector Machine (SVM)

Support Vector Machine (SVM)

SVM is a powerful supervised learning algorithm used for both classification and regression tasks. In the context of viscosity prediction, SVM works by finding the hyperplane that best fits the data in a high-dimensional space. It is particularly effective when dealing with non-linear relationships between variables39. SVM’s strength lies in its ability to handle complex, non-linear relationships using kernel functions, which implicitly map the input data to a higher-dimensional space where linear separation becomes possible. This makes SVM well-suited for capturing the complex relationships between polymer properties and critical parameters that influence viscosity.

3.Decision Tree (DT)

3.Decision Tree (DT)

Decision Tree (DT)

This model uses a tree-like structure of decisions and their possible consequences. In regression tasks like viscosity prediction, decision trees partition the input space into regions and assign a prediction to each region38. They are valued for their interpretability and ability to capture complex relationships in data40. Decision trees have the advantage of being easily interpretable, allowing researchers to understand the hierarchical importance of different factors affecting polymer viscosity. They can capture non-linear relationships and interactions between variables without making assumptions about the underlying data distribution.

4.Artificial Neural Network (ANN)

4.Artificial Neural Network (ANN)

Artificial Neural Network (ANN)

Inspired by biological neural networks, ANNs are a class of machine learning models capable of learning complex, non-linear relationships in data. They consist of interconnected nodes organized in layers, with each connection having an associated weight that is adjusted during the learning process38. ANNs have shown remarkable success in various prediction tasks, including in the field of EOR41. The power of ANNs lies in their ability to approximate any continuous function, making them particularly well-suited for modelling complex, multi-dimensional relationships in polymer viscosity prediction. Their hierarchical structure allows them to learn both low-level features and high-level abstractions from the data.

To evaluate the prediction accuracy of the ML models, three performance indicators were used: R2, root mean square error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE). An R2 value closer to 1 implies strong predictability of the model and a good agreement between the actual and model response. On the other hand, the lower the RMSE values, the higher the accuracy of the model. Likewise, lower MEA values indicate an efficient and reliable model.

Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI)

Machine learning models, while highly accurate, often operate as black boxes, making it challenging to interpret the influence of individual features on predictions. To address this, Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques were implemented to elucidate how the models, particularly the best-performing model of the initial step, arrive at their viscosity predictions. XAI provides a bridge between complex model architectures and human understanding, ensuring that predictive performance is complemented by interpretability and transparency. In this study, model-agnostic explainability methods were selected to evaluate the relative importance and interaction of input variables with the predicted viscosity. Model-agnostic techniques were preferred because they separate the explanation mechanism from the learning algorithm, making them suitable for any model architecture. The chosen techniques, Permutation Feature Importance (PFI) and Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), provide complementary insights; PFI offers a global measure of variable significance, whereas SHAP delivers both global and local interpretability through feature contribution analysis53.

The workflow for XAI analysis involved training the best performing model followed by applying PFI and SHAP to quantify and visualize each feature’s effect in order to optimise feature selection. Inputs to these analyses included the trained model, the feature matrix, target vector, and error metrics (R², RMSE, and MAE). The results enabled the identification and removal of features with negligible contribution to model predictions, streamlining the input vector for greater efficiency and interpretability54,55.

Permutation Feature Importance (PFI)

Permutation Feature Importance (PFI) quantifies the dependence of model accuracy on each input feature by measuring how randomizing a feature’s values affects predictive performance. As a model-agnostic method, PFI disrupts the relationship between an individual feature and the target variable by shuffling the feature values while keeping others constant. The resulting change in the model’s error metric (e.g., RMSE or R²) represents the feature’s importance. A significant increase in error upon permutation implies that the feature plays a critical role in the model’s predictions, while minimal change indicates lesser influence. In this study, PFI was implemented using Scikit-learn’s permutation_importance function56. The technique was applied fifty times per feature to ensure statistical robustness. The Extra Trees model, being non-parametric and ensemble-based, benefits particularly from PFI since it captures non-linear interactions and random thresholds that can otherwise obscure direct feature ranking.

PFI’s main advantages lie in its simplicity and interpretability; it does not require model retraining and delivers an intuitive global importance measure. However, one limitation is the potential uncertainty regarding whether importance should be computed on training or test data, as this choice may influence the interpretation of feature relevance57. Nonetheless, PFI served as a robust and efficient first layer of model interpretation.

Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP)

To complement PFI and provide deeper interpretive granularity, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) were employed to quantify the contribution of each feature to the model’s predictions at both global and instance levels. The Shapley framework, derived from cooperative game theory58, assigns each feature an “attribution value” that represents its average marginal contribution to the overall prediction across all possible feature combinations. This ensures that the feature importance distribution adheres to four key axioms, efficiency, symmetry, dummy, and additivity, providing a mathematically fair and consistent explanation.

For this study, SHAP values were computed using the open-source SHAP library, which integrates seamlessly with tree-based models such as Extra Trees. The method decomposes each viscosity prediction into additive feature contributions, enabling visualization of how each variable influences the deviation of a specific prediction from the mean viscosity59. This dual capacity to explain both global trends (e.g., overall influence of temperature) and local predictions (e.g., contribution of salinity at a particular condition) makes SHAP especially powerful for polymer systems where feature interactions are non-linear and context dependent60. The integration of PFI and SHAP analyses provided a comprehensive interpretive framework, offering both statistical robustness and physical insight into the complex interactions governing polymer viscosity61.

Model selection and hyperparameter optimization

Machine learning model performance is highly sensitive to the choice of algorithm architecture and hyperparameters. Inappropriate parameter settings (e.g., tree depth, learning rate, kernel parameters, or number of neurons) can lead to underfitting or overfitting. Extensive hyperparameter tuning using cross-validation was therefore essential to balance model complexity and generalization capability, ensuring robust predictive performance across the full viscosity range62,63.

Results and discussion

Our study revealed a comparative analysis between different parameters to understand the change in viscosity with respect to shear rate, temperature, salinity, and polymer concentration. The study also compared four simple machine learning models: Linear Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Decision Tree (DT), and Artificial Neural Network (ANN), and two advanced ML models: Ensemble Models and Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) for predicting the viscosity of SAV10 synthetic polymer under varying conditions. Simpler models were included primarily as benchmarks to assess the performance gain achieved by advanced ensemble methods. They also provide interpretability references and offer practical alternatives in cases with limited data or computational resources, highlighting the relative advantages of the stacked ensemble approach.

Effect of polymer concentration

Figure 2 shows the trend of SAV10 viscosity as a function of polymer concentration at 25 °C and seawater as makeup brine. It can be seen from Fig. 2 that solution viscosity increases with increasing polymer concentration as a greater number of polymer molecules are available for hydration. At all concentrations, the solutions exhibit shear-thinning behavior, where viscosity decreases with increasing shear rate due to progressive alignment and disentanglement of polymer chains under flow64. The effect of polymer concentration is more pronounced at lower shear rates, indicating stronger intermolecular interactions and network formation in more concentrated solutions. At higher shear rates, the viscosity curves converge slightly, suggesting that chain orientation dominates over concentration effects in controlling flow resistance65.

Effect of salinity

The viscosity of the polymer solution increased as the dilution of seawater increased (decreasing salinity), as shown in Fig. 3. This means solutions with higher salinity exhibited lower viscosity compared to solutions with lower salinity. In more concentrated seawater, the presence of dissolved salts influences the conformation of the polymer chains. Stronger ionic interactions between the polymer and the salts lead to a more expanded conformation of the chains66. This expanded state experiences less friction and entanglement, resulting in lower viscosity. Conversely, in diluted seawater, these interactions are weaker, allowing the polymer chains to adopt a more coiled or entangled conformation. This more compact state experiences greater friction and entanglement, leading to higher viscosity67.

Effect of temperature

Temperature significantly impacted the viscosity of the polymer solution as expected. Figure 4 shows the decreasing trend in viscosity with increasing temperature at all shear rates investigated, aligning with the well-known effect of temperature on polymer chain mobility. This is because higher temperatures enhance thermal motion, making the chains more flexible and less likely to entangle, resulting in lower resistance to flow and consequently, lower viscosity68,69. Furthermore, the solution exhibited shear-thinning behaviour at all temperatures. Shear thinning refers to the decrease in viscosity with increasing shear rate. This commonly observed phenomenon arises from the alignment and stretching of polymer chains under applied shear forces. Notably, the magnitude of the change in viscosity with temperature appeared more pronounced at lower shear rates. For example, the viscosity reduction between 25 °C and 80 °C was greater at 10 s− 1 compared to 100 s− 1. This suggests that at lower shear rates, the polymer chains are more entangled and rigid. The increased thermal motion at higher temperatures has a more significant effect on disrupting these entanglements, leading to a larger decrease in viscosity.

Exploratory data analysis and response distributions

Before the implementation of machine learning models, an extensive exploratory data analysis (EDA) was conducted to examine the characteristics of the dataset and understand the relationships between the input parameters and the target variable, viscosity. This analysis is critical for identifying underlying trends, potential data imbalance, and correlations that could influence model performance.

Correlation matrix analysis

Figure 5 presents the Pearson correlation matrix computed across all primary input features — shear rate, polymer concentration, temperature, and salinity — as well as the response variable, viscosity. The correlation matrix highlights several notable relationships. Polymer concentration demonstrated a strong positive correlation with viscosity (r = 0.71), indicating that higher polymer concentrations generally led to increased solution viscosity. This is consistent with polymer solution behaviour where an increased number of polymer chains in the medium results in higher entanglement and resistance to flow70,71. Shear rate and temperature both exhibited moderate negative correlations with viscosity (r = − 0.29), suggesting that increasing shear rate or temperature tends to reduce viscosity. This aligns with shear-thinning behaviour and thermal effects on polymer chain mobility, respectively72.

Salinity had a weaker, yet still negative, correlation with viscosity (r = − 0.21), highlighting the salting-out effect typical of partially hydrolyzed polyacrylamides in brine, where higher ionic strength may cause coil contraction and reduced viscosity. Interestingly, the matrix indicates minimal intercorrelation among the independent variables themselves (off-diagonal correlations near zero), suggesting low multicollinearity. This independence is advantageous for machine learning regression tasks as it enables each feature to contribute unique variance to the prediction of viscosity.

Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI)

Model explainability and interpretability

Machine learning models, while highly capable of capturing complex nonlinear relationships, often lack transparency in how predictions are derived. This “black-box” nature can limit their acceptance in scientific applications, where understanding the driving mechanisms behind predictions is essential85. Therefore, explainable artificial intelligence methods were implemented in this study to elucidate the internal reasoning of the chosen predictive model.

The Extra Trees Regressor was identified as the most accurate and stable model for predicting polymer viscosity after comprehensive testing and cross-validation. To interpret its predictive behavior, two model-agnostic explainability techniques, Permutation Feature Importance (PFI) and SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), were applied86. These approaches provide complementary insights: PFI quantifies the effect of each feature on model performance, while SHAP explains the contribution of each feature to individual and global predictions. Together, they enhance the interpretability and trustworthiness of the predictive framework.

Feature importance derived from the extra trees model

The intrinsic feature importance obtained from the Extra Trees Regressor is shown in Fig. 18. This method evaluates feature relevance based on the reduction in prediction error across all decision trees in the ensemble. The analysis revealed that polymer concentration is the most influential variable, contributing approximately 52.0% of the total model importance. Shear rate followed with 23.0%, while temperature and salinity accounted for 13.2% and 11.9%, respectively. These findings are consistent with established polymer rheology principles87. Polymer concentration directly controls molecular entanglement and chain overlapping, which markedly increases solution viscosity. Shear rate exerts a negative effect on viscosity through shear-thinning behavior typical of non-Newtonian polymeric fluids. Temperature and salinity, although less dominant, remain physically significant, temperature affects chain flexibility and intermolecular interactions, while salinity influences the ionic environment, leading to the contraction of polymer chains.

Permutation feature importance analysis

To ensure robustness beyond model-dependent metrics, Permutation Feature Importance was conducted. PFI measures the change in model performance after randomly shuffling each input feature, effectively breaking its relationship with the target variable. A larger decrease in the model’s R2 value signifies a greater dependence on that feature57.

Two PFI analyses were performed; one using cross-validation and another on the test dataset (Fig. 19). Both results consistently identified concentration as the most critical feature. In the cross-validation PFI, the mean R2 reduction caused by permuting concentration was 1.40, followed by shear rate (0.54), temperature (0.31), and salinity (0.29). In the test-set PFI, the corresponding reductions were 1.58, 0.62, 0.38, and 0.30, respectively.

The close agreement between the training and testing analyses demonstrates the model’s generalizability and confirms that all four features contribute meaningfully to the prediction of polymer viscosity. No feature exhibited redundancy or spurious influence, leading to the decision to retain all variables in the final model. This conclusion aligns with the findings of Otchere et al.88, who emphasized the importance of model-agnostic techniques in identifying causally relevant predictors rather than purely correlative ones.

SHAP global feature importance

The SHAP framework provides an alternative yet complementary method to evaluate feature influence. Originating from cooperative game theory58, SHAP assigns each feature a Shapley value, representing its average marginal contribution to the model’s output across all possible feature combinations. Unlike PFI, SHAP not only quantifies global feature importance but also provides local interpretability, explaining individual predictions. The global SHAP summary plots (Fig. 20) reveal consistent feature rankings across both training and testing datasets. Concentration exhibited the highest mean absolute SHAP value, with averages of 5.95 (train) and 5.90 (test). Shear rate, temperature, and salinity followed with mean |SHAP| values of 2.92, 2.48, and 2.04 for the training data, and 2.82, 2.63, and 1.95 for the test data. This close agreement confirms that each feature exerts a stable and interpretable influence on the viscosity predictions.

SHAP Beeswarm analysis and physical interpretations

While the global SHAP summary quantifies the overall magnitude of influence, the Beeswarm analysis (Fig. 21) provides a more detailed picture of feature behavior and directionality. Each point in these plots represents a single prediction instance, with the color indicating the feature’s actual value (blue for low, red for high).

Fig. 21SHAP Beeswarm analysis for feature importance on test data.

SHAP Beeswarm analysis for feature importance on test data.

The horizontal position corresponds to the SHAP value, which measures the feature’s impact on the model output; this visualization simultaneously conveys feature importance, direction, variability, and value dependence, making it one of the most informative interpretability tools in explainable AI reports89. The beeswarm analysis revealed several meaningful trends. Concentration displayed a strong positive correlation with viscosity; higher concentrations shifted SHAP values positively, indicating increased viscosity due to enhanced polymer entanglement and reduced solvent mobility; a pattern similar to how SHAP diagrams have been used to interpret structure–property relationships in polymer viscosity modeling90. Shear rate showed a negative correlation; as shear rate increased, viscosity predictions decreased, reflecting the shear-thinning nature of polymeric fluids, also captured through SHAP values in complex material systems91. Temperature exerted a moderate negative effect, consistent with the decrease in viscosity at higher temperatures as thermal agitation disrupts molecular interactions. Salinity had a weaker but systematic impact, whereby higher salinity levels slightly reduced viscosity, likely due to polymer chain contraction caused by ionic shielding. These interactions confirm that the Extra Trees model captures physically interpretable rheological behavior rather than spurious correlations. The alignment of SHAP-derived insights with polymer science principles validates both the credibility and scientific consistency of the predictive framework90.

Ensemble model optimization

The basic idea here was to run the best hyperparameters and optimize them using Bayesian optimization to ensure the best possible performance, a strategy shown in recent ensemble regression studies to improve both accuracy and generalization by systematically searching the hyperparameter space rather than relying on manual tuning or grid search92,93.

Stacking regressor. To further advance predictive accuracy beyond individual ensemble models, a stacking ensemble approach was tested. Stacking combines multiple base learners to exploit their diverse strengths, training a meta-learner to optimally blend their predictions. Unlike bagging or boosting, which aggregate over similar models (often trees) by voting or weighted error corrections, stacking explicitly learns how to best combine heterogeneous models’ outputs, thereby reducing both bias and variance more effectively in complex, multi-factor regression94,95.

Base models and hyperparameter optimization. The stacking regressor was constructed with three carefully optimized base learners, including Extra Trees Regressor, XGBoost Regressor, and CatBoost Regressor. Hyperparameter optimization for these constituent models was conducted using Bayesian search strategies embedded within cross‑validation loops to efficiently locate configurations yielding superior predictive performance96. The optimized hyperparameters for each regressor are reported in Table 10. The three base models were collectively stacked under a Ridge regression meta-learner (alpha = 1.0) to learn optimal linear combinations of their predictions. The stack was trained using a 10-fold cross-validation (CV) within the stacking framework to ensure robust blending.

GPR model optimization

An optimization campaign was conducted to fine-tune the Gaussian Process Regression model’s hyperparameters and achieve the best generalization performance for viscosity prediction among the GPR models. The optimized hyperparameters included the kernel function, sigma, basis function, and data standardization options. The optimization identified the Nonisotropic Matern 3/2 kernel as the best-performing kernel function, with a constant basis function and standardized inputs. The optimized sigma value was 0.0091, which represents the noise level in the data. As shown in Fig. 25, the optimization curve indicates that the minimum MSE (0.11715) was achieved early in the iterations, confirming a stable convergence toward the global minimum109.

The optimized GPR model showed excellent predictive accuracy. During validation, the model achieved an RMSE of 0.3423, MAE of 0.1897, and R² of 0.999. Testing on unseen data further confirmed its robustness, yielding an RMSE of 0.3975, MAE of 0.1828, and R² of 0.998. The high R² value and consistent RMSE across training and testing sets confirm the model’s strong generalization and minimal overfitting. Conclusions

In this research, various machine learning techniques, including linear regression, support vector machine, decision tree, artificial neural network, and advanced Ensemble ML models, were employed to predict the bulk viscosity of SAV10 polymer as a function of concentration, shear rate, temperature, and salinity. The results yielded the following conclusions.

From the classical machine learning models deployed in this study, Wide ANN resulted in an acceptable R2 value of 0.998, RMSE of 0.31 and MAE of 0.22. The optimum performance in terms of accurate viscosity predictions while minimizing root mean squared error was obtained by the Stacking Ensemble model that was developed by stacking three best-performing Ensemble regressors with their optimized hyperparameters. The optimized Ensemble model yielded an R2 of 0.998 with the lowest RMSE of 0.208, and MAE of 0.22 among all the tested ML models.

The integration of feature importance, PFI, and SHAP analyses provided a comprehensive understanding of how each variable influences polymer viscosity predictions. All four input features, i.e. concentration, shear rate, temperature, and salinity, play essential roles and were therefore retained in the final model. Concentration is the most dominant parameter, followed by shear rate, temperature, and salinity, in agreement across all XAI methods. Both PFI and SHAP analyses confirmed the model’s robustness and interpretability, demonstrating consistency between training and testing datasets. The Extra Trees Regressor effectively balances predictive power and explainability, making it a reliable tool for modeling polymer viscosity behaviors.

Overall, the stacked Ensemble model demonstrated exceptional capability in modeling the complex interplay between shear rate, polymer concentration, temperature, and salinity on solution viscosity. Its performance metrics and graphical diagnostics were confirmed as the most accurate and reliable predictive framework in this study, making it an ideal candidate for deployment in polymer flooding operational planning. Our study demonstrates the successful application of data analysis tools for oilfield problems such as predicting the viscosity of polymer solutions in a fast and reliable manner within the ranges of input parameters used in this study. Moreover, the viscosity of other polymers can be modelled using analogous ML models, and the prediction power of the model can be further improved by expanding the range of the input parameters.

Despite these promising outcomes, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The current models were trained on laboratory-scale data for a single polymer type (SAV10), which may limit their generalizability to other polymer chemistries, salinity ranges, or field-scale conditions. In addition, the dataset size, though sufficient for model convergence, may not fully capture the variability encountered under dynamic reservoir conditions. As a forward-looking perspective, future work could investigate the use of additives or formulation strategies (e.g., co-polymers, crosslinkers, nano-additives, or salinity modifiers) to deliberately tailor the rheological behavior of the polymer toward specific operational objectives. Formulation-related parameters such as additive concentration, molecular weight distribution, degree of hydrolysis, or nanoparticle loading could then be incorporated as additional input features in similar machine learning frameworks. This integration of formulation chemistry with data-driven modeling represents a promising direction toward more generalized and adaptive viscosity prediction tools for complex reservoir environments.
