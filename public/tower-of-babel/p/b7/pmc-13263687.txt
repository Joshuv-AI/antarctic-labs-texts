Low net energy diet compromises pork tenderness by promoting larger muscle fibers and diminishing intramuscular fat through fiber type reprogramming
Authors: Hongzhi Wu, Xilong Yu, Jianlou Song, Fengjie Ji, Chengjun Hu, Weiqi Peng, Liangmei Xu, Renlong Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate the effects of low net energy (NE) diets on the meat quality of Tunchang pigs, a total of 96 Tunchang pigs at 55 d of age and with an initial body weight of 26.42 ± 1.04 kg were randomly assigned to four treatment groups with NE levels of 9.82 (control group [CG]), 9.57 (EY1), 9.32 (EY2), and 9.07 MJ/kg (EY3). Each treatment consisted of 6 replicates with 4 pigs per replicate. The trial lasted for 63 d. The results showed that dietary NE levels had no significant effects on average daily gain or feed conversion ratio (P > 0.05), whereas the average daily feed intake was significantly higher in the EY3 group than other groups (P = 0.018). Compared with CG group, pigs in the EY3 group exhibited higher shear force (P = 0.008) and lower intramuscular fat (IMF) content (P = 0.046) in the longissimus dorsi muscle. In addition, low NE diets increased muscle fiber diameter, cross-sectional area, and relative mRNA expression of MYHC IIb (P < 0.05), while reducing the relative mRNA expression of MYHC I and MYHC IIa (P < 0.05). Proteomic analysis indicated that low-NE diets modulated key cytoskeletal proteins in muscle cells, including myosin-binding protein C slow type, myosin regulatory light chain 3, troponin C slow type, myosin heavy chain 2, and cysteine and glycine-rich protein 3, thereby promoting a shift from oxidative to glycolytic muscle fibers. Metabolomic results further confirmed this fiber-type transition, showing an accumulation of glycolytic metabolites, depletion of tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates, and reduced biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acid metabolites. Overall, reducing dietary NE to 9.32 MJ/kg had no adverse effects on growth performance or meat quality of Tunchang pigs; however, a further reduction to 9.07 MJ/kg induced glycolytic fiber remodeling, increased muscle fiber diameter and cross-sectional area, and decreased IMF deposition, ultimately impairing meat tenderness.Keywords: Tunchang pig, Low net energy diet, Meat quality, Muscle fiber type, Intramuscular fat

Introduction

As the world’s largest pork producer, China’s pig farming industry generates an immense demand for feed (Hasan et al., 2024). At present, the structure of China’s compound feed remains predominantly reliant on corn and soybean meal. However, domestic corn production has long fallen short of meeting the demand, resulting in increasing annual imports, approximately 80% of which are utilized in feed production. Furthermore, fluctuations in international trade have significantly driven up corn prices, thereby exacerbating the costs associated with breeding (Shen and Qiu, 2024). It is reported that energy feed constitutes as much as 70% of the total feed costs in pig production, representing a significant proportion of overall production expenses (Noblet et al., 2022). Therefore, against the backdrop of tightening corn supply and rising prices, exploring the impact of low-energy diets on pig production performance is of considerable practical significance for reducing feed costs while maintaining production efficiency.

Pig production is predominantly driven by the demand for meat. The quality attributes of meat, such as color, tenderness, flavor, and water-holding capacity, are largely determined by the muscle fiber type composition and intramuscular fat (IMF) deposition, both of which are influenced by muscle energy metabolism and storage (Yan et al., 2023). Muscle energy metabolism primarily involves the process by which muscle mitochondria utilize energy substrates, such as glucose and glycogen, to generate ATP. Based on their oxidative characteristics of glycogen utilization, muscle fibers can be categorized into oxidative fibers (type I and type IIa), glycolytic fibers (type IIb), and intermediate fibers (type IIx) (Li et al., 2025). Muscles dominated by oxidative fibers generally exhibit higher IMF content (Yan et al., 2022). Therefore, dietary energy levels may influence meat quality by affecting muscle energy metabolism, which subsequently modulates muscle fiber type composition and IMF deposition. The current body of research predominantly suggests that low-energy diets generally lead to a reduction in IMF content and induce alterations in fatty acid composition, as demonstrated by Choi et al. (2008) and Razmaite et al. (2021). However, the effects of such diets on muscle fiber type composition and the mechanisms underpinning these changes remain poorly understood.

Tunchang pig is an indigenous breed from Hainan, China, valued for its robustness, adaptability, and high meat quality (Wang et al., 2024a). However, its commercial expansion is constrained by a slow growth rate and low economic efficiency. Implementing low-energy diets presents a viable strategy to mitigate these issues by reducing feed costs and improving profitability. Given the breed’s innate tolerance for roughage and its high IMF content (Liu et al., 2024), it is hypothesized that a moderate reduction in dietary energy would preserve its production performance. Consequently, investigating the effects of low-energy diets on Tunchang pigs could provide a valuable strategy for enhancing the breed’s commercial viability.

The net energy (NE) system enables a more precise assessment of feed energy content, allowing pig energy requirements to be met more accurately and feed costs to be reduced (Li et al., 2018; Velayudhan et al., 2015). Accordingly, this study will formulate low NE diets based on this system, with the objective of systematically investigating their effects on myofiber type composition, IMF deposition, and meat quality in Tunchang pigs. Furthermore, proteomic and metabolomic analyses were integrated to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Material and methods

Animal ethic statement

The management and care procedures for the animals used in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Chinese Academy of Tropical Agricultural Sciences (approval No.: CATAS-20241018–2).

Animals, diets, and experimental design

The trial employed 96 Tunchang pigs (48 gilts and 48 castrated barrows) sourced from Bangxi Town Farm in Baisha Li Autonomous County, Hainan, China. The initial body weight of the 55-d-old pigs was 26.42 ± 1.04 kg. The pigs were randomly assigned to four distinct dietary treatments, with each treatment comprising six replicates and each replicate containing two gilts and two castrated barrows. Based on previous research findings that a 3.2% reduction in dietary NE levels showed no significant impact on the growth performance of finishing pigs, whereas approximately a 5.0% reduction may adversely affect it (Aymerich et al., 2020; Fu et al., 2023). Considering the strong adaptive capacity of Tunchang pigs, this study adopted a conservative decremental gradient of 0.25 MJ/kg to formulate four corn-soybean meal-based diets with NE levels of 9.82, 9.57, 9.32, and 9.07 MJ/kg, designated as CG, EY1, EY2, and EY3, respectively. All diets complied with the Swine Nutrient Requirements (GB/T 39235–2020) (China National Standard, 2020). Ingredients and nutrient levels of diets is shown in Table 1. Pigs were housed individually in pens randomly distributed throughout the same barn, with ad libitum access to feed and water. The experiment lasted for 63 d. Housing conditions were maintained using a natural ventilation system. The pigs’ living environment was meticulously maintained through daily removal of manure. Additionally, their feeding behavior, activity levels, excretion patterns, and overall health status were regularly monitored and closely observed.

Feed chemical analyses

Net energy and lysine levels were calculated according to the China Feed Database (2023). The crude protein content was analyzed using the Kjeldahl method, in accordance with GB/T 6432-2018 (China National Standard, 2018d) and an automatic nitrogen analyzer (Kjeltec 8400, FOSS Analytical A/S, Horsens, Denmark) was employed for the analysis. The crude fat content was analyzed using the Soxhlet extraction method in accordance with GB/T 6433-2006 (China National Standard, 2018c), employing a crude fat analyzer (SZF-06A, Shanghai Xingjia Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The crude fiber content was analyzed in accordance with GB/T 6434-2022 (China National Standard, 2022) using a crude fiber analyzer (SLQ-6A, Shanghai Xingjia Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The crude ash content was determined in accordance with GB/T 6438-2007 (China National Standard, 2018a) utilizing a muffle furnace (MF-1100C–S, Anhui BEQ Equipment Technology Co., Ltd., Hefei, Anhui, China). Organic matter content was calculated as the difference between dry matter content and crude ash content. The calcium content in the feed was analyzed in accordance with GB/T 6436-2018 (China National Standard, 2018b). The phosphorus content was analyzed using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (UV-6100, Shanghai Metash Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) following GB/T 6437-2018 (China National Standard, 2018e).

Growth performance

The initial and final body weights of each Tunchang pig were measured on d 1 and 63, respectively. The average daily gain (ADG) was subsequently computed based on these two sets of body weight data. Throughout the experimental period, the daily feed allocation for each pig was meticulously recorded, and any residual feed was also carefully noted. These records enabled the determination of the average daily feed intake (ADFI). The feed conversion ratio (FCR) was then calculated as the quotient of ADFI divided by ADG.

Sample collection, carcass traits, and organ indices

At the end of the experiment, after a 12-h overnight fasting period, one pig close to the average body weight was selected from each replicate group and slaughtered at a commercial slaughterhouse, following electrical stunning and exsanguination. The internal organs were removed, and the carcass was split vertically along the midline. The weights of the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were recorded to calculate the organ indexes. The organ index was calculated as the ratio of organ weight (g) to body weight (g). The hot carcass weight was then recorded, and the dressing percentage was calculated based on this data. Subsequently, the backfat thickness at the first and last ribs, as well as the last lumbar vertebra, was measured on the left half of the carcass using a vernier caliper. The loin eye area (cm2) was measured at the 10th rib and calculated as height × width × 0.7. The left half of the carcass was meticulously dissected by a professional meat technician in accordance with standard commercial requirements. This process involved the careful separation of lean meat, fat, bone, and skin. The percentages of skin, bone, and lean meat were then calculated in line with the technical specifications for measuring the carcass traits of lean-type pigs. Fat percentage was calculated as the ratio of adipose tissue mass to hot carcass weight. The longissimus dorsi (LD) muscle was excised from the right carcass half, with subsections allocated for molecular, histological, and meat quality analyses. Molecular biology samples were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen after being wrapped in aluminum foil and stored at −80 °C for subsequent proteomic and metabolomic profiling. Histological specimens were fixed in 4% formaldehyde for later myofiber morphological assessment. The remaining LD portions were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −20 °C pending physicochemical evaluation of meat quality.

Meat quality and nutritional measurements

Following slaughter, the LD muscle pH was measured at 45 min and 24 h post–slaughter using a calibrated portable pH meter (PHS-3E, REX Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) (Garrido et al., 1995). The color parameters lightness (L∗), redness (a∗), and yellowness (b∗) were determined on the intact, exposed surface of the peeled LD muscle at 45 min postmortem, subsequent to a standard blooming period, with a handheld chroma meter (CR-400, Konica Minolta Holdings, Inc., Osaka, Japan) (Rahman et al., 2020). The pH and color values of each sample were measured in triplicate, and the mean of these measurements was used as the final result. To quantify drip loss, LD samples were cut into small cubes (2 cm × 2 cm × 2 cm), oriented parallel to the muscle fiber direction. These cubes were then weighed to obtain the initial weight (W1). Subsequently, the samples were suspended in polyethylene bags and stored at 4 °C for a period of 24 h. After this storage period, the samples were reweighed to obtain the final weight (W2). For shear force determination, entire LD samples (6 cm × 3 cm × 3 cm) were used, with surface connective tissue and fat removed. A thermometer was inserted into the geometric center of the sample, which was subsequently placed in an 80 °C water bath until the core temperature reached 70 °C. After cooling to 4 °C, cylindrical cores 1.27 cm in diameter were drilled parallel to the muscle fiber direction, and shear force was measured using a digital shear device (C-LM3B, Tenovo International Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), with three replicates per sample being averaged to obtain the final results. The concentration of inosine monophosphate (IMP) was measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) on an Agilent 1200 series instrument (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA), following a procedure adapted from a previous study (Tikk et al., 2006). Briefly, 5 g of minced LD muscle was homogenized in 10 mL of 0.1 mol/L perchloric acid at 4 °C for 15 min, centrifuged at 1840 × g for 10 min, after which the supernatant was collected; the residue was subsequently re-extracted with 5 mL of perchloric acid and centrifuged once more. The combined supernatant was adjusted to a pH of 6.4 using 1 mol/L potassium hydroxide, then diluted to a final volume of 50 mL. Subsequently, it was filtered through a 0.45-μm millipore syringe filter prior to HPLC analysis. The HPLC conditions were as follows: an Agilent ZORBAX SB-C18 column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm); mobile phase A, 0.01 mol/L potassium dihydrogen phosphate, mobile phase B, methanol; column temperature 30 °C; flow rate 1.0 mL/min; injection volume 20 μL; and UV detection wavelength 254 nm. Finally, the freeze-dried muscle was ground into powder. The IMF content was determined via Soxhlet extraction using petroleum ether as the solvent (Wang et al., 2019). Meanwhile, the crude protein content in the muscle samples was measured using the Kjeldahl method, with a conversion factor of 6.25 (Benedict, 1987).

Hydrolytic amino acid analysis

The hydrolytic amino acid profile of freeze-dried LD muscle was determined using an amino acid analyzer (LC5090, Fuli Development Group Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). A 0.25 g sample was hydrolyzed with 5 mL of 6 mol/L hydrochloric acid in a sealed glass tube at 110 °C for 24 h. After hydrolysis, the resulting solution, along with triple rinses of the tube using ultrapure water, was transferred to an evaporation dish and concentrated to dryness in an 80 °C water bath. The residue was subsequently reconstituted by washing the dish multiple times with derivatization buffer, and the combined eluates were adjusted to 25 mL in a volumetric flask using the same buffer. The final solution was filtered through a 0.45 μm microporous membrane prior to analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed on an Ultimate AQ-C18 column (5 μm, 4.6 mm × 250 mm; Welch Materials Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) maintained at 27 °C, with detection at 360 nm. Quantification of hydrolytic amino acids was achieved using the external standard method, based on calibration curves established from standard amino acid solutions (Rittich and Standara, 1992).

Fatty acid composition analysis

The fatty acid composition of the LD muscle samples was analyzed using an Agilent 7090B gas chromatograph equipped with a flame ionization detector (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) and an Agilent J&W CP-Sil 88 FAME capillary column (100 m × 0.25 mm × 0.20 μm; Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA), following the previously described method (Martin et al., 2008). Fatty acids were identified by comparing retention times with those of authentic standards (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Hesse, Germany). Quantification was performed by normalizing the peak area, and the results were expressed as percentages of the total fatty acids.

Myofibrillar morphology

The diameter and cross-sectional area of muscle fibers were evaluated using the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining method. Muscle tissue samples were first fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h to preserve cellular morphology. Following fixation, samples were rinsed thoroughly under running tap water overnight to remove residual fixative. Subsequently, the tissues were dehydrated through a graded acetone series (70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%; 30 min at each concentration) to gradually remove water. After dehydration, the samples were cleared in xylene and embedded in paraffin wax. Serial transverse sections (thickness: 5 μm) were obtained from the paraffin-embedded blocks using a rotary microtome (RM2235, Leica Biosystems Nussloch GmbH, Wetzlar, Hessen, Germany). The sections were mounted on glass slides, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through a descending ethanol series (100%, 95%, 80%, and 70%) before undergoing H&E staining. Hematoxylin was applied for 5 min to stain nuclei, followed by eosin for 2 min to stain the cytoplasm. After staining, slides were dehydrated, cleared, and coverslipped using neutral resin. Histological images were captured using a DMi8 inverted microscope (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Hessen, Germany) equipped with a digital imaging system. For each Tunchang pig, 60 well-preserved and randomly selected muscle fibers were measured from non-overlapping fields. The diameter and cross-sectional area of individual fibers were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). All measurements were conducted by the same trained observer to minimize variability.

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the LD muscle using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). RNA integrity and quality were evaluated by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, while RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) to eliminate potential genomic DNA contamination. Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted using the Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA), with β-actin serving as the internal reference gene. Relative mRNA expression levels of MYHC isoforms MYHC I, MYHC IIa, MYHC IIb, and MYHC IIx were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to β-actin expression. The full names of the abbreviations of all genes can be found in Table S1. Primer sequences are provided in Table S2. Final gene expression values were reported as the average relative expression normalized to the internal control.

Proteomics analysis

The LD muscle samples were cryogenically ground, and an appropriate volume of SDT protein lysis buffer (containing 100 mmol/L NaCl) was added. The mixture was vortexed and subjected to ultrasonic treatment, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected, and iodoacetamide was added. The solution was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 1 h. To precipitate proteins, four volumes of pre-chilled acetone (−20 °C) were added, and the precipitate was subsequently dissolved in an appropriate volume of DB buffer. Protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford assay kit (Beyotime Biotech. Inc., Shanghai, China). Each sample was adjusted to a final volume of 100 μL with DB lysis buffer (8 mol/L urea, 100 m mol/L triethylammonium bicarbonate, pH 8.5), followed by digestion with trypsin in the presence of 100 mmol/L triethylammonium bicarbonate. After digestion, the solution was acidified with formic acid and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was collected and purified using a C18 desalting column, which was washed three times with wash buffer (0.10% formic acid, 3.00% acetonitrile). Peptides were eluted using elution buffer (0.10% formic acid, 70% acetonitrile), and the eluate was lyophilized for storage. Peptide separation was performed using an L-3000 HPLC system (Scilogex, LLC, Rocky Hill, CT, USA) on a Waters BEH C18 column (4.60 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm). Fractions were collected at 1-min intervals and subsequently pooled into 10 combined fractions. These were lyophilized and reconstituted in 0.10% formic acid. Further analysis was carried out using the EASY-nLC 1200 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA), coupled to a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA), generating raw data files (.raw). Protein identification was performed using the UniProt database via Proteome Discoverer 2.5 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Identified peptides and proteins were filtered using a 1% false discovery rate threshold to ensure high confidence in the results. Quantitative analysis was conducted using a Student’s t-test to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). Volcano plot visualization and kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed for the DEPs, and protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were predicted using the STRING database.

Western blotting

For total protein extraction, 0.1 g of LD muscle tissue was placed into a 2.0 mL centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of 500 μL of radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime Biotech. Inc., Shanghai, China) was added, along with one sterile steel bead. The tissue was homogenized using a grinder at a frequency of 60 Hz for 60 s, followed by centrifugation at 11,300 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was then carefully collected for further analysis. The protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (P0010S, Beyotime Biotech. Inc., Shanghai, China). Equal quantities of protein were resolved by 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Following transfer, the membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in tris-buffered saline with tween 20 and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including anti-MYHC I (MA5-43923), anti-MYHC IIa (PA5-119293), anti-MYHC IIb (14–6503-82), anti-MYHC IIx (PA5-145317), anti-CSRP3 (PA5-29155), anti-MAPK12 (PA5-17371), and anti-GAPDH (MA5-15738). The full names of the abbreviations of all proteins can be found in Table S1. All antibodies were sourced from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (Waltham, MA, USA) and diluted at a concentration of 1:1000. The membranes were subsequently washed three times with tris-buffered saline with tween 20 (10 min each) and then incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cat. No. 31460 and 31430, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) diluted at 1:5000 for 1 h at room temperature. Following the washing procedure, the protein bands were visualized employing an enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (P10100, Suzhou Xinsaimei Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, Jiangsu, China). Subsequently, the images were captured using a ChemiDoc XRS + imaging system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA). The grayscale intensity of the protein bands was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), with GAPDH serving as an internal reference for normalization.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

A 100 mg aliquot of liquid nitrogen-pulverized LD muscle sample was homogenized in 200 μL of 80% methanol, vortexed briefly, incubated on ice for 5 min, and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was diluted with MS-grade water to a final concentration of 53% methanol and centrifuged again under the same conditions. For liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis, metabolites were separated using a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with a Hypersil GOLD column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm particle size). The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and methanol (solvent B) for positive ion mode, or 5 mmol/L ammonium acetate (pH 9.0, solvent A) and methanol (solvent B) for negative ion mode. The gradient program was as follows: 0–3 min, 2% B; 3–10 min, 85% B; 10.0–10.1 min, 100% B; and 10.1–12.0 min, 2% B, with a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min at 40 °C. The eluates were ionized using electrospray ionization (ESI) and analyzed with a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) under the following conditions: spray voltage, 3.5 kV; sheath gas flow rate, 35 psi; auxiliary gas flow rate, 10 L/min; capillary temperature, 320 °C; S-lens RF level, 60; and auxiliary gas heater temperature, 350 °C. Raw data files (.raw) were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) for metabolite detection, alignment, peak extraction, and quantification. Metabolite identification was performed through database matching with mzCloud, mzVault, and MassList, followed by normalization and relative quantification. Data analysis was conducted using Linux-based platforms and implemented in R and Python. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was employed to assess differences among treatment groups, with permutation tests used to validate model robustness. Differential metabolites (DMs) were identified based on t-tests and variable importance in projection scores, and subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment analysis.

Proteomic profile of the longissimus dorsi muscle tissue

In this study, mass spectrometry analysis of LD muscle samples from Tunchang pigs subjected to different dietary NE levels generated a total of 1,455,733 MS/MS spectra, from which 648,873 valid spectra were retained. At a false discovery rate of 1%, a total of 19,714 peptides and 1769 proteins were identified. Functional annotation of the identified proteins was conducted using general databases, including KEGG, GO, and COG. As illustrated in the Venn diagram (Fig. 2A), KEGG provided the most comprehensive functional coverage, annotating 1441 proteins. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that these proteins were primarily associated with pathways such as Global and overview maps, signal transduction, and the Endocrine system (Fig. S1A). Principal component analysis showed that the first two components accounted for 12.83% and 11.59% of the total variance, respectively (Fig. 2B). The CG formed a distinct cluster, clearly separated from the low NE groups, indicating a unique proteomic profile. Hierarchical clustering based on Euclidean distance further supported these findings: the treatment groups were divided into two major clusters, with the CG forming one cluster and the EY1, EY2, and EY3 groups forming the other (Fig. S1B). Collectively, these results suggest that dietary NE restriction induces a distinct shift in the muscle proteome, separating the CG from the energy-restricted treatments.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

The KEGG pathway database serves as a comprehensive resource for the systematic integration of biological pathways, metabolic reactions, and associated proteins, facilitating functional annotation and network-based analysis of proteins. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DEPs in the LD muscle revealed distinct functional patterns across comparison groups.

In the EY1 vs. CG comparison, DEPs were predominantly enriched in pathways related to cytoskeleton in muscle cells (LOC100736765, MYBPC1, NRAP, MYL3, CSRP3, TNNC1, and NID2), motor proteins (LOC100736765, MYL3, MYL6B, and TNNC1), and cardiac muscle contraction (LOC100736765, MYL3, and TNNC1) (Fig. 3A). For the EY2 vs. CG group, significant enrichment was observed in Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis (BIN1 and ACTR2), tight junction (MYL6B and ACTR2), and cholesterol metabolism (APOH) (Fig. 3C). Establishment of PPI network and correlation analysis

Protein–protein interaction analysis elucidates the intensity of intracellular activities mediated by physical interactions, including van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonds, and hydrophobic interactions, among two or more proteins, which are crucial for driving essential biological processes. The PPI analysis of DEPs was performed using the StringDB database, and the resulting interaction networks were visualized using Wekemo Bioincloud (Gao et al., 2024). As depicted in Fig. 3B and D, the PPI networks of EY1 vs. CG and EY2 vs. CG each predominantly consisted of a single central cluster, comprising 30 and 22 nodes (proteins) with 54 and 22 edges (interactions), respectively. Both clusters featured CSRP3 as the central hub node and were functionally associated with cytoskeletal organization and signal transduction. Similarly, the PPI network of EY3 vs. CG exhibited a central cluster containing 43 nodes and 68 edges, with CSRP3 and MAPK12 as the primary hub nodes, representing cytoskeletal organization, signal transduction, and pyruvate metabolism (Fig. 3F). These findings are consistent with the results of KEGG pathway enrichment analysis.

Untargeted metabolomic profile of the longissimus dorsi muscle tissue

To investigate the metabolic variations among different NE level feed treatment groups, LC-MS/MS was applied in both positive and negative ion modes to identify metabolites extracted from CG, EY1, EY2, and EY3 groups. In the positive ion mode, a total of 394 metabolites were detected, of which 234 were structurally classified into 11 distinct categories using the ClassyFire database. The predominant classes included organic acids and derivatives (78 metabolites), lipids and lipid-like molecules (54), organoheterocyclic compounds (26), nucleosides, nucleotides, and analogues (25), benzenoids (17), organic nitrogen compounds (15), and organic oxygen compounds (9). Additionally, minor categories comprised phenylpropanoids and polyketides (7), along with single representatives from alkaloids and derivatives, organosulfur compounds, and one unclassified metabolite (Fig. S2A). Conversely, negative ion mode analysis identified 359 metabolites, with 295 classified into nine major categories. The most abundant classes were lipids and lipid-like molecules (141 metabolites), followed by organic acids and derivatives (53), nucleosides, nucleotides, and analogues (40), and organic oxygen compounds (30). Other notable categories included organoheterocyclic compounds (16), benzenoids (7), phenylpropanoids and polyketides (5), organic nitrogen compounds (1), and two unclassified metabolites (Fig. S2B). To distinguish between DMs within groups, we employed supervised OPLS-DA. The OPLS-DA score plots revealed clear cluster separations between groups (Fig. 4, Fig. 5A), corroborated by permutation test validation (Q2 > 0.5: 0.697 in positive mode, 0.770 in negative mode; P < 0.01, Fig. S3). Correlation between metabolites and proteins

Through integrative proteomic and metabolomic profiling, we elucidated shared metabolic pathways potentially governing meat quality attributes. Comparisons between the EY1 and EY2 groups and the CG revealed four and one common KEGG pathways in the proteome and metabolome, respectively (Fig. 6A and B). These included the pentose phosphate pathway, biosynthesis of amino acids, carbon metabolism, and biosynthesis of cofactors (Table S3). Notably, Similarly, in the comparison between the EY3 group and CG, eight common KEGG pathways were identified, including carbon metabolism, citrate cycle (TCA cycle), 2-oxocarboxylic acid metabolism, fatty acid degradation, cysteine and methionine metabolism, glutathione metabolism, arachidonic acid (ARA) metabolism, and biosynthesis of cofactors (Fig. 6C and Table S3).

The integrated proteomic and metabolomic data between the basal diet and low NE diet treatments, particularly in the EY3 group, indicate that DEPs and DMs are primarily involved in carbon metabolism, unsaturated fatty acid metabolism, and amino acid biosynthesis (Fig. 6G). Specifically, glycolytic metabolites, such as D-fructose 1,6-bisphosphate, phosphoenolpyruvate, and 6-phospho-D-glucono-1,5-lactone, were upregulated in the LD muscle of Tunchang pigs fed low NE diets, while TCA cycle metabolites, including citric acid, α-ketoglutaric acid, and succinic acid, were downregulated. Notably, proteins involved in pyruvate metabolism, such as PDHB and ADH5, exhibited elevated expression in the low NE diet groups. The low NE dietary regimen induced substantial remodeling of amino acid and unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis networks. Within amino acid metabolism, upstream metabolites (phenylpyruvic acid and L-cystathionine) linking to glycolysis were upregulated, while downstream products (L-ornithine and L-asparagine) of TCA cycle-derived pathways were suppressed. Regarding lipid metabolism, reduced palmitic acid accumulation in LD muscle under low NE feeding corresponded with decreased levels of its ω-3/ω-6 derivatives (ARA, eicosapentaenoic acid, and docosahexaenoic acid), suggesting attenuated long-chain PUFA biosynthesis.

To elucidate the potential regulatory mechanisms underlying the effects of low NE diets on meat quality, a comprehensive correlation analysis was conducted (Fig. 6D–F). The results revealed that proteins MAPK12, STIM1, and MYH2 exhibited significant positively correlated with TCA cycle intermediates, unsaturated fatty acid metabolites, and oxidative fiber genes (MYHC I, MYHC IIa), but negatively correlated with glycolytic metabolites and the glycolytic gene (MYHC IIb). Conversely, CSRP3 exhibited the opposite pattern. To verify the actual expression changes suggested by these correlations, Western blot analysis was further conducted at the protein level (Fig. 6H). Consistent with the correlation results, compared with the CG group, the EY3 group showed markedly decreased expression of oxidative fiber marker proteins MYHC I (P = 0.032) and MyHC IIa (P = 0.001), whereas glycolytic fiber marker proteins MYHC IIb was significantly upregulated (P = 0.001); additionally, the upregulation of CSRP3 (P = 0.020) and the downregulation of MAPK12 (P = 0.001) were confirmed. Collectively, these findings indicate that the expression changes of these key proteins are closely associated with the shifts in muscle energy metabolism and fiber-type composition. In a complementary fashion, the analysis of meat quality indicators further confirmed that glycolytic fiber genes correlated with higher shear force, larger fiber area, and lower IMF content, whereas oxidative fiber genes were associated with superior meat quality. Notably, the lack of significant association between PDHB and most metabolites suggests that PDHB plays a limited role in the observed metabolic reprogramming.

Discussion

Pigs exhibit energy-dependent feeding behavior, characterized by a general decline in feed intake as dietary energy density increases (Suarez-Belloch et al., 2013). Moreira et al. (2022) demonstrated that elevated NE levels in diets linearly reduced ADFI in pigs, while reduced NE levels elicited compensatory increases in ADFI to offset the lower energy density (Smit et al., 2017). This finding is consistent with this study, in which Tunchang pigs progressively increased their ADFI as dietary NE levels declined. However, this compensatory mechanism operates only within a limited range of NE reduction and is constrained by factors such as dietary fiber content, which inherently limit voluntary intake (Aymerich et al., 2020). Furthermore, neither the energy evaluation system (metabolizable energy, NE, or digestible energy) nor energy density (within a certain range) significantly influenced the ADG or FCR of growing-finishing pigs (Yang et al., 2022). Similarly, the ADG and FCR of Tunchang pigs remained unaffected by variations in dietary NE levels ranging from 9.82 to 9.07 MJ/kg in this study. Regarding carcass traits, previous studies have shown that dietary energy density influences fatty acid deposition in swine (Bee et al., 2002). Higher energy diets typically elevate body fat percentage in finishing pigs (Ettle et al., 2003; Strath et al., 2019). This aligns with our results, which demonstrate a progressively reduced body fat percentage in Tunchang pigs following a reduction in dietary NE density. This effect may be mediated by the coordinated expression of lipogenic and lipolytic enzymes (Gondret et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2007).

Objective indicators of meat quality, including meat color, pH, drip loss, and shear force, are widely utilized for evaluation (Ruan et al., 2024). However, the influence of dietary energy density on meat quality remains a topic of debate. Previous studies have suggested that energy levels exert no significant impact on muscle quality (Hinson et al., 2011). This conclusion is supported by the findings of Suarez-Belloch et al. (2013), who evaluated pork meat color, shear force, drip loss, cooking loss, and pH through dietary NE modulation. In the current study, our results for meat color, pH, and drip loss are consistent with these previous observations, showing no significant variation across different dietary NE levels. However, shear force exhibited a progressive increase with decreasing NE levels, which was concurrent with a reduction in IMF content in the LD muscle. Shear force, a commonly used indicator of meat tenderness, is generally negatively correlated with IMF content (Hopkins et al., 2006; Jelenikova et al., 2008; Wolcott et al., 2009). Specifically, for every 1% increase in IMF content, meat tenderness scores improve by 2.3 ± 0.83 units, and compression force decreases by 0.73 ± 0.21 N (Hopkins et al., 2013). The decrease in IMF content in Tunchang pigs fed low NE diets may be attributed to its higher protein-to-energy ratio. In contrast, high NE diets with lower protein-to-energy ratios may limit lysine intake, which in turn indirectly promotes IMF accumulation (Madeira et al., 2013; Yan et al., 2023). This mechanism is supported by hydrolytic amino acid composition analysis, which revealed that pigs fed high NE diets exhibited relatively lower lysine content in the LD muscle. It should be noted that hydrolyzed amino acid data predominantly reflect the overall composition of muscle proteins, offering indirect evidence for the aforementioned metabolic mechanism, with limited direct relevance to flavor characteristics. Therefore, to more directly evaluate flavor characteristics, this study measured the concentration of inosine IMP in the LD muscle (Tikk et al., 2006; Yan et al., 2018), which have demonstrated that IMP concentration remains stable across different dietary NE levels. This finding aligns with the observations made by Engel et al. (2001), who reported minimal influence of dietary energy on the flavor profiles of pork loin. Overall, it appears plausible to suggest that diets with lower NE diminish IMF deposition in the LD muscle of Tunchang pigs. Consequently, this reduction in IMF is likely to elevate shear force values and compromise the tenderness of the meat.

Dietary fat sources and energy density influence fatty acid profiles. For instance, soybean oil supplementation increases the proportion of PUFA in adipose tissue of Large White pigs, while decreasing the proportions of saturated fatty acid (SFA) and monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFA) relative to tallow (Park et al., 2009). Reduced dietary energy levels elevate muscle SFAs and decrease PUFAs in finishing pigs (Fang et al., 2019), whereas higher energy intake conversely modifies these proportions. However, this study demonstrated no significant effects of dietary NE levels on the composition of SFA, MUFA, or PUFA in Tunchang pigs, consistent with the findings reported by Suarez-Belloch et al. (2013) in their earlier study. Notably, decreasing dietary NE levels reduced the n-3 PUFA content in the LD muscle, likely due to the progressive reduction in soybean oil supplementation (Vehovsky et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2011). This decline raises nutritional concerns, as n-3 PUFAs, which cannot be interconverted endogenously, are crucial for enhancing essential n-3 long-chain PUFA concentrations in muscle and improving pork nutritional quality (Corino et al., 2014; Nong et al., 2020; Smink et al., 2013). Furthermore, exogenous PUFAs can enhance IMF deposition to optimize meat characteristics (Wang et al., 2022). The observed reduction in n-3 PUFA under lower NE conditions aligns with the aforementioned decrease in IMF content, suggesting a potential mechanistic linkage between these parameters.

The architecture and distinct characteristics of muscle fibers are fundamental determinants of meat quality and yield (Deng et al., 2024). The IMF deposition is regulated by muscle fiber type, with muscles containing a higher proportion of oxidative fibers typically exhibiting greater IMF content (Hosotani et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2023). The data from this study are consistent with this pattern: the LD muscle from pigs fed low NE diets exhibited significantly higher MYHC IIb expression and lower MYHC I/IIa expression compared to those fed a basal diet, with MYHC IIb showing a significant negative correlation with IMF content. This observation indicates a shift from oxidative (type I/Ⅱa) to glycolytic (type IIb) fibers in skeletal muscle under reduced NE conditions, resulting in reduced IMF deposition. This finding is consistent with Yang et al. (2022) report of decreased oxidative fibers in energy-restricted LD muscle. Given that glycolytic muscle fibers typically have larger diameters and cross-sectional areas compared to oxidative fibers (Joo et al., 2013), it is hypothesized that the shift towards type IIb fibers induced by the low NE diet would result in increased muscle fiber size. This hypothesis was validated experimentally through the finding that decreasing dietary NE from 9.82 to 9.07 MJ/kg significantly increased both the diameter and cross-sectional area of muscle fibers in the LD muscle of Tunchang pigs. This was further reinforced by a significant positive correlation between MYHC IIb expression and both fiber diameter and area. Moreover, shear force is generally positively correlated with muscle fiber diameter and cross-sectional area, and negatively correlated with muscle fiber density (Su et al., 2014). Thus, the observed increase in muscle fiber diameter and concomitant reduction in IMF deposition, both of which are directly attributable to shifts in muscle fiber type composition, emerge as pivotal factors underlying the diminished meat tenderness.

Proteomic analysis of the LD muscle in Tunchang pigs has provided critical insights into the mechanisms by which low NE diets influence meat quality, revealing the coordinated roles of cytoskeletal organization, signaling pathways, and glycolytic metabolism in the development of meat texture. The PPI network analysis and KEGG enrichment of DEPs in the EY1, EY2, and EY3 groups (compared with CG) identified cytoskeletal proteins as the predominant cluster, with CSRP3 serving as the central hub. Cytoskeletal organization regulates the assembly, arrangement, and disassembly of cellular components, which is critical for meat tenderness (Taye et al., 2017). In previous research conducted on Pietrain pigs, a significant association was identified between actin cytoskeleton pathways and muscle fiber type differentiation (Liu et al., 2015). In the LD muscle, ten DEPs were identified, including cytoskeletal proteins (MYBPC1, NRAP, MYL3, CSRP3, TNNC1, MYH2, and ATP1A2), signaling proteins (MAPK12), and glycolytic metabolism-related proteins (PDHB and ADH5). Myosin binding protein C1, predominantly expressed in slow-twitch (type I) fibers, plays a critical role in regulating oxidative fiber function (Ha et al., 2013). Myosin light chain 3, a marker of slow-twitch fibers, is also primarily localized to type Ⅰ fibers (Amann et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2025). Upregulation of TNNC1 may increase the proportion of slow-twitch fibers (Cassar-Malek et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2022), while MYH2 encodes MYHC IIa, which is characteristic of fast-twitch type IIa fibers (Cassini et al., 2023; Willis et al., 2016). Cysteine and glycine-rich protein 3, a Z-disc associated LIM domain protein, plays a crucial role in maintaining sarcomeric structural integrity and function (Weiskirchen and Gunther, 2003). Previous studies indicate that CSRP3 interacts with calcineurin to promote the formation of oxidative fibers (da Costa et al., 2007), in which it is predominantly expressed (Zhu et al., 2016). Furthermore, its ability to promote satellite cell differentiation and myogenesis suggests an important role in muscle growth and repair (Xu et al., 2010; Han et al., 2019). Therefore, the downregulation of MYBPC1, MYL3, TNNC1, and MYH2 in the low NE diet group, may represent a pivotal molecular mechanism driving the observed transition in muscle fiber type, specifically a shift from oxidative type I/IIa fibers to glycolytic type IIb fibers. In this context, the observed upregulation of CSRP3 appears to be a compensatory response that supports muscle remodeling and repair in response to the dietary energy challenge. This interpretation is functionally supported by our correlation analysis, which showed that CSRP3 was positively correlated only with the glycolytic gene MYHC IIb and not with oxidative fiber genes.

The compensatory upregulation of PDHB (a key subunit of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex catalyzing pyruvate-to-acetyl-CoA conversion) and ADH5 (involved in formaldehyde metabolism and glycolytic regulation via NADH modulation) further supported the increased glycolytic fiber proportion (Li et al., 2016; Tian et al., 2022). Furthermore, MAPK12, a member of the mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathway expressed in skeletal muscle, regulates glucose and lipid metabolism as well as muscle fiber type switching (Lawan and Bennett, 2017; Kuhnen et al., 2022). Previous research in mice has identified that MAPK12 is specifically activated in slow-twitch (oxidative) muscle and its absence leads to a significant decrease in the mass of oxidative fibers (Foster et al., 2012). In line with this role, our data reveal a significant positive correlation between MAPK12 and the expression of the MYHC I gene. Therefore, its downregulation further corroborates the occurrence of a muscle fiber type shift. Finally, the expression alterations of these key differentially expressed proteins, including CSRP3 and MAPK12, as well as the myofiber-type-specific marker proteins (MyHC I, IIa, and IIb) in the EY3 group, were directly verified via Western blot analysis. Collectively, these findings indicate that low NE diets may promote a phenotypic shift in muscle fiber types toward a more glycolytic phenotype. This shift appears to be mediated through the regulation of cytoskeletal proteins, signaling pathways, and proteins associated with glycolytic metabolism.

The integration of proteomics and metabolomics data offers a robust and promising approach to elucidate the key pathways and molecular mechanisms that influence pork quality. Joint analysis revealed that low NE diets predominantly affect meat quality through pathways such as carbon metabolism, amino acid biosynthesis, and the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids. Specifically, carbon metabolism pathways, including glycolysis and the TCA cycle, exhibited differential regulation between LD muscles at varying NE levels. Oxidative muscle fibers, which are rich in mitochondria, preferentially generate energy via the TCA cycle, whereas glycolytic fibers rely predominantly on glycolytic metabolism to produce ATP (Barnard et al., 1971). In this study, a low NE diet significantly increased the levels of metabolites in the glycolysis pathway, such as D-fructose 1,6-bisphosphate and phosphoenolpyruvate, as well as their downstream metabolites in amino acid biosynthesis pathways, including phenylpyruvic acid and L-cystathionine. Conversely, metabolites in the TCA cycle, such as citric acid, α-ketoglutaric acid, and succinic acid, along with their downstream products (L-asparagine and L-ornithine), exhibited a downward trend. Additionally, the MYH2 protein (MYHC IIa) was significantly positively correlated with metabolites in the TCA cycle, whereas CSRP3 was significantly positively correlated with metabolites in the glycolysis pathway. These findings further provide metabolite-level evidence that a low NE diet regulates the ratio of oxidative muscle fibers to glycolytic muscle fibers. Notably, PDHB showed no significant correlation with metabolites from either glycolysis or the TCA cycle. Given its established function in promoting muscle differentiation and increasing muscle fiber area (Jiang et al., 2023), this result suggests that the upregulation of PDHB under a low NE diet likely serves to meet the anabolic demands of muscle growth.

Previous studies have indicated that the suppression of unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis pathways is a hallmark of pigs under low-energy states and hinders IMF deposition (Lu et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2024b). Key PUFAs like eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and ARA have been positively correlated with increased IMF content and improved meat tenderness (Huang et al., 2011; Quinones et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2023). In the present study, it was observed that under low NE dietary conditions, metabolites involved in the biosynthesis pathways of unsaturated fatty acids, including EPA, ARA, palmitic acid, and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), exhibited a significant downward trend. Notably, these metabolites were positively correlated with the oxidative fiber-associated protein MYH2, but negatively correlated with the glycolytic fiber-associated protein CSRP3. This pattern suggests that the downregulation of these metabolites may indirectly influence IMF deposition by modulating muscle fiber type. Consistent with this mechanism, prior research has demonstrated that EPA can increase the proportion of type I muscle fibers in rats by activating the PPARδ and AMPK pathways (Komiya et al., 2024). Similarly, ARA has been shown to increase the proportion of type I fibers and decrease the proportion of type IIb fibers in rat slow-twitch muscles (Hashimoto et al., 2016). In contrast, palmitic acid inhibits myotube formation and hypertrophy while also suppressing the expression of fast-twitch fiber-specific isoforms (MHC IIb/IIx) (Matsuba et al., 2023). Additionally, DHA upregulates the expression of oxidative fiber genes, including MHC1 and MHC2A (Hashimoto et al., 2016; Soni et al., 2016). Therefore, the observed downregulation of these key fatty acid metabolites in this study likely collectively drives a shift in muscle fiber type from oxidative to glycolytic. Given the significant correlations between these metabolites and proteins, as well as their well-established functions, they hold considerable promise as biomarkers for indicating the transition from oxidative to glycolytic muscle fibers induced by low NE diets.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that reducing dietary NE levels to 9.32 MJ/kg does not significantly negatively impact the growth performance or meat quality of Tunchang pigs; however, when NE is further reduced to 9.07 MJ/kg, it increases ADFI and adversely affects meat quality (Fig. 7). The low NE diet induces a shift in muscle fiber type from oxidative to glycolytic, resulting in increased fiber diameter and cross-sectional area, reduced IMF deposition, and ultimately diminished tenderness. Key DEPs like MYBPC1, MYL3, TNNC1, MYH2, and CSRP3 in the LD muscle are potential targets for regulating fiber-type transition under low NE conditions. Metabolic profiling reveals significant changes in glycolytic and TCA cycle pathways, as well as reduced unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis metabolites, further confirming muscle fiber type transformation. These results provide novel mechanistic insights into how low NE diets compromise meat quality. Maintaining optimal dietary NE levels is crucial for balancing growth and meat quality in swine production.
