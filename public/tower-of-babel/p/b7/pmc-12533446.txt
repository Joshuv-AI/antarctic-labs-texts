Idiopathic polyneuropathy with neurogenic autonomic failure – an early manifestation of Lewy body disease? a case report
Authors: Naja Helt Andersen, Per Borghammer, Kathrin Doppler, Hanne Gottrup, Karoline Knudsen, Anastasia Kuzkina, Nathalie Van Den Berge, Marit Otto, Astrid Juhl Terkelsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the present case we suggest that idiopathic large-fiber polyneuropathy with autonomic failure, pathological cardiac [123I]Metaiodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) scintigraphy and α-synuclein positivity in cutaneous autonomic nerves is a prodromal manifestation of body-first Lewy body disease (LBD).Case Presentation: A previously healthy 74-year-old man presented with painful polyneuropathy and orthostatic intolerance. Phenotypic characterization demonstrated small- and large-fiber sensory-motor-autonomic polyneuropathy, cardiac sympathetic denervation and cardiovagal and cardiovascular adrenergic dysfunction. Despite thorough examination and exclusion of diabetes, primary- and hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis, no underlying cause was found. Interestingly, LBD was suspected due to abnormal cardiac MIBG scintigraphy, constipation, hyposmia, REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD), and mild cognitive impairment. Seed amplification assay (SAA) for pathological α-synuclein was positive in a skin biopsy (neck) with deposits localized in the autonomic nerves based on immunohistochemistry. He was clinically diagnosed with mild dementia with Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) pathology. However, given the presence of severe autonomic dysfunction, RBD, pathological α-synuclein in addition to AD pathology, dual pathology with Lewy bodies (representing a body-first LBD) should be considered. Conclusion: This case highlights the importance of evaluating patients with idiopathic large-fiber polyneuropathy with autonomic failure for underlying LBD. We propose that both neuropathy and cardiac denervation may stem from the widespread peripheral neurodegeneration associated with pre-motor body-first LBD, although there is no direct evidence of causality. In this case, with idiopathic small- and large-fiber sensory-motor-autonomic neuropathy and abnormal MIBG scintigraphy, detection of α-synuclein in cutaneous autonomic nerves, supports our proposal. Thus, we suggest idiopathic polyneuropathy with autonomic failure to represent a non-motor prodromal manifestations in LBD.Keywords: Polyneuropathy, REM-sleep behavior disorder, Mild cognitive impairment, Neurogenic autonomic failure, Alpha-synuclein seed amplification assay, Cardiac 123I-MIBG, Lewy body disease, Alzheimer's Disease

Introduction

Polyneuropathy (PN) is an understudied non-motor manifestation in Lewy body disease (LBD) but has been reported to occur in up to 65% of patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD) compared with just 9% of the general population [1, 2]. There is accumulating evidence of α-synuclein aggregates in skin nerve fibers and associated autonomic denervation in early prodromal LBD suggesting the length-dependent PN to be part of the neurodegenerative process. Despite this, the causal link between PN and PD is still not fully understood and may be considered controversial.

Histopathologically, α-synuclein aggregates contribute to the formation of Lewy pathology in LBD, including PD and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) [3]. These aggregates can accumulate in both the central and peripheral nervous systems (CNS, PNS), and propagate throughout the autonomic connectome, affecting multiple organs along the gut-brain axis [4].

Recent research in de novo and prodromal PD/DLB gave rise to the brain-first/body-first hypothesis in LBD. This hypothesis suggests α-synuclein pathology begins either in the brain or gut, creating distinct early disease profiles that converge as disease progresses. Body-first subtypes show abnormal cardiac Metaiodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) scintigraphy and idiopathic REM-sleep behavior disorder (iRBD) in the pre-motor phase with overall faster disease progression, including faster cognitive decline and hyposmia [5]. DLB patients exhibit early abnormal cardiac MIBG scintigraphy and iRBD and therefore likely represent a body-first disease origin [6].

Importantly, PD animal research has demonstrated that peripheral α-synuclein disease initiation can in fact propagate to skin, heart, and CNS [4]. Furthermore, a recent study employed cluster analysis to divide patients with neurogenic orthostatic hypotension in two groups; one with and one without Lewy body pathology based on the presence or absence of cutaneous pathological α-synuclein. The Lewy-positive cluster also showed abnormal cardiac [18 F]dopamine positron emission tomography (PET) and hyposmia [7].

Considering these findings, we speculate that large-fiber PN with concomitant autonomic failure and LBD share the pathological process of α-synuclein accumulation in PNS, with deposits in the cutaneous nerves potentially contributing to both large-fiber and autonomic PN development as part of the non-motor LBD symptomatology. Since our case demonstrates PN with abnormal cardiac MIBG scintigraphy, we propose that large-fiber PN combined with autonomic failure may constitute one of many prodromal body-first LBD manifestations.

Case

A 74-year-old man with no prior medical history other than statin-treated hypercholesterolemia, and no known family history of neurological or psychiatric disease, was referred to the Department of Neurology, Aarhus University Hospital, Denmark. For the past few years, he had experienced “walking on cotton wool” and numbness in his legs and for two months gradually increasing motor weakness, initially over his right ankle, progressing to the knee and hip. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) of the lumbar spine excluded radiculopathy.

Neurological examination confirmed slightly reduced muscle strength in the right versus left leg (Medical Research Council (MRC) scale: hip flexion 4/5, knee flexion 4/5, knee extension 4/5, and ankle dorsiflexion 4/5) and weak but preserved deep tendon reflexes symmetrically with normal response to plantar stimulation. In addition, a decreased sensation to cold, pinprick, and vibration was found bilaterally in the lower limbs, with the left side being most affected (from the hip and distally), and inability to stand independently with closed eyes. There was no evidence of extrapyramidal symptoms or cerebellar ataxia. The right site motor weakness later resolved, but the sensory deficits remained.

Paraclinical test results were normal, including blood test (glucose, glycosylated hemoglobin, renal, thyroid, and liver parameters; complete blood count; vitamin status (cobalamin, homocysteine, methylmalonic acid, and vitamin E); serum protein immunofixation electrophoresis, light chains, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody and hepatitis B and C virus). Hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis was excluded (sanger sequencing of the TTR gene).

An initial neurophysiological examination supported the diagnosis by revealing findings indicative of mild sensory-motor PN (Fig. 1A: Abnormal results). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed an elevated level of neurofilament light chain (NfL), 5498ng/L, but was otherwise normal (cell count, protein level, oligoclonal band, IgG-index, cytology and paraneoplastic antibodies (GAD65-IgG, Zic4-IgG, Titin-IgG, SOX1-IgG, Hu-IgG, Recoverin-IgG, Amphiphysin-IgG, Tr-IgG, CDR2-IgG, NOVA1-IgG, PNMA2-IgG, DRP5-IgG)). Brain, cervical, and thoracic spine MRI were unremarkable except for degenerative spine changes. No underlying cause was identified despite thorough medical assessment. Importantly, there was no history of toxin exposure related to work, leisure activities or during childhood, and except from cholesterol-lowering therapy, the patient did not take any prescribed or over the counter medicine. The patient was prescribed oral gabapentin for neuropathic pain. The elevated NfL was considered non-specific and later normalized using blood sample.

During the following 2 years, the patient developed multiple progressive symptoms: initial daytime sleepiness, 7 kg weight loss, and ascending sensory loss affecting hands and knees; followed by autonomic dysfunction (orthostatic lightheadedness, constipation confirmed by computed tomography (CT), urinary symptoms, erectile dysfunction), dream enactment (RBD), and hyposmia; ultimately progressing to memory loss and concentration difficulties two years after initial presentation. A repeat nerve conduction study (NCS) was performed (shown in Fig. 1B) due to the progression of sensory symptoms (clinically developing from a reduced sense of touch, pin prick, cold, and vibration below the right knee to above, and from no affection of the upper limbs to a loss of sense of touch and pin prick). Repeat NCS revealed a progression of abnormal results compared with the initial NCS. A diagnosis of axonal sensory-motor PN with a certain degree of asymmetry was made. Considering clinical progression, a diagnosis of length-dependent axonal sensory-motor PN was made rather than superimposed focal neuropathy. Although formal criteria for demyelination was not fulfilled; a slightly prolonged distal latency and F-wave latency, may reflect a loss of axonal rapid fibers in axonal PN. Right vastus medialis electromyography was normal. No further nerve conduction studies were performed after this stage.

Attempted tapering of gabapentin was ineffective. Malignancy was excluded via CT of chest, abdomen and pelvis, and [18F]fluorodeoxyglucose(FDG)-PET. Based on orthostatic intolerance, the patient was included into an ongoing research project with comprehensive testing: 16 item Sniffin’ Sticks test revealed hyposmia; RBD-screening questionnaire consistent with probable RBD; Composite Autonomic Scoring Scale score 2 indicated mild autonomic failure; sudomotor index 1 with reduced sweat at the distal leg; cardiovagal index 1 (reduced heart rate response to deep respiration, normal Valsalva maneuver); cardiovascular adrenergic index 1 (prolonged pressure recovery time of 10 s and loss of late phase II during Valsalva maneuver, delayed orthostatic hypotension (OH) during upright tilt).

Cardiac [123I]MIBG scintigraphy revealed severe cardiac sympathetic denervation despite the absence of both diabetes and heart failure (early and late heart-to-mediastinum (H/M) ratio of 1.18 and 1.2, respectively; neck skin biopsy was positive for pathological α-synuclein by seed amplification assay (SAA) and immunohistochemistry. Deposits are located around and colocalize with peripheral nerves (Fig. 1D). These findings led to further investigations. RBD was confirmed by video-polysomnography. Neuropsychological assessment demonstrated multiple domain amnestic mild cognitive impairment (MCI) (Mini-Mental State Examination score of 26, Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination score of 85) affecting memory, language, executive function, and visuospatial abilities. 18F-(E)-N-(3-iodoprop-2-enyl)−2ß-carboflouroethoxhy-3ß(4’-methylphenyl) nortropane ([18F]PE2I)-PET-CT showed normal dopamine transporter imaging with Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale part III score of 0. At this point, research criteria for probable MCI with Lewy bodies were fulfilled [8]. Most recently, the patient was diagnosed with mild dementia supported by FDG-PET and CSF-confirmed Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) pathology.

Discussion

This case of idiopathic large-fiber PN with autonomic failure exhibited abnormal MIBG scintigraphy and iRBD, both indicative of body-first LBD. There was no history of toxin exposure, although subclinical exposure could not be excluded. Apart from hypercholesterolemia, the patient had no other cardiovascular risk factors or metabolic disorders, nor did any develop during the follow-up period. Therefore, the detection of pathological α-synuclein by SAA and histological assessment additionally confirming affection of cutaneous peripheral autonomic nerves is highly interesting. With a prevalence of nearly 10% of PN in the general population, one would expect that every 10th patient with PD and DLB will have PN by chance. However, the higher prevalence of PN in PD patients favors an association between PD and PN. While a causal relationship between PN and LBD cannot be directly investigated, our finding suggests α-synuclein deposition in PNS as a shared pathological mechanism underlying PN and LBD. Animal studies have previously demonstrated that pathological α-synuclein can spread centrally and to the skin after peripheral introduction, with reduced nerve conduction velocity in large myelinated fibers and pathology extending to sensory nerves, spinal cord, and midbrain [9, 10].

Although the lower reported prevalence of large-fiber PN compared to small-fiber neuropathy in idiopathic LBD, these preclinical findings suggest that both idiopathic small- and large-fiber PN may represent a non-motor manifestation of human LBD at any disease stage. Since body-first LBD is characterized by early pre-motor PNS dysfunction, we propose that the α-synucleinopathy present in our case with PN, together with abnormal cardiac MIBG scintigraphy and iRBD, likely represents an early body-first LBD (PD or DLB), and more likely DLB considering fulfilment of probable DLB criteria [11]. While a nerve biopsy is required to definitively establish a causal relationship, this was not performed due to its invasiveness and risk of permanent complications. A comparable case from 2021 documented sensory neuropathy preceding DLB diagnosis, with cutaneous α-synuclein deposits confirmed a decade later [12]. If these two cases represented brain-first phenotypes, one would expect limited peripheral nerve damage, relatively preserved cardiac MIBG uptake, a lower burden of non-motor symptoms in the early disease stages, and a premotor phase without RBD [13]. Notably, a recent brain-first rodent PD model study showed no evidence of PN, further suggesting PNS pathology may drive both PN and body-first disease [14].

Most iRBD patients develop LBD (or another synucleinopathy) within a decade. Though unlikely, non-conversion in our case remains possible. We emphasize it is the combined presence of biomarkers that allows identification of iRBD patients at highest risk for phenoconversion. Notably, those with abnormal MIBG scintigraphy and cognitive dysfunction face higher short-term phenoconversion risk, particularly to DLB. Therefore, our case’s iRBD, abnormal cardiac MIBG scintigraphy, and cognitive dysfunction suggest early body-first LBD (PD or DLB), rather than persistent iRBD. In LBD, these symptoms indicate Braak stage 3–4 [15], consistent with widespread CNS Lewy pathology, and probable AD co-pathology contributing to cognitive symptoms.

Clinically, AD co-pathology manifests as a poorer prognosis and more severe symptoms, including impairment of memory. Notably, AD co-pathology occurs in up to 90% of DLB cases, which are considered body-first LBD. Recent studies have hypothesized that the presence of AD co-pathology may drive the earlier onset of dementia in DLB compared to pure body-first PD. Ad co-pathology in LBD is therefore indirectly associated to both pre-motor cognitive dysfunction and pre-motor dysautonomia [6]. In our case, the involvement of memory, language and visuospatial domains most likely reflect AD co-pathology, and executive dysfunction – both commonly observed in DLB [16]. Elevated CSF NfL could reflect early LBD, though the interpretation at the time was deemed ‘non-specific’. It may indicate pre-diagnostic neurodegeneration and support central nervous system involvement driven by LB- and AD-pathology. Prodromal DLB is characterized by elevated CSF NfL with gradually increasing levels from prodromal DLB to fully developed DLB. Notably, DLB patients with AD co-pathology show even higher NfL levels compared to those with pure DLB [17].

The strict brain-first/body-first dichotomy has been challenged, particularly for RBD, suggesting overlapping neurodegeneration pathways rather than mutually exclusive routes [18]. While no model fits all cases, it is important to note that this framework primarily aims to explain early disease differences. Given the co-pathology of AD, neither small-fiber nor large-fiber PN has, to the authors’ knowledge, been reported as a characteristic feature. Therefore, the authors do not consider AD-pathology to contribute to the peripheral neuropathy-phenotype. Both brain-first and body-first subtypes show progressive involvement of peripheral and central structures, causing their initial phenotypic distinctions to blur and gradually converge toward a more similar phenotype at advanced disease stage. Hence, RBD may appear relatively early even in brain-first LBD cases, shortly following the development of motor symptoms. This complexity highlights that a constellation of non-motor manifestations, rather than a single feature like iRBD, is required for accurate prediction of conversion to body-first LBD. Our case should be interpreted with these important nuances in mind.

Conclusion

This case suggests that large-fiber PN with autonomic failure may represent an early prodromal manifestation of body-first LBD, supported by cutaneous α-synucleinopathy, abnormal cardiac MIBG scintigraphy, and iRBD. Therefore, when idiopathic large-fiber PN co-exists with other prodromal body-first features (iRBD, hyposmia, constipation, OH and/or abnormal cardiac MIBG scintigraphy) we suggest evaluation for underlying α-synucleinopathy and not to be excluded in future LBD research. Larger prospective studies are needed to conclusively demonstrate whether idiopathic large-fiber PN with autonomic failure can result from prodromal LBD. Confirmation of this relationship would enhance our capability to detect early prodromal neurodegenerative disease in the general population.
