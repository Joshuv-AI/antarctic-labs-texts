The role of neuropsychology in stereo‐EEG: A narrative review
Authors: Emily Cockle, Charles B Malpas, Rubina Alpitsis, Terence J O'Brien, Genevieve Rayner, Andrew Neal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStereo‐EEG is accompanied by unique neuropsychological challenges in the workup, during the admission, and following the procedure. While established principles from epilepsy surgery programs provide a guiding framework for clinical practice, specialized guidelines to inform the role of neuropsychology in stereo‐EEG warrant further consideration. In this review, we consider the role of neuropsychological assessments in assisting with localization of the epileptogenic network in the context of discordant noninvasive investigations, the paucity of research examining the cognitive risks associated with radiofrequency thermocoagulation, and the role for neuropsychological counseling in stereo‐EEG. Through a narrative review, we then critically appraise the current literature surrounding the challenges of cognitive examination during cortical stimulation and subsequent clinical interpretation of elicited deficits. Overall, we propose a model of practice for clinical neuropsychology within stereo‐EEG programs that includes neuropsychological assessment, neuropsychological counseling, and neuropsychological involvement for cortical stimulation.Plain Language SummaryStereo‐EEG is a complex investigative procedure that may be undertaken in people with drug‐resistant epilepsy. Clinical neuropsychologists can help assess the cognitive risks and benefits associated with the procedure, in turn supporting patients to make informed decisions about their treatment options. Despite this, the role of clinical neuropsychology in stereo‐EEG programs has been largely overlooked. As such, we propose a framework that highlights the clinical utility of neuropsychology before, during, and after stereo‐EEG.Keywords: cognitive mapping, drug‐resistant epilepsy, neuropsychological assessment, neuropsychological rehabilitation, RFTHC, SEEG

INTRODUCTION

Specialist clinical neuropsychology is fundamental to epilepsy surgical programs. 1 , 2 , 3 Clinical neuropsychological opinion before surgery can aid with localization of the epileptogenic network and establishment of cognitive risks associated with proposed resections. 2 , 4 , 5 Additionally, neuropsychological prehabilitation and rehabilitation reduce the functional implications of cognitive deficits sustained from surgery, and can support patients from a psychological and psychosocial perspective as they adapt to either living without seizures or seizure reoccurrence. 1 , 6 The role of neuropsychology in stereo‐EEG programs, however, has been largely overlooked.

For a third of people with drug‐resistant epilepsy, surgical resection of the epileptogenic zone may be the only chance for seizure control. 7 For a subset of potential surgical candidates, however, noninvasive data is discordant or inconclusive, preventing direct progression to resective surgery; in these cases, stereo‐EEG may help identify the epileptogenic zone. 8 , 9 Stereo‐EEG is an invasive investigative procedure that involves implantation of depth electrodes into the brain to facilitate localization of the epileptogenic zone. 10 It is now considered the primary intracranial investigative procedure used in epilepsy surgery programs internationally, as it allows interrogation of diffuse and/or bilateral networks as well as epileptogenic sampling of subcortical brain structures. 10 Following stereo‐EEG, around two‐thirds of patients will progress to resective surgery, with over half achieving seizure control. 11 Stereo‐EEG can also inform further treatment therapies such as neuromodulation for patients not deemed to be suitable surgical candidates. 12

Cortical stimulation is a fundamental component of stereo‐EEG that supports the assessment of epileptogenicity and examination of cognition networks. 13 Outcomes of the cognitive examination during cortical stimulation are dependent on the paradigms administered, criteria applied for the classification of deficits, and neurostimulation parameters. 14 , 15 Despite this, approaches to cognitive examination are heterogeneous. 16 , 17 The localizing capacity and prognostication of observed deficits also remain poorly understood. 18 , 19 Clinical neuropsychologists are well placed to provide expert commentary on the complex interplay between these variables to aid in the interpretation of the results from cortical stimulation.

Stereo‐EEG guided radiofrequency thermocoagulation (RFTHC) is a minimally invasive therapy that can be undertaken before electrode explantation. 10 , 20 A radiofrequency current is delivered between a pair of electrode contacts to create a small focal lesion within the brain. 20 , 21 The location and number of coagulation sites are individualized based on the hypothesized epileptogenic zone. 20 While only 10%–20% of patients experience seizure control following RFTHC, 50%–70% experience a reduction in seizure frequency that can be used as a predictive marker of likely seizure control following a subsequent resection. 22 , 23 Despite the clinical utility of RFTHC, evaluation of its neuropsychological impacts has been limited. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25

To date, guidelines for neuropsychology in stereo‐EEG have not been developed. As such, we aim to draw upon well‐established literature that delineates the role of clinical neuropsychology in resective epilepsy surgery to inform a specialized framework for practice in stereo‐EEG. In doing so, we consider the role of the pre‐stereo‐EEG neuropsychological assessment in contributing to clinical hypotheses around the topography of the epileptogenic network, as well as evaluating cognitive outcomes associated with RFTHC. We also consider the role of neuropsychological counseling in stereo‐EEG to aid with preparedness for admission, as well as to assist in weighing up the potential cognitive risks and benefits of RFTHC or subsequent resective surgeries. Lastly, cognitive examination during cortical stimulation is an additional area where clinical neuropsychology can be considered in stereo‐EEG. To inform clinical practice guidelines, we undertook a narrative review of the empirical literature surrounding cognitive examination during cortical stimulation to understand the clinical significance of elicited deficits and the capacity to use these findings to mitigate the risk of cognitive decline associated with subsequent surgical resections.

NEUROPSYCHOLOGICAL ASSESSMENT IN STEREO‐EEG

Ninety percent of responders to a global survey of stereo‐EEG practices reported undertaking a neuropsychological assessment with patients as part of the workup for stereo‐EEG. 17 Routine neuropsychological assessment following stereo‐EEG, however, has not been reported. While similar principles from neuropsychological assessments in resective surgery can be considered, unique challenges are present with stereo‐EEG patients.

Neuropsychological assessment and epileptogenic network hypothesis

Given the advances in neuroimaging techniques over the past few decades, there has been a decreasing reliance on neuropsychology for localization of the epileptogenic zone. 2 , 26 Instead, clinical neuropsychology has evolved to become fundamental for characterization of the cognitive syndrome in order to establish the cognitive risks associated with proposed surgical resections. 2 , 3 Patients considered for stereo‐EEG are inherently complex, given they typically have either no identifiable MRI epileptogenic lesion or insufficient concordant noninvasive data to enable direct pursuit of surgical treatment for their epilepsy. 10 In this patient group, the neuropsychological assessment can provide additional information to support the formulation of hypotheses regarding the epileptogenic network and in turn guide implantation planning. 1 , 27

Epileptogenic networks often overlap with cognitive networks. 28 As such, neuropsychological deficits can become an emergent property of dysfunction within these networks. 1 , 29 , 30 Comparable memory decrements have been demonstrated in MRI lesion‐positive and ‐negative temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) cases, highlighting that network dysfunction can arise as a consequence of various epilepsy etiologies rather than only being caused by discrete lesions. 31 This is further exemplified through the case examples (Table 1). Case 1 illustrates a verbal memory impairment despite no identifiable MRI lesion, while case 2 depicts a multimodal verbal and visual memory deficit suggestive of bi‐TLE, despite neuroimaging suggestive of a dominant temporal epileptogenic zone. Case 3 highlights dysexecutive features characterized by reduced set shifting, inhibition of prepotent responses, and orthographic lexical in a patient with MRI negative nondominant orbitofrontal epileptogenic zone. These cases demonstrate the added utility of a neuropsychological assessment as a noninvasive measure to aid with localization of the epileptogenic network in stereo‐EEG candidates.

The epileptogenic network can be diffuse and in turn implicate multiple cognitive networks. 32 , 33 , 34 The epileptogenic network encompasses the epileptogenic zone, being the region where seizures primarily organize, as well as the propagation zone, being the cortical and subcortical networks through which seizures spread. 32 As such, a seemingly focal epileptogenic zone has the capacity to cause dysfunction in distal regions and give rise to a multidomain cognitive impairment. 29 , 33 , 34 Consequently, the pattern of neuropsychological impairments within epilepsy syndromes can be heterogeneous. In an effort to understand variability, there has been a shift away from evaluating patients in aggregate to trying to understand the distinct presentations that emerge within an epilepsy syndrome. 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 Within TLE, three broad cognitive phenotypes are often revealed: patients with memory and language specific deficits, patients with a generalized cognitive impairment, and patients with no cognitive impairments. 35 , 40 Generalized cognitive impairments tend to be associated with widely distributed brain abnormalities, while less pervasive deficits often align with more restricted abnormalities. 35 , 36 , 37 , 40 Unsurprisingly, a focal cognitive phenotype has been associated with a higher likelihood of progressing to surgery following stereo‐EEG. 41 Consideration of cognitive phenotypes has important clinical implications for patients undergoing stereo‐EEG. A memory or language‐specific phenotype may support a hypothesis regarding a focal temporal epileptogenic zone, while a multimodal cognitive phenotype may raise the possibility of a diffuse epileptogenic network.

While cognitive phenotypes have advanced the conceptualization of cognition in epilepsy, diagnoses of epilepsy syndromes within these studies are not supported by intracranial EEG or seizure control post‐resection. 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 It is possible that a subset of TLE patients within these studies had a temporal‐plus, extratemporal, or bi‐temporal epilepsy, contributing to variability in cognitive profiles. 42 , 43 Classification of the epileptogenic zone in this manner could facilitate differentiation of temporal from temporal‐plus cognitive syndromes as well as enable the evaluation of sublobar cognitive phenotypes (i.e. mesial temporal, posterior temporal, diffuse temporal, lateral temporal) to further improve the precision of neuropsychological assessments.

Neuropsychological assessment and radiofrequency thermocoagulation (RFTHC)

RFTHC involves creating a targeted lesion in the brain between two contiguous electrode contacts within the suspected epileptogenic zone. 44 The number and location of coagulation sites are individualized based on whether the region is part of the epileptogenic or early propagation zone, or within an epileptogenic lesion. 44 Safety factors are also considered, including proximity to blood vessels and the primary eloquent cortex. 44 Each coagulation site is approximately 100 mm3, 45 considerably smaller than the mean resection cavity of a corticectomy being around 9000 mm3 or anterior temporal lobectomy being around 30 000mm3. 46 As such, only a small proportion of patients are rendered seizure‐free following RFTHC alone; however, it can also be undertaken with the goal of obtaining prognostic information regarding a subsequent surgical resection, i.e., temporary periods of seizure freedom after RFTHC indicate that the true epileptogenic zone has been disrupted and, as such, are positive predictors of later seizure freedom after resective epilepsy surgery. 20 , 44 Despite widespread use, there has been limited evaluation of the neuropsychological sequelae of RFTHC and whether this can predict a subsequent cognitive deficit following resection. In part, this may be attributed to a neuropsychological assessment post‐stereo‐EEG not being considered routine clinical practice.

RFTHC has been posed as relatively safe from a cognitive perspective 47 ; however, this is often reported in the absence of a neuropsychological assessment. Instead, there has been a reliance on self‐report or observation of cognitive deficits. 20 , 21 , 22 , 24 , 25 , 48 Retrospective analyses of 153 stereo‐EEG cases that underwent RFTHC identified one instance of an unexpected complex neuropsychological syndrome, whereby the patient experienced a severe impairment in reading, writing, and arithmetic following RFTHC of the left parietal lobe. 22 One additional patient experienced a prolonged memory deficit that was considered notable but not a complication, with no further details outlined. 22 Another retrospective chart review of 14 patients that underwent RFTHC identified one patient who experienced a transient verbal memory disorder following RFTHC within the hippocampus. 24 Further information regarding the nature of the verbal memory deficit or the side of RFTHC was not provided. 24 Reliance on reporting of cognitive changes in the absence of routine neuropsychological assessment may lead to underdetection of subtle, but functionally impactful deficits.

Routine neuropsychological assessment following RFTHC has revealed a much higher incidence of cognitive deficits than those identified through self‐report studies. Retrospective review of 25 patients with MRI negative focal epilepsy who underwent a neuropsychological assessment after RFTHC found that 24% experienced a decline, as defined by reliable change analysis with a confidence interval of 90%, in at least one cognitive domain. 23 Language decline was associated with RFTHC within the dominant lateral and mesial temporal structures; verbal memory decline was observed in one patient following RFTHC within the dominant parietal lobe, posterior cingulate, and insula; and visual memory decline occurred following RFTHC within the dominant temporal pole and mesial temporal structures in one patient and the nondominant hippocampus and parietal lobe in another patient. 23 However, the neuropsychological measures used were not outlined, which is highly relevant, as identification of cognitive change following epilepsy surgery is partially dependent on the neuropsychological tasks used. 49 Inclusion of this information is important to aid interpretation of the lack of decline in verbal memory occurring following RFTHC of the dominant mesial temporal structures. Although it has been suggested that less invasive procedures are associated with a lower risk of decline in memory, restricted ablation to the hippocampus does not preclude the possibility of a memory deficit. 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 Despite a subset of patients within the sample undergoing subsequent resective surgery, neuropsychological outcomes were not reported. 23 It remains unclear whether neuropsychological outcomes post RFTHC are predictive of outcomes following surgical resection. Future research is essential to address these methodological limitations, as neuropsychological outcomes following RFTHC may offer individualized information to further optimize presurgical counseling.

Proposed recommendations for clinical neuropsychological assessment before and after stereo‐EEG

All patients should complete a neuropsychological assessment before and after stereo‐EEG, as depicted in Table 2. In keeping with guidelines proposed by the ILAE Neuropsychology Task Force in the workup for resective epilepsy surgery, the stereo‐EEG neuropsychological assessment should evaluate performance across all principal cognitive domains: attention and speed of processing, language, memory, visuospatial, executive function, and general intellect. 54 For stereo‐EEG cases, particularly where the noninvasive data is less consistent, as depicted in the cases outlined in Table 1, the neuropsychological assessment can be conceptualized as an additional investigation that may provide support for proposed hypotheses regarding the epileptogenic network. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests some patients may be at risk of experiencing a cognitive deficit post‐stereo‐EEG RFTHC. 23 Routine neuropsychological assessment post‐stereo‐EEG will support early cognitive rehabilitation, where indicated, to reduce functional implications.

NEUROPSYCHOLOGICAL COUNSELING IN STEREO‐EEG

Preparedness for stereo‐EEG

Within epilepsy surgical programs, canvassing a patient's expectations and goals for pursuing surgery is key to gauging how they may adjust to life after surgery. 55 The establishment of individualized, concrete, and attainable goals provides patients with a clear benchmark to quantify the success of the procedure, which has been associated with improved quality of life and well‐being. 1 , 56 By contrast, unrealistic or abstract goals have been associated with an increased likelihood of feeling dissatisfied with the outcomes of surgery, irrespective of seizure control. 1 , 56 Although only a small proportion of patients are rendered seizure‐free following RFTHC, 20 exploring these concepts in pre‐stereo‐EEG neuropsychological counseling may support positive outcomes in those who become seizure‐free and provide a framework that can be explored further in patients referred for resective surgery.

There has been a paucity of research into psychiatric outcomes associated with stereo‐EEG. A case report was the first of its kind to outline severe posttraumatic stress disorder following stereo‐EEG. 57 It was speculated that the emergence of psychiatric conditions following stereo‐EEG may be underreported, with this case highlighting the need for psychiatric evaluation before and after stereo‐EEG and psychological support during the admission. 57 A recent retrospective review of 164 stereo‐EEG cases identified seven patients (4.2%) who experienced either transient or persistent psychiatric symptoms, including depression, anxiety, and/or psychosis, with the emergence of symptoms across the acute, subacute, or chronic stages. 58 Patients who experienced psychiatric symptoms underwent bilateral or unilateral RFTHC within the amygdala and/or hippocampus, were of female sex, and had a history of a prior psychiatric condition. 58 This is in keeping with several known risk factors associated with de novo or recurrence of depressive or anxiety disorders following epilepsy surgery. 59 Given the benefits of psychological support surrounding epilepsy surgery, 59 the potential benefit of stereo‐EEG warrants further consideration.

Fundamental to presurgical counseling is ensuring informed consent. The intended goal of stereo‐EEG must be clear for all patients, whether that be to aid identification of the epileptogenic zone to determine suitability for resective surgery, as a definitive treatment of seizures (e.g. periventricular nodular heterotopia), or to guide subsequent neuromodulation. 10 , 20 The requirements of the admission should also be outlined, including the basic principles of cortical stimulation and RFTHC, aiming for spontaneous seizures, and associated steps that may be taken to increase the likelihood of seizures (e.g., reduced antiseizure medications, sleep deprivation) and the intended timeframe of the admission. 60 For patients with lower cognitive functioning or an intellectual disability, information should be appropriately modified and repeated over multiple sessions, ideally in the presence of a family member or carer. 1 , 61 Adapting to a patient's individual requirements fosters autonomy and in turn improves engagement.

Counseling around cognitive risks associated with RFTHC and subsequent resections

Cognitive risks are often considered one of the most concerning consequences of epilepsy surgery. 4 Although the region ablated during RFTHC is significantly smaller than that resected with surgery, emerging research suggests it may not necessarily be cognitively benign. 23 Counseling patients about possible cognitive outcomes is key to ensuring informed consent and preventing unanticipated deficits. 6 Although the exact location of RFTHC before the admission remains unknown, the planned electrode implantation can be used to guide neuropsychological counseling. Initiating these discussions at the bedside when the definitive plan for RFTHC has been established may not allow sufficient time for the patient to weigh up the costs and benefits.

When considering the likelihood of an acquired cognitive deficit post RFTHC, similar principles to epilepsy surgery can be considered. Fundamental to predicting decline post‐surgery is consideration of the cognitive capacity of the brain region to be resected. 62 , 63 The presence of a cognitive deficit that is consistent with the neuroanatomical substrate to be resected tends to be associated with a lower likelihood of decline. 63 On the contrary, the absence of a cognitive deficit has been associated with a higher risk of decline. 63 Additional factors such as older age of onset of seizures, older current age, and lower overall intellectual ability can also increase the likelihood of cognitive decline. 5 , 64 Given the region ablated during RFTHC is significantly smaller than that resected with a surgery, it is likely to be associated with a lower risk of cognitive decline. The likelihood of seizure control following RFTHC, however, is also significantly reduced. 22 , 23 Furthermore, not all patients will be considered a surgical candidate following stereo‐EEG. 11 As such, the possibility of RFTHC causing a cognitive decline with ongoing seizures should be carefully considered to avoid patients incurring a ‘double loss’. 5 Further research is required, however, to characterize neuropsychological outcomes following RFTHC to support more accurate presurgical counseling.

Individual preferences for accepting cognitive risks may impact the likelihood of progressing to surgery. 6 , 65 Understanding tolerance for cognitive risks before stereo‐EEG is also important for providing informed consent. For instance, a patient with a dominant temporal lobe implant and low tolerance for accepting a decline in verbal memory function may be provided with a lower likelihood of progressing to a surgical resection. Surgery would be dependent on the identification of a focal epileptogenic zone that supported a tailored resection, sparing the hippocampus. Pre‐stereo‐EEG neuropsychological counseling around cognitive risk may not only be relevant to the current admission but also conceptualized in regard to the broader pathway to surgery.

Proposed recommendations for stereo‐EEG neuropsychological counseling

The feasibility and efficacy of a neuropsychological counseling framework that draws upon principles from epilepsy surgical programs warrants consideration (Table 2). 1 , 6 In line with this model, patients should be seen after the neuropsychological assessment for feedback and to discuss cognitive risks associated with possible RFTHC and subsequent surgical resections. In weighing up the cognitive risks, the likelihood of seizure control associated with RFTHC or progression to surgery should also be outlined. At this time, it may be helpful to canvas the patient's goals for pursuing stereo‐EEG. Obtaining a detailed psychiatric and psychosocial history at this stage can aid identification of patients at elevated risk of experiencing difficulties following stereo‐EEG to triage for closer monitoring.

Where feasible, review of patients within 4–6 weeks would support early identification of changes in psychological status and prompt interventions if required. Review of patients following the post stereo‐EEG neuropsychological assessment would enable feedback regarding any cognitive changes and also support monitoring of patients' progression toward their goals. In cases of seizure recurrence, it may include weighing up the costs and benefits of proposed treatments or assisting in adapting goals to support living a meaningful life despite seizures.

SPEECH AND LANGUAGE ASSESSMENT DURING STEREO‐EEG CORTICAL STIMULATION

Cortical stimulation involves the delivery of an electrical current to a specific location on an electrode that either causes excitation of neurons, which may be associated with an activation behavior, or a disruption of neuronal activity, which may be associated with an inhibitory response. 14 , 15 , 66 , 67 Disruption in performance on a cognitive task during cortical stimulation has been conceptualized as increasing the likelihood that a deficit would be sustained post‐resection of the region. 66 , 68 In a recent survey of stereo‐EEG centers, over three‐quarters of respondents considered a speech or language deficit elicited during cortical stimulation a contraindication to resective surgery in the same brain region. 17 Despite the reliance on cortical stimulation findings guiding surgical resective boundaries, the clinical significance of elicited deficits remains contentious, particularly for more complex cognitive functions. 69 , 70 , 71

Deficits elicited during cortical stimulation tend to be attributed to the stimulated regions rather than more appropriately being interpreted in the context of the broader cognitive network. 72 , 73 Disregard for the cognitive network that a stimulated region lies within risks reverting the field to a phrenological model of cognition where specific cognitive functions are localized to discrete regions. 74 Using a narrative approach to synthesize the literature surrounding cognitive assessment during stereo‐EEG cortical stimulation, here we evaluate the clinical significance of deficits elicited during cortical stimulation to better inform surgical decisions and improve patient outcomes. To date, examination of cognition during cortical stimulation has almost exclusively assessed speech and language function; therefore, it is the predominant focus of this review.

Narrative review methods

This section includes a narrative review of studies reporting language or speech assessment undertaken during stereo‐EEG cortical stimulation. We searched PubMed and Medline databases to November 2023 using the terms (1) ‘epilepsy’ and (2) ‘stereo‐EEG’ or ‘SEEG’ or ‘stereoelectroencephalography’ and (3) ‘cortical stimulation’ and (4) ‘functional mapping’ or ‘language’ or ‘speech’. Inclusion criteria included original research and adult population. Reference lists were searched for relevant studies to be added. Ultimately, only 14 studies met inclusion criteria following full text review; they were incorporated into the narrative review.

Conceptualizing speech and language networks during cortical stimulation

Comparisons between functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies and high‐frequency activity (HFA) with stereo‐EEG cortical stimulation aim to reveal the clinical significance of deficits observed during cortical stimulation. HFA that coincides with completion of a cognitive task has been considered a putative biomarker of network involvement. 69 , 75 Likewise, fMRI depicts individualized activation patterns within the anterior and posterior language networks to highlight the topography of the broad regions involved in speech and language. 26 Considerable variability is seen between language maps generated by HFA or fMRI with those from stereo‐EEG cortical stimulation. 67 , 76 , 77 fMRI and HFA analyses predict language deficits elicited from cortical stimulation with high specificity; that is, it can dependably classify true negative sites. 67 , 76 , 77 By contrast, sensitivity tends to be insufficient, meaning there is less consistency in the identification of true positive regions. 67 , 76 , 77

Discrepancies are postulated to reflect differences in the fundamental principles of each technique. By nature of cortical stimulation being an inhibitory technique, deficits are only observed when disruption to a small region of cortex is unable to be compensated for by the remainder of the network. 76 As such, deficits during cortical stimulation may in fact reflect disruption to essential nodes within the language network, contrasting analyses of HFA and fMRI that aim to highlight all regions involved in language function. 69 , 70 , 71 As such, language maps generated by HFA or fMRI may facilitate more targeted assessments during cortical stimulation, in turn reducing the laborious nature of these sessions, 67 , 76 while cortical stimulation may be prioritized for guiding resective surgeries that aim to reduce the risk of deficits.

Localization of deficits observed during cortical stimulation is challenged by possible propagation and disruption to distal regions within the network. 18 Indeed, speech arrest during cortical stimulation has been associated with a higher probability of HFA changes that extend to bilateral fronto‐parieto‐temporal networks, suggesting deficits are more likely to arise when there are changes to a broader network. 19 Consequently, when surgical boundaries are inferred by apparent regional dysfunction, unexpected deficits can present. 78 On the contrary, resection of positive language sites has also been associated with no chronic deficits. 79 In this instance, it was proposed that the stimulation‐induced deficit was caused by the disruption of a distal node within the network that was outside the surgical boundary. 80 , 81 Analyses of HFA associated with cortical stimulation may facilitate interpretation of the deficit as being localizing or secondary to disruption of a broader language network.

As outlined in Table 3, consideration of neurostimulation parameters is also essential when interpreting the localizing value of an observed deficit. Increased currents reduce the localizing capacity of the stimulation as it is more likely to engage a diffuse neuronal network that inevitably results in a language deficit. 14 , 76 , 82 Similar language deficits have been stimulated across various fronto‐parieto‐temporal regions. 18 , 19 Given the low sensitivity of cortical stimulation relative to fMRI and HFA analysis, 67 , 76 , 77 deficits observed outside key language regions should be interpreted cautiously. In these instances, neurostimulation parameters should be carefully considered, and the EEG reviewed for any indication of disruption to a more diffuse network. To improve the sensitivity of language assessment during cortical stimulation, intermediate frequencies have been proposed. Intermediate (between 6 and 12 Hz) and high frequency cortical stimulation have been shown to produce similar rates of positive language responses, with intermediate frequencies less likely to lead to an after discharge. 83 , 84 Intermediate frequencies warrant further consideration to improve localizing capacity of speech and language assessment during cortical stimulation.

Speech and language outcomes following resective surgery guided by stereo‐EEG

Speech or language sites identified during cortical stimulation tend to be spared in surgical resections. As such, it is challenging to disentangle the capacity of cortical stimulation to identify essential regions within a network that, when spared, reduce the likelihood of permanent deficits. To overcome this methodological limitation, a retrospective study reviewed outcomes following resection of the basal temporal area that were planned independently from the results of the cortical stimulation language assessment. 85 One year post‐surgery, confrontation naming deficits were more likely when the resection cavity included positive language sites; however, group differences did not persist five years post‐surgery. 85 In a single case study, resection of positive language sites within the hippocampus and temporal pole was also associated with a decline in confrontation naming 6 months post‐resection that improved by 12 months. 86 Together, these studies highlight the potential capacity for cortical stimulation to identify interindividual variability in network organization that can inform the likelihood of early neuropsychological deficits. 85 , 86

Further evaluation of the clinical utility of cortical stimulation in predicting outcomes has been limited by the absence of neuropsychological assessments post‐surgery. 15 , 87 , 88 While two additional case series have reported no deficits following surgeries guided by speech and language examination during cortical stimulation, conclusions about the clinical utility were limited by neither study outlining whether positive sites were included or excluded from resection boundaries. 15 , 88 Additionally, they did not outline the criteria used for the classification of speech or language deficits post‐resection. To exclude the possibility of subtle changes, a comprehensive neuropsychological assessment post‐resection is required.

Visual confrontation naming is the most frequently used task. Although it is shown to be the most sensitive task for detecting deficits within the inferior frontal gyrus, inferior and middle temporal gyrus, and mesial temporal region, within the insula, speech deficits were most notable on reading or automatic speech tasks. 14 A single case study demonstrated cortical stimulation within the posterior fusiform gyrus caused a reading impairment, while confrontation naming, writing, repetition, and facial recognition were preserved. 72 Together, these studies highlight that deficits can be partially attributed to the task administered. Unfortunately, the remaining studies that used multiple tasks did not distinguish between deficits elicited by visual naming from other tasks, 15 , 76 limiting the clinical translation for the development of assessment protocols.

Classification criteria for positive sites were inconsistent. Rarely was it stipulated that a reproducible deficit was required for the site to be classified as positive. 15 , 77 A nonreplicated language deficit identified during stereo‐EEG was not observed during an awake craniotomy and was resected with no longstanding cognitive impairment, 87 contrasting with reproducible deficits identified during stereo‐EEG, being consistent with findings during an awake craniotomy. 15 Clinical guidelines for classification criteria are required to avoid misinterpretation of results that may preclude a surgical resection due to overestimation of the likelihood of a cognitive deficit.

Categorization of speech and language deficits also differs across studies. While differentiating between anomia, paraphasias, and speech arrest aims to identify subnetworks of speech and language, 91 it has been argued that standard visual naming tasks prevent distinguishing between deficits caused by a motor difficulty from a lexico‐semantic access impairment. 19 Future research should consider using paradigms that can distinguish between error types.

Speech and language assessment during cortical stimulation proposed recommendations

Cortical stimulation is posited as the gold standard for assessment of cognition. 18 Despite this, the current review demonstrates that assessment remains heterogeneous and the interpretation of deficits is variable. As outlined in Table 3, there is a complex interplay between factors that can impact the sensitivity and specificity of assessment during cortical stimulation. There is an unquestionable need for future research in this area. To understand the prognostic value of deficits elicited during cortical stimulation, neuropsychological follow‐up after a surgical resection is essential.

A few guiding principles can be inferred from the current review. Task selection can impact findings. 14 , 72 Current research prevents recommendations regarding the use of specific tasks within different regions. Clinical expertise regarding the function supported by the underlying cortex is required to guide task selection that avoids false negative results. The second key finding is that when deficits are elicited, they should be interpreted in the context of cognitive networks. 18 , 19 This includes considering the neurostimulation parameters, reviewing network changes on the EEG, and considering language maps generated from fMRI and HFA analyses. Lastly, deficits identified during cortical stimulation should be replicated. 15 , 87 This is important to prevent patients from being unduly precluded from a surgical resection based on the perceived cognitive risk.

ASSESSING OTHER ASPECTS OF COGNITION DURING CORTICAL STIMULATION

Memory deficits are often the most debilitating consequence of epilepsy surgery 27 , 92 ; yet there has been limited evaluation of the feasibility of assessing memory during stereo‐EEG. 14 , 93 The possible clinical utility was outlined in a recent case study of a 51‐year‐old woman with left TLE who failed a Wada test and was subsequently implanted with a single depth electrode for the purpose of memory examination during stimulation to the hippocampus. 94 During continuous stimulation of the hippocampus (100 Hz with a pulse width of 500 μs and ~1.3 mA to each of the four pairs of contacts), the patient scored 8/8 on a recognition trial with only one false positive; a stereotactic laser amygdalohippocampectomy was undertaken with no memory deficits. 94 A stereo‐EEG case study described reduced memory performance on a verbal list learning task during a 50–70 s focal aware seizure consistently elicited following 50 Hz stimulation of the left hippocampus. 95 Auditory working memory and speed of information processing were unaffected. 95 The prognostic value remains unknown; however, as surgery was not undertaken due to concerns regarding a permanent memory deficit raised from cortical stimulation. 95 This case study takes advantage of consistently elicited prolonged focal aware seizures, preventing translation to cortical stimulation, where time constraints are much narrower. Additionally, cognitive assessment during high frequency stimulation of mesial structures is not always feasible due to the symptoms or seizures elicited that may impede completion of the task. 14

Assessment of memory has also been reported during cortical stimulation of the posterior cingulate. Reduced verbal list recall was associated with high‐frequency stimulation delivered over 25 s. 96 Given that the stimulation timeframe is significantly longer than the recommended limits, 10 the translational value of this paradigm is limited. Furthermore, analysis of gamma band activity within the hippocampus was associated with posterior cingulate stimulation, limiting the localizing value of the memory deficit. Further research is required to determine the feasibility of assessing memory during cortical stimulation.

Although visual hallucinations or illusions elicited during stereo‐EEG cortical stimulation are frequently reported, 97 , 98 , 99 assessment of visuospatial function is often overlooked. One study identified a rightward bias on a line bisection task during cortical stimulation of the right inferior parietal lobule, which was not apparent during cortical stimulation of the frontal or temporal lobes. 100 In addition to line bisection, fundamental visuospatial tasks such as line orientation, mental rotation, or perceptual discrimination may be suitable for cortical stimulation. Deficits on these tasks have been consistently associated with damage to the nondominant parietal lobe, and completion within the time restraints of cortical stimulation is feasible. 93

CONCLUSION

In sum, clinical neuropsychology plays a critical role in stereo‐EEG programs. For complex stereo‐EEG patients, the neuropsychological assessment is another noninvasive investigation that can contribute to hypothesis generation regarding lateralization and localization of the epileptogenic network. As with surgical programs, the value of neuropsychology reaches far beyond diagnostic localization. 2 , 30 A framework that incorporates neuropsychological counseling can ensure individualized discussion of cognitive risks, monitoring and management of psychological and psychosocial risk factors, and development of realistic and attainable goals for the admission. Clinical neuropsychology can also contribute unique expertise to the assessment of cognition during cortical stimulation.

Despite the potential clinical utility, neuropsychology in stereo‐EEG has often been overlooked. The lack of neuropsychological assessments following stereo‐EEG has limited the prognostic value of deficits identified during cortical stimulation, and the cognitive sequelae of RFTHC remain unclear. An internationally standardized approach is required to provide a common clinical and research framework for optimizing outcomes for people with drug‐resistant epilepsy.
