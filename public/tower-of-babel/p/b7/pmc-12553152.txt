From variability to consistency: building a Kellgren-Lawrence gonarthrosis dataset
Authors: Salih Beyaz, Sahika Betul Yayli, Kutay Kılıç
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to address variability in Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) staging by analyzing interobserver differences between radiologists and orthopedic surgeons to build unbiased datasets for AI-driven gonarthrosis solutions.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed 15,159 weight-bearing AP knee radiographs from patients aged 18 years and older collected from five centers between 2011 and 2020. The radiographs were labeled by two radiologists and two orthopedic surgeons independently, followed by a consensus evaluation by two experienced orthopedic specialists. KL staging was assessed both on a 0–4 scale and in two clinically relevant categories: early stage (KL ≤ 2) and advanced stage (KL ≥ 3). In clinical practice, low stage cases are generally managed with conservative treatments, while high-stage cases often indicate a higher need for surgical intervention.ResultsA total of 15,159 knee radiographs were analyzed, with participants comprising 6,207 (40.9%) males and 8,952 (59.1%) females, with a mean age of 56.97 ± 4.2 years. The agreement between radiologists and experts in the KL staging ranged from κ = 0.085 to κ = 0.172, while agreement between orthopedic surgeons and experts ranged from κ = 0.18 to κ = 0.307. These values indicate slight to fair agreement among radiologists and fair to moderate agreement among orthopedic surgeons on the original KL stage The categorization of the KL stages into two groups (KL ≤ 2 vs. KL ≥ 3) resulted in enhanced inter-rater reliability, with orthopedic surgeons demonstrating substantial agreement (κ = 0.637–0.692) compared to radiologists (κ = 0.183–0.230). The highest intra-professional agreement was observed between Radiologist 1 and Radiologist 2 (κ = 0.55) on the 0–4 the KL scale and between Orthopedic Surgeon 1 and Orthopedic Surgeon 2 (κ = 0.642) in the two-category classification.ConclusionsOrthopedic surgeons showed higher agreement with experts and among themselves, particularly in advanced stages (KL ≥ 3), compared to radiologists, who demonstrated only mild to moderate agreement. Categorizing KL stages into early (KL ≤ 2) and advanced (KL ≥ 3) groups improved interobserver reliability, thereby facilitating the development of standardized AI datasets.Keywords: Kellgren Lawrence; Interobserver reliability; Algorithm training, gonarthrosis; Knee

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is characterized by the loss of cartilage, which in turn results in increased mechanical loading and joint instability. This leads to subchondral sclerosis, cyst formation, and osteophyte formation on the joint surface [1]. OA affects 4% of the world’s population [2]. The prevalence of knee OA is rising due to risk factors such as an aging population and increasing rates of obesity, but also due to growing participation in high-impact sports among middle-aged individuals, which may accelerate cartilage degeneration and joint overload [1, 3, 4]. In addition to clinical assessment, weight-bearing knee radiography is a common and cost-effective diagnostic modality [5, 6].

The Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grading system for gonarthrosis is a subjective staging system primarily based on joint space narrowing and osteophyte formation within the knee joint [7]. Despite the existence of alternative classification systems, such as Ahlbäck and the International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC), KL remains the most prevalent system worldwide [8, 9]. An anteroposterior (AP) knee radiograph taken under weight-bearing conditions categorizes the degree of deformation on a scale from 0 to 4. Grades ≤ 2 are primarily classified as early-stage gonarthrosis, while grades KL ≥ 3, characterized by significant joint space narrowing, extensive osteophyte formation, and accompanying subchondral cysts, are deemed advanced-stage arthrosis. In the initial stages of the disease, non-surgical interventions are typically employed, whereas in more advanced cases, total knee arthroplasty is often the recommended course of treatment [10, 11]. One of the major drawbacks of KL grading is its low interobserver and intraobserver reliability, which often leads to discrepancies in treatment decisions among clinicians.

Multiple studies have reported that automated KL grading systems based on deep learning consistently achieve their highest accuracy at the extremes of the scale, particularly in grades 0 and 4, where radiographic features are more distinct and reproducible. However, classification performance drops substantially for intermediate grades (KL 1–3), likely due to ambiguous radiographic findings, inconsistent labeling, and the inherent subjectivity of the KL system itself [12–14] Since consistent labeling is essential for developing robust AI algorithms, we hypothesized that interobserver agreement in KL grading would be limited—particularly between radiologists and orthopedic surgeons—and that dichotomizing KL grades into early (KL ≤ 2) and advanced (KL ≥ 3) categories would enhance consistency across annotators. This study aims to address variability in the KL staging by analyzing interobserver differences between radiologists and orthopedic surgeons to build unbiased datasets for AI-driven gonarthrosis solutions.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cohort study received ethical approval from the Başkent University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee on 14/10/2022 (Project number KA22/434). The dataset initially comprised of 24,193 weight-bearing AP knee radiographs collected from five centers between 2011 and 2020. Since the primary aim of the study was to create a high-quality, expert-labeled dataset both for internal use and for future research purposes, no formal power analysis or sample size calculation was conducted. Instead, the dataset included the maximum number of eligible radiographs available from the defined period to ensure the representativeness and robustness of the final dataset.

Before the labeling phase, several exclusion criteria were applied to ensure consistency and diagnostic quality across the dataset. Specifically, 3,670 radiographs were excluded due to the presence of knee implants, as metallic hardware may obscure key anatomical landmarks and interfere with KL grading. Additionally, 1,910 images with evident fractures were excluded to avoid distortion in joint morphology assessments. A total of 1,505 non-weight-bearing radiographs were also excluded, since evaluating joint space narrowing in the absence of physiological loading does not reflect standard clinical assessment practices for gonarthrosis. Furthermore, 1,176 radiographs containing visible foreign objects and 84 low-resolution images—extracted from the PACS system—were excluded due to concerns about diagnostic clarity and reproducibility. After applying these criteria, the final dataset consisted of 15,848 radiographs. The image selection and exclusion process is summarized in Fig. 1.

Selection of weight-bearing anteroposterior knee X-rays collected from multiple hospitals

All patient data (e.g., age, birthdate, protocol number) on the radiographs were anonymized. The images were converted from DICOM to JPEG format and uploaded to the Computer Vision Annotation Tool (CVAT), an interactive video and image annotation tool commonly used in computer vision tasks. The research team self-hosted CVAT on a dedicated server to ensure data security, maintain optimal performance, and avoid risks associated with mainstream cloud platforms. This setup safeguarded confidentiality while providing labelers with a user-friendly interface.

Each labeler was assigned unique login credentials, allowing them to access the system securely through a web browser. Labelers classified gonarthrosis grades using the KL classification criteria for AP radiographs. The labeling process was conducted without access to demographic data, including age, gender, comorbidities, and the source hospital, to mitigate the likelihood of bias. Additionally, a system restriction limited labeling to 300 radiographs daily to ensure quality and reduce fatigue. All participating labelers and experts were compensated for their contributions to the study.

Labeling process

The labeling process consisted of two distinct stages:

First stage

Four specialists, two orthopedic surgeons and two radiologists, each with at least ten years of experience independently labeled the radiographs. During this stage, labelers were allowed to exclude any radiograph they deemed unsuitable for the KL gonarthrosis staging, resulting in the omission of 689 images.

Second stage

Two senior orthopedic specialists, each with a minimum of 20 years of experience and performing at least 100 total knee arthroplasty procedures annually, collaboratively reviewed and labeled the radiographs. These specialists, referred to as the “experts,” finalized the labels during this stage. Their joint evaluation outcomes were designated as the “expert assessment.”

Statistical analysis

Cohen’s kappa statistics were used to evaluate inter-rater reliability between two raters, while Fleiss’ kappa was calculated to assess agreement among multiple raters [15]. Kappa values were interpreted as follows:

Results

This study analyzed 15,159 knee radiographs, comprising 6,207 male (40.9%) and 8,952 female (59.1%) participants, with a mean age of 56.97 ± 4.2 years. Among the participants, 10,506 (69.3%) were aged 50 years or older, while 4,653 (30.7%) were younger than 50 years.

The distribution of KL stages (0–4) varied substantially across annotators, with notable differences observed between radiologists and orthopedic surgeons (Fig. 2, left). Radiologists tended to assign lower KL stages, whereas orthopedic surgeons more frequently labeled higher stages. To further explore interpretability, we also grouped KL stages into two clinically meaningful categories—early (KL ≤ 2) and advance (KL ≥ 3)—and evaluated these dichotomized labels in our analysis (Fig. 2, right). It is important to note that annotators only provided labels on the original 5-point scale; dichotomized analyses were performed post hoc.

Interobserver agreement between radiologists and the expert reference was weak for the full KL stage classification, with κ ranging from 0.085 (Radiologist 2) to 0.172 (Radiologist 1). Orthopedic surgeons showed slightly better agreement: κ = 0.180 (Orthopedic Surgeon 2) and κ = 0.307 (Orthopedic Surgeon 1). These trends are reflected in the multiclass confusion matrices shown in Fig. 3 (top row). When KL stages were dichotomized into early (KL ≤ 2) and advance (KL ≥ 3) groups, agreement with the expert improved across all annotators. Radiologist 1 demonstrated slight agreement (κ = 0.183), and Radiologist 2 showed fair agreement (κ = 0.230). Orthopedic Surgeon 1 and 2 achieved substantial agreement with the expert (κ = 0.692 and κ = 0.637, respectively). The corresponding binary confusion matrices are shown in Fig. 3 (bottom row) ROC curve analyses further supported these findings. For the original 5-class labels, AUC values varied across annotators and KL stages (Fig. 4, top row), with radiologists generally demonstrating lower performance. Orthopedic Surgeon 1, in particular, showed stronger alignment with the expert reference. Binary ROC analyses (Fig. 4, bottom row), based on the dichotomized stages, revealed higher discriminatory performance by orthopedic surgeons (AUCs: 0.90 and 0.87) compared to radiologists (AUCs: 0.57 and 0.58).

Discussion

The findings of this study demonstrate that orthopedic surgeons achieved higher accuracy in Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) staging of knee osteoarthritis (OA) compared to radiologists, particularly when the KL scores were categorized into early (KL ≤ 2) and advanced (KL ≥ 3) stages. The implementation of this categorization led to a notable enhancement in interobserver agreement across both groups. In the two-category system, orthopedic surgeons exhibited kappa values ranging from 0.637 to 0.692, whereas radiologists demonstrated a comparatively lower degree of agreement, with kappa values fluctuating between 0.183 and 0.230. However, despite these improvements, interobserver reliability for intermediate the KL grades (1 and 2) remained limited, with overall kappa values between 0.08 and 0.18 for both professions. These results underscore the variability inherent in the KL staging, particularly for intermediate grades, and emphasize the need for more robust strategies, such as AI-driven tools and standardized training protocols, to enhance the reliability and clinical utility of this widely used classification system.

Although dichotomization into early (KL ≤ 2) and advanced (KL ≥ 3) stages improves interobserver reliability, this approach inevitably reduces the granularity of information captured by the full five-grade KL scale. Fine distinctions between adjacent KL grades—particularly between grades 1 and 2, or 2 and 3—may be clinically meaningful in monitoring disease progression or tailoring conservative treatment strategies [16]. For instance, in patients with KL grade 2 or 3, joint-preserving surgical interventions such as high tibial osteotomy (HTO) may be considered, especially in younger and active individuals. In such cases, a dichotomized approach may not sufficiently support nuanced clinical decision-making. However, the inherent subjectivity and limited reproducibility of these intermediate stages diminish their practical utility, especially in real-world datasets. By prioritizing reliability, the dichotomized system offers a more robust foundation for algorithm development and decision-making in advanced OA cases, where treatment pathways —particularly surgical intervention —are more clearly defined.

In their study, Pan et al. examined 4,074 patients with the objective of developing a hierarchical classification method capable of AI-based Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) gonarthrosis staging [17]. They achieved the highest accuracy of 98.5% in distinguishing between KL grades ≤ 2 and KL grades ≥ 3. Similarly, in our study, we observed that reducing the number of stages to two categories (early: KL ≤ 2 and advanced: KL ≥ 3) significantly improved interobserver agreement. These findings suggest that focusing on the separation of early and advanced stages in dataset design can enhance both the classification accuracy and the clinical applicability of AI-based algorithms.

In a study by Guermazi et al., field researchers with training approved by a specialist musculoskeletal radiologist assessed osteophytes and joint space narrowing, achieving a kappa value of 0.52 [18]. However, when KL staging was categorized as KL ≥ 2 in their study, interobserver agreement decreased to a kappa value of 0.41. In contrast, the compliance rates observed in our study were lower, with kappa values ranging between 0.08 and 0.18 for both specialist radiologists and orthopedic surgeons. Interestingly, when the KL staging was categorized as KL ≥ 3 in our study, compliance improved slightly (kappa 0.18–0.19; ). Gunther et al. reported that the global KL score was highly reproducible. However, while intra-observer reliability for joint space narrowing was excellent, inter-observer reliability for subchondral sclerosis and chondrocalcinosis was less consistent [19].

The study conducted by Lee et al., which utilized the Multicenter Osteoarthritis Study (MOST) dataset, demonstrated that the accuracy of KL grading was 84%, 43%, 70%, 81%, and 96%, respectively, for KL grades 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4, with an overall accuracy rate of 75.7% [20]. These results highlight the highest accuracy in KL grades 0 and 4, demonstrating the challenges associated with intermediate grades like KL 1 and 2 due to their less distinct features. Similarly, in our study, we observed very low kappa values (0–2) between radiologists and orthopedic specialists for KL classification across both groups. This highlights the restricted interobserver reliability in KL grading, particularly in intermediate stages, and emphasizes the necessity for enhanced classification strategies or AI-based models to enhance consistency and clinical applicability.

In the treatment of advanced OA, TKA is a successful treatment method for relieving pain and improving patients’ life quality. However, TKA was appropriately performed in only two-thirds of patients and was defined mainly by radiographic criteria [21]. Although researchers have developed various radiographic classification systems to guide clinical decisions, there is no consensus on a comprehensive and practical method [22]. Our study compared the interobserver reliability of KL classification between experienced orthopedic surgeons and specialist radiologists using a large, unfiltered radiographic dataset. The results showed that orthopedists performed the best OA staging. However, in this study, compliance varied according to the field of specialization (musculoskeletal radiology). In comparison to the experts, both orthopedic surgeons and radiologists exhibited a greater degree of consensus. In contrast, the orthopedic surgeons exhibited a lower level of consensus than the radiologists. This supports the idea that gonarthrosis can be interpreted differently depending on the specialty that scores KL. Given the importance of observer reliability in AI model development, we further utilized this dataset in a separate study. In a related publication we employed the same dataset used in the current study to compare two different deep learning strategies. Notably, this study adhered to the original five-level Kellgren–Lawrence (KL 0–4) grading system, as also used in the present manuscript. The single-model approach, which directly classified KL grades from radiographs, achieved better results than the multi-model setup. Specifically, the single-model yielded an F1-score of 0.763 and an accuracy of 0.767, while the multi-model approach scored 0.736 and 0.740, respectively. This comparison highlights the practical relevance of consistent and reliable annotations in developing high-performance AI systems for gonarthrosis classification [23]. On the other hand, though the two radiologists participating in this study were musculoskeletal imaging specialists, it has been hypothesized that radiologists may be more frequently engaged with cross-sectional imaging modalities, particularly MRI, in their daily practice. This may limit their routine exposure to plain radiographs and affect their consistency in KL grading. Supporting this view, prior reports have shown that the proportion of MRI studies relative to conventional radiography has significantly increased in radiology practice, particularly in academic and hospital-based settings [24]. The workload and diversity of the radiologists in the centers where the study was conducted may have affected the results. The present study is more representative of actual practice since it is unlikely that there are radiologists with expertise in the musculoskeletal system in general health institutions. On the other hand, the secondary purpose of this study was to determine which group of specialists would correctly label mistakes during the development of an AI algorithm. In this regard, orthopedic surgeons are a better option in terms of evaluating the radiographs of patients with a PR diagnosis of OA. In clinical practice, KL classification stages ≥ 3 are commonly associated with surgical decision-making; however, this association may vary depending on physician expertise and institutional protocols [8]. In gonarthrosis cases at this stage, the surgical option is at the forefront of consideration. In our dataset, orthopedic surgeons demonstrated higher agreement levels than radiologists when scoring KL ≥ 3 stages, which are typically considered more clinically relevant in surgical planning. As stated, there is still no consensus on a comprehensive and practical method for diagnosing OA [22]. To our knowledge, our study is the first to categorize OA scoring as KL ≤ 2 and KL ≥ 3. The reason for categorizing KL classification in this way is that more surgical treatment options are applied in KL ≥ 3 clinical practice. The reliability of the orthopedic surgeons was also high when categorized in this way. These results suggest that orthopedists may offer more consistent assessments in OA staging, particularly for cases that typically inform surgical treatment decisions; however, this inference requires further validation in clinical outcome studies. Given their routine engagement in surgical planning, orthopedic surgeons may be more accustomed to interpreting radiographic findings in conjunction with MRI during perioperative decision-making In Abdelaziz et al.’s study, the knee condition observed during surgery was worse than during radiography and MRI [25]. For this reason, orthopedic surgeons may tend to stage radiographs at a higher grade than radiologists. However, this situation does not necessarily lead to patients being referred for more surgery.

Wright et al. reported that no radiographic scoring system has been demonstrated to have excellent interobserver reliability. However, they have been shown to have moderate or good reliability. The reliability of the KL classifications reported by researchers has been found to vary considerably between observers (0.51 to 0.89) [18, 22]. In contrast, several studies have suggested that the KL classification system is well-defined and should be the mainstay classification for research purposes and for developing a radiographic atlas of knee OA [26–28]. In recent studies conducted in relatively small populations based on these assumptions, the interobserver reliability of the KL classification ranged from 0.51 to 0.89 [29]. When the observers (experienced orthopedic surgeons, musculoskeletal radiologists, and rheumatologists) had musculoskeletal expertise, this increased the interobserver reliability of the KL classification [29]. In a study of the KL classification system, Felson et al. reported high interobserver reliability (r = 0.85) in the evaluation of 1,454 plain knee radiographs by two expert radiologists [27]. In Scott et al. studies, two skeletal radiologists and two rheumatologists evaluated radiographs, and the interobserver reliability ratio was 0.68 [30].

In the early stages of gonarthrosis, the most common radiographic findings on anteroposterior knee images are joint space narrowing and the presence of osteophytes in weight-bearing areas. However, the primary issue lies in the fact that joint space narrowing is not based on objective measurements, and a standardized quantitative evaluation is lacking. This limitation becomes particularly problematic in ambiguous categories such as KL grade 1. For instance, whether a radiograph labeled as KL grade 1 but classified by an AI algorithm as grade 0 (or vice versa) should be considered a misclassification remains debatable. Similar uncertainties exist between KL grades 3 and 4. As previously noted, both the traditional five-grade KL system and alternative dichotomous categorizations (e.g., KL ≤ 2 vs. (KL ≥ 3) suffer from substantial gray zones and labeling inconsistencies. Although staging plays a significant role in clinical decision-making, it should not be considered the sole determinant. A patient’s symptoms, functional status, expectations, and overall quality of life are multifactorial elements that equally influence treatment planning. Therefore, when discussing the role of KL classification in the management of early OA, it is essential to acknowledge the inherent limitations of the system and consider its implications for both AI model performance and clinical treatment algorithms [28–31]. There is a lack of consensus among major osteoarthritis (OA) cohort studies on the definition and grading of the disease according to the original KL classification system. This highlights the urgent need for a single, valid, and applicable classification method that can be universally accepted [31]. AI-based algorithms have emerged as promising tools for improving consensus among experts. Studies such as that conducted by Nehrer et al. have demonstrated that the use of AI can lead to increased agreement rates among physicians [32]. While the KL system demonstrates robust interobserver agreement on standardized (filtered) radiographic datasets, its consistency decreases significantly when applied to unfiltered AP knee radiographs. Our study, which utilized unfiltered radiographs, revealed reduced interobserver reliability among experienced orthopedic surgeons and radiologists. To address the challenges of suboptimal images, an “exclude” option was provided, leading to the omission of 689 radiographs.

The findings revealed that radiologists exhibited a higher degree of agreement with experts in the initial stages (KL ≤ 2) compared to the advanced stages (KL ≥ 3), where compatibility levels significantly declined. This trend may reflect radiologists’ tendency to classify patients earlier in the disease progression, potentially avoiding “advanced stage” diagnoses that imply surgical decisions. Conversely, orthopedic surgeons, although less aligned with experts in early-stage classifications, demonstrated greater consistency in their agreement across both early and advanced stages. This is likely to reflect their routine involvement in surgical decision-making, where staging plays a critical role in treatment planning. These findings underscore the variability in KL grading across specialties and the importance of integrating practical and AI-based solutions to improve reliability in clinical settings.

For this reason, labeling should be done by physicians with high clinical experience and surgical responsibility in developing AI algorithms. Another problem during the development of AI algorithms is bias. The primary reason for decision bias in the algorithm is that biased labelers develop the labeling of the data. These findings highlight a potential gap between algorithm performance in academic validation settings and their applicability in routine clinical practice, especially when the training data may reflect labeling inconsistencies or context-specific biases.

This study has several limitations. First, all evaluations were based solely on anteroposterior (AP) knee radiographs, without the use of additional imaging modalities. Second, the raters—although experienced—were not subjected to standardized KL training sessions prior to labeling. Third, the radiographs used were unfiltered, reflecting real-world variability in image quality. Finally, each image was labeled only once per rater, without repeated assessments to evaluate intra-observer reliability. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of our findings and the potential impact on AI model development.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that orthopedic surgeons exhibited superior accuracy in KL staging compared to radiologists, particularly when KL scores were categorized into early (KL ≤ 2) and advanced (KL ≥ 3) stages. However, interobserver reliability remained limited, particularly for intermediate KL grades, underscoring the challenges of consistent application of the KL classification system. The findings align with prior studies, emphasizing the potential of simplifying KL staging into two categories to improve both clinical applicability and AI training efficiency. The variability observed between radiologists and orthopedic specialists further highlights the need for standardized training protocols and the development of AI-based tools to enhance consistency. Future research should focus on integrating multimodal datasets and advanced AI methodologies to address these limitations and advance the clinical utility of automated KL grading systems. Additionally, when algorithms are developed using such datasets, collecting structured feedback from end-users during real-world deployment may help identify potential misclassifications and enable continuous model refinement.
