Lassa fever in West Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis of attack rates, case fatality rates and risk factors
Authors: Grace Opeoluwa Ogundele, Kelvin Olutimilehin Jolayemi, Segun Bello
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLassa fever is an acute viral haemorrhagic disease endemic to West Africa, with Nigeria, Sierra Leone, and Liberia bearing the greatest burden. Despite repeated outbreaks and rising incidence, a regional synthesis of epidemiologic indicators remains lacking. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate pooled attack rates (proportion of a population affected during an outbreak), case fatality rates (CFRs), and identify consistent risk factors associated with Lassa fever in West Africa.MethodsFollowing PRISMA 2020 guidelines, we systematically searched PubMed, Scopus, AJOL, and Web of Science for observational studies published between 1969 and 2025. Eligible studies reported attack rates, CFRs, or risk factors for Lassa fever among human populations. Only English-language, peer-reviewed studies were included and included study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Data were analyzed using random-effects meta-analysis. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, and subgroup analyses were conducted by country, diagnostic method, study design, and setting.ResultsOut of 333 identified studies, thirty-five studies were included. The pooled attack rate was 21.0% (95% CI: 18.0%-24.0%, I² = 98.6%) and CFR was 27.0% (95% CI: 20.0%-35.0%, I² = 99.4%), with significant heterogeneity. Subgroup analysis showed higher CFRs in Sierra Leone (48%) and Liberia (40%) compared to Nigeria (16%). Cohort studies and hospital-based settings reported markedly higher attack rates and CFRs. RT-PCR-based diagnostics were associated with lower CFRs than serological or mixed-method studies. The pooled odds ratio for risk factors was 1.29 (95%CI: 1.10–1.49), with environmental exposures (rodent infestation, poor housing) having the strongest association (OR = 2.18). Evidence also pointed to a shift in disease transmission from rural to peri-urban settings, driven by urbanization, habitat loss, and climate change.ConclusionLassa fever continues to pose a severe public health threat in West Africa, characterized by high transmission, elevated mortality, and evolving ecological dynamics. To reduce morbidity and mortality, integrated strategies are needed such as early diagnosis via decentralized RT-PCR, environmental hygiene, risk communication, and vaccine preparedness within a One Health framework.Keywords: Lassa fever, Attack rate, Meta-analysis, West africa, Case fatality rate, Risk factors

Introduction

Over the years, Lassa fever has been one of the leading endemic diseases/illnesses affecting Africa, especially West Africa and it has remained a public health challenge [1]. It is a viral zoonotic illness caused by the Lassa virus, a single-stranded, enveloped Ribonucleic Acid (RNA) virus belonging to the family Arenaviridae [2–4]. The virus has an incubation period of 6–21 days, primarily transmitted via exposure to faeces, urine, or other bodily fluids of the reservoir, Mastomys natalensis [1, 5] and it is excreted in urine between 3 and 9 weeks of infection while it can be retained for 3 months in semen [6, 7]. The main carrier of Lassa fever, Mastomys natalensis, produce large numbers of offspring and are abundant in the savannah and forests of West, Central, and East Africa [8, 9]. Despite this wide geographic distribution of the rodent reservoir, Lassa virus disease is endemic primarily in West Africa. This disparity may be attributed to ecological, socio-behavioural, and virological factors, including region-specific strains of the virus, heightened human-rodent interaction due to housing and food storage practices, and gaps in surveillance and diagnostic capacity in other regions [9, 10]. Globally, the overall case fatality rate (CFR) of Lassa fever is estimated at 1–2% among all infections, largely due to mild or asymptomatic presentation [11]. However, in hospitalized patients particularly in endemic countries such as Nigeria, Sierra Leone, and Liberia, the CFR rises markedly, typically ranging from 15% to over 20%, with regional meta-analyses reporting pooled CFRs of 19–30% [11]. Moreover, specific subpopulations including children, pregnant women, and during healthcareassociated outbreaks experience substantially higher mortality, with CFRs reported between 30% and as high as 80% [11]. About 80% of infected patients are asymptomatic or present with mild symptoms [6]. For people who are symptomatic, the clinical manifestation of the viral infection includes fever, malaise, cough, chest pain, difficulty in breathing, abdominal cramps, vomiting, diarrhoea, and sore throat [12]. Patients can also present with severe symptoms such as bleeding from mucosal surfaces, pleural effusion, pericardial effusion, seizures, disorientation, and unconsciousness [13].

Lassa fever’s primary risk factor is living in proximity to the reservoir (Mastomys natalensis) especially in settings with poor housing quality that facilitate rodent intrusion [14, 15]. Other important risk factors include inadequate food storage, lack of rodent control, and environmental degradation that increases human-rodent interaction [14, 16]. Specific population groups more at risk include women in rural communities due to their roles in food handling and household hygiene, children and the elderly who may have weaker immune responses, and occupational groups such as healthcare workers, slaughterhouse personnel, and miners who face increased exposure to contaminated environments or infected individuals [16–18]. However, person-to-person (secondary) transmission is also possible, particularly in healthcare and household settings, through direct contact with blood, urine, feces, vomit, or other bodily fluids of an infected person or contaminated surfaces [12]. While zoonotic transmission from rodents account for the majority of cases, nosocomial transmission has been responsible for up to 20% of infections during certain outbreaks, especially where infection prevention and control (IPC) measures are weak [13, 19]. Healthcare workers without adequate personal protective equipment (PPE) are especially vulnerable, and several documented outbreaks have involved secondary transmission chains within hospitals and households [20].

Recent studies suggest that the incidence of Lassa fever has been rising, with more frequent outbreaks and sustained transmission in previously less-affected areas [21–23]. For example, Nigeria has experienced a marked increase in confirmed annual cases since 2018, surpassing 1,000 cases per year, with infections now being reported from states historically considered non-endemic [24]. Similarly, seroprevalence surveys and environmental models indicate that the geographic range of the Lassa virus is expanding likely driven by factors such as climate change, deforestation, rodent migration, and increasing urbanization [25–27]. Climate change, through altered rainfall patterns and seasonal temperature shifts, has been linked to fluctuations in rodent population dynamics, urbanization and deforestation increase human contact with rodent habitats, facilitating zoonotic spillover. These trends reinforce the need for an integrated One Health approach that combines human, animal, and environmental surveillance systems to understand and control emerging infectious diseases like Lassa fever. Despite this expansion, the burden remains underestimated at the community level due to weak surveillance and limited diagnostic capacity. Existing reviews on Lassa fever have been predominantly narrative in nature, offering descriptive insights into epidemiology, clinical features, and control strategies without providing pooled estimates of key indicators such as attack rate or case fatality rate [13, 15, 21]. This gap underscores the need for meta-analytic approaches to inform evidence-based decision-making and intervention designs in line with One-Health strategies. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the pooled attack rate, case fatality rate and identify risk factors associated with Lassa fever outbreaks in West Africa.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies on the attack rate, case fatality rate and risk factors of Lassa fever in West Africa. The preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) checklist was used for the design of this systematic review (Suppl.1). It was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023393135). The following were the inclusion criteria for selecting eligible studies: Studies including participants of all ages and genders were eligible, regardless of symptom status. Both community-based and hospital-based studies were included, as long as they reported attack rates, case fatality rates, or risk factors related to Lassa fever infection; studies that assessed and reported the risk factors of Lassa fever in any sub-Saharan country as defined by the United Nations Statistics Division [28].

Data sources and search strategy

This review followed the PRISMA guidelines. A comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, African Journals Online (AJOL), Scopus, and Web of Science, using combinations of the following keywords: “Lassa fever,” “attack rate,” “case fatality”, “incidence,” “risk factors,” “West Africa,” and “outbreak.”

We defined “attack rate” as the proportion of individuals in a defined population who developed Lassa fever during a specified outbreak or study period, in line with epidemiological definitions of incidence proportion. Case fatality rate was defined as the proportion of deaths among individuals with laboratory-confirmed Lassa fever, unless otherwise stated in the original study [29]. Both primary and secondary attack rates were considered eligible if explicitly reported. However, given the inconsistencies in terminology across studies, we also included studies that reported incidence-like measures (e.g., number of cases per population exposed over a defined period), where sufficient data were available to calculate attack rates. We recognize that prevalence is more commonly reported in Lassa fever literature, particularly in seroprevalence studies, but our focus remained on studies measuring new infection rates to capture outbreak dynamics. The search covered literature published between January 1969 and June 2025 and the last search was conducted on June 30, 2025. Only peer-reviewed articles reporting quantitative attack rates, case fatality rates or risk factors were included. Non-English studies, reviews, and editorials were excluded due to resource limitations in translation and data extraction capacity. While this may have excluded some relevant studies from francophone West African countries, English remains the dominant language of publication for epidemiological research on Lassa fever in West Africa. A comprehensive electronic search strategy designed to allow exhaustive identification of relevant studies was applied to all the databases (Suppl. 2). The list of references from the included studies and other relevant articles were manually screened for additional records.

Selection and extraction of data from included studies

After the searches were completed, all retrieved citations were imported into EndNote X9 (Clarivate Analytics) for reference management. Duplicate records were automatically identified and removed using EndNote’s “Find Duplicates” function, followed by manual checking to ensure accuracy. The remaining records were exported into Microsoft Excel, where two reviewers (GOO, KOJ) independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility using a standardized screening form. Full-text screening and data extraction were also conducted independently by two reviewers (GOO, KOJ) using the same Excel-based form, with disagreements resolved through discussion or by a third reviewer (SB). The extracted data contained the name of the author(s), the year of publication, the study design, the inclusion criteria (Lassa fever, attack rate, case fatality, risk factors), country, sampling method, study period, Lassa Fever detection marker (virus, antigen, RNA, IgM or IgG, PCR), age, sample size, type of sample used for Lassa fever detection and number of positive cases for Lassa fever.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Eligible studies were those conducted in West Africa, reported either an attack rate, case fatality rate (CFR), or assessed risk factors for Lassa fever. These studies had to include human participants of any age or gender and be published in English between January 1969 and June 2025. Observational study designs, such as cross-sectional, cohort, and case-control studies, were considered. Studies were excluded if they involved non-human subjects, did not report any of the outcomes of interest, were not accessible in full text, or were published in non-English languages.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they did not take place in West Africa or if they involved non-human subjects such as rodent studies or animal serosurveys. Additionally, studies were excluded if they lacked quantitative data on attack rates, case fatality rates, or risk factors. Other exclusions included reviews, editorials, commentaries, case reports, and outbreak alerts that did not provide sufficient epidemiological data. Studies published in languages other than English were not considered due to resource limitations in translation. Finally, articles that lacked full-text access or failed to provide adequate methodological detail necessary for quality assessment were also excluded.

Study quality

Study quality and risk of bias were assessed using tools appropriate to the study design. For cohort and case-control studies, the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was applied, evaluating selection, comparability, and outcome domains. For cross-sectional studies, a modified version of the NOS adapted for cross-sectional design was used, which included domains such as sample representativeness, ascertainment of exposure, and outcome measurement. Studies were independently assessed by two reviewers, and disagreements were resolved by consensus [30].

Data analysis

Data were extracted into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using R software (version 2025.05.1 + 513) with the meta and metafor packages. Meta-analyses were conducted separately for attack rates, case fatality rates (CFR), and risk factors using random-effects models (DerSimonian-Laird method), which account for between-study variability. All proportions (attack rates and CFRs) were logit-transformed to stabilize variances and back-transformed for interpretation. Heterogeneity across studies was quantified using the I² statistic, with values above 75% considered to represent substantial heterogeneity. Statistical significance of heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran’s Q-test. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity by diagnostic method (RT-PCR, ELISA, IFAT), study design (cohort, cross-sectional, case-control), and study setting (hospital-based vs. community-based). For the analysis of risk factors, studies that reported odds ratios (ORs) or provided sufficient raw data for computation were included. A pooled odds ratio and corresponding 95% confidence interval were calculated using the generic inverse-variance method under a random-effects model. Variables were grouped based on common themes (e.g., environmental exposure, occupational exposure, demographic factors), and heterogeneity was assessed similarly. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test for asymmetry, however, for outcomes with fewer than 10 studies (risk factors), publication bias was not formally assessed due to low statistical power. A p < 0.05 was considered indicative of small-study effects. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially excluding studies with extreme effect sizes or high risk of bias to assess the robustness of pooled estimates. For the analysis of risk factors, we used the Hartung-Knapp (HK) random-effects model to obtain more conservative and robust confidence intervals, particularly given the small number of studies (n < 10) and the high degree of heterogeneity. The HK method is known to provide better control of Type I error rates under such conditions compared to the conventional DerSimonian-Laird approach. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The meta-analytic results are presented in forest plots with corresponding confidence intervals and study weights, and detailed subgroup analyses are displayed to provide a comprehensive view of effect size variation across study characteristics.

Results

Result of search

The initial database search across PubMed, Scopus, African Journals Online (AJOL), and Web of Science yielded a total of 333 records. Out of the 333 identified records, 288 unique studies were screened after removing duplicates. After title and abstract screening, 75 full-text articles were reviewed and out of these, 40 were excluded for reasons such as; not reporting any of the three outcomes (n = 25), non-human subjects (n = 8), insufficient methodological detail (n = 7). (Fig. 1). Ultimately, 35 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis. Out of these, 31 studies provided data on attack rates and/or case fatality rates, and 8 studies reported risk factors (Suppl. 5) for Lassa fever. However, some studies contributed to more than one outcome (Table 1). While 31 studies were included in the meta-analysis of attack rate, not all explicitly reported it as such. In some instances, data were derived from outbreak settings with clearly defined at-risk populations, while in others, estimations were made based on incident cases over specified time periods. Seroprevalence-only studies were excluded from this synthesis to avoid conflating cumulative exposure with incident infection.

Characteristics of studies

A total of 35 studies were included in this review, all conducted in West Africa and published between 1977 and 2025. The studies were carried out in seven countries, with Nigeria (n = 18) contributing the highest number of studies [17, 31–34, 36, 43, 48, 51–57, 59–61] followed by Sierra Leone (n = 8) [16, 19, 35, 41, 42, 47, 49],, and [58]. Liberia had four studies: [37, 38, 46, 50] while Guinea accounted for three studies: [14, 39, 40]. One study was conducted jointly in Guinea and Ivory Coast [44], and one was conducted in Sierra Leone, Liberia, and Guinea [45] (Table 1).

The study designs had 18 cohort studies [17, 19, 35–38, 43, 47–56, 60]; 10 cross-sectional studies [14, 16, 34, 39, 40, 44–46, 59], and [61]; five case-control studies [31–33, 41], and [58]. The studies were classified as hospital-based or community-based depending on their recruitment settings. Most hospital-based studies were conducted in Nigeria and Sierra Leone, while several of the cross-sectional studies from Guinea and Liberia were community-based. Sample sizes across the studies varied widely, ranging from as small as 5 participants [49] to 28,780 [59]. The populations studied included children, adults, and pregnant women, with reported mean or median ages ranging from 7.5 years [31] to 38.8 years [40]. Diagnostic methods used in the studies included RT-PCR, ELISA, IFAT (indirect immunofluorescence assay), and in some cases a combination of methods. RT-PCR was the most frequently used method, appearing in many of the retrospective hospital-based studies from Nigeria and Liberia. ELISA was also common, particularly in the cross-sectional studies from Guinea and Sierra Leone (Table 1). Attack rate (AR) was reported in 21 of the studies, with values ranging from 0.4% [36] to 100.0% in [49, 54], and [55], which were all very small studies with fewer than 50 participants (Table 1). A total of 22 studies reported CFRs, with values ranging from 2.64% [59] to 69.0% [47]. High CFRs were also observed in [19] (63.0%) [35], (61.0%), and [55] (57.0%), many of which were hospital-based and included vulnerable populations such as children or pregnant women (Table 1). Risk factors were assessed in eight studies: [14, 16, 39, 44, 48, 58, 60], and [61]. Reported risk factors included rodent contact, presence of rodent burrows or holes in houses, age, occupation, poor food storage practices, and proximity to bushland or refuse (Table 1).

Risk of bias and assessment of study quality

Risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Among the 35 included studies, 8 were rated as high (NOS score ≥ 7), 17 as moderate (score 5–6), and 10 as low (score ≤ 4). Common limitations included lack of representativeness, unclear outcome ascertainment, and absence of control for confounders (Suppl. 3). Due to the inclusion of > 10 studies in the meta-analysis of attack rate and case fatality rate, we assessed publication bias using funnel plots (Fig. 2) and Egger’s test (Suppl. 4). Egger’s test for funnel plot asymmetry did not show significant publication bias in studies reporting attack rates (t = 1.33, df = 19, p = 0.1984), however, it indicated significant publication bias in studies reporting case fatality rates (t = 4.55, df = 20, p = 0.0002).

Attack rate

Figure 3 shows the pooled attack rate of Lassa fever derived from 21 studies (n = 71,207) conducted between 1977 and 2025 across West Africa. The pooled estimate of the attack rate was 21.0% (95% CI: 18.0%−24.0%), suggesting that nearly one in four individuals in outbreak settings were affected. However, substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed (I² = 98.6%, p < 0.001), reflecting marked differences in study populations, methodologies, and diagnostic tools.

To explore potential sources of this heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were conducted and presented in Figs. 4, 5, 6 and 7. Figure 4 shows the pooled attack rates of Lassa fever stratified by country across West Africa. Studies from Guinea reported the highest pooled attack rates of 30% (95% CI: 8.0%−58.0%, p < 0.0001), followed by Sierra Leone with 23.0% (95% CI: 18.0−30.0%, p < 0.0001) and Nigeria with 16.0% (95% CI: 13.0%−20.0%, p < 0.0001). The observed differences likely reflect variations in outbreak magnitude, population exposure, and diagnostic capacity. Although subgroup differences by country were evident, heterogeneity remained extremely high across all groups (I² = 98.6%, p < 0.001), indicating that additional unmeasured factors contributed to variability in attack rate estimates.

In Fig. 5, studies using RT-PCR as a diagnostic method reported a pooled attack rate of 20.0% (95% CI: 16.0%−24.0%, p < 0.001), while those using ELISA had a slightly lower pooled estimate of 18.0% (95% CI: 7.0%−24.0%, p < 0.001). Studies employing indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFAT) reported pooled attack rates of 21.0% (95% CI: 17.0%−24.5%, p < 0.001). The test for diagnostic method subgroup differences was significant (χ² = 17.70, df = 4, p = 0.001), however, heterogeneity remained high (I² = 98.6%, p < 0.001), indicating other contributing factors such as study design or population characteristics. Figure 6 shows the attack rate by study design with retrospective-cohort studies having the highest pooled attack rate at 36.0% (95% CI: 27.0%−45.0%, p < 0.001), followed by cross-sectional at 20.0% (95% CI: 16.0%−26.0%, p < 0.001), and case-control studies (7.0%, 95% CI: 2.0%−13.0%, p < 0.001). The test for study design subgroup differences was significant (χ² = 28.29, df = 2, p < 0.001), however, heterogeneity remained high (I² = 98.6%, p < 0.001). Hospital-based studies showed a higher pooled attack rate of 24.0% (95% CI: 17.0%−32.0%, p < 0.001), whereas community-based studies had a lower pooled estimate of 22.0% (95% CI: 17.0%−27.0%, p < 0.001). This difference may reflect greater severity and diagnostic intensity in hospital environments. However, heterogeneity remained high in both settings (I² = 98.3% and 99.0%, respectively), warranting caution in interpretation (Fig. 7).

Case fatality rate

Figure 8 shows the pooled case fatality rate (CFR) of Lassa fever across 22 studies (n = 45,531) conducted in West Africa between 1977 and 2025. The pooled CFR was estimated at 27.0% (95% CI: 20.0%−35.0%), indicating a substantial burden of mortality among confirmed cases. This analysis also demonstrated extremely high heterogeneity (I² = 99.41%, p = 0.001), likely driven by variability in health system capacities, outbreak settings, and case severity.

Figure 9 shows the pooled case fatality rate of Lassa fever stratified by country across West Africa. Studies from Sierra Leone reported the highest pooled attack rates of 48% (95% CI: 23.0%−73.0%, p < 0.0001), followed by Liberia with 40.0% (95% CI: 34.0%−47.0%, p = 0.02) and Nigeria with 16.0% (95% CI: 11.0%−22.0%, p < 0.0001). These country-level variations highlight differences in case management, healthcare infrastructure, and surveillance systems. Although the pooled estimates varied by country, heterogeneity remained extremely high across all subgroups (I² = 99.4%, p = 0.03), indicating substantial between-study variability. The observed differences underscore the need for country-specific strategies to improve clinical outcomes and reduce Lassa fever mortality.

In Fig. 10, ELISA and RT-PCR-based studies reported a pooled CFR of 51.0% (95% CI: 18.0%−84.0%, p < 0.001), RT-PCR-based studies reported a pooled CFR of 23.0% (95% CI: 16.0%−30.0%, p = 0.001), while ELISA-based studies had pooled CFR at 21.0% (95% CI: 11.0%−33.0%, p = 0.13). Studies relying on mixed diagnostics showed the highest CFR estimates, consistent with possible selection of more severe cases. Heterogeneity remained significant high across all subgroups (I² >90%), except for ELISA (I² = 55.7%). Figure 11 shows the influence of study design on CFR. Retrospective-cohort studies showed the highest CFR at 31.0% (95% CI: 20.0%−44.0%, p = 0.001), followed by case-control studies (22.0%, 95% CI: 5.0%−45.0%, p < 0.001) and cross-sectional studies (7.0%, 95% CI: 0.0%−23.0%). Again, the wide confidence intervals and high heterogeneity (I² = 99.4%) suggest caution in making firm comparisons. The test for study design subgroup differences was significant (χ² = 26.56, df = 4, p < 0.001).

The study setting revealed that hospital-based studies reported a pooled CFR of 25.0% (95% CI: 16.0%−34.0%, p = 0.001), while community-based studies had CFR of 35.0% (95% CI: 9.0%−68.0%, p < 0.001). This reflects the clinical severity of hospitalized patients and the possible under-reporting of deaths in community settings. Furthermore, the heterogeneity within subgroups remained high (I² = 98.1% and 99.7%, respectively), reaffirming the influence of multiple uncontrolled variables (Fig. 12).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides a comprehensive synthesis of the epidemiology of Lassa fever in West Africa, highlighting a pooled attack rate of 21.0% and a case fatality rate (CFR) of 27.0%. These figures underscore the considerable public health threat posed by the disease, particularly in outbreak-prone areas with fragile health systems and limited diagnostic and treatment infrastructure. The attack rate suggests that, on average, one in five individuals in an at-risk population may contract Lassa fever during an outbreak, while the CFR points to the significant mortality associated with clinical cases, especially in the absence of timely diagnosis and effective clinical management. These findings align with previous regional analyses, including Kenmoe et al. [62], who reported CFRs nearing 30% in sub-Saharan Africa, and are supported by recent hospital-based studies showing high mortality among children and pregnant women [19, 57].

A central finding of this study is the high degree of heterogeneity across the included studies for both attack rates (I² = 98.6%) and CFRs (I² = 99.4%). This variability persisted even after subgroup analyses stratified by country, diagnostic method, study design, and setting, suggesting that numerous contextual and methodological factors contribute to the observed differences. Country-level analyses revealed that studies conducted in Guinea reported the highest attack rates (30.0%, 95% CI: 8.0%−58.0%), followed by Sierra Leone (23.0%, 95% CI: 18.0–30.0%) and Nigeria (16.0%, 95% CI: 13.0%−20.0%). In terms of CFRs, Sierra Leone (48.0%) and Liberia (40.0%) had substantially higher pooled estimates than Nigeria (16.0%). These disparities may reflect differences in outbreak magnitude, surveillance capacity, healthcare access, and diagnostic infrastructure. Notably, countries with more robust laboratory testing (RT-PCR) and earlier intervention capacity tended to report lower fatality estimates, highlighting the critical role of timely diagnosis and care [63–65].

The design of included studies significantly influenced the pooled estimates. Retrospective-cohort studies consistently reported higher attack rates (36.0%), and CFRs (31.0%) compared to cross-sectional (AR: 20.0%, CFR: 7.0%) and case-control studies (AR: 7.0%, CFR: 22.0%). This trend is consistent with previous observations that retrospective hospital-based records are more likely to capture severe and fatal cases, potentially inflating CFRs while underrepresenting mild or asymptomatic infections [66, 67]. Moreover, hospital-based studies showed higher pooled attack rates (24.0%) and CFRs (25.0%) than community-based studies (AR: 22.0%, CFR: 35.0%), reflecting both the intensity of clinical presentation and access to diagnostic resources. These findings underscore the need for balanced surveillance systems that include both facility-based and community-level data to accurately assess disease burden.

Diagnostic method was another important source of heterogeneity. RT-PCR-based studies yielded attack rates of 20.0% and CFRs of 23.0%, while ELISA-based studies had slightly lower attack rates (18.0%) but variable CFRs. Studies using mixed diagnostic techniques reported the highest CFRs, potentially due to the selection of severe cases or reliance on retrospective data. RT-PCR’s capacity for early detection enables earlier clinical intervention, potentially improving outcomes, whereas serological tests may capture infections at a later, more severe stage [63, 67]. These results highlight the importance of strengthening molecular diagnostic capacity across endemic regions.

Risk factor analysis revealed a pooled odds ratio of 1.29 (95% CI: 1.10–1.49), indicating that individuals exposed to certain risk conditions were 29% more likely to acquire Lassa fever. Subgroup analysis further revealed that environmental exposures such as poor housing, presence of rodent burrows, uncovered food storage, and proximity to refuse had the strongest association with infection (OR = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.73–2.76). This supports findings from studies in Guinea and Sierra Leone that identified poor infrastructure and high rodent density as significant drivers of transmission [14, 16, 58, 68]. Occupational exposures (healthcare workers, abattoir staff) were also notable, with a pooled OR of 1.29 (95% CI: 0.84–1.98), highlighting the risk posed by inadequate infection prevention and control (IPC) measures. Demographic factors such as age and gender showed weaker but notable associations (OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 0.93–1.37), suggesting that underlying vulnerability may also modulate infection risk. Importantly, environmental exposures were initially underemphasized in earlier discussions, but this analysis clearly establishes them as the most statistically and epidemiologically significant risk category.

Ecological and climatic factors are also critical to understanding Lassa fever dynamics. Climate change, deforestation, and unregulated urban expansion have altered rodent habitats and increased human-rodent contact, facilitating viral spillover into human populations [15, 26, 69]. Traditionally associated with rural regions, Lassa fever has increasingly been reported in peri-urban and urban settings, particularly in Nigeria and Sierra Leone [22, 26, 70]. This transition necessitates a rethinking of public health interventions, which must now extend beyond rural rodent control to encompass urban planning, waste management, and environmental sanitation.

The elevated CFRs observed in hospital-based paediatric and pregnant women (some exceeding 50%) are particularly alarming [19, 57]. These findings point to systemic deficiencies in early case detection, supportive care, and antiviral availability. Moreover, significant publication bias detected in CFR studies suggests that the literature may overrepresent severe cases, further complicating accurate estimation of population-level mortality. Expanding access to rapid diagnostics like RT-PCR, decentralizing laboratory services, and integrating Lassa fever surveillance into national reporting systems for febrile illnesses are essential steps to improve early detection and outcomes [63, 71].

Despite the zoonotic origin of Lassa fever, few studies have assessed environmental or animal reservoirs, representing a missed opportunity for integrated control. A One Health framework, acknowledging the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health is vital to preventing future outbreaks. Targeted interventions such as rodent-proofing homes, improving household food storage, and implementing community waste management can reduce rodent-human interaction [69]. Recent modeling studies have shown that landscape changes and rodent population dynamics are key predictors of Lassa virus spread, providing strong rationale for environmental interventions [15, 26].

Vaccine development is a critical area of future research, particularly studies addressing the acceptability, delivery mechanisms, and prioritization strategies for high-risk populations such as healthcare workers and residents of endemic areas. While several vaccine candidates are in development, their deployment will depend on community readiness, logistical planning, and cross-sectoral collaboration. Preliminary acceptability studies suggest that healthcare workers and rural populations are likely to adopt vaccines if provided with appropriate education and risk communication [60]. In summary, this review confirms that Lassa fever remains a complex and urgent public health challenge in West Africa. Our findings support the implementation of integrated, multi-level strategies that combine clinical care improvements, environmental hygiene, occupational safety, and robust surveillance systems. Such interventions must be context-sensitive, equity-focused, and grounded in One Health principles to effectively mitigate both current and emerging threats posed by Lassa fever.

Limitations of the study

Publication bias was evident in the case fatality rate analysis, suggesting possible overrepresentation of severe cases in the literature. The review was limited to English-language, peer-reviewed publications, potentially excluding relevant data from francophone countries and grey literature. This language restriction may have introduced geographic bias, particularly underrepresenting findings from French-speaking West African countries such as Côte d’Ivoire, Togo, Benin, and Burkina Faso, where valuable epidemiological data may exist but remain inaccessible due to translation constraints. Most included studies were retrospective-cohort and hospital-based, limiting generalizability to community settings and likely underestimating asymptomatic or mild cases. Additionally, risk factor analysis was based on a small number of studies with varying definitions and measurement approaches, introducing the potential for residual confounding.
