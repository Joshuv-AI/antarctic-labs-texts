Structural valve deterioration of a transcatheter aortic bioprosthesis at 4 years in a young adult: a case report
Authors: Lucy Ann McGuire, Paul Fox, Brídóg Nic Aodha Bhuí, Richard Sheahan, Brendan McAdam, Roman Komorovsky, Florian Genske, Stefano Nistri, Deepti Ranganthan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTranscatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is the recommended treatment for severe aortic stenosis (AS) in patients ≥70 years old or those deemed high surgical risk. While there is growing interest in the expansion if its use to younger, lower risk patients with AS and select groups of patients with bicuspid aortic valve (BAV), its use in these individuals remains controversial due to limited durability data and the exclusion of these groups from major randomized controlled trials (RCTs).Case summaryA 28-year-old woman with a history of congenital BAV and prior balloon valvuloplasty in infancy, underwent TAVI with a 27 mm SAPIEN-3 valve overseas, aged 24. At four years post-TAVI, she presented to hospital following an episode of presyncope and dyspnoea. ECG showed left bundle branch block (LBBB). Echocardiography demonstrated a well seated TAVI but severe restenosis with a maximum velocity of 5.5 m/s and a mean pressure gradient of 79 mmHg. Normal coronaries without ostial compromise and no significant aortopathy was observed on cardiac computed tomography. The patient underwent surgery with a modified Bentall procedure and implantation of a 23 mm St. Jude mechanical valve. Histology revealed degenerative valvular changes without hypoattenuated leaflet thickening. Her post-operative period was unremarkable.ConclusionThis case illustrates the potential risks of performing TAVI in a young patient with severely calcified BAV, including conduction disturbance, structural valve degeneration and avoidable intervention to a normal aorta. The paucity of long-term outcome data precludes the use of TAVI in young, low-risk BAV patients and recommended intervention for severe AS in these individuals remains SAVR.Keywords: Aortic stenosis, Bicuspid aortic valve, Transcatheter aortic valve implantation

Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has revolutionized the management of severe aortic stenosis (AS) in elderly and high surgical-risk patients and is an attractive, minimally invasive alternative for patients who would otherwise undergo surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR). While there is growing interest in the use of TAVI in younger, lower-risk individuals, and those with bicuspid aortic valves (BAVs), the ESC guideline recommendation for SAVR in the management of AS in this patient cohort is supported by robust safety and efficacy data from major randomized controlled trials (RCTs).1 BAV patients have been largely excluded from major RCTs evaluating TAVI, resulting in limited evidence to support its use in this population.2,3

Case report

A 28-year-old Caucasian female developed sudden dyspnoea and presyncope while disembarking an international flight. Emergency services recorded LBBB on ECG, prompting urgent hospital evaluation.

She was diagnosed with congenital BAV at birth and with non-calcific stenosis treated with balloon valvuloplasty at 6 months of age. Surveillance thereafter included serial transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) and routine cardiology outpatient clinic attendance. At 24 years, the patient was diagnosed with severe calcific stenosis after experiencing dyspnoea and exertional presyncope. Metabolic causes for accelerated calcification, including lipoprotein(a), were not investigated at the time, however serum calcium was normal. Intervention was therefore indicated, and she was offered mechanical SAVR or TAVI. A discussion regarding family planning was held, during which the patient explained she had no plans for future pregnancy, broadening options to include SAVR requiring life-long anticoagulation. Following counselling, the patient understood TAVI to be a standard minimally invasive alternative to surgery, with evidence demonstrating success in young, BAV stenosis patients. By report, a Ross procedure was not offered, and the patient was unaware of the increased risks of SAVR after TAVI.

She subsequently successfully underwent TAVI with a 27 mm SAPIEN-3 valve, complicated only by persistent post-procedural LBBB on ECG. At her three-year review, she remained asymptomatic with normal TAVI function on TTE (mean gradient 17 mmHg). At 4 years, she reported recurrent exertional symptoms, and having made plans for international travel, prolonged ambulatory Holter monitoring was initiated with TTE scheduled on her return.

On index presentation to our hospital from the airport, the patient was haemodynamically stable, 172 cm tall without marfanoid features and examination revealed a crescendo-decrescendo systolic murmur. Laboratory investigations (see Supplementary material online, Table S1) included serial troponins (normal) and NT-proBNP (mildly elevated for age 180 pg/mL). Lp(a) was not measured at this time. A chest x-ray showed a central aortic prosthesis (Figure 1A) and ECG confirmed LBBB (Figure 1B). TTE demonstrated a well-seated TAVI with no regurgitation and left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction was preserved with concentric LV hypertrophy, with relative wall thickness of 0.625. The calcified and thickened TAVI leaflets demonstrated significantly reduced motion. Severe stenosis was noted, with maximum velocity of 5.51 m/s, and maximum and mean pressure gradients of 121 and 79 mmHg respectively (Supplementary material online, Figure S1A and B). Other measurements included LV end-diastolic volume index of 76.82 mL/m2, LV end-diastolic diameter of 4.16 cm and elevated LV mass (193.2 g). Transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) confirmed restricted right and non-coronary cusps (Supplementary material online, Figure S2A and B) with aortic valve area of 0.4cm2 by short axis planimetry and normal ascending aorta dimensions.

Admission investigations including chest x-ray and ECG. (A) Posteroanterior chest x-ray performed on admission demonstrating a transcatheter aortic valve implantation, highlighted by a blue arrow. (B) ECG recorded on admission demonstrating a persistent left bundle branch block (LBBB).

CT coronary angiogram (CTCA) demonstrated normal coronaries without ostial compromise (Supplementary material online, Figure S3A and B) and the proximal ascending aorta diameter measured 32 mm. Retrospective cardiac CT out-ruled hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALTs) and confirmed increased LV mass (206 g). The Cardiothoracic Multidisciplinary Team recommended urgent inpatient surgical intervention. On rediscussing family planning, the patient confirmed she did not desire to have children. Although there was no family history of aortopathy or connective tissue disease, more extensive surgery was necessary due to TAVI positioning and prosthesis endothelialisation into the aortic root wall. The patient underwent a thorough consenting process and successfully underwent TAVI explant, mechanical SAVR insertion [23 mm St. Jude] and root replacement with a modified Bentall procedure followed by coronary buttons re-implantation. Histology of the explanted TAVI revealed degenerative valvular tissue changes without HALTs and calcific atheromatous degenerative changes in the aortic wall media. Despite persistent post-operative LBBB, the patient remained well, returning home safely following discharge from hospital.

Discussion

This case highlights the risks of TAVI in young patients with BAV, a population largely excluded from major RCTs. At the time of her TAVI procedure, the patient was unaware of this evidence gap suggesting that the risks, uncertainties and guideline limitations may not have been addressed during pre-procedure counselling.

While TAVI has been primarily reserved for elderly, high-surgical risk patients, there is growing interest in TAVI’s use in younger, lower risk cohorts2,3 The NOTION-2 trial4 published 4 years after our patient’s TAVI procedure, was the first large international trial directly comparing TAVI to SAVR in younger, lower-surgical risk patients (aged 60–75), with 27% of participants having BAV. At three years, a higher incidence of peri-procedural conduction disturbance and permanent pacemaker insertion (PPI) was observed in the TAVI cohort in cases of severely calcified BAV stenosis (20.4% vs. 7.8%).5 This patient developed early persistent LBBB post-procedurally, a complication carrying an increased risk of PPI,6 cardiovascular mortality and heart failure-related rehospitalization. In contrast, guideline-recommended SAVR for a young BAV patients carries a substantially lower risk of conduction disturbance (4% vs. 16%)7and lower risk of PPI.8,9

In young patients, durability is critical, making premature valve failure and requirement for re-intervention particularly concerning. One study reported a 25.6% cumulative haemodynamic valve deterioration (HVD) at 10 years, which increases reintervention risk fivefold.10 In contrast, the Ross procedure offers superior long-term durability11 with life expectancy comparable to a patient’s natural life expectancy,12 without the burden of anti-coagulation. Alternatively, SAVR remains the guideline recommended treatment since the time of the patient’s original procedure,13 though it necessitates lifelong anticoagulation, impacting family planning for females patients.

While altered haemodynamics in BAV contribute to valve degeneration, they rarely alone explain the extent of calcification seen in severe cases. While not investigated in this case, emerging evidence highlights metabolic factors—particularly elevated Lp(a)—as important drivers of the calcific process. A 2024 meta-analysis further strengthens this view by demonstrating that elevated Lp(a) levels are independently associated with accelerated haemodynamic progression of AS, underscoring a multifactorial pathogenesis beyond mere flow disturbance.14 Of note this patient had no premature atheroma on the CTCA.

This case stresses the necessity of evidence-based practice, multidisciplinary team decision-making and the importance of transparent, informed consent regarding the risks and benefits of interventions.

Conclusion

TAVI is an established first line treatment for severe AS in older or high operative-risk individuals but has not previously been,13 nor is it currently recommended by ESC1 for young patients with BAV stenosis. While there is reported recent success in expanding TAVI to include younger patients, including BAV cohorts,4 this case uniquely illustrates the potential complications of TAVI in this population. The paucity of long-term data therefore precludes its use in these patients. We advocate for cautious case selection when considering TAVI for this population.

The case also highlights that obtaining fully-informed consent is a physician’s ethical duty, ensuring patient autonomy in clinical decisions regarding their care. It underscores the need for transparency about available treatment options, including referral to specialist centres as needed. The ‘Heart Team’ approach is essential to prioritize patient autonomy, with treatment centreing around lifetime management principles.
