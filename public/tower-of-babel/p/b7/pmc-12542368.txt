Genome-wide association study and genomic selection for growth-related traits in Eastern oyster (Crassostrea virginica)
Authors: Rodrigo Marín-Nahuelpi, Constanza Urzúa-Encina, Amanda Xuereb, Martin Mallet, André Mallet, Eric Normandeau, Charles Babin, José M Yáñez, Louis Bernatchez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the genetic architecture of economically important traits is essential for the design and implementation of efficient breeding programs. Here, we analysed 3,653 Eastern oysters (Crassostrea virginica) from 62 crossing groups and 307 full-sib families, all genotyped with a 200 K SNP array, to (i) estimate heritability and genetic correlations for six growth-related traits, (ii) detect quantitative trait loci (QTL) and candidate genes, and (iii) compare the accuracy of pedigree- (PBLUP) versus genomic-based (GBLUP) predictions across marker densities, selection strategies (physical distance (PD) versus linkage disequilibrium (LD) and phenotype-based genotyping strategies. SNP-based Heritabilities ranged from 0.33 to 0.56, and genetic correlations among traits exceeded 0.61, indicating strong pleiotropy. GWAS revealed a polygenic architecture with a shared QTL on chromosome 2; the top SNP explained ≤ 1.1% of phenotypic variance. Except for the 0.5 K LD panel—where PBLUP was more accurate—and the 1 K LD panel—where both methods performed similarly—GBLUP outperformed PBLUP in all scenarios, with the greatest advantage observed for physically spaced (PD) panels. The highest accuracy (0.80) was achieved with the full 100 K SNP set. GBLUP models required panels containing ≥ 0.5 K PD (accuracy ≥ 0.55) or ≥ 2 K LD (accuracy ≥ 0.56) to significatively surpass PBLUP predictions. Training sets built from extreme phenotypes boosted accuracy in small to intermediate sample sizes (e.g., 0.74 at n = 1,500 with 5 K PD SNPs versus 0.53 under random sampling). These results provide novel insights into not only the genomic regions controlling growth in this species but also about the utility of PD-based SNP panels and balanced sampling designs for enhancing GS accuracy in C. virginica.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12100-z.Keywords: SNP, Heritability; genetic correlation, GWAS, Candidate genes, QTL

Introduction

Oysters are among the oldest cultured bivalves and are the leading mollusk species in aquaculture production globally [1]. The Eastern oyster (Crassostrea virginica), which is native to eastern North America and ranges from the Gulf of St. Lawrence in Canada to Venezuela, is highly valuable for aquaculture production because of its broad environmental adaptability and long shelf life [2]. In the last decade, the production of C. virginica has dramatically increased in its native range with the development of suspension culture gear [3]. In 2021, the annual production amounted to 134,469 equivalent tons, valued at 185 million USD, with the United States of America and Canada being the principal producers [4]. To support the growth of this developing industry, considerable efforts have been dedicated to establishing genetically improved populations, with a primary focus on enhancing traits related to growth and survival [5, 6]. In Canada, seeds produced from wild broodstock constitute the primary source of oysters for C. virginica cultivation, as there are no commercially available domesticated strains. As the demand for oysters increases, the adoption of advanced aquaculture techniques will become essential to sustain production, with genetic improvement playing a pivotal role in increasing productivity across aquaculture species [7].

Traditional selective breeding programs typically rely on the measurement of production traits, such as growth and pedigree information, to predict the genetic value of selection candidates via estimated breeding values (EBVs) [8]. Pedigree-based best linear unbiased predictor (PBLUP) [9] evaluations are well suited for traits that can be directly recorded in the selection candidates. However, the PBLUP approach has limitations in terms of prediction accuracy when the trait of interest is difficult or impossible to measure directly in the selection candidates, as is the case for several important traits in oysters, e.g., meat quality, disease resistance, and tissue weight. In such cases, the trait must be assessed via sib-testing, which may limit the rate of genetic progress achieved in each generation and increase the rate of inbreeding accumulation, as its ability to discriminate between full-sibs without individual phenotypes is limited. This often results in selection decisions at the family level, which may lead to the exclusion of genetically superior individuals from less-performing families and the repeated use of individuals from the same top families. While proper cross-design and family balancing can help mitigate inbreeding, the lack of within-family resolution in PBLUP may still promote the accumulation of relatedness over generations. Additionally, the PBLUP approach imposes constraints on the breeding design and rearing methods when animals cannot be assigned to a specific family (e.g., oyster breeding).

Genomic selection (GS) via genomic best linear unbiased prediction (GBLUP) [10] has emerged as an alternative and is now widely used in aquaculture species [11]. GS can be used to estimate the breeding values of individuals without pedigree information and achieve more accurate predictions through genomic-based estimated breeding values (GEBVs). GBLUP uses thousands of genetic markers to construct a genomic relationship matrix (GRM) that better reflects realized kinship among all the animals from the breeding population [12]. Moreover, genomic selection is particularly useful for exploiting within-family variability to increase the rates of genetic gain, reduce generation intervals and minimize inbreeding accumulation [13]. Genomic resources, including linkage maps [14–18], single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) arrays [19–21], and chromosome-level genome assemblies [20, 22–25], have become available for oyster genetic research. These resources have allowed exploration of the genomic basis and the potential of genomic selection for growth traits and disease resistance (i.e., against ostreid herpesvirus-1 (OsHV-1) and dermo disease (Perkinsus marinus) in different oyster species [17, 25–30].

The increase of prediction accuracy using GS over traditional PBLUP has been well established in different aquaculture species such as barramundi (Lates calcarifer) [31], Pacific white shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei) [32], salmonids [33–35], Nile tilapia [36] (reviewed by Song et al., 2023 and Yáñez et al., 2023) [11, 37], among others. These studies have demonstrated an increased prediction accuracy compared with traditional PBLUP approaches, ranging from 4 to 41%, depending on the species and trait analyzed. Similar advances have been demonstrated for mollusks species such as the Pacific oyster (Crassostrea gigas) [26, 38, 39], European flat oyster (Ostrea edulis) [27] and Portuguese oyster (Crassostrea angulata) [40].

Genomic approaches have the potential to accelerate genetic gains in oyster breeding by employing either marker-assisted selection (MAS) for traits controlled by a few large-effect loci or genomic selection for polygenic traits, i.e., those controlled by many small-effect loci [41]. When a few quantitative trait loci (QTL) explain a moderate to high proportion of the genetic or phenotypic variance for a particular trait, it might be feasible to implement MAS as a cost-effective strategy to select candidates. For example, MAS for a QTL explaining at least 13% of the phenotypic variance in resistance to OsHV-1 resulted in 47% greater survival rates than did pedigree selection in a Pacific oyster (C. gigas) population [42]. However, detecting a QTL does not necessarily imply that it explains a large proportion of the genetic (GVE) or phenotypic variance (PVE) of a trait. In some cases, QTLs show strong statistical associations but have only minor effects on the phenotype. For instance, in Pacific oyster, QTLs significantly associated with growth traits have been identified, yet they account for only a limited portion of the observed phenotypic variation [27, 43].

Very recently, medium-density (66 K) and high-density (200 K and 566 K) SNP arrays for Eastern oysters have been developed [44, 45]. To our knowledge, no studies have aimed to uncover the genetic basis of growth traits via high-density SNP information in C. virginica. The objectives of this study were to (i) investigate the genetic architecture and genetic parameters (heritability and genetic correlations) for growth-related traits; and (ii) evaluate and compare the accuracy of genomic prediction for growth under different scenarios, including pedigree- versus genomic-based models (PBLUP vs. GBLUP), varying SNP densities, SNP selection strategies, different population sizes and phenotyping strategies.

Materials and methods

Breeding program and life history

We used a total of 3653 eastern oysters (C. virginica) belonging to an F2 breeding nucleus established at L’Étang Ruisseau Bar, Ltd. (ERB) in Shippagan, New Brunswick, Canada. The ERB strain was founded from 464 oysters sampled from 11 bays, representing 6 genetic clusters across New Brunswick [45, 46] and bred in a series of interpopulation crosses. In winter 2018, 80 crossing groups were created (using a total of 341 males and 239 females) consisting of 6 to 8 animals per group: from 1 to 5 females and from 2 to 6 males. After fertilization, embryos were placed into rearing tanks (rockets). Larval development lasted approximately 2–3 weeks, after which settlement was induced on suitable substrate. Larvae were fed ad libitum with hatchery-cultured microalgae. Juveniles were harvested at a size of ~ 400 μm and reared in the hatchery until reaching 2–3 mm in length. At this point, juveniles were transferred to an outdoor nursery system using upwelling silos with ambient seawater and natural phytoplankton. Once individuals reached an average weight of 1 gram, animals from each crossing group were deployed to two evaluation sites—St. Simon North (47.751784°N, 64.807939°W) and St. Simon South (47.720525°N, 64.761832°W)—and placed into four bags, each representing a replicate of 25 animals. These suspended bags were deployed in high-productivity zones of the water column and maintained using standard cultivation practices. The bags were periodically flipped onto buoys every two weeks and left to desiccate for approximately 48 h. This exposure to sun and air effectively eliminated biofouling organisms and ectoparasites without harming the oysters. The evaluation sites were located in New Brunswick (Maritime Provinces), which is near the northern limit of the distribution range of C. virginica. In this region, the annual cycle of the oyster is dominated by winter, during which coastal bays can be frozen (−1.5 °C) to depths of 0.5 to 1 m for five months of the year (from December to May). During this period, oyster valve activity enters a quiescent phase, where filtration and valve opening almost completely cease. The animals used in this study were subjected to 3 growing seasons before phenotyping (2018, 2019 and 2020).

Pedigree assembly

The 3,653 individuals used in this work represents 62 crossing groups according to the pedigree assignment reported in a previous work [45]. The remaining crossing groups were not included due to the absence of surviving offspring. Briefly, parentage was reconstructed using 545 SNPs in Hardy‒Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) with minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.3 and filtered by Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) using the Sequoia v2.8.3 R package [47, 48]. This software uses the year of birth and sex as prior information to discriminate between generations. Reconstruction was performed in 62 different groups according to the outcrossing records of each F1 animal and the traceability of the outcrossing from which each available F2 animal originated. The assembled pedigree consisted of 307 full-sib families (with a median of 8 animals per family, mean = 11.7 ± 11.9, minimum = 1, and maximum = 77 animals per family), 109 maternal half-sib families, 94 paternal half-sib families. 60 animals were assigned only to one parent (3 to only dams and 57 only sires).

Trait definition

Phenotypes from 3653 individuals, Hereafter referred to as dataset 1 (DS1), were recorded for whole weight (WGT), length (LGT), width (WDT) and area (AR). WGT (g) was measured using a digital balance immediately before imaging. Morphometric traits (LGT, WDT, AR) were extracted from standardized digital photographs taken at a dedicated imaging station. This station included a high-resolution digital camera mounted vertically above a matte blue platform with a reference scale and uniform lighting to ensure image consistency. Oysters were placed on the platform, and photographs were processed using an automated macro developed in Fiji (ImageJ). LGT (cm) was defined as the maximum vertical dimension of the bounding rectangle enclosing each oyster, while WDT (cm) corresponded to the minimum horizontal dimension. AR (cm²) was computed as the projected two-dimensional surface area of the oyster, based on standardized dorsal-view photographs. The images were processed in Fiji (ImageJ) using an automated macro that extracted the segmented contour of the Shell. The individuals were sampled in November 2020, and muscle tissues were preserved in 95% ethanol. A total of 837 animals were selected as breeding candidates, leaving 2816 animals for which shell weight (SWT) (g) and muscle tissue weight (TWT) (g) were additionally measured, Hereafter referred to as dataset 2 (DS2). Both traits were measured via digital scales. Pearson’s correlations were estimated among all the traits. We evaluated the normality of the phenotypes through graphical analysis via histograms and quantile‒quantile (QQ) plots, as well as the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test implemented in R [48].

Genotyping

Genomic DNA was extracted from the muscle tissue via the NucleoMag Tissue Kit for DNA purification (Macherey-Nagel). DNA quality was checked via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and only samples that presented high-molecular-weight bands were retained for further genotyping. DNA concentrations were measured via an AccuClear® Ultra High Sensitivity dsDNA Quantitation Kit (Biotium). All animals were genotyped via the high-density 200 K SNP chip developed by Xuereb et al., (2023). Genotyping was carried out at the Génome Québec Centre d’expertise et de services (Montréal, QC, Canada) following standard protocols for the Axiom Affymetrix platform. Quality control (QC) analysis of the sample call rate, dishQC (DQC), and plate QC, followed by genotype calling, was performed via the “Best Practices Workflow” provided in the Axiom® Analysis Suite Software (Thermo Fisher, Affymetrix). After SNP calling, additional filtering was performed using PLINK v1.9 [49] to remove SNPs with call rates < 0.9, MAFs < 0.05 and SNPs departing from HWE following Bonferroni correction (p value = 0.05/No. of SNPs remaining after previous filters). After QC, the SNPs were cross-referenced with those showing a Mendelian inheritance error (MIE) rate < 0.01 according to previous results [45]. The resulting SNPs were used for downstream analyses. To visualize the population structure, a principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using PLINK v1.9.

Growth candidate genes

To identify candidate genes associated with each trait and all traits together, we performed a genome-wide gene-level analysis and meta-analysis via MAGMA (v1.10) [54]. Once we obtained the summary statistics from single-trait GWAS, MAGMA was used to test for associations between genetic variants and specific gene sets. MAGMA first maps SNPs to genes on the basis of their physical positions within or in the vicinity of genes according to the chromosome-level assembly of C. virginica (C_virginica-3.0: assembly Acc. No. GCF_002022765.2). For each trait, the SNP p values obtained from the GWAS analysis were subsequently used as input to identify joint signals from all variants in or near a gene while accounting for linkage disequilibrium between variants. The result is a gene-level p value, which provides a measure of the association between each gene and the trait, taking into account the combined effect of all mapped SNPs. Finally, we performed a gene-based meta-analysis by using the “--meta” option and the “correlations” modifier to account for overlapping samples, which requires the estimated genetic correlations among traits (SNP effect (β) Pearson’s correlations) as input. The gene-based meta-analysis implemented in MAGMA uses a multiple linear PC regression model and computes p values for each gene via an F test. We considered SNPs to show genome-wide significant associations if their p value was lower than a threshold value calculated using a Bonferroni correction (0.05/No. of SNPs remaining after quality control).

Genomic prediction analyses

Owing to the high genetic correlation between the traits evaluated, we assessed the genomic prediction accuracy for only the whole weight by using two different approaches: pedigree best linear unbiased prediction (PBLUP) and genomic best linear unbiased prediction (GBLUP). PBLUP estimates breeding values using phenotypes and pedigree information (no genotypes) [9], whereas GBLUP estimates breeding values using phenotypes and genomic information (no pedigrees) [10, 55]. The GBLUP method replaces the pedigree relationship matrix with the genomic relationship matrix , which describes the genetic relationships among animals using SNP genotyping [55].

The random and fixed effects fitted were the same in both approaches. The variance components were obtained using a restricted maximum likelihood method implemented in the AIREML module from the BLUPF90 statistical software [56]. This module is also implemented in the HIBLUP software, which we used along with a custom R script to perform both PBLUP and GBLUP analyses [57]. We compared the heritability estimated using PBLUP with that obtained using GREML in GCTA. AIREML was used to solve mixed linear models and calculate the estimated breeding values for both the PBLUP and GBLUP models.

Different SNP densities were also evaluated for the GBLUP model to further assess the balance between genomic prediction accuracy, and cost-effectiveness. We used two different SNP pruning approaches: (i) Based on Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) and (ii) based on Physical Distance (PD). We defined 8 different SNP densities, the whole dataset post quality control (100 K), followed by 50 K, 25 K, 10 K, 5 K, 2 K, 1 K and 0.5 K SNP. Each one of these was composed based on LD or by PD. We used these SNP densities to test how genomic prediction is affected by different densities and pruning, thus identifying an optimum balance of densities and accuracy. Therefore, genomic prediction scenarios were the 14 pruned scenarios, plus the whole data (100 K) and the PBLUP scenario. The LD pruning was done by selecting SNPs using the option --indep-pairwise of Plink v1.90 software [49] using the following parameters: a window size of 40 kb, a step of one SNP and a variable LD to achieve the desired SNP number for each scenario. The PD pruning aimed to ensure a uniform distribution based on physical distance. To ensure uniform genomic representation, SNPs density (i.e., 50 K SNP) were selected proportionally to chromosome size, according to their physical length (in base pairs) in the reference genome of C. virginica. Within each chromosome, SNPs were ordered by genomic position, and equidistant markers were selected to achieve an even spacing along the chromosome.

To evaluate the effect of training population size and sampling strategy on genomic prediction accuracy, we implemented two approaches: (i) random sampling, where subsets of 250, 500, 1000, 1500, 2000, 2500, 3000 were sampled randomly plus the full dataset (3653 individuals); and (ii) extreme phenotype sampling, in which individuals were selected from the tails of the phenotypic distribution (lowest and highest values) in equal proportions, with similar sample sizes of 250 to 3000 individuals.

For each combination of sample size and strategy, we conducted 10 replicates of 5-fold cross-validation. The animals selected for each fold were randomly separated into five validation sets. Predictions were made by masking the phenotypes of 20% of the animals and using the remaining 80% as a training group to estimate the breeding values of the masked-phenotyped individuals. Results

Descriptive statistics and genetic parameters

Summary statistics for all growth traits assessed in this study are shown in Table 1. The whole weight of the animals ranged considerably, with a minimum of 7.79 g and a maximum of 121.50 g. Length ranged from 3.49 cm to 9.68 cm. The area ranged from 6.79 cm² to 53.67 cm². The width had a minimum value of 2.53 cm and a maximum value of 7.51 cm. The Shell weight ranged from 5.48 g to 86.05 g. The tissue weight also highly varied, with a minimum of 0.76 g and a maximum of 20.88 g. The coefficient of variation (CV) was > 20% for WGT, AR, SWT and TWT, which coincided with deviations from normality based on the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K–S) test. The CVs for LGT and WDT were less than 15%, and these traits were found to be statistically normal by the K‒S test (Table 1). In contrast, the CVs for LGT and WDT were < 15%, and the K–S test did not reject the null hypothesis of normality for these traits (Table 1). Visual inspection via histograms and Q–Q plots showed approximately bell-shaped distributions for all traits, with most values falling within the expected theoretical quantiles (Figure S1). Slight positive skewness was observed for WGT, SWT, and TWT, as indicated by moderate deviations in the upper quantiles of their Q–Q plots. Nonetheless, these deviations were not severe and did not warrant data transformation, especially considering the robustness of the linear mixed models used in this study. Therefore, we considered the phenotypic distributions to be sufficiently normal for subsequent analyses.

Quality control and principal component analysis

A total of 144,570 SNPs presented a minimum call rate of 90%, 138,821 SNPs presented an MAF > 0.05, and 108,719 SNPs presented HWE (Bonferroni corrected). After filtering by the MIE rate, 100,342 SNPs remained available and were used in the downstream analyses. PCA revealed the presence of genetic structure in the population. The first three principal components explained 9.91%, 7.53%, and 6.86% of the total genomic variance, respectively. Figure S2 shows the distribution of individuals along PC1 and PC2 with individuals spreading along both axes, suggesting heterogeneous genomic backgrounds among the sampled individuals.

Genome-wide association studies

GWAS revealed one QTL associated with all six growth traits on chromosome 2 (GenBank Acc. No. CM008242.1). Three common SNPs were identified in the same genomic region among the four traits available for DS1, which explained 0.7–1.07% of the phenotypic variance (PVE) (Table 4). After Bonferroni correction, we found 3 SNPs associated with WGT, 3 with LGT, 5 with WDT and 5 with AR. We found a smaller number of SNPs associated with traits in DS2: 1 SNP associated with SWT and 2 SNPs associated with TWT (Fig. 1; Table 4). Strong correlations between SNP effects were detected among traits (Table 3). Q‒Q plots for each trait GWAS are shown in Figure S3. The Q‒Q plots indicate that most of the p values follow the expected distribution under the null hypothesis, except for a deviation in the right tail of the distribution, suggesting a low likelihood of false positives [60].

Genomic prediction with varying population size

The predictive accuracy of genomic selection improved consistently with increasing training population size across all methods (Fig. 3 – left panel). This trend was evident under both LD and PD SNP selection strategies. PD-based SNP selection yielded higher accuracies than LD-based selection across most training population sizes and SNP densities. This performance advantage was particularly noticeable at intermediate densities (e.g., 2 K to 25 K SNPs), suggesting that physical spacing of markers promotes more robust genomic predictions. Variability in predictive accuracy was higher at small population sizes and decreased substantially as sample size increased.

Genomic prediction using extreme phenotypes

The use of extreme phenotypes in the training population led to increased prediction accuracy across all SNP densities and selection strategies (Fig. 3 – right panel). At low sample sizes (250–1000 individuals), extreme phenotypic sampling consistently outperformed random sampling. For example, using 1 K PD and 2 K PD panels with n = 1000 yielded accuracies of 0.61 ± 0.11 and 0.68 ± 0.12, respectively, compared to 0.49 ± 0.12 and 0.59 ± 0.13 under random sampling. This improvement was particularly evident for PD panels at low and intermediate densities. For instance, at n = 1500, the 5 K PD panel achieved an accuracy of 0.74 ± 0.09 with extreme phenotypes, compared to 0.53 ± 0.10 under random sampling—a difference of over 0.20 in predictive accuracy which continued at higher SNP densities. In general, LD panels under extreme sampling achieved lower accuracies than their PD counterparts, although improvements over random sampling were also observed. Prediction variability was reduced at larger sample sizes and higher marker densities. Discussion

An increased growth rate is the main breeding objective of most aquaculture breeding programs [61]. Thus, characterizing the genetic architecture and assessing the accuracy of genomic prediction for growth traits is highly important for decision-making in selective breeding. This is the first work that leverages a high-density genomic tool (200 K SNP) to explore the genomic architecture of growth traits, identify candidate genes and perform a genomic prediction analysis in C. virginica.

In general, SNP heritability estimates (ranging from 0.33 to 0.56) for all measured traits suggest a substantial contribution of the additive genetic component to the phenotypic variance for growth traits, highlighting their suitability for inclusion in selective breeding programs with an anticipated positive response to selection. The SNP-based heritability was considerably greater than the pedigree-based heritability for WGT, LGT, WDT, and AR. This may be explained by the fact that the genomic relationship matrix (GRM) captures realized relationships more accurately than the pedigree-based relationship matrix. However, for SWT, this difference was only moderate, and for TWT, the pedigree-based heritability estimate (PBLUP) exceeded the genomic estimate. This result may be attributed to the exclusion of 837 pre-selected individuals from the GREML analysis of TWT and SWT, which reduced the sample size and potentially the additive genetic variance captured by the genomic relationship matrix. The reduced GREML heritability detected when using N = 2816 compared with N = 3653 for WGT, LGT, WDT, and AR (h2 = 0.33, 0.37, 0.38 and 0.38, respectively) supports this hypothesis. PBLUP models for TWT and SWT included information from a larger number of animals, including those related to the excluded individuals, thereby capturing greater familial genetic variance. Therefore, the observed discrepancy between PBLUP and GBLUP heritability estimates likely reflects differences in population composition and the amount of genetic variance accessible to each model, rather than an overestimation by the pedigree.

SNP-based heritabilities were higher and more accurate than previous reports of growth traits in C. virginica [62], C. gigas [63–67], C. angulata [68] and O. edulis [27], ranging from 0.32 to 0.54 depending on the trait and species. Similarly, heritabilities for growth traits have been estimated in other shellfish species, such as Mytilus chilensis (0.73 ± 0.37) [69] and Yesso scallop (Patinopecten yessoensis) (0.32 ± 0.08 for body weight and 0.85 ± 0.12 for shell height) [70]. In Zhikong scallop (Chlamys farreri), heritabilities for shell length (0.42 ± 0.07), shell height (0.47 ± 0.07), shell width (0.54 ± 0.11) and whole wet weight (0.28 ± 0.03) have been estimated via genome-wide SNPs [71].

Phenotypic and SNP-effect correlations

We found strong and positive phenotypic and SNP-effect correlations among all the assessed traits. The observed correlation between phenotypic and SNP effects suggests that a positive response to selection should be expected for all traits when selecting for only one of them. Consequently, tissue weight, which is of greatest importance to producers and consumers, is expected to respond favorably to selection for whole weight. Previous works on a black shell strain of C. gigas reported lower phenotypic correlations between whole weight and shell length (0.54) and between whole weight and shell width (0.55) [63], whereas other studies have shown that wet weight is highly correlated with shell height (0.78) and shell length (0.83) [26]. Several factors may explain the differences in the correlations between two traits in different studies, such as sample size, age or time of measurement, genotype‒environment interactions, genetic background, farming practices, measurement equipment and culture gear [3]. Previous works on C. virginica suggest that growth traits may be highly correlated in this species, as strong correlations have been shown between shell height and whole weight (0.92) and between shell height and tissue weight (0.82) [72], which are closer values to our correlation estimates (Table 2).

QTL detection

Compared with those of other aquaculture species, genome-wide association studies of C. virginica are scarce. Using a ddRAD-seq approach, only one recent study revealed significant QTL associated with survival under low salinity on both LG01 and LG09 [73]. In other species, such as the Pearl oyster (Pinctada fucata), growth-related SNPs have been discovered via GWAS, with almost 5 million SNPs genotyped in 60 individuals [74]. In the European flat oyster, Peñaloza et al., (2022) described a polygenic architecture where a few SNPs surpassed the genome-wide threshold on chromosomes 1 and 4 by using ∼4.5 K SNPs and 840 oysters. In Yesso scallop, 2 SNPs were detected as significantly associated with growth traits genome-wide by conducting a GWAS using ∼9 K SNPs [75]. In Pacific abalone (Haliotis discus hannai), 263 SNPs (distributed among unplaced scaffolds and 5 chromosomes) were found to be associated with 10 growth traits in 222 individuals and ∼1.5 million SNPs [76]. Additionally, several studies have been carried out in Pacific oysters (C. gigas) to map genomic regions associated with nutritional traits [77–79], Zn and Cu accumulation [80], and resistance to diseases such as herpesvirus [17] and Vibrio alginolyticus [81]. Several SNPs mapped to different scaffolds (not chromosomes) have been associated with growth traits in this species, suggesting a polygenic architecture of growth in this species [76, 82]. QTL have been previously found for several growth traits, such as the length/wet weight ratio and foot muscle/wet weight ratio, in Pacific abalone [76, 82].

Although the genetic architecture of growth-related phenotypes is usually polygenic, it can vary across species. For example, body weight in hybrid catfish is oligogenic [83], whereas in yellow catfish, the same traits have a polygenic architecture [84]. In addition, similar genomic architecture among different growth-related or body traits has also been shown in other species, such as Nile tilapia [85]. Growth traits in this C. virginica population exhibited a polygenic architecture, as only a few SNPs on chromosome 2 were statistically associated with the traits, each explaining no more than 1.1% of the phenotypic variance, making GS more suitable than MAS. Nearby SNPs (located within ± 500 kb) showed varying association significance and LD with the top SNP up to r2 > 0.75 (Figure S4). The presence of nearby but non-associated SNPs and the varying LD values suggests that the LD block is not uniformly strong across the entire region, possibly due to historical (recent) recombination events and is consistent with the observed LD decay in the F2 cohort [45].

In a European flat oyster (O. edulis) population, the SNP showing the strongest association with total weight explained 3% of the phenotypic variance [27]. In Portuguese oyster (C. angulata), the PVE of SNP associated with Shell Height, length and tissue weight at chromosome-level varied from 14.13% to 18.56% [40]. In a Pacific oyster (C. gigas) population, the PVE of a SNP commonly associated with growth traits ranged from 8.09 to 10.81% [86]. In a different C. gigas population, it was described that no loci exceeded 2% PVE for growth traits [26]. This variability suggests that PVE is highly dependent on population-specific factors such as allele frequency spectrum, LD structure, and genetic background. Additionally, differences in sample size, environmental variation, and SNP panel design (e.g., density, uniformity) may also influence the detection power and magnitude of PVE estimates.

The ability to detect a QTL may be influenced by sample size, SNP density, genetic background, and diversity, among other factors. Compared with GWASs performed in other Shellfish species, our work has substantial advantages, including the utilization of more than 2,800 individuals with random-sampled phenotypes (not targeting extreme phenotypes) and genotyping with a high-density SNP Chip. Moreover, the population used in this work was founded by animals representing 6 genetic clusters [45, 46], which enhances the likelihood of detecting both common and rare genetic variants influencing growth. The inclusion of the first ten principal components as covariates in the GREML and GWAS models did not substantially affect the results, as heritability estimates remained unchanged (0.50 ± 0.03), correlation of SNP effects was 0.99 and only a minor reduction in the genomic inflation factor (λ = 0.924 to 0.918), indicating a limited impact of population structure on the analyses. Additionally, the GWAS Q‒Q plots shown in Figure S3 suggest a low likelihood of p value inflation and false positives. The combination of these factors contributes to the robustness of our findings.

Candidate genes for growth traits

A gene-based meta-analysis combines p-values from multiple SNPs within gene boundaries, thereby capturing cumulative genetic effects and mitigating the limitations of single-SNP association tests. Gene-based methods are particularly advantageous when dealing with polygenic traits, as they allow for biologically meaningful interpretation and improve the ability to detect modest signals that may not reach genome-wide significance individually. The gene-based meta-analysis performed here revealed two candidate genes for growth traits in the studied breeding population of Eastern oysters. The most significant gene was leucine-rich repeat serine/threonine-protein kinase 1-like (LRRK1), which is associated with astral microtubule growth and the regulation of mitotic spindle orientation, both of which are critical processes for cellular growth [87]. This gene has been shown to play an important role in the regulation of bone mass in human cells [88]. It also plays a key role in the regulation of endosomal trafficking of the epidermal growth factor receptor in interphase cells [87]. Oysters lack true dermal and epidermal tissues, and little is known about the function or expression patterns of LRRK1 in bivalves. However, LRRK1 also regulates autophagy, a highly conserved process that enables catabolic and degradative pathways [89]. Autophagy is one of the central induced responses to organisms living in suboptimal conditions, supporting cell survival by eliminating damaged and potentially harmful cellular structures and releasing products such as nutrients that can then be reused by the cell or exported for use by other cells [90]. Under conditions conducive to cell growth, high rates of biosynthesis with low rates of autophagy allow cellular resources to be headed toward growth with maximum efficiency. Under nutrient-limited conditions, cell growth inhibition and autophagy are correlated [90]. We hypothesize that variants in this gene may impact cell growth processes by limiting autophagy under favorable conditions and inducing autophagy under unfavorable conditions. However, further research is needed to test this hypothesis.

The second significant candidate gene was Chitotriosidase-1-like (CHIT1). Chitinase is a key enzyme in many organisms and functions as a defense against chitin-coated pathogens or as a digestive enzyme that hydrolyzes dietary chitin, an essential process for shell formation [86]. Interestingly, CHIT1 is highly expressed in the digestive glands and mantle of Hong Kong oysters (Crassostrea hongkongensis) [91] and in granulocytes, as well as in the inner and outer folds of mantle tissue in C. gigas [92]. Interestingly, an important source of nutrients in mollusks is zooplankton, which have exoskeletal chitin structures [93]. We hypothesize that variations in this gene may increase chitinase activity, feed efficiency and therefore growth. However, further research is needed to test this hypothesis.

Genomic prediction across SNP densities and selection strategies

The higher heritabilities estimated using the genomic relationship matrix (G) compared to the pedigree matrix (A) were generally associated with increased prediction accuracies under GBLUP. This is consistent with theoretical expectations, as the G matrix captures more detailed genetic relationships, contributing to improved partitioning of additive genetic variance. However, this pattern did not hold in all cases. For example, despite PBLUP showing a higher heritability estimate (0.29) than the 0.5 K LD (0.11) and 0.5 K PD (0.13) scenarios, its prediction accuracy (0.47) was lower than that of the 0.5 K PD panel (0.55) (Table 5). This suggests that beyond heritability, the genome-wide distribution and informativeness of selected SNPs strongly influence predictive performance, particularly at low densities. These findings highlight that while heritability is a key factor for prediction accuracy, it is not sufficient alone to ensure optimal genomic prediction—marker selection strategy and genomic coverage also play critical roles.

Overall, prediction accuracies were strongly influenced not only by SNP density but also by the method used to select the markers. The lowest average accuracy was observed for the 0.5 K GBLUP panel based on LD pruning (0.43 ± 0.07), followed by the PBLUP scenario (0.47 ± 0.08). Notably, at lower densities (e.g., 0.5 K to 5 K), SNP panels constructed using uniform physical distance (PD) outperformed those based on LD pruning. As SNP density increased, prediction accuracies improved for both LD- and PD-based panels, though the differences between selection strategies narrowed. The highest accuracy was obtained with the 100 K GBLUP panel (0.80 ± 0.07), followed closely by the 50 K PD and LD scenarios (0.79 ± 0.07 each).

In this study, as in many others [39, 59, 94, 95], we used the correlation between the observed phenotype and the predicted EBV as a measure of accuracy. However, for a less biased estimation, corrected phenotypes (adjusted for fixed effects and covariates) should ideally be used. To ensure comparability with previous studies, we chose to maintain the use of uncorrected observed phenotypes. Recent works have estimated the genomic prediction accuracy for growth traits in European flat oyster via an SNP panel of 4.5 K SNPs, achieving accuracies of nearly 0.83 for all traits [27]. In a C. gigas population, decreasing the SNP density from 17 K to 500 SNPs had a minimal impact on the genomic prediction accuracy (near 0.8) for a disease resistance trait [17]. It has also been reported that using 2 K SNPs for genomic selection usually achieves a similar accuracy to that obtained with a full dataset, at least for carp, Pacific oysters, and salmonids [96]. However, in our C. virginica population, this seems not to be the case. When animals in training and validation sets are highly related, increased genomic prediction accuracy with low SNP density is likely to occur [97]. The lower accuracies with reduced SNP densities obtained here may be due to the population structure of the studied population and species.

Our results are based on a breeding nucleus composed of 307 full-sib families, while other reports used from 23 to 80 full-sib families in C. gigas [26, 94]. Gutierrez et al. 2018 estimated the genomic prediction accuracy for growth traits with 6 K SNPs achieving a maximum accuracy of ~ 0.68, which is similar to our 0.68 accuracy achieved with the GBLUP 2 K PD scenario [26]. In the latter case, the genomic prediction accuracy of growth traits using GBLUP ranged from 0.67 to 0.79 using 10 K SNP genotyped with DArT-Seq, which is similar to the accuracies we estimated with our GBLUP 10 K LD (0.69) and PD (0.77). Recently, Wang et al. 2024 using whole-genome sequences (WGS) of 300 animals, detected accuracies for growth traits from 0.52 (shell height) to 0.84 (shell width) and concluded that 10 K SNP can accurately estimate the breeding value of growth traits in an autotetraploid C. gigas population [39].

The average accuracy of genomic prediction using PBLUP was slightly greater (0.46 ± 0.05) than that via GBLUP 0.5 K LD (0.43 ± 0.05) and comparable to that via GBLUP 1 K LD (0.48 ± 0.06). On the other hand, it was considerably lower when compared to GBLUP 0.5 K PD (0.55 ± 0.04) and GBLUP 1 K PD (0.56 ± 0.05). At low SNP densities, LD-pruned panels may fail to capture the full spectrum of genetic diversity due to uneven genomic coverage and the removal of potentially informative regions, limiting the ability of the GRM to accurately represent genetic relationships, reducing the advantage of GBLUP over PBLUP. In contrast, PD-based panels ensure a more uniform genome-wide distribution of SNPs, preserving the effectiveness of GBLUP even with fewer markers.

LD-pruned markers are selected by removing SNPs that are highly correlated with one another, which reduces redundancy. However, this pruning process depends heavily on the local LD structure and may lead to an uneven distribution of SNPs across the genome. In regions of high LD, a single SNP may capture a large haplotype block, whereas in regions with low LD or high recombination rates, pruning may result in large genomic gaps devoid of informative markers. This issue is particularly relevant in our population, where LD decays rapidly over short physical distances [45]. As a consequence, LD-pruned panels may fail to ensure adequate genome-wide coverage, especially at low and medium SNP densities, reducing their ability to capture variation linked to causal loci and potentially weakening the genomic relationship matrix. In contrast, physically spaced SNPs are selected independently of local LD patterns, providing more uniform genomic coverage. Although our study focuses on a single generation, the efficiency of genomic selection over time depends on the persistence of LD between markers and causal variants. The relatively stable LD structure observed between the F1 and F2 generations in our population [45] suggests that the predictive accuracies may hold across short-term selection cycles. Nevertheless, the uniformity of physically spaced panels offers greater robustness in terms of capacity to consistently capture genetic diversity and improve prediction accuracy—particularly when SNP density is limited.

Genomic prediction with varying population size

In this population, to achieve a median predictive accuracy of 50%, at least 2000 individuals combined with 2 K SNPs selected based on physical distance (PD) are required (Fig. 3 – left panel). Alternatively, a training population of 2500 individuals is sufficient when using lower-density PD panels (0.5–1 K SNPs). In contrast, reaching the same level of accuracy using SNPs selected based on LD demands a larger training set of approximately 3000 individuals, even when using 2 K SNP panels. These findings highlight the improved efficiency of physical distance-based SNP selection and provide practical guidance for designing cost-effective reference populations under different genotyping strategies. Importantly, no clear plateau in predictive accuracy was observed across the range of training population sizes evaluated. Even between 3000 and 3653 individuals, incremental improvements in accuracy were still detectable, suggesting that the optimal sample size threshold for performance saturation was not reached in this study.

Genomic prediction using extreme phenotypes

Although the use of extreme phenotypes led to consistently higher prediction accuracies across all SNP densities and training population sizes, caution is warranted when interpreting these results. In several scenarios—particularly with small to intermediate sample sizes (e.g., n = 250 to 2000)—accuracy values approached or even reached 1.0, which may reflect an overestimation due to overfitting. Because this sampling strategy excludes intermediate phenotypes, models trained exclusively on the extremes are more likely to capture spurious associations that do not generalize well to the full population. This effect is especially pronounced in LD-based panels, where local linkage may exacerbate the influence of a few informative markers. Notably, such inflated accuracies tend to stabilize only at larger sample sizes (≥ 3000), where the influence of sampling bias is reduced. While extreme phenotype sampling enhances genetic contrast and is particularly valuable for maximizing information content in small or moderately sized datasets, its application should be accompanied by strategies to mitigate bias—such as external validation using random samples. Furthermore, when comparing SNP selection strategies, PD-based panels outperformed LD-based panels under extreme sampling conditions, highlighting the advantage of uniform genomic coverage for capturing additive genetic variance in datasets with high phenotypic dispersion.

While the results presented here provide valuable insights into the genetic architecture and genomic prediction of growth traits, it is important to note that they are specific to the environmental conditions under which this breeding population was evaluated. These findings may not be directly extrapolable to other cultivation environments due to the potential for genotype-by-environment (G×E) interactions. In fact, G×E effects have been documented in the Pacific oyster (C. gigas) [98], where differential environmental responses among genotypes have impacted trait expression and selection outcomes. Therefore, future studies should explicitly investigate G×E interactions in C. virginica, as accounting for these effects could further improve the accuracy, consistency, and generalizability of genomic predictions in breeding programs.

Conclusion

This study delivers the first comprehensive assessment of the genomic architecture and potential for genomic selection of growth traits in Eastern oyster (Crassostrea virginica) using a 200 K SNP array. By analyzing 3,653 individuals from 62 crossing groups and 307 full-sib families, we show that growth traits exhibit moderate-to-high SNP-based heritability (0.33–0.56) and strong genetic correlations (> 0.61), confirming their suitability as breeding objectives. GWAS detected a shared QTL on chromosome 2, yet the top SNP explained no more than 1.1% of phenotypic variance, reinforcing the polygenic nature of growth. In predictive analyses, GBLUP outperformed pedigree-based PBLUP in every scenario except the 0.5 K LD panel (where PBLUP was superior) and the 1 K LD panel (comparable performance). Physically spaced SNP panels (PD) proved especially advantageous, achieving accuracies ≥ 0.55 with as few as 2 K SNPs. Training populations built from extreme phenotypes further boosted accuracy in small-to-intermediate sample sizes, though they require independent validation to avoid overfitting. Taken together, our results provide practical guidelines for optimizing GS in this C. virginica breeding program: (i) selecting for whole weight will indirectly select in favor of the muscle tissue weight; (ii) the SNP selection strategy of low density panels affects the genomic prediction (for a similar density, PD panels surpass accuracy of LD panels); (iii) at low sample size, genotype individuals from extreme phenotypes may enhance the genomic prediction accuracy. Collectively, these insights establish a robust genomic framework that will help farmers in the decision making in planning and executing selective breeding of Eastern oyster.

Data Availability Statement

The data analyzed in this study were collected from animals that were provided by and are part of the commercial breeding programs of L’Étang Ruisseau Bar Ltd. (Mallet oysters). The reconstructed pedigree, the phenotypes and the genotype has been deposited been deposited in the European Variation Archive (EVA) at EMBL-EBI underaccession number PRJEB96926 ([https://www.ebi.ac.uk/eva/?eva-study=PRJEB96926](https:/www.ebi.ac.uk/eva/?eva-study=PRJEB96926)).
