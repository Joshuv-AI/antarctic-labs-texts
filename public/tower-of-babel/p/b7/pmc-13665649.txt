Target Trial Emulation in Observational Research—Strengths, Limitations, and Methodological Considerations
Authors: Sebastian‐Edgar Baumeister, Ignacio Leiva‐Escobar, Ryan T Demmer, Panos N Papapanou, Thomas Kocher, Birte Holtfreter, Michael Nolde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCohort studies are vulnerable to confounding and time‐related biases. Recent advances emphasize the importance of emulating a hypothetical target trial to minimize several of these biases. The target trial framework is a critical tool for designing cohort studies. It requires explicit specification of protocol components: eligibility criteria, treatment strategies, outcomes, timing of follow‐up (time zero), and the causal contrast to be estimated. Critical to this framework is defining “time zero” when treatment assignment takes place and follow‐up begins, which prevents prevalent user and immortal time bias and ensures proper temporal sequencing of confounders, exposure, and outcome. By guiding design, data collection, and analysis, the target trial framework helps align observational studies with the principles of randomized trials, improving the validity and interpretability of their findings. While it cannot eliminate unmeasured confounding, this approach promotes transparent, bias‐conscious cohort design, minimizes time‐related bias, and supports more robust causal inference with observational data.Keywords: cohort study, epidemiology, longitudinal study, observational study, study design, target trial emulation

Introduction

Observational studies serve diverse purposes: they may be descriptive (e.g., estimating the prevalence or secular trends of periodontitis) or predictive (e.g., predicting the first occurrence of disease). However, a primary goal of many observational studies is to assess causal relationships [1]. Because investigators in observational settings do not control the assignment of the exposure, choosing an appropriate design is critical to establishing valid associations and minimizing the biases that frequently plague this research. In this tutorial, we focus on observational studies designed to estimate the effect of a treatment on disease incidence or progression. Specifically, we explore the target trial emulation (TTE) framework [2]. The use of observational studies that seek to explicitly emulate a hypothetical randomized trial has received much attention in medical and dental studies in recent years. The idea of posing causal questions in terms of explicit interventions and hypothetical randomized experiments has a long history [3, 4, 5].

Randomized trials are the gold standard for causal inference not only because they use randomization to balance groups, but because they ask precise questions defined by a rigorous protocol. In contrast, observational researchers often overemphasize the lack of randomization while neglecting the equally critical need for a well‐defined research question. This oversight frequently leads to flawed study designs [6]. The TTE framework addresses this by forcing researchers to explicitly specify the protocol of the hypothetical trial they wish to conduct. While TTE cannot eliminate unmeasured confounding—the inherent limitation of observational studies—it effectively prevents other major sources of error, such as immortal time bias and prevalent user bias [7]. These biases typically arise when researchers fail to synchronize the start of follow‐up (“time zero”) with the moment participants meet eligibility criteria and are assigned a treatment strategy.

We introduce the TTE framework as an overarching design principle for cohort studies and provide practical recommendations for its application. We begin by outlining the fundamental sources of systematic error in observational research, including confounding, selection bias, and time‐related bias. We then explain how the TTE approach mitigates these biases by applying the structural rigor of randomized trials to observational data, complemented by statistical methods to adjust for confounding and selection bias. We illustrate the application of TTE using a concrete case study, discuss key implementation challenges, and conclude with a balanced assessment of the framework's strengths and limitations. Methodological Challenges in Cohort Studies

To understand the value of the TTE framework, we must first recognize the common methodological pitfalls that produce erroneous findings in standard cohort studies. In the following sections, we describe these biases before outlining how TTE design principles, implementation steps, and analysis strategies systematically address them.

Confounding and Selection Bias

In cohort studies evaluating treatment effects, confounding and selection bias are major challenges. Consider the case study in this paper, which assesses whether thiazides—a common antihypertensive medication—increase the risk of dental caries. We compared users initiating thiazide monotherapy to those initiating non‐thiazide monotherapy [8]. Confounding occurs when a variable influences both the treatment assignment and the outcome, thereby creating a non‐causal association. For example, obesity is a cause of both antihypertensive choice and caries. Without adjustment, a comparison of caries incidence would be biased by the uneven distribution of obesity between groups. This can be visualized using a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) (Figure 1A), where confounding is represented by an open “backdoor path” from the outcome to the treatment [9]. DAGs are powerful tools for explicitly mapping causal assumptions and identifying the necessary variables to close these backdoor paths.

Confounding, mediation and selection bias illustrated using causal diagrams.

However, incorrect adjustment can induce bias. One form is overadjustment, which occurs when researchers adjust for a mediator on the causal path between treatment and outcome. For instance, xerostomia (dry mouth) is a mechanism by which thiazides may cause caries. Adjusting for xerostomia blocks this path, obscuring the total effect of the drug (Figure 1B). Another form of bias is collider bias. A collider is a variable influenced by two other variables (e.g., treatment and outcome). In a DAG, a path containing a collider is naturally blocked. However, conditioning on the collider—either by statistical adjustment or by selecting participants based on it—unblocks the path, creating a spurious association. For example, if thiazide use increases the risk of diabetes, and a high‐sugar diet also increases diabetes risk, then diabetes is a collider (Figure 1C). Adjusting for diabetes would induce a non‐causal association between thiazides and diet (and subsequently caries). Similarly, selecting participants based on a collider introduces selection bias. In our example, if we restricted the analysis only to patients who visit the dentist regularly, we might introduce bias. Thiazides cause dry mouth, which prompts dental visits; caries also prompts dental visits. Conditioning on dental visits (the collider) creates a false association between thiazides and caries even if none exists (Figure 1D).

Selecting the right confounders is therefore critical. The general principle is to adjust for covariates that close backdoor paths without blocking causal paths (mediators) or opening collider paths. Ideally, confounders should be measured temporally prior to the exposure. When the full causal structure is unknown, researchers can apply the disjunctive cause criterion: adjust for all pre‐exposure covariates that are causes of the treatment, the outcome, or both, while avoiding instrumental variables [10].

Time‐Related Bias: Immortal Time and Prevalent Users

A second major challenge involves the misalignment of time scales, leading to immortal time bias and prevalent user bias. Immortal time bias arises when there is a period of follow‐up during which the outcome cannot occur by design, yet this time is credited to the treatment group [11, 12]. For example, if patients are classified as “users” based on a prescription filled months after the study starts, the time between baseline and that prescription is “immortal” (Figure 2A). Because the patient had to survive event‐free to eventually fill the prescription, this period artificially lowers the event rate in the treated group, biasing results in favor of the treatment. Conversely, prevalent user bias occurs when a study includes individuals who have already been taking the medication for some time before baseline. This design inherently selects “survivors” of the early treatment period [13]. This relates to the depletion of susceptibles: high‐risk individuals—those susceptible to early side effects or adverse events—may have stopped treatment or experienced the outcome before the study began. The remaining prevalent users effectively represent a “healthier” sub‐population that tolerates the drug well. As shown in Figure 2B, counting only new caries after baseline excludes patients (D and E) who developed caries early in their treatment course. Furthermore, because treatment started in the past, baseline confounders are measured after the decision to treat has been made, making it difficult to adjust for the patient's true prognostic profile [14]. A classic example is the discrepancy in hormone replacement therapy studies: prevalent user designs suggested a protective effect on heart disease, which was later disproven by randomized trials and new‐user observational designs [15].

Depiction of immortal time and prevalent user bias.

Both biases are best avoided by using a new user design, which restricts analysis to individuals initiating the exposure. By aligning the start of follow‐up with the start of exposure (time zero), researchers ensure that all downstream events are captured and that “immortal” time is eliminated [11, 16]. This principle applies equally to medical treatments, behavioral interventions (e.g., smoking cessation), and policy changes [17, 18, 19, 20, 21].

Overview of the Target Trial Emulation Framework

Over the past two decades, methodologies for minimizing bias in observational research have advanced significantly. The TTE framework represents a culmination of these advances, applying the rigorous design principles of randomized trials to observational data. TTE involves designing a hypothetical randomized trial—the “target trial”—that would ideally answer the causal question, and then emulating its key components as closely as possible using observational data. Hernán and Robins [22] codified this process into two distinct steps: (1) specifying the protocol of the hypothetical target trial, and (2) explicitly emulating each protocol component (eligibility, treatment strategy, assignment, follow‐up, outcome, causal contrast, and analysis plan) using the available data. This structured approach forces researchers to define the causal question precisely before analysis begins. While observational data often prevent perfect emulation of every component (e.g., randomization is impossible), this process makes deviations explicit and manageable. To guide this process, researchers can utilize tools like CERBOT [23] and report their findings using the TARGET Statement [24]. In the following sections, we outline these key protocol elements, highlighting essential principles and illustrating their application in our case study.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligibility criteria define the study population and determine the generalizability of the results. In a randomized trial, criteria such as age, comorbidities, and diagnostic history must be met before randomization. TTE must mirror this: all eligibility criteria must be satisfied prior to “time zero” (baseline). Using information from the future (e.g., “patients who survived 6 months”) introduces selection and immortal time biases.

To mimic the active engagement seen in trials, TTE often requires criteria like regular prior healthcare contact [6]. Additionally, a “run‐in” period (e.g., 180 or 365 days prior to baseline) is typically used to exclude prevalent outcomes and exposures and to assess comorbidities and other covariates. If observational data lack specific variables found in a trial (e.g., specific lab values), researchers must modify the target trial protocol to reflect what is feasible, reporting these modifications transparently using a CONSORT‐style flow diagram.

Case Study: We aligned our eligibility criteria closely with a hypothetical trial (Table 2), including individuals ≥ 18 years with a hypertension diagnosis. We excluded those with prior antihypertensive use or recent caries diagnosis (past 180 days) to ensure a “new user” cohort free of the outcome at baseline.

Treatment Strategies

The protocol must specify exactly what participants are assigned to receive (drug, dose, frequency). Researchers must choose between emulating an initiation strategy (assessing the effect of starting treatment, regardless of adherence) or a sustained strategy (assessing the effect of adhering to treatment over time). Constraints of observational data mean that placebo controls and blinding cannot be emulated; thus, TTE resembles an open‐label pragmatic trial [25].

Case Study: We emulated an initiation strategy comparing: (1) initiation of thiazide monotherapy versus (2) initiation of non‐thiazide monotherapy. Both strategies allowed for any dose or frequency.

Treatment Assignment

In a target trial, randomization ensures exchangeability between groups. In an emulation, treatment assignment is determined by clinical practice. Therefore, we “assign” participants to the strategy consistent with their observed data at baseline. To mimic randomization, we must adjust for baseline covariates under the assumption of conditional exchangeability (no unmeasured confounding).

Case Study

We assigned participants based on their first prescription. We assumed measuring age, gender, ethnicity, region, prescription year, polypharmacy, xerostomia‐inducing drugs, and comorbidities was sufficient to control for confounding.

Outcomes

Outcomes must be pre‐specified. Because emulations rely on secondary data rather than adjudicated clinical measures, researchers typically use validated diagnostic codes [26]. When selecting codes, the heuristic is to prioritize specificity over sensitivity [27]. Low specificity (false positives) biases relative risks toward the null, whereas low sensitivity (false negatives) primarily reduces precision without introducing substantial bias.

Case Study

Our outcome was incident dental caries, operationalized by identifying specific diagnostic codes in dental records.

Follow‐Up

In a target trial, “time zero” is the synchronized moment when eligibility is met, treatment is assigned, and outcome counting begins. TTE must rigorously replicate this synchronization. Misalignment—such as starting follow‐up after treatment initiation—introduces immortal time bias [6]. Follow‐up ends at the event, death, or censoring. If censoring is “informative” (related to the outcome), it introduces selection bias, which may require statistical adjustment (e.g., inverse probability of censoring weighting).

Case Study

Time zero was the date of the first relevant prescription. We ensured eligibility was met at this date. Follow‐up continued until caries diagnosis, death, loss to follow‐up (defined by the last healthcare encounter), or the administrative end of study (5 years).

Causal Contrasts

Two contrasts are standard: the intention‐to‐treat (ITT) effect and the per‐protocol (PP) effect. The ITT effect (effect of assignment) is robust in randomized trials but requires baseline adjustment in TTE. The PP effect (effect of adherence) is harder to estimate; in both trials and TTE, it requires censoring and reweighting non‐adherent participants and adjustment for time‐varying confounding [9].

Case Study

We estimated the observational ITT effect: “The difference in 5‐year caries risk if all eligible adults initiated thiazide vs. non‐thiazide monotherapy.” This required adjustment only for baseline confounders.

Statistical Analysis

The choice of method depends on the causal contrast. For ITT analyses, baseline adjustment via multivariable regression or propensity score matching is often sufficient. However, for PP analyses involving time‐varying treatments or censoring, standard regression fails if confounders are affected by prior treatment. In these cases, g‐methods (like inverse probability weighting) are necessary to correctly adjust for time‐varying confounding.

Case Study

We used inverse probability of treatment weighting to adjust for baseline confounders and estimated the risk difference for the ITT contrast.

How Target Trial Emulation Avoids Time‐Related Bias

A fundamental requirement of TTE is the synchronization of eligibility, treatment assignment, and the start of follow‐up (time zero). Failure to align these elements introduces prevalent user and immortal time biases [7]. Defining time zero is particularly challenging when comparing treatment initiation to “no initiation” (non‐users). Because non‐users have no “index event” (like a prescription), there is no obvious start date. Comparing treated patients to all non‐users inadvertently credits the non‐users with immortal time. Risk‐set (incidence density) sampling: Matching each initiator at time t to an eligible non‐initiator at time t, assigning them the same start date [12].

How Target Trial Emulation Minimizes Confounding and Selection Bias

Addressing Confounding by Design: The Active Comparator

Since TTE lacks randomization, causal identification relies on the untestable assumption of conditional exchangeability (no unmeasured confounding). While DAGs help identify confounders, design choices are the first line of defense. The most effective strategy is the active comparator design [11, 13]. Comparing treated patients to non‐users often yields bias because non‐users differ in health‐seeking behavior. Comparing the target drug to another active drug prescribed for the same indication (e.g., thiazides vs. non‐thiazides for hypertension) ensures groups are more exchangeable regarding unmeasured health factors.

Statistical Adjustment: Inverse Probability Weighting

While design reduces confounding, statistical adjustment removes remaining imbalances. For complex scenarios involving time‐varying treatments or informative censoring, g‐methods like inverse probability weighting are essential [9].

Inverse probability of treatment weighting: Weights participants by the inverse probability of receiving their observed treatment. This creates a pseudo‐population where treatment is independent of measured confounders.

When combined, these weights allow for unbiased estimation provided the assumptions of no unmeasured confounding, positivity, and correct model specification are met.

Assessing Robustness: Bias Analysis

Because unmeasured confounding is always a risk, sensitivity analyses are crucial. Quantitative bias analysis—such as the E‐value—gauges how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be to explain away the observed effect [30]. Triangulation with negative controls (outcomes not plausibly affected by the treatment but sharing a similar bias structure) provides a falsification test; non‐null associations suggest residual bias. Additional design‐based probes include varying run‐in and washout periods, redefining outcomes, adjusting grace periods, and altering confounder sets [31].

Conclusions: Strengths, Limitations and Methodological Considerations

Observational research has long been criticized for producing inconsistent or misleading results. Historically, much of this failure was attributed to residual confounding. However, re‐analyses of major discrepancies between observational studies and randomized trials—such as those involving hormone replacement therapy or statins—reveal that the culprit was often not unmeasured confounding, but rather “researcher‐inflicted” design errors [15, 32]. Specifically, the common practice of identifying participants based on current treatment status creates cohorts of prevalent users who have “survived” early treatment effects. This leads to the depletion of susceptibles and immortal time bias, fundamentally skewing risk estimates.

The TTE framework offers a corrective lens. By forcing researchers to explicitly define and emulate a hypothetical randomized trial, the framework mandates the synchronization of eligibility, treatment assignment, and the start of follow‐up at a precise “time zero.” This rigorous alignment minimizes time‐related biases and ensures a clear temporal sequence where confounders are measured prior to intervention. Importantly, this logic extends far beyond pharmacoepidemiologic research. The TTE framework has been successfully applied to evaluate complex public health interventions, including physical activity, smoking cessation, housing policies, and political reforms [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. It compels researchers to move from searching for associations to defining precise causal questions with high clinical or policy relevance. Implementing a target trial emulation is rarely a linear process; it is an iterative dialog between the research question and the available data. Investigators must first specify the “ideal” target trial—the perfect experiment they would conduct with unlimited resources. They must then attempt to map this protocol to their observational dataset. This process often exposes critical gaps: the temporal granularity of records may be insufficient to define time zero, or data on key confounders may be missing.

This friction between the ideal and the realistic is a feature of the framework, not a bug. It necessitates deep collaboration between clinicians and epidemiologists. If the data cannot support the emulation of essential protocol components, the question must be modified, or the study abandoned. This “fail fast” mechanism prevents the generation of low‐quality evidence. Conversely, if emulation is feasible, the protocol should be registered prior to analysis to ensure transparency. Merely labeling a study as a ‘target trial’ does not validate it; validity comes from strict adherence to the principles of trial emulation [33].

Despite its structural rigor, the TTE framework is not a panacea. It addresses design‐based biases (selection and time), but the fundamental limitation of observational research remains: the lack of randomization. TTE cannot guarantee the absence of unmeasured confounding [33]. Sophisticated emulation techniques cannot compensate for poor‐quality data. The success of TTE relies heavily on the quality of the underlying data. While fields like cardiology or oncology benefit from massive repositories like the UK Biobank or the All of US Program. The data landscape in dentistry presents unique challenges. High‐quality, longitudinal dental data linked to systemic health records remain scarce.

To fully leverage the TTE framework, the dental research community must advocate for and construct integrated data infrastructures [34]. Future success depends on accessing longitudinal cohorts with frequent, time‐stamped re‐measurements of oral health status or integrated routine data that links dental claims (procedures) with medical claims (diagnoses/medications) and electronic health records (e.g., periodontal charts). Ideally, large administrative databases should be supplemented with primary data collection or “deep phenotyping” in sub‐populations to capture granular confounders often missing in billing codes, such as smoking status, specific oral hygiene behaviors, or socioeconomic determinants [34]. By improving data infrastructure and adopting rigorous design principles, dental researchers can move from simple association studies to robust causal inferences that shape clinical guidelines.

In sum, the target trial framework is a pragmatic, design‐based roadmap for observational causal inference. It elevates question formulation, tames time‐related biases, and improves interpretability, while openly acknowledging the limits of nonrandomized data. When paired with transparent protocols, appropriate causal methods, rigorous diagnostics, and fit‐for‐purpose data, TTE can yield evidence that complements randomized trials and better informs clinical practice and public policy.
