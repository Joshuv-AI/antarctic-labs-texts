Work-Related Quality of Life and Health Issues Among United States Anesthesiologists: Results of a National Survey
Authors: Alopi Patel, Jason Thiagaram, Jasmine Gamboa, Steven Staffa, Amy Vinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo assess the work-related quality of life (WRQoL) of anesthesiologists and various health issues that may have an impact on it.Patients and MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional, anonymous electronic survey of active American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) members to evaluate sex-specific health conditions and WRQoL. The WRQoL included validated domains of job satisfaction, stress at work, home-work interface, control at work, and working conditions, along with demographic and professional characteristics. Descriptive statistics and multivariable analyses were used to identify factors independently associated with lower WRQoL scores.ResultsThe survey was distributed to approximately 37,000 ASA members from September 2024 to December 2024, yielding 2292 responses. About 45.9% of respondents were women, slightly overrepresenting female anesthesiologists compared with the ASA population. Occupational and psychosocial exposures were common: 78.8% reported lifetime exposure to occupational hazards, 49.7% to harassment, and 74.6% to burnout, all of which significantly correlated with lower WRQoL (P<.001). Multivariable analysis identified occupational hazards, harassment, and burnout as independent predictors of lower WRQoL. Female anesthesiologists had higher odds of burnout and occupational hazard exposure impacting their quality of life, whereas older physicians reported lower burnout risk. Commonly endorsed workplace improvements included consistent schedules, reduced call burden, and flexible leave policies.ConclusionThis national survey represents the first large-scale assessment of WRQoL among US anesthesiologists. Findings highlight the multidimensional nature of professional well-being and underscore the importance of organizational culture, workload management, and targeted strategies to support high-risk subgroups. Addressing these factors may improve workforce sustainability.

Anesthesiologists are involved in high-stress health care environments with unpredictable hours, intense patient care responsibilities, and high physical, cognitive, and emotional demands of the profession contributing to higher rates of burnout.1 Among medical specialties, anesthesiologists are reported to experience burnout at higher rates, yet the underlying factors contributing to this phenomenon are not fully understood.2

Studies conducted in countries such as Brazil, France, and India have explored aspects of work-related quality of life (WRQoL) in anesthesiologists. There have been no large-scale investigations into the WRQoL for United States (US) anesthesiologists.3, 4, 5, 6 Given the structure of the US health care system and growing anesthesiologist shortage, it is essential to examine how various factors—such as personal health conditions, work environment, and institutional support—impact the well-being of the anesthesiologist workforce.7,8 Additionally, certain physiological issues can impact males and females uniquely in the workplace (eg, menstruation, menopause, benign prostate, hypertrophy). This study aimed to fill this gap by investigating the WRQoL as well as sex-specific health conditions among US anesthesiologists.

Methods

This cross-sectional, survey-based study was determined to be exempt by the institutional review board of Rutgers, Robert Wood Johnson Medical School (New Brunswick, NJ) in August 2024.

Participants

An email with the study’s objectives and a survey link was distributed by the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) to all members who had opted to receive emails and were actively practicing, residents/fellows, and retired anesthesiologists in the US. The email noted that participation was not mandatory, there were no incentives to participate, and all responses were anonymous. The initial survey was sent on September 21, 2024, with reminders sent on September 28, 2024, and October 5, 2024. A second reminder email was sent on November 10, 16, and 23, 2024. The survey link officially closed on December 15, 2024. Multiple responses to the survey were not allowed based on IP address as determined by the Qualtrics platform.

Survey Questionnaire

A 5-part survey was created by JT and AP based on sex-specific and overall health issues that can affect overall QoL and the ability to perform work responsibilities (WR). The 5-part survey included: (1) demographic characteristics, (2) overall health issues (2a) male-specific health issues and (2b) female-specific health issues, (3) practice management recommendations, (4) WRQoL for healthcare professionals survey, and (5) home life-related questions. The WRQoL is a multidimensional framework that evaluates an employee’s well-being, job satisfaction, and work–life balance, considering both psychological and organizational factors.9 The full survey is included in Appendix A (available online at http://www.mcpiqojournal.org).

The survey was input into the Qualtrics survey platform. Survey pretesting, including usability, functionality, and relevant questioning, was tested on a target group of 10 anesthesiologists reflective of the ASA population (eg, males, females, and early-late career). The testing phase data were erased before survey distribution to the ASA membership and were not included in the statistical analysis. Explicit consent was not required by the institutional review board for participation in this survey study. Questions were presented in a forced response format with branching logic based on the selection of sex. The authors acknowledge that sex is an assessment based on external genitalia and gender is an identity that is expressed by an individual.

Participants were asked to provide demographic characteristics (such as age, gender, sex, and ethnicity) as well as information regarding their primary practice environments. The survey included the full version of the WRQoL scale, which consists of 24 questions asked on a Likert scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree. The WRQoL scale is a validated instrument used to assess the psychosocial dimensions of work that contribute to overall well-being among professionals, including health care workers. It evaluates 6 domains—job and career satisfaction, working conditions, general well-being, stress at work (SAW), control at work (CAW), and home-work interface. It provides a comprehensive assessment of how occupational environments impact QoL.9 Higher scores indicate more favorable perceptions of occupational well-being across its 6 domains. Lower scores reflect poor WRQoL. The WRQoL scale has been internationally employed in studies of physician burnout, job satisfaction, and work–life balance.

Statistical Analyses

Nonresponses were handled using available-case analysis. Cases with missing covariates were excluded from multivariable models. Demographic characteristics comparisons between respondents and the ASA membership were performed to assess representativeness and potential non-response bias. Descriptive statistics of responses are presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables. One response was removed as a potential artifact (eg, reported having an orchiectomy and hysterectomy, childcare reported to be done by nurses). Denominators are presented for variables with missing data. Descriptive analyses were performed assessing conditions, QoL, and impact on the ability to perform WR. For analyses of WRQoL, lower WRQoL was defined as a total WRQoL score below 75, and average/higher WRQoL was defined as a score of 75 or higher. Comparisons of conditions between respondents with lower WRQoL versus average/higher WRQoL were performed using the χ2 test and Fisher’s exact test. Multivariable logistic regression was implemented to determine the independent association between conditions and lower WRQoL in all respondents and stratified by sex. Results from regression analyses are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and P values. A two-tailed P<.05 was considered statistically significant due to the exploratory nature of all analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata software (version 18.0, StataCorp LLC).

Results

Physician Characteristics

There were 2 waves of emails sent for the survey. For the first wave of emails, the average delivered emails over the 3 eblasts was 35,251, the number that opened the email was 20,822, and the number of recipient clicks to the survey was 1358 (click rate of 3.85%). For the second wave of emails, the average delivered emails were 33,865, the number that opened the email was 20,143, and the number of recipient clicks was 1456 (click rate of 4.3%). The final number of responses to the survey was 2292.

Participant demographic characteristics are displayed in Table 1. The ASA analytics and research team provided the investigators with basic demographic characteristic data for the membership contemporaneous with the study period for the purpose of establishing how representative the study cohort was of the whole. As of November 2024, there were 36,999 members on the member list. In the survey sample, 71.1% of respondents were full time practicing attending physicians and in the ASA population, 72.2% of members are actively practicing physicians. Age of respondents in the survey sample ranged from under 30 years to >75 years, and the distribution was similar to the ASA population. The analysis sample included 52.7% male respondents, and the ASA population includes 68.3% male members. In this study, occupational hazards refer to workplace exposures and conditions that may adversely affect the physical or mental health of anesthesiologists. These include chemical exposures (waste anesthetic gases, and antiseptics), physical hazards (ionizing radiation, prolonged standing, and sharps injuries), biological exposures (bloodborne pathogens), and psychosocial stressors (long and irregular hours, sleep deprivation, high cognitive load, and time pressure during critical events). Burnout is defined as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, characterized by 3 dimensions: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and a reduced sense of personal accomplishment.

Work-Related Quality of Life

The WRQoL was scored based on a validated assessment (Appendix B). There were 6 subfactors, including general well-being, home-work interface, job and career satisfaction, CAW, working conditions, SAW, and one overall WRQoL score. Higher values indicate a better WRQoL. Of all respondents, 49.7% (990/1992) reported a higher WRQoL, About 14.3% average (284/1992), and 36% (718/1992) lower WRQoL. Discussion

This was the first survey-based, cross-sectional study to assess sex-specific health issues and WRQoL in US anesthesiologists. Our findings show that all surveyed anesthesiologists report high rates of exposure to burnout, harassment, and occupational hazards, which is consistent with previously published literature.2,10,11 The key characteristics that drive lower levels of WRQoL in US anesthesiologists are SAW, CAW, and home-work interface. The prevalence of sex-specific conditions reported here should not be interpreted as evidence that the practice of anesthesiology alters the underlying biology of these disorders. We include them to characterize the health burden of this workforce alongside conditions more directly attributable to occupational exposure. Mechanisms by which the anesthesia work environment—waste gas exposure, shift work, sleep disruption, prolonged standing, and deferred voiding—may modulate symptom severity have been proposed in prior literature for other health care professions and merit further study in anesthesiologists. Although many of the health challenges identified in this survey are not exclusive to anesthesiology, raising awareness of their prevalence and workplace impact within the specialty remains critically important. This study was designed to systematically characterize these issues among anesthesiologists and to spark a broader conversation about occupational health.

Harassment

Previous studies on US anesthesiologists focused on either sexual or racial/ethnic harassment within the workplace but not general harassment exposure.12,13 This survey shows that 49.7% of anesthesiologists reported experiencing some form of workplace harassment (sexual, physical, mental, etc.), with 78.2% indicating their experience(s) affected their QoL and 54.5% indicating that their experience(s) affected their ability to perform WR. These rates exceed those reported in national and physician-specific surveys. Targeted organizational interventions, such as bystander training and implicit bias training, aimed at promoting respect and protecting individuals from mistreatment, can be considered.

Burnout

Burnout is a work-related psychological syndrome characterized by emotional exhaustion, low personal accomplishment, and depersonalization. Anesthesiologists appear particularly vulnerable to burnout. Our data indicate a lifetime prevalence of 74.2% of anesthesiologists reporting having experienced burnout at some point. Burnout was also significantly associated with lower WRQoL scores. A multivariate analysis of our data also found that female sex is associated with increased odds of burnout exposure affecting QoL. Our findings suggest that key contributors to lower WRQoL in anesthesiology include high stress, limited autonomy, and work–home conflict. Future research should evaluate interventions addressing these specific domains to improve well-being and outcomes. Occupational Hazards

Exposure to occupational hazards, including ergonomic, biological, physical, chemical, and psychological hazards, has long been studied for anesthesiologists.14,15 Data from our study shows that 78.8% of respondent anesthesiologists indicate that they have been exposed to occupational hazards over their career. These findings underscore the persistent occupational risks faced by anesthesiologists and highlight the need for targeted safety measures and institutional monitoring to mitigate cumulative exposure over time.

Male-Specific Health Concerns

Common health issues affecting males throughout their working years include cardiovascular, endocrine, and urological conditions. Mental health disorders, such as depression, remain under-recognized, often due to stigma and reluctance to seek care.16 Male respondents reported several conditions involving the prostate, testes, and penis. Only 17.9% of respondents reported experiencing any sex-specific health issue. Among these, prostate-related concerns were the most frequently cited, followed by cardiopulmonary conditions. Although cardiopulmonary diseases are not exclusive to males, the survey question may have been subject to variable interpretation by respondents. These findings underscore the need for more detailed and structured investigations into male-specific health conditions, particularly for urologic and reproductive health, mental health, and cardiopulmonary disease stratified by age.

Female-Specific Health Concerns

The reproductive-aged female anesthesiologist may experience conditions including menstruation, pregnancy, miscarriage, fertility treatments, lactation, postpartum mood disorders, and menopausal symptoms (Figure 2). Dysmenorrhea

The worldwide prevalence of dysmenorrhea ranges from 45-95% in reproductive-aged females, with up to 29% experiencing severe pain.17,18 In an international survey done by Deloitte, 40% of women reported experiencing high menstrual pain, with 40% working through it rather than taking leave or seeking accommodations.19 Our survey found that 59.9% of female respondents reported having experienced menstrual pain, with most indicating that it affected their QoL, but a minority indicated that it affected their ability to perform WR.

Contraceptive Use

Data from the National Center for Health Statistics approximates estimates that the US prevalence of contraceptive use is closer to 65.3% for females from 2017 to 2019.20 Studies indicate that female physicians are more likely to use contraception than the general population, regardless of socioeconomic status.21, 22, 23 One study found that physicians tend to favor intrauterine devices, diaphragms, and condoms and are less likely to undergo female sterilization.22 Our survey showed a higher prevalence of contraceptive use in US female anesthesiologists (78%) than in the general population. Further studies can evaluate how career progression may influence contraceptive use in anesthesiologists.

Pregnancy, Miscarriages, and Infertility

Multiple studies demonstrate that women physicians tend to delay childbearing, have fewer children, and experience higher rates of infertility compared to the general population.24,25 As of 2020, in the US, 15% of pregnancies result in fetal loss.26 Our survey showed 28.4% female respondents experienced at least one miscarriage, greater than the national percentage. No conclusion can be made as to why the rate of fetal loss was higher among anesthesiologists. According to the Kaiser Family Foundation, 13% of reproductive-aged women report that they or their partner have ever needed fertility services to help them become pregnant or prevent a miscarriage.27, 28, 29 Our study showed that 24.3% of female respondents reported having fertility treatments, which is consistent with the percentage of women physicians in general requiring fertility support.30 No conclusion can be made as to why a greater percentage of women physicians require fertility support; however, theories may include delayed childbearing due to prolonged medical training and high-stress working conditions.24 Fertility treatments affected the QoL for the majority of anesthesiologists and impacted their ability to perform WR. This is a key finding reporting the need to acknowledge that female anesthesiologists may require fertility treatments more than the general population and that it impacts their QoL. This can be an opportunity to destigmatize women who require fertility treatments and to encourage flexible workplace policies and support systems.

Postpartum Mood Disorders

According to the World Health Organization, 17.22% of women who have recently given birth experience a mental disorder.31 This number is higher for women without adequate support and those who return to work sooner.32 Alarmingly, there are no known studies evaluating the rates of postpartum mood disorder (PPMD) in physicians or anesthesiologists. This was the first known survey assessing the incidence of PPMD in anesthesiologists. About 16% of women anesthesiologists reported experiencing a PPMD, which was consistent with the rates reported by the WHO. Of the 16%, 91.1% reported it impacted their QoL, and 42% reported it impacted their ability to complete their WR. Most health care organizations do not offer paid time off for maternity leave, resulting in physicians returning to work sooner than recommended, with potential ramifications to their health that should be studied further.33

Lactation

The causes of early breastfeeding cessation rates vary, but studies have shown correlations between professional occupations and early breastfeeding cessation. In our survey, 63.8% of female respondents report a lifetime exposure to lactation, with 74.2% stating it impacted their QoL and 55.5% saying it impacted their ability to complete WR. The ASA Statement on Lactation Among Anesthesia Clinicians emphasizes the need for institutional policies that provide protected time, appropriate facilities, and staffing support to enable lactating anesthesia professionals to safely express milk without compromising patient care.34,35

Menopause

The aging female anesthesiologist will be faced with the impact of menopause on their physical and mental health. The average age of menopause is 51, with perimenopausal symptoms starting up to 10 years before that.36 Forty percent of practicing US anesthesiologists are over the age of 55, which reports that female anesthesiologists will experience perimenopause and menopause during their working years.37 In a 2019 questionnaire-based study of 409 women, around one-third of women reported moderate/severe difficulties coping at work because of menopausal symptoms.38 Our survey reported that 50% reported a history of menopause, with 70.4% reporting it impacted their QoL. Further studies are needed to identify the impact of menopause and perimenopause on female anesthesiologists and ways to provide workplace support for menopause symptoms.

Limitations

A major limitation in this survey is that there were no mandatory sex-specific questions for the male sex, as there was for the female sex. This was in part due to the generalization of what physiological conditions could affect females versus males. All females born with a uterus and ovaries will inevitably experience menstruation or menopause. Although there is no shared physiological experience like this in males. Furthermore, we received respondent feedback that there was no place to report voluntary termination of pregnancy or elective abortions. Finally, the terms male-specific or female-specific conditions can be variably interpreted. Many male respondents endorsed cardiopulmonary issues, chronic pain, depression/stress, and other conditions that are not specific to males.

Conclusion

To the authors’ knowledge, there is no data on the incidence of dysmenorrhea, contraception use, and PPMD in anesthesiologists. This survey was able to identify rates of major physiological conditions across a female anesthesiologist's lifespan. The authors hope that this study can create more discourse and research on health issues for both male and female anesthesiologists to better identify periods of a person’s life where workplace support may be required. Workforce sustainability and retention in anesthesiology are growing concerns amid ongoing staffing shortages. Understanding the health-related determinants of WRQoL is essential to inform strategies that support and retain clinicians.
