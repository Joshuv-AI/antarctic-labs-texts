Continuous intra-arterial versus noninvasive blood pressure monitoring for preventing hypotension during anesthesia induction in patients aged ≥ 75 years undergoing noncardiac surgery: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yihang Li, Shuang Liang, Yuqi Zhang, Ziyu Ye, Qulian Guo, Wangyuan Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypotension is common during the period after induction of general anesthesia and before surgical incision, particularly in older patients. The objective of this study was to compare the effectiveness of continuous intra-arterial pressure monitoring with that of noninvasive blood pressure monitoring in preventing hypotension during anesthesia induction in older patients (≥ 75 years) undergoing noncardiac surgery.MethodsIn this single-center, randomized controlled trial, 82 older patients who were scheduled for elective noncardiac surgery under general anesthesia were randomly assigned to the continuous intra-arterial pressure monitoring group or the noninvasive blood pressure monitoring group. The primary outcome was the area under the curve for mean arterial pressure (MAP) < 65 mmHg during the first 15 min after induction, based on invasive blood pressure measurements.ResultsThe area under the curve for mean arterial pressure < 65 mmHg was significantly lower in the continuous intra-arterial pressure monitoring group (median (interquartile range), 0 (0–6.09) mmHg∙min) than in the noninvasive group (3.67 (0–26.45) mmHg∙min; P = 0.008). The continuous intra-arterial pressure monitoring group also had a shorter duration of MAP < 65 mmHg (0 (0–1.42) vs. 0.87 (0–3.88) minutes; P = 0.010), received lower doses of propofol (16.67 (13.34–24.15) vs. 30 (24.17–40.83) mg; P = 0.011) and remifentanil (25.34 (16.67–40.00) vs. 34.18 (25.00–57.00) µg; P = 0.045), and received vasoactive drugs earlier (0.87 (0.67–1.00) vs. 2.33 (1.87–3.33) minutes; P < 0.001) and at higher doses in the continuous intra-arterial pressure monitoring group.ConclusionsCompared with noninvasive blood pressure monitoring, continuous intra‑arterial pressure monitoring initiated before induction was associated with reduced the incidence, severity, and duration of hypotension during anesthesia induction in older patients (≥ 75 years) undergoing noncardiac surgery.Trial registrationThis trial was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400088505) on August 20, 2024 (https://www.chictr.org.cn/).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-04123-0.

Background

Intraoperative hypotension is common, particularly during the period immediately after the induction of anesthesia. This condition is primarily attributable to the cardiovascular depressant and vasodilatory effects of anesthetic agents and the absence of surgical stimulation [1–3]. Intraoperative hypotension has been associated with postoperative complications, including acute kidney injury and myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery [4–7]. The risk of postoperative organ dysfunction in noncardiac surgery patients increases with progressively lower intraoperative arterial pressure. Postinduction hypotension is common in older patients, particularly those aged 75 years or older, who are at increased perioperative risk [8]. Previous studies have shown that advanced age is an established independent risk factor for PIH, with incidence rates reaching as high as 80% in frail older patients [1, 9, 10]. Therefore, the developing strategies to reduce the risk of hypotension during anesthesia induction in patients aged 75 years or older remains an important clinical objective.

Anesthesia induction-related hypotension is a common and potentially modifiable event associated with adverse outcomes. Prophylactic or responsive administration of vasopressors and crystalloid infusions can help maintain blood pressure, thereby ensuring organ perfusion and microcirculatory regulation [2]. Continuous blood pressure monitoring may facilitate earlier detection and treatment of hypotension than intermittent noninvasive monitoring. However, noninvasive blood pressure monitoring (NIBP) provides only intermittent measurements. Compared with NIBP monitoring, the AWAKE randomized trial demonstrated that continuous intra-arterial pressure (CIAP) monitoring reduced hypotension during anesthesia induction in patients aged ≥ 18 years undergoing noncardiac surgery [11].

For older patients aged ≥ 75 years who already require arterial catheterization for surgery, anesthesiologists may consider performing arterial line placement and initiating CIAP monitoring before anesthesia induction. This approach could provide real-time hemodynamic data to guide interventions, potentially reducing the incidence and severity of PIH and improving outcomes. Our study builds on the findings of the AWAKE randomized trial by focusing specifically on older patients, a population with reduced cardiovascular reserve and increased susceptibility to anesthetic-induced hypotension. We therefore hypothesized that CIAP monitoring initiated before anesthesia induction would reduce hypotension during induction compared with NIBP monitoring in older patients undergoing noncardiac surgery.

Methods

This single-center randomized controlled trial enrolled older patients at Xiangya Hospital, Central South University, from August 2024 to January 2025. The trial was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University, Registration No. 202407147) and registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400088505). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and reported following CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) [12]. All participants provided written informed consent.

Participants

We enrolled older patients who were scheduled for elective noncardiac surgery under general anesthesia and for whom radial arterial catheterization for continuous intra-arterial pressure monitoring during surgery was planned.

We excluded patients who had contraindications to radial artery catheterization or NIBP monitoring, patients who had clear indications requiring CIAP monitoring before anesthesia induction, patients who required non-radial artery cannulation, and patients whose inter-arm systolic blood pressure was > 20 mmHg. We also excluded patients who underwent emergency surgery, patients who had a non-sinus cardiac rhythm, patients who were classified as having an ASA physical status of V or VI, and patients who were obese (body mass index ≥ 35). Patients whose position changed within 15 min postinduction were excluded.

Randomization and blinding

Participants were allocated in a 1:1 ratio using a computer-generated randomization sequence. The allocation sequence was concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. The envelope was opened after arterial puncture and catheterization, and the research was conducted according to the groups the participants were in after confirming the correct allocation of the groups. Participants were blinded to group allocation. The attending anesthesiologist was not involved in the postoperative data collection. Researchers performing postoperative follow-up were blinded to the group assignment. During the data processing and analysis phase, the personnel responsible for these tasks remained blinded until the completion of data processing and analysis.

Study procedures

The medical records of older patients who were scheduled for elective surgery the next day were reviewed by the research team in the anesthesia surgery system, and the eligible patients were screened by a study coordinator who was responsible for randomization and grouping assignments and who was not involved in the perioperative care of patients or data collection. After the screening, the research team conducted preoperative visits and collected preoperative data, including age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification, sex, baseline risk factors, premedication, type of surgery, and baseline blood pressure, from those who met the inclusion criteria and were willing to participate in the study.

After entering the operating room, the patient was given local anesthesia with lidocaine, and a 22G arterial catheter was used to perform peripheral radial artery puncture and catheterization under ultrasound guidance. The arterial pressure waveform and numeric values in the CIAP monitoring group were displayed on the patient monitor. In the NIBP group, the monitor screen was configured to hide the continuous arterial pressure waveform and numeric values so that anesthesiologists could see only intermittent NIBP readings. Standard basic ASA monitoring was used for all patients, including EKG, pulse oximetry, and other standard monitoring methods in addition to blood pressure measurement. In the NIBP group, blood pressure was measured at least every 3 min, and the anesthesiologist could adjust the measurement interval as clinically indicated. Anesthesia was induced with the following drugs and according to the conventional induction regimen: 0.2–0.5 µg/kg sufentanil, 0.15–0.3 mg/kg etomidate, 0.6–0.8 mg/kg rocuronium, and 0.1–0.3 mg/kg remimazolam. Etomidate was chosen for its hemodynamic stability in older patients. Tracheal intubation was performed 3–5 min after the induction of anesthesia. For anesthesia maintenance, starting three minutes after tracheal intubation, propofol (2–6 mg/kg/h) and remifentanil (0.05–0.2 µg/kg/min) are administered by continuous infusion, combined with intermittent sevoflurane inhalation, to maintain the bispectral index (BIS) value between 40 and 60. Patients with hypotension (mean arterial pressure < 65 mmHg) were treated with vasoactive agents such as ephedrine and phenylephrine or with crystalloid infusion to maintain a mean arterial pressure (MAP) > 65 mmHg (see Additional file 1). When the patient developed hypotension, an appropriate vasoactive drug was selected on the basis of the patient’s heart rate. If the heart rate was less than 60 beats per minute, ephedrine was administered at a dosage of 3 mg or 6 mg. If the heart rate exceeded 60 beats per minute, phenylephrine was administered at a dosage of 50–100 µg. We recorded the MAP values based on the patients’ invasive blood pressure for 15 min starting from the administration of the first intravenous general anesthesia induction agent in both groups.

Data processing

Arterial pressure data were first processed in nonoverlapping 10-second windows. For each window, the MAP was calculated. Missing MAP values (due to signal loss or artifact rejection) were imputed using the average of the nearest valid MAP values from the preceding and subsequent 10-s windows. Artifacts were identified and excluded on the basis of the following predefined criteria: (1) a systolic arterial pressure > 300 mmHg or < 30 mmHg; (2) a systolic arterial pressure less than the diastolic arterial pressure plus 5 mmHg; (3) a diastolic arterial pressure < 10 mmHg or > 150 mmHg; and (4) transient fluctuations exceeding 20 mmHg from the preceding MAP that returned to near‑baseline within 10 s [11, 13].

To calculate the area below and above a specified MAP threshold, the threshold was subtracted from the mean MAP of each 10‑s window (see Additional file 2) [14, 15]. When the difference was positive (i.e., mean MAP > threshold), the positive value represented the area above the threshold. When the difference was negative (i.e., mean MAP < threshold), its absolute value represented the area below the threshold. Each difference was multiplied by 10 s and divided by 60 to convert the value to mmHg∙min. The resulting values were summed over each participant’s 15‑minute study period to obtain the total area below and above the threshold.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the area under the curve for MAP < 65 mmHg during the first 15 min after induction. The secondary outcomes were as follows (see Additional file 3): (1) areas under the MAP values of 60, 50 and 40 mmHg; (2) durations of MAP values less than 65, 60, 50, and 40 mmHg; (3) proportion of participants with MAP values below 65, 60, 50, and 40 mmHg for at least one continuous minute (%); (4) areas above the MAP values of 100, 110, 120, 130, and 140 mmHg; (5) use, dosage, and response time of vasoactive drugs (ephedrine, phenylephrine within 15 min after anesthesia induction); (6) cumulative crystalloid volume administered within 15 min after anesthesia induction; (7) total doses of induction and maintenance anesthetics within 15 min after anesthesia induction; (8) intensive care unit (ICU) admission and length of stay; (9) incidence of postoperative delirium within 3 days; (10) postoperative hospital stay; and (11) postoperative complications, readmission, and mortality within 30 days.

Sample size estimation

A pilot feasibility study of 10 patients in our center indicated a standard deviation (SD) of 9.6 mmHg∙min for the primary outcome (see Additional file 4). On the basis of previous studies reporting a standard deviation of 12 mmHg∙min for the same parameter, with an alpha of 0.05 and a power of 90%, and allowing for a 20% dropout rate, a sample size of 41 participants per group was calculated [11]. Therefore, a total of 82 participants were included in the study.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data are presented as absolute values and percentages, whereas continuous data are presented as the means ± standard deviations or medians (interquartile ranges, IQRs). We considered a P value < 0.05 to indicate statistical significance. The primary endpoint was analyzed using the two-sample bilateral Wilcoxon rank sum test. The corresponding nonparametric 95% confidence intervals were calculated. A similar analysis was performed for continuous secondary endpoints. The secondary endpoints of the categorical data were analyzed via Pearson’s chi-square test. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0.

Results

From August 21, 2024, to January 23, 2025, 276 patients were screened for eligibility. A total of 118 participants were excluded from the study because they met the exclusion criteria: 38 participants declined to participate in this research, 11 participants had already participated in another study, 10 participants had technical problems with arterial pressure monitoring, 12 participants had difficulty inserting an arterial catheter, 3 participants had new-onset cardiac arrhythmia, and 2 participants were excluded for other reasons. Eighty-two subjects were available for the final analysis, among which 41 (50%) were assigned to the CIAP and 41 (50%) were assigned to the NIBP (Fig. 1). The baseline clinical features were well balanced between the two groups, and the ASA classification of all participants was Grade III (Table 1). No arterial injuries, hematomas, or access-related complications were observed in either group.

The median (IQR) area under the curve (AUC) for MAP < 65 mmHg was 0 (0–6.09) mmHg∙min in participants who were assigned to CIAP monitoring, whereas the median (IQR) in participants assigned to NIBP monitoring was 3.67 (0–26.45) mmHg∙min (P = 0.008; [95% CI, 5.94 to 18.36] mmHg∙min). Compared with the NIBP group, the CIAP group demonstrated a significantly lower incidence of hypotension every minute after induction, and the standard deviation (SD) of the NIBP group was greater in most cases (Fig. 2). Compared with those in the NIBP group, the participants assigned to the CIAP monitoring group also had smaller AUCs of 60, 50, and 40 mmHg (Table 2, Additional file 5).

The participants assigned to the CIAP monitoring group had a median (IQR) of 0 (0–1.42) minutes of MAP less than 65 mmHg, whereas that in the NIBP group was 0.87 (0–3.88) minutes (P = 0.010). The participants assigned to CIAP monitoring also had shorter durations at MAP values below 60, 50, and 40 mmHg (Table 2, Additional file 6).

Among those assigned to the CIAP monitoring group, 13 patients experienced a MAP below 65 mmHg for at least one minute compared with 22 patients in the other group (P = 0.044). Within 15 min after anesthesia induction, all patients successfully completed tracheal intubation, but surgery had not yet begun. Compared with the NIBP monitoring group, the CIAP monitoring group received lower doses of propofol (P = 0.011) and remifentanil (P = 0.045) within 15 min after anesthesia induction. In addition, compared with the NIBP group, the CIAP group had a shorter time from hypotension detection to vasoactive drug administration (P < 0.001) and received higher doses of ephedrine (P = 0.001) and phenylephrine (P = 0.003) (Table 2). In the NIBP group, the measurement frequency increased after vasopressor administration, as clinically indicated. No significant differences were observed in postoperative outcomes (Table 3).

Discussion

In this randomized trial of older patients (≥ 75 years) undergoing noncardiac surgery, continuous intra-arterial pressure monitoring reduced the occurrence, severity, and duration of postinduction hypotension compared with noninvasive blood pressure monitoring.

Older patients are more likely to have frailty, reduced vascular compliance, arteriosclerosis, and diminished cardiovascular reserve, which make them highly susceptible to the vasodilatory and myocardial depressant effects of anesthetic agents [10, 16]. In addition, the use of multiple medications in older patients may increase the risk of hypotension during anesthesia induction through drug interactions. For example, the synergistic effect of sedative drugs, opioids, and antihypertensive drugs may significantly exacerbate hemodynamic fluctuations and may even lead to different degrees of perioperative complications. Unlike the AWAKE randomized trial, this study focused on older patients (aged ≥ 75 years) because the anesthesia induction risk and perioperative complications in older patients increase significantly with age, and hemodynamic fluctuations during anesthesia induction are relatively elevated. These findings are broadly consistent with the AWAKE trial and suggest that preinduction arterial monitoring may also be beneficial in older surgical patients. This research may provide guidance and evidence supporting improvements in hemodynamic fluctuations during anesthesia induction in older patients.

The monitoring method does not affect blood pressure; it affects detection and clinician response. The first and most obvious reason is that continuous intra-arterial monitoring may allow earlier recognition of rapid hemodynamic changes and earlier therapeutic intervention than intermittent NIBP measurements. This temporal advantage of continuous intra-arterial pressure monitoring likely explains the shorter time to vasopressor administration and reduced cumulative hypotension duration observed in our study. The second possible reason is that continuous intra-arterial pressure monitoring may enable anesthesiologists to dynamically adjust the dosage of maintenance anesthetic drugs on the basis of the real-time hemodynamic feedback of older patients. This timeliness and accuracy may alleviate the inhibitory effects of propofol and remifentanil on the cardiovascular system, thereby reducing the occurrence of hypotension in older patients within 15 min after anesthesia induction. It remains unclear whether the former or the latter mechanism played a greater role in the primary outcome observed in our study. This ambiguity introduces a potential confounder that must be considered when interpreting the present results.

The baseline features of the two groups were generally similar; thus, the observed effect of continuous intra-arterial pressure monitoring in reducing hypotension was not due to confounders of the baseline health status of patients in either group. Hypotension during anesthesia induction is caused mainly by circulatory inhibition due to anesthesia drugs and a lack of surgical stimulation [17, 18]. Considering that hypotension could be detected more quickly, the number of vasoactive drugs administered for CIAP monitoring was nearly twice that administered for NIBP monitoring, and the response time to vasoactive drugs was significantly shorter than that in the NIBP group. In the AWAKE randomized trial, CIAP monitoring was associated with earlier administration and higher doses of vasoactive drugs. On the basis of our center’s institutional practice regarding vasoactive agents, ephedrine and phenylephrine were selected for use in this study. A recent study by Vokuhl and colleagues demonstrated that continuous infusion of norepinephrine during the induction of general anesthesia improved blood pressure stability in high-risk patients undergoing noncardiac surgery, indicating that norepinephrine can also be considered a viable option as a vasoactive agent [19]. However, whether these differences translate to improved patient outcomes remains uncertain and requires confirmation in prospective studies. We concluded that CIAP monitoring did not lead to overtreatment or shallow induction of anesthesia without a corresponding increase in hypertension (areas above the MAP values did not differ). A previous study revealed that longer durations of hypotensive episodes are associated with further increased perioperative morbidity rates, demonstrating that intraoperative hypotensive episodes of as little as 1–5 min can be associated with unfavorable outcomes, underscoring the clinical importance of our findings [20].

There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of postoperative complications or long-term outcomes. This discrepancy may be attributed to the modest reduction in the area under the curve, the relatively short duration of hypotension, the limited sample size, the short postoperative follow-up period, and the short monitoring window for postoperative delirium observed in the CIAP group, despite the statistically significant difference in the primary outcome. Whether this significant difference translates into a clinically meaningful improvement in organ perfusion or a reduction in complications remains to be determined in larger-scale trials. Therefore, further studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to investigate postoperative complications and evaluate long-term benefits.

Our findings regarding hypotension, crystalloid administration, and the use of vasoactive drugs within 15 min after anesthesia induction are consistent with those of the AWAKE trial [11]. The primary outcome was set to occur within 15 min after the start of anesthesia induction in the present study, as this early period is commonly associated with a higher risk of severe hypotension and hemodynamic instability [1, 21]. There is currently no widely accepted definition of hypotension exists. It is typically defined either by an absolute blood pressure threshold (MAP < 65 mmHg) or by a relative decrease from baseline (e.g., a 20% drop in systolic blood pressure) [22, 23]. In this study, we selected the absolute threshold of a MAP < 65 mmHg to define hypotension for the following reasons. Evidence suggests that a MAP less than 65 mmHg represents the critical limit for autoregulation of renal and cerebral blood flow. Moreover, retrospective studies have shown that this absolute threshold improves the prediction of myocardial injury and acute kidney injury compared with a relative threshold [22].

However, the potential drawbacks of preinduction arterial line placement center mainly on patient discomfort and delays in the procedure. In recent years, continuous noninvasive blood pressure monitoring has begun to be applied in clinical practice, which may represent a future trend in clinical blood pressure monitoring [24, 25].

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center trial with a modest sample size, which may limit its generalizability. Therefore, we did not conduct subgroup analyses on the basis of the comorbidities of the subjects, as the difference was not significant. Second, the lack of a strictly protocolized algorithm for vasopressor administration and fluid therapy may have introduced clinician-dependent variability, which could affect internal validity. Third, the association of etomidate with adrenocortical suppression may limit its adoption in broader clinical applications. Consequently, the generalizability of the study findings may be limited. Finally, the impact of the frequency of noninvasive blood pressure measurements on the results was not assessed in the NIBP group. The difference in measurement frequency may have affected the time of detecting hypotension, potentially leading to an underestimation of the advantages of CIAP monitoring.

Conclusions

In conclusion, compared with noninvasive blood pressure monitoring, continuous intra-arterial pressure monitoring initiated before anesthesia induction was associated with reduced the incidence, severity, and duration of hypotension during anesthesia induction in older patients (≥ 75 years) undergoing noncardiac surgery.
