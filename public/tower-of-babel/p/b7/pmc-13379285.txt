Post-Injection Sciatic Neuropathy: MR Neurography Findings in a Case of Iatrogenic Nerve Injury
Authors: Sumit Kumar, Ritwik Sahoo, Mohammad Hashim, Saransh Puri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Post-injection sciatic neuropathy is a recognized iatrogenic complication of intragluteal injection. We report a case of a young adult presenting with progressive complete paralysis of the left lower limb following intragluteal injection. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated focal thickening and T2/short-tau inversion recovery (STIR) hyperintensity of the sciatic nerve in the gluteal region, without neuroma or discontinuity and without associated muscle denervation changes. These findings were consistent with post-injection sciatic neuropathy, likely representing axonotmesis. MR neurography was useful in confirming the site and extent of injury and in excluding structural complications. This case highlights the characteristic imaging appearance of injection-related sciatic nerve injury and emphasizes the importance of correct injection technique.

Introduction

Post-injection sciatic neuropathy is a recognized iatrogenic complication of improperly administered intragluteal injections and may result in significant neurological morbidity, ranging from transient sensory symptoms to severe motor deficits. 1 2 The risk is highest when injections are placed outside the recommended safe quadrant, leading to direct needle trauma or chemical neurotoxicity involving the sciatic nerve. Although more commonly described in children, similar patterns of injury and functional impairment are observed in adults.

Diagnosis is primarily clinical and supported by electrodiagnostic studies; however, imaging serves a complementary role in confirming the site and extent of injury. Magnetic resonance neurography (MRN) enables direct visualization of the affected nerve, demonstrating signal alteration, nerve thickening, and preservation or disruption of continuity. 3 4 These features can help characterize the severity of injury and exclude complications such as neuroma or discontinuity. We report a case of delayed progressive lower-limb paralysis following intragluteal injection, highlighting the characteristic MRN findings.

Case

A young adult presented with a 4-month history of progressive weakness of the left lower limb following an intragluteal injection administered for abdominal pain. Weakness began soon after the injection and progressed over 3 months to complete motor loss, rendering the patient unable to ambulate. Mild numbness over the posterolateral leg and dorsum of the foot was also reported. There was no relevant past neurological, spinal, or systemic history.

Neurological examination demonstrated complete loss of motor power (Medical Research Council (MRC) grade 0/5) in sciatic-innervated muscles, including the hamstrings and all muscles below the knee, with preserved hip flexion and adduction. Sensation was reduced in the sciatic nerve distribution, and deep tendon reflexes were absent at the knee and ankle. Electrodiagnostic studies confirmed a severe axonal sciatic neuropathy, predominantly affecting the common peroneal division, without evidence of conduction block.

MRI of the pelvis and thigh using a dedicated peripheral nerve protocol (including T1-weighted and fat-suppressed T2/short-tau inversion recovery [STIR] sequences) demonstrated focal thickening and T2/STIR hyperintensity of the left sciatic nerve at the level of the sciatic notch and gluteal region, with loss of normal fascicular architecture ( Figs. 1– 2 3 ). No neuroma or nerve discontinuity was identified. The surrounding soft tissues were unremarkable, and no denervation changes were seen in the regional musculature.

Loss of the normal fascicular honeycomb architecture is noted at this level. No intraneural hemorrhage or surrounding fibrotic mass is identified.

The signal abnormality is maximal at the presumed injection site. The right sciatic nerve (arrowhead) is normal for comparison.

No discrete neuroma or nerve discontinuity is identified. The surrounding gluteal musculature appears normal without early T1 fatty infiltration.

A diagnosis of post-injection sciatic neuropathy (Sunderland grades II–III, consistent with axonotmesis) was made. The patient was managed conservatively with physiotherapy and ankle-foot orthosis. At 3-month follow-up, early motor recovery was noted, with return of tibialis anterior function (MRC grade 2/5).

Discussion

Post-injection sciatic neuropathy most commonly results from improper intragluteal injection technique, leading to direct needle trauma or chemical neurotoxicity. 1 2 The common peroneal division is more frequently and severely affected due to its relatively superficial and lateral position within the sciatic nerve at the level of the sciatic notch, 2 5 correlating with the predominant peroneal involvement in the present case.

MRN serves as a useful adjunct in the evaluation of such injuries by directly demonstrating nerve signal alteration, thickening, and continuity. 3 4 The characteristic finding is T2/STIR hyperintensity with enlargement of the affected nerve segment and loss of normal fascicular architecture. 3 6 The absence of neuroma or discontinuity, as in this case, is consistent with axonotmesis (Sunderland grades II–III) and is generally associated with a more favorable prognosis, whereas focal neuroma formation or nerve discontinuity suggests more severe injury and may warrant surgical consideration. 5 7

This case highlights the characteristic MRN appearance of post-injection sciatic nerve injury at the level of the gluteal region. The absence of denervation changes in this case may be explained by the proximal level of injury, where gluteal branches have already arisen, limiting proximal muscle involvement. Denervation changes, if present, would be expected in distal lower limb musculature. Importantly, the absence of muscle signal abnormality on MRI does not exclude significant nerve injury, and clinical and electrodiagnostic correlation remains essential. Given the characteristic history and imaging findings, the diagnosis is usually straightforward. In this context, imaging primarily serves to confirm the diagnosis, localize the site of injury, and exclude surgically relevant complications. 1 5

Conclusion

This case demonstrates the characteristic MRN findings of post-injection sciatic neuropathy, with focal nerve thickening and T2/STIR hyperintensity without neuroma or discontinuity, consistent with axonotmesis and a relatively favorable prognosis. MRN serves as a useful adjunct in confirming the site and extent of injury and in excluding structural disruption. The absence of denervation changes on MRI does not exclude significant nerve injury and should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical and electrodiagnostic findings. Prevention through appropriate injection technique remains essential.
