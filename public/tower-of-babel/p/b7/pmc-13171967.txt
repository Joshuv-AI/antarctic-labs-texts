First Case of TAP1 Deficiency with EBV B-Cell Lymphoma Treated with Cellular Immunotherapy
Authors: Francisco Javier Bermejo-Olivera, Miriam Velasco-Sidro, Rodrigo Íñiguez-García, Daniel Arroyo-Sánchez, Daniel Enrique Pleguezuelo-Garrote, Manuel Serrano-Blanco, Aurora Fernández-Galván, Tycho Baumann, Francisco Javier López-Jiménez, Enrique Revilla-Sánchez, Estela Paz-Artal, Luis M Allende, Oscar Cabrera-Marante
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeMutations in TAP1 represent one cause of Bare Lymphocyte Syndrome (BLS) type I, characterized by impaired human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class I expression and increased susceptibility to infections. EBV-driven lymphomas are rarely reported in BLS patients. Here, we describe the clinical management of a patient with TAP1 deficiency and discuss the treatment of an Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-associated B cell lymphoma using cellular therapy.MethodsWe report the first case of a TAP1-deficient patient who developed EBV-associated diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL). Clinical, immunological, histopathological and genetic evaluations were conducted. Treatment included standard chemotherapy regimens and adoptive immunotherapy with EBV-specific allogeneic T-cells (Tabelecleucel).ResultsA male patient presented with childhood-onset chronic respiratory infections and treatment-refractory cutaneous granulomas. Genetic testing revealed a homozygous pathogenic nonsense mutation in TAP1. The patient developed EBV+ DLBCL, refractory to rituximab-based therapies. Partial clinical stabilization was achieved with Tabelecleucel, but disease progression ensued.ConclusionsThis is the first reported TAP1-deficient case developing EBV+ lymphoma, highlighting the malignancy risk in BLS. Adoptive T-cell therapy showed transient benefit, suggesting a promising, though limited, approach in refractory EBV-associated malignancies in immunodeficient patients.Keywords: Inborn errors of immunity (IEI); Adoptive immunotherapy; B-Cell lymphoma; Epstein-Barr virus infections; Lymphoproliferative disorders; Bare lymphocyte syndrome, type I.

Introduction

Human inborn errors of immunity (IEI) are a heterogeneous group of genetic disorders that can present symptoms such as increased susceptibility to infections, autoimmunity, autoinflammatory diseases, allergy, bone marrow failure, and/or malignancy. Some of them can produce a combined immunodeficiency affecting cellular and humoral immunity, generally less profound than a severe combined immunodeficiency [1]. One of these IEI is the Bare Lymphocyte Syndrome (BLS), characterized by a severe reduction of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class I or class II molecules expression. Severe HLA class I deficiency has been related to mutations in the transporters associated with antigen processing (TAP) 1 or 2, TAPASIN (TAPBP) and β2-microglobulin (B2M). TAP1 is involved in the peptide loading in HLA class I molecules [2, 3]. Loss-of-function mutations in the TAP1 gene cause an immunodeficiency characterized by chronic respiratory tract infections (upper-to-lower progression from sinusitis/otitis with H. influenzae/S. pneumoniae to Pseudomonas-colonized bronchiectasis), necrotizing granulomatous skin lesions (possibly linked to rubella virus), encephalomyelitis, and severe herpetic infections [4]. Regarding malignancy, only rare squamous cell carcinomas arising from persistent granulomas have been reported [5, 6]. To date, TAP1 deficiency has been reported in only 20 patients, associated infections and dermatologic lesions [3, 7–9].

Some patients with combined immunodeficiencies develops Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV)-associated polymorphic lymphoproliferative disorders (PLD). PLD are a diverse and uncommon group of disorders caused by the clonal expansion of EBV-infected lymphocytes [10]. In those cases, adoptive T-cell therapy may be a treatment option for life-threatening viral infections or their related complications [11–13].

Here we report the first patient with TAP1 deficiency and EBV-associated B-cell lymphoma treated with the novel therapy tabelecleucel (EBV-specific allogeneic cytotoxic T cells), achieving transient disease stabilization.

Methods

Clinical Assessment

A male patient presenting with chronic respiratory tract infections and cutaneous granulomas has been followed longitudinally since the age of three.

Immunological and Genetic Evaluation

Peripheral blood immunophenotyping was performed using the following monoclonal antibodies: anti-CD45-FITC, anti-CD4-RD1, anti-CD8-ECD, anti-CD3-PC5, and anti-human HLA-ABC FITC (Clone G46-2.6). Samples were incubated with antibodies for 30 min prior to analysis with the AQUIOS flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, California, USA). Serum immunoglobulin levels were quantified using the IMMAGE 800 protein chemistry analyzer (Beckman Coulter).

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) was conducted to identify variants in genes associated with IEI, using the SureSelect XT HS kit (Agilent Technologies, California, USA) and a MiSeq sequencer (Illumina, California, USA). Variants identification, filtering and interpretation was carried out with Alissa software (Agilent Technologies). HLA genotype was identified using high resolution typing and confirmed using a low-resolution typing (PCR-SSP).

Histopathology

Biopsies of skin and lymph nodes were analyzed to assess granulomatous inflammation and lymphoid architecture. EBV detection was performed via EBV RNA (EBER)expression in situ hybridization using a commercial kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland).

Results

Clinical History and Dermatological Manifestations

A 31-year-old male presented to the immunology clinic with a history of recurrent infections and chronic cutaneous lesions. Cutaneous manifestations began at age 3, initially on the left knee, later spreading to both lower limbs. The lesions were characterized by recurrent erosions and ulcerations, poorly responsive to topical and systemic immunosuppressive treatments (corticosteroids, tacrolimus, colchicine, cyclosporine). All microbiological cultures were negative. Histological examination revealed chronic suppurative non-necrotizing granulomatous dermatitis (lipid necrobiosis).

Background and Respiratory Infections History

The patient had recurrent upper and lower respiratory tract infections since childhood, including sinusitis, tonsillitis, suppurative otitis media, and bronchiolitis, requiring multiple antibiotic courses. In adolescence, there was a worsening of infections, with frequent pneumonias necessitating hospital admission and intravenous antibiotics. Imaging revealed diffuse bronchiectasis, and sputum cultures demonstrated persistent colonization by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.

Progression of Cutaneous Lesions

During dermatologic follow-up at 31 years of age, the cutaneous lesions progressed into irregular, asymmetric plaques measuring 3–10 cm, characterized by a papulonodular appearance, hyperkeratosis, and a pink coloration surrounded by a purplish-brown halo. The lesions were predominantly distributed on the knees, legs, ankles, and right thigh, with some showing erosions and ulcerations with raised borders (Fig. 1).

Immunological Evaluation and Initial Management

At 32 years of age, the patient was evaluated for a possible IEI. The lymphocyte immunophenotyping showed an increase in γδ T-cell percentage (29%), decreased CD4 + T cells (426/µL), profound reduction in naïve CD8 + T lymphocytes (1%), increased effector memory CD8 + T lymphocytes (72.6%), activated CD8 + T-cell phenotype (HLA-DR + 38%) and moderate B lymphopenia (66/µL) without hypogammaglobulinemia (Table 1).

Following the diagnosis of BLS type I, the patient was started on antimicrobial prophylaxis with azithromycin and inhaled colistin, along with intravenous immunoglobulin therapy (30 g every 28 days) to prevent infections and limit the pulmonary damage observed on imaging. Despite immunoglobulin replacement therapy, no improvement was noted in the cutaneous lesions; however, a reduction in both the frequency and severity of bacterial infections was observed.

Rituximab monotherapy (four cycles) was administered without clinical response, prompting escalation to R-CHOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone), and subsequently to BV-ESHAP (brentuximab vedotin, etoposide, methylprednisolone, cytarabine, and cisplatin) chemotherapy due to persistent refractoriness.

During treatment, the patient developed recurrent fever, dyspnea, and mucositis, findings consistent with neutropenic fever (ANC before treatment: 6.9 × 10³/µL; after treatment: 0.1 × 10³/µL) and tumor progression. Owing to persistent EBV viremia, Brentuximab Vedotin was administered. Aspergillus fumigatus was identified in sputum samples, prompting initiation of isavuconazole prophylaxis. Imaging demonstrated progression of the mass (10 cm) with contralateral pulmonary infiltration. Biopsy confirmed EBV-positive diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), showing high EBER expression.

Adoptive T-cell Immunotherapy, Follow Up and Outcome

After progression through three lines of therapy and clinical deterioration, adoptive cellular immunotherapy with tabelecleucel was proposed via clinical trial enrollment (NCT04554914). Tabelecleucel (Allo-501 A) consists of EBV-specific allogeneic cytotoxic T lymphocytes (2 × 10⁶ cells/kg IV) generated from healthy donors. This therapy aims to restore EBV-specific cellular immunity impaired by TAP1 deficiency and absent HLA class I antigen presentation. Within 72 h, the patient showed a reduction in fever and a slight improvement in general condition. Physical examination revealed a decrease in the number and size of lymphadenopathies, with stabilization of the lesions on CT, although some—particularly in the lungs—had developed a necrotic appearance.

The second dose of Tabelecleucel was administered six days after first dose. Then, transaminase, bilirubin, and lactate dehydrogenase levels increased. Cytomegalovirus viremia rose from 9,104 IU/mL to 25,000 IU/mL within a few days, prompting initiation of ganciclovir therapy. Liver ultrasound revealed hepatomegaly with multiple hypoechoic lesions diffusely distributed throughout all hepatic segments, consistent with prior CT findings. The patient subsequently developed increased respiratory effort, fluid retention, and worsening respiratory status, leading to death from lymphoma progression 11 days after the first Tabelecleucel infusion.

Discussion

Granulomatous lesions, autoimmunity and recurrent infections clinically characterize patients with HLA class I deficiency due to TAP1 mutations [9]. Granulomas arise from non-specific inflammation triggered by foreign antigens detected by innate immunity receptors. The innate immunity receptors detect these antigens and initiate an inflammatory response. M1 macrophages stimulate a cellular/Th1 response that cannot be controlled due to the inability to regulate natural killer cells (NK) activity in the absence of HLA class I molecules [15–17]. HLA class I peptide presentation is also essential for thymic T lymphocyte selection, preventing autoimmunity [8].

Despite the cellular defect produced by the absence of the HLA class I, involved in the presentation of intracellular antigens to cytotoxic CD8 + lymphocytes, only a few of the previously described cases presented viral infections [18]. This may be due to a possible protection mediated by TAP-independent immune responses [9]. Only the Rubella virus has been implicated, associated with granulomatous inflammation, cutaneous plaques, and non-ulcerated nodules in BLS [8]. Recent studies confirm rubella virus (RuV) persistence as a key driver of granulomatous lesions in MHC class I deficiency. Haskologlu et al. [7] report persistent anti-RuV IgM positivity suggesting ongoing antigenic stimulation without tissue clearance. Other exceptions include post-vaccine hearing loss/exanthema after smallpox vaccine, recurrent herpetic complications (ocular, cerebral, strokes), and disseminated VZV/HSV pneumonia in a Nepalese child. Interestingly, most cases occurred in the context of immunosuppression [3, 19].

In absence of viral infections, bacterial respiratory tract infections are reported as one of the most prevalent manifestations, leading to lung damage and bronchiectasis [20, 21] (Table 2). Our patients developed multiple respiratory infections in the adulthood.

Analytically, in TAP deficiency patients other cellular alterations present in our patient have been reported: an increase in γδ T lymphocytes, a decrease in the number of CD8 T lymphocytes and an increase in the number of autoreactive NK [17]. HLA class I (HLA-ABC) surface expression in TAP1/TAP2-deficient patients ranged from 0% to 73% of healthy controls, with persistently low mean fluorescence intensity (MFI 0.4–3.8 relative units). Our patient exhibited a 45.4% reduction (MFI 6,120 vs. 11,200).

The lymphoproliferative involvement or malignancy risk is frequent in some cellular immunodeficiencies [29] but it has not been described in BLS. Nonetheless, BLS patients face increased risk of chronic viral infections due to impaired HLA class I presentation of intracellular antigens, potentially explaining susceptibility to virus-related hematological malignancies. This likely contributed to progression from PLD to refractory EBV+ DLBCL. Some IEI with an increased susceptibility to persistent active infections by EBV in B cells may develop lymphoma [30–32]. In this case, we describe the progression from a PLD to a EBV+ DLBCL, refractory to treatment.

Patients with humoral defects respond well to immunoglobulin replacement therapy. Immunoglobulin replacement (30 g IVIG every 28 days) did not improve cutaneous granulomas but significantly reduced bacterial infection frequency and severity, consistent with Haskologlu et al. [7] where all 11 TAP1/2-deficient patients on IgRT maintained normal IgG levels with stable respiratory infections, though granulomas persisted despite therapy initiation post-diagnosis.

Cellular compartment deficiencies are primarily managed with antibiotic prophylaxis, vaccination, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in select cases. Lymphopenia or alterations in the T lymphocyte function do not have specific alternatives specifically in adults patients. At present, the development of adoptive T-cell immunotherapy approaches emerges as a potential treatment for EBV-active infections or related lymphomas [33]. In our patient, disease stabilization following tabelecleucel suggests partial restoration of EBV-specific immunity via expansion of allogeneic T lymphocytes recognizing TAP-independent EBV epitopes and reducing viremia, despite absent patient HLA class I expression [34]. So far, only one case of a patient with a human IEI has been described with Tabelecleucel treatment. In this case the indication was to reduce the risk of reactivation of EBV infection after unrelated donor hematopoietic cell transplantation [35].

To our knowledge, this represents the first reported case of progression from EBV-positive PLD to aggressive EBV+ DLBCL in BLS. The inability to clear EBV-infected B cells may reflect a fundamental failure of CD8 + cytotoxic responses, resulting in unchecked viral replication and lymphomagenesis.

Standard antimicrobial prophylaxis and immunoglobulin replacement are effective in controlling infections in humoral deficiencies, but they offer limited protection in cellular immunodeficiencies like BLS. Bone marrow transplantation may be curative but is rarely undertaken in adult patients [36].

Adoptive immunotherapy using EBV-specific T cells (e.g., Tabelecleucel) represents a promising approach. In our case, this intervention achieved temporary clinical and radiological stabilization. However, the rapid clinical decline suggests the disease was already at an advanced and irreversible stage. Our case contributes to the limited clinical experience with Tabelecleucel in inborn errors of immunity (IEI).

This case illustrates the complexity of managing BLS caused by TAP1 mutations, characterized by dermatologic granulomas, recurrent bacterial infections, and novel association with EBV-driven lymphoproliferative malignancy. The immune dysregulation associated with HLA class I deficiency may predispose to chronic viral persistence and transformation, warranting early detection and consideration of cellular immunotherapies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case summarizes the previously described BLS manifestations (recurrent infections and dermatologic granulomas) together with a progression to an EBV+ DLBCL refractory to treatment. The underlying cellular immunity defect resulting from the absence of HLA class I molecules may lead to a cascade of immune dysregulation, predisposing the patient to chronic viral infections and ultimately malignancy. Despite challenges in managing such complex immunodeficiencies, emerging therapies like adoptive T-cell immunotherapy show potential in addressing specific immune defects and related complications in the future.
