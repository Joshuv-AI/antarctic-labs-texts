Assessing the geospatial distribution and association of microplastics with water quality in the lakes of Vellore, Tamil Nadu, India
Authors: Nivetha Paranthaman, Biswajita Mohanty, M. Annie Jenifer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microplastics (MPs) are emerging contaminants which of global concern impacting water quality of an aquatic ecosystem. However, the investigation on the MPs is uncommon as compared to the water quality parameter analysis particularly in South India. This study analysed the MPs abundance, their polymer characterisation and physicochemical parameters of water quality in five lakes fed by the River Palar in South India. Water samples were collected from the lakes to analyse 14 physico-chemical parameters to determine the Water Quality Index (WQI)MPs were extracted from the water samples using density separation, and their polymer characterisation was performed using light microscopy, SEM & FTIR spectroscopy. The concentration of MP ranged from 89 to 637 mg/L across the five lakes with the highest concentration from Lake Saduperi showing (673 mg/L). Nine major Polymer types were identified, which includes Polyamide (Nylon), Polyethylene (PE), Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET), Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC). SEM micrographs revealed irregular, surface-cracked microplastic structures with co-contaminants. Geospatial analysis of water quality parameters indicated sharp regional variations in water quality, showing higher contamination around urban settlements, solid waste disposal sites, and from agricultural runoff zones. The estimation of Pearson correlation revealed a statistically significant correlation (r = 0.9) between the concentration of MP and Phosphate (r = 0.98, FDR-adjusted p = 0.0496)—which clearly indicates a direct effect of MP on water. An overall WQI comparison of the five lakes highlighted that the water quality was poor and unsuitable for drinking in all lakes except one i.e., Lake Sathyamangalam. This study provides novel insights on the MP- Phosphate co-occurrence in the study area. A targeted monitoring of phosphate-MP and polymer-specific source tracking is recommended to prevent contamination in the freshwater systems. This study also supports regional water management, and aids urban planners in environmental management and policy modifications.

Introduction

Water quality is determined by the it’s the physical, chemical and biological characteristics. Unsustainable water resource management leads to poor water quality, which limits its safe utilization and impairs the aquatic ecosystem1. Primary water quality parameters, such as pH, Electrical Conductivity (EC), Turbidity, Total Solids (TS), Total Dissolved Solids (TDS), alkalinity, hardness, Chloride, Sulphate, Ammonia (Nitrogen), Nitrate, Phosphate, Dissolved Oxygen (DO), Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD), are commonly used to assess the suitability of water for drinking, irrigation or domestic use2. Various past studies examined these indicators for water quality assessment in general3,4. While these parameters can indicate the level of contamination, the emerging contaminants pose a serious threat and demands an integrated assessment approach5. One such emerging contaminant that stands apart from the others in terms of its ubiquitous presence is1, Microplastic (MP). MPs are synthetic solid particles or polymer matrices with regular and irregular forms, and their size ranges from 1 μm to 5 mm. They exhibit a unique behaviour; their hydrophobicity facilitates contaminant absorption and their buoyancy influence long distant transportation6. The past studies have reported that the physicochemical parameters and the MPs have potential influences on each other. A study showed that turbidity, DO, BOD, ammonia, TS, and EC of water bodies were influenced by MPs2–4,7. Another study by Dhea et al.8, indicated that water quality parameters, such as temperature, current velocity, and DO, are significantly affected by the abundance of MPs. The amount of microplastics at high concentrations in water also deteriorates the availability of total ammonia9. The microplastic concentration affects the light penetration, which impacts photosynthesis and aquatic plant growth, resulting in higher turbidity and lower DO levels10. Owing to their physicochemical properties, microplastics can act as both sources (leaching additives) and sinks (adsorbing ambient contaminants) for pollutants, potentially influencing the distribution and bioavailability of nutrients11.

Due to various environmental factors, the physical and chemical properties of MPs change, either by cracking, foaming, chemical bond breakage or by molecular cleavage. These changes directly affect the spatial migration and distribution patterns of pollutants6,12,13. The adsorption rates for hydrophobic contaminants by microplastics vary with their shape and type of polymer, and these act as vectors carrying chemicals and microorganisms, which pose a significant risk to aquatic biota6. Microplastics were assessed as emerging contaminants and analysed alongside conventional physicochemical water quality parameters to better understand their association with overall water quality conditions14. However, limited studies have reported on the relationship between the water quality parameters and MPs concentrations in freshwater particularly in low and middle-income nations in Asia, Africa and South America5.

In the Asian subcontinent, India remains are the largest producer of plastic products [CPCB] substantially increasing the risk of MP contamination in water bodies. Few studies focus either the marine water contamination or the freshwater water contamination in the major rivers systems of India vis., Ganges and Yamuna14. However, the studies on contamination of lakes that are critical sources of freshwater are inadequate owing to the data scarce conditions prevailing in the country.

The Palar River basin in the State of Tamil Nadu located in the Southernmost part of peninsular India epitomized this research gap. This seasonal River supports numerous lakes that sustain domestic water supply, irrigation, and groundwater recharge15,11. Rapid urbanization, intense irrigated agricultural and inadequate water management have raised concern about the contamination of water bodies in this basin. However, the data on MP concentration or water quality parameters remains deficient. To add aimed toe gaps, this study was aimed to (i) Quantify the MP concentration and characterisation of MPs; (ii) Quantitative analysis of water quality parameters and calculate the WQI; (iii) Examine association of MPs and water quality parameters. The study also provides the first ever integrated approach for MP- WQI association in this study area and delivers a strong methodology replicable to any other data scarce regions.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study area included five lakes located in the Palar River Basin extending within latitudes 12°55′25.42″ and 12°56′19.20″N, and longitudes 78°57′17.96″ and 79°8′54.77″ E in Vellore district, Tamil Nadu, India (Fig. 1). This region experiences a tropical climate with a total annual rainfall of 953 mm predominantly received during the Northeast Monsoon season from October to December. The Palar River, which draining the northern Tamil Nadu, is characterized as a seasonal river16. This River plays an essential role in the local hydrology, supporting agriculture, irrigation, and ecosystems17. Its distributaries contribute to the overall surface water flow, influencing water availability and land use patterns of this region. For this study, five lakes, namely Gopal Samuthram Lake (L1), Saduperi Lake (L2), Melkavanur Lake (L3), Sathyamangalam Lake (L4), and Sedhuvalai Lake, are considered. These lakes are located within a basin of River Palar, and they are a critical source for meeting the water demands during the dry season, including domestic water supply, irrigation, and groundwater recharge. The hydrological connectivity of the River Palar and the five lakes are depicted in Fig. 118. The maps are generated using QGIS, a Freeware, that is readily available Geographic Information System licensed under the GNU General Public License (https://www.gnu.org/licenses). To delineate the base maps and river networks, we utilized the Quick OSM and QuickMap Services plugins within QGIS to source data directly from Open Street Map. Open Street Map data is open -source data.

Gopal Samuthram Lake, also known as Dharapadavedu Lake, is an urban lake located in Katpadi Blockin Vellore District with a catchment area of 51.78 sq.km19. Saduperi Lake plays a crucial role in storing rainwater and recharging groundwater levels with catchment area of 69.54 sq.km. It receives runoff from surrounding urban areas apart from the River Palar20,21. Melkavanur Lake with catchment area of 52.52 sq.km located in the western region of Vellore district. The lake primarily receives agricultural runoff from surroundings and is connected to local drainage channels within Palar River Basin21. Sathyamangalam, and Sedhuvalai Lakes are connected through lined canals to the Palar River, and these lakes act as a major water reservoir for irrigating agricultural lands in the surrounding villages. The lakes geographical distribution, proximity to urbanization, and hydrological connectivity to the River Palar are the subjects of concern as it increases the risk of contamination 22,23.

Sample collection

The water samples were collected during the pre-monsoon season in Tamil Nadu, (January -May 2024), using the grab sampling technique. to ensure minimal runoff, lower dilution factor, and relatively stable water conditions24,25. The sampling sites were strategically chosen with the possibility of degraded water quality and potential for microplastic pollution due to urbanization, agricultural lands, road runoff and industrial establishments. The geographic coordinates, elevation, and the number of samples collected from each lake are provided in Table 1. A total of 100 samples were collected from all five lakes with the sampling density varying based on the areal extent of the lakes: n = 10 for Gopal Samuthram Lake (L1), n = 15 for Saduperi Lake (L2), n = 30 for Melkavanur Lake (L3), n = 20 Sathyamangalam Lake (L4), n = 25 for Sedhuvalai Lake (L5) (Fig. 2). At each sampling point, water samples were collected at a depth of approximately below 25–30 cm from the surface using a 10 L stainless steel bucket pre-rinsed with the water from the sampling site. For physicochemical parametric analysis, 2 L samples were collected in sterile polyethylene terephthalate (PET) bottles. Prior to sampling, all bottles were presterilized by autoclaving at 121℃ for 15–20 min with loosened caps and were thoroughly rinsed with water from sampling sites before sample collection to minimize other potential plastic contamination. The sampling procedure followed established surface water sampling guidelines as described by Musselman26 The collected samples were then stored at temperatures ranging from 4℃ to 10℃ temperature range in an ice cooler during transportation and taken to the laboratory, where they were stored at 4℃ for physical and chemical analysis. The procedures followed for water sample collection were in accordance with standard water and wastewater analysis methods and sampling procedure for lake or stream surface water chemistry27,26. Statistical analysis

The tested water quality parameters were compared with the Bureau of Indian Standards for Drinking Water (IS10500:2012)34, for physico-chemical variabilities in pH, EC, Alkalinity, Hardness, TDS, TS, DO, BOD, Ammonia (N), Sulphate, Chloride, Nitrate, Phosphate, Turbidity. The degree of association between the 15 variables (14 water quality parameters + Microplastic concentration) was determined using the Pearson Correlation coefficient using the statistical tool “John’s Macintosh Project” JMP Student Edition Version 19.0.4 (Build 907436) (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA; https://www.jmp.com/)35. to address the multiple testing inflation, Benjamin- Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was also applied (Q = 0.05; m = 15).

Microplastic extraction

Microplastics were isolated from surface water samples using a density separation technique, according to the sampling procedure of NOAA (National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, 2015)43. In the laboratory, the samples were transferred into glass beakers and dried in a hot air oven at 90 °C for 46 h to evaporate the water. Wet peroxide oxidation was carried out to oxidize the organic material. This involved adding 20 mL of 0.05 M Ferrous sulphate (FeSO4) solution and 20 ml of 30% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) to the dried sample. The mixture was left to react for 5 min at room temperature. A magnetic stir bar was added, and then beaker was placed on a hot plate at until the bubbling stopped, which indicated that the Fenton reaction was complete. After digestion, 6 g of Sodium Chloride (NaCl) was added to the mixture to increase the density of the solution for the density separation process. The mixture was gently reheated until the salt was fully dissolved and then transferred into a density separator. The density separator was loosely covered with aluminium foil to prevent contamination from the air and left to settle overnight. After sedimentation, the settled particles at the bottom of the density separator were collected using 150 μm stainless- steel sieve by carefully opening the stopcock valve. The particles were air-dried while loosely covered with aluminium foil. The particles were then analysed using bright-field microscope (40× magnification) equipped with digital imaging system, as per NOAA guidelines43.

The total concentration of microplastics (mg/L) was gravimetrically determined after final drying. However, it is recognized that by gravimetric analysis alone, it is impossible to definitively establish that all residues recovered are plastic. Hence, microscopic analysis and Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) were conducted to verify the type of microplastic samples collected from five Lakes. A light microscope (40× magnification) with the attached screen was used to analyse the morphological structure and colour of the microplastics obtained from each lake, and they were compared with the reference images of past studies1,19,12,13. FTIR has been widely used to identify the chemical composition and polymer type. The instrument model IRAffinity-1 with Thermo Nicolet iS50 with inbuilt ATR from Shimadzu was used to analyse in this study.

Morphological identification and characterization of MPs

The MPs were examined under bright-field microscope at 40x magnification equipped with a digital imaging and were categorized based on its colour and morphology8,16,44,45. Blue, red, black, yellow and transparent were the predominant colours mostly identified from the microplastic samples. Morphologically the elongated, thin, and uniform-shaped particles are considered as fibres, hard-edged particles are considered as films, hard and round shaped particles are pellets. Based on the colour and morphological characterizations, the polymer types were compared and identified46–48.

Results and discussion

Descriptive analysis

The standard limits for drinking water quality parameters were referenced according to the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) drinking water specifications (IS 10500:2012)49. The descriptive statistics of the measured parameters are summarized in Table 2.

pH: The minimum and maximum pH values for the five Lakes L1, L2, L3, L4, L5 were (8.4–9.5), (8.2–8.8), (8.8–9.2), (9.8–10.1), (9.5–10.3), respectively. The average pH values of each of the lakes exceed the permissible limit, with a minimum of 8.2 and a maximum of 10.3, prescribed by the Bureau of Indian Standards for drinking water. According to BIS guidelines, pH values below 6.5 are considered acidic while values above 8.5 are alkaline. Therefore, all the lakes are found to be alkaline in nature, which can adversely affect the palatability of water.

EC: The BIS Standard limit of EC values for drinking water is 300 µS/cm. EC values measured for L1 is 1607 µS/cm, L2 (1561 µS/cm), L3 (4350.13 µS/cm), L4 (6692 µS/cm), and L5 (2592.8 µS/cm). In all five Lakes, the EC values exceeded the permissible limit prescribed by BIS guidelines for drinking water. The range for EC value varies from 1430 µS/cm to 6692 µS/cm, indicating the saline condition due to high dissolved salt contents. Sathyamangalam (L4) has the highest EC, i.e., 6692.7 µS/cm compared with others, indicating substantial dissolved salts in the water. It is also observed that it exhibits a normal distribution pattern from its CV of 3.3%.

Turbidity: Indian standards prefer a turbidity of 10 Nephelometric Turbidity Unit (NTU) in freshwater, and it should not exceed 5 NTU for drinking purposes. The turbidity value in L4 is 9.3 NTU, which is less when compared to other four lakes. L1 (30.8 NTU), L2 (34.9 NTU), L3 (30.2 NTU), L5 (12.4 NTU). High turbidity in water is caused by the suspended tiny solid particles. The increase in TDS and TS value subsequently increases the turbidity value. High turbidity also decreases the DO level. It decreased the light penetration and energy absorption which affects the aquatic ecosystem thriving below to the subsurface layers of the Lakes50.

TS: The TS concentration exceeded the permissible limit of 500 mg/L of the five Lakes L1 is (1072 mg/L), L2 is 909 mg/L, L3 is 3278 mg/L, L4 is 3377 mg/L, and L5 is 2614 mg/L. It also decreases the light penetration in to the lakes which affects the photosynthesis.

TDS: It is one of the significant parameters which analyse the quality of water due to its direct relationship with turbidity. High concentrations of TDS not within the permissible limit of 300–1000 mg/L are considered unsafe and not potable. The TDS value for the study area is measured as L1 (973 mg/L), L2 (779 mg/L), L3 (2273 mg/L), L4 (1838 mg/L), and L5 (1284.8 mg/L). The TDS from Lakes L1 and L2 were within the permissible limit, whereas the values in L3, L4 and L5 exceeded the standard limit, with L3 showing the highest value. Increasing TDS values in Melkavanur Lake (L3) which results from domestic effluents, garbage disposal, agricultural runoff, and fertiliser inputs. High TDS enriches the nutrient status, and the accumulation of tiny particles results in eutrophication in water bodies51.

Alkalinity: The alkalinity of water is determined based on the presence of carbonates, bicarbonates and weak acid salts, and 200 mg/L is considered the permissible limit for drinking water purposes. The alkalinity measured for different Lakes are L1 (100.8 mg/L), L2 (65.5 mg/L), L3 (174.2 mg/L), L4 (20.2 mg/L), L5 (11.9 mg/L). L4 marginally exceeded the permissible limit. L5 shows the CV of 28.9%, indicating high spatial variability among sampling points. L2 shows a CV of 2.9%, which determines the normal distribution pattern. In summer, the decrease in water level is a practical reason for the decomposing of plants and living organisms, resulting in the inclusion of carbonate and bicarbonate concentration in the water body, which then increases the alkalinity46.

Total Hardness: The measured hardness values for the five Lakes L1, L2, L3, L4, and L5 are 199.4 mg/L, 185.6 mg/L, 418.5 mg/L, 490.4 mg/L, 269.4 mg/L, respectively. 200 mg/L is the acceptable range for drinking water prescribed by BIS. Out of 5 lakes, Gopal Samuthram Lake and Saduperi Lake, show higher hardness values, representing higher concentrations of calcium and magnesium ions in the water body.

Chloride: The permissible range of chloride for drinking water is 200–250 mg/L. The chloride contents range in L1 is 362 − 288 mg/L, L2 is 658–860 mg/L, L3 is 640–680 mg/L, L4 is 1360–1390 mg/L, L5 is 1262–1390 mg/L. The entire study area shows the maximum limit of chloride beyond the standards. The minimum value is shown in Gopal samuthram Lake (L1) and the maximum value in Sathyamangalam Lake (L4). According to52, the high amount of chloride in water bodies is directly related to the addition of sewage water into the water bodies. The urban settlements close to Sathyamangalam lake are one of the promising reasons for chlorine inclusion in the water body.

Sulphate: Sulphate is one of the major anions commonly present in freshwater systems. According to BIS drinking water specifications, the acceptable limit for sulphate is 200–400 mg/L. A high level of sulphate gives a bad taste to water. L3 shows the sulphate values of 318 mg/L were within the permissible limit, L2 is 402 mg/L, L1 is 502 mg/L, L4 is 1754 mg/L, L5 is 766 mg/L were observed as above the permissible limits.

Ammonia (NH3): Ammonia is one of the several forms of nitrogen that exist in the aquatic environment. The level of ammonia describes the quality of water and the living organisms in water bodies7. The BIS standard limit for Ammonia concentration in surface water bodies, which is suitable for drinking, is 0.5–1.5 mg/L. The average amount of ammonia calculated for the study area L1 is 2.4 mg/L, L2 is 1.4 mg/L, L3 is 1.4 mg/L, L4 is 0.3 mg/L, and L5 is 1.1 mg/L. Gopal Samuthram Lake (L1) is above the permissible range of ammonia, which may cause eutrophication. The anthropogenic activities, such as effluent discharge, agricultural runoff are the major sources.

Nitrate: The amount of nitrate found in the water samples of Gopalsamuthram lake is 47.3 mg/L, Saduperi lake is 2 mg/L, Melkavanur lake 23.3 mg/L, Sathyamangalam lake 131.1 mg/L, Sedhuvalai lake 74.9 mg/L. According to the BIS standard, 45 mg/L is the permissible nitrate level in water for drinking purpose. The Nitrate levels increased due to the application of inorganic fertilizer and animal manure in the agricultural fields adjacent to the lakes53.

Phosphate: Phosphate levels were observed as L1 (2.8 mg/L), L2 (2.1 mg/L), L3 (1.3 mg/L), L4 (0.4 mg/L), and L5 (0.8 mg/L). The permissible limit of phosphate in drinking water bodies is 0.5 mg/L. The phosphate concentration in all lakes except Sathyamangalam Lake (L4) exceeds the permissible limit. The significant anthropogenic sources of phosphorus in the aquatic environment are plant decomposition, fertiliser, urban effluents. The excessive phosphorous promotes the algal growth in the aquatic environment.

DO: As per Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) surface water quality criteria, dissolved oxygen concentrations > 6 mg/L are prescribed for drinking water without conventional treatment (CPCB, 2008)54. The measured DO value range for the study area L1 is 3.7 mg/L, L2 is 0.2 mg/L, L3 is 0.5 mg/L, L4 is 6.0 mg/L, L5 is 2.3 mg/L. The maximum amount was found in L4, whereas L1, L2 and L3 show very little amount, which indicates the high temperature, high microbial activity and addition of sewage waste. Low concentration of DO causes harm to aquatic organisms like fish, as it depletes oxygen for their survival.

BOD: BOD value in the samples of L1 is 4.1 mg/L, L2 (0.2 mg/L), L3 (1.6 mg/L), L4 (2.1 mg/L), and L5 (1.3 mg/L). L1 shows the maximum amount of BOD concentration where the DO level was lower, and it enhances the microbial activity. In L2, there is a low concentration of BOD, suggesting a good environment for the aquatic organisms.

Interrelationships among physicochemical parameters

The correlation matrix revealed several strong associations among conventional water quality parameters. Electrical Conductivity (EC) exhibited a very strong positive association with hardness (r = 0.98), Similarly, Total Dissolves Solids (TDS) showed strong positive correlations with hardness (r = 0.90) and Total Solids (TS) (r = 0.91), reflecting the interconnected nature of dissolved and suspended mineral content.

Sulphate and Nitrate demonstrated a very strong positive association (r = 0.94), suggesting potential common sources such as agricultural runoff or domestic wastewater inputs. Turbidity also showed strong positive relationship with Phosphate (r = 0.83), indicating that nutrient enrichment may coincide with increased particulate matter in the surface water.

Association between microplastics and water quality parameters

MPs were detected in all 100 samples, confirming ubiquitous contamination across the five lakes. Concentrations varied substantially among lakes and exhibited varying degrees of association with several physicochemical parameters. A very strong positive correlation was observed between microplastics and Phosphate (r = 0.98), indicating that lakes with elevated Phosphate levels also exhibited higher microplastic concentration. Strong positive associations were also observed between microplastics and turbidity (r = 0.71) as well as Water Quality Index (WQI) values (r = 0.83). In contrast, strong negative associations were observed with Chloride (r=− 0.86).

To control for multiple comparisons, the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction (Q = 0.05; m = 15)55 was applied concentration of set of microplastic with fourteen parameters correlation, the results are presented in Table 4. After adjustment, only the association between microplastics and phosphate remained statistically significant (FDR-adjusted p < 0.05). All other associations were statistically in significant following correction at 0.05 level of significance is considered as an exploratory trend rather than statistically robust relationships.

Characterization of microplastics

The FTIR polymer identification revealed that nine different types of polymers across the five lakes. The most predominant being Nylon followed by polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polyvinyl chloride (PVC), and polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE). The three regions: 4000 − 2750, 2750 − 1850, and 1850 − 700 are considered as the fingerprint region in the FTIR spectra of Microplastics, due to high intensity and specificity. In this study, the microplastic samples analysed fell in the size range of 300 μm and 150 μm. The fibers and films were mostly observed which have the fractures and cracks in the surface, these weathering is due to biological degradation and oxidative weathering by ultra violet exposure in the lakes14.

MPs contain chemicals, including plasticisers, stabilisers, UV filters, flame retardants, and other chemical leaches, such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, polybrominated diphenyl ethers, and polychlorinated biphenyls, which are associated with MPs characterisation, concentration, polymer types, degradation period and environmental conditions. The impact on water quality parameters and WQI is based on these factors57–59. Even though all five Lakes showed the positive correlation of pH, Ammonia, Phosphate and turbidity with WQI as well MPs concentration. The concentration level and the accumulation of MPs are factors which influence the water quality parameters and alter the WQI. From the results, it was confirmed that among the five lakes, Saduperi lake had the least WQI value of 476.2, which is considered poor and unsuitable for drinking as per “World Health Organisation (WHO) guidelines for drinking water quality”39. The high WQI at Saduperi Lake has been found to be mainly due to higher pH, alkalinity, turbidity, ammonia, and phosphate. Along with WQI, it showed the highest concentration MPs (637 mg/L) compared to other lakes. The correlation between the parameters and MPs concentration indicated important connections, which could be the reason for Saduperi lake to show more variation from standards and higher WQI. The spatial distribution of pH and phosphate in Saduperi Lake showed the places where there is higher accumulation, overlapping with the regions with high fishing activities, dumping of waste, urban settlements, and road runoff. Ammonia and turbidity show higher concentrations on the side of agricultural fields, whereas higher phosphate accumulation occurs in places near fishing and waste disposal zones. These sources could increase the accumulation of MPs. One of the possible reasons could be due to water passing canals connecting the Saduperi Lake and the Palar River, which could theoretically bring more MPs and other pollutants. This study has shown evidence supporting the poor water quality with the presence of MPs in Saduperi Lake. Even though Sathyamangalam Lake shows the lowest WQI of 73.6, it shows the highest amount of chloride, sulphate, and nitrate because of agricultural runoff, which is quite higher when compared with other lakes. In spatial mapping, phosphate, turbidity, and WQI show similar trends, and a high correlation was also observed. Higher levels of microplastic particles can increase the turbidity of the water. Similarly, their hydrophobic surface paves the way for the accumulation of biofilms, which act as a vector for the absorption of other pollutants, including phosphate.

Notably, the concurrent presence of microplastics (MPs)- notably polyethylene and PVC- with high phosphate levels in the dry season sampling period in the study lakes indicates a common anthropogenic source pathway, wherein both microplastics and phosphate-rich materials are discharged into the lakes via common carriers like agricultural runoff and domestic wastewater. This concurrent presence is indicated by the significant correlation between MPs density and phosphate (r = 0.98, BH adjusted p = 0.0496, n = 5) which was the only parameter pair that remained significant after Benjamin- Hochberg False Discovery Rate adjustment for multiple testing (Q = 0.05, m = 15 tests). This result agrees with Troung et al. and Nkosi et al., who emphasized the concurrent presence of microplastics and nutrient enrichment in freshwater ecosystems under agricultural and urban influences. The results clearly show the deterioration of these water quality parameters indicates the collective impact of urban runoff, agricultural activities, domestic sewage, and poor solid waste management practices that contribute to the enrichment of nutrients and microplastic pollution in the study area.

Recommendations

Based on the current study the Saduperi Lake needs urgent action to mitigate the water quality since it is greatly deteriorated compared to other four lakes. To effectively mitigate MPs contamination and improve water quality, a combination of regulatory, technological, and community-based strategies is essential. Controlling and characterizing non-point and point source pollution sources (agricultural runoff, urban drainage, industrial effluents) are important to prevent pollutants from directly entering lakes. Solid waste management should be strengthened alongside, strict regulations on plastic waste disposal to minimise MPs entry into water bodies. Phosphate should be included as a potential tracer for estimating the MP concentration in water while conducting seasonal water quality monitoring in the lakes. Target specific polymer sources must be identified across all the lakes like Nylon contamination from fishing nets so that alternatives can be identified and promoted. Expanding the spatial scope to cover additional lakes and catchment areas would help identify broader contamination patterns and sources. Integrating geospatial tools such as remote sensing and GIS can provide high resolution assessments of land use, drainage networks, and pollution sources. Satellite imagery could further aid in tracking environmental indicators like changes in lake surface area, vegetation cover, and urban development. Additionally, employing advanced methods such as micro FTIR, Raman spectroscopy, and drone -based sensors may greatly enhance the accuracy and efficiency of microplastic detection and environmental assessment.

Conclusion

The present study aimed to evaluate microplastic pollution and physicochemical water quality parameters of five Palar River lakes located in Vellore, Tamil Nadu, India, during the post-monsoon season (January-May 2024). Fourteen Physicochemical analysis for water quality was carried out and were compared with BIS drinking water standards (IS 10500:2012). The analysis results could potentially aid in identifying the broader cause of pollution in these lakes. Based on the analysis of quantitative measurements of 14 water quality parameters, this study concludes that all five Lakes had varied concentrations of the pollutants above permissible limits of Drinking water. The parameter analysis gave an idea about the difference among the lakes, whereas WQI denoted the overall water conditions. A total of ten polymer types were identified in the sampling locations: Polyethylene (PE), Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET), Nylon, Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), Nitrile, Polyvinyl alcohol/ Cellulose, Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC), Polysulfide, Polypropylene (PP). Spatial distribution mapping revealed that pH, Turbidity, Ammonia, BOD, and Phosphate showed similar patterns with WQI. Among the water quality variables considered, Microplastics was found to have a statistically significant positive with phosphate (r = 0.98, BH-adjusted p = 0.0496) after FDR correction, indicating the phosphate is the most strongly correlated variable with MPs contamination in the study lakes. Although several other variables showed moderate correlation coefficients, none of these remained statistically significant after BH-FDR correction (BH adjusted p > 0.05) except phosphate and thus be considered as spatial trends that need to be confirmed by larger sample sizes and multi-seasonal studies.

The results of WQI indicated that five Lakes except Sathyamangalam Lake (L4) assessed in this study were categorized as poor water quality. Saduperi Lake (L2) had WQI value of 476.2, indicating unsuitable water for drinking due to increased pH, alkalinity, turbidity, ammonia, and phosphate levels. Gopal Samuthram Lake (L1), Melkavanur Lake (L3), and Sedhuvalai Lake(L5) were found to be very poor water quality. Sathyamangalam Lake (L4) was found to be good water quality, with a WQI of 73.6, even though it had the highest levels of chloride, sulphates, and nitrates among all the lakes under study, indicating its unique agricultural pollution patterns compared to other lakes that had urban and sewage pollution patterns.

Further, the microplastic extraction process also confirmed that MPs were present in all five lakes, but the highest abundance of microplastic was observed in Saduperi Lake (L2) which had 637 mg/L. It is to be noted that Saduperi was the lake which had the highest WQI value among all lakes and is located closer to fishing activities, waste dumping sites, and canal connectivity with Palar River. Microscopic analysis of MPs also revealed that fibres and films were the dominant forms of microplastic in all lakes, predominantly of blue and red coloured fibres, and films, measuring between 150 and 300 μm in diameter. The FTIR analysis revealed that nine different polymer type microplastics were present in these five lakes: Polyethylene (PE), Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET), Nylon/Polyamide, Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC), Polypropylene (PP), Polysulfide, Nitrile, and a polymer containing bromine. The SEM analysis confirmed that the microplastics had surface cracking, fracturing, and biofilm, indicating that these microplastics are of secondary origin due to weathering in the environment.

Pearson correlation analysis, with Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate correction applied (Q = 0.05; m = 15 tests), revealed a statistically significant positive correlation between MPs concentration and phosphate concentration (r = 0.98, BH-adjusted p = 0.0496; n = 5). This result suggests that lakes with higher MPs concentration consistently had higher phosphate concentration, implying that these lakes had anthropogenic input sources in common, e.g., agricultural runoff and wastewater discharges. Spatial interpolation using ordinary kriging in QGIS revealed that pH, turbidity, phosphate, and ammonia concentration had spatial distribution patterns consistent with WQI in all five lakes, with hotspots of lake contamination located near urban settlements, agricultural fields, and waste disposal sites.

The study concludes finding a co-variation between parameters and MPs closely linking with WQI. The Water Quality Index (WQI) reflects the overall physicochemical condition of the water, while microplastic contamination was observed to co-occur with degraded water quality conditions across the studied lakes. Thus, the present study reveals that the distributary lakes of the River Palar are subject to considerable anthropogenic stress, as evidenced by microplastic contamination and physicochemical water quality deterioration resulting from the cumulative effects of urban runoff, agricultural runoff, domestic sewage, and mismanagement of solid waste. This study provides the first ever integrated evidence of the MP-phosphate co-occurrence in Southern India. The research findings also indicate that the MP Pollution does not occur in isolation, but it is influenced by the water quality deterioration due to the anthropogenic factors. As there is a rigorous increase in plastic production globally, further studies are needed to assess the ecological risk of the identified polymers and mitigate the compound effects of emerging contaminants.
