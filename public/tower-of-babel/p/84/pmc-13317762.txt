Social Identity and Wellbeing of Australian Vegan Men: A Qualitative Interview Study
Authors: Megan F. Lee, Julian Madsen, Jessica Bayes, Courtney McLean, Angela Gilmour, Sophia Blom, Aiden Van Elst, Talitha Best
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionWith the global rise of veganism, shifting dietary habits are reshaping the social roles and identities of Australian vegan men. Despite a predominance of research in female experiences of veganism, this topic remains underexplored for men.MethodUsing the social identity theory framework, this phenomenological, semi‐structured interview study explores how 27 Australian vegan men perceive and navigate their social identities within their social relationships. Using reflexive thematic analysis, we found four main themes.ResultsThe first two illustrating out‐group interactions: (i) masculinity and moral identity and (ii) navigating vegan identity: community and cultural integration and the final two illustrating within‐group interactions, (iii) Negotiating dietary boundaries: whole‐food and flexible veganism and (iv) close connections: strengthening personal bonds.ConclusionAustralian vegan men navigated their social identities through out‐group dynamics involving masculinity, ethical commitments and community integration, and in‐group dynamics marked by dietary boundary negotiation and solidarity with vegan communities. However, they also faced social isolation and strained relationships.So What?Their experiences reflected identity negotiation processes shaped by moral values, subgroup tensions and the importance of supportive social relationships in sustaining vegan practices. We recommend awareness of vegan male experiences of stigma, false perceptions and struggles with masculine ideals versus masculine values.

Introduction

In 2019, the number of plant‐predominant dietary followers grew to 2.5 million Australians since 2013 [1]. More recently, the Australian Bureau of Statistics [2] reported that 5% of the Australian adult population identified as vegetarian, with vegans, who avoid all animal products, representing 2% of the population [3]. There has also been a 47% increase in plant‐based meat sales in Australia between 2020 and 2023 [4]. Motives for adopting a vegan diet are intricate, including animal welfare, health, economic considerations, religious beliefs and environmental concerns, such as climate change [5, 6]. In addition, motives in uptake or adherence to a vegan diet may also be related to gendered attitudes. However, there is limited research on potential gendered motives behind dietary choices.

Recent research indicates divergent attitudes of men and women towards meat consumption and meat‐free diets [7]. Men tend to express more favourable attitudes towards meat consumption than women [8] and, in general, exhibit lower consumption of fruits and vegetables and a higher affinity for meat when compared to their female counterparts [9, 10]. Within an Australian context, eating meat has an ‘embedded symbolism of power to which Australian men are accustomed’ [11]. However, greater meat consumption is associated with comparatively poorer health outcomes in men and a higher risk of chronic disease development, such as cardiovascular disease, Type II diabetes and some forms of cancer in all individuals [12]. Consequently, there is a potentially disparate and contrasting set of motives through which Australian men navigate dietary choices to adopt a vegan diet.

In addition to these sociocultural influences, veganism intersects with health in ways particularly relevant to masculine identity. Dietary choices are not only linked to physical health outcomes but also to how men understand and express strength, discipline and self‐care [13]. While traditional masculine norms in Australia often celebrate meat consumption, plant‐based diets are increasingly associated with health optimisation, disease prevention and physical performance [14]. For vegan men, this creates a complex dynamic in which health behaviours may both challenge and reinforce aspects of masculinity, positioning veganism with the potential for redefining what it means to be a ‘healthy man’ [11].

Australian vegan men often face ridicule, shame, stigma, and social isolation due to dietary choices [11]. These gender disparities may stem from societal norms and stereotypes that assume it is ‘manly’ to eat large, meat‐based meals. By contrast, choosing lighter, plant‐predominant meals is seen as feminine [8]. When choosing what to eat, an individual embedded within a particular social circle can be influenced by logic, nutritional reasoning and the communal perspective on their food choices [15]. For this reason, vegan men may experience internal conflict between their values and the expectations of their gender identity [16].

Social identity theory provides a valuable lens to explore the experiences and motives of Australian vegan men through a sociocultural context [16]. Masculinity functions as a powerful form of social identity that shapes men's behaviours, choices and self‐perception [17]. Within the framework of social identity theory, masculine identity represents a, particularly, influential category membership that confers both privileges and constraints on those who identify as men [18]. For vegan men in meat‐centric cultures like Australia, dietary choices represent a complex site where masculine identity is both challenged and renegotiated. Traditional associations between meat consumption and masculinity [19, 20] create potential identity conflicts for vegan men, requiring them to navigate how they express masculinity while maintaining dietary practices that may be perceived as contradicting masculine norms.

Social identity formation reflects a dynamic interaction between individual agency and societal structures [21]. While individuals make choices that express their values and beliefs, these choices occur within existing social frameworks that shape which expressions are available and how they are interpreted [22]. For men who adopt veganism, their dietary choices may simultaneously reflect a response to social norms of masculinity and an expression of masculine values such as moral conviction, strength of character, and protection of the vulnerable [23]. This bidirectional process of identity formation is particularly evident in contexts where individuals adopt practices that challenge dominant cultural narratives, as is often the case with men who choose veganism in meat‐centric cultures [23].

The role of masculine norms in society is becoming increasingly researched as society navigates the expectations of men in sociocultural changes/sociocultural construction of masculinity in mental health [24, 25]. Although gendered dimensions of food practices and meat consumption have long been recognised, comparatively less attention has been paid to men's qualitative experiences of veganism and the ways vegan identities are negotiated in relation to traditional masculine norms [13, 14]. These intersections are particularly relevant within a health promotion context, where dietary practices are both behavioural and identity‐driven [26]. Understanding how vegan men interpret the relationship between health, dietary choices, and masculine identity may offer insight into how alternative models of masculinity can support healthier behaviours and wellbeing outcomes.

The present study aims to explore how Australian vegan men perceive and navigate their social identities within their social relationships. We examine the bidirectional relationship between individual values and societal norms, exploring how vegan men's sense of masculinity is both shaped by and expressed through their veganism. Rather than assuming that identity is solely constructed by individuals or imposed by society, we consider how Australian vegan men interpret, negotiate and express masculine identity within their social relationships and cultural context.

Methods

Research Design and Methodology

We employed a qualitative, reflexive‐phenomenological approach and a semi‐structured interview design to explore how Australian vegan men perceive and navigate their social identities within their social relationships. Our ontological and epistemological stance views reality as subjective, constructed through the interaction between participants' lived experiences and broader social contexts. This perspective recognises that while individuals actively interpret and make meaning of their experiences, these interpretations occur within and are shaped by existing social structures and norms. Our phenomenological approach emphasises understanding how vegan men perceive, navigate and express their identities within these social contexts [27, 28].

Data were analysed through reflexive thematic analysis as a suitable approach for exploring how vegan men interpret, negotiate and express masculine identity within their social relationships and cultural context [29]. Reflexivity within this design allowed us to recognise and address potential biases stemming from researcher preconceptions, personal experiences, and cultural backgrounds, influencing the study from inception to publication [30]. The research team comprises diverse genders and dietary types (meat‐eating, plant‐based and vegan), which positions the authors within a context that may influence the research design, data collection, interactions with participants and interpretation of findings. Therefore, to uphold qualitative validity standards, the lead researcher and facilitators kept a reflexive journal throughout the research project to document and explore perspectives as insider/outsider researchers [30].

Participants

Purposive, targeted sampling was used to recruit participants through word of mouth and social media posts on relevant Australian vegan communities on Facebook and Instagram. Semi‐structured interviews were conducted with 27 Australian vegan men. Ages ranged from 18 to 58 (M = 35.04, SD = 11.91). Most lived in Australian metropolitan centres: Sydney (n = 10), Melbourne (n = 7), Brisbane (n = 3), Gold Coast (n = 3) and Canberra (n = 1). Some lived in rural, regional and remote areas in Queensland (n = 1), Victoria (n = 1) and the Northern Territory (n = 1). The number of years adhering to a vegan diet ranged between two and 36 years (M = 8.84, SD = 7.22).

Procedure

Ethics approval was granted from Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee (#37461). Face‐to‐face and Zoom interviews ranged between 40 and 60 min. Participants who expressed interest were emailed an information sheet and consent form before each participant interview. Participants met with a facilitator (member of the research team) whereby consent was addressed verbally and audio‐recorded at the beginning of the interview. Interview questions were semi‐structured (see Supporting Information for full list of interview questions and prompts). No incentive was offered for participation. All interviews were audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim, without the use of field notes, to ensure the flow of the conversation. Participants were reminded that they could decline to answer any question, stop without explanation and withdraw data up to 1 week after the interview. No participants declined to answer or withdraw from the study. No repeat interviews were conducted. Participants were offered the opportunity to review the transcripts, but none accepted.

Data Analysis

Six stages of reflexive thematic analysis, guided by Braun and Clarke [29], were applied to the dataset. Stage 1 (data familiarisation) involved transcribing each interview verbatim. At this stage, all participant‐identifying information was removed and each participant was assigned a pseudonym: a letter and two numbers (e.g., N26, M24). The lead researcher repeatedly read the transcripts alongside audio recordings to preserve tone and meaning. Initial analytic observations were documented in a reflexive journal to capture early insights specific to participants' accounts of masculinity and vegan identity. Stage 2 (data coding) required the lead researcher to use NVivo to create code labels from the dataset. Coding was conducted inductively, with code labels actively constructed rather than passively identified, with attention to both the semantic and the latent content underlying assumptions about masculinity, morality and social belonging. Stage 3 (creating themes) involved categorising the codes into relevant themes. Code labels were scribed onto Post‐it notes, arranged across a wall, and physically categorised into codes during a collaborative process with several members of the research team. Preliminary themes were created by further categorisation of code labels and codes on the physical Post‐it notes. This step was also discussed as a team to ensure themes reflected both convergence and diversity in participants' experiences. Stage 4 (theme review) required researchers to review each theme and any sub‐themes identified within the dataset. During this phase, code labels, codes and preliminary themes were included in a thematic analysis code table (Supporting Information). Themes were refined by returning to the full dataset to assess coherence and distinction, with particular attention to how themes captured both within‐group (vegan community) and out‐group (non‐vegan social contexts) dynamics. Reflexive discussions were consistently applied throughout this stage to interrogate how researchers' perspectives may have shaped interpretation. Stage 5 (define themes) involved finalising each theme and reapplying to each line of the transcribed interviews. Themes were defined through analytic writing and the development of detailed theme summaries, including identification of central organising concepts and how each theme related to the study's social identity framework. Stage 6 (report writing) required the researchers to write a manuscript of the findings of the reflexive thematic analysis. The final analytic narrative was shaped through ongoing interpretation, during the write‐up stage, with the research team refining theme names and included quotes to ensure the findings authentically represented participants' accounts while offering a theoretically informed interpretation.

Results

We found three themes that illustrate how Australian vegan men perceive and navigate their social identities within their social relationships. The first two themes illustrate out‐group interactions: (i) masculinity and moral identity and (ii) navigating vegan identity: community and cultural integration. The final two themes illustrate within‐group interactions: (iii) negotiating dietary boundaries: whole‐food and flexible veganism and (iv) close connections: strengthening personal bonds. While some of the social processes identified (e.g., stigma management, community support and identity negotiation) may be relevant to vegans more broadly, our analysis highlights how these processes are distinctly shaped by participants' positioning as men, particularly, in relation to dominant norms of masculinity and expectations surrounding male behaviour. We begin by exploring the first two themes, which focus specifically on out‐group dynamics, before moving to explore interactions within vegan communities.

Theme 1: Masculinity and Moral Identity

By resisting traditional meat‐based diets, men described developing a compassionate understanding of their masculinity while integrating veganism as a core part of their self‐concept. Their vegan identity reflected a strong ideological commitment and personal alignment with values of compassion, justice, and environmental sustainability. Participants often framed veganism as more than a dietary choice, but rather a significant ethical and ideological commitment shaping their worldview. Abstaining from animal products was frequently tied to deeper values of justice, environmental responsibility, and compassion. N26 described veganism: ‘It's not an ideological position, it's not a diet, I see it as justice’. This framing highlights participants' ethical stance and commitment, in which they believed vegans held stronger personal, prosocial and moral motivations. The influence of veganism on self‐perception was evident in numerous participants; for example, M23 articulated, ‘I care about animal welfare, the environment, health. If anything, it added to what I am now …. I feel like I am a happier person’. He suggests that adopting veganism heightened his sense of moral responsibility, enhancing self‐satisfaction and emotional stability.

Participants described the way in which they managed their identities across various social contexts by controlling when and how they revealed their veganism to avoid stigma. S29 explained his process of deriving self‐esteem from group membership with non‐vegans:At work I've sort of taken a different approach where I let people get to know me first and then I'd slip it [veganism] in conversation. There's certain assumptions about what a vegan would look like, act like. I'm glad I have that effect on people because it's demonstrating you don't have to be from a certain mould to be vegan. (S29)

At work I've sort of taken a different approach where I let people get to know me first and then I'd slip it [veganism] in conversation. There's certain assumptions about what a vegan would look like, act like. I'm glad I have that effect on people because it's demonstrating you don't have to be from a certain mould to be vegan. (S29)

By adjusting how they shared their veganism, participants reported being able to better handle social reactions and pressures. These strategies reflect active conversational manoeuvres used to manage identity in potentially stigmatising interactions, such as timing disclosure, softening ideological positions and tailoring communication to audience expectations. This search for solidarity and emotional support was also evident within vegan groups, as described by S35. ‘Joining the vegan community provided an avenue to connect with like‐minded individuals, and it has been a positive experience in terms of finding social support’. By selectively sharing his vegan identity, S35 reports being able to better manage social reactions and pressures, which helped him feel more included.

Similarly, expressing the vegan identity related to visibility in social settings and groups. S34: ‘I'm very proud to be vegan but I don't need to splash it across my shirt. I would rather have a calm and light‐hearted conversation about it’. The awareness and negotiation of vegan identity in social settings were reflected in the perceived ‘effeminate and weak’ view of them as males due to their lifestyle choices and moral beliefs, as noted by N26:[I received]comments about myths about soy and estrogen or ‘You're not gonna be a man’ because you're eating all these phytoestrogens or real men have steak—especially because I wasn't going to [the] gym at this time—but I feel like as I've gone to the gym more and I've gotten physically more muscular, those comments have gone away. (N26)

This suggests that for Australian vegan men, physical muscularity serves as a strategic ‘identity shield’. By achieving a traditionally masculine physique, men can validate their lifestyle to others who may share their gender identity but not their dietary practices, positioning these audiences as both gender ingroup members and dietary outgroup members. This highlights the complexity of social identity processes in this context, where participants are not simply negotiating a single out‐group, but rather navigating overlapping and sometimes competing identity memberships (e.g., masculinity and dietary identity) simultaneously.

N26 further explains how changing his physical appearance helped counter stereotypes: ‘Before it was like, how are you going to grow’? And now it's more, ‘hey, what are your protein choices'? It's subtle, but there's a difference’. Participants described challenging social norms and stereotypes by redefining masculinity, often emphasising moral integrity and personal values over traditional notions of male dominance. S29 reports:I think it was the stereotypes and harmful misconceptions about what being vegan is and particularly traditional masculinity. [Friends] Saying because you've gone vegan that's a sign that you're weak or soft, sort of gender bullying. I actually think it's a sign of strength to live by your values and not to just follow the crowd. (S29)

S29 suggests that true strength lies in living by one's values and not just following societal norms. He described adhering to his ethical beliefs as a sign of strength, rather than conforming to traditional expectations of masculinity, such as resisting gendered expectations of toughness or dominance [8].

Theme 2: Navigating Vegan Identity: Community and Cultural Integration

Theme 2 describes the social wellbeing for Australian vegan men and the influence of community support and engagement on their social wellbeing, in line with social identity theory and in‐group/out‐group dynamics. Participants expressed the importance of a vegan community and a space that felt familiar and together but also recognised that sharing veganism did not guarantee quality social connections. Several participants highlighted the importance of engaging with the vegan community to maintain their mental health. These accounts positioned vegan communities as spaces of emotional respite, where men could reconnect with shared values and temporarily escape the social and moral pressures encountered in non‐vegan contexts.You need people to feel like they're part of a community. It's like when I was involved in uni[versity] activism, if you feel like you're part of a family you feel like you're doing something right, like we're achieving something together. That's very empowering and it combats the social othering and feeling like an outsider because you feel you're not alone. (S29)

You need people to feel like they're part of a community. It's like when I was involved in uni[versity] activism, if you feel like you're part of a family you feel like you're doing something right, like we're achieving something together. That's very empowering and it combats the social othering and feeling like an outsider because you feel you're not alone. (S29)

S29 highlights that a shared sense of identity with other vegans was not only necessary for a sense of belonging but also for validating his sense of achievement and the morality of his beliefs. On the other hand, G28 shared how community engagement had a negative impact on their mental health: ‘I used to be heavily involved in animal rights activism on social media … But I realised that it was taking a toll on my mental health to constantly witness the suffering of animals’. These experiences suggest a contrast between community engagement for social identity, which nurtures a positive culture beneficial to social health and community engagement for activism, which could lead to increased loneliness.

Another participant, N26, highlighted the balance between advocating for veganism and maintaining a vegan social identity: ‘When I have a negative experience with somebody over veganism, like an argument or an online endless tweet war, I tend to go back to my vegan social groups and vent, get it out’. N26 suggests that while vegan advocacy could sometimes lead to negative experiences of social tension, conflict, frustration and uncertainty, having a supportive group with shared experiences to return to can help mitigate these effects and validate their feelings and identity.

Some participants suggested that the main challenge of being vegan was not missing animal‐based foods but feeling excluded from cultural and social traditions centred around shared meals. For example, attending events like barbecues often meant limited options and a sense of disconnect from communal practices, reinforcing feelings of cultural loss rather than dietary deprivation. A24 expressed the difficulty of making connections with non‐vegans:The only bad thing about being vegan is—it's not that I miss cheese—it's not that I miss meat. It's the cultural aspect of it, I'm missing out on so much culture. Say if I go to a barbecue, they probably aren't going to have vegan options and so I'll just eat before I go and then everyone will be like—how good's me steak mate, how good—and I'll be like yeah true. (A24)

The only bad thing about being vegan is—it's not that I miss cheese—it's not that I miss meat. It's the cultural aspect of it, I'm missing out on so much culture. Say if I go to a barbecue, they probably aren't going to have vegan options and so I'll just eat before I go and then everyone will be like—how good's me steak mate, how good—and I'll be like yeah true. (A24)

This distinction highlights that for this participant, the ‘social isolation’ is rooted in a symbolic exclusion from masculine bonding rituals like the barbecue, rather than a longing for meat itself. A24 reports that although a vegan identity creates a positive experience within the vegan in‐group, it also makes it more difficult to find meaningful intergroup social connections. This sentiment was shared by N26, who expressed: ‘It's social isolation. Being vegan, there's this whole injustice happening day in day out that's incomprehensible to me, and when I go out, I see everybody with this apathetic view, it makes me feel lonely’. N26 suggests that being vegan not only makes intergroup relationships more challenging due to cultural divides but also that these relationships can be isolating and detrimental due to moral discomfort. There is a lot of judgement of others and reflects a frustration about ‘others’. Thus, despite the need for and importance of vegan identity and community, there is still a gap created by veganism that makes intergroup relationships with non‐vegans challenging to maintain and detrimental to social connection.

While some participants faced social difficulties due to their vegan identity, others noted a connection with local business and community. S32 emphasised the importance of local vegan culture in facilitating his diet and lifestyle: ‘We have a whole vegan market that pops up down the road regularly. I've become really good friends with a couple of different vegan business owners who own cafes and other stores, which has been really nice’. S32 suggests that having local opportunities for vegans to engage with their identity has a positive impact on their social connection. This sentiment was shared by B54, ‘It's always nice to be surrounded by like‐minded people’, indicating that these opportunities are not only valued for making the vegan lifestyle simpler but also because they make meeting and bonding with other vegans easier.

In contrast, several participants reported social barriers due to their vegan identity. B34 shared their experience with dining away from home:I would say eating out can be tricky. Trying to navigate your way through a menu or even speaking to the waiter about accommodating your meal, and making something for you on the fly, like that can be hard. (B34)

BO34 expresses how lacking opportunities to attend vegan‐friendly restaurants or markets can have a detrimental impact on social wellbeing, as they rely on non‐vegan‐oriented restaurants when eating out, which may not be accommodating of the vegan diet. This finding reflects the broader barbecue culture in Australia, where vegans may not always have alternatives to non‐vegan gatherings, leading to negative social wellbeing and interactions with non‐vegans. The following two themes build on this by moving from these out‐group encounters to the dynamics that unfold within the vegan in‐group.

Theme 3: Negotiating Dietary Boundaries: Whole‐Food and Flexible Veganism

Theme 3 explores a perceived behavioural divide in the vegan community, highlighting tensions between those who adhere strictly to a Whole‐Food, Plant‐Based diet (WFPB) and those who take a more flexible approach to veganism. Among the men interviewed, there was a notable distinction between ‘pure vegans’—who pride themselves on a diet centred around nutrient‐dense, whole foods—and those who allowed more flexibility in their plant‐based options.

Most men who adopted veganism for health reasons tended to favour a WFPB diet. They suggested that consuming whole plant foods helped them ensure they were eating a diet best for their bodies and contributed to increased energy levels and improved physical health:I was a convert, I didn't have any interest in consuming animal products anymore just because I discovered that they can be so inflammatory and when you're eating whole foods and whole grains and fresh food that you create everything from scratch, it's sort of … I found a much healthier relationship with what food is. (M32)

I was a convert, I didn't have any interest in consuming animal products anymore just because I discovered that they can be so inflammatory and when you're eating whole foods and whole grains and fresh food that you create everything from scratch, it's sort of … I found a much healthier relationship with what food is. (M32)

The WFPB movement is a dietary lifestyle choice that focuses on eating plants as close to their natural state, with as little processing as possible [31, 32]. Colin Campbell pioneered the movement with his China study [33], a large‐scale survey of over 6500 Chinese residents across 65 counties exploring diet, longevity and disease. This and other research found that moving from a diet high in animal products to one rich in whole plant foods was effective for weight loss, longevity and reduced risk of chronic lifestyle illnesses like cardiovascular disease, hypertension, Type II diabetes and dementia [34, 35]:In terms of a whole foods plant‐based diet, it is vegan, but it also means that you predominantly eat or choose foods that are minimally processed, they're in the whole food form, without any sort of extraction or processing. Minimal processing, if that makes sense. So, in a way, the way nature intended for us to eat these foods, that's how a whole foods plant‐based diet should be eaten. (M35)

In terms of a whole foods plant‐based diet, it is vegan, but it also means that you predominantly eat or choose foods that are minimally processed, they're in the whole food form, without any sort of extraction or processing. Minimal processing, if that makes sense. So, in a way, the way nature intended for us to eat these foods, that's how a whole foods plant‐based diet should be eaten. (M35)

The WFPB movement has gained significant traction within vegan communities and has been anecdotally likened to a ‘cult‐like’ following, stemming from its strong ideological commitment, community cohesion and intense dedication by those who follow it [31]. Across the interviews, those who consumed a WFPB seemed to shun those who did not conform to this strict dietary adherence, seemingly creating a minority within the vegan community:Definitely lots of other vegans out there who don't eat whole foods, who don't think like that. Predominantly processed foods … hot chips, burgers, pizzas. But just ones that don't have meat. (M35) A lot of people who went vegan for ethical reasons tend to be more ‘junk food vegans, but those who go plant‐based for health reasons tend to be more whole food vegans. (M34)

Definitely lots of other vegans out there who don't eat whole foods, who don't think like that. Predominantly processed foods … hot chips, burgers, pizzas. But just ones that don't have meat. (M35)

A lot of people who went vegan for ethical reasons tend to be more ‘junk food vegans, but those who go plant‐based for health reasons tend to be more whole food vegans. (M34)

The WFPB vegans often viewed their approach as the ideal, emphasising purity, discipline and a commitment to optimal health. This categorisation suggests that for some men, the WFPB ‘gold standard’ may serve as a tool for self‐legitimisation, where dietary rigour is used to reclaim a sense of masculine discipline [36]. Consequently, the internal ‘shunning’ of flexible vegans reveals that social isolation for vegan men can originate from within their own community, not just the meat‐eating public supported by the following quotes:I'd say that probably more vegans are ‘junk food vegans’ than whole foods plant‐based diet vegans. (M35) I also think from my experience dealing with like knowing people who are vegan, lot of the people who went vegan for ethical reasons tend to be more ‘junk food vegans’, but those who go plant‐based for health reasons tend to be more whole food vegan. (M34)

Vegans who do not consume a WFPB diet may face judgement or criticism from other vegans who judge them for their food choices, such as the consumption of mock meats. Ironically, this is a double‐edged sword as the male vegan community also faces social isolation from omnivores due to their food choices and not fitting within social norms [8].

Consistent with prior research, this theme illustrates a divide within the vegan community, where strict WFPB vegans often distanced themselves from more flexible vegans who incorporated all vegan food choices into their diets. The WFPB advocates, often motivated by health concerns, emphasise purity, discipline, and nutritional superiority while viewing ‘junk food vegans’ as compromising core vegan values. This tension creates a unique internal dynamic within the vegan movement, where solidarity is challenged by differing interpretations of what constitutes a ‘pure’ vegan diet.

Theme 4: Close Connections: Strengthening Personal Bonds

Men described the role of close relationships in supporting a vegan lifestyle. Participants expressed how essential the support of their friends and family is in maintaining their social wellbeing as vegans. Conversely, they also explored how the transition to veganism can strain these close relationships and make it difficult to develop new ones. This theme highlights the juxtaposition between the importance of close relationships in becoming vegan and maintaining veganism.

Participants reported the importance of close relationships and support from family and friends in their social wellbeing. C24 attributed their close family members as support for their social identity as a vegan: ‘My immediate family are quite supportive, but for the most part, I wouldn't say I have a community around me that supports that part of my identity’. This sentiment is echoed by M23, who states: ‘[Despite personal ridicule or rude comments], I don't really care as long as my friends still hang out with me, as long as my family is still comfortable with me eating out with them then that's fine’. These participants suggest that close relationships are essential for the social wellbeing of vegan men. While a sense of wider community is beneficial, maintaining a vegan identity becomes increasingly difficult without supportive close relationships due to the fear of social isolation.

Other participants reported that their partners help maintain their wellbeing as vegans and support their transition to becoming and staying vegan. S34 stated:My partner at the time was vegan and she was a big part of what made me or enabled me to make that transition. Because I had the support and the experience. Because she'd been vegan for well over a decade and she grew up as a vegetarian. In terms of my personal support structures I didn't have any adversity. (S34)

My partner at the time was vegan and she was a big part of what made me or enabled me to make that transition. Because I had the support and the experience. Because she'd been vegan for well over a decade and she grew up as a vegetarian. In terms of my personal support structures I didn't have any adversity. (S34)

These experiences suggest that the support of one meaningful person, such as a partner, can empower, stabilise and maintain lifestyle and dietary choices with more ease, thus making it a more stable part of identity as part of a long‐term habitual dietary pattern.

Several participants suggested that a reliance on close relationships for support created a change in relationships and behaviour changes in friends as they reflected on how transitioning to veganism could strain current relationships and make it difficult to develop new ones. One such case was that of S32:When I first made the change, I lost a few friends who were kind of at odds with the decision I made. But on reflection, I think it's kind of ridiculous for someone to stop being friends with someone because they've changed what they eat or how they shop, especially because I didn't come at it with any kind of ultimatum saying, if you want to be my friend, don't consume meat around me or anything like that. (S32)

S32's experience illustrates how adopting veganism can create tension within existing relationships, highlighting the social challenges men may face when their dietary choices conflict with the values or expectations of others.

Vegan men who didn't yet have a supportive partner at the time of becoming vegan also reported difficulty in dating. M24 stated: ‘Some chick took me out to Max Brenner[chocolate restaurant] on a date once, and I was like, why am I here?’ G28 shared a similar experience: ‘Well, it's mainly had an impact on my dating life. When it comes to dating, it's a bigger issue because I feel uncomfortable being intimate with someone who's consuming meat’. These experiences suggest that dating someone who does not share or respect the vegan lifestyle is a difficulty that for some men is a point of internal conflict and dissonance. There is an emotional and ethical conflict that creates discomfort and barriers to forming close, intimate relationships with non‐vegans.

Discussion

This phenomenological, semi‐structured interview study explored how Australian vegan men perceive and navigate their social identities within their social relationships through four key themes. The first two themes illustrated out‐group interactions: (i) masculinity and moral identity and (ii) navigating vegan identity: community and cultural integration. The final two themes illustrated within‐group interactions: (iii) negotiating dietary boundaries: whole‐food and flexible veganism and (iv) close connections: strengthening personal bonds. The experiences of Australian vegan men through the lens of social identity theory highlight that vegan men's sense of self is partly derived from their group memberships and the emotional significance attached to those memberships, but also in their experience of veganism their values, strength, change social connection and masculinity [37].

The first theme masculinity and moral identity highlighted how vegan men expressed masculine values through their ethical food choices. Participants framed veganism not merely as a dietary choice but as an ethical commitment aligned with values such as compassion, justice and environmental sustainability. Rather than seeing these values as contradicting masculinity, participants reinterpreted strength through moral conviction. This perspective reflects the process of positive distinctiveness in social identity theory, where individuals interpret group characteristics in ways that maintain a positive self‐concept [38]. As suggested in recent literature, veganism can serve as a vehicle for expressing traditional masculine values such as courage and conviction, rather than necessarily challenging them [23].

The findings suggest that vegan men in our study expressed their masculine identity in ways that integrated their ethical commitments, particularly, through values of courage, conviction and strength of character. They described meaningful connections and support from within vegan communities, which often helped them manage social interactions and pressures. Close relationships with family, friends and partners played a crucial role in supporting their lifestyle, although transitioning to veganism sometimes strained existing relationships and presented challenges in forming new ones. These patterns likely reflect key social identity processes: identity negotiation, group boundary management and the search for positive distinctiveness [22].

These findings also reflect conversational practices used to manage potentially contentious interactions with non‐vegans, in which individuals anticipate and navigate ‘flashpoints’ by moderating when and how they disclose their dietary identity. Recent qualitative work suggests that vegetarians and vegans actively employ such interactional strategies to minimise conflict, maintain social relationships and avoid stigma in discussions with meat‐eaters [39]. In our study, similar processes were evident, with participants strategically shaping conversations to maintain both social cohesion and a coherent sense of self across differing social contexts.

The second theme navigating vegan identity: community and social integration explored the dual influences of community support and social contexts on the wellbeing of vegan men. Participants emphasised the importance of engaging with vegan communities to address feelings of social isolation. This aligns with research suggesting that collective engagement, such as participation in vegan gatherings or communities, can provide emotional respite and reinforce shared identity among members of moralised minority groups [40]. Such spaces may help buffer against burnout, stigma and the emotional strain associated with maintaining a value‐driven lifestyle that differs from dominant norms. In our study, a sense of belonging validated the participants' ethical beliefs and supported their social identity. They demonstrated flexibility in how they discussed their veganism in different contexts and adapted their self‐presentation across different social situations [41]. Our findings highlight that participants often found support within vegan communities while navigating food‐centred social settings, consistent with research that shared group membership provides emotional support and validation [42]. Within vegan communities, participants found solidarity that helped them navigate varied social interactions. Importantly, for men in this study, these supportive spaces also appeared to provide an alternative context for expressing masculinity that diverged from dominant meat‐centric norms, suggesting that the benefits of community were not only social but also tied to the renegotiation of gender identity.

Theme 3: negotiating dietary boundaries: whole‐food and flexible veganism, revealed that the vegan community itself is not monolithic; rather, it contains internal divisions that shape identity and wellbeing. While such heterogeneity within vegan communities has been documented in existing literature, including distinctions based on health, ethics and lifestyle orientations [43], our findings build on this work by examining how these divisions are experienced and negotiated specifically by men. The tension between WFPB vegans and ‘flexible’ vegans reflects competing interpretations of what constitutes authentic veganism. WFPB adherents often framed their approach as the ‘gold standard’, emphasising purity, discipline and health optimisation. This framing aligns with identity‐enhancing strategies described in social identity theory, where individuals seek positive distinctiveness by elevating their subgroup's values [37]. In this study, these distinctions were not only about dietary preference but were also involved with gendered identity, where some participants drew on ideals of discipline, control, and bodily self‐regulation, traits often culturally associated with masculinity, to legitimise their positioning within the vegan hierarchy. For men, strict adherence to minimally processed foods was not only a health choice but also a marker of moral and personal superiority, reinforcing their sense of self as disciplined and principled.

Conversely, flexible vegans, those who incorporate processed vegan foods or prioritise convenience, were sometimes labelled ‘junk food vegans’, a term that carries implicit judgement and signals intra‐group boundary policing. Such dynamics suggest that while veganism offers a shared ideological foundation, subgroup differentiation can undermine solidarity and create micro‐level identity threats [44]. Importantly, for participants in this study, these tensions were navigated within the context of masculine identity, where alignment with particular dietary practices (e.g., WFPB) could function as a resource for performing a credible and socially recognisable form of masculinity within both vegan and non‐vegan contexts. Participants described feeling criticised or excluded within vegan spaces when their dietary practices did not align with WFPB ideals. This internal tension mirrors broader processes of normative influence and value signalling within lifestyle movements, where purity discourses can foster hierarchy and exclusion [16].

Importantly, these divisions may have implications for wellbeing. For WFPB vegans, the pursuit of dietary purity was often linked to feelings of empowerment and health gains, but also to perceptions of superiority that risk alienating others. For flexible vegans, judgement from stricter peers compounded the social isolation already experienced in omnivorous contexts, creating a ‘double marginalisation’. This dynamic highlights how identity negotiation occurs not only across vegan–non‐vegan boundaries but also within the vegan in‐group [45]. Future research could explore whether these intra‐group tensions influence long‐term adherence, community cohesion and mental health outcomes, particularly, for men navigating masculine norms alongside competing vegan ideals. Importantly, for men in this study, these supportive spaces also appeared to provide an alternative context for expressing masculinity that diverged from dominant meat‐centric norms, suggesting that the benefits of community were not only social but also tied to the renegotiation of gender identity. Taken together, our findings extend existing literature on heterogeneity within vegan movements by demonstrating how these internal distinctions are not only contested but are actively mobilised by men as part of ongoing masculine identity negotiation [43].

The fourth theme, close connections: strengthening personal bonds, highlighted the role of close relationships in supporting a vegan lifestyle. Participants emphasised that support from family, friends, and partners was essential for maintaining their vegan practices. These close relationships provided emotional support and helped participants navigate social interactions. From a social identity perspective, these relationships validate and reinforce participants' sense of self [46].

This phenomenological, semi‐structured interview study offers insights that may have implications for understanding vegan men's experiences. While our findings cannot support broad policy recommendations, they highlight how dietary choices can be meaningful expressions of values and identity for men. Our participants described both supportive experiences and challenges in social contexts, suggesting that recognition of diverse masculine expressions may be valuable in various settings. For healthcare professionals, awareness of how food choices can be tied to personal values and identity might enhance culturally sensitive care when discussing lifestyle and dietary practices [13]. The experiences shared by participants suggest that social support networks are important for men navigating lifestyle choices that differ from mainstream practices. Future research with larger, more diverse samples could further explore whether vegan men experience unique challenges that warrant specific support approaches or interventions. This study contributes to a growing body of literature on masculinity and food choices [23], highlighting the need for nuanced understanding of how men express values through dietary practices.

The strengths of this study include its reflexive thematic and phenomenological approach, providing in‐depth insights into the lived experiences of Australian vegan men. However, the study has limitations, such as potential self‐selection bias as participants were recruited from vegan networks and social media communities. Vegan men who were less socially connected may not have been exposed to our study recruitment. The Australian context likely influenced our findings in several important ways. Australia's reported high meat consumption and barbecue culture (Cheng and Liu 2018) may present unique social dynamics for vegan men compared to countries with more established plant‐based traditions. Additionally, Australian expressions of masculinity, particularly those connected to outdoor cooking and meat consumption, may create specific contexts for negotiating vegan practices. These cultural particularities suggest that vegan men's experiences may vary considerably across different national and cultural settings. While we sought to include Australian men from diverse geographical and socioeconomic regions, the findings may not be generalisable to vegan men from different geographical locations, cultural backgrounds, or regions. The specific Australian context regarding food practices and masculinity expressions may also differ from other countries and cultures. It is also important to acknowledge that some experiences described by participants may reflect broader aspects of vegan identity rather than being exclusively linked to masculinity. However, our analysis suggests that participants' positioning as men shaped how these experiences were interpreted, expressed, and navigated within their social contexts.

Future research could build on this study in several ways. Quantitative approaches could validate these themes across larger samples, particularly using specific measures of masculine ideologies and values to explore how masculinity is expressed through dietary choices. Studies using gendered‐specific tools to measure masculine values and strengths could further illuminate how vegan men perceive and express their masculine identity through food choices. Longitudinal studies could explore how these expressions of identity evolve over time, while cross‐cultural research could examine how different social contexts influence the relationship between veganism and masculinity.

This study explored how Australian vegan men perceive and navigate their social identities within their social relationships. Our findings suggest these men navigate their social relationships by integrating ethical values with expressions of masculinity. Social identity theory helps us understand how they find meaningful connections within vegan communities while managing diverse social contexts. Participants' experiences reflect the bidirectional nature of identity formation—their sense of self is shaped by social contexts while also reflecting personal values and choices. Taken together, these findings have important implications for health promotion. They highlight that dietary practices among men are not solely driven by knowledge or individual behaviour change, but are also shaped by identity processes, social norms, and gendered expectations. Interventions that fail to account for the role of masculinity in shaping food choices may therefore have limited effectiveness. Instead, health promotion strategies may benefit from engaging with masculine identities more directly, by reframing plant‐based diets in ways that align with values such as strength, performance and self‐discipline, or by supporting alternative, health‐oriented forms of masculinity. Such approaches may help create more inclusive and socially resonant pathways for promoting healthier and more sustainable dietary behaviours among men.
