Aging-related signature stratifies LUAD prognosis and uncovers senescence-induced chemoresistance via NF-κB/RELA activation
Authors: Chengyue Zhu, Ying Dong, Yue Dong, Xinyu Shang, Jintong Dong, Tingting Ge, Zhijun Jie, Jia Li, Yi Zang, Jindong Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common subtype of lung cancer and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Aging has emerged as a significant risk factor for LUAD, with evidence suggesting that age-associated biological changes may influence prognosis. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms linking aging to tumor progression and therapy resistance are not fully understood.MethodsWe integrated transcriptomic data from LUAD cohorts to identify aging-related genes that are differentially expressed and associated with prognosis. Then Cox regression was utilized to establish an aging-related signature. Patients were stratified based on the signature, and we examined survival differences and transcription factor expression between groups. In vitro, cellular senescence was induced in A549 cells by bleomycin treatment, and subsequent changes in gene expression, transcription factor activity, and chemoresistance were assessed. Mechanistic studies included RNA interference and inhibitor treatment to investigate the dependence of chemoresistance on specific signaling pathways.ResultsA six-gene (H2AX, IGFBP1, CCL20, TFAP2A, TNFRSF11A, TNFRSF13C) aging-related signature was identified, stratifying patients into low- and high-risk groups with distinct overall survival outcomes. High-risk patients exhibited significantly poorer survival. Transcriptomic and protein analyses revealed that the transcription factor RELA was specifically upregulated in high-risk and senescent cells. Functional studies confirmed that induction of cellular senescence increased RELA expression, NF-κB signaling activation and conferred resistance to paclitaxel. Notably, genetic knockdown or inhibition of NF-κB signaling restored chemosensitivity in senescent tumor cells without reversing the senescent phenotype itself.ConclusionsThis study established a clinically relevant aging-related signature for risk stratification in LUAD and provides mechanistic insights into how aging-driven cellular senescence promotes chemoresistance through RELA/NF-κB pathway activation. Targeting NF-κB signaling may represent a promising therapeutic approach to overcome senescence-associated chemoresistance and improve outcomes for LUAD patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-025-03355-x.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma, Aging, Cellular senescence, Chemoresistance

Background

Lung cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide, with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) as the predominant subtype [1, 2]. Over recent decades, LUAD diagnosis and treatment have improved. However, the 5-year survival rate has increased only modestly and remains less than 15%, indicating that the etiology of LUAD is more sophisticated than we formerly appreciated [3]. Therefore, there is a pressing need to identify biomarkers and elucidate molecular mechanisms.

Aging is a major risk factor for LUAD, with incidence and mortality rising sharply with advancing age [4, 5]. Cohort studies indicate that individuals with accelerated biological aging have a significantly higher lung cancer incidence compared to age-matched peers [6]. Additionally, the association between aging and prognosis has drawn considerable attention among researchers. Zhang et al. and Ru et al. independently developed aging-related signatures through distinct analytical approaches, achieving accurate stratification of patients according to prognostic risk [7, 8]. However, their studies relied largely on bioinformatics-based evidence without further experimental validation of phenotype or mechanistic insights into how aging affects LUAD prognosis. Furthermore, gene-expression signatures inherently possess limitations, notably due to methodological variations in sequencing and batch effects, making it challenging to establish a universal threshold for patient classification. Consequently, aging-related signatures hold greater potential for mechanistic exploration than direct clinical application. Aging is accompanied by chronic inflammation in the tissue microenvironment, primarily resulting from the progressive accumulation of senescent cells that secrete senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors [9]. Cellular senescence refers to a stable state of cell cycle arrest triggered by diverse stressors, including DNA damage, telomere shortening, and oxidative stress. Senescent cells are present in diverse tumor types, where persistent SASP actively reshapes the tumor microenvironment [10]. This remodeling contributes to angiogenesis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition and metastasis [11]. However, the characteristic phenotypes and underlying molecular mechanisms specifically induced by cellular senescence in lung cancer cells remain poorly understood.

In this study, we characterized the expression patterns and clinical prognostic value of aging-related signature developed with our own strategy. Then, we identified transcription factors (TFs) that were aberrantly activated in the high-risk group. Finally, we established a cellular senescence model to validate the activation of these TFs, examine the resulting malignant phenotypes, and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Methods

Data collection

We downloaded transcriptome data of LUAD samples and normal samples from TCGA database. The corresponding clinical data of the cohort were obtained simultaneously. Specimens (for Cox regression) were excluded according to the criteria: (1) Normal para-cancer tissue; (2) Tissue fixed by paraformaldehyde; (3) Multiple samples from the same patient. We also downloaded an RNA array profile and corresponding clinical data (GSE31210) [12] as testing dataset from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. ChIP-seq data (GSE91020) were downloaded to analyze the target gene of RELA [13]. Finally, single-cell RNA sequence (scRNA-seq) dataset GSE189357 [14] was also downloaded for further research.

The aging-related genes (ARGs) were obtained from Aging Atlas Consortium (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/aging/index) [15] and showed in sTable 1. The List of 786 human TFs (sTable 2) was extracted from TRRUST database (http://www.grnpedia.org/trrust) [16].

We assessed the different protein expressions between LUAD tissues and normal tissues using immunohistochemistry (IHC) figures obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/).

Bulk RNA-seq and RNA array analysis

For the TCGA cohort, the mRNA raw count data of each sample were collated into a matrix and normalized with ‘DESeq2’ algorithm. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in LUAD tissue and normal tissue were then calculated with cutoff values of |log2 fold change| >1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. For the GEO dataset, normalized RNA expression values were then used to calculate risk scores in downstream analyses.

To investigate the biological function of genes, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed. We first obtained significantly enriched Gene Ontology (GO) terms and then visualized them in ridge plot (|Normalized enrichment score (NES)|>1, p-value < 0.05, and FDR q-value < 0.25). P values were adjusted with the BH methods.

scRNA-seq analysis

The Seurat v4.3 package was used for analysis of the GSE189357 dataset. Low-quality cells were filtered out if they expressed fewer than 200 genes, had more than 15% mitochondrial reads, or more than 5,000 unique gene counts. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on log-normalized and scaled data using the top 4,000 variable genes. The first 10 principal components were then used for clustering and visualized via t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE). Cell subpopulations were annotated according to canonical markers for different lung cell types. Copy number variations were inferred with the COPYKAT package to distinguish tumor from non-tumor cells [17]. Finally, the AUCell algorithm was applied to calculate single-cell pathway activation scores.

ChIP-seq analysis

Raw FASTQ files were downloaded and quality-checked using FastQC. Adapter trimming and low-quality base removal were performed using Trim Galore. The cleaned reads were then aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using Bowtie2 [18]. Peak calling was conducted with MACS2, which provided genomic peak locations and enrichment scores by quantitatively counting read coverage in each peak region. The resulting BAM files were converted to BigWig format and visualized using the UCSC Genome Browser.

Establishment of prognostic aging related signature

By using univariate Cox regression analysis, we identified genes markedly related to overall survival (OS). Then the selected genes were overlapped with DEGs and ARGs to narrow down the selection of genes. Multivariate Cox regression was performed to establish an aging-related signature. The risk score of each person was calculated according to the formula: risk score = Ʃ (βi × Expi). Expi represented the expression value while βi was the coefficient from the regression model. LUAD cases were divided into two groups: low- and high-risk groups.

For the LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression analysis, we used the ‘glmnet’ R package, setting the candidate genes as independent variables and patient outcome data as the dependent variable. The optimal value of λ was determined through 10-fold cross-validation. Based on this model, we identified five genes with non-zero coefficients that were considered statistically significant contributors to the prediction. The predictive performance of the resulting regression model was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.

Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves and ROC curves

The KM method was utilized to evaluate the association between OS and aging related signature. We also visualized the survival risk of single TF with KM curve. Besides, we conducted time-dependent ROC curve analyses to evaluate the predictive power of the gene signature. Area under the curve (AUC) were applied as a quantitative factor of predictive accuracy.

Protein-protein interactions network

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed by Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING version 11.5; https://string-db.org/). All genes were grouped by k-means clustering. We recombined the data with Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1).

Cell culture and treatment

A549 cells were cultured in RPMI media (11875093, Gibco Life Technologies, Oshima, New York) + 10% FBS + penicillin-streptomycin. Cellular senescence was induced by continuous treatment with bleomycin at 2.5 µg/mL (Z8020, Solarbio Life Science, Beijing) for 6 days. Senescent A549 cells were then treated with the NF-κB inhibitor JSH-23 (HY-13982, MCE, China) at 50 µM for 24 h before phenotypic experiments.

Western blot

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, Shanghai) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (HY-K0010, MCE, China) and 1 mM sodium orthovanadate (HY-D0852, MCE, China). Protein concentrations were determined by the BCA protein assay kit (P0009, Beyotime, Shanghai). Protein lysates (20 µg) were resolved by Tris-Glycine SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore, Billerica). All membranes were incubated with the indicated primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and were diluted in TBST (20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20) supplemented with 5% bovine serum albumin (A9418, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis). Primary and secondary antibodies were diluted 1:1000 (except GAPDH, 1:10,000) or 1:2500 in 5% BSA/TBST. Primary antibodies were detected with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies followed by exposure to ECL reagents (SQ201, Epizyme, Shanghai), and densities of bands were quantified by Image J software. The catalog of antibodies used is detailed in sTable 3.

RNA purification and polymerase chain reaction

Cells were treated with TRIzol (15596026, Invitrogen, Waltham). The subsequent purification process was carried out according to the instructions. Once the RNA purification was complete, we assessed the purity and concentration of the RNA using NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher, Waltham). For each sample, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using ABScript III RT Master Mix for qPCR (RK20428, Abclonal, Wuhan). The amplification step was completed by use of Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (RK21203, Abclonal, Wuhan). The 2−ΔΔCT method was used to calculate the results. The primer sequences were shown in the sTable 4.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) assay

We used SA-β-gal kits (C0602, Beyotime) to label senescent cells. Typically, cells were fixed with β-gal staining fixative solution at room temperature for 15 min. Subsequently, the cells were washed 3 times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then incubated with SA-β-gal staining solution at 37 °C overnight. SA-β-gal-positive cells (blue staining) were considered as senescent cells. The cells were then rinsed in 1x PBS, stained with DAPI for 15 min and mounted for observation.

SiRNA knockdown (KD)

A549 cells were plated in 100 mm dishes and grown to ~ 50% confluence. For each dish, 50 nM siRNA and 15 µL Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Thermo Fisher, USA) were each diluted in 250 µL Opti-MEM (31985062, Gibco, USA), mixed and incubated 10 min, then added dropwise to dishes containing antibiotic-free RPMI. After 24 h, medium was replaced with fresh complete RPMI. The siRNA sequences were shown in the sTable 5.

Paclitaxel (PTX) cytotoxicity assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 1E4 cells/well in RPMI-1640 + 10% FBS and allowed to adhere overnight. The following day, cells were treated with a gradient (10 nM, 25 nM, 50 nM, 100 nM, 1 µM, 10 µM, 25 µM and 50 µM) of PTX (P7930, Solarbio Life Science, Beijing) for 48 h. After treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (K1018, APExBIO, USA) was added to each well and plates were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured, and cell viability was calculated relative to untreated controls. Dose–response curves were plotted in GraphPad Prism, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) of PTX was determined.

Statistical analysis

Chi-Square Test (with or without Yate’s correction) or Fisher’s Exact Test was used for univariate comparison. Mean + SD (Standard deviation) or Median + IQR (Interquartile range) were applied to describe the distribution of numerical variables, and the choice of statistical methods depends on the conclusion of the Normality Test. We compared the differences between numerical variables by ANOVA Test, Welch’s Test or Wilcoxon Rank-Sum Test according to the results of Normality Test and Homogeneity of Variance Test.

All tests were two-tailed and P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Computations were carried out with R version 4.0.2.

Results

Identification of the prospective aging-signature genes

To investigate aging-associated transcriptional features in lung cancer, we first performed differential expression analysis on RNA-seq data from 489 TCGA-LUAD patients. This analysis identified 6523 DEGs. Subsequently, univariate Cox regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the prognostic significance of each gene, resulting in the identification of 5538 potential genes. By separately overlapping these two gene sets with the ARG List, we identified 82 aging-related DEGs and 132 aging-related Cox genes (sFigure 1A). Then the intersection of these two Lists produced 37 overlapping genes for further study (Fig. 1A). We then constructed a protein–protein interaction network from these 37 genes and applied k-means clustering, revealing three prominent clusters—among them, the cluster 1 (including PCNA, TOP2A, CDK1, etc.) displayed the strongest interconnectivity, indicative of DNA replication stress, a hallmark of senescent cells (Fig. 1B). To further characterize the biological significance of these clusters, we performed KEGG enrichment analyses separately for clusters 1 and 2. The analysis indicated that genes in cluster 1 were significantly associated with biological processes related to DNA homeostasis and the cell cycle. In contrast, among the top five enriched terms for cluster 2 was the “negative regulation of apoptotic signaling pathway,” suggesting a potential biological link between the senescent phenotype and the regulation of cell death (sFigure 1B). In Fig. 1C, Pearson’s correlation analysis confirmed that genes within this cluster not only physically interact but also exhibit highly correlated expression patterns, underscoring their potential regulatory roles in tumor biology. Differential expression patterns of these genes were visualized using a heatmap (Fig. 1D), revealing distinct gene expression shifts across multiple aging-related pathways. Univariate Cox regression analysis further quantified the prognostic relevance of each gene (Fig. 1E). Notably, several genes showed statistically significant associations with patient survival. For example, H2AFX (hazard ratios (HR) = 1.4707, P < 0.0001), involved in genomic instability, and IGFBP1 (HR = 1.2481, P < 0.0001), linked to senescence-associated phenotypes, were associated with poorer prognosis. In contrast, PIK3R2 (HR = 0.5613, P = 0.0332), related to intercellular communication, exhibited a protective effect. Genes were annotated by functional categories based on the Aging Atlas Consortium, reflecting their roles in aging-related processes. Collectively, this workflow preliminarily identifies an aging‐signature gene set with significant prognostic relevance in the TCGA lung cancer cohort, laying the groundwork for subsequent evaluation of its clinical utility.

Aging-related signature predicts the risk of mortality

Multivariate Cox regression narrowed the genes set into a six-gene aging signature. In Fig. 2A, the forest plot indicated that high expression of 5 genes correlated with a poor prognosis while the last one was a protective factor (Fig. 2A). The expression levels of the five proteins were significantly upregulated in tumor tissue (CCL20 data were not included in HPA database) (Fig. 2B). Although the images are from unmatched patients, we provide three representative immunohistochemical images per group for each of the signature proteins (sFigure 1 C). At the mRNA level, IGFBP1 did not reach statistical significance despite a clear upward trend, while the other five genes were significantly overexpressed in tumors (Fig. 2C). The risk score was calculated as follows: Risk Score = 0.3129 × [H2AX] + 0.2306 × [IGFBP1] + 0.1080 × [CCL20] + 0.2125 × [TFAP2A] + 0.2235 × [TNFRSF11A] – 0.1730 × [TNFRSF13C]. All patients were stratified into low- and high-risk groups (n = 244 and 245, respectively) according to the median cut-off value (8.2012), with the distribution of risk scores presented in Fig. 2D. The detailed clinical characteristics are summarized in sTable 6. As shown in Fig. 2E, within the low-risk group (to the left of the dashed line), the ratio of alive to death was 182:62, whereas in the high-risk group, this ratio was significantly lower at 130:115, indicating a substantially higher mortality rate (p < 0.0001). Additionally, the median follow-up duration in the low-risk cohort was 2.03 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 1.32–3.52 years), significantly longer than that observed in the high-risk cohort, at 1.59 years (IQR: 0.822–2.6 years; p < 0.0001). KM analysis demonstrated a clear survival disadvantage for the high-risk cohort compared to the low-risk group, with cumulative 5-year survival rates of 0.27 and 0.54, respectively (Fig. 2F, sTable 7; p < 0.0001). The predictive capability of the risk model was further assessed in Fig. 2G by time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, yielding area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.699, 0.733, and 0.706 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively, thus confirming robust predictive performance. To validate the robustness of this aging-related signature, we applied the same workflow to independent GEO dataset (GSE31210). Expression patterns of all six genes were consistent with the TCGA findings (sFigure 2A). As shown in sFigure 2B, patients were also stratified into groups by median risk score (7.6550), and their clinical characteristics are summarized in sTable 8, where high-risk cases showed significantly higher pathologic grade (P < 0.001). In sFigure 2C, the ratio of alive to death in the low-risk group was 101:6, whereas in the high-risk group it was 81:27, indicating a significantly lower mortality rate in the low-risk group (P = 0.0002). Additionally, the median follow-up time in the low-risk group was longer than that in the high-risk group. KM analysis further confirmed a survival advantage in the low-risk group, with 5-year cumulative survival rates of 0.94 and 0.75 in the low- and high-risk groups, respectively (sFigure 2D, sTable 7). The classification performance of the aging-related signature in the GEO cohort was supported by ROC analysis, which yielded AUCs of 0.854, 0.732, and 0.746 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively (sFigure 2E).

Tissues from high-risk patients performed aberrant TF activity

To explore the Link between elevated risk scores and poorer outcomes, we performed differential expression analysis between low- and high-risk tumor samples, uncovering 531 up-regulated and 715 down-regulated genes in the high-risk group (Fig. 3A). In Fig. 3B, GSEA highlighted significant alterations in transcription-related biological processes, pointing to shifts in TF activity. To pinpoint key regulators, we first retrieved TF list from TRRUST database and analyzed the Spearman correlation between TFs and risk scores (Cut-off: |r| >0.3, P value < 0.05). Prognostic TFs were also screened by univariate Cox regression. We overlapped the subsets to get the hub TFs—RELA, RFXAP, and LDB1 (Fig. 3C). In high-risk tissues, RELA was significantly up-regulated while RFXAP and LDB1 were down-regulated (Fig. 3D). KM curves and correlation scatter plots confirmed that each of these factors was significantly associated with overall survival and risk score (Fig. 3E). Importantly, these regulatory signatures were independently validated in external GEO cohort. LDB1 showed no significant difference, while the expression of the other two genes was consistent with TCGA (sFigure 2 F). However, how alterations in TF activity that are associated with elevated risk scores contribute to poorer prognosis remains unclear. Among these TFs, RELA, which encodes the RelA protein (also known as p65), forms the most common NF-κB complex and plays a role in transcription initiation [19], enhancing NF-κB DNA-binding activity [20], and regulating a broad spectrum of essential cellular processes [21]. RFXAP is a critical TF involved in immune responses [22, 23]. In contrast, LDB1 functions as a transcriptional co-factor and architectural protein; it does not directly bind DNA but is recruited to cis-regulatory elements (CREs) via tissue-specific DNA-binding proteins [24]. As a next step, we will establish a cellular senescence model to simulate these aging effects at the cellular level.

Senescence promotes chemoresistance and increases RELA expression

We induced senescence in A549 cells via chronic bleomycin treatment, which significantly elevated mRNA levels of the genes encoding the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (CDKN1A and CDKN2A), as well as those encoding the key SASP factors (IL6, TNF, and MMP1) (Fig. 4A). Correspondingly, bleomycin-treated cells exhibited a significant increase in SA-β-gal staining, confirming successful senescence induction (Fig. 4B). In Fig. 4C, these senescent cells showed significantly enhanced resistance to PTX, offering a potential explanation for the poorer outcomes seen in patients with high-risk scores (sTable 9). Examination of expression levels of the three candidate TFs revealed that RELA was substantially upregulated at both the mRNA (Fig. 4D) and protein levels (Fig. 4E) in senescent cells, whereas RFXAP and LDB1 exhibited expression trends opposite to those seen in the omics analysis (Figs. 3D and 4D-E). Consequently, we prioritized investigation of RELA’s role in linking cellular senescence to tumor chemoresistance.

RELA KD enhances chemosensitivity without affecting senescence

To determine whether RELA specifically mediates chemoresistance and senescence independently, we designed and synthesized three siRNAs targeting RELA. Transfection of A549 cells with these siRNAs produced robust RELA KD at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5A, B). Because senescence induction by bleomycin requires continuous treatment for six days, we performed a time-course experiment to define the window of siRNA efficacy. RELA suppression was stable between days 2–4 post-transfection, thus a second siRNA transfection was administered on day 3 to maintain KD (Fig. 5C). Figure 5D shows that RELA depletion lowered the IC₅₀ for PTX, indicating improved drug sensitivity (sTable 9). Importantly, assessment of senescence markers (p21, p53) showed no reduction upon RELA KD (Fig. 5E), nor was there any decrease in β-gal staining intensity (Fig. 5F). Together, these data demonstrate that RELA KD reverses chemoresistance in senescent tumor cells without impairing the established senescent state.

Senescence mediates chemoresistance via activation of the NF-κB signaling

To define RELA’s role in senescence-driven chemoresistance in vivo, we analyzed lung adenocarcinoma scRNA-seq data (GSE189357). First, we extracted epithelial cells and performed dimensionality reduction and clustering to resolve major subpopulations (Fig. 6A, B). Our analysis revealed multiple alveolar type II (AT II) subclusters; notably, AT II cells are recognized as a principal cell-of-origin in lung adenocarcinoma. Malignant epithelial clusters were then identified via copy-number variation inference (Fig. 6C). Using AUCell scoring for senescence hallmarks (R-HSA-2559583), we mapped senescent cells and found that they spatially co-localized with the tumor cell clusters (Fig. 6D). In parallel, cells with high NF-κB pathway activity—scored by a curated NF-κB gene set (R-HSA-209560)—overlapped the same regions, implicating RELA (which encodes NF-κB protein p65) in this phenotype (Fig. 6D). Supporting this observation, quantitative analysis of the co-localized cell populations revealed that 74.9% of the cells shared identical classification based on senescence and NF-κB pathway scoring, underscoring their concordance at single-cell resolution (sFigure 3 A). Consistent with this, bleomycin‐induced senescent A549 cells showed markedly increased phosphorylation of IκK and p65, confirming NF-κB activation (Fig. 6E). Finally, treatment with the NF-κB inhibitor JSH-23 restored PTX sensitivity in senescent cells without reversing the β-gal positivity (Fig. 6F, G, sTable 9). These data demonstrate that senescence‐associated chemoresistance in lung tumor cells is mediated by RELA‐dependent NF-κB signaling, while the senescent program itself remains intact. To further delineate RELA downstream effectors, we analyzed a public RELA ChIP-seq dataset (GSE91020). Following lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation, we observed TSS-centered enrichment of RELA occupancy (sFigure 3B) and apoptosis pathway over-representation (hsa04215, p = 0.0058). Notably, RELA bound promoter regions of BIRC2/BIRC3 and BCL2, canonical anti-apoptotic genes, and binding was also detected at FADD, NGFR, and BBC3 (sFigure 3 C), suggesting a balance between pro- and anti-apoptotic signaling downstream of RELA (Fig. 6H).

In non-senescent cells, low RELA/NF-κB activity allows chemotherapy-induced apoptosis; in senescent cells, RELA activation upregulates NF-κB target genes and promotes survival, leading to chemoresistance

Discussion

Cellular senescence influences the progression of many age-related diseases by secreting a SASP that remodels the microenvironment and alters the function of neighboring cells [25, 26]. Recent studies—such as those by Zhang, Ru, and Xu et al.—have each developed aging‐related gene signatures that predict LUAD prognosis with high accuracy [7, 8, 27]. However, these models remain limited to clinical prediction: none of the teams pursued mechanistic follow-up, and the technical variability and batch effects inherent in RNA-seq data hinder the establishment of universally applicable risk thresholds, thus constraining their practical utility. In our study, the median value was used as the cutoff in each dataset, resulting in different thresholds across cohorts. Although this approach is common in transcriptome studies, it fundamentally restricts clinical translatability. The lack of standardized cutoff values stems largely from technical challenges in RNA-seq analysis, including batch effects and the absence of universally accepted normalization protocols. Even for the same gene, expression values can vary by orders of magnitude across different platforms or laboratories, making it difficult to apply published signatures directly across institutions [28, 29]. Molania et al. have reported that even within the same study, expression values fluctuate substantially due to differences in library size and sequencing platforms, impacting cross-cohort portability [30]. Although external RNA spike-in normalization is an attractive route to cross-sample calibration, their routine use remains limited and there is no field-wide consensus protocol. More specifically, when spike-ins are added in known amounts and processed alongside endogenous RNA, they enable estimation of sample-specific scaling factors, placing gene-level expression on an anchored scale across batches and platforms; this supports more stable model training and the use of fixed, transferable risk-score thresholds. Algorithmically, ComBat performs empirical-Bayes batch correction on expression matrix when batch labels are known [31]. Remove Unwanted Variation (RUV) removes unwanted variation by estimating latent factors from control genes or replicate samples and regressing them out [30]. Both methods work well in practice but are sensitive to biology–batch confounding or quality control; thus, where high-quality spike-ins are available, spike-in–anchored calibration remains a promising complementary strategy.

Furthermore, while LASSO regression is widely used to enhance predictive performance, it may introduce bias in variable selection. LASSO is particularly useful when the number of candidate predictors far exceeds the number of samples, as it helps avoid overfitting in high-dimensional datasets [32]. In contrast, our study narrowed down the candidate gene set to 37 before multivariate modeling, with a training cohort of 489 cases, of which 180 experienced death events. The final model incorporated six variables, resulting in an event-per-variable ratio of 30, which exceeds 10 and supports the suitability of traditional Cox regression in this context. The robustness of our model is further supported by its performance in an external validation cohort (sFigure 2). For comparison, we also applied LASSO-Cox regression using the same 37 candidate genes (sFigure 4 A). Under 10-fold cross-validation, the optimal λ value corresponded to the model with minimal bias (sFigure 4B), selecting only a small number of genes, which indicates their strong predictive relevance. The resulting LASSO-Cox model included five genes, which overlapped with the original model (sFigure 4 C). Risk classification using the LASSO model was largely consistent with our original model, with concordant predictions in 431 out of 489 cases (Fig. 2G, sFigure 4D). However, ROC analysis showed that the predictive performance of the LASSO model was inferior to that of the traditional Cox model (sFigure 4D), reinforcing the validity of our modeling strategy. Khadirnaikar et al. constructed aging related genetic background by comparing the differential genes of lung fibroblasts induced by ionizing radiation or drugs [33]. Subsequently they identified a subtype of LUAD with reduced Wnt signaling and high drug resistance. However, the aging gene set here is too limited to be widely accepted. By combining this robust analytic framework with an aging-related gene list drawn from a trusted database, we achieved accurate survival predictions—and we will now leverage the key variables identified by our Cox model for subsequent mechanistic investigations.

In our study, we systematically investigated the expression of 500 ARGs in LUAD tumor tissues and their associations with OS. The Aging gene set was obtained from a newly emerging Aging Atlas Database where the data was proven to be trustworthy. A novel prognostic model integrating 6 ARGs (H2AX, IGFBP1, CCL20, TFAP2A, TNFRSF11A, TNFRSF13C) was established and validated in an external cohort (sFigure 2 A). The six aging signature genes we identified were also mentioned in other studies about human tumors. H2AX is a type of histone protein and could be phosphorylated into γ-H2AX when double-strand breaks appear [34]. Yang et al. identified H2AX as a prognostic marker for relapse-free survival in LUAD [35]. Pan et al. suggested that H2AX could influence the efficacy of radiotherapy by affecting DNA damage repair [36]. Activation of DNA damage response and the increase in H2AX foci are important features of cellular senescence according to previous studies [37]. CCL20 and IGFBP1 were reported as related factors about autophagy and inflammation in the development of LUAD [38, 39]. IGFBP1 was found to be positively correlated with age and might be an adaptive response to counter senescence through Akt signaling [40, 41]. In our research, there was no statistical difference in the IGFBP1 expression levels between the two groups, but an upward trend could still be observed in the tumor group. TNFRSF11A and TNFRSF13C were members of the TNF receptor superfamily [42, 43]. Both would be involved in immunoreaction in LUAD. Furthermore, TFAP2A can induce KRT16 and PAG9 to potentiate the metastasis and EMT of LUAD. Hänzelmann et al. found a significant alteration of histone methylation during long-term culture of human fibroblasts and TFAP2A was significantly enriched in methylated regions [44].

Alterations in the transcriptional profile reflect shifts in cellular transcriptional programs, often driven by changes in TFs. We identified three TFs—RELA, RFXAP, and LDB1—that correlate with the risk score, yet only RELA exhibited consistent changes in our senescent cell assays. RELA binds NF-κB1 to form the canonical p65/p50 heterodimer, which translocates to the nucleus to regulate target gene expression. Its activation has been documented in LUAD and is regarded as a critical effector of oncogenic KRAS [45]. In our cohort, risk scores positively correlated with RELA mRNA levels, and patients with elevated RELA expression experienced significantly worse overall survival. At the cellular level, senescent A549 cells showed both upregulation and increased phosphorylation of p65. Interestingly, when PTX-sensitive A549 cells were driven into senescence, they acquired marked resistance to PTX—an effect that may underlie the poorer prognosis observed in high-risk patients. Genetic ablation of RELA or treatment with JSH-23, an NF-κB inhibitor, effectively reversed this drug-resistant phenotype without altering the senescent state itself, indicating that RELA activation and NF-κB signaling function as effectors of cellular senescence rather than drivers. During our research, we noted substantial variability in control-group IC₅₀ values across independent experiments, likely attributable to differences in cell passage number—this represents a limitation of our study. Nevertheless, the reproducible phenotypic shifts observed within each passage-matched experimental set support the reliability of our findings. Mechanistically, the study supports a model in which RELA promotes senescence-associated chemoresistance by upregulating anti-apoptotic effectors. RELA ChIP–seq identified six potential target genes. Among them, BIRC2 and BIRC3 have been reported to encode the human inhibitor of apoptosis (IAP) proteins cIAP1 and cIAP2, respectively [46, 47], which suppress apoptosis. BCL2 is known to inhibit apoptosis by preventing the release of cytochrome C from mitochondria [48]. Consistently, both RELA KD and NF-κB inhibition restored PTX sensitivity without reversing senescence, supporting the RELA-(BIRC2/BIRC3, BCL2)-chemoresistance axis. In contrast, FADD, NGFR, and BBC3 have been reported to promote apoptosis [49–51], suggesting that RELA may modulate the balance between pro- and anti-apoptotic signaling via transcriptional control. As the ChIP–seq was generated under LPS stimulation in a non-LUAD context, we consider it orthogonal evidence and prioritize targeted ChIP–qPCR in LUAD models for future work.

Finally, we applied the TIDE (Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion) model to assess patients’ responsiveness to immunotherapy [52]. Correlation analysis revealed a very weak association between the risk score and TIDE (R = 0.164), with correlation coefficients between the risk score and various immune features all below 0.6 (sTable 10). These findings indicate that the aging-related chemoresistance mechanism captured by our signature is not dependent on immune features. In addition, the in vitro experiments were performed only in A549 cells. Although A549 is widely used in lung cancer research, reliance on a single cell line limits the generalizability of the findings. We plan to validate our conclusions across multiple LUAD cell lines (e.g. H1299) in the future.

Conclusions

In conclusion, by integrating conventional bioinformatic analyses with the application of an aging-related signature for patient risk stratification and validating findings in senescence models, we established the clinical significance of this signature in LUAD and uncovered a NF-κB–mediated, senescence-driven mechanism of tumor chemoresistance.
