TIPs: a deep learning-guided proteogenomic framework to expand the landscape of transposable element-derived antigens with immunopeptidomics
Authors: Qian Wu, Xinyue Zhou, Qizhen Feng, Zixiang Shang, Jiayi Shen, Xiaoxiang Huang, Xiaobing Liu, Wenguang Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transposable elements (TEs) represent an abundant and important source of HLA-presented antigens, but their immunopeptidomic characterization remains challenging due to the inflated search space. We present TIPs (TE-derived Immunopeptidomic Search), a deep learning-guided proteogenomic framework that integrates de novo sequencing, database refinement, multiple search engines and stringent FDR controls. Across various cell lines and cancer types, TIPs identified 20-fold more TE-derived peptides on average than conventional approaches. It further revealed many recurrent, tumor-specific antigens from TEs, including candidates induced by epigenetic therapy. These findings highlight the potential of TIPs to expand the antigenic landscape beyond canonical sources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-026-04191-y.

Background

As a promising class of cancer treatments, T cell-based cancer immunotherapies rely on the recognition of antigens presented on the surface of tumor cells by human leukocyte antigen (HLA) molecules [1]. Characterizing and understanding the nature and extent of antigen presentation is therefore essential for the development of targeted immunotherapies, including personalized vaccines and engineered T-cell therapies [2, 3]. Recent advances in immunopeptidomics, particularly through mass spectrometry (MS) coupled with novel experimental and computational methods, enable the direct and high-throughput identification of HLA-bound peptides, thereby facilitating the large-scale discovery of various types of naturally presented tumor antigens [4–6]. While early research focused on canonical tumor antigens arising from protein-coding regions of the genome, recent studies have increasingly highlighted the importance of non-canonical antigens, originating from transcripts of putative non-protein-coding regions or dysregulated transcriptomic, translational, and antigen-processing mechanisms in cancer [7–13]. Being tumor-specific and shared across patients, non-canonical antigens are attractive targets for allogeneic “off-the-shelf” immunotherapies [13].

Transposable elements (TEs) are repetitive genomic sequences that collectively account for nearly half of the human genome. They are increasingly recognized as an important source of non-canonical antigens in cancer [11, 14]. Based on transposition mechanism and evolutionary origin, TEs are commonly categorized into five major classes, including long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs), short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs), long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons, DNA transposons, and composite families such as SINE–VNTR–Alu (SVA) elements [15, 16]. Each class contains multiple subfamilies that vary in genomic copy number, evolutionary age, and sequence homology [17]. These differences shape how readily individual elements become transcriptionally active and, in turn, can influence the repertoire of TE-derived peptides presented by HLA molecules.

In tumors, widespread TEs, once activated, can generate numerous cryptic or alternative promoters, leading to the transcription of otherwise silent genomic regions [18, 19]. These transcripts may encode tumor-specific unannotated proteins; either TE–gene chimeric proteins (originating from the fusion of TE and other gene sequences) or TE-derived proteins (originating solely from TE sequences), any of which may give rise to immunogenic antigens [14, 20, 21]. Evidence is accumulating that antigens arising from various TE subfamilies are an important class of tumor-specific antigens (TSAs) with demonstrated immunogenicity [16, 22, 23]. Despite the potential, accurately and sensitively identifying TE-derived antigens remains challenging. Current computational pipelines are not optimized for detecting TE-derived peptides, and no dedicated tool exists to specifically profile the TE immunopeptidome. Moreover, TE-derived peptides, similar to other non-canonical peptides, suffer from computational challenges in immunopeptidomic analysis. These include the immense size of the non-canonical search space and the incompletely characterized fragmentation patterns in mass spectrometry, which collectively hinder comprehensive profiling of the TE-derived immunopeptidome [11, 23, 24]. As a result, the nature and extent of TE antigen presentation—both in tumors and following treatment with epigenetic agents—remain largely unexplored. In particular, decitabine (DAC), a DNA methyltransferase inhibitor that promotes DNA hypomethylation and reactivates epigenetically silenced TEs [21], provides a tractable system to investigate how TE derepression alters the TE-derived immunopeptidome.

To address these questions, we developed and benchmarked a new computational method, TIPs (TE-derived Immunopeptidomic Search), designed for the comprehensive detection of TE-derived peptides in immunopeptidomic data. TIPs integrated de novo sequencing powered by deep learning with a refined and TE-centric database search strategy, to sensitively identify antigens while controlling the false discovery rate (FDR) accurately. TIPs enhanced the sensitivity of TE detection by more than 20-fold over existing approaches. By applying TIPs to several representative immunopeptidomic datasets, we provided the first in-depth characterization of the TE immunopeptidome in glioblastoma, lung tumor, and melanoma. We delineated the respective contributions of coding and non-coding TEs, compared the antigen repertoires of native tumors and drug-treated cells, and investigated how genomic features shaped TE antigen presentation. Complementing previous studies of TE-chimeric peptides, this work reveals a more complete landscape of TE-derived antigens, suggesting an important but underappreciated source of potential targets for cancer immunotherapy [14, 20].

Results

TIPs, a proteogenomic workflow enabling accurate and sensitive detection of TE-derived peptides

The abundance and sequence diversity of TEs make them an attractive source of tumor antigens [16]. Advances in TE genomics have enabled the construction of a comprehensive TE protein database from genomic annotations, and the resulting TE protein database can then be used for immunopeptidomic analysis [25, 26]. However, the weak correlation between transcript abundance and antigen presentation necessitates the inclusion of a vast number of potential transcripts, independent of their abundance in the database [11]. This practice leads to a drastically inflated database size for proteogenomic searches, which in turn reduces the sensitivity and specificity of TE identification.

To address the challenge of database inflation, we developed TIPs, a proteogenomic workflow that is dedicated to precise and sensitive profiling of the TE immunopeptidome. TIPs improved the sensitivity and specificity of TE-derived peptide identification by refining the search space using experimental evidence (Fig. 1A). Step 1: construction of an inflated TE reference database

We first built a comprehensive (“inflated”) TE protein database by extracting TE-annotated genomic regions and translating them in all six reading frames, followed by stop-codon splitting and length filtering (≥ 8 aa), independent of transcript abundance or prior evidence of translation. Each entry was annotated with TE class/subfamily information to enable downstream stratified analyses. While this comprehensive database maximizes theoretical coverage, the resulting database size reduces search sensitivity and inflates false discoveries in conventional proteogenomic searches.

Step 2: extraction of high-confidence sequence tags

We next applied deep learning–based de novo sequencing methods to immunopeptidomic MS/MS spectra to extract high-confidence peptide sequence tags. Importantly, the extracted short sequences do not by themselves determine peptide origin or ensure TE derivation; instead, they serve as unbiased tags that guide downstream database refinement. We benchmarked and used three tools (Casanovo [27], InstaNovo [28], and PepNet [29]) in parallel and integrated their sequence tags to take advantage of the different tools and to maximize coverage (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A).

Step 3: generation of a sample-specific TE database

High-confidence de novo tags were mapped to the inflated TE protein database, and TE protein entries supported by at least one tag match were retained to generate a sample-specific streamlined TE database. This refinement step substantially reduced database size while preserving TE candidates supported by experimental measurements (i.e., tandem mass spectra) (Fig. 1A).

Step 4: database search, statistical validation and integration

The streamlined TE database was appended to the canonical human proteome to enable simultaneous identification and group-wise FDR control. We then searched the MS/MS data against the database using MSFragger [30], MS-GF+ [31], and Comet [32], followed by PeptideProphet [33, 34] for statistical validation and iProphet [35] for integration. Because TE-derived and canonical identifications are highly imbalanced and exhibit distinct target–decoy characteristics (Additional file 1: Fig. S1B, C), we applied grouped FDR control separately for TE-derived versus canonical identifications at the peptide level. Finally, we used MixMHCpred [36] to predict HLA binding affinities.

Our analysis revealed that TIPs was able to integrate the reliable signals from all three search engines, as indicated by its ability to identify more peptides with elevated HLA binding affinities (Fig. 1B, C and Additional file 1: Fig. S1D). Binding affinity predictions revealed that 83.4% of TE-derived peptides identified by TIPs were classified as binders (Fig. 1D).

TIPs demonstrated higher sensitivity and reliability in identifying TE-derived peptides

To evaluate performance, we compared TIPs with NewAnce, a method specialized for identifying the non-canonical immunopeptidome [11]. Across a diverse set of cell lines and cancer types (Additional file 2: Table S1), TIPs identified, on average, 20-fold more TE-derived peptides than NewAnce (Fig. 2A and B), a substantial improvement achieved under the same group-specific FDR control strategy. Importantly, this significant increase in sensitivity did not compromise quality, as the proportion of HLA binders was nearly identical between the methods (TIPs: 83.4% vs. NewAnce: 83.3%; Fig. 2C and Additional file 1: Fig. S2A), collectively affirming the accuracy and effectiveness of the TIPs method. To further illustrate these advantages, we conducted a case study using the 0D5P sample (melanoma; DAC vs control; PXD013649; see Additional file 2: Table S1). Among the 11 TE-derived peptides originally reported by NewAnce, merely two were experimentally validated by mass spectrometry using synthetic peptides. Using a significantly smaller database, TIPs successfully captured both validated peptides and discovered 187 additional novel TE-derived peptides (Fig. 2D). Consistent with these findings, comparative analysis showed that the average peptide-spectrum match (PSM) score for peptides uniquely identified by NewAnce was lower than that for peptides detected by TIPs (Additional file 1: Fig. S2B), further confirming the enhanced reliability of the TIPs method. To assess the reliability of these identifications, we performed a multi-dimensional validation. Specifically, TIPs-identified peptides exhibited reliable mass spectral features, expected physicochemical properties, and characteristic HLA-I binding properties (Additional file 1: Fig. S2C-H). In addition to its sensitivity and reliability, TIPs also demonstrated improved computational efficiency. For instance, in sample T1185B (melanoma; interferon-γ (IFN-γ) vs control; PXD013649; see Additional file 2: Table S1), the search space of TIPs (i.e., the number of peptides considered during database searching) was merely 39% of that used by NewAnce. Despite this substantial reduction in computational and statistical burden, TIPs successfully identified 79.1% (19/24) of the TE-derived peptides identified by NewAnce, while additionally uncovering 488 novel TE-derived peptides (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A and B). To determine the source of the improved performance afforded by TIPs, we compared the score distributions of target and decoy PSMs between the methods. Using the NewAnce-derived TE database, the resulting target and decoy PSM score distributions of search engines showed poor separation (Additional file 1: Fig. S4), particularly in the high-score range. In contrast, using the TIPs-derived streamlined TE database by the same search engines with the same set of parameters, high-scoring target PSMs were clearly separated from decoys. This superior discrimination yielded more reliable results at the same FDR threshold (Fig. 2E).

To further validate the gained TE-derived peptides by TIPs, we investigated their sequence similarities and biochemical properties. TE-derived peptides identified by TIPs exhibited biochemical characteristics indistinguishable from those of canonical peptides (Fig. 2F, G and Additional file 1: Fig. S5A-C), a finding consistent with previous studies on other types of non-canonical peptides (Additional file 3: Supplementary Note 2) [13]. This consistency was repeatedly observed across multiple orthogonal dimensions: their hydrophobicity strongly correlated with retention time; peptide length, HLA-binding score distribution, and spectral counts (i.e., the number of spectra identified to the same peptide) were all comparable to those of canonical peptides. Their biological role as potential HLA ligands was further supported by matching binding motifs and co-clustering in uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis. Together, these results provided robust validation for the TE-derived peptides identified by TIPs.

Genomic drivers shaped the TE-derived immunopeptidome

By applying the TIPs method to immunopeptidomic datasets, it was possible to significantly expand the repertoire of TE-derived peptides. Its sensitivity and accuracy allowed for the direct, comprehensive analysis of TE presentation across different TE classes and subfamilies, within and among individual samples (Fig. 3A and Additional file 2: Table S1). To explore the presentation of TE-derived peptides across diverse biological contexts, we analyzed three malignancies (glioblastoma, non-small-cell lung carcinoma, and melanoma) using both patient tissues and cell lines. At the TE class level, the relative abundance of TE-derived peptides followed a consistent order across cancer types: LINEs were the most abundant, followed by LTRs, SINEs, DNA, and SVAs. This order largely correlated with the genomic prevalence of each TE class (Fig. 3A and Additional file 1: Fig. S6A, B) [37]. Due to the high sequence similarity among TE elements, 25.4% of the identified peptides were ambiguously mapped to multiple genomic loci (Additional file 1: Fig. S6C). Despite this, the multi-mapped TE-derived peptides did not exhibit weaker HLA binding affinity than those with a unique genomic locus (Additional file 1: Fig. S6D). Furthermore, over 80% of peptides from each class were predicted as HLA binders (Additional file 1: Fig. S6E).

At the TE subfamily level, genomic prevalence was the primary driver for the presentation of unambiguously mapped TE-derived peptides, as evidenced by a strong positive correlation with element number (r = 0.72; Fig. 3B). Further analysis investigated evolutionary age as a secondary factor. A weak negative correlation was found between the number of peptides and insertion time (r = −0.14; Fig. 3C), which strengthened for LTR-derived peptides (r = −0.28). This indicated that younger elements, particularly LTRs, contribute disproportionately more peptides, probably due to their higher sequence integrity and transcriptional activity.

To investigate their specificity, we compared the overlap of TE-derived peptides across different cancer types and treatment conditions. Our analysis revealed that TE-derived peptides were more specific than canonical peptides (Fig. 3D, E). While substantial overlap was observed between treatment groups within the same sample (though less than that of canonical peptides), this overlap was largely reduced across different samples. This sample-specific signature was maintained at the subfamily level, as each sample presented a distinct set of peptide-producing TE subfamilies (Additional file 1: Fig. S6F).

The presentation of HLA-I peptides derived from transposable element-encoded proteins

A key question in the biology of TE is whether proteins produced by TE activity are expressed, processed and presented on the surface via HLA molecules, thereby potentially exposing transpositional events to the immune system [16]. To this end, we mapped identified peptides to the open reading frames (ORFs) of LINEs and the protein-coding genes of endogenous retroviruses (ERVs), as these two classes represent the primary sources of TE-derived peptides with protein-coding capacity (Additional file 1: Fig. S6A, B).

For LINEs, our analysis identified eight peptides derived from their ORFs (Fig. 4A and Additional file 1: Fig. S6B). These identifications were of high confidence, supported by manual validation of PSMs, strong HLA binding affinities (Additional file 2: Table S2), and further confirmed via MS analysis of synthetic peptides (Fig. 4B and Additional file 1: Fig. S7). Specifically, we detected six peptides derived from four distinct regions of the LINE ORF2 protein, along with two peptides from two sites in ORF1. For ERVs, our analysis identified 26 distinct peptides originating from 3,173 known intact elements [39]. Interestingly, these intact ERVs constituted a highly productive source of HLA-I ligands, generating peptides more frequently than other TE classes (Fig. 4C and D). The majority of these peptides were derived from ERV intermediate sequences (e.g., HERVH-int), which encode viral proteins such as enzymes and structural components (Additional file 1: Fig. S6B). Notably, two representative peptides, ELIALTQALR and TEEEKTRAW, were found to perfectly match the ERV-pol protein, highlighting the translational activity of intact ERVs. We subsequently confirmed their identifications via MS analysis using synthetic peptides (Additional file 2: Table S2, Additional file 1: Fig. S7). These findings suggested that ERVs are not silent genomic relics but are active contributors to the HLA-I immunopeptidome [17].

Our comparison between the TE-derived immunopeptidome and the whole TE-encoded proteome revealed that the presentation of peptides is a highly selective process. Whole-proteome analysis of the glioblastoma (GBM) cell line uncovered 67 TE-derived peptides—42 from LINE, 14 from LTR, 7 from SINE, and 4 from DNA—and revealed extensive subfamily overlap with the immunopeptidome of the same cell line. Yet, not a single peptide sequence was shared between the two datasets (Fig. 4E). This absence of overlap reinforced the previous findings that the immunopeptidome is not a snapshot of the most abundant TE proteins, but is a highly selective subset of the proteome, shaped by the antigen processing machinery and HLA binding preferences, especially for non-canonical antigens [13]. Therefore, the TE-derived immunopeptidome enabled by TIPs offered a unique perspective on transpositional activity that is usually invisible to conventional methods.

Novel antigens derived from non-coding regions of SVA elements in glioblastoma

SVA elements, a young and active non-coding TE family, are particularly relevant in oncology due to their ability to mobilize and their disruptive potential [24, 37]. Aberrant SVA activation in cancer had previously been implicated as a source of tumor antigens [23]. Since SVA activity is largely regulated by epigenetic silencing, we examined how DAC, a DNA methylation inhibitor, influenced the presentation of SVA-derived peptides.

In glioblastoma (GBM) cell lines profiled by TIPs, DAC treatment enhanced the presentation of HLA-I peptides derived from SVA subfamilies. We identified 18 DAC-specific SVA peptides, a subset of which were validated by synthetic peptide matching (Fig. 5A-C, Additional file 1: Fig. S8 and Additional file 2: Table S3). However, this global increase in SVA subfamily-level transcription did not directly translate into a proportional increase in peptide presentation. Specifically, the expression levels of individual SVA loci encoding DAC-induced peptides were only moderately elevated and, in most cases, not statistically significant (Fig. 5D). This discrepancy between the transcriptome and the immunopeptidome revealed a key limitation of transcriptome-based proteogenomic approaches, which might have overlooked potential antigens due to insufficient transcriptional upregulation.

To better understand the origin and characteristics of these peptides, we performed clustering analysis on all the SVA-derived peptides identified in LNT-229 (glioblastoma; DAC vs control; PXD003790; see Additional file 2: Table S1). This revealed two distinct groups defined by sequence similarity (Additional file 1: Fig. S10A), indicating that each group was encoded by a set of highly homologous SVA elements. Genomic mapping localized these peptides to three major "hotspot" regions within the SVA consensus sequence: (1) the (CCCTCT)ₙ repeat region, (2) the Variable Number of Tandem Repeats (VNTR) region, and (3) the SINE-R domain (Fig. 5E and Additional file 1: Fig. S10B). This genomic localization explained the observed sequence similarities. For instance, the peptide pair ALWNGAGGKVGK and ALWNGGGGKVGK were both encoded from the (CCCTCT)ₙ repeat region and differed by only a single amino acid substitution (A → G).

Distinct and targetable TE-derived antigens were induced by tumorigenesis and epigenetic therapy

To explore the specificity of TE-derived peptides in cancer, we compared their presentation in two contexts that induced TE presentation via distinct yet complementary mechanisms. We compared lung tumors with matched normal adjacent tissues to capture naturally occurring epigenetic dysregulation, and employed DAC treatment in cell lines to pharmacologically mimic a key epigenetic event in tumorigenesis [16, 37].

At the TE subfamily level, tumorigenesis and DAC treatment generated distinct antigenic profiles (Fig. 6A). Lung tumors exhibited a broader diversity of enriched TE subfamilies (enrichment ratio > 2), while DAC treatment induced stronger enrichment within fewer subfamilies (Mann–Whitney U test, p < 0.0001; Fig. 6B). Interestingly, LINE elements were preferentially enriched in tumors, whereas DAC primarily induced LTR elements (Fig. 6C). This contrast likely reflected the chronic, stochastic nature of epigenetic drift during tumorigenesis versus the acute pharmacological action of DAC. At the peptide level, the two contexts generated largely non-overlapping sets of TE-derived peptides, consistent with their distinct TE antigen profiles at the subfamily level (Fig. 3D and Fig. 6D). To confidently identify specific peptides, we applied a stringent three-step filtering strategy to exclude peptides present in normal tissues or potentially affected by central tolerance (Fig. 6E, Methods) [12]. This yielded 503 tumor-specific and 318 DAC-induced TE-derived peptides (Fig. 6E). Interestingly, these condition-specific peptides collectively comprised ~ 30% of the entire TE-derived immunopeptidome identified — a proportion that far exceeded the minimal fraction observed among canonical peptides (< 0.1%) (Fig. 6F). This sharp contrast underscored the potential of TEs to generate neoantigens, revealing a substantial and underexplored reservoir for immunotherapeutic development. Although arising from distinct biological processes, both sets of specific peptides were predominantly derived from LINE elements (Fig. 6G). This was likely due to their high abundance in the human genome, which made them frequent targets of epigenetic dysregulation. Among these, we identified 22 antigens that were shared across multiple patients (Fig. 6H). Importantly, several shared antigens, such as MPPLNTRQL and TIFPGSLAL, also demonstrated high abundance in the individual samples (Fig. 6H). The combination of recurrence and high abundance made these TE antigens ideal targets for "off-the-shelf" immunotherapies, as it ensured both widespread applicability and sufficient presentation for successful and effective recognition by T-cells.

Discussion

The discovery of tumor-specific antigens (TSAs) remains a major challenge for cancer immunotherapy. Although somatic mutation–derived neoantigens are a major focus, tumors with low mutational burden often present only a small number of mutation-derived peptides on the cell surface due to the constraints imposed by antigen processing and HLA presentation [13, 40]. In contrast, transposable elements (TEs) constitute nearly half of the human genome and provide an abundant, stable reservoir with high copy number and extensive sequence diversity, making them attractive candidates for both tumor-specific and shared antigens [14, 18]. However, their contribution to the immunopeptidome has likely been underestimated because no dedicated search tools are specifically designed to account for the unique characteristics of TE-derived peptides.

To address this, we developed TIPs, a proteogenomic method that leverages deep learning-based de novo sequencing to build streamlined databases for MS analysis. This approach enabled a more than 20-fold improvement in terms of the number of TE-derived peptides over the existing tools. The underlying principle of TIPs—refining a targeted, compact search space directly from experimental evidence (i.e., tandem mass spectra)—was broadly applicable to other fields, such as metaproteomics and spliced-peptide presentation, which face similar challenges of database inflation [41].

Leveraging the sensitivity of TIPs, we could probe how the TE-derived immunopeptidome was shaped by the unique biological features. The complex evolutionary history of TEs, characterized by the stratified expansion of diverse subfamilies, had produced a genome-wide landscape of diversity. This inherent heterogeneity was directly translated into a diverse repertoire of presented peptides. Our analysis using TIPs demonstrates that the presentation of TE-derived peptides is primarily dictated by the genomic abundance and evolutionary age of the parent subfamily. This relationship was further compounded by the high sequence identity among TE elements, which allowed for the production of similar peptides from numerous genomic locations and complicates the attribution of their origin. A recent study by Ting Wang et al. revealed that genomic TEs in cancer cells can be transcribed into chimeric transcripts with protein-coding genes, and the resulting chimeric peptides can be presented on the cell surface [14, 20]. Complementary to this, our study demonstrated that peptides derived solely from TEs also constituted a significant source of the immunopeptidome.

Further analyses, together with experimental validation, had confirmed that some presented TE-derived peptides originated from the coding regions of functionally annotated TE proteins (e.g., ORF1p and ORF2p of LINEs, and protein-coding genes within ERVs). This provides direct evidence of immune surveillance monitoring ongoing TE transcriptional activity. Research by Qinqin Jiang et al. had demonstrated that these peptides derived from functional TE proteins could serve as specific antigens in human kidney cancer cells, capable of activating T cell-mediated cytotoxicity [22]. On the other hand, beyond those from known functional TE proteins, we found a large number of TE-derived peptides originate from TE subfamilies and regions conventionally considered non-coding. Given that recent studies had demonstrated the existence of promoter-independent translation mechanisms (such as RAS-mediated oncogenic translation) in cells, it was plausible that these non-coding TEs produced peptides through similar processes [42].

In the tumor genome, DNA demethylation within TE regions led to their transcriptional activation, thereby generating tumor-specific, TE-derived antigens [16]. This process could be therapeutically harnessed using DNA methylation inhibitors, such as DAC. Our findings provided direct experimental evidence that DAC treatment effectively induces the presentation of these TE-derived tumor antigens. Concurrently, previous studies had indicated that some of these DAC-induced, TE-chimeric antigens were highly immunogenic, implying a compelling rationale for investigating the immunogenic capacity of TE-derived peptides [20]. Given that a substantial fraction of the TE-derived immunopeptidome comprises tumor-specific antigens, these endogenously expressed or pharmacologically induced TE-derived peptides can substantially expand the repertoire of targetable cancer-specific antigens.

The utility of the TIPs pipeline extends beyond this context. It can be readily adapted to identify other classes of non-canonical peptides, such as those derived from circular RNAs (circRNAs) and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) [7, 13]. Furthermore, its high sensitivity makes it ideal for profiling rare clinical samples, where sensitive and robust antigen detection from limited material is essential.

Conclusions

In summary, by developing and applying the TIPs method, our work provides a detailed map of the TE-derived immunopeptidome. We reveal TEs as a promising source of antigens and provide direct evidence for their clinical targeting, encompassing both endogenous tumor-specific peptides and drug-induced peptides. Ahead of further immunogenicity and safety testing, this work supplies the field with a highly sensitive tool to exploit this promising yet underexplored class of antigens for cancer therapy.

Methods

Mass spectrometric data preparation

The mass spectrometric RAW files (.raw format) were downloaded from the ProteomeXchange database under the accession codes PXD013649, PXD034772, and PXD003790 [11, 43, 44]. The RAW files were converted into mzML and MGF formats using ThermoRawFileParser (v1.4.5) (https://github.com/compomics/ThermoRawFileParser). Each sample group, such as the DAC-treated and control groups, was processed and analyzed independently in the downstream search workflows.

In silico TE database construction

The corresponding genomic DNA sequences were extracted using bedtools getfasta (v2.31.1) [45]. These DNA sequences were then translated in all six reading frames using seqkit translate (v2.9.0) [46]. The following parameters were used: -f 6, --append-frame, -m 8, and -s. The resulting polypeptide sequences were split at stop codons, and any sequences shorter than 8 amino acids were discarded.

Next, we classified and annotated the protein sequences according to their TE subfamily of origin. Copy number information for each element was derived from GENCODE annotation files [47]. Age estimates were based on prior literature [48]. Family consensus sequence information was obtained from the Dfam database (Release 3.9) [25].

RNA-seq data processing pipeline

The RNA-seq dataset was retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession ID GSE80137. Raw SRR files were downloaded and converted to FASTQ format using parallel-fastq-dump (v0.6.7) (https://github.com/rvalieris/parallel-fastq-dump). Quality control was performed with FastQC (v0.12.1) (https://github.com/s-andrews/FastQC), followed by adapter trimming and low-quality read filtering using Trimmomatic (v0.33) [49] with the following parameters: seedMismatches = 2, palindromeClipThreshold = 30, and simpleClipThreshold = 10.

Processed FASTQ files were aligned to the hg38 reference genome using STAR (v2.7) with the following parameters: outFilterType = BySJout, outFilterMultimapNmax = 20, alignSJoverhangMin = 8, alignSJDBoverhangMin = 1, outFilterMismatchNoverLmax = 0.04, alignIntronMin = 20, alignIntronMax = 1,000,000, and alignMatesGapMax = 1,000,000 [50]. To account for the repetitive nature of TEs, we configured STAR to handle multi-mapping reads by setting outFilterMultimapNmax = 20. The resulting BAM files were sorted, indexed, and deduplicated using Picard MarkDuplicates (v3.0) (https://github.com/broadinstitute/picard). For gene-level expression, raw count matrices were generated using featureCounts (v2.0.6) with the GENCODE v45 gene annotation [51]. For TE expression, we used TEtranscripts (v2.2.3) with a custom TE annotation file derived from GENCODE to accurately quantify TE-associated reads, including those that map to multiple locations [52]. Differential expression analysis of TEs was performed using the OmicVerse Python package [53], which implements the DESeq2 algorithm [54]. TEs with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change > 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

TIPs search workflow

TIPs is an easy-to-use and highly reproducible workflow. It contains mainly four modules: (i) construction of an inflated TE protein database by six-frame translation of TE-annotated genomic regions; (ii) extraction and quality control of high-confidence de novo peptide sequence tags from MS/MS spectra; (iii) tag-guided generation of a sample-specific streamlined TE database; and (iv) multi-database search engines with probabilistic integration and grouped FDR control. Throughout the workflow, de novo outputs are used only as sequence tags for database refinement and do not alone assign TE origin. Key software versions and parameters are summarized in Additional file 2: Table S4.

Step 1: construction of an inflated TE reference database

TE-annotated genomic regions were extracted using bedtools getfasta (v2.31.1) [45]. The corresponding DNA sequences were translated in all six reading frames using seqkit translate (v2.9.0) with parameters -f 6, --append-frame, -m 8, and -s [46]. Translated sequences were split at stop codons, and resulting polypeptides shorter than 8 amino acids were discarded. This procedure generated the inflated TE protein database (in silico TE proteins) to maximize coverage of potential TE-derived coding regions independent of transcript abundance or prior evidence of translation. TE class/subfamily annotations were obtained from GENCODE [47]. Copy number information was derived from the corresponding TE annotation files, and TE age estimates were obtained from prior literature [48].

Step 2: extraction of high-confidence de novo peptide sequence tags

Three de novo sequencing algorithms—Casanovo (v2) [27], PepNet (v2.0) [29], and InstaNovo (v1.0.0) [28] — were applied to immunopeptidomic MS/MS data to extract candidate sequence tags. To obtain robust sequence tags for database refinement, stringent quality control was performed on de novo outputs: (i) a minimum per-residue confidence score of 0.75 for each amino acid; and (ii) a minimum tag length of 8 amino acids. For longer sequences passing these criteria, we extracted the subsequence with the highest average confidence to yield an optimal high-confidence tag. The final set of tags was obtained by merging high-confidence tags from all three tools into a non-redundant union set after deduplication by tag sequence, masking I/L as equivalent, to maximize coverage.

Step 3: tag-guided generation of a sample-specific TE database

High-confidence de novo tags were mapped to the inflated TE protein database using BLAST+ blastp-short (v2.12.0+) [55], which is optimized for short peptide queries. Given the short queries, we did not rely on e-value as the primary filter; instead, we required exact full-length matches (100% identity and coverage), which is stricter than typical significance cutoffs for short peptides. Leucine and isoleucine are isobaric residues due to their identical molecular weights in MS-based identification and were masked as equivalent during alignment. A TE protein entry was retained if it contained at least one tag meeting the above criteria, yielding a sample-specific streamlined TE database for downstream database searching.

To reduce misassignment to canonical proteins, candidate TE-derived sequences were additionally screened against the UniProt human proteome database [56]. Tags or sequences overlapping with canonical proteins were excluded.

Step 4: multi-database search engines, statistical validation and integration, and grouped FDR control

MS/MS spectra were searched against a combined database consisting of the sample-specific streamlined TE database appended to the canonical human proteome, and MSFragger (v4.1) [30], MS-GF+ (v2023.01.12) [31], and Comet (v2021.01) [32] were applied with a consistent set of parameters: precursor and fragment ion tolerance of 20 ppm, no enzyme specificity, oxidation of methionine as a variable modification, and peptide length constrained to 8–15 amino acids for HLA-I peptides. Decoy sequences were generated by a reverse-database strategy and appended to the target database for FDR estimation.

Downstream statistical validation was conducted using the Philosopher toolkit (v5.1.0) [34]. For the output from each search engine, PSMs were rescored using PeptideProphet (v5.2.1) [33], and multi-engine evidence was integrated using iProphet (v5.01) [35]. Given imbalance between TE-derived and canonical identifications and their distinct target–decoy characteristics, we applied grouped FDR control separately for TE-derived and canonical identifications at the peptide level. Unless otherwise specified, results were filtered at 3% FDR at peptide level, which was consistent with the benchmark comparison [11].

Finally, MixMHCpred (v3.0) [36] was used to predict HLA binding affinities of identified peptides. Peptides with RankBestAllele < 2% were considered predicted binders.

Performance benchmarking against the NewAnce pipeline

To evaluate the performance of TIPs, a head-to-head benchmark was conducted against NewAnce, a published analytical approach specialized in the identification of non-canonical immunopeptidomes. The comparison utilized a dataset comprising five distinct cell line and tissue samples derived from two different cancer types.

A fundamental methodological distinction between the two pipelines lies in their database construction strategy. NewAnce primarily constructs its search database from sample-matched RNA-seq data, whereas TIPs employs the de novo sequencing, seed sequence filtering, and homology search strategy detailed herein. To ensure a fair and rigorous comparison, both methods applied a similar, stringent, group-specific FDR control strategy for the quality control of identified non-canonical peptides.

The performance of the two pipelines was assessed using several key metrics. We compared (i) the total number of identified TE-derived peptides, (ii) the proportion of these peptides predicted to be HLA binders, and (iii) the quality of the corresponding PSMs. Statistical significance of the differences in peptide counts and PSM quality scores between the two methods was assessed using a two-sided Mann–Whitney U test.

UMAP (Uniform manifold approximation and projection) clustering of peptides

To investigate whether canonical peptides and TE-derived peptides exhibit distinct sequence characteristics, we performed UMAP clustering analysis. Specifically, we encoded the peptides using the BLOSUM62 matrix, and applied UMAP. UMAP can cluster peptides binding to the same HLA based on sequence features, revealing distinct groups that share common motifs. To determine if TE-derived peptides conform to these characteristics, we co-embedded them with canonical peptides. This UMAP analysis allows us to assess whether the sequence features of TE-derived peptides are consistent with those of canonical peptides and the established HLA-binding motif.

Hydrophobicity index prediction based on peptide sequences

To assess the hydrophobic properties of identified peptides, we used DeepLC (v3.1.8) to predict hydrophobicity index based solely on peptide sequences [57]. In parallel, we extracted the observed retention times from the PSM results. We then calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient between the predicted hydrophobicity index and the observed retention times, and visualized the relationship using seaborn (https://seaborn.pydata.org/).

Analysis of TE-derived peptides across samples

TE-derived peptide identification results were integrated from all samples, and distribution plots were created to compare their HLA-binding affinity, sequence length, and the number of supporting spectra per peptide across experimental groups. To assess the similarity of peptide repertoires between samples, we calculated the pairwise peptide overlap proportion using the formula |A ∩ B|/min(|A|, |B|), where A and B represent the sets of unique peptides in two respective samples. These proportions were then visualized as a heatmap using seaborn.

Additionally, we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficients between the number of TE-derived peptides in each sample and the corresponding TE element number and TE insertion age, to explore potential genomic determinants of peptide presentation. To ensure the robustness of the correlation analysis, peptides that could not be unambiguously mapped to a single genomic TE locus were excluded from this specific analysis.

Validation of TE-derived peptides with synthetic standards

All synthetic peptides were obtained from Genscript at > 95% purity. The peptide analysis was performed on a liquid chromatography system coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris™ 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) via a nano-electrospray ionization source. Peptides were loaded onto a reverse-phase analytical column and eluted using a 45-min linear acetonitrile gradient in 0.1% formic acid containing 1% DMSO. Data were acquired in positive ion mode.

Full MS scans were acquired at a resolution of 120,000 over a scan range of 250–1600 m/z, with an Automatic Gain Control (AGC) target of 3e5. MS2 spectra were acquired in parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) mode. Following a full MS1 survey scan (resolution 120,000; max injection time 60 ms; AGC target 3e5), the instrument sequentially targeted parent ions from a predefined inclusion list. Fragmentation was performed with a normalized higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) energy of 25%. The resulting MS2 spectra were acquired at a resolution of 60,000 with a max injection time of 120 ms and an AGC target of 5e4.

The acquired PRM spectra were then manually compared against the corresponding fragmentation spectra from the initial discovery search results. This manual inspection served to validate the accuracy of the original PSMs.

Spectral annotation

The Universal Spectrum Explorer (https://www.proteomicsdb.org/) was utilized for annotating and visually comparing mass spectra matched to peptides by search engines with synthetic peptide spectra. This facilitated a direct comparison between experimentally obtained spectra and those generated from synthetic peptides, enhancing the confidence in peptide identifications.

Multiple sequence alignment of protein and cDNA sequences

To conduct multiple sequence alignment (MSA) of protein sequences and cDNA sequences, we employed MEGA12, selecting the ClustalW algorithm with default parameters [58]. The alignment parameters were adjusted according to the type of sequences being analyzed, following MEGA12's recommendations. After obtaining the MSA results, we used TBtools (v2.225) for visualizing and annotating these alignments, aiding in the interpretation of sequence relationships and conservation patterns [59].

Association between TE-derived peptide counts and corresponding TE subfamilies

Initially, we filtered TE-derived peptides based on their binding affinity to corresponding HLA allotypes (RankBestAllele < 2%). In examining the correlation between TE-derived peptide counts and the insertion time of TE subfamilies into the human genome, we further normalized the number of TE-derived peptides by considering the element number of different TE subfamilies within the human genome. This normalization step aimed to account for variations in TE subfamily abundance and evolutionary history, providing insights into the dynamics of TE-derived peptide presentation.

Bioinformatic pipeline for translation filtering

To identify actively translated ORFs in normal tissues, we developed a comprehensive filtering pipeline. Publicly available Ribo-seq datasets (GEO accessions GSE182377 and GSE59820) were used, from which 49 high-quality samples were selected for downstream analysis. These samples encompassed a range of primary cells and tissues, including kidney, brain, fat, fibroblasts, human aortic endothelial cells, human coronary artery endothelial cells, hepatocytes, and vascular smooth muscle cells. A custom transcriptome annotation was constructed using gene and TE annotations from GENCODE. The analysis pipeline involved trimming raw sequencing reads with Trimmomatic (v0.33), followed by an alignment against a contaminant database (tRNA, rRNA, and mitochondrial RNA) using Bowtie2 (v2.4.0) with the --very-sensitive preset [60]. Unaligned reads were then mapped to the human reference genome using STAR (v2.7) with the following parameters: alignIntronMax = 1, outFilterMismatchNoverLmax = 0.05, outFilterMultimapNmax = 20, seedSearchStartLmax = 30, and outFilterMatchNmin = 16. The resulting BAM files were sorted, indexed, and their quality was validated using the Ribo-TISH (v0.2.6) quality function. Finally, translated ORFs were empirically defined using the Ribo-TISH prediction function against our custom annotation with the --longest, --aaseq, and --seq parameters [61]. ORFs with a frame-shifting quality value (FrameQvalue) below 0.05 were considered bona fide translated events in healthy tissues.

Filtering pipeline for tumor-specific or induced-specific peptides

To rigorously isolate enriched and potentially immunogenic peptides, a sequential, three-tiered filtering pipeline was applied. First, sample-specific background was removed by excluding any peptides that were also detected in their corresponding paired control samples (adjacent normal tissues or untreated cells). Next, the remaining candidates were filtered against a comprehensive reference library of open reading frames (ORFs) compiled from Ribo-Seq data of eight normal tissues (n = 49 samples) to eliminate peptides with evidence of translation in normal physiological contexts. In the final step, candidates were screened against a database of known thymic-presented peptides; those found in the database were removed to enrich for peptides capable of evading central immune tolerance.
