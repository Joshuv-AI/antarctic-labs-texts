Longitudinal assessment of renal allograft function in donors and pediatric recipients by arterial spin labeling MRI perfusion quantification
Authors: Tijana Radovic, Brankica Spasojevic, Mirjana Cvetkovic, Gordana Milosevski-Lomic, Milica M. Jankovic, Ivana Gojkovic, Polina Pavicevic
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We analyzed cortical renal blood flow (cRBF) by ASL-MRI in ten donors (4 females, mean age 46 ± 6) and dedicated pediatric recipients (4 females, mean age 14 ± 4) before transplantation and at 3-, 6-, and 12-months post-transplant to identify allograft functional adaptation. Baseline values were compared to post-transplant values and correlated with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Additionally, renal plasma flow (RPF) and estimated filtration fraction (eFF) were calculated. Both cRBF and eGFR demonstrated a highly significant increase at 3 months compared to baseline (mean cRBF 251.71 ± 78.27 vs. 486.61 ± 156.08 ml/kidney/min, p = 0.0001; mean eGFR 46.62 ± 16.36 vs. 69.81 ± 24.83 ml/min/1.73 m2; p = 0.02) and remained consistent until the end of the observed period. Patients with impaired function and lower eGFR at 3 months exhibited a smaller increase in cRBF compared to the rest of the group (p = 0.01). eFF did not change significantly throughout the observed period (p = 0.07, p = 0.07, and p = 0.68, respectively), suggesting absence of hyperfiltration. cRBF may serve as a significant renal biomarker for longitudinal follow-up of renal allografts, enabling tracking of perfusion adaptation and identification of critical timepoints for clinical intervention.

Introduction

Kidney transplantation is the gold standard for treating patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD)1. However, numerous post-transplant complications are common in both early and late postoperative stages, with allograft failure before the end of a recipient’s lifetime being the most significant major long-term risk2. It is traditionally believed that late renal allograft loss results from repeated episodes of acute rejection leading to chronic immune-mediated allograft rejection. Nevertheless, late renal allograft loss is also known to occur in the absence of prior acute rejection episodes3. Renal tissue ischemia and the associated hypoxia are critical factors in the initiation and progression of renal failure, primarily through nephron loss that leads to glomerular hyperperfusion and hyperfiltration in the remaining nephrons, ultimately resulting in progressive renal dysfunction and late allograft failure4. Currently, reliable techniques for assessing renal tissue perfusion are highly limited, with no universally accepted gold-standard technique available.

Arterial spin labeling (ASL) is an MRI technique that uses arterial blood water as a freely diffusible natural tracer to track and quantify tissue perfusion5,6. The non-invasive, short-lived nature of the tracer, combined with the rapid acquisition time, makes renal ASL inherently suited to repeated measurements over a range of timescales7. These repeated measurements would not be possible with any other perfusion technique in humans and may provide new insights into renal pathophysiology.

Previous, mostly cross-sectional studies have consistently demonstrated that ASL measurements positively correlate with estimated GFR (eGFR) in both native kidneys and renal allografts, also reporting that moderately impaired renal function corresponds to a substantial reduction in renal perfusion8,9. Despite these promising results, few longitudinal studies have investigated perfusion changes in renal allografts using ASL-MRI to track renal functional reserve in the early post-transplant period10.

To our knowledge, this is the first longitudinal study to evaluate pediatric and young adult allograft recipients using ASL over 12 months, assessing changes in renal perfusion to track renal adaptation mechanisms and to detect the time points of subclinical or clinical renal function impairment.

Methods

This prospective study was approved by the University Children’s Hospital Ethics Committee (number 16/238/08/10/2024) and was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians, if necessary.

Patients

From November 2023 to March 2025, 10 donor–pediatric recipient pairs were evaluated during the longitudinal follow-up. The demographics of all participants at the beginning of the study are presented in Table 1.

In all cases, healthy donors were parents with a mean age of 46 ± 6 years, who underwent extensive clinical preparation prior to kidney explantation. All donors were admitted to the hospital one day before transplantation, during which a routine clinical check-up, blood and urine sampling, and MR imaging were performed (in four donor–recipient pairs, baseline MR imaging had been conducted during an earlier hospital stay due to the recipient’s contraindication for surgery at that time and the clinical need for additional preparation for successful transplantation). The eGFR was measured according to the MDRD (Modification of Diet in Renal Disease) formula11. Body weight (kg) and body height (cm) were measured to calculate body mass index (BMI—kg/m2). All donors were instructed to refrain from food and water intake for at least 4 h prior to their check-up and MR imaging. The donor’s left kidney was transplanted in 7 cases, and the right kidney in the remaining 3 cases.

All transplant recipients (4 females, 6 males) were pediatric and young adult patients with a mean age 14 ± 4 years, who attended routine clinical check-ups or hospital stays at 3-, 6-, and 12 months post-transplantation. In all recipients, the cold ischemia time was under 3 h, and no immediate post-transplant allograft complications (vascular thrombosis, urinary obstruction, or acute graft rejection) were reported. Recipient inclusion also depended on their ability to cooperate during non-sedated MRI scanning. Along with MR imaging, blood and urine sampling were conducted early in the morning, between 7 and 8 a.m., after at least 4 h of fasting and before the administration of any prescribed medications, particularly calcineurin inhibitors. The eGFR was measured at every visit according to the modified Schwartz method12 or MDRD formula for patients older than 16 years. The longitudinal follow-up also monitored changes in body height and BMI. The study design is presented graphically in Fig. 1.

Image acquisition and analysis

MR imaging

MR examinations were performed on a 1.5-T whole-body MRI scanner (Magnetom Aera, Siemens AG). Patients were examined in the supine position after undergoing respiratory training prior to imaging. They were instructed to remain still and breathe as slowly and shallowly as possible. Image acquisition was performed during free breathing using a six-channel body matrix array coil placed over the abdominal and/or pelvic region, in combination with the spine matrix coil integrated into the scanner table. In all subjects, standard coronal T2-weighted HASTE (Half-Fourier Acquired Single Shot Turbo Spin Echo) and axial T1-weighted 3D volumetric VIBE (Volume Interpolated Breath-hold Examination) sequences were acquired for anatomical evaluation of the kidneys and volume measurement. For quantitative ASL assessment of kidney perfusion, a flow-sensitive alternating inversion recovery (FAIR) approach combined with a multi-shot 3D gradient and spin-echo (3D-GRASE) imaging module and background suppression was used13,14. An adiabatic frequency offset correction inversion pulse in line with the imaging slab was used15. The selective inversions slab with non/selective and selective pulses was dependent on the imaging stack thickness. Images were acquired in an oblique-coronal orientation for native kidneys and in an oblique-sagittal orientation for allografts in order to avoid inclusion of the aorta or pelvic arteries within the acquisition window. To clearly define the bolus duration, Q2TIPS was applied to cover the feeding arteries14,16. Imaging parameters were as follows: number of segments—3 (multi-shot), field of view 360 × 360 mm, TE/TR 20/6000 ms, Flip angle 180, without parallel imaging, voxel size 2.8 × 2.8 × 6 mm, slice thickness 6 mm, number of slices 8, phase encoding direction right →left, bandwidth = 3004 Hz/pixel; centric partition ordering; echo-spacing = 0.49 ms; EPI factor 31; reconstruction matrix 128 × 128 × 8, number of measurements 6 (paired tag and control images). To allow sufficient labeled blood to perfuse into the tissue, an inversion pulse delay time (TI) of 2000 ms was used17. The total scan time for the FAIR sequence was 3 min and 42 s. An additional proton density image without inversion was acquired for the determination of the equilibrium magnetization (M0), which is necessary for perfusion quantification, using identical field of view, slice orientation, slice thickness, and reconstruction matrix settings to match FAIR ASL. The total scan time for this acquisition was 1.36 min. ASL-MRI measurements were repeated during the same visit in donors and in two out of three visits in the best-cooperating recipients in order to assess intra-visit repeatability.

Image analysis

For ASL-MRI parametric image analysis, in-house software developed in the Python environment version 3.7.6 (Stichting Mathematisch Centrum, Amsterdam) was used. The input images for the software included tag and control images obtained after voxel-wise signal averaging of multiple measurements, as well as the corresponding structural proton density weighted image (PDW), which was automatically registered to the others. A rigid registration was performed using the SimpleITK library (https://simpleitk.org/). Additionally, manual correction of the result of this automatic procedure was available. The transformation was initialized using Euler Transform based on image geometry, and registration was conducted using the Mattes Mutual Information metric with 50 histogram bins. Optimization was performed via gradient descent with a learning rate of 1.0 and 100 iterations. In each participant, 8 PWI/RBF maps were generated and qualitatively and quantitatively assessed by an author experienced in abdominal and urogenital imaging in order to select the best slice for final quantification (Fig. 2). Maps with significant noise—mostly those generated from slices at the beginning or end of the imaging stack, where partial volume effects with adjacent structures were common—were excluded. In addition, all images were qualitatively assessed for motion artefacts, and images that were not aligned were discarded. As a result, one of the middle slices (3rd, 4th, or 5th, depending on the exact positioning of the imaging stack and image quality) was selected for quantification. Cortical regions of interest (ROI) were manually drawn for each kidney on the parametric maps by two authors (T.R. and M.J.) with experience in abdominal and urogenital imaging. Since donating kidneys are typically flipped along both the longitudinal and transverse axis during implantation, compared to their original orientation in donors, the interpolar region was selected in each participant to ensure the most reliable longitudinal perfusion follow-up (Fig. 3). A T1 value was set to 1.5 s, as recommended in the literature5.

Functional analysis

After quantification of cRBF (expressed in ml/100 g/min), cortical volume was measured at all timepoints by manually contouring the renal cortex on anatomical 3DT1VIBE sequences using dedicated external software (available for free at: https://www.parametricmri.com/, The Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania). cRBF values were subsequently converted to whole cortical perfusion values, expressed in ml/kidney/min. This DF was then used to divide the global eGFR to split eGRF value between the two kidneys, applying the same ratio (%) as obtained from cRBF. In addition, the filtration fraction was estimated (eFF) at each timepoint by calculating the ratio of eGFR to RPF, in order to monitor filtration changes over time. All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS v. 25.0, IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). Paired sample t-tests were conducted to assess the significance of changes between observation periods and baseline values. Furthermore, a repeated-measures ANOVA was performed to assess the overall longitudinal within-subjects effect during the observation period, with post-hoc Bonferroni correction applied to identify specific timepoints at which significant differences occurred. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the distribution of variables between patient groups with impaired and good allograft function. For repeatability of measurements, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was analyzed. Additionally, cRBF was correlated with GFR values using Pearson’s correlation coefficient to determine its association with allograft function. Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05.

Results

All studies generated overall good-quality maps. Although cooperation in some pediatric recipients was at times inadequate, the pelvic extraperitoneal positioning of the allograft was not significantly affected by respiratory motion during image acquisition. Intestinal peristalsis was identified as a potential source of motion artefacts, which, along with other important clinical factors, was successfully addressed by instructing patients to fast from food and water starting the night before imaging. Two recipients haven’t finished their final visit. Cooperation with adult donors was satisfactory. Repeatability of ASL measurements in native donor kidneys during the same visit was very good, with a high ICC of 0.89 (95% CI 0.60–0.97, p < 0.001), as well as in pediatric recipients, with an ICC of 0.77 (95% CI 0.36–0.92, p = 0.003; Fig. 4). The reader agreement in segmentations was also very good, with a high ICC of 0.84 (95% CI 0.59–0.94, p = 0.0001).

All pre-donation results for eGFR, cRBF, and RPF were within normal limits and are presented in Table 2 and longitudinal changes in observed parameters are shown in Table 3; Fig. 5. Each allograft individually showed a significant increase in kidney cortical volume at 3 months compared to baseline (mean baseline volume vs. 3-month volume 83.79 ± 18.77 cm3 vs. 119.54 ± 24.12 cm3; p = 0.001) with no significant changes when comparing consecutive follow-up timepoints to each other (mean volume at 3 vs. 6 months 119.54 ± 24.12 vs. 117.95 ± 28.62, p = 0.740; mean volume at 6 vs. 12 months 110.66 ± 27.36 vs. 113.99 ± 22.82 cm3, p = 0.556). In parallel with the volume changes, a significant increase in mean eGFR was observed at 3 months compared to baseline (46.62 ± 16.36 vs. 69.81 ± 24.83 ml/min/1.73 m2; p = 0.025). However, at this timepoint, 4 patients had an eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73 m2, suggesting allograft nephropathy, with a significantly different eGFR compared to recipients with normal allograft function (p = 0.010). At 6 months, the mean eGFR remained relatively stable compared to the 3-month value (69.81 ± 24.83 vs. 71.30 ± 18.88 ml/min/1.73 m2; p = 0.819), with 3 patients still showing impaired function (p = 0.036). By 12 months, the mean eGFR was 78.39 ± 22.89 ml/min/1.73 m2, without a significant difference compared to the 6-month value (p = 0.214). At this timepoint, however, all recipients were clinically stable, with good allograft function.

Renal cortical perfusion followed changes in eGFR and showed a highly significant correlation (r = 0.82, p = 0.004 at 3 months and r = 0.74, p = 0.014 at 6 months; Fig. 6). Both cRBF and RPF demonstrated a highly significant increase at 3 months compared to baseline (mean cRBF 251.71 ± 78.27 vs. 486.61 ± 156.08 ml/kidney/min; p = 0.0001, and mean RPF 145.31 ± 45.76 vs. 320.22 ± 109.47 ml/kidney/min; p = 0.0001). Although patients with lower eGFR at 3 months exhibited some degree of cortical hyperperfusion, their cRBF remained significantly lower than that of the rest of the group (p = 0.010, Fig. 7), as did their RPF (p = 0.038). At 6 months, cortical perfusion was not significantly different compared to the value at 3 months (3 months vs. 6 months 486.61 ± 156.08 vs. 492.18 ± 116.60 ml/kidney/min; p = 0.889); however, the difference in cRBF among the 3 patients with impaired function persisted (p = 0.036). Thereafter, cRBF remained unchanged until the end of the observation period (6 months vs. 12 months 492.18 ± 116.60 vs. 461.08 ± 168.63 ml/kidney/min; p = 0.791). No significant difference in cRBF and RPF of the corresponding donor kidneys prior to transplantation was observed (p = 0.475, p = 0.114, respectively). eFF reflected stable renal function throughout the entire post-transplant period, without significant difference from the baseline value (mean eFF 33 ± 13% at baseline vs. 23 ± 7% at 3 and 6 months, and 31 ± 13% at 12 months, p = 0.074, p = 0.071, and p = 0.681, respectively).

The overall longitudinal within-subjects effect during the observation period showed a highly significant increase in almost all parameters after transplantation compared with baseline values: mean cortical volume (p = 0.008), cRBF (p = 0.012), RPF (p = 0.0004), and eGFR (p = 0.0007). The most pronounced effect on cortical volume (p = 0.03), together with increases in converted cRBF and RBF (p = 0.007 and p = 0.006, respectively), was observed at 3 months post-transplant. While mean cortical volume remained stable at 6 months post-transplant, both cRBF and RPF continued to rise significantly, reflecting hyperperfusion as the main adaptation mechanism (p = 0.007 and p = 0.009, respectively). Furthermore, these perfusion changes resulted in an overall increase in mean eGFR (p = 0.0007), although with some delay—the most prominent eGFR increase was observed at 6 and 12 months post-transplant (p = 0.02 and 0.017, respectively). Finally, eFF remained stable throughout the entire observation period (p = 0.228), excluding hyperfiltration as an early adaptation mechanism.

Overall, good allograft function, as detected by cortical hypertrophy and hyperperfusion, was clinically reflected in a significant increase in BMI from baseline to the end of the observation period (baseline mean BMI 18.4 ± 4.4 kg/m2 vs. 12-month mean BMI 21.3 ± 5.4 kg/m2; p = 0.023), principally due to an increase in height (baseline mean height 152.1 ± 20.3 cm vs. 12-month mean height 155.9 ± 18.5 cm; p = 0.016).

Discussion

In this study, we applied ASL-MRI perfusion, as a non-invasive tool, to longitudinally monitor changes in cortical renal blood flow (cRBF) in pediatric and young adult kidney transplant recipients in order to detect possible allograft failure.

All recipients received organs from healthy, living, related adult donors under very well-controlled circumstances (cold ischemia time less than 3 h, no immediate postoperative complications), and all were clinically stable and healthy. With this study design, we eliminated well-known causes of acute ischemic injury (prolonged cold ischemia time, cadaveric or unrelated donors, surgical complications), thereby minimizing the risk of acute nephron loss. During the observation period, we investigated allograft hypertrophy, changes in eGFR, cortical perfusion alterations, renal plasma flow, and estimated filtration fraction. In all patients, marked cortical hypertrophy occurred during the first 3 months, followed by significant increases in perfusion and eGFR at the subsequent timepoint, although some exceptions were noted. Four patients exhibited poor allograft function, with eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73 m2, despite an increase in cortical volume. At 6 months, 3 of these patients continued to show the same impairment. This impaired function could likely be explained by a significantly lower post-transplant increase in cortical renal blood flow and renal plasma flow compared to patients with good allograft function, a finding further supported by the highly significant correlation between cRBF and eGFR. Thereafter, cortical volume and perfusion remained stable throughout the observation period, along with mean eGFR, suggesting that in our patient group (with few exceptions), early post-transplant increases in eGFR reflected the activation of renal functional reserve through hypertrophy and hyperperfusion. Stable eGFR and RPF values throughout the remainder of the observation period, along with a consistent estimated filtration fraction, excluded the possibility of hyperfiltration. Most importantly, the activation of renal functional reserve in our pediatric patients was clinically manifested by significant growth.

Previous studies in normal volunteers and transplant recipients using ASL-MRI have demonstrated a positive correlation between cortical perfusion and eGFR, with some prominent findings indicating that cRBF values differ between patients with good and impaired graft function8,19–21. Most of these cross-sectional studies evaluated the ASL method in adult patients, employing varying MRI approaches, participant numbers, and selection criteria, often without considering factors such as donor type, possible surgical complications, or episodes of acute rejection. Some studies compared renal allografts to native kidneys in random healthy volunteers22. However, a major flaw in such study designs is that intersubject variability can sometimes be substantial, making comparisons between healthy volunteers and allograft recipients highly disputable, especially if patient preparation protocols, such as fasting periods and hydration status, are not strictly standardized. We included recipients who received allografts from healthy, living, related donors and followed them longitudinally over a 12-month period. By selecting participants with stable function and no episodes of rejection, we aimed to establish the most physiological environment possible for the allografts, thereby potentially excluding immune-mediated renal injury and enabling us to track the activation of renal functional reserve, which is also promoted by a large number of growth factors. The final result was clinically manifested as intensive growth in our patient group. To date, the only longitudinal study that followed 15 matched pairs of living kidney donors and recipients over 2 years reported a significant decrease in perfusion at all timepoints, possibly explained by renal ischemic and reperfusion injury10. Conversely, our study group demonstrated significant post-transplant hyperperfusion, which could potentially be attributed to the selection of living related donors with better match and shorter cold ischemia times, thus reducing initial ischemic injury. Finally, activation of the renal functional reserve is a well-known adaptive mechanism in situations of nephron loss, such as after nephrectomy, and has so far been analyzed only in healthy donors before and after transplantation, but not yet in recipients23.

We acknowledge several limitations of the present study that warrant further discussion.

First, our study population was limited by the number of donor–recipient pairs who successfully completed an extensive pre-transplant preparation process at the time of investigation. This limitation can be explained by the fact that the number of available pediatric patients was significantly restricted due to factors such as the specific age and body weight requirements essential for transplantation. These factors may also contribute to the general scarcity of studies involving pediatric transplant patients compared to those focused on adults.

Second, we have longitudinally assessed recipients who were clinically stable throughout the entire observed period, with no clinically significant allograft dysfunction or rejection detected. Therefore, the relationship between functional MRI measures and long-term outcomes remains unclear, particularly in patients who demonstrated lower perfusion during the early post-transplant period. To detect further changes in renal allograft function, we plan to continue the longitudinal follow-up for several years and to expand it with new participants.

Third, the calculation of filtration fraction typically requires a measured GFR, which is usually obtained through a renal scintigraphy study. We did not perform scintigraphy in our recipients, as it is an invasive procedure that requires clinical justification. Instead, we estimated filtration fraction using repeated eGFR measurements to approximate whether changes in filtration occurred over time, potentially recognizing glomerular hyperfiltration in individual participants.

Fourth, assessing the accuracy of cRBF or RPF as measured by ASL-MRI is very difficult, as it requires comparison to procedures that are considered either invasive or unreliable and thus unsuitable for repeated measurements (such as positron emission tomography using oxygen-15-labeled water with arterial blood sampling for RBF, or constant infusion PAH urinary clearance to measure effective RPF)24. Nevertheless, reproducibility of RBF or RPF measurements is more clinically relevant when using ASL-MRI due to its non-invasive nature, especially in longitudinal studies such as ours. Previous studies have demonstrated good reproducibility of ASL, with high intraclass correlation coefficients for cortical perfusion7,25,26. Future studies could potentially include comparisons of ASL-based measurements with dynamic contrast-enhanced MR imaging; however, this approach remains controversial, as the intravenous administration of gadolinium-based contrast agents in allograft recipients is highly disputable27,28.

Fifth, the number of repeated measurements during ASL acquisition was limited to 6 in our study, since it determined the total acquisition time. In some papers, the number of control/tag measurements was recommended to go up to 20 in the case of a single TI to increase SNR; however, this was not considered mandatory5. The main reason for limiting the number of measurements was that increasing it would extend the acquisition time to 12 min and 6 s, which was unacceptable in our (or any) study group. While increasing SNR would provide better cortical delineation, motion artifacts during scanning could deteriorate image quality, potentially leading to miscalculation or participant exclusion from the analysis. Furthermore, the 3D GRASE readout scheme with multisegment acquisition used in our study was previously shown to provide higher SNR compared to other readout schemes (2D or single segment 3DGRASE), with good relative perfusion-weighted signal9,21,29.

Finally, the lack of acquired T1 maps represents a notable limitation, as T1 mapping complements ASL. We are aware that T1 maps are crucial for tissue characterization, particularly in tracking longstanding chronic allograft rejection, and that neglecting them could lead to overestimation of tissue perfusion. However, following the recommendations outlined in the consensus paper, we employed a mathematical model that neglects MRI signal relaxation differences between blood and tissue, referred to as the single-compartment model, with a single-delay acquisition approach. In this model, a fixed T1 value of 1.5 s at 1.5T for arterial blood was recommended and applied as a constant across subjects and measurements5.

In conclusion, despite the limited cohort, our study has identified clear adaptive changes in cortical perfusion and renal plasma flow, which represent promising early biomarkers of renal functional reserve activation in clinically stable pediatric allograft recipients. While the full clinical implications of these findings require further investigation, ASL-MRI demonstrates strong potential for noninvasive, long-term functional monitoring and could significantly enhance the management and assessment of renal health in transplant recipients.
