Biosynthesis of metal/metal oxide nanoparticles via Deverra tortuosa: characterization, GC/MS profiles, and biological potential
Authors: Yasser A. El-Amier, Balsam T. Abduljabbar, Mustafa M. El-Zayat, Tushar C. Sarker, Ahmed M. Abd-ElGawad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Since the beginning of humanity, people have used wild medicinal plants for the treatment of various diseases. Nowadays, researchers and scientists pay attention to exploring new tools to maximize the efficacy of natural products from natural resources, and among these tools formulation of nanoparticles is very promising. The green synthesis of metal/metal oxide nano-solutions using the Deverra tortuosa extract has still not been explored well. This study aims to prepare many metal/metal oxide nanoparticle solutions such as ZnO-NPs, SeO2-MNPs, and Ag-NPs via the methanol extract of D. tortuosa as safe, easy, green, and economic approach as well as characterize the chemical components of D. tortuosa plant by GC-MS spectroscopy analysis. The synthesized M-NPs solutions were characterized by UV–visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy, Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM), zeta potential, Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, and phytochemical analyses. The potential antioxidant capacity was estimated for the methanol extract of this plant along with the metal/metal oxide nanoparticles solutions by DPPH assay. In addition, the cytotoxic activity was in vitro assessed for the inspected samples against various tumor and normal cell lines applying MTT assay. The application of the D. tortuosa methanolic extract resulted in NPs with a range of 11.79–85.9 nm which was characterized by UV-Vis spectroscopy, TEM, zeta potential, and FT-IR spectroscopy that revealed various functional groups of the tested samples. The GC-MS analysis of the D. tortuosa methanolic extract showed the presence of 31 chemical compounds with 2-methyl-3-oxocyclopent-1-ene-1-carboxylic acid, methyl oleate, and 6-allyl-4,5-dimethoxybenzo[d][1,3]-dioxole as major constituents. The extract showed considerable antioxidant activity as well as antimicrobial activity that was assessed also against varied bacterial and fungal species. Remarkable potencies for the investigated metal/metal oxide nanoparticle solutions hinder the growth of the tumor cell lines, in addition to the growth of the microbial species. Based on the determined biological activities of the produced NPs, future study is recommended to characterize the pure authentic compounds that are identified within the D. tortuosa as major compounds as well as evaluate their modes of action.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74471-9.

Introduction

Currently, nanotechnology is estimated as the source of several biotechnological explores, and inventions in this century and is considered the impending industrial innovation. Nanomaterials were deliberated as a phenomenon of advanced medicine, which prompted considerable attention over the past few years1. The bulky phases of the nanomaterials reflected their values with notable biological and physicochemical features. The particles at nano-size (1–100 nm) have gained much interest, as they are characterized by increased surface area which improved their reactivity. This enables various applications in medicinal, pharmaceutical, and biotechnology fields2. The synthesis of nanomaterials via biosynthetic protocols, or green protocols gained more attention by scientists and researchers worldwide3.

The development of employing inorganic nanoparticles for biomedical requests is particularly noteworthy. For instance, zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO–NPs) have been extensively studied for their antimicrobial properties, making them suitable for coating medical devices and implants to prevent infections4. Silver nanoparticles (Ag–NPs) are widely recognized for their potent antibacterial and antifungal activities and are used in wound dressings, antibacterial coatings, and water purification systems5. Selenium dioxide nanoparticles (SeO2–NPs) are explored for their anticancer properties, as selenium is an essential micronutrient known to have chemopreventive effects6. Moreover, metal/metal oxide nanoparticles have applications beyond the biomedical field. In environmental science, these nanoparticles are used in water treatment processes due to their ability to degrade pollutants and contaminants7. In the agricultural sector, they are used to develop nanopesticides and nano-fertilizers, which enhance crop yield and protect against pests more efficiently than traditional methods8. In the energy sector, metal oxide nanoparticles are integral components of solar cells and batteries, improving energy conversion and storage capacities9. Numerous researchers have recommended that plants appear to be the chief candidate and are suitable for a great level of biosynthesis of metal nanoparticles with various nanoparticles’ shapes comprising increased rates than the other bio-sources10. The imperative chemical constituents of the plant extract such as phenols, flavonoids, alkaloids, lipids, and enzymes, participate in the bioproduction process and hence stabilize the formed nanoparticles11,12. Metal oxide nanoparticles demonstrated remarkable features such as band gap, great binding energy, and extraordinary piezoelectric properties13. The green protocols for the biosynthesis of metal/metal oxide nanoparticles delivered safe, economical, biocompatible, and nontoxic nanomaterials, which these nanomaterials can be applied in different fields of industry, medicine, and nanotechnology14,15.

Deverra tortuosa (Desf.) DC. is a common medicinal, grazing, and salt-permissive wild desert plant that can grow naturally in different habitats of the Arabian deserts16,17. D. tortuosa is a candidate of the Apiaceae family, which panels a distinctive spicy or aromatic odor. Many studies have focused on exploring the chemical profile of the different organs of this plant and found that it is rich in various active constituents such as phenols, terpenes, coumarins, sterols, and essential oils18–21. The plant has been utilized efficiently in traditional medicine as an analgesic, carminative, diuretic, antiasthmatic, terminate stomach pain, and against intestinal parasites22.

Recently, Selim, et al.22 have prepared zinc oxide nanoparticles using the aqueous extract of D. tortuosa and assessed the cytotoxic activity against human colon adenocarcinoma, and human lung adenocarcinoma. Fayed, et al.21 have explored the chemical constituents of the essential oil of D. tortuosa aerial issues and applied this extract to allelopathy, antimicrobial, and antioxidant features. Guetat, et al.23 have also studied the characterization of the chemical constituents, and bioactivity of D. tortuosa grown in Saudi Arabia. Moreover, Oueslati, et al.24 have characterized the marmin and furanocoumarins from the plant materials grown in Saudi Arabia and assessed their α-glucosidase, cytotoxic, and antibacterial impacts. In addition, the bioactivity of the essential oil of this plant extract and its nanoemulsion was estimated by Almadiy, et al.20.

The integration of wild plants for medicinal, pharmaceutical, agricultural, and industrial applications supports environmental balance and reduces the negative impacts associated with industrial methods. In this study, the synthesis method used is based on the green synthesis of metal and metal oxide nanoparticles (ZnO, SeO₂, Ag) using D. tortuosa methanol extract. In connection with our previous work, the objectives of the present work were to investigate and characterize the chemical profile of the volatile components of D. tortuosa methanol extract of the aerial parts, and to apply a green synthesis of metal/metal oxides nano solutions including zinc oxide, selenium dioxide, and silver nanoparticles. The aim of the work was extended to assess the bioactivity of these nano-solutions, besides the methanol extract of the plant issues as antioxidant, cytotoxic, and antimicrobial agents.

Results and discussion

Phytochemical analysis

The chemical composition of D. tortuosa extract and solutions containing metal/metal oxide nanoparticles were assessed, specifically measuring the contents of phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and tannins. The D. tortuosa extract exhibited the highest amounts of phenolics (109.0 mg gallic acid/1 g dry extract), flavonoids (17.13 mg catechin/1 g dry extract), and tannins (13.36 mg tannic acid/1 g dry extract) when compared to solutions containing metal nanoparticles (Table 1). The presence of phenolic components, which are responsible for converting metal ions into metal oxide nanoparticles, is consistent with the results reported in the literature25.

The values of the metal ions were lower in the metal nanoparticle solution compared to the original plant extract. This is due to the phytochemical components assisting in the oxidation of the metal ions into their respective nano-sized metal/oxides respectively. Specifically, ZnO–NPs exhibited the highest phenolic content (57.60 mg gallic acid/1 g dry extract) compared to the phenolic contents of SeO2–NPs and AgNPs. The flavonoid contents of the SeO2–NPs and AgNPs were found to have similar performance, with 8.79 mg catechin/1 g dry extract for SeO2–NPs and 7.25 mg catechin/1 g dry extract for AgNPs. Similarly, there was a significant decrease in the tannin content values compared to the extracted D. tortuosa (13.36 mg tannic acid/1 g dry extract) when using metal nanoparticle solutions, with values ranging from 10.10 to 11.81 mg tannic acid/1 g dry extract.

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry analysis (GC–MS)

The GC–MS analysis was used to characterize the components of the extracted plant. The identification of the chemical components was determined by analyzing their molecular structure, molecular weight, and estimated fragments at a certain retention time. The interpretation of the components was assembled based on the conducted mass spectrum GC–MS utilizing the database of WILEY 09, and NIST 14. The component name, classification, retention time, area percentage, molecular weight, and formula of the components of the extracted plant were ascertained. The amount of comparative percentage of each component was deliberated by fitting its average peak area to the total areas. It is crucial to ascertain if these species possess any distinct compounds or groups in order to establish and strengthen their commercial and traditional use as herbal remedies. Additionally, it is beneficial to explore the most appropriate attitudes of extracting these components. These results will successively be considered in the light of their predictable biological or therapeutic features. The recorded data were obtained during 35.72 min of retention time of the scan. The structures of the most abundant components of the extracted D. tortuosa are presented in Fig. 2. The GC–MS analysis showed and characterized thirty-one components at different retention times. The major compounds were hydrocarbons e.g. 2-methyl-3-oxocyclopent-1-ene-1-carboxylic acid (13.24%), and 6-allyl-4,5-dimethoxybenzo[d][1,3]-dioxole (10.06%) and esters of fatty acids e.g. methyl oleate (11.44%), and (2-phenyl-1,3-dioxolan-4-yl)methyl oleate (7.58%).

The GC–MS analysis also investigated other major volatile components from the methanol-extracted sample of D. tortuosa e.g. (S)-2-(4-methylcyclohex-3-en-1-yl)propan-2-ol (5.61%), 1-allyl-2,3,4,5-tetramethoxybenzene (6.40%), 1,1,7-trimethyl-4-methylenedecahydro-1 H-cyclopropa[e]azulen-7-ol (6.72%), and (2-phenyl-1,3-dioxolan-4-yl)methyl oleate (7.58%). The third order of abundance for these groups of components was interpreted for 2-(4a-methyl-8-methylene-decahydronaphthalen-2-yl)propan-2-ol (3.23%), (Z)-octadec-11-enoic acid (3.63%), ethyl palmitate (4.30%), and 6,10,14-trimethylpentadecan-2-one (4.44%). The identified components can be categorized into three main classes (terpenes, hydrocarbons, and fatty acids and esters of fatty acids). The hydrocarbons, fatty acids, and esters of fatty acids classes demonstrated the majority of the components according to the area percentages with 42.32, and 42.12%, respectively. The least abundant components were terpenes which show 15.56%. The class of terpenes included three basic components with the most abundant area for 1,1,7-trimethyl-4-methylenedecahydro-1 H-cyclopropa[e]azulen-7-ol (6.72%). The hydrocarbons class includes eleven components with the major abundant component interpreted as 2-methyl-3-oxocyclopent-1-ene-1-carboxylic acid (13.24%). Moreover, the category of fatty acids and esters comprised 17 components with the most majority of composition for methyl oleate (11.44%), and (2-phenyl-1,3-dioxolan-4-yl)methyl oleate (7.58%).

Makhlouf-Gafsi, et al.26 have reported the monosaccharide from D. tortuosa. In this context, twenty-three volatile components have been characterized via the GC/MS analysis by Sharaf27. The most abundant components were identified as methyl palmitate (20.12%), methyl oleate (28.39%), and methyl (9Z,12Z)-octadeca-9,12-dienoate (16.35%). The investigated volatile components vary between aromatics, fatty acids or their esters, hydrocarbons, and terpenes. More recently, Fayed, et al.21 have developed the characterization of the essential oil components of the aerial parts of D. tortuosa extracted by hydrodistillation. The results specified that 85.57% of the basic constituents are interrelated to the terpene category. The rest of the components are related to acyclic molecules, hydrocarbons, and carotenoids. Generally, the most abundant molecules were identified as α-terpinene, m-cymene, β-phellandrene, trans-piperitol, cubedol, widdrol, citronellyl tiglate “ester”, and (E)-heptadec-10-ene-8-ynoic acid, methyl ester.

Characterization of the metal/metal oxide nanoparticles

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy

Spectrophotometer runs for the FT-IR spectrum studies were performed using a Thermo-Fisher Nicolet IS10 in the USA. To determine the distinct functional groups of the materials examined, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) was used. Across a broad spectrum, a large amount of high-resolution spectral data was examined. The transmittance percentages were displayed against the frequency scale range of v = 4000 to 500 cm−1. The typical functional groups of all samples were identified by the measurement of FT-IR. The FT-IR analysis revealed the development of metal/and or metal oxide nanoparticles by revealing the removal of certain functional groups and the change in frequencies of other groups. All the samples exhibited absorption bands within the range of v = 3420–3447 cm−1, which correspond to vibrations of very pronounced O–H stretching groups. This suggests the existence of alcoholic groups. At a wavenumber of 3420 cm−1, there was a noticeable change in value, suggesting the involvement of this group in the synthesis of the metal/metal oxide nanoparticles28. On the other hand, silver nanoparticles had moderate absorption bands at ν = 2925–2937 cm−1 for the C–H stretching group, whereas D. tortuosa extract, silver, and selenium dioxide nanoparticles did not exhibit this band. It was discovered that a certain functional group experienced a reduction in quantity during the formation of the metal/metal oxide nanoparticles or did not engage with the metal ions in the solution29. There is an unmistakable absorption band seen at v = 1616–1651 cm−1 in FT-IR, which corresponds to the stretching of C–O bonds or the presence of amidic carbonyl groups. The D. tortuosa extract and its metal/metal oxide nanoparticle samples were analyzed using IR spectroscopy to investigate potential alterations in these categories. Studies on metal/metal oxide nanoparticles revealed alterations within the same frequency range (v = 1387–1419 cm−1). Conversely, analyses of D. tortuosa extract consistently attributed a moderate absorption band at ν = 1385 cm−1 to the C–H bending frequency. The findings confirm the existence of aliphatic chain segments in the structures of the separated components using vibrational analysis. In the analysis of D. tortuosa extract, a significant absorption peak at ν = 1515 cm−1, attributed to C–H bending, was observed. However, this peak was not seen in the analysis of metal/metal oxide nanoparticles.

The presence of sp3 C–H bonds in the alkane chains was shown by these absorption bands. In addition, during the analysis of D. tortuosa extract, selenium dioxide, and zinc oxide nanoparticles, the absorption bands within the range of v = 1179–1263 cm−1 were observed. However, in the case of silver nanoparticles, this absorption band was not present30. The absorption bands at v = 1050–1079 cm−1 observed in the investigation of the D. tortuosa extract and its metal/metal oxide nanoparticles are attributed to the presence of medium C–N stretching groups. Upon analysis of the material containing silver nanoparticles, the C–N stretching band disappeared. A medium S–OR ester was seen at a wavenumber of 865 cm−1 in the analysis of selenium dioxide nanoparticles. However, this functional group was not discovered in the analysis of the plant extract or the other metal/metal oxide nanoparticles. The presence of C-H bending vibrations was verified by the prominent absorption bands seen at ν = 600–742 cm−1. The observed alterations in these values provide strong evidence of the involvement of this group in the synthesis of metallic and oxide nanoparticles.

UV–visible spectrophotometer

The optical characteristics of the generated ZnO–NPs, SeO2–NPs, and AgNPs solutions were assessed using UV–Vis spectroscopic investigations. The UV-Vis spectrophotometer with scan range of 190.0 to 982.0 nm was used. A course is a notable tool to attentive the surface state of the nanoparticle and imagine the stability long-range of the metal/metal oxides nanoparticle. The zeta potential of nanoparticles, which is defined as the electric potential at the boundary of the double layer and typically ranges from + 100 to −100 mV, is measured as the ion mobility of the nanoparticles in solution. Thus, silver nanoparticles revealed a zeta potential value at −13.6 mV, zeta deviation at 13.4 mV, and conductivity at 3.35 mS/cm. Particularly, selenium dioxide nanoparticles displayed a zeta potential value at −18.6 mV, zeta deviation at 13.4 mV, and conductivity at 0.0277 mS/cm. In accordance with what Honary and Zahir33 found, nanoparticles having zeta potential values below + 25 mV or above − 25 mV exhibited an exceptionally high level of stability.

Additionally, selectivity index (SI) values were determined for each cell line and compared to those derived for WI-38. The high SI values indicate that the nanoparticles target cancer cells more effectively than normal cells. The IC50 is the concentration at which the tested substance induces cell death in 50% of the cells, and it may serve as an indicator of the extent of its cytotoxic impact.

In particular, ZnO–NPs displayed the most potent cytotoxicity against HePG2 cell lines (IC50 = 25.28 mg/mL, SI- 3.85) relative to the result of D. tortuosa extract (IC50 = 73.57 mg/mL, SI = 2.31). The result of ZnO-NPs with moderate cytotoxic impact is comparative to that of the chemotherapeutic standard, doxorubicin (IC50 = 6.03 mg/mL). The SeO2–NPs revealed improved cytotoxicity on HePG2 cell lines than AgNPs. The cytotoxic results also indicated that the most improved activity for AgNPs on MCF-7 cell lines (IC50 = 33.91 mg/mL, SI = 1.52) relative to the plant extract (IC50 = 91.07 mg/mL, SI = 1.11), and other nano-solutions. Also, the AgNPs solution revealed the most amended cytotoxicity on PC3 cell lines (IC50 = 45.89 mg/mL, SI = 1.66) relative to the plant extract (IC50 = 83.81 mg/mL, SI = 1.20), and other nano-solutions (IC50 = 56.90 & 50.31 mg/mL). All the samples displayed very weakly non-cytotoxic effects on the normal cell line (WI-38) (IC50 = > 100 mg/mL).

The cytotoxic effects of metal/metal oxide nanoparticle solutions on tumor cell lines are more significant compared to those of plant extracts, as reported in the literature4,6. The strong cytotoxicity of nanoparticles could be linked to their ability to induce oxidative stress in cancer cells by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS). This ROS generation leads to mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA damage, and apoptosis, particularly in cancer cells, which are more susceptible to oxidative stress44,45. Based on results the SI values for the NPs against cancer cell lines (ranging from 1.37 to 3.85) further support the potential of these nanoparticles to selectively target cancer cells while sparing healthy cells. Figure 7 also presented the estimated percentages of inhibition at different concentrations (1.56–100 mg/mL) of the tested samples against the tumor, and normal cell lines. Thus, the higher sample concentration led to the most potent activity against all cell lines.

At a concentration of 100 mg/mL, the D. tortuosa extract recorded 49.74% of inhibition percentage against HePG2 cells, 52.84% of inhibition percentage against MCF-7 cells, 51.05% of inhibition percentage on PC3 cells, and 9.22% of inhibition percentage against WI-38 cells. AgNPs displayed the most potent inhibition percentages against HePG2 (73.89%), AgNPs against MCF-7 (71.78%), and SeO2-NPs against PC3 cell lines (69.89%) at the higher concentration of the samples (100 mg/mL) (Figure S3). We observed that concentrations below 100 µg/mL seem to be a safe threshold for therapeutic use, as none of the nanoparticles showed significant cytotoxic effects on normal cells at levels below this concentration. This range aligns well with cytotoxicity data for other metal nanoparticles found in the literature30,31,39. Therefore, the studying of the mechanism of action of the tested samples against the varied tumor cell lines requires the investigation of the effects of the nature of the tested cell line, the nature of the nanoparticles (shape, size, and surface morphology), and the structural nature of the plant extracted components. Recent reports confirmed the induced cytotoxic impacts of zinc oxide nanoparticles for wound healing44, in addition to the amended cytotoxic activity of selenium dioxide nanoparticles45, and silver nanoparticles42.

It was noted that plants such as Pyrus communis L. which are rich in lipids, and fatty acids, methyl oleate displayed potent cytotoxic activity46. In this route, three categories were classified for this plant extract according to the GC/MS analysis including terpenes, hydrocarbons, fatty acids, and lipids. The active ingredients of the plant extracted components, e.g. phytochemical constitutes, have shown a cytotoxic influence against the varied cell lines, whereas the decreased values of the phytochemical contents of the nanoparticle solutions did not affect their cytotoxic impacts owing to the large surface area of these nanoparticles, which enable the excessive interaction with the tumor cell lines47.

Conclusions

The methanol extract of D. tortuosa formulated metal/metal oxide nanoparticles as a green synthesis tool which was confirmed by various methods such as TEM, zeta potential, and UV-visible spectroscopy. The technique operates by using the reactive components present in the plant extract to either oxidize the metal ions or convert them into a zero state, resulting in the creation of metal oxides in the solution at a nanoscale. The plant extract’s active ingredients have the ability to form complex compounds with metal ions and facilitate the reduction of metal/metal oxide nanoparticles by biological means. The GC-MS analysis of the D. tortuosa methanolic extract showed the presence of 31 chemical compounds with 2-methyl-3-oxocyclopent-1-ene-1-carboxylic acid, methyl oleate, and 6-allyl-4,5-dimethoxybenzo[d][1,3]-dioxole as major constituents. The extracted plant and its metal/metal oxide nanoparticles exhibited reduced antioxidant properties compared to the original plant extract but showed enhanced cytotoxic and antibacterial activity. As a result, a set of nano-solutions was created utilizing the environmentally friendly procedure and shown significant efficacy as cytotoxic and anti-microbial agents, making them suitable for incorporation in advanced pharmaceutical research. Furthermore, it is necessary to record the findings of the current research by conducting tests to evaluate the practical use of these treatments in living organisms.

Materials and methods

The preparation of the plant materials

The shoots of D. tortuosa (Fig. 8) were gathered from two populations, one km away, growing within Wadi Araba located in the northern part of the Eastern Desert of Egypt (29° 4ʹ16.19ʹʹ N, 32°15ʹ39.80ʹʹ E). The samples were gathered during the spring of 2022, cleaned from any dust, kept in paper bags, and immediately transferred to the lab. Under shade conditions, the plant specimens were dried at ambient temperature (25 °C ± 3) till complete dryness, then ground to fine powders, and kept in a refrigerator till further analyses and bioassays. The plant specimen was identified by Yasser El-Amier ( an author) PhD of plant taxonomy, Faculty of Science, Mansoura University. A herbarium specimen with no. Mans.0010420003 was deposited the Herbarium of Faculty of Science, Mansoura University, Egypt.

Fig. 8Deverra tortuosa (Desf.) DC. plant growing in sandy habitat.

Deverra tortuosa (Desf.) DC. plant growing in sandy habitat.

Preparation of the plant extract

In a 1000 mL conical flask, 50 g of the plant material was mixed with 500 mL of methanol. After four h of shaking in a horizontal shaker at room temperature, the mixture was settled and filtered using Whatman filter paper no. 1. (125 mm, Cat No 1003 125, Germany). The filtrate was stored in a sterile vial container and kept in the refrigerator at −5 °C till further analysis.

Green synthesis of metal/metal oxides nanoparticles

A green protocol was employed for the synthesis of metal nanoparticle solutions using D. tortuosa plant extract according to the methodology of Joshi, et al.48. In brief, 1 mmol of metal slat solutions of zinc sulfate, selenium sulfate, and silver nitrate were prepared in 20 mL of deionized water. In each case, the salt solution was gradually added to the plant extract (0.5 mg/mL) while the mixture was being stirred (20 mL). After letting the combination settle at 40 °C for 90 min in the dark, it was stirred for two hours at room temperature before any color change was observed. After 3 days, the mixture’s absorbance was measured and compared to that of plant extract and metal salt solution. The produced MNPs solutions were reserved in a dark sterilized bottle and stored at −5 °C.

Characterization of metal nanoparticles

The initial characterization for the confirmation of biosynthesis of NPs was done by UV-VIS (Shimadzu UV-VIS 2450) spectrum analysis in the wavelength range of 190–1100 nm. At room temperature, readings from an FT-IR spectrometer (model number Mattson-5000) were taken between 400 and 4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 8 cm−1. The TEM (JEOL TEM-2100, Tokyo, Japan), located at the Electron Microscope Unit at Mansoura University, Egypt, was used to determine the physical properties and chemical structure of the prepared nanoparticles, including the particle’s size, shape, surface nature, crystal structure, and morphological data according to the method of Otunola, et al.5. A magnification of 200 nm was used in the study. According to Bhattacharjee32, the Zeta potential method to evaluate the surface charge of the produced zinc nanoparticles in the suspension using Malvern Instruments Ltd. Zeta Potential Ver. 2.3 (Kassel, Germany). The approach is useful for investigating nanoparticle surfaces, and the particles should be rather stable over time33.

Characterization of the chemical component

Phytochemical analysis

Total phenolic contents

The phenolic content was quantitatively analyzed using methanol extracts from plants and nanoparticle solutions of metals and metal oxides. Characteristic values were calculated using a standard curve of Gallic acid and represented as mg Gallic acid equivalents per 1 g of dry extract using the Folin–Ciocalteu (F-C) assay, as described by Lamuela-Raventós49. Methodology details are provided for constructing a standard curve in terms of gallic acid (y = 0.0065x, r2 = 0.991).

Total flavonoid contents

Flavonoid concentration is reported in terms of mg catechin equivalent per 1 g of dry extract. In situ, the analysis was performed using aluminum chloride according to the method of Zhishen, et al.50. The catechin standard curve was utilized using the following surveyed equation (y = 0.0031 x, r2 = 0.979).

Total tannin contents

The vanillin hydrochloride assay was used to determine the tannin concentration51, in which a vanillin hydrochloride solution in methanol was produced in situ and used to treat the sample before measuring its absorbance. The obtained tannin contents were reported as tannic acid equivalents in mg/1 g of dry extract. The following formula was used to get the tannin concentration from the tannic acid standard curve: (y = 0.0008x; r2 = 0.962).

Gas chromatography–mass spectroscopic analysis (GC–MS)

GC/MS spectrometry was employed to investigate and interpret the active constituents of the volatile components of the extracted D. tortuosa. The sample was implemented on a Trace GC-TSQ mass spectrometer (Thermo-Scientific, Austin, TX, USA)52. The chemical constituents were scrutinized based on the databases of WILEY 09, and NIST 14 (cf. Supplementary file).

Biological activities assays

Antioxidant activity

The DPPH• assay was used to determine the antioxidant activity of the plant extract and the metal nanoparticle solutions as described by Kitts, et al.53. Each sample was diluted serially in methanol until it reached the same concentration across the series. In the serial dilution, an equal amount of 0.135 mM DPPH• solution was added to each sample. The samples were then stored in the dark at 25℃ for 30 min. Ascorbic acid was used and treated the same as the samples as a standard antioxidant. To determine the IC50, the percentage of DPPH• that was not depleted was plotted against the concentration of the sample using an exponential curve. The IC50 values represented the concentrations of antioxidants needed to reduce the initial concentration of DPPH• solution by 50%54.

Antibacterial activity

Culture media

The nutrient agar (28.0 g) was subjected to a conical flask (2 L) and mixed with 1 L of distilled water. The contents of the conical flask were heated until boiling point for the complete dissolution of the medium. Sterilization of the mixture by autoclave was accomplished at 15 lbs pressure, 121 for 15 min. The medium was left to cool to 45–50 °C, enriched with 5–10% blood or biological fluids. The mixture was shaken and poured into sterile Petri plates.

Microbial species

The microbial species were obtained from the Cairo Microbiological Resources Centre (Cairo MIRCEN), Faculty of Agriculture, Ain Shams University. Gram-negative bacterial species: Escherichia coli (ATCK 10527), Salmonella typhimurium (ATCK 25594), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCK 9052), and Klebsiella pneumoniae (ATCK 10076). Gram-positive bacterial species: Bacillus cereus (EMCF number 1099), Staphylococcus aureus (ATCK 6566), Bacillus subtilis (DMSF 1083), and Staphylococcus epidermidis (ATCK 12233). Pathogenic yeast: Candida albicans (EMCF number 182).

Microbial assessment

Using an agar well diffusion technique with inoculums of 106 bacterial cells/mL to disseminate over nutrient agar plates, the antibacterial activity of the extracted plant problems was assessed17. Overnight, sterile filter paper discs (Whatman no.1, 6 mm in diameter) were saturated with the plant extract, while a second set of discs was saturated with methanol. The discs were then placed over agar plates already containing certain bacteria. After incubating the plates at 37 °C for 18–24 h, the diameters (mm) of the inhibitory zones were measured55.

Cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity assay using D. tortuosa extracts was conducted via three different eukaryotic cell lines provided by VACSERA, a holding company for biological products and vaccines, Cairo, Egypt. These cell lines are HePG2 (hepatocellular carcinoma), MCF-7 (breast cancer), PC3 (human prostate), and WI-38 (fibroblasts derived from lung tissue). To assess the cytotoxic potential of the methanol extract of D. tortuosa and its metal/metal oxide nanoparticles, an MTT colorimetric assay was implemented by seeding the tested cells onto 96-well plates. MTT solution was prepared in a water/ethanol mixture and mixed with buffer solution, and media. Under sterilized conditions, the technique was retained through the use of a laminar air-flow cabinet for the culturing processes following the adopted procedure by Opoku, et al.56. The percentages of inhibition, and cell viability, were calculated at different concentrations, as well as the IC50 values.

Statistical analysis

The experiments of both antioxidant activity and antibacterial activity were performed three times with three replications for each treatment. The data were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), afterward, Duncan’s test using the CoStat software program (version 6.311, CoHort Software, Monterey, CA, USA). The data were expressed as mean values with standard error. The average values of IC50 for assays were calculated graphically via an exponential line analysis using MS-Excel 2016.
