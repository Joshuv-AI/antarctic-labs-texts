Patterns of shared variation in knee ultrasound for osteoarthritis: a machine learning approach
Authors: Sahar Sawani, Liubov Arbeeva, Katherine A. Yates, Carolina Alvarez, Todd A. Schwartz, Serena Savage-Guin, Jordan B. Renner, Catherine J. Bakewell, Minna J. Kohler, Janice Lin, Jonathan Samuels, Amanda E. Nelson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo identify phenotypes of knee osteoarthritis (KOA) based on demographic and clinical variables, symptoms, and ultrasound (US) features using a novel machine learning (ML) approach.DesignParticipants in the Johnston County Health Study provided demographics, symptomatic and functional assessments, and joint radiographs, which were transformed into the clinical data block. Standardized knee US were obtained, and US features composed the second data block. The Angle-based Joint and Individual Variation Explained (AJIVE) algorithm was used to identify shared and individual modes of variation. We focused on shared structure to explore how US features and non-US clinical data varied together overall, and in the subset with radiographic KOA (rKOA).ResultsThis analysis included 861 participants (mean age 55 years, mean BMI 33 kg/m2); 335 (39 %) had rKOA. AJIVE identified two components of shared variation (subgroup and SC2). SC1 associated osteophytes and cartilage damage on US with higher BMI, older age, and worse symptoms and outcome scores. SC2 correlated effusion and synovitis but less cartilage damage on US, with better physical function and lower BMI. A similar pattern was seen in those with rKOA.ConclusionsWe identified two shared directions of variation that may represent distinct phenotypes of KOA. The first is consistent with prior studies linking osteophytes and cartilage damage to worse symptoms and function. The second may represent an inflammatory subtype of KOA, with greater effusion and synovitis but less osteophytosis and cartilage damage. These clinically feasible phenotypes should be confirmed in future studies.

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common joint disease in the world, and recent studies have demonstrated an increased age-adjusted prevalence in the United States over the last two decades1. As a major contributor to disability and health care utilization, OA is associated with a high socioeconomic burden1. Effective management of OA is of great interest, but treating OA as a single, homogeneous disease may be misguided. Several phenotypes of knee (K) OA have been described, including age and cellular senescence-associated, post-injury, inflammatory-predominant, and obesity-associated2. Differentiating these phenotypes through clinical features and imaging is a key step in designing and testing future therapeutics.

OA is most frequently diagnosed through plain radiographs, which are widely accessible and relatively inexpensive. Radiographs can demonstrate joint space narrowing, osteophyte formation, and subchondral sclerosis and cysts, but radiographic features do not reliably correlate with symptoms. This may be partly attributed to low sensitivity for early changes of OA and inability to assess soft tissues and inflammation. Ultrasound (US) captures these features and can be performed efficiently, reliably, and affordably in clinical and research settings3. US features including cartilage damage, osteophytes, and synovial effusions have been associated with poorer WOMAC scores4. US can also serve as a helpful prognostic tool in patients with or without initial diagnosis of KOA. Medial meniscus extrusion on US can predict development and progression of KOA5. When combined with clinical data and radiographs, US features can help clinicians classify patients into subtypes of KOA

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have numerous uses in KOA. A recent review of deep learning in rheumatologic imaging found that KOA is frequently investigated, but most studies have focused on interpreting radiographs and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) rather than US6. One study, using Norwegian registry data, evaluated the predictive power of US features for total knee replacement and found that osteophytes and femoral cartilage thickness on US were significant predictors in a univariate analysis7.

ML methods can utilize multidimensional and multisource data to delineate different phenotypes of OA8. Prior work by our group has applied novel ML methodologies to interpret MRI-based cartilage maps and incorporate clinical data to propose distinct phenotypes of KOA9. The aim of the present study was to apply a ML approach to explore potential phenotypes of KOA based on demographic and clinical variables, symptoms, and US features in a general population and in the subset with radiographic KOA (rKOA).

Design

We used data from participants enrolled in the population-based Johnston County Health Study (JoCoHS)10. Participants provided demographic and clinical information and completed symptomatic and functional questionnaires including the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) and Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS). Standardized knee radiographs were obtained and scored according to Kellgren-Lawrence grade (KLG), as previously described10. These data were standardized and combined into one set of measurements—the clinical data block (Supplemental Table A). Continuous variables were transformed with a shifted log transformation. to ensure that distributions were close to normality and to bring variables to the same scale. Binary variables were centered by subtracting the mean of each variable.

US images were obtained by a trained sonographer and scored independently by two expert readers, with disagreements adjudicated by a third, independent reader. 3 For this analysis, US features were scored for each knee and a worst knee was defined and used for all subsequent analyses at the person-level as follows: (1) the knee with the highest KLG; (2) if KLG was the same, the knee with the highest pain, aching, stiffness (PAS) in the past year; (3) if this PAS was the same, the knee with the highest PAS in the past month; and (4) if this PAS was the same, a random knee was selected. US features—including effusion/synovitis, medial and lateral osteophytes, meniscal extrusion, and articular cartilage damage—were graded. The US data comprised the second data block.

Additional analyses were performed for the subset of knees with rKOA (ie, KLG ≥ 2). Variables derived from the knee KLG (e.g., symptomatic KOA) were excluded from the clinical data block with non-US features for this subset.

The ML approach utilized the Angle-based Joint and Individual Variation Explained (AJIVE) algorithm9, which identifies shared (i.e., common to both data blocks) and individual (i.e., specific to each block) modes of variation (see Supplemental Materials, Supplemental Figure A and B)., We focused on shared structure to explore how US features and non-US clinical data vary together overall and among knees with rKOA. Loading plots were used to visualize the contribution of each US and non-US variable to a given mode of variation. Variables with absolute values for loadings less than an arbitrarily defined threshold of 0.1 are not shown.

Discussion

We identified two directions of shared variation that represent two patterns, or potentially different phenotypes, of KOA, incorporating both US and clinical features. The first shared direction (SC1) linked the presence of osteophytes and cartilage damage on US to worse symptoms and function in KOA, as measured by KOOS and PROMIS scores, overall (Fig. 1) and in the subgroup with rKOA (Fig. 2). The second shared direction (SC2) in both groups may represent a more inflammatory type of KOA, associating presence of effusion and synovitis but less cartilage damage/osteophytosis on US with lower BMI and better physical function, seen more frequently in men.

Concordance between the severity of rKOA and knee pain is known to be imperfect, but US may help bridge this gap with features such as osteophytes and cartilage thickness being associated with worse pain scores4, 11. In particular, medial osteophytes on US have been identified as predictors of worse WOMAC scores11. Similarly, in our analysis, the presence of medial osteophytes is more strongly associated with this shared direction compared to lateral osteophytes, possibly due to the predominance of medial OA. Cartilage damage —both medially and laterally —was also associated with more severe symptoms and worse functional status, corroborating previous observations4.

The first shared direction of variation was associated with higher BMI. Other studies12, 13 have linked high BMI with presence of osteophytes and cartilage damage on MRI and radiographs, but fewer studies have examined this with US. Analysis of data from the Osteoarthritis Initiative demonstrated that patients with higher BMI had more prominent synovial inflammation and associated cartilage compositional degeneration on MRI13. Hart et al. found that high BMI and older age were associated with osteophytes on radiographs12, consistent with SC1 in our study.

The second shared direction, SC2, appears to represent a more inflammatory type of KOA, with presence of effusion and synovitis but with less cartilage damage (or less osteophytosis among those with rKOA). Clinical features associated with variation in this direction included faster gait speed, better physical function, lower BMI, male sex, and older age. This shared direction could represent a distinct clinical phenotype (supported by our data in Fig. 2 and Supplemental Figure C) or an earlier stage of disease; longitudinal follow up is needed to assess the evolution of clinical and US features over time.

The relationship between less cartilage damage and improved physical function in SC2 corroborates previously mentioned studies4. A recent systematic literature review found a significant association between inflammation (effusion, synovitis, and Baker’s cyst) on knee US and MRI and pain, although this association was moderate (correlation coefficients 0.19 to 0.61)14. The authors of this systematic review noted that it was unclear whether synovitis itself is a cause of pain or a surrogate for other aspects of knee pathology. This moderate association compared with the strong association of medial osteophytes and cartilage damage with worse pain scores11 aligns with our models, in which KOOS symptoms contributed more strongly to SC1 than SC2.

SC2 is particularly of interest from a treatment standpoint. A recent multi-center, randomized, double-blind placebo-controlled trial of methotrexate (Methotrexate) versus placebo in 207 patients with symptomatic KOA found statistically significant pain reduction in the methotrexate (Methotrexate) group at 6 months, but this benefit was not sustained at 12 months15. This study and other previous studies of therapeutics in KOA have not utilized US to phenotype patients prior to treatment or during data analysis. Given the more inflammatory features identified in SC2, it is possible that this KOA subtype would respond more robustly to immunosuppressive treatment like methotrexate (Methotrexate) compared to the non-inflammatory subtype. If so, targeted treatment could halt or slow progression of symptomatic KOA in these patients, a previously elusive goal.

ML has proven to be promising in identifying KOA subtypes, prognosticating outcomes, and identifying target populations for treatment8. Previous studies using ML have identified a cluster of patients with KOA and higher BMI, high comorbidity burden, and low physical activity8 —consistent with SC1 in our study. To our knowledge, ML methods have not previously identified the more inflammatory KOA subset represented by SC2. This finding speaks to the importance of US—an easily accessible but largely underutilized tool6 —in phenotyping KOA in the era of ML.

Strengths of our study include the large sample size and inclusion of participants with and without KOA. This population-based study was conducted in a diverse county, so our participants represent the wider American population, and the identified phenotypes are potentially more generalizable. Another strength of our study was the novel methodology used to identify the directions of shared variation, which may identify distinct features and phenotypes9.

One limitation of our study is that symptomatic and functional assessments, as well as imaging, were captured at one point in time. We cannot say with certainty whether patients with the more inflammatory subtype represent an earlier stage of KOA or a distinct subtype altogether, although the former seems more likely. Following this cohort over time, and repeating these analyses in other longitudinal cohorts, may provide additional insight into the natural progression of clinical and US features in KOA. Additionally, the optimal sample size for AJIVE analyses is not known, and although we applied AJIVE overall and in the rKOA subgroup, we did not separately assess the non-rKOA subgroup.

Conclusion

Our study identified two shared directions of variation, which may represent distinct phenotypes of KOA, based on a variety of clinical and US features. These clinically feasible phenotypes may provide opportunities for targeted therapy, and should be confirmed in larger, longitudinal studies.
