Ultrasonographic verification of hepatic hydatidosis in a female dromedary camel: a case report
Authors: Mohamed THARWAT, Hazem M EL MOGHAZY, Shin OIKAWA
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis report describes for the first time, the antemortem diagnosis of hydatidosis in dromedary camel based on the WHO informal working groups on echinococcosis guidelines of echinococcosis classification. The case was admitted at the University Veterinary Hospital, Qassim University, Saudi Arabia, with a history of progressive weight loss and decreased appetite for the past 4 months. Alterations in hematobiochemical parameters included leukopenia, neutropenia, decreased hematocrit percent, red blood cells count and hemoglobin concentration, hypoproteinemia, hypoalbuminemia, hyperglobulinemia, hyperglycemia and azotemia. Ultrasonographically, multiple cysts were imaged within the hepatic tissue containing anechoic fluid and fibrin tags. Ultrasound-guided aspiration of a cyst revealed a clear non-turbid fluid. Ultrasonographic diagnostic is recommended for the work-up of cystic lesions of camel liver suspected to be due to Echinococcus granulosus.Keywords: animal, camel, hydatidosis, pathology, ultrasound

Hydatidosis or hydatid disease (cystic echinococcosis) is a worldwide parasitic zoonotic disease caused by taeniid tapeworms, belonging to the Echinococcus granulosus sensu lato complex (E. granulosus s.l.) [18]. The domestic life cycle of this infection involves dogs as definitive hosts and a broad spectrum of mammals (e.g. sheep, goats, water buffalo, cattle) as intermediate hosts. The intermediate hosts become infected through ingestion of pasture grass contaminated with E. granulosus s.l. eggs released by infected dogs. The cycle is completed when definitive hosts ingest cysts (metacestodes) present in different organs (e.g., liver, lungs, spleen, heart) of infected intermediate hosts [3]. Hydatidosis depends on the dog-sheep cycle and is actively transmitted in all pastoral regions where sheep, cattle and camelids predominate [2].

Human is an accidental intermediate host where the organs commonly affected by this disease include the liver (70%) and lungs (20%), while involvement of other organs is rare. However, intra-abdominal hydatidosis can be observed in other organs such as the peritoneum, spleen, kidney, and pancreas [7, 10, 11, 13, 46]. In a large-scale abattoir study conducted in Saudi Arabia on 91348 animals (camels 541; cattle 615, goats 48370 and sheep 41822), the prevalence of hydatidosis was 6.86%, 3.63%, 69.6% & 19.85% in camels, cattle, sheep and goats respectively, and liver was the predominant site of infection in all animals [42]. In another abattoir study conducted in Iran on 198 dromedary camels, 29 (14.64%) of camels were infected [19]. Hydatid cysts were also discovered from 35.2% (233/661) and 30.82% (135/438) of camels (Camelus dromedarius) slaughtered in 5 different regions of Iran [1, 9]. The overall prevalence rate of hydatidosis in another study conducted on 304 camels in Nigeria was 14.14% [45]. In a recent study conducted in Egypt on 152 slaughtered dromedary camels, the prevalence of hydatidosis was 21.7% and the highest infection rate observed in lungs was 87.87%, while it was 9% in livers [22]. Therefore, hydatidosis in animals is always discovered incidentally postmortem at abattoirs, as it is difficult to suspect and detect the disease antemortem.

In human, liver cystic lesions are detected by different diagnostic imaging techniques, such as ultrasonography, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or computed tomography (CT) [23]. Ultrasound represents a safe and cost-efficient superior method to both computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging in the visualization of the cystic lesions morphology because it carries no risk of radiation exposure and does not require general anesthesia [17]. Also, ultrasonography facilitates the recognition of cystic ecchinococosis in asymptomatic disease stages [14]. The WHO informal working group on echinococcosis (IWGE) guidelines for cyst staging provide us with a worldwide uniform sonographic classification of echinococcosis and it considered a definitive diagnostic method in human being. The criteria allow for simple assessment and interpretation of cystic liver lesions in routine clinical settings [4]. We regard that, application of this method in animals will be effective, so we tried to apply these ultrasonographic classification criteria to an animal case admitted at our university hospital. This report describes and for the first time antemortem diagnosis of hydatidosis in a female dromedary camel with hepatic hydatidosis. Parallel, the clinical and hematobiochemical findings are described.

A 10-year-old female dromedary camel was presented at the University Veterinary Hospital, Qassim University, Saudi Arabia, with a history of progressive weight loss and decreased appetite for the past 4 months. The owner stated that, the camel was previously treated with different medications including, broad spectrum antibiotics, analgesics, anti-inflammatories, and stomachics; however, with no response and the case condition deteriorated rapidly.

A complete blood count [total and differential leukocytic count, erythrocyte count, hematocrit, hemoglobin, mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH) and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC)] was carried out on the EDTA sample using the VetScan HM5 (Abaxis, Union City, CA, USA). The automated biochemical analyzer VetScan VS2 (Abaxis) was used to determine the serum concentrations of total protein, albumin, globulin, glucose, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, total bilirubin, sodium and potassium.

The foreleg of the female camel was flexed and tied at the carpal joint; the head was then held and the animal pushed to sternal recumbency. The fore and hind legs were then tied near the carpal and hock joints, respectively. Both thoracic and abdominal sides were clipped and the skin shaved. Ultrasonographic examination was carried out using a 3.5 MHz convex probe of the SonoScape ultrasound device (Sonoscape Medical Corp., Shenzhen, Guangdong, China). After the application of transmission gel to the transducer, the thorax and abdomen were examined thoroughly as previously reported [30,31,32,33, 35, 38]. Twenty-four hours after admission, the animal collapsed and unfortunately postmortem examination was not carried out.

Ultrasound images were interpreted in accordance with the WHO IWGE [44] classification. Based on this classification, liver cysts were categorized as cystic lesions (CLs) or cystic echinococcosis in stages 1–5 (CE1-CE5) [6, 24]. CE stages 1 and 2 are considered active disease stages in the WHO IWGE guidelines. Patients were classified as “infected” or “not infected” based on the ultrasound diagnostic criteria as following; CE1: Unilocular, round to oval, echo-free cysts, often double walled “Snowflake effect” apparent on shifting the patient’s position due to free-floating protoscolices. CE2: Daughter cysts visible as “wheel spokes” or “honeycomb” pattern within mother cysts. CE3: is considered a transitional stage of disease as activity still possible. The laminate membrane on the cysts’ inner surface dissolves as part of the degenerative process and produces the “water lily” sign. CE4: Heterogeneous internal structure with appearance of a “ball of wool”. The cysts are inactive. CE5: The inactive cyst has a thick, arch-like calcified wall that casts a conical ultrasound shadow. No protoscolices remain visible [4].

This female camel had a history of grazing on pastures near areas with stray dogs, progressive weight loss and decreased appetite for the past 4 months. It was treated with different medications including, broad spectrum antibiotics, analgesics, anti-inflammatories, and stomachics; however, with no response. Clinical examination revealed that, the animal was suffered from severe emaciation, weakness, depression, dullness, subnormal temperature (35°C), weak and irregular ruminal contractions, dehydration and dyspnea.

Hematological examination revealed hematocrit 10.6% (reference range 28.9 ± 2.7%), RBCs 4.4 × 106/µL (reference range 11.3 ± 1.4 × 106/µL), hemoglobin 7.4 g/dL (reference range 16.0 ± 2.3 g/dL), MCV 24 fL (reference range 25.5 ± 1.5 fL), MCH 17.1 pg (reference range 14.7 ± 2.4 pg), MCHC 69.8 g/dL (reference range 57.6 ± 9.0 g/dL), white blood cell count 7,780/µL (reference range 16.9 ± 2.7 × 109/L), neutrophils 6,120 /µL (reference range 9.8 ± 3.0 × 109/L), lymphocytes 690 /µL (reference range 5.9 ± 2.4 × 109/L) and eosinophils 900 /µL (11.5%) (reference range 1.5−13.8%).

Blood chemistry panel showed total protein 7.1 g/dL (reference range 7.9 ± 0.4 g/dL), albumin 2.4 g/dL (reference range 4.2 ± 0.4 g/dL), globulin 4.8 g/dL (reference range 3.7 ± 0.5 g/dL), glucose 188 mg/dL (reference range 61 ± 19 mg/dL), ALP 58 U/L (reference range 32−110 U/L), ALT 12 U/L (reference range 6–25 U/L), BUN 29 mg/dL (reference range 17 ± 10.0 mg/dL), creatinine 1.2 mg/dL (reference range 1.3 ± 0.2 mg/dL), calcium 9 mg/dL (reference range 8.6 ± 0.7 mg/dL), total bilirubin 0.3 mg/dL (reference range 0.8 ± 0.3 mg/dL), and potassium 4.8 mmol/L (reference range 3.8 ± 0.2 mmol/L). Reference values for hematological and serum biochemistry was taken from [25, 28, 40].

There are several classifications for hepatic hydatid cysts based on their ultrasonographic appearances. The initial classifications by the World Health Organization are the most commonly preferred. On the right side of the abdomen, the ultrasonographic findings included presence of different forms and stages of hepatic hydatid cysts. This was in the form of anechoic cysts, well-defined borders with echo wall enhancement. Daughter cysts were also imaged as a characteristic sign forming multiple cysts and finally producing a honeycomb appearance (Fig. 1). In addition, presence of localized thickening in the cyst wall corresponding to early daughter cysts (Fig. 2). Separation of membranes (ultrasonic water lily sign) due to germinal layer collapse seen as an undulating linear collection of echoes (Fig. 3). Ultrasound-guided aspiration of a cyst revealed a clear non-turbid fluid. There was immediate detachment of the germinal layer in all cysts during aspiration as shown in (Fig. 4).

The mechanism of detachment of cell membrane may be due to decreased intracystic pressure, degeneration, trauma or following fluid aspiration. Hepatic hydatid cysts were classified as small, medium and large cysts based on its sizes. Mean diameter of small cysts was between 10.16 mm to 35.30 mm; medium cysts were between 49.64 mm and 69.90 mm; large one was between 88.91 mm and 127.28 mm in diameter.

In human medicine, ultrasonography was effective in prediction and diagnosis of echinococcosis [5, 8, 20, 43]. Similar, in veterinary medicine, ultrasound has been proven effective in the diagnosis of cystic echinococcosis in sheep and goats [12, 16, 21] and cattle and buffaloes [15]. According to a study carried out on a total of 1,054 children, 24 of them were diagnosed as carriers, in the town of Ingeniero Jacobacci, Rio Negro, Argentina, it was found that when using ultrasound in diagnosis of human hydatidosis, a sensitivity of 100%, a specificity of 95.6% and a global test value of 96.7% were estimated [8].

Several sonographic features were recorded in humans with echinococcosis. These patterns include hyperechogenicity, mixed echogenicity type, dotted calcification with hypoechogenicity, sharp margin, rim enhancement and piece-like non-enhanced areas could be seen as the main ultrasonographic features of small lesion of hepatic alveolar echinococcosis [5, 20].

In sheep infected with cystic echinococcosis, hydatid cysts appeared sonographically as a round hypoechoic structure and the in vivo sonography of infected sheep provided a useful screening tool for echinococcosis [12]. In another study carried out on a total of 1039 sheep in Tunisia, all hydatid cysts detected by ultrasound were located in the liver [16]. The later study concluded that ultrasonography confirms the importance of ovine hydatid cyst and that its use as a mass screening approach for cystic echinococcosis in sheep could be helpful for the monitoring of this disease in a hydatid control program without great stress for the animals. In another study carried out on 16 sheep and 284 goats, the sensitivity and specificity of ultrasound examination for detecting hydatid cysts in sheep and goats was 54.36% and 97.64%, respectively [21].

In a study conducted on 37 buffaloes and 8 cattle suffering from lung and liver cysts, it was observed that 46.7% of the animals had both lung and liver cysts, whereas 33.3% had only lung and 20% had only liver cyst [15]. In the later report, for diagnosing lung cysts, radiography (71.1%) and ultrasonography (62.2%) had similar diagnostic utility. However, for detecting liver cysts, ultrasonography was the only imaging tool. Ultrasonography was sufficient to detect single or multiple, encapsulated, thin or thick walled cavitary lesions, with anechoic to hyperechoic contents, within the hepatic parenchyma of 68.9% of the diseased animals [15].

At abattoirs, echinococcosis is always detected incidentally in camels [19, 22, 42, 45]. To the authors’ knowledge, this report shows first case of antemortem diagnosis of hydatidosis in a female dromedary camel with hepatic cystic lesions using ultrasonography. In dromedary camels, ultrasonography was proved during the last decade to be effective in examining camels either in the healthy [26, 35,36,37, 41] or in the diseased state [27, 29,30,31,32,33,34, 38, 39]. In this report, multiple cysts were found within the hepatic tissue that is containing anechoic fluid and fibrin tags. These findings are correlated well with similar data in humans and also in sheep, goats, cattle and buffaloes [5, 12, 15, 16, 21].

The ultrasonographic findings of liver hydatid cysts in this report are coincided with the WHO informal working group on echinococcosis [44] classification. In conclusion, the current case report represents the first investigation evaluating the use of the ultrasonography as a potential fast focused technique for CE hepatic lesions detection according to WHO classification, in dromedary camel under field conditions. Ultrasonography provides clinically evidence to the localization of hydatid cysts in the internal organs and the stage of the disease. In summary, although this literature was conducted on one case, the data obtained may contain some illuminating results.
