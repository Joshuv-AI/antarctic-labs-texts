Maternal Perinatal Gut Microbiome Is Shaped by Traditional Farming Lifestyle and Associated With Early Childhood Atopic Disease
Authors: Erin C Davis, Courtney M Jackson, Nichole S Diaz, Jazmine Susana, Ashley Nelson, Richard Insel, Antti E Seppo, Kirsi M Järvinen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMaternal exposure to a traditional farming lifestyle during pregnancy is associated with protection against allergic disease in childhood; however, the mechanism remains unclear. Pre‐clinical work has demonstrated a role for the maternal gut microbiome in fetal immune programming. Given the diverse microbial exposure on farms, we sought to assess whether the maternal gut microbiome may mediate the relationship between maternal farm exposure and protection against offspring allergic disease. Deep shotgun metagenomic analysis of the perinatal fecal microbiome showed that women from an Old Order Mennonite traditional farming community (OOM, n = 68) harbored a more diverse gut microbiome relative to women from urban/suburban Rochester, NY (ROC, n = 55). We identified several bacterial species differentially abundant between lifestyle groups, including those from Dorea, Anaerobutyricum, Bifidobacterium, and Bacteroides genera, which translated to marked differences in microbiome functional capacity. These differences in the gut microbiome composition were accompanied by targeted metabolite findings indicating higher serum acetate and isobutyrate levels in OOM women that were positively correlated with cord plasma levels and infant systemic IgA concentrations. Among urban women, maternal microbiome composition was associated with early childhood atopic disease outcomes. Specifically, Dorea longicatena and Segatella copri were least abundant in urban mothers whose infants developed atopic disease (atopic dermatitis) or IgE‐mediated food allergy alone, respectively, and were most abundant in the OOM mothers. Together, these findings highlight the maternal gut microbiome and metabolites as potential contributors to prenatal farming lifestyle protection against early childhood allergic disease.Keywords: allergic disease, farming lifestyle, gut microbiome, pregnancy, SCFA

Introduction

The prevalence of allergic diseases, including atopic dermatitis (AD), food allergy (FA), asthma, and allergic rhinitis, has dramatically increased over the last half of a century and now affects more than 1 in 4 children in the United States [1]. Genetics play a role in the development of allergic disease; however, the rapid increase in disease rates in a short period of time highlights a role for environmental factors associated with lifestyle modernization. Exposure to a traditional farming lifestyle has been reproducibly associated with protection against allergic disease across the lifespan [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. The exact mechanisms underlying this protection are not known but remain of great interest due to their potential application to prevention strategies.

While much of the available research on the preventive farm life effect has focused on early childhood and school‐age children [2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9], a timepoint beyond the onset of many atopic conditions, a small body of literature suggests that farm‐life protection is initiated prenatally [10, 11, 12, 13]. Maternal exposure to farms during pregnancy has been associated with decreased risk of hay fever, asthma, and atopic sensitization in childhood, with prenatal farm exposure shown to confer stronger protection against offspring allergic disease than postnatal exposure alone [13, 14]. The premise for prenatal immune priming by farming lifestyle is further supported by immunological differences between farming and non‐farming infants present already at birth. Infants born to farming families have been shown to have a higher frequency of regulatory T (Treg) cells in cord blood (CB), critical for promoting tolerance to innocuous antigens, as well as lower concentrations of Th2 cytokines [15] associated with allergic responses. Maternal contact with farm animals and stables [14] and consumption of raw farm milk [15] during pregnancy are the specific factors that have been associated with protection against early atopic disease, suggesting that sustained and diverse microbial exposure during pregnancy plays a role in the prenatal farm‐life effect [14, 16].

Increasing evidence points to a role for the maternal gut microbiome during pregnancy in fetal immune programming and protection against allergic disease [17, 18, 19]. Protection may be afforded through multiple mechanisms, including transplacental delivery of maternal microbial metabolites. The metabolite class most widely studied for its effect on fetal immunity to date is short‐chain fatty acids (SCFA), which constitute a major byproduct of microbial fermentation [17]. In murine models, high fiber or acetate feeding during pregnancy increases production and vertical transfer of maternal SCFA, promotes Treg differentiation, and attenuates allergic disease in offspring [20, 21]. Recent findings have also demonstrated higher microbial diversity and enrichment of SCFA‐producing microorganisms in pregnant women with healthy infants vs. infants that develop AD [22]. Maternal carriage of Prevotella copri , a known SCFA‐producing bacterial species, has also been associated with a decreased risk of clinical FA at 1 year [23]. Westernized populations have reduced bacterial diversity [24] and lower SCFA levels [25] compared to those following a rural or traditional lifestyle. Therefore, it stands to reason that a traditional farming lifestyle may shape a gut microbiome that increases production and transplacental delivery of SCFA during pregnancy, potentially programming a tolerogenic phenotype in utero and a lower risk for later allergic disease.

To our knowledge, there are no reports to date on the maternal gut microbiome or microbial metabolites in traditional farming communities. Herein, we sought to assess differences in the maternal perinatal fecal microbiome and circulating microbial metabolites in women from an Old Order Mennonite community, who follow a traditional farming lifestyle and are largely protected against allergic disease, compared to women from high‐risk urban/suburban families. We hypothesized that mothers from farming communities would have a more diverse microbiome with increased capacity to produce SCFA, which would be further associated with protection against early childhood atopic disease.

Methods

Participants and Sample Collection

Samples included in this study were drawn from the “Zooming into Old Order Mennonites” (ZOOM1) study—a prospective longitudinal birth cohort study with the goal of identifying microbial and immune biomarkers associated with allergic disease risk in early childhood [26]. The study is comprised of two populations with different risk for allergic disease—the Old Order Mennonites (OOM) of Western, NY, who practice a single family, traditional farming lifestyle and are at low risk for allergic disease and high‐risk families from urban/suburban Rochester, NY. Between June 2017 and June 2020, ZOOM1 recruited 90 OOM mothers and 98 ROC mothers carrying a fetus at high risk for allergic disease (first degree blood relative with allergy) during the second or third trimester of pregnancy. Mothers were generally healthy, and all infants were born full‐term. Timeline of sample and data collection relevant to this manuscript is shown in Figure 1A. Our protocol (RSRB52971) was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Rochester Medical Center, and all parents provided written consent prior to enrollment to the study. Data related to maternal perinatal antibiotic usage were obtained from study questionnaires collected during pregnancy and at 6 weeks postpartum and from medical records. Details of the cohort, including demographics and atopic outcomes by 18 months, have been published [26].

One maternal stool and serum sample were collected during the second and third trimester of pregnancy or the first 10 weeks postpartum, and infant blood samples were collected at birth (umbilical cord blood) and 6 months (Figure 1A). Sample numbers used for each analysis are outlined in Figure 1B. Numbers of maternal stool and serum samples collected during each perinatal period were proportionate between OOM and ROC, as shown in Table S1. Briefly, maternal stool samples were collected using a stool hat and instructions provided by study coordinators. Once stool was passed, a disposable spatula was used to transfer the sample to a sterile tube, which was stored in the participant's home freezer, transported to the lab, and stored at −80°C. Maternal blood was collected by a trained research coordinator using an 8 mL sodium heparin anticoagulated vacuum and SST tubes. Cord blood samples were collected after being separated from the baby using tubes supplemented with an additional 150 USP of sodium heparin to reduce clot formation. Upon receipt in the lab, the maternal SST tube was centrifuged at 1400 × g for 10 min to obtain serum. Heparinized blood samples were diluted two‐fold with PBS and processed using density centrifugation (Ficoll‐Paque) to harvest plasma. Serum and plasma samples were stored at −80°C and used for downstream analyses included in this manuscript.

Allergy Diagnosis

Physician diagnosis of IgE‐mediated food allergy (IgE‐FA) was based on guidelines from the NIAID‐sponsored expert panel report [27]. Atopic dermatitis was diagnosed by a physician based on recurring or chronic red, pruritic rash. For the analyses included in this paper, ROC infants were defined as being atopic if diagnosed by a physician with atopic dermatitis and/or IgE‐FA by 2 years of age. By 2 years of age, 18 ROC infants had been diagnosed with atopic dermatitis (AD) alone, 3 with IgE‐mediated food allergy (IgE‐FA) alone, and 12 with both AD and IgE‐FA. Two OOM infants were also diagnosed with atopic dermatitis and none with IgE‐FA. The most common IgE‐mediated FA in ROC was to egg (n = 10) (Figure S1).

DNA Extraction and Shotgun Sequencing

DNA was extracted from roughly 200 μg of maternal stool using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to manufacturer's instructions. DNA was quantified using a Qubit dsDNA Assay Kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) and stored at −80°C until sequencing and qPCR analysis. DNA was submitted to the University of Rochester Genomics Research Center for library preparation and sequencing. The quality of extracted genomic DNA (gDNA) was assessed on a Fragment Analyzer (Agilent Technologies Inc.) and quantified using Qubit (ThermoFisher Scientific). Libraries were constructed using the Nextera XT kit (Illumina Inc.) with 1 ng of gDNA as input. Fragment size profiles and quantification of the libraries were measured using the Fragment Analyzer and Qubit, respectively. The libraries were normalized to 2 nM, pooled at equimolar concentrations, and sequenced on NovaSeq 6000 with 150 cycles to a depth of 100 million paired end reads. On average, 93,327,245 paired end reads were sequenced per sample.

Metagenomic Sequence Processing

Raw sequencing reads were quality filtered using the KneadData pipeline (v0.12.0) using standard settings. Adapters were trimmed using Trimmomatic [28], and reads corresponding to host DNA were filtered using bowtie2 [29] and the hg38 human reference database. After quality control and subsequent merging of reads, taxonomy was analyzed using MetaPhlAn v4.1.1 [30]. Unclassified reads were retained in the dataset for relative abundance calculation. Next, functional profiles of the quality‐controlled reads were analyzed using the HUMAnN 3.0 [31] pipeline. Functional pathway abundance, expressed as relative abundance, was utilized for functional analysis.

qPCR for Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis

To assess the presence of Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis (B. infantis) , we utilized a B. infantis ‐specific primer set targeting the Blon0915 [32] region in a probe‐based qPCR analysis. To derive the relative abundance of B. infantis and ensure adequate bacterial DNA per sample, universal primers that anneal to conserved regions of the 16S rRNA were also used to quantify total 16S copies via qPCR as previously described by us [8].

Maternal Serum SCFA Analysis

SCFA in maternal serum were measured at The Metabolomic Innovation Center (Victoria, CA) by adapting a pre‐analytical chemical derivatization—liquid chromatography‐multiple reaction monitoring mass spectrometry (LC‐MRM/MS) method described by Han et al. [33], with necessary modifications and optimization. Human serum samples were thawed on ice and vortexed, and 20 μL of serum was aliquoted and mixed with 50 μL of acetonitrile in an Eppendorf tube. The mixture was vortexed at 3000 rpm for 1 min, ultra‐sonicated in an ice‐water bath for 2 min and then centrifuged at 21,000 g for 5 min. 40 μL of the clear supernatant from each sample or 40 μL of each of nine serially diluted calibration solutions containing standard substances of SCFAs and quinolinic acid was mixed with 40 μL of a 200‐mM 3‐nitrophenylhydrazine (3‐NPH) HCl solution and 40 μL of a 150‐mM 1‐ethyl‐3‐(3‐dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide. The mixed solutions were incubated at 50°C for 30 min. To each cooled solution, 80 μL of a pre‐made internal standard (IS) solution containing 13C6‐3‐NPH derivatized SCFAs and quinolinic acid was added. 5‐μL aliquots of the resultant solutions were injected onto a Waters BEH C18 column (2.1 *100 mm, 1.8 μm) to run LC‐MRM/MS on an LC–MS system composed of an Agilent 1290 Infinity II liquid chromatograph hyphenated to an Agilent 6495B triple‐quadrupole mass spectrometer via a jet‐stream electrospray ionization source. The mass spectrometer was operated in the negative‐ion mode. 0.01% formic acid in water and 0.01% formic acid in acetonitrile were used as the mobile phase for binary solvent gradient elution under optimized chromatographic separation and mass spectrometric detection conditions.

Cord Blood Plasma SCFA Analysis

SCFA in cord blood plasma samples were quantified by Arome Science (Farmington, CT) using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). Sample preparation involved liquid–liquid extraction: Cord blood was extracted with an acidified cold methanol and water mixture followed by methyl tert‐butyl ether (MTBE), resulting in a 3‐fold final dilution. Internal standards (deuterated acetic acid‐d4, propanoic acid‐d5, and butyric acid‐d7) were spiked at 5 μg/mL prior to extraction for quantification.

GC–MS analysis was performed on an Agilent 7890B GC system coupled to LECO BT time‐of‐flight mass spectrometer, equipped with a Phenomenex Zebron ZB‐Wax Plus column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d. × 0.25 μm film thickness). Hydrogen was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 2.0 mL/min. The inlet was operated in split mode (split ratio 10:1) at 250°C, with a septum purge flow of 0.3 mL/min and total split flow of 22 mL/min. Oven temperature programming started at 50°C (held for 0.2 min), ramped at 17.5°C/min to 255°C, and held for 2.0 min (total runtime 13.54 min). The transfer line temperature was maintained at 250°C. Mass spectra were acquired in scan mode (m/z 40–400) at 30 spectra/s after a 120‐s solvent delay. The filament was off for the first 2 min to protect the detector. Data were processed using internal processing software, with quantification based on internal standard calibration curves for acetic, propionic, and butyric acids. While 2‐Methylbutyrate and valerate were measured in maternal samples, they were not measured in cord blood.

Infant Plasma IgA Assessment

Affinity purified goat anti‐human IgA (Bethyl Labs, Montgomery, TX) capture antibody (1:100 with carbonate–bicarbonate buffer) was added to Maxisorp flat bottom 96‐well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Plates were washed with PBS‐Tween 20 (PBS‐T) and then blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA, MilliporeSigma Burlington, MA) + PBS for 1 h. All sample dilutions were made using 1% BSA + PBS‐T (PBS + 0.05% Tween‐20). Plasma samples were diluted 1:1500, added to the plate, and incubated for 1 h. Following another plate wash, the detection antibody [goat anti‐human IgA‐HRP (Bethyl Labs)] was diluted 1:100,000 in 1% BSA + PBS‐T, added to wells, and incubated for 1 h. After plates were washed, 100 uL of TMB substrate (BD Biosciences) was added to the wells and incubated for 15 min. 50 uL of stop solution (0.18 M sulfuric acid) was added to each well and plates were read at 450 nm on a spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA).

Dietary Intake

One, 3‐day food record was collected from 55 women across both cohorts and included 2 weekdays and 1 weekend day, all consecutive. Only records collected between the 2nd trimester and 6 months postpartum were included in this analysis. Food records were entered into Nutrition Data System for Research 2019 (NDSR), developed by the Nutrition Coordinating Center (NCC), University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN [34]. Serving sizes are based on those defined by the 2000 Dietary Guidelines for Americans; those not included in the guidelines were based on serving sizes determined by the FDA. Nutrient and food group intakes for each day were exported, and averages calculated across the 3 days were subsequently used for analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Unclassified reads were filtered from the taxonomic dataset prior to diversity calculations. To assess beta diversity, Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was calculated on species level taxonomy, and PERMANOVA was used to assess association between group and week of sample collection relative to childbirth on taxonomic structure. Alpha diversity, including both Shannon diversity and species richness, was calculated using the vegan package [35] in R, and statistical differences were assessed using Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Differentially abundant species and pathways were analyzed using MaAsLin 3 [36], which fits linear regression models that account for compositionality. Group (OOM vs. ROC, atopic vs. non‐atopic, or no IgE‐FA vs. IgE‐FA) and week of sample collection relative to childbirth were included as main effects in the model. Within MaAsLin, data were filtered to species present at 0.0005% relative abundance in at least 15% of samples. Unclassified read percentage was retained for differential abundance analysis. Functional pathway data were filtered to pathways present in at least 15% of samples with no abundance filter. Data were log‐transformed within MaAsLin prior to running the model, and only differential abundance, rather than abundance and prevalence, was evaluated for all analyses. Difference in prevalence of Bifidobacterium species between groups was assessed using Fisher's exact test and difference in Bacteroideaceae to Prevotellaceae ratio and differential abundance of Bifidobacterium species and S. copri between groups were assessed via Wilcoxon rank‐sum test after padding with a value of 0.001. For both maternal and infant SCFA, data points plus or minus three standard deviations above or below the mean were considered outliers and removed prior to downstream analyses. Group comparisons of maternal and infant SCFA and food group intake were assessed via Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Analysis of maternal SCFA across trimesters was analyzed using a Kruskal–Wallis test appropriate for 3 groups. Correlations between maternal and infant SCFA and maternal SCFA and immunoglobulins were performed with Spearman correlation. The rate of breastfeeding was universally high in both OOM and ROC groups—100% OOM and 95% (N = 3) Rochester urban/suburban infants were still breastfeeding at 6 weeks and the rates at 6 months were 96% and 80% [26], respectively. We have also shown delivery mode to be highly colinear with lifestyle; therefore, we did not adjust for either covariate in our analyses [26]. All statistics were performed using R v4.4.2. Visualization was performed in both R and GraphPad Prism.

Results

Farming Lifestyle Women Have a Distinct Gut Microbiome During the Perinatal Period

We performed deep shotgun sequencing of the fecal microbiome of 123 OOM and ROC women across the perinatal period (one sample per mother). Analysis of Bray Curtis dissimilarity of taxonomic composition demonstrated that overall microbial structure tended to differ between the two lifestyle groups (p = 0.067) (Figure 2A). Week of sample collection relative to childbirth had no effect on microbiome structure (p > 0.05). OOM women also exhibited higher Shannon diversity as well as species richness relative to ROC (Figure 2B). At the family level, Lachnospiraceae and Oscillospiraceae were most abundant across both groups followed by Bifidobacteriaceae and an unclassified family of Eubacteriales (Figure 2C). We next explored the Bacteroidaceae to Prevotellaceae ratio in these groups, a measure previously shown to be a microbial marker of industrialization [24]. While traditionally calculated using Bacteroides and Prevotella genera abundance, we calculated the ratio at the family level given the relatively recent reclassification of many taxa that were previously members of both genera. As anticipated, OOM women had a significantly lower Bacteroidaceae to Prevotellaceae ratio (Figure 2D). Further investigation of taxa driving differences between groups identified 35 bacterial species differentially abundant between the lifestyle groups (Figure 2E and Table S2). Focusing on the top 10 most differentially abundant species, ROC women were enriched with GGB45432 SGB3101, Adlercreutzia equolifaciens , Bifidobacterium longum , and Gemmiger formicilis while OOM were enriched with Romboutsia timonensis, 3 species of Dorea, Anaerobutyricum soehngenii, and Lachnospiraceae bacterium CLA AA H244 (Figure 2F and Table S2). Adlercreutzia equolifaciens and Bifidobacterium longum were the differentially abundant species with the greatest log 2‐fold change between the groups, both higher in ROC—the species more abundant in OOM with the greatest log 2‐fold change was Blautia stercoris (Table S2). Lastly, we investigated the prevalence of antibiotic usage in relation to stool sample collection due to the potential impact on microbiome composition. Only 1 OOM and 5 ROC mothers reported antibiotic usage within the 3 months prior to sample collection, and we identified no difference in Shannon diversity or species richness of the fecal microbiome between ROC mothers who did and did not take antibiotics (Figure S2). Taken together, the maternal perinatal gut microbiome is more diverse and characterized by several differentially abundant species in farming communities at low risk for allergic disease.

Urban Women Harbor Greater Prevalence and Abundance of Fecal Bifidobacterium Species

Bifidobacteria are among the most predominant microbes present in the infant gut microbiome, and lower abundance in infancy has been associated with development of atopic disease [37]. We have previously identified higher abundance of Bifidobacterium in OOM infants [8]; therefore, we were surprised by Bifidobacterium longum being significantly higher among ROC women (Figure 2E,F). Thus, we next took a deeper look at the eight Bifidobacterium species detected in at least 10% of maternal stool samples across both lifestyle groups (Figure 3). Bifidobacterium longum ( B. longum ), a common inhabitant of the gut microbiome across the lifespan [38] and a predominant gut microbe during infancy [39], was the most prevalent Bifidobacterium species identified in both groups, with over 80% of ROC and 50% of OOM women harboring detectable levels (Figure 3A). We identified a significantly higher prevalence of B. longum and B. breve , as well as B. adolescentis and B. pseudocatenulatum among ROC women, with the latter being the most differentially prevalent. B. longum , B. breve , and B. bifidum are highly prevalent and abundant in the early infant gut microbiome due to their ability to metabolize human milk oligosaccharides [38, 39, 40]. A significantly greater proportion of ROC women harbored one or more these infant‐associated Bifidobacterium species (longum, breve, bifidum), with roughly 40% of OOM women harboring no detectable infant‐associated species (Figure 3B,C). Using simple pairwise comparisons, we further show that ROC women harbored a significantly greater abundance of B. adolescentis, B. animalis, B. pseudocatenulatum , and B. breve in addition to B. longum (Figure 3D). We were interested in whether the higher prevalence and abundance of Bifidobacterium species in ROC were a result of probiotic supplementation among the urban mothers. We did not systematically collect information on probiotic supplementation for all study participants; however, mothers who completed a 3‐day food diary were asked to report dietary supplements. Of the 55 women who completed a food diary, 35% reported consuming any probiotic supplement—41% of OOM women (14/34) and 24% of ROC women (5/21). The reported probiotic supplements contained variable bacterial species and total CFU of bacteria, prohibiting sensitivity analysis; however, it appears unlikely that differences in Bifidobacterium abundance between groups are due to probiotic consumption given the higher prevalence of supplementation in OOM.

B. longum has two subspecies commonly identified in humans— Bifidobacterium longum subsp. longum ( B. longum longum ) and Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis ( B. infantis ). We have previously shown high levels of B. infantis in OOM infants at 2 months of age [8], prompting us to investigate whether any of the B. longum identified in mothers was in fact B. infantis . Using qPCR, we detected B. infantis in only one OOM and two ROC women, suggesting that most of the B. longum in these samples was likely B. longum longum . Together, these findings highlight that the maternal perinatal gut microbiome harbors multiple Bifidobacterium species, many of which are more prevalent in urban women.

Circulating SCFA Levels Are Higher in Farming Lifestyle Women and Associated With Infant Plasma IgA

Given the preclinical evidence suggesting protective effects of SCFA on fetal immunity and differences in microbial diversity and abundance of taxa known to produce SCFA between groups, we next measured the concentration of seven SCFA in 146 maternal serum samples. SCFA are generally measured in stool given the site of their production. However, SCFA can be reabsorbed into portal circulation, and maternal circulation is the most relevant metabolite reservoir for a fetus given the potential for transplacental transfer to fetal circulation. Concentrations of SCFA were not different across samples collected in the second and the third trimester and the early postnatal period (Figure S3), thus samples across time points were combined for analysis. Acetate was the most abundant SCFA in maternal circulation followed by propionate and butyrate, while branched SCFA concentrations were considerably lower (Figure 4A). Pairwise comparisons showed higher levels of acetate and isobutyrate in serum of OOM compared to ROC women with an additional trend for higher propionate (Figure 4A).

Due to the potential for transplacental transfer of metabolites, we next assessed concentrations of SCFA in infant cord blood plasma. Similar to the findings in maternal circulation, acetate was the most abundant SCFA in infant cord blood (Figure S4A). Unlike maternal serum, we did not identify differences in acetate or isobutyrate concentrations in infant cord blood by lifestyle group. Levels of isovalerate were higher among OOM infants, though concentrations were very low (Figure S4A). Despite the difference in timing of cord blood and maternal blood collection, we identified a weak but significant positive correlation between maternal serum acetate and infant cord blood acetate (Figure S4B), suggesting transport from mother to infant across the placenta.

Given the propensity for most SCFA to induce IgA production [41, 42], we also investigated whether maternal circulating SCFA were associated with infant IgA concentrations, which we recently showed to be elevated in OOM relative to ROC infants [43]. Maternal serum acetate was in fact positively associated with infant plasma IgA concentration at 6 months, but this relationship was only identified among OOM dyads (Figure 4B). Systemic concentrations were assessed as indicative of the infant's own IgA production, unlike fecal IgA levels which are confounded by IgA from mother's milk in infancy [43]. Collectively, these results suggest that differences in microbial composition between these lifestyle groups may further translate to differences in circulating metabolites with capacity to be transferred to and exert immunomodulatory functions in the fetus.

Functional Capacity to Produce SCFA May Differ Between Lifestyle Groups

We next turned to investigate whether differences in maternal serum SCFA were due to differences in the functional capacity of their microbiomes. We identified 79 functional pathways differentially abundant between lifestyle groups (Figure 5A and Table S3). Four of these pathways were related to lactate and SCFA metabolism (Figure 5B). OOM mothers' microbiomes exhibited increased capacity for mixed acid fermentation—this was in fact the most differentially enriched pathway in OOM women, with pathway byproducts including acetate, lactate, and succinate. The OOM maternal microbiomes further showed greater capacity for propane‐1,2‐diol degradation, a pathway for propionate production. In line with taxonomic results, ROC mothers exhibited higher abundance of the Bifidobacterium shunt pathway, with potential byproducts including acetate and lactate, as well as a greater capacity for homolactic fermentation, with a primary end product of lactate. While several other pathways related to SCFA metabolism were not differentially abundant, these data suggest that the OOM and ROC microbiota may have different capacities to produce SCFA, which in turn may relate to differences in maternal serum SCFA demonstrated between groups. Aside from differences in these fermentation pathways, OOM women harbored higher abundance of multiple pathways related to polyamine, tetrahydrofolate, and L‐methionine synthesis. ROC women showed greater abundance of pathways related to micronutrient metabolism including menaquinol biosynthesis, thiamine salvage, and biotin biosynthesis as well as several pathways related to lipid metabolism, among others (Figure 5A and Table S3).

Farming and Urban Lifestyle Diets Differ During Perinatal Period

Due to the differences in microbiome composition and SCFA concentrations between OOM and ROC, we next explored whether dietary intake varies between the groups in a subset of study participants who completed a 3‐day diet record (n = 55). We primarily focused our analysis on food groups as past work has shown greater associations between microbiome composition and food groups versus individual nutrients [44]. Looking first at 10 major food groups, analysis showed that OOM women ate more servings per day of starchy vegetables, red meat, and eggs, while ROC consumed more servings of poultry, fish, and meat alternatives (Figure 6A). We also identified trends for higher fruit and lower refined grain intake in OOM. No difference in overall intake of dairy foods was identified between groups; however, OOM women consumed more fluid cow's milk per day, most of which was unpasteurized. Intake of cheese, yogurt, ice cream, and cream were comparable between groups (Figure 6B). Furthermore, due to the enrichment in serum SCFA concentrations in OOM, we anticipated that OOM women consumed more dietary fiber than ROC. Overall, the median dietary fiber intake in both groups was higher than the average fiber intake of roughly 17 g/day among pregnant individuals in the U.S. [45] We identified similar average intake levels of total daily fiber, insoluble fiber, pectin, and starch between groups (Figure S5); however, ROC women tended to consume more soluble fiber on average per day. These data collectively point to differences in consumption of major food groups, which may, alongside other divergent lifestyle exposures, shape the microbiome and metabolite differences identified between OOM and ROC women.

Maternal Microbiome Structure Is Associated With Infant Atopic Disease Outcomes

The differences identified in the maternal microbiome and metabolites between populations at differing risks for allergic disease prompted us to next evaluate their association with atopic disease (AD and/or IgE‐FA) or IgE‐FA outcomes by 2 years of age. Given the strong underlying effect of lifestyle on microbiome composition, low rates of allergic diseases in OOM, and a chance of differences in genetic factors between cohorts, we investigated the association between the maternal microbiome and metabolite composition and atopic outcomes within the ROC cohort only. In addition to any diagnosis of AD and/or IgE‐FA by 2 years, we chose to pursue additional analysis on just the subgroup who were diagnosed with FA (most of whom also had AD) as the maternal gut microbiome has been previously associated specifically with infant clinical FA [23], suggesting that there may be a maternal microbial signature specific to IgE‐FA perhaps not related to development of AD alone. The overall structure of the maternal microbiome was associated with infant atopic disease (predominantly atopic dermatitis) and IgE‐FA outcomes (Figure 7A,B). We also identified a trend (p = 0.06) for lower Shannon diversity in the perinatal microbiome of mothers whose children developed IgE‐FA (Figure 7B). Differential abundance analysis identified multiple maternal species associated with infant atopic disease outcomes and 2 species associated with IgE‐FA alone (p < 0.05; Tables S4 and S5). While none of these taxa remained significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons, likely due to small sample sizes and thus low statistical power, we present them here as exploratory findings. Firmicutes bacterium (p = 0.002) and Dorea longicatena (p = 0.002) exhibited the most differential enrichment between groups, both lower in ROC mothers whose children developed atopic disease (AD and/or IgE‐FA) by 2 years (Table S4). Dorea longicatena was the only species different among atopic groups that was also identified as differentially abundant between lifestyle groups—the highest abundance is identified among OOM women and the lowest among ROC women whose children develop atopic diseases (predominantly AD) by 2 years of age (Figure 7C). We additionally identified two pathways related to heme biosynthesis that were more abundant in ROC mothers of non‐atopic children (Figure S6). Analysis related to child IgE‐FA outcomes alone identified Odoribacter splanchnicus (p = 0.001) to be lower and Clostridiales bacterium (p = 0.04) to be higher in the fecal microbiome of mothers whose children developed IgE‐FA (Table S5). Lastly, looking at metabolites, serum butyrate tended to be lower in mothers whose children went on to develop IgE‐FA (Figure S7B); however, no other differences were identified in maternal serum or infant cord blood SCFA based on infant atopic or IgE‐FA outcomes (Figures S7 and S8).

Segatella Copri Is Highly Abundant in Farming Women but Largely Absent in Mothers of Children With FA

A previous study identified maternal carriage of Prevotella copri (now renamed Segatella copri) to be associated with a lower risk of clinical FA at 12 months of age [23]. Segatella copri has also been identified to be more prevalent in the gut microbiome of non‐industrialized communities; though, to our knowledge, it's not been investigated whether this finding remains consistent in women during the perinatal period [46]. Thus, we probed this species further within both our farming and urban lifestyle cohorts. Previously represented as four separate clades, Segatella copri (S. copri) has been extended to a complex of 13 distinct species, known as the Segatella copri complex (ScC) [46]. We identified seven of the ScC species across our samples, a greater number of which were detected in OOM samples (Figure 7D). S. copri was the most prevalent ScC species, detected in the microbiome of 75% of OOM women and just 22.4% ROC (Figure 7D). Shannon diversity was also associated with presence of S. copri (Figure 7E), with OOM women who were S. copri positive demonstrating a more diverse microbiome than OOM women without detectable S. copri and ROC women irrespective of S. copri carriage. Colonization of S. copri was associated with family size in OOM but not in ROC families (Figure S9). In addition to a higher prevalence, OOM women also harbored a higher abundance of S. copri relative to ROC women (Figure 7F), regardless of whether or not their children developed FA, as demonstrated among other non‐industrialized populations [46]. Lastly, we analyzed whether maternal S. copri carriage was associated with infant IgE‐mediated FA outcomes in our ROC cohort (Figure 7G). Only one ROC mother (11%) whose child developed IgE‐mediated FA by 2 years had detectable S. copri in their microbiome during the perinatal period relative to 26% of ROC mothers whose children did not develop IgE‐FA and 75% of OOM women. While the relative risk (RR) of IgE‐FA was not different between ROC children whose mothers did or did not have detectable S. copri (RR = 0.417, 95% CI 0.070 to 2.07, p = 0.33), these findings are important as they demonstrate that S. copri is highly prevalent in the perinatal gut microbiome of mothers from farming communities whose children are largely protected against IgE‐FA. Collectively, these results warrant further investigation in a larger urban cohort with a specific focus on IgE‐FA.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study investigating the maternal gut microbiome and microbial metabolites in a traditional farming population largely protected against atopic diseases. Here we demonstrate that exposure to a traditional farming lifestyle results in a significantly more diverse microbiome during the perinatal period. Multiple differences in taxonomic composition translated to marked differences in microbiome functional capacity between OOM and ROC women, including pathways related to SCFA production, which may be related to higher levels of maternal circulating acetate and isobutyrate levels identified in OOM relative to ROC women. Furthermore, we show that maternal serum acetate was positively associated with cord blood acetate and plasma IgA levels in OOM infants at 6 months of age. Lastly, we show that the overall structure of the maternal microbiome is related to infant atopic disease outcomes among urban dyads and present Dorea longicatena and Segatella copri as two important species potentially associated with protection against atopic disease in early childhood. Taken together, our data demonstrate that the maternal gut microbiome during the perinatal period is a novel target of traditional farming lifestyle exposure and associated with early childhood atopic disease outcomes among urban families.

Our results align with previous data showing reduced bacterial diversity and higher Bacteroides to Prevotella ratio in Westernized populations [24, 25, 47] compared to those following a rural or traditional lifestyle. Lower alpha diversity of the maternal gut microbiome has also been associated with increased risk for development of atopic dermatitis in infancy [22]. We further identified differences in abundance of a number of bacterial species between OOM and ROC, some of which have been associated with allergic disease development in past studies. Among those most differentially enriched in OOM were three species of Dorea and Anaerobutyricum soehngenii, all of which are known to be potent SCFA producers [48, 49, 50, 51]. Dorea longicatena , also more abundant in ROC mothers of non‐atopic children, has been negatively associated with asthma and atopic dermatitis in a Dutch population based cohort [52], and lower fecal Dorea abundance has also been associated with sensitization to foods and food allergy in U.S. children [53]. These collective findings related to D. longicatena warrant further exploration into potential protective mechanisms, starting during pregnancy. Anaerobutyricum soehngenii (previously Eubacterium hallii ) has also been shown to be lower in the infant gut microbiome of children who are diagnosed with allergic disease by 5 years of age [54]. Species shown to be higher in ROC mothers, such as Eggerthella lenta , have been positively associated with diagnosis of allergic disease in early childhood [54]. A higher prevalence and abundance of multiple bifidobacteria species in ROC women was unanticipated given both our previous work showing that OOM infants are enriched in Bifidobacterium [8] and data showing that infant fecal abundance of Bifidobacterium is inversely related to infant allergic disease risk [37]. However, our findings do align with previous studies showing a higher abundance of Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides in industrialized relative to non‐industrialized adults [24, 55, 56]. Still, how these differences in the maternal microbiome between lifestyle groups may shape differences in the early infant microbiome remains a key outstanding question that will be pursued in future analyses in these cohorts.

The distinct community structure between OOM and ROC women translated to several differences in functional pathway abundance between groups, suggesting that taxa differentially abundant between groups are not simply functionally redundant. Among those were a collection of pathways related to carbohydrate fermentation, including elevated capacity for mixed acid fermentation and propionate production in OOM, which we hypothesize may be partly responsible for increased serum SCFA in OOM mothers. Mixed acid fermentation has been associated with a non‐industrialized microbiome, in particular of Hadza hunter‐gatherers [57]. ROC mothers demonstrated increased capacity for lactate production through homolactic fermentation and the Bifidobacterium shunt pathways, the latter of which can also be a pathway for acetate production. Still, these differences tell us little about cross‐feeding of lactate and SCFA between species and highlight the potential utility of further measuring SCFA in stool of these cohorts. While we were primarily interested in understanding how functional capacity might explain differences in serum SCFA, our analysis revealed several additional pathways related to fatty acid, vitamin, polyamine, and amino acid metabolism that were differentially abundant between farming and urban women, which should be further interrogated in future studies.

The increased maternal serum levels of acetate and isobutyrate in OOM are noteworthy for several reasons. Although they were not associated with increased cord plasma levels—a one‐time snapshot at the end of a 9‐month pregnancy—our data show correlations between maternal and infant acetate, further suggesting transfer of these metabolites across the placenta. It is well‐appreciated that SCFA can modulate the function of several immune cell populations [58] through binding of G‐protein coupled receptors (GPR) or via epigenetic mechanisms by acting as histone deacetylase inhibitors [58, 59, 60]. High fiber diets and acetate feeding to pregnant mice have been shown to increase both numbers of lung Tregs and attenuate allergic airway disease in offspring [21] as well as thymic and peripheral Tregs via transplacental transfer of SCFA [20]. Comparably in humans, higher maternal serum acetate levels during late pregnancy have been associated with decreased symptoms of cough/wheeze and parent‐reported wheeze in the first 12 months of life [21]. Thus, higher concentrations of circulating acetate in farming lifestyle women during pregnancy could directly or indirectly modulate fetal Tregs or other immune cell subsets, promoting tolerance and protection against allergic diseases in childhood. In fact, we recently demonstrated elevated numbers of gut homing and TIGIT+ Tregs in OOM infants compared to those from ROC infants [61].

SCFA have also been shown to support B cell metabolism and differentiation through interactions with T cells or dendritic cells [59, 62, 63]. SCFA can also increase expression of AICDA and PRDM1 genes related to class switch recombination, affinity maturation, plasma cell differentiation, and IgA production [59, 64], interactions which may underlie the positive correlation detected between maternal serum acetate and plasma IgA levels at 6 months of age in OOM infants. Although we did not identify differences in maternal serum acetate, isobutyrate, or propionate between ROC mothers of atopic and non‐atopic children, the association between maternal serum SCFA and infant atopic diseases should be interrogated in a larger, population‐based, non‐high risk urban cohort. This is because previous reports have shown that atopic adults, which represent over 80% of mothers in our urban cohort, have lower concentrations of SCFA in stool [65] and also human milk [66], suggesting that maternal atopic status may affect production or availability of SCFA in circulation. Given the number of differences in taxa, pathways, and diet between these lifestyle groups, we also anticipate differential transplacental transfer of other metabolite classes that may shape fetal immunity and will be important to investigate in future work.

Consistent with previous studies comparing Westernized and non‐Westernized communities [46], OOM women harbored more members of the S. copri complex and a higher abundance of S. copri. Maternal carriage of Prevotella copri (now Segatella copri) during pregnancy has been associated with a lower risk of infant clinical FA at 12 months [23]. Relative abundance of Prevotella copri has also been shown to be lower in children with asthma [67] and atopic dermatitis [68] and adults with FA [69] relative to healthy controls. Its enrichment in non‐Westernized individuals has been attributed to fiber‐rich diets and associated with improved metabolic health [46]. In our study, presence of S. copri within the OOM microbiome was associated with increased Shannon diversity and family size among OOM, similar to results published by Vuillermin and colleagues [23]. While the relationship was not statistically significant with the small sample size here, only one (11%) of the mothers whose children developed IgE‐mediated FA by 2 years was colonized with S. copri during the perinatal period. In contrast, 26% of ROC women whose children did not develop IgE‐mediated FA and 75% of OOM women were colonized with S. copri. Detection rates in ROC women were similar to prevalence identified by Vuillermin and colleagues. It remains unclear whether S. copri directly promotes oral tolerance, perhaps through production of SCFA or other metabolites, or if carriage is simply a biomarker of additional protective factors, such as diet or lifestyle exposures. Given the more homogenous lifestyle within traditional farming communities, they represent a model population to disentangle the factors associated with S. copri presence as this may be valuable information for potential lifestyle interventions.

Increased bacterial diversity in non‐Westernized populations, compared to those following a rural or traditional lifestyle, is in part attributed to higher intake of dietary fiber [24, 25, 47]. While we show no significant differences in fiber or starch intake between OOM and ROC women in subsets of our cohort, we did identify differential intake of multiple food groups, which may collectively contribute to microbiome differences. While not investigated here, it will also be important in the future to more thoroughly investigate how food processing and other additives, such as emulsifiers or preservatives, may also affect microbial differences between these lifestyle groups. Interestingly, Vangay and colleagues demonstrated a progressive loss of microbiome diversity as individuals migrated from a non‐Western country to the United States even with little change to diet, implicating the industrialized environment as a factor regulating microbial composition [24]. Therefore, other lifestyle exposures differentially experienced between these cohorts, such as high exposure to livestock, stables, unpasteurized milk, and low exposure to antibiotics and medications in OOM women [26] likely also played a role alongside diet in shaping microbial structure.

The strengths of this analysis include deep shotgun sequencing of the microbiome, with corresponding targeted metabolites and physician‐diagnosed infant atopic outcomes in a unique cohort comprised of communities with different risks of allergic disease. Limitations include a high‐risk, not population‐based setup and small sample sizes of atopic/non‐atopic and FA/no FA groups within our urban ROC cohort, which surely affected our power to detect bacterial species and metabolites associated with atopic outcomes. While our focus here was on early atopic diagnoses, there may be a maternal gut microbiome signature associated with atopic outcomes typically diagnosed beyond the first 2 years of life, which we were unable to capture here due to the age of our study participants but will be relevant to explore in the future. We had a limited set of dietary and oral probiotic intake data as we had access to one 3‐day diet and supplement record for less than half of the women with corresponding microbiome data. Therefore, the dietary data may not be representative of all the women included in this study, and we were unable to control microbiome analyses for probiotic supplementation. Further, we primarily focused our dietary intake analysis on food groups and did not address potential differences in food processing or additives, which may further impact microbiome composition. While we show no effect of maternal antibiotic intake within the 3 months prior to stool sample collection on microbial diversity, we did not have antibiotic usage data immediately prior to pregnancy, which would be important to collect and potentially adjust for in future studies. Moreover, because stool samples, serum, and dietary data were not collected at the same time for most mothers, we were unable to perform correlation analyses between the fecal microbiome, serum SCFA, and diet datasets. Sample and dietary data collection should be carefully timed in the future in order to enable dataset integration. Lastly, we only had stool and blood samples from mothers at one time point. Future studies should employ serial sampling throughout pregnancy to account for any changes within the microbiome and metabolite pools within an individual over time.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that traditional farming lifestyle shapes a more diverse maternal gut microbiome with numerous differentially abundant species and functional pathways and increased circulating SCFA across the perinatal period compared to urban lifestyle, all of which have the potential to modulate fetal immunity through multiple mechanisms. Given these findings and those from preclinical data demonstrating a role for the maternal microbiome during pregnancy in fetal immune programming, we propose the maternal gut microbiome, including D. longicatena and S. copri, and SCFA as potential mediators of the prenatal farm life effect on the protection against early childhood atopic disease. Future work should explore the upstream regulators of maternal gut microbiome composition in farming lifestyle communities as well as how maternal microbes and their metabolic byproducts are associated with the infant immune milieu at birth.
