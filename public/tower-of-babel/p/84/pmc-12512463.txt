Taurine can restrict MtdsRNA mediated pyroptosis by enhancing mitophagy in nucleus pulposus cells
Authors: Zixuan Ou, Junyu Wei, Bide Tong, Jie Lei, Huaizhen Liang, Dingchao Zhu, Hongchuan Wang, Xingyu Zhou, Di Wu, Hanpeng Xu, Zhi Du, Yifan Du, Shuchang Peng, Xiaoguang Zhang, Huipeng Yin, Kun Wang, Cao Yang, Zhiwei Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondrial double-stranded RNA (mtdsRNA), a long-stranded RNA molecule generated by bidirectional transcription of the circular mitochondrial genome, can trigger the type 1 interferon response by activating pattern recognition receptors in the cytoplasm during mitochondrial dysfunction. However, its regulatory and pathogenic mechanisms in intervertebral disc degeneration are poorly understood. Here, we showed that mtdsRNA was abnormally elevated in degenerative nucleus pulposus tissues. Furthermore, we found that mtdsRNA leakage into the cytoplasm could cause MAVS oligomerization by binding to RLRs, and oligomerized MAVS could then promote nucleus pulposus cell pyroptosis by activating NLRP3. To further elucidate the upstream regulatory mechanism of mtdsRNA, we performed untargeted metabolomics analysis, which revealed that the downregulation of taurine under external stimuli could drive an imbalance in mtdsRNA homeostasis. RNA-seq further revealed that exogenous supplementation with taurine protected cells from mtdsRNA-induced pyroptosis by increasing mitophagy. In conclusion, our study links taurine metabolism, mitochondrial nucleic acids and pyroptosis and suggests that mtdsRNA is an important causative molecule and a potential therapeutic target in intervertebral disc degeneration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02431-5.Keywords: MtdsRNA, Pyroptosis, Taurine, Mitophagy, Intervertebral disc degeneration

Introduction

Lower back pain (LBP) is a prevalent symptom of the musculoskeletal system [1, 2]. As a principal cause of disability, it frequently results in considerable expenditures on medical care and places a significant burden on society [3]. It is now established that intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is the primary cause of lower back pain [4]. The intervertebral disc is a structurally complex joint comprising the nucleus pulposus (NP), the annulus fibrosus (AF), and the cartilaginous endplates (CE) [5]. The NP is situated in the core of the disc and comprises a gel-like substance, containing nucleus pulposus cells (NPCs) and an extracellular matrix [6]. The functional state of the NPCs is critically important for the maintenance of disc homeostasis. Nevertheless, the effective treatment of disc degeneration remains challenging because of the lack of clarity surrounding the underlying molecular mechanisms involved.

Mitochondrial dysfunction and IVDD are closely linked [7]. Impaired fusion, fission and mitochondrial clearance can lead to inflammation or death of NPCs, which in turn promotes the progression of disc degeneration [8]. In recent years, mitochondrial double-stranded RNA (mtdsRNA), a novel damage-associated molecular molecule (DAMP), has received much attention [9–15]. Owing to the cyclic nature of the mitochondrial genome, bidirectional transcription can lead to cross-linking of the RNA produced, resulting in the formation of long double-stranded RNA. mtdsRNAs is not released into the cytoplasm to trigger downstream immune responses under normal physiological conditions [16, 17]. However, when mitochondrial dysfunction occurs, PNPT1, located in the mitochondrial membrane space, is unable to remove excess mtdsRNA in a timely manner, resulting in the release of mtdsRNA into the cytoplasm in a BAX/BAK-dependent manner [9, 16–18]. Excess dsRNA in the cytoplasm can activate downstream pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), such as RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs), which promote MAVS oligomerisation, resulting in massive production of IFN-1 [18, 19]. Cytoplasmic accumulation of mtdsRNA has been shown to play an important role in osteoarthritis (OA), Huntington’s disease, Sjӧgren’s disease, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NFALD) etc [12, 13, 17, 20]., but the role of mtdsRNA in IVDD is poorly understood.

Inflammation is thought to be closely associated with IVDD [1, 21]. Degenerated NP tissues can produce large amounts of inflammatory factors, such as IL-1β, to promote the progression of degeneration [1, 22]. Pyroptosis is a form of programmed cell death induced by inflammasomes and manifests as the continuous swelling of cells until the cell membrane ruptures, leading to the release of cellular contents and a strong inflammatory response [1, 23]. The NLRP3 inflammasome can activate caspase-1, driving inflammatory cytokine release and GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis through its pore-forming execution [1, 24]. Studies have shown that stressed NPCs undergo pyroptosis and that MAVS is essential for NLRP3 activation [1, 25], but the relationship between mtdsRNA and pyroptosis is unknown.

The production of mtdsRNA can be regulated by several factors [13, 14, 19]. The JAK-STAT pathway has been reported to modulate mtdsRNA production, while the antioxidant drug resveratrol can also reduce the cytoplasmic accumulation of mtdsRNA [13, 14]. In addition, metabolites are thought to be closely related to the innate immune response. As reported in the literature, a decrease in NAD+ can induce the release of mtDNA to promote the activation of the cgas-sting pathway, whereas the overaccumulation of fumarate can also disrupt the mitochondrial membrane potential, thus promoting the release of mtDNA into the cytoplasm in the form of mitochondrial-derived vesicles (MDVs) [26, 27]. However, the relationships between metabolites and mtdsRNA have been less reported. Understanding the relationship between metabolic homeostasis and nucleic acid homeostasis will help us to regulate the accumulation of abnormal nucleic acids and promote the maintenance of mitochondrial homeostasis.

In this study, we found that mtdsRNA was abnormally elevated in degenerated NP tissues and, more importantly, that NPCs with dysfunctional mitochondria released mtdsRNA into the cytoplasm to activate the RLR-MAVS-NLRP3 axis to promote pyroptosis. Using metabolomics, we found that intracellular taurine levels were critical for mtdsRNA homeostasis and that taurine deficiency promoted mitochondrial dysfunction, whereas exogenous taurine supplementation inhibited mtdsRNA release by increasing mitophagy. In conclusion, our study suggests that mtdsRNA is an important causative molecule and a potential therapeutic target for IVDD.

Results

mtdsRNA is elevated in degenerated NP tissues

To investigate whether mtdsRNA is abnormally elevated in clinical degeneration samples, a bioinformatics analysis of the GSE245147 sequencing data was first performed. As shown in Fig. 1a and Fig. S1a, degenerated NP tissue overexpressed 1,991 genes and downregulated 2,409 genes compared with normal NP tissue. Among the differentially expressed genes, we found that dsRNA-related genes, including IFIT5, MX1, RIG-I and IFIH1(MDA5), were significantly up-regulated in degenerated samples. In addition, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on the differential genes (Fig. 1b). The results revealed that the type 1 interferon-related pathway was significantly upregulated in the degenerated NP tissues, and that the RLR pathway associated with viral infection was also significantly upregulated. To further investigate the role of mtdsRNA in IVDD, we collected tissue samples from patients who underwent open spine surgery or lumbar discectomy for idiopathic scoliosis or lumbar disc herniation. According to the Pfirrmann MRI grading system, healthy or degenerated disc tissues presented different signal intensities on T2-weighted MR images. The normal disc structures have a uniform high white signal and the disc height is normal. However, degenerative disc structures exhibit a weak black signal. We defined the collected level III or IV NP tissue as the degenerated group and the collected level I NP tissue as the control group. As indicated in Fig. 1c, the degenerated NP tissues presented a decrease in water content. In addition, H&E staining revealed that the degenerated NP tissues exhibited aggregation of cells with loss of the normal structure of the extracellular matrix. To investigate the changes in mtdsRNA expression in degenerated discs, we extracted RNA from degenerated NP tissues and performed quantitative real-time fluorescence polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR). The results indicated that the total mtRNA level increased in degenerated NP tissues (Fig. 1d). mtdsRNA is a double-stranded RNA formed by pairing two complementary RNA strands [17]; therefore, to further quantify the content of mtdsRNA in degenerated NP tissues, we performed strand-specific qRT-PCR on normal and degenerated tissues. As shown in Fig. 1e, mtdsRNA was highly expressed in degenerated NP tissues, and the increase in ND5-L and ND6-L was more pronounced, which is in accordance with the literature [11]. Furthermore, proteins were extracted from normal and degenerated samples and subjected to western blot (WB) analysis (Fig. 1f-g). The results demonstrated a notable decrease in PNPT1 expression in degenerated NP tissues. Additionally, RIG-I and MDA5 protein levels were markedly elevated in degenerated samples. Although there was no significant alteration in the level of MAVS observed, there was a notable increase in MAVS oligomerisation, as evidenced by SDS-AGE analysis (Fig. 1f-g). These findings suggest that the RLR-MAVS pathway might be activated in degenerated NP tissues. Moreover, NLRP3 expression was also elevated in degenerated NP tissues (Fig. 1f-g). Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis revealed increased colocalisation of MDA5 and RIG-I with dsRNA in degenerated NP tissues (Fig. 1h-i). These findings suggest that the aberrant accumulation of dsRNA in degenerated discs may activate RLR proteins. Furthermore, IF revealed that PNPT1 expression was downregulated, accompanied by an increase in NLRP3 in degenerated NP tissues (Fig. 1j and Fig. S1b-c). Finally, the immunohistochemical (IHC) results confirmed the upregulation of RLR proteins in degenerated NP tissues (Fig. 1k). In conclusion, the aforementioned results indicated that mtdsRNA was markedly elevated in degenerated NP tissues, thereby triggering tissue inflammation through the activation of the RLR signalling pathway.

Oxidative stress promotes the leakage of mtdsRNA

Because oxidative stress can induce the cytosolic escape of mtdsRNA [13, 14], we used lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to stimulate the cells in subsequent experiments. As shown in Fig. 2a-b, LPS reduced the proliferative capacity of NPCs. Furthermore, the results of flow cytometry (FCM) analysis demonstrated that LPS disrupted the mitochondrial membrane potential and led to the accumulation of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Fig. 2c-f). As shown in Fig. 2g, LPS administration resulted in a reduction in the intracellular ATP level, which is indicative of damage to the mitochondrial respiratory chain. The expression of PNPT1 and BAX was subsequently examined, and the results revealed a decrease in intracellular PNPT1 expression and an increase in BAX expression upon LPS stimulation (Fig. 2h-l). A reduction in PNPT1 levels has been reported to result in an increase in mtdsRNA [16, 18]. To investigate whether the mtdsRNA content within the cytoplasm increases, we extracted the cytoplasm and subsequently extracted the RNA from it in accordance with the instructions (Fig. 2m). The successful extraction of the cytoplasm was confirmed, as shown in Fig. S2a. The qRT‒PCR results demonstrated that the cytoplasmic mtdsRNA content increased in response to oxidative stress (Fig. S2b). Furthermore, the strand-specific qRT‒PCR results corroborated these findings (Fig. 2n). As shown in Fig. 2o, mitochondrial and dsRNA colocalisation was more enhanced under LPS stimulation, with an increase in dsRNA fluorescence intensity, indicating that the mtdsRNA content increased in NPCs under oxidative stress. The use of RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (RNA FISH) has been demonstrated to be an efficacious methodology for the study of mtdsRNA [12, 15]; consequently, it was employed in this investigation to observe the content of mtdsRNA. As indicated in Fig. 2p and Fig. S2c, the fluorescence intensities of ND4-H and ND4-L increased significantly in response to LPS stimulation. In conclusion, the aforementioned results indicated that cellular mitochondrial function was impaired under oxidative stress, which was accompanied by an abnormal accumulation of mtdsRNA in the cytoplasm.

mtdsRNA triggers the RLR-MAVS-NLRP3 axis and promotes pyroptosis in NPCs

mtdsRNA has been demonstrated to induce cellular inflammation [18], and NLRP3 has been identified as a pivotal mediator of pyroptosis [21]. To explore the relationship between mtdsRNA and pyroptosis, we initially measured pyroptosis indicators. As shown in Fig. 3a-b, LPS induced pyroptosis of NPCs. Furthermore, the expression of genes associated with inflammation was quantified via qRT‒PCR, and the data in Fig. 3c demonstrated that LPS facilitated the progression of inflammation. To observe the morphological changes in cells stimulated with LPS, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used for further experiments. As shown in Fig. 3d, in response to LPS stimulation, the mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum and other organelles underwent significant swelling, resulting in the formation of bubble-like protrusions on the cells. The enzymatic activity of CASP-1 was determined by measuring the cleavage rate of Ac-YVAD-pNA, and it was observed that CASP-1 enzymatic activity increased in the presence of LPS (Fig. 3e). Furthermore, the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) results demonstrated a notable increase in the level of IL-1β in the cell culture supernatants, indicating that oxidative stress was a significant contributor to the increase in inflammatory factor levels within the extracellular microenvironment (Fig. 3f). Because RLR is downstream of mtdsRNA [17, 28, 29], we performed WB analysis to investigate the expression of RIG-I, MDA5 and MAVS in cells stimulated with LPS. As indicated in Fig. 3g-h, the expression of RIG-I and MDA5 gradually increased under stimulation with different concentrations of LPS. Furthermore, although no significant changes were observed in the overall content of MAVS, the oligomerisation of MAVS gradually increased under LPS stimulation by SDS-AGE. As shown in Fig. S3a-b, LPS increased the colocalisation of RLR with MAVS, indicating that the RLR-MAVS pathway was activated by LPS stimulation. To further explore the binding of mtdsRNA and RLR in NPCs, an RNA binding protein immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay was used for further investigation (Fig. 3i). Figure 3j shows that RIG-I and MDA5 both bound with mtdsRNA. Moreover, IF was further employed, and we found that the colocalisation between dsRNA and RLR increased (Fig. 3k-l, Fig. S3c-d). These results indicate that dsRNA has the potential to induce RLR-MAVS activation and contribute to the progression of pyroptosis. To gain further insight into the relationship between RLR-MAVS and pyroptosis, IF was subsequently conducted. As shown in Fig. 3m and Fig. S3e, LPS increased the degree of colocalisation between MAVS and NLRP3. As shown in Fig. 3n-o, coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) analysis revealed that MAVS combined with more NLRP3 under LPS stimulation. These results demonstrated that the interaction between MAVS and NLRP3 was enhanced in the presence of LPS. To further reveal that RLR-MAVS is indispensable for the progression of pyroptosis, the expression of MDA5, RIG-I and MAVS was knocked down in an experiment. WB, qRT‒PCR and IF were performed to verify the knockdown efficiency, as shown in Fig. 3p-s and Fig. S3f-i. Following the knockdown of RIG-I, MDA5 and MAVS, alterations in the indicators of pyroptosis were observed, and the results were presented in Fig. 3t and Fig. S3j. These results indicated that the knockdown of both RLR and MAVS resulted in a reduction in the expression of indicators of pyroptosis. However, notably, the knockdown of MAVS was more effective at alleviating the progression of pyroptosis than the knockdown of RIG-I or MDA5 was. This may be attributed to the fact that MAVS is downstream of RLR and is essential for pyroptosis. Consequently, partial activation of MAVS still promotes the progression of pyroptosis when RIG-I or MDA5 is knocked down. In conclusion, the aforementioned results indicated that the release of mtdsRNA in response to LPS stimulation facilitated the progression of pyroptosis by activating the RLR-MAVS pathway.

Inhibiting the leakage of mtdsRNA can alleviate the pyroptosis of NPCs

The downregulation of PNPT1 in response to oxidative stress can result in the release of mtdsRNA into the cytoplasm, which occurs in a BAX/BAK-dependent manner [16, 17]. Furthermore, IMT1 is a pharmacological agent that inhibits the POLRMT enzyme, thereby reducing the production of mtdsRNA [12]. To explore the relationship between mtdsRNA and pyroptosis, the degree of pyroptosis was subsequently investigated through the overexpression of PNPT1, the knockdown of BAX, and the utilisation of IMT1. PNPT1 was overexpressed by plasmid transfection, and in addition, the expression of BAX was knocked down via siRNA. The overexpression of PNPT1 and knockdown of BAX were verified via WB and qRT‒PCR, as shown in Fig. 4a-b and Fig. S4a-d. Moreover, JC-1 and DCFH-DA staining was performed, as shown in Fig. 4c-d and Fig. S4e-f, to observe the influence on the mitochondrial membrane potential and ROS content in NPCs. The results indicated that inhibiting the leakage of mtdsRNA alleviated mitochondrial function under LPS stimulation. The expression of mtdsRNA was further observed via IF. As indicated in Fig. 4e and Fig. S4g, following the three treatments, the colocalisation of mtdsRNA with mitochondria decreased, accompanied by a reduction in the fluorescence intensity of mtdsRNA. These results suggested that the three treatments effectively inhibited the leakage of mtdsRNA. We subsequently extracted the cytoplasm of NPCs and performed strand-specific qRT‒PCR, as shown in Fig. 4f. The results illustrated that the content of mtdsRNA was reduced in NPCs. Moreover, the RLR-MAVS-NLRP3 axis was measured in Fig. 4g and Fig. S4h-m. IMT1, siBAX and OE-PNPT1 effectively alleviated the activation of the RLR-MAVS-NLRP3 axis. As shown in Fig. 4h and Fig. S4n, the three treatments diminished the colocalisation of MAVS and NLRP3. Additionally, the fluorescence intensity of NLRP3 decreased. Co-IP, as shown in Fig. 4i-j, revealed that MAVS combined with less NLRP3 after the leakage of mtdsRNA was inhibited. CASP-1 activity and ELSA data were also shown in Fig. 4k-l, which indicated that the inflammation of NPCs was alleviated after the leakage of mtdsRNA was inhibited. In conclusion, the inhibition of mtdsRNA effectively alleviated the pyroptosis of NPCs by suppressing the RLR-MAVS-NLRP3 axis.

Taurine is essential for maintaining the mitochondrial function in NPCs

To gain further insight into the upstream regulatory mechanisms of mtdsRNA, untargeted metabolomics analysis was employed to investigate the alterations in metabolites in response to LPS stimulation. As indicated in Fig. 5a, a total of 137 metabolites were upregulated, whereas 73 metabolites were downregulated in the LPS group. The results of the principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated that LPS induced significant alterations in the metabolite composition of the NPCs, as shown in Fig. 5b. The different metabolites used for metabolic pathway analysis were shown in Fig. 5c. As indicated in Fig. 5c, the taurine and hypotaurine metabolism pathways had the greatest effects. As shown in Fig. S5a, LPS disrupted cell functional states according to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis. The results of the KEGG and pathway analyses revealed that LPS led to an imbalance in the antioxidant system and dysfunction of cell function. Taurine is a widely distributed conditionally essential amino acid whose homeostasis is important for maintaining the functional state of cells [30]. As shown in Fig. 5d, the content of taurine was reduced under LPS stimulation. A taurine content assay kit was subsequently used to determine the intracellular taurine content (Fig. 5e). The results were consistent with those depicted in Fig. 5d, indicating that taurine was downregulated by LPS stimulation. We further detected the taurine content in the clinical samples shown in Fig. S5b. The results indicated that the degenerated NP tissues contained less taurine than did the normal NP tissues. Although liver cells are capable of synthesising taurine, the majority of the taurine present within the cell is obtained from the surrounding environment [31]. SLC6A6 is a pivotal protein located on the cell membrane that is responsible for the uptake of taurine. Its downregulation has the potential to result in a lack of taurine inside cells [30, 31]. The expression of SLC6A6 was further investigated by WB analysis, as shown in Fig. 5f and Fig. S5c, and the results indicated that the expression of SLC6A6 was downregulated under LPS stimulation. Additionally, the expression of SLC6A6 was explored in clinical samples. Fig. S5d showed that degenerated NP tissues contained less SLC6A6 than did normal NP tissues. The WB result shown in Fig. S5e was consistent with the results shown in Fig. S5d. Moreover, the analysis of RNA sequencing data from the GSE245147 dataset al.so revealed that normal NP tissues expressed more SLC6A6. The above results of the clinical samples demonstrated that degenerated NP tissues presented less SLC6A6, indicating that taurine was deficient (Fig. S5f-g). To further investigate the influence of a lack of taurine on cells, siRNA was used in our experiments. The efficiency of SLC6A6 knockdown was verified via WB, as shown in Fig. S5i. As expected, the taurine content in NPCs was downregulated after SLC6A6 was knocked down, as shown in Fig. S5j. It was observed that the lack of taurine could induce the accumulation of ROS in NPCs (Fig. S5k). Additionally, we found that taurine depletion could promote the downregulation of PNPT1 expression, which might be attributed to the dysfunction of mitochondrial function (Fig. S5l). As shown in Fig. S5m-o, the expression of RLR was upregulated. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. S5p-q, the downregulation of SLC6A6 induced an increase in mtdsRNA. It could be concluded that a lack of intracellular taurine promoted an increase in and leakage of mtdsRNA. To further investigate the role of taurine in maintaining mitochondrial health, 1 mM taurine was used to treat cells cocultured with LPS. As shown in Fig. S5r, taurine increased the intracellular taurine content in NPCs even after stimulation with LPS. Additionally, taurine increased the expression of SLC6A6 in NPCs treated with LPS (Fig. S5s). The effects of taurine treatment on dysfunctional cells were also observed. As shown in Fig. 5g, taurine protected cells from oxidative stress and decreased the ROS content in cells. As shown in Fig. 5h-i, taurine promoted the recovery of proliferative ability. Moreover, mitochondrial health was maintained by taurine (Fig. 5j-l). The impact of taurine on the RLR-MAVS-NLRP3 axis was further investigated. As shown in Fig. 5m-n and Fig. S5t, taurine inhibited the activation of the RLR-MAVS-NLRP3 axis. Moreover, as shown in Fig. 5o, taurine protected cells from LPS stimulation and decreased the degree of pyroptosis. Finally, co-IP revealed that the interaction between NLRP3 and MAVS decreased (Fig. 5p and Fig. S5u). In conclusion, taurine was essential for cell function, and a lack of taurine promoted the leakage of mtdsRNA.

Taurine can inhibit the leakage of mtdsRNA

As shown in Fig. S6a, taurine restored cell viability. Furthermore, the rescue effect was defined as the relative increase in cell viability caused by taurine. To further explore the importance of mtdsRNA in the protective effect of taurine, IMT1 was used to treat cells before they were stimulated. Notably, depletion of mtdsRNA reduced the rescue effect of taurine (Fig. S6b). As shown in Fig. S6c-f and Fig. 6a, IF was employed to observe mtdsRNA expression. The results indicated that taurine could protect cells from mtdsRNA leakage. We further used qRT‒PCR and strand-specific qRT‒PCR to explore the amount of mtdsRNA, as shown in Fig. 6b-c. The results were consistent with the IF results, indicating that taurine could inhibit the leakage of mtdsRNA. WB was used to further observe BAX and PNPT1 expression, and we found that taurine increased the expression of PNPT1 and decreased the expression of BAX (Fig. 6d-e). As indicated in Fig. 6f-g and Fig. S6g-h, taurine increased the fluorescence intensity of PNPT1 and reduced the degree of colocalisation between BAX and mitochondria. Poly(I: C), reported as a mtdsRNA stressor, can induce the leakage of mtdsRNA [12–15, 19]. To gain further insight into the mechanism by which taurine inhibits the increase in mtdsRNA, we conducted an experiment in which the cells were treated with taurine while also being transfected with poly (I: C). Poly (I: C) transfection indeed induced an increase in mtdsRNA, which is consistent with the literature (Fig. 6h and Fig. S6i). Moreover, taurine treatment inhibited the increase in mtdsRNA in the cytoplasm, as shown in Fig. S6j. Taurine can inhibit the mtdsRNA-MAVS-NLRP3 axis by enhancing mitophagy

The mechanism by which taurine regulates mitophagy was further explored. 3-MA is a pharmacological agent that has the capacity to inhibit mitophagy [32]. Consequently, in subsequent experiments, we employed 3-MA to further observe the effect of taurine on mitophagy. As shown in Figs. 3 and 7a-b-MA led to an increase in ROS in cells and decreased the protective effect of taurine. As shown in Fig. 7c and Fig. S7a, 3-MA treatment reversed the protective effect of taurine on the mitochondrial membrane potential under oxidative stress. The mRFP-GFP-LC3 adenovirus is considered the gold standard for observing alterations in autophagy [5]. mRFP is employed for labelling and tracking LC3, whereas the attenuation of GFP can indicate the fusion of lysosomes with autophagosomes, resulting in the formation of autophagic lysosomes. The fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes results in the quenching of GFP fluorescence, which is subsequently replaced by red fluorescence. As shown in Fig. 7d and Fig. S7b, taurine increased the number of red or yellow puncta in the cells, whereas 3-MA inhibited puncta formation in the cells. These results suggested that taurine promoted autophagy and that 3-MA inhibited the formation of autophagosomes. The indicators of mitophagy were subsequently measured via WB. The pink1-parkin signalling pathway plays a pivotal role in mitophagy, orchestrating the selective clearance of superfluous or compromised mitochondria and contributing to the autophagic process [33]. As shown in Fig. 7e and Fig. S7c, taurine promoted the activation of the pink1-parkin pathway to clear impaired mitochondria. In addition, 3-MA reversed the protective effect of taurine and led to a decrease in mitophagy. We further performed IF to observe the mitophagy level in the cells shown in Fig. 7f-g and Fig. S7d-e. Taurine increased the colocalisation of pink1 and parkin. Moreover, taurine promoted the clearance of mitochondria, whereas 3-MA reversed the protective effect of taurine. We further observed the mtdsRNA level in NPCs. As shown in Fig. 7h and Fig. S7f, 3-MA led to an increase in cytoplasmic mtdsRNA, suggesting that taurine-induced mitophagy plays a very important role in the clearance of mtdsRNA. The expression of PNPT1 and BAX shown in Fig. S7g-h indicated that taurine removed impaired mitochondria and promoted mitochondrial homeostasis via mitophagy. The RLR-MAVS-NLRP3 axis was further investigated, as shown in Fig. 7i-k and Fig. S7i-k, and the results revealed that taurine inhibited the RLR-MAVS-NLRP3 axis via mitophagy and that inhibiting mitophagy reversed the protective effect of taurine. In conclusion, taurine could inhibit the mtdsRNA-MAVS-NLRP3 axis by enhancing mitophagy.

Taurine can alleviate IVDD by inhibiting mtdsRNA-MAVS-NLRP3 in rats

To establish the LPS-induced IVDD model, a 10 µl solution containing LPS at a concentration of 2 µg/ml was administered into the Co8/9 intervertebral discs in rats. For the treatment group, the LPS solution was supplemented with 1 M taurine, while the control group received an equivalent volume of PBS. All animals received a single injection at the initiation of the experiment. Subsequent pathological changes and relevant biomarkers were monitored over a one-month observation period following the intervention. The results revealed that taurine treatment restored the height of the discs destroyed by LPS (Fig. 8a-c). Additionally, the MRI results demonstrated that the signal intensity of the intervertebral disc following the administration of LPS exhibited greater heterogeneity, and the T2-weighted signal intensity was lower than that in both the taurine-treated group and the control group (Fig. 8b-c). Moreover, H&E staining revealed severe degeneration and fibrosis of the intervertebral discs in the LPS-treated group, as evidenced by a reduction in the intervertebral space, loss of the typical boundary structure between the NP and the AF, clustered chondrocytic proliferation of NPCs, and loss of the annulus (Fig. 8d). Taurine intervention resulted in partial preservation of the intervertebral disc’s histological structure, with retention of a portion of the NPCs and the matrix, as well as maintenance of the boundary between the nucleus pulposus and AF and the AF lamellar structure. The effects of taurine on the degeneration of the intervertebral disc and partial maintenance of the intervertebral disc structure were further verified via Safranin O-fast green staining and Masson’s trichrome staining in Fig. 8d. As shown in Fig. 8e and g, the IHC results of PNPT1 revealed that LPS reduced the expression of PNPT1 in NP tissues, whereas taurine restored PNPT1 expression. Moreover, the IF results for BAX indicated that mtdsRNA might be released from mitochondria (Fig. 8f-g). IF also revealed that LPS induced the expression of NLRP3, indicating that there was high inflammation in the LPS group. However, taurine alleviated the inflammatory response and inhibited the expression of NLRP3, as shown in Fig. 8f-g. To evaluate the dsRNA level in the different groups, IF was performed, as shown in Fig. 8h. LPS promoted the colocalisation of RLR and dsRNA, indicating that the dsRNA-MAVS-NLRP3 axis was activated in the LPS group. Taurine reduced activation and protected NP tissues from dsRNA. These data demonstrated that taurine ameliorated damage in a needle puncture model of IVDD in rats.

Disscussion

This study elucidated the pathogenic role of mtdsRNA in IVDD, a primary contributor to LBP [34, 35]. We demonstrated that NPCs with mitochondrial dysfunction aberrantly released excessive mtdsRNA into the cytoplasm, which activated RIG-I/MDA5 receptors and induced MAVS oligomerization. This cascade recruited NLRP3 inflammasomes, triggering pyroptosis and inflammatory responses in degenerated disc tissues. Notably, metabolomic analysis revealed significant taurine deficiency in degenerative NPCs, which exacerbated mtdsRNA accumulation by impairing mitochondrial homeostasis. Critically, taurine supplementation suppressed mtdsRNA release through enhanced mitophagy, effectively mitigating NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis (Fig. 9). These findings position mtdsRNA as both a key pathogenic driver of IVDD progression and a promising therapeutic target, while highlighting the therapeutic potential of metabolic modulation through taurine to restore mitochondrial nucleic acid homeostasis in disc degeneration.

Taurine can restrict mtdsRNA mediated pyroptosis by enhancing mitophagy

Emerging evidence highlights mtdsRNA as a critical mediator of sterile inflammation [10, 12, 15–17]. However, few studies have reported the role of mtdsRNA in IVDD. Zhu et al. and Kim et al. demonstrated that PKR served as a key sensor for mtdsRNA, triggering cellular stress responses through binding cytoplasmic mtdsRNA [11, 16]. Conversely, Dhir et al. identified MDA5 as the primary sensor for mtdsRNA [18], while Tigano et al. reported contradictory findings, indicating that RIG-I, rather than MDA5, acted as the mtdsRNA receptor [29]. In this study, NPCs were treated with paraformaldehyde, and mtdsRNA was pulled down using MDA5- and RIG-I specific antibodies. Results revealed that mtdsRNA could simultaneously bind both MDA5 and RIG-I. This finding suggested that mtdsRNA interactions with downstream proteins might vary under different stress conditions, warranting further investigation to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. Additionally, upstream regulatory mechanisms of mtdsRNA remain poorly characterized. Literature indicates that JAK-STAT activation promotes mtdsRNA leakage [13], while the antioxidant resveratrol reduces mtdsRNA production [14]. PNPT1, an enzyme localized in the mitochondrial intermembrane space, degrades mtdsRNA [25]. Zhu and Lee et al. demonstrated that PNPT1 downregulation under external stress was critical for mtdsRNA release [16, 17]. Our study corroborated that LPS-induced stress could downregulate PNPT1 and facilitate mtdsRNA release. Furthermore, we investigated the upstream regulatory mechanisms of mtdsRNA. The results revealed that taurine depletion could also induce the downregulation of PNPT1 and trigger the release of mtdsRNA, which might be associated with mitochondrial functional impairment. Taurine, a non-essential amino acid in humans, plays a pivotal role in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis [31]. Studies have shown that taurine deficiency disrupts mitochondrial cristae structure, induces swelling, and accelerates cellular senescence [30, 36]. Although no direct link between taurine and mtdsRNA has been reported, taurine deficiency may promote cell death via PNPT1 downregulation and subsequent mtdsRNA leakage. Future studies should clarify the mechanistic relationship between taurine depletion and PNPT1 regulation. In conclusion, our findings emphasized that intracellular taurine levels were essential for mitochondrial integrity, with taurine deficiency driving mtdsRNA release, while exogenous taurine supplementation preserved mitochondrial health through mitophagy.

This study has some limitations. While the rodent models for studying IVDD provide a platform to assess taurine’s in vivo protective effects, but several critical constraints should be noted. Firstly, the anatomical structure and biomechanical properties of the rodent caudal vertebrae are significantly different from those of the human spine, and therefore its degeneration mechanism cannot completely mimic human pathological processes [37]. Secondly, rodent models are typically induced by acute injury, which does not fully reflect the progressive nature of the natural disease process [6]. Finally, the small size of the tail intervertebral disc necessitates precise control of the needle type and the depth of needle entry, which can introduce experimental errors [38]. Consequently, it is recommended that the in vivo protective effects of taurine be explored in combination with large animal models in future research, with a view to promoting its clinical translation.

In conclusion, the results demonstrated that mtdsRNA played important roles in IVDD. The pathogenic and regulatory mechanisms of mtdsRNA were explored in this study. Specifically, NPCs subjected to oxidative stress can trigger pyroptosis by releasing mtdsRNA, which in turn activates the RIG-I/MDA5-MAVS-NLRP3 axis. Additionally, under stress, NPCs exhibited an imbalance in taurine homeostasis. The supplementation of taurine was demonstrated to inhibit the release of mtdsRNA and to maintain mitochondrial health by enhancing mitophagy. Consequently, mtdsRNA may be potential target for the development of disease-modifying treatments for IVDD and LBP.

Methods

Human NP tissues

The tissues were obtained from six patients who had undergone open spine surgery or lumbar discectomy for idiopathic scoliosis or lumbar disc herniation. The detailed information of patients was showed in Table. S1. The tissues were used to perform qRT-PCR, WB or histological analysis. The Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (No. S341) approved our studies.

Culture of NPCs

The aforementioned healthy samples were partially employed for the isolation of human NP cells. PBS was utilized to preserve NP tissues. Following the addition of collagenase and centrifugation to isolate the cells, the cell culture was performed at 37 °C and 5% CO₂ in 1:1 DMEM/F12 in combination with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Servicebio). Fluorescent-activated cell sorting was employed to identify NP cell markers, namely CD24 (Biolegend, 311117) and KRT18 (Biolegend, 628404). The second generation of cells was employed for further in vitro experimentation. The NP cells were exposed to 1 µg/ml or 2 µg/ml LPS-containing medium for 72 h, forming the degenerate group, while the control group was left untreated. 100 µM IMT1 was employed to inhibit mtdsRNA production, while 1 mM taurine was used as a treatment for the cells.

EDU measurement

EDU measurement was performed by using a kit (Beyotime, C0078S). 5-Ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine was employed for the purpose of labelling and counting. Cell nuclei were stained using hoechst (Beyotime, C1011). Thereafter, the cells were observed by fluorescence microscopy, images were captured and the number of positive cells was analysed using ImageJ software (Version 1.52).

RNA FISH

The experiments were performed according to the instructions (Beyotime, R0306S). Specifically, the treated cells were first fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, followed by room temperature digestion for 2–10 min by adding an appropriate amount of 5-20ug/ml Proteinase K. After PBS washing, the cells were immersed using 0.5 M HCI and washed for 5 min at room temperature on a shaker. Subsequently, the samples were submerged by adding an appropriate amount of acetylation solution and washed for 10 min at room temperature. Then, appropriate amount of hybridization solution were taken, and Yeast RNA (100X) were added according to 1:100. Finally, the probe was added until the final concentration was 0.5–1 µg/ml and the liquid was dropped on the surface of the cells. The images were taken using a fluorescence microscope.

MDA5/RIG-I RIP

As inspired by Kim et al. [12, 13], the proteins and RNA were initially crosslinked with 0.1% paraformaldehyde. Thereafter, magnetic beads were employed to facilitate the isolation of the RNA for qRT-PCR analysis. Specifically, the cells were initially fixed with 0.1% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, after which the reaction was terminated by the addition of 1 M glycine. Subsequently, the cells were lysed using a lysate supplemented with RNase and protease inhibitors, and Anti-RIG-I/Anti-MDA5 or control IgG was added to the supernatant for a period of 16–18 h at 4 °C. On the following day, magnetic beads were added to the supernatant and subsequently washed. RNA in the precipitate was extracted using Trizol and analysed by RT-qPCR. Three independent replicates of the experiment were conducted, and the fold change of mtRNA in the RIG-I/MDA5 antibody was calculated compared to the mtRNA in the IgG antibody.

RNA isolation from cytosol

The experiment was conducted according to manufacturer’s instruction (Thermo Scientific, 78840). Cytoplasmic extraction buffer was firstly added to the cell pellet, and then Trizol LS was added to the supernatant at a 3:1 ratio to extract RNAs.

Western blot

Cell samples were collected and lysed by adding RIPA lysate (Biosharp, BL504A) containing 1mM PMSF (Biosharp, BL507A) to each sample. Following this, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was then separated and used as the protein samples for subsequent analyses. The protein concentration in the supernatant was accurately determined using a protein quantification kit (Biosharp, BL521A). Subsequently, the protein samples were combined with loading buffer (Biosharp, BL529B) and heated by boiling water for 10 min to denature the proteins. Subsequently, the proteins were subjected to 10% SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis (Epizyme, PG110) and transferred onto a PVDF membrane via semi-dry transfer. The proteins on the membrane were initially incubated with the primary antibody at 4 ℃ overnight, followed by an incubation with the secondary antibody at room temperature the next day. Finally, a ChemiDoc-It 610 Imaging System (UVP, USA) was performed to visualize the protein bands. Subsequently, the protein bands were quantitatively analysed using ImageJ software (Version 1.52), and the grey scale ratio of the target band to the β-actin internal reference band was calculated to assess the relative expression level of the target protein. The antibodies used in the study were shown in Table. S2.

SDS-AGE

The cells were lysed using RIPA lysate (Biosharp, BL504A) and subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min to remove any precipitates and retain the supernatant. Subsequently, 5×loading buffer (Biosharp, BL529B) was added in a 1:4 ratio to the supernatant. Moreover, a solution of 1×TBE and 0.1% SDS was prepared to serve as the electrophoresis buffer. Furthermore, a 1.5% agarose gel was prepared using 0.5×TBE and heated in a microwave oven until the solution was transparent. Once the solution had cooled to 42 °C, 0.1% SDS was added. The gel was then prepared by thorough mixing and introduced into the gel maker. The subsequent steps were identical to those employed in the Western blot procedure, with the bands incubated with anti-MAVS antibody and visualised using the ChemiDoc-It 610 Imaging System (UVP, USA).

Quantitative real-time fluorescence polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted from NPCs using the Super FastPure Cell RNA Isolation Kit (RC102, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.). Reverse transcription was performed using HiScript III RT SuperMix (R323-01, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd). Finnally, SYBR fluorescent dye (Q712-02, Vazyme) was used for qRT-PCR. The expression levels of GAPDH were normalised. The deltaCq replicates of control and subject samples were normalised to the geometric mean of the internal reference gene for the purpose of conducting statistical tests and estimating p-values. To perform specific qRT-PCR, gene specific primer was employed in the step of reverse transcription. The primers used for qRT-PCR were indicated in Table. S3 and Table. S4.

Immunofluorescence

NPCs were seeded uniformly at a density of 5 × 10⁵/ml on 24 mm slides and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at the conclusion of the treatment period. Thereafter, the cells were permeabilised with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min and blocked with 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 2 h. Subsequently, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C using a primary antibody and incubated the following day with a secondary antibody after washing. Moreover, DAPI was employed to stain the nuclei for a period of 10 min at room temperature. The images were taken using a fluorescence microscope.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Cells were lysed with NP40 lysate (Beyotime, P0013F), placed on ice for 30 min, and then centrifuged at 12,000 r/min for 10 min at 4 °C. 100 µL of cell supernatant was retained for Western blot analysis. The remaining cell supernatant was divided into two equal portions, respectively.

After adding anti-IgG (negative control) and anti-MAVS antibody, protein A/G magnetic beads (MCE, HY-K0202) were added to the mixture at 4 °C for 8–12 h. The beads were washed with NP40 lysate for three times, and then the Liquid was discarded and added with loading buffer to prepare the protein samples. Finally, the samples were used for Western Blot and a ChemiDoc-It 610 Imaging System (UVP, USA) was performed to visualize the protein bands.

RNA sequencing

The cells were divided into two groups, the taurin + poly(I: C) group and the poly(I: C) group. Both groups of cells were transfected with poly(I: C) for 16 h. Cells in the taurin + poly(I: C) group were simultaneously treated with 1 mM taurine. Three samples were selected from each of the taurin + poly(I: C) and poly(I: C) groups to establish biological replicates, and after RNA extraction, the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer was used to determine RNA integrity and total amount. After passing the quality control test, the sequencing library construction was started. Library construction and transcriptome sequencing were performed on the Illumina sequencing platform of Beijing Qinglian Biotech Co.,Ltd. Relevant differentially expressed genes and core genes were analysed using DESeq2 software. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were p < 0.05 and the fold difference was 1.5-fold. Based on the screened DEGs, the differential genes were analysed for GO and KEGG enrichment using ClusterProfiler. The RNA-seq data generated in this study are available on the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database under PRJNA1189283.

Non-targeted metabolomics analysis

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC.MS/MS) (Biotree Biotech Co., Ltd, Shanghai) was employed for metabolomics studies. The cells were divided into two groups: the control group and the LPS group. The LPS group was stimulated with 2 µg/ml of LPS for 72 h. The cells were scraped off and snap-frozen with liquid nitrogen. Following the completion of sequencing, the raw data were imported into the metabolomics processing software Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) in order to obtain a data matrix comprising retention time, mass-to-charge ratio and peak intensity. In order to reduce the errors caused by sample preparation and instrument instability, the response intensities of the sample mass spectral peaks were normalised to form a normalised data matrix. Concurrently, variables exhibiting a relative standard deviation (RSD) exceeding 30% for the QC samples were excluded, and a log10 transformation was applied to generate the final data matrix for subsequent analysis. Furthermore, metabolite information was obtained by matching the MS and MSMS mass spectral data with the metabolic public database HMDB. The differential metabolites were subjected to principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) using the R package ropls (Version 1.6.2), and the stability of the model was evaluated through seven cycles of cross-validation. The selection of significantly different metabolites was based on two criteria: the variable weight values (VIP) obtained from the OPLS-DA model and the Student’s t-test P-value. Metabolites with VIP > 1 and P < 0.05 were considered significantly different. The principal metabolites derived from disparate tissues were subjected to pathway enrichment analysis and KEGG analysis. Source data have been deposited to the EMBL-EBI MetaboLights database with the identifier MTBLS11745.

Taurine content measurement

The taurine content was determined using a taurine assay kit (Cell Biolabs, MET-5071). The cells or tissues were pretreated and homogenised in cold PBS, and the protein content was determined by the BCA method. Subsequently, the homogenate was combined with the taurine reaction buffer and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The reaction was terminated by the addition of a stopping solution, and the absorbance of each sample was measured at 405 nm using an enzyme marker. The concentration of taurine in the samples was calculated using a standard curve generated from the absorbance values of the taurine standards provided in the kit.

ATP measurement

The intracellular ATP content was determined using the ATP measurement kit. The samples were first placed at 4 °C and then 200 µl of lysate was added. After lysis of the cells, the Liquid was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 5 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was collected. After configuring the ATP assay working solution, an appropriate amount of solution was added to the assay wells, followed by the addition of standards or samples for ATP content determination. A multiscan spectrum (Bio-Rad Laboratories Co., Ltd.) was used for detection and the ATP content of the samples was converted from the standard curve.

Transmission electron microscope

Firstly, in accordance with the experimental requirements, NPCs seeded in cell culture plates were gently washed with PBS. After removing PBS, a 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution was added to the cells. Then the cells were scraped off and transferred into EP tubes. The tubes were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 800 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, and then washed three times with clean PBS for 10 min each time. Following the final centrifugation, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were suspended and fixed in 1% osmic acid for one hour. Subsequently, the cells were washed with PBS for a period of 10 min on three occasions. Following this, the cells were washed with 30% ethanol for a further 15 min and treated with gradient dehydration in ethanol. The specimens were then embedded by paraffin and subsequently cut into slices measuring between 70 and 90 mm in length. Finally, the specimen sheets were stained twice with a solution of uranium acetate and lead citrate. The copper mesh with thin slices of the samples was positioned within the electron microscope (Japan Electronics Co., Ltd) for observation, and images were captured.

Human interleukin-1β enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

The IL-1β in the supernatant was measured by a IL-1β ELISA kit (Elabscience, E-EL-H0149). The standards and samples were initially added to a 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for a period of 90 min. Immediately following the removal of the Liquid, 100 µL of the biotinylated antibody/antigen working solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for one hour. Subsequently, 350 µL of wash solution was added to each well and allowed to soak for a period of 1–2 min. Subsequently, 100 µL of enzyme conjugate working solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Moreover, the Liquid in the wells was discarded and the plate was washed five times. Furthermore, 90 µL of substrate solution (TMB) was added to each well, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C for approximately 15 min, protected from Light. Finally, 50 µL of termination solution was added to each well to terminate the reaction. The OD value at 450 nm was then measured using an enzyme marker (Bio-Rad Laboratories Co., Ltd.).

CASP-1activity measurement

CASP-1 activity was measured using a kit (Beyotime, C1101). Briefly, standard samples were prepared by dilution of pNA (10 mM). Cells were then collected by centrifugation at 600 g for 5 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, the lysate was added to the precipitate and placed in an ice bath for 15 min. An appropriate amount of Ac-YVAD-pNA (2 mM) was removed and the reaction system was configured according to the instructions. The final OD value of 405 nm was determined using an enzyme marker (Bio-Rad Laboratories Co., Ltd.).

Intracellular ROS measurement

A ROS kit was used to measure intracellular ROS levels (Beyotime, S0033S). Specifically, DCFH-DA was diluted 1:1000 with serum-free culture medium to give a final concentration of 10 µmol/l. After incubation at 37℃ for 20 min, the cells were washed three times with serum-free cell culture medium. Finally, ROS levels were assessed by fluorescence microscopy or flow cytometry after cell collection.

JC-1 measurement

Measurements were performed using the Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime, C2003S). JC-1 (200×) was first configured as a 1× solution with staining buffer. It was then added to the cells and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing with PBS, fluorescence microscopy or flow cytometry was used for detection.

SiRNA transfection

Firstly, NPCs were first seeded one day in advance at 70–90% cell density. After that, the original medium was removed and the cells were washed 2–3 times with PBS and 900 µL of fresh serum-free medium was added. Subsequently, PolyFast transfection reagent (MCE, HY-K1014) was diluted. For each well of a 6-well plate, 50 µL of serum-free medium was mixed with 3 µL of PolyFast transfection reagent for 5 min at room temperature. To dilute the siRNA, for each cell well in a 6-well plate, 50 µL of serum-free medium was mixed with 1 µg of siRNA for 5 min at room temperature. Finally, the diluted PolyFast transfection reagent and siRNA were gently mixed to obtain the PolyFast transfection reagent/RNA complex, which was incubated at room temperature for 15 min and then added to the cells. 72 h later, the cells were treated differently to observe the changes of the indexes. The primer sequences for siRNA were shown in Table. S5.

The transfection of poly(I: C) (Selleck, E0518) is similar to the transfection of siRNA. It is noteworthy that successful transfection of poly(I: C) can be achieved by replacing siRNA with poly(I: C) in the above steps. After transfection for 16 h, corresponding observation was conducted.

PNPT1 plasmid transfection

PNPT1 plasmid was purchased from Beijing Yiqiao Shenzhou Technology Co. Ltd. Before transfection, NPCs were placed in complete medium. After dilution of DNA plasmid and PolyFast transfection reagent (MCE, HY-K1014) with Opti-MEM medium, both were mixed. The mixture was added to the wells of culture plates containing cells and fresh medium for 16 h. The culture was continued for 48 h. The relative expression of PNPT1 was detected by qRT-PCR and WB.

Autophagy double-labeled adenovirus infection

NPCs were seeded onto cell climbing tablets, and then the cells were infected with mRFP-GFP-LC3 adenovirus (MOI = 50) (Shanghai Hanhundsun Technology Co., Ltd.). Subsequently, after cells had grown to 70% density, NPCs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature and then washed with PBS three times for 5 min each. The images were taken using a fluorescence microscope.

Cell viability measurement

The cell viability was measured by using AC11L054-Cell Counting Kit-8 (Life-iLab, Shanghai, China). The procedure was as follows. The initial step involved the preparation of the cell suspension. Subsequently, the cell suspensions were seeded in 96-well plates with approximately 100 µl per well, allowing for three replicates of the same samples to be conducted. Subsequently, 10 µl of CCK-8 solution was introduced following the co-culture of cells with 1 M taurine or LPS for a period of 72 h. The plates were then incubated for a period of between one and four hours. Finally, the absorbance value (OD) at 450 nm was determined using a multiscan spectrum (Bio-Rad Laboratories Co., Ltd.).

Animal experiment

Three-month-old male Sprague-Dawley rats were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Centre of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (HUST) and were housed in a 12 h:12 h Light-dark cycle at 21 °C. The number of rats used in each group was five, in accordance with the methodology employed in previous studies [1, 5]. The rats were housed in facilities designated as SPF-grade, with access to a sufficient quantity of feed and water. All animal experiments were approved by the committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (No. 4094), and were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki. Following weighing, the rats were anaesthetised with pentobarbital (2%, 40 mg/kg). A 33-gauge needle was utilised to puncture the Co8/9 intervertebral discs of rats in each group, with 10 µl of LPS (2 µg/ml) subsequently injected in order to establish the IVDD degeneration model. In the treatment group, 1 M taurine was incorporated into the 10 µl solution containing LPS. The control group was administered an equal volume of PBS. All animals received a single injection at the initiation of the experiment. X-ray, CT and MRI scans were conducted on the rats’ tails, and NP tissue samples were obtained for histological analysis following one-month.

Radiological analysis

One month following the surgical procedure, a series of radiological analyses were conducted on all rats, utilising X-ray, CT and MRI techniques. Following anaesthesia, the rats were maintained in a supine position with their tails aligned in a straight Line. A 3D reconstruction of the CT was created using NRecon. The DHI and MRI degeneration levels were determined based on the Pfirrmann grading system.

Hisotogical analysis

One month following the treatment, all rats were sacrificed. The NP tissues were collected and decalcified for a month. Subsequently, the tissue were embedded in paraffin. H&E staining, safranin O-fast green staining and Masson staining were performed to asses the histological degree of rat discs. To further asses the expression of RLR, PNPT1, BAX and dsRNA, IF and IHC were performed.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. The number of experimental replicates and the level of significance are detailed in the figure legends. The generation of charts and the performance of statistical tests were conducted using the GraphPad Prism software, version 8.0. The two-way ANOVA was employed to ascertain significant differences between groups. The level of statistical significance was indicated by stars: n.s. (not significant), *p < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.
