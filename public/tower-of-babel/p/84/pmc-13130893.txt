The Trojan Horse Around the Heart
Authors: Lucas Lentini Herling de Oliveira, Thiago Luis Scudeler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

In Greek mythology, the fall of Troy began not with an army at the gates but with a hidden threat within the city walls. The Trojan horse symbolized a threat that appeared distant and harmless yet ultimately unleashed destruction from within. In medicine, rare diseases sometimes follow a similar clinical course. In the report by Katouzi et al1 in this issue of JACC: Case Reports, pneumopericardium emerges as the first visible sign of a hidden and life-threatening process—a gastropericardial fistula that developed decades after gastrectomy. The case illustrates how an apparently remote surgical history may re-emerge as a catastrophic cardiothoracic complication, leading to infection of the pericardial cavity exposed to the contents of the gastrointestinal tract.

Gastropericardial fistula is among the rarest causes of purulent pericarditis and pneumopericardium. Fewer than 100 cases have been described in the literature, most occurring in patients with previous upper gastrointestinal surgery, peptic ulcer disease, or malignancy.2 The pathophysiological mechanism involves progressive erosion of the gastric wall or postoperative anatomical changes that eventually create communication between the gastric lumen and the pericardial sac. The latency between the original gastric intervention and clinical presentation may develop decades after the original gastric intervention, emphasizing that surgical history—even remote—may remain biologically relevant in the diagnostic evaluation of cardiothoracic syndromes.

The clinical manifestations of gastropericardial fistula are frequently nonspecific. Chest pain, dyspnea, fever, and systemic inflammatory response may mimic more common cardiovascular disorders, including acute coronary syndromes, acute pericarditis, or pulmonary infection.3 In this context, pneumopericardium becomes a crucial diagnostic clue. Although uncommon, the presence of air within the pericardial sac should immediately prompt careful evaluation for possible communication with adjacent hollow organs. Pneumopericardium may occur after trauma, mechanical ventilation, or invasive procedures, but when accompanied by an air-fluid level it strongly suggests infection or fistulous communication with the gastrointestinal tract. In severe cases, accumulation of intrapericardial air may even lead to hemodynamic compromise and cardiac tamponade.4

Imaging therefore plays a decisive role in diagnosis. Transthoracic echocardiography remains the first-line modality for pericardial disease; however, intrapericardial air often limits acoustic windows and compromises image quality.3 In such circumstances, computed tomography becomes the imaging modality of choice, providing improved visualization of pneumopericardium, mediastinal structures, and potential fistulous tracts. In the case presented by Katouzi and colleagues,1 computed tomography revealed a characteristic air-fluid level surrounding the heart and later demonstrated direct communication between the gastric remnant and the pericardial space, findings strongly suggestive of gastropericardial fistula.

Once gastric contents enter the pericardial cavity, the consequences can be dramatic. The pericardial space, normally sterile, becomes contaminated by enteric microorganisms, leading to polymicrobial infection and rapidly progressive purulent pericarditis.2 This condition is associated with high morbidity and mortality, particularly when diagnosis is delayed. Pericardial fluid in such cases often reveals enteric bacteria and fungal organisms, reflecting contamination from the gastrointestinal tract.

Management of gastropericardial fistula highlights one of the most fundamental principles of infectious disease and critical care: Definitive source control is essential. Pericardial drainage may temporarily relieve tamponade physiology and reduce microbial burden, but antimicrobial therapy alone cannot resolve an ongoing anatomical communication between the stomach and the pericardium. Surgical or endoscopic closure of the fistula therefore represents the cornerstone of definitive treatment. Historical cases consistently demonstrate extremely poor outcomes in patients managed conservatively without repair of the underlying defect.2

Beyond its rarity, this case has broader implications for cardiovascular practice. Cardiologists and emergency physicians are often the first clinicians to evaluate patients presenting with chest pain and abnormal imaging findings. Recognizing pneumopericardium as a potentially life-threatening signal rather than an incidental radiographic finding is therefore essential. In particular, when pneumopericardium occurs in patients with prior gastric or esophageal surgery, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for gastropericardial fistula. Early multidisciplinary collaboration involving cardiology, thoracic surgery, gastroenterology, and critical care is critical to optimize outcomes.

The metaphor of the Trojan horse provides a useful conceptual framework for understanding this condition. Just as the hidden warriors within the wooden horse ultimately led to the fall of Troy, a seemingly remote surgical intervention may carry latent consequences capable of emerging decades later as severe cardiovascular complications. In this context, pneumopericardium represents the moment when the hidden threat finally becomes visible. Recognizing this signal and acting promptly may determine whether the clinical course ends in recovery or tragedy.
