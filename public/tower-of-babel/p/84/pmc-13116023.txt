Addressing Frailty in the Emergency Department: Early Outcomes of a Geriatric Rapid Access Clinic
Authors: Amine Kaab, Kristina M Kokorelias, Kristine Kim, Lindy Romanovsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesOlder adults represent a growing proportion of emergency department (ED) visits, with an increased risk of adverse outcomes following discharge, particularly when frailty is present. Access to post‐ED geriatric follow‐up is often delayed, leaving patients without timely support and increasing their risk of ED revisits and subsequent hospital admissions. This study evaluates a novel Geriatric Rapid Access Clinic (Geri‐RAC) designed to provide expedited, specialized follow‐up for high‐risk older adults discharged from the ED.MethodsA retrospective chart review was conducted of all patients who attended the Geri‐RAC at a large academic hospital in Toronto from January 2023 to July 2024. Older adults identified as high‐risk in the ED were referred to the clinic. Patient outcomes included ED revisit rates and anticipated hospital admission avoidance; data on clinic performance indicators, patient characteristics, and accessed supports were also measured.ResultsThirty‐three patients attended the Geri‐RAC during the 19‐month pilot. Mean age was 82.6 years, with 81.8% having a Clinical Frailty Scale score ≥ 4. Functional limitations were present in 69.7% of patients. Median wait time from ED referral to appointment was 7 days. Cognitive testing was conducted in 78.7% of attendees and medication de‐prescribing was initiated in 48.5%. Most patients (87.9%) were newly connected to support services, averaging 2.6 ± 2.1 service linkages per patient. 45.5% of patients received new specialist referrals. The 7‐, 30‐, and 90‐day ED revisit rates were 0%, 12.1%, and 24.2%, respectively. Admission avoidance was anticipated in 42.4% of cases.ConclusionsThe Geri‐RAC provided timely access to post‐ED follow‐up and multidisciplinary care for frail older adults. Early findings highlight the potential for this model to enhance ED discharge planning, facilitate structured care transitions, and decrease reliance on emergency services in this high‐risk population.Keywords: frailty, geriatric care, geriatric emergency medicine, rapid access clinic, transitional care

Introduction

Older adults—persons aged 65 years or older—are disproportionately high users of the emergency department (ED), representing between 20% and 40% of all visits to Canadian EDs [1, 2]. As the population continues to age, healthcare systems face increasing pressures to streamline care for older adults and reduce ED overcrowding [1, 3, 4].

An ED visit often signals a critical turning point for older adults, marking an increased risk of adverse outcomes such as functional decline, repeat ED visits, and hospitalization [1, 5]. Compared to younger patients, older adults frequently present with complex health concerns, multiple comorbidities, and polypharmacy, making it difficult for traditional ED environments to meet their needs effectively [6, 7].

A key contributor to the complexity of care in this demographic is the rising prevalence of frailty [7]. A common health concern in the geriatric population, frailty is characterized by aging‐associated declines in function across multiple physical and psychosocial domains [8, 9, 10]. It can lead to diminished physiological reserve and heightened vulnerability to stressors which results in a reduced ability to restore homeostasis [8, 9, 10]. Consequently, frail older adults are at risk of rapid deterioration and are more likely to experience adverse health outcomes including delirium, falls, hospitalization, diminished quality of life, and death [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. In Canada, 22% of community‐dwelling older adults were estimated to be frail in 2013 to 2014, with the proportion rising with age and reaching 48% among those aged 85 and older [14].

In a recent international study, the prevalence of frailty in older adults presenting to the ED was found to range from 26% to 51% across different countries [15]. The challenge of caring for frail older adults in the ED will intensify pressures on healthcare systems as the global population continues to age [1, 7]. Due to the complex, multidimensional nature of frailty, this population has distinct and multifaceted care needs [8, 13]. However, prompting frail older adults to visit multiple healthcare services can result in poor care coordination if there is inadequate communication between providers [16]. While ED‐based solutions and transitional care programs have shown only modest benefit in previous studies, geriatric rapid access clinics (Geri‐RACs) represent a promising but underexplored approach to meet the care needs of frail older adults post‐ED discharge [17].

Rapid access clinics (RACs) exist as outpatient clinics servicing patients referred from the ED or general practice [18]. Recently, RACs have been adopted by healthcare systems around the world to meet time‐sensitive health needs and reduce overcrowding of the ED [4, 19, 20, 21, 22]. Most RACs are designed to manage single symptoms or specific diseases and have been associated with decreased hospital admissions and improved patient health outcomes [23, 24]. In Canada, there are single‐condition RACs where many patients are older adults, but these clinics do not target older adults specifically nor provide the multi‐domain care that frailty demands [25, 26].

To address this gap, Mount Sinai Hospital, a large academic hospital in Toronto, launched a Geri‐RAC to provide timely, specialized care for high‐risk older adults following ED discharge. Before its implementation, patients referred to an outpatient geriatric clinic often waited up to 5 months for an appointment. The Geri‐RAC was designed to reduce repeat ED visits, prevent hospital admissions, and support safe discharges by offering a comprehensive geriatric assessment within 14 days of ED discharge, along with referrals to community and hospital‐based services. Given the scarcity of similar clinics in North America, evaluating this model of care presents an opportunity to enhance geriatric emergency medicine and inform broader implementation efforts.

This study aims to evaluate the Geri‐RAC's patient profile, performance, and early outcomes over its 19‐month pilot period. Findings will provide insights into its impact on post‐ED care for frail older adults and identify opportunities for improvement to guide the expansion of similar models.

Methods

Study Population

This study reviewed all patients presenting to the Geri‐RAC at Mount Sinai Hospital in Toronto, Canada, from its inception in January 2023 to July 2024. Patients eligible for referral to the Geri‐RAC were aged 65 years or older, presented to the Mount Sinai Hospital ED, and identified as high‐risk by either the ED physician or the geriatric emergency management nurse (GEM RN) based on the presence of two to three criteria as outlined in Figure 1. Patients with terminal illness and limited life expectancy were excluded from referral. Referrals were made by ED physicians with guidance from GEM RNs prior to ED discharge. All referred patients were scheduled for a Geri‐RAC appointment within 7 to 14 days of their initial ED visit.

Geriatric Rapid Access Clinic Setting

The Geri‐RAC operated on an outpatient model with one afternoon half‐day session per week and accommodated up to three patients per session. Each patient attended a 90‐min in‐person appointment with a geriatrician. Comprehensive geriatric assessments (CGAs), safety assessments, and medication reviews were conducted during each visit, with some elements of the CGA often initiated by the GEM RN in the ED to streamline the consultation. Additional evaluations, including mobility assessments and cognitive testing using tools such as the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE), or Rowland Universal Dementia Assessment Scale (RUDAS), were performed at the geriatrician's discretion based on clinical need. Following completion of investigations and care planning, patients were discharged from the clinic to their primary care provider or referred to appropriate specialists.

Referrals were also made to provincial home care services through Ontario Health atHome (OHaH), as well as various community‐based programs and resources (Appendix S1 available as supplemental material accompanying the online version of this paper). OHaH included physiotherapy (PT), occupational therapy (OT), home safety assessments, social work (SW), personal support workers (PSWs), registered nurses (RNs), dietetics, or speech‐language pathology (SLP), as determined by patient need. Follow‐up appointments, if necessary, were arranged through the Geriatric Medicine Clinic.

The core clinical team consisted of two geriatricians, one nurse, one medical secretary, and one ambulatory clinic manager. Medical residents or fellows may have participated and supported services at the Geri‐RAC; however, clinic operations were not dependent on their presence. To maximize clinic resource use, if two Geri‐RAC appointment slots remained unfilled 48 h prior to a clinic day, the medical secretary reassigned one of these to an urgent referral from the general Geriatric Medicine Clinic waitlist. The hospital electronic medical record (EMR) system (Cerner PowerChart, North Kansas City, MO) was utilized to minimize potential delays in data sharing and duplication of information sourcing between ED physicians, GEM RNs, and Geri‐RAC geriatricians.

Study Design

A retrospective chart review was conducted of all patients who attended the Geri‐RAC from January 2023 to July 2024. This study was approved by the institutional Research Ethics Board at Mount Sinai Hospital and verbal consent was obtained from all participants for chart review. Patient and appointment data were collected from the hospital EMR to evaluate the following:

Primary outcomes: (1) patient‐related outcomes, including ED revisits and hospital admission avoidance; and (2) clinic performance indicators, such as wait times, appointment adherence, and assessment completion. Secondary outcomes: (3) demographic and clinical characteristics of Geri‐RAC patients; and (4) types of services and supports accessed, including referrals to specialists, provincial home care services, and community‐based programs.

Measures

ED revisits were measured as unscheduled return visits to the Mount Sinai Hospital ED within 7, 30, and 90 days following Geri‐RAC appointment. Admission avoidance was determined based on the ED physician's clinical judgment at the time of referral, reflecting their assessment that a timely follow‐up appointment through the Geri‐RAC post‐ED discharge would have prevented a hospital admission. Wait times were calculated in days from the time of ED referral to the scheduled Geri‐RAC appointment.

Patient comorbidity was measured using the Charlson comorbidity index, with a higher score indicating greater comorbidity and risk of mortality [27]. Frailty level was evaluated using the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) [28]. Furthermore, patients' best possible medication histories were reviewed and compared against the American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria (AGS Beers Criteria) to identify potentially inappropriate medication use in older adults [29].

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient and clinic performance data. Unless otherwise stated, all continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and all categorical variables are reported as number of subjects with percentage of total numbers in brackets. Wait time to Geri‐RAC appointment is reported using median and interquartile range (IQR) in brackets. All analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient Characteristics

During the pilot period between January 2023 to July 2024, 33 patients were seen at the Geri‐RAC. The full list of patient characteristics is summarized in Table 1. Patients ranged from 61 to 95 years of age, with a mean of 82.6 ± 8.4 years. There was a predominance of female patients with 25 women (75.8%) in attendance. 51.5% of Geri‐RAC patients reported living alone, and 39.4% required regular home supports in the form of private caregivers, PSWs, or OT services. A total of 48.5% of patients required assistive devices for ambulation, and 69.7% had some degree of functional dependence in their activities of daily living (ADLs) or instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs). Geri‐RAC patients had a mean Charlson comorbidity index score of 5.1 ± 1.6, with a score of 5 representing a 21% estimated 10‐year survival [27]. Notably, 33.3% of patients had a history of or current major depressive disorder, and 21.2% had preexisting, documented cognitive impairment. 81.8% of patients had a CFS score of 4 or greater, with the most common patient CFS scores being 5 and 6, representing mild and moderate frailty, respectively (Figure 2). Geri‐RAC attendees were also found to be taking an average of 8.2 ± 4.5 medications per patient, with a mean of 1.3 ± 1.3 medications deemed potentially inappropriate for use in older adults based on the AGS Beers Criteria.

Emergency Department Visit Characteristics

Geri‐RAC patients presenting to the ED were classified using the Canadian Triage and Acuity Scale (CTAS) as levels 3, urgent (72.7%) and 4, less urgent (27.3%), representing complaints that require assessment within 30 and 60 min, respectively [30]. The most common ED presenting complaints among Geri‐RAC patients included: confusion (24.4%), falls (18.2%), syncope and pre‐syncope (12.1%), and head injury (9.1%).

Clinic Performance and Assessments

During the clinic pilot period, a total of 61 patient referrals were made to the Geri‐RAC. However, only 33 patients attended their scheduled appointments, resulting in a 54.1% attendance rate (Figure 3). The median (IQR) wait time from ED referral to Geri‐RAC appointment was 7 days [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. The most common reasons for referral included: cognitive decline (51.5%), falls and mobility concerns (30.3%), and new or worsening health concerns (21.2%) (Table 2). Geriatrician‐led CGAs, medication reviews, and safety assessments were routinely conducted at the Geri‐RAC and completed for 100% of patients. Notably, 78.7% of patients received formal cognitive testing, of which 73.1% were found to have mild to severe cognitive decline.

Treatment Recommendations and Referrals

Following investigations, 87.9% of patients were connected to new support services, receiving an average of 0.9 ± 1.2 referrals to OHaH services and 1.8 ± 1.4 referrals to community‐based programs and resources. This amounted to an average of 2.6 ± 2.1 total service linkages per Geri‐RAC patient. Additionally, 45.5% of patients required new specialist referrals, such as to audiology (18.2%), cardiology (12.1%), geriatric psychiatry (9.1%), and neurology (9.1%) for further evaluation or treatment. Medications deemed unnecessary or potentially harmful by the staff geriatricians were discontinued in 48.5% of patients. Follow‐up appointments were scheduled for 63.6% of patients at the end of the visit.

Patient Outcomes

No ED revisits occurred within the first 7 days following the Geri‐RAC appointment. Within 30 days of their Geri‐RAC visit, only four patients (12.1%) returned to the ED, and none of these revisits were for the same reason as their initial presentation. An additional four patients (12.1%) returned to the ED between 31 and 90 days post‐visit, resulting in a total 90‐day ED revisit rate of 24.2%. Of the later revisits, two were related to complications from pedestrian motor vehicle accidents and were not associated with patients' prior health issues. Hospital admission avoidance was anticipated for 42.4% of patients referred to the Geri‐RAC.

Discussion

Older adults represent a disproportionate percentage of ED visits, and the aging population is expected to place additional strain on healthcare services [1, 2]. The ED often serves as a default entry point into the healthcare system for frail older adults, many of whom have complex needs that are not adequately addressed in acute care settings [3, 16, 31]. Without timely and specialized follow‐up, these individuals are at elevated risk of functional decline, hospital admission, and mortality [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. In response, existing policy recommendations advocate for the need for integrated, patient‐centered care models that address the unique needs of frail older adults, with the goal of reducing avoidable ED utilization, hospitalizations, and associated health declines [7, 32, 33]. The Geri‐RAC pilot addresses this critical gap by offering rapid, post‐ED follow‐up grounded in geriatric expertise.

This pilot study evaluated the Mount Sinai Hospital Geri‐RAC as a novel approach to delivering expedited, specialized care for high‐risk older adults following ED discharge. To our knowledge, this represents one of the first structured evaluations of a Geri‐RAC in a North American hospital setting, contributing early empirical evidence to a largely underdeveloped area of transitional geriatric care. The findings offer preliminary insights into the potential of Geri‐RAC models to improve patient outcomes and alleviate pressure on the ED and broader healthcare system.

The older adult population seen at the Geri‐RAC was marked by advanced age, high medical complexity, polypharmacy, functional limitations, and dependence on external supports. In comparison to the 40% aggregate prevalence of frailty among older ED patients reported by the European Taskforce on Geriatric Emergency Medicine, this cohort demonstrated a markedly larger proportion of frail patients, with 81.8% classified as very mild to severe on the CFS [15]. This higher level of frailty reflects the complexity of their health needs, particularly in the context of cognitive decline and the challenges associated with managing multiple chronic conditions. Frailty has also been associated with increased rates of ED return visits, hospital admission, and mortality in older adults presenting to the ED [34]. Additionally, there was a moderate degree of concomitant mood disorders and unaccompanied living in this cohort, which can contribute to social isolation and necessitate a more multidisciplinary approach to support healthcare engagement and overall well‐being [35].

Strengths

This pilot study demonstrated that the Geri‐RAC addresses several challenges faced by frail older adults after ED discharge. A key strength of this model is the ability to provide timely access to specialized care, including CGA, within a median of 1 week following the index ED visit. CGAs are essential for high‐risk older adults, identifying functional, cognitive, psychosocial, and medical risks to guide early intervention and care planning [36]. This clinic reduces the long wait times traditionally associated with outpatient geriatric services at Mount Sinai Hospital—ranging from one to five months—which has been a major barrier to accessing timely care in this population. Moreover, by providing patients with Geri‐RAC appointments prior to discharge from the ED, this referral model helps address common challenges in navigating ED‐to‐community care transitions [37].

In this pilot, a substantial proportion of patients (78.7%) underwent cognitive testing, an essential step for early detection and management of conditions such as dementia, which has been linked to a greater risk of hospital admissions and ED visits [38, 39, 40]. Geriatric interventions resulted in identifying an average of 1.3 medications per patient as inappropriate or potentially harmful with initiation of deprescribing in nearly half (48.5%) of patients through this clinic. This Geri‐RAC intervention aims to minimize medication burden and its effects on quality of life, adverse drug events, and drug‐related ED visits [41, 42]. A 2014 study found that adverse drug events in older adults accounted for 0.8% of all ED visits in Ontario, Canada, with 21.5% of these patients requiring hospital admission [43]. Moreover, polypharmacy and exposure to potentially inappropriate medications are well‐established risk factors for adverse outcomes in older adults, including falls, functional decline, hospitalizations, and mortality [41, 42].

The use of a multidisciplinary approach within the Geri‐RAC, including ED physician referrals, geriatricians, nurses, and social workers, is consistent with best practices for geriatric care, as outlined in existing healthcare policy frameworks [7, 32, 33]. The involvement of community services is a central tenet of national policies aimed at supporting aging in place and has been associated with improvements in physical and mental health outcomes for older adults [44, 45]. In this study, the majority of Geri‐RAC patients (87.9%) received new community service linkages to provincial home care services and community‐based programs, with an average of 2.6 referrals per patient, highlighting the critical role of post‐ED transitional care in addressing the unmet needs of frail older adults in the acute setting [3, 46, 47]. Additionally, 45.5% of patients received new specialist referrals, reflecting the Geri‐RAC's role in facilitating timely access to specialized care and serving as a key touch point for geriatric care coordination.

This pilot study observed relatively low ED revisit rates following Geri‐RAC appointment—0% at 7 days, 12.1% at 30 days, and 24.2% at 90 days—compared to 7%, 24%, and 41%, respectively, observed in a 2024 multicenter study of frail older adults [34]. Crucially, none of these representations were for the same or related complaints, suggesting resolution of the acute index issue or effective management through the Geri‐RAC. However, ED revisits for new or low‐acuity concerns may reflect unmet needs beyond the initial reason for referral, highlighting the importance of anticipatory care planning. This further reinforces the value of timely specialist referrals and follow‐up to investigate and manage broader health issues. However, not all return ED visits are avoidable and should not be confused with inappropriate use of emergency services. Notably, two of the eight (25%) 90‐day ED revisits observed in this study were due to complications from pedestrian motor vehicle accidents. Certainly, such ED visits were unrelated to prior health concerns and could not have been reasonably prevented by outpatient geriatric care, underscoring the need for nuanced interpretation of ED revisit data.

Prior studies indicate that RACs serving individuals with complex health needs are associated with reductions in hospital admissions, a trend also reflected in the results of this pilot [21, 48, 49, 50]. Carmel et al. demonstrated that a similar rapid primary care follow‐up model resulted in ED admission avoidance for 16% of patients [49]. Moreover, earlier integration of geriatric assessments and care planning for older adults in the ED has been associated with higher rates of discharge and lower rates of hospital admissions [51]. In this pilot study, 42.4% of patients were predicted to avoid hospitalization, reinforcing the potential of these models to prevent admissions among frail older adults. The Geri‐RAC model further facilitates enhanced ED discharge planning, allowing for appropriate outpatient clinical referrals, timely follow‐up, and individualized care planning [46]. By providing older patients with a more structured and reliable discharge pathway, this ED‐integrated model can contribute to hospital admission avoidance and offers a promising avenue to alleviate ED boarding and subsequent overcrowding by safely transitioning suitable patients out of acute care settings [3, 4, 49, 52, 53].

Areas of Improvement

Despite these positive findings, the Geri‐RAC model also faces challenges that reflect broader issues within the healthcare system. The 54.1% attendance rate for Geri‐RAC appointments is lower than expected and is the subject of current investigation. The resultant 45.9% “no‐show” rate may reflect barriers to access or systemic challenges in engaging frail older adults in care. Prior studies have identified that transportation barriers, greater comorbidity, memory issues, depression, and a lack of caregiver support can contribute to missed appointments, particularly for older adults [54, 55, 56, 57]. The relatively high rate of nonattendance in this study suggests that future iterations of the Geri‐RAC should consider solutions to enhance patient engagement, such as offering flexible appointment times, addressing transportation needs, and ensuring that patients have sufficient support systems in place.

Another noteworthy finding from this study is that only 78.7% of patients received cognitive testing, a critical component of comprehensive geriatric care. While this can be attributed to factors such as clinical judgment or time constraints, it may also indicate an opportunity for improvement in the delivery of comprehensive assessments. Research suggests that early detection and intervention for cognitive decline can significantly improve outcomes for older adults [38]. Future clinic development should focus on ensuring that cognitive assessments are systematically included in the care process for all frail older adults, particularly those presenting with risk factors such as increased frailty, functional decline, mood disorders, and polypharmacy [58].

Limitations

The limited sample size of 33 patients may not be representative of the broader older adult population, particularly in different regions or healthcare settings. Additionally, the single‐site design of this pilot may limit the generalizability of its findings. As such, evaluation of patient outcomes in this descriptive study should be interpreted within the context of a restricted sample and absence of a control group. The study also lacked data on patients who did not attend their scheduled appointments, which prevents a comprehensive understanding of factors influencing attendance and outcomes. Only ED revisit rates at Mount Sinai Hospital were captured and, as such, visits to other hospital systems were not recorded, which may not fully represent the overall risk of ED re‐presentation for this population. While the novel Geri‐RAC model demonstrated promise, further studies with larger, more diverse populations and longer follow‐up periods are needed to more definitively assess its long‐term impact on patient outcomes and healthcare resource utilization.

Conclusions

The novel Geri‐RAC model offers a promising solution to address the needs of frail older adults after ED discharge, providing timely, comprehensive geriatric care that aims to prevent hospital admissions, reduce ED revisits, and improve patient outcomes. While this pilot study provides valuable early insights, further research is needed to evaluate the long‐term impact of the Geri‐RAC model and to refine its design to address challenges such as patient engagement and comprehensive cognitive testing. This study also highlights the importance of ED‐integrated follow‐up care models, emphasizing the Geri‐RAC's potential to improve care transitions, decrease reliance on emergency services, and ultimately support more efficient and effective healthcare provision for frail older adults.
