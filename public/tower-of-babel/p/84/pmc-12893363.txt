Proton Radiography of Different Human Tissues Using Proton Microscope with Imaging Resolution
Authors: Seyede Nasrin Hosseinimotlagh, Abuzar Shakeri

ABSTRACT
Proton imaging can provide better density resolution and thus higher tissue contrast and does not suffer from artifacts due to beam hardening typically affecting X-ray imaging. Proton radiography generates two-dimensional projection images of an object and has applications in patient alignment and verification procedures in preparation for proton beam radiation therapy. Proton radiography enables fast and effective high-precision lateral alignment of the proton beam and target volume in the patient’s body irradiation experiments with limited dose exposure. Our study has clearly demonstrated the potential of a proton microscope for imaging because a proton microscope compensates for image blur using magnetic lenses to the sub-mm level. This innovative work applies physical models of proton transport (including Beth-Bloch energy dissipation, cutoff energy, and Coulomb multiple scattering) to simulate the theory of proton imaging of various human tissues using a proton microscope and study image resolution using Maple software. Our obtained results from this work are given below. (i) To determine the appropriate dose without risks, researchers in the domains of nuclear medicine and radiotherapy must examine the parameters of proton interactions with different tissues. (ii) The current investigation is highly important in the proton radiotherapy and radiography of different human tissues. (iii) The cortical bone has the highest Zeff values, while the adipose tissue has the lowest values at all proton energies. (vi) CSDA range of protons was higher in high-density tissue until 200 MeV energy protons. (v) Total stopping power of a proton is directly proportional to the energy of protons. (iv) The blurring coefficient, ξy(γ), increases with decreasing A.

INTRODUCTION

High-energy proton radiography is a new diagnostic technique to investigate the imaging of objects.[123] Proton radiography has high penetrating power, high detection efficiency, small-scattered background, inherent multipulse capability, and large standoff distances between test objects and detectors.[45678] Proton imaging, especially proton computed tomography (pCT), is an advanced imaging technique that uses a beam of protons to create images, particularly useful for proton therapy due to its ability to accurately determine the relative stopping power (RSP) of tissues, leading to better proton range accuracy and smaller planned tumor volumes.[89101112131415] While still in development, it holds promise for improving radiation therapy by providing more precise visualization of the target volume and its surrounding tissues compared to X-ray CT. In proton imaging, a source of protons, often a beam, is directed through the object or patient being imaged.[1617181920] Protons interact with the material, causing deflections due to their passage through electric and magnetic fields within the plasma or tissues. A detector captures the resulting distribution of protons after they have traversed the object, revealing changes in their trajectory and energy.[202122] Algorithms are used to reconstruct a three-dimensional (3D) image, where each voxel represents the RSP of the material in that region.[232425] While not yet in clinical routine, research suggests that proton imaging could potentially achieve comparable RSP accuracy with a lower dose compared to equivalent X-ray images. For a long time, the practical applicability of proton imaging remained limited, mostly because of insufficient spatial resolution (a few mm) due to multiple Coulomb scattering (MCS) inside an imaged object:[2630] When a proton beam traverses matter, the protons are not only slowed down in interactions with atomic electrons but they are also deflected from their initial direction in electromagnetic interactions with atomic nuclei. The angular spread of a proton beam leaving an object is the result of many such deflections. If the scattered protons fall on a screen behind the object, their spatial distribution is very nearly Gaussian.[3132] This makes proton images blurry unless a more sophisticated approach is chosen. Single particle tracking is one way to compensate for blurring induced by MCS, and current research has become focused on this method. In this paper, we use a magneto-optics-based method that compensates for the image blurring caused by MCS, known as the proton microscope. A proton microscope is particularly useful for imaging dynamic events and for studying objects with a wide range of densities. Proton microscopes can be used to study a variety of applications, including medical imaging, material science, and high-energy physics. Magnification is achievable by either repowering the existing imaging-lens quadrupoles, using new high-gradient quadrupoles.

The aim of this paper is to calculate the key physical parameters in proton imaging using a proton microscope of various human tissues and to investigate image blurring for the first-time via Maple software[33] and using the NIST PSTAR program for determining proton CSDA range and mass stopping power. Therefore, the following topics are brought up respectively. In Section 2, MCS is discussed, and followed by Section 3, proton microscope with a magnetic lens is examined. In Section 4, we introduce proton radiography detector systems. In Section 5, simple physical model of proton radiography is represented. In Section 6, we perform a discussion of multiple scattering effects. Furthermore, in Section 7, proton radiography and tomography of biological samples are discussed. Then, in Section 8, proton radiography resolution is introduced. Finally, conclusions are presented.

MULTIPLE COULOMB SCATTERING

Unlike X-rays, protons undergo a random walk as they pass through an object. This is due to many small-angle collisions with charged particles in the atoms of the object. At first glance, MCS seems like a major drawback to proton radiography, because protons do not travel in a long straight line, and because of angular scattering, the image taken immediately of the object is somewhat blurry.

As we have known, we assumed the negligible energy loss effects of the MCS yield Gaussian distributions for the mean square angle of scattering and mean square lateral displacement. However, in reality, the angular distribution of the MCS has much larger tails than those of the Gaussian, because there are very few (single scattering) protons deflected at very large angles from atomic electrons, contributing to small, negligible energy losses of the MCS. Attempts to achieve a close Gaussian representation of the scattering angle distribution involved a maximum likelihood fit by truncating the large tail ends of the scattering curve.[33] A dataset for measured angular distribution that reflects this scattering curve was fitted with the Molière distribution and with Gaussian distribution. As expected, both Molière and Gaussian perfectly coincide with each other in the central, small-angle scattering region of the angular distribution.[34] The plane of the MCS angular distribution depicts the protons leaving the object which is a Gaussian distribution to detect by a root mean square (rms). PROTON MICROSCOPE WITH MAGNETIC LENS

The charged particle community is looking for techniques exploiting proton interactions instead of X-ray absorption for creating images of human tissue. Due to the MCS inside the measured object, it has proven to be highly nontrivial to achieve sufficient spatial resolution. We present imaging of biological tissue with a proton magnetic lens system. This device relies on magnetic optics, distinguishing it from most published proton imaging methods. For these methods, reducing the data acquisition time to a clinically acceptable level has turned out to be challenging. The primary medical application will be image guidance in proton radiosurgery. Proton images demonstrating the potential for this application are presented. Tomographic reconstructions are included to raise awareness of the possibility of high-resolution proton tomography using magneto-optics.

To develop an appropriate ion-optical system serving as a proton microscope, one should fulfill the following basic principles. Note that in proton imaging experiments, typically the radiochromic film (one or more) is used as a detector, placed in the plane of the image. The detector placement with respect to the object position corresponds to the optical system magnification.

A proton microscope is shown in Figure 1. It basically consists of an arrangement of four magnetic quadrupole lenses followed by a counting detector. In a proton microscope, two-dimensional images with sub-mm resolution are created at the focal plane of a magnetic quadrupole lens on a detector [Figure 1]. After crossing a part of the patient with thickness d and radiation length Lrad, protons exit tissue with angles θ with respect to the initial beam axis z. The trajectory through the PM depends on the exit angle θ. A first set of magnetic lenses (A) transforms the angle θ into a distance from the beam axis at the position of the collimator (B). The larger θ, the larger the distance from the beam axis z. The collimator (B) suppresses protons having more than a certain distance from the beam axis and thus a scattering angle larger than a critical scattering angle θc. A second set of magnetic lenses (C) after the collimator reverses the effect of the lenses before the collimator (A) such that one obtains a point-to-point mapping between the exit coordinates at the patient (x0, y0) and at the detector (x1, y1). Magnetic fields can be chosen such that the image on the detector is larger than the radiographed object itself. Therefore, the name of this device is proton microscope.

PROTON RADIOGRAPHY DETECTOR SYSTEMS

For proton radiography, two different detector systems using three different cameras are available, suiting the different needs of the experimental community. The dynamic detector system consists of 7 Rockwell/Teledyne cameras focused on a 2-mm lutetium oxyorthosilicate (LSO) scintillator using a mirror system. The cameras are equipped with a 105-mm Nikon F2.0 lens and offer a dynamic range of 11 bits. As the decay time of the used LSO is roughly 50 ns, all bursts in one pulse are integrated in one image. The minimum interframe time between two images is 250 ns, which does not limit the effective interframe time given by the accelerator’s capabilities.[25] The effective pixel size at the image plane is just 166.7 μm, which is less than the theoretical limit of the radiographic setup itself. For static imaging, all 7 cameras fire at the same time, resulting in 7 identical images per pulse and 21 images per shot in the case of a line beam pattern. The effective resolution of the image is reduced to just 600 × 600 px as part of the frame holding the scintillator crystals in place is also imaged. For static imaging, a pco.dimax camera as well as a 2-tile lutetium yttrium orthosilicate (LYSO) scintillator is available. The camera offers high-resolution images with an effective pixel size of 60 μm, matching the x3 setup’s spatial resolution performance. The used camera was equipped with correlated double sampling, which is an automated dark field subtraction mechanism. Here, the camera averages pixel values between image capture and subtracts the average value from the captured image. Investigations showed that the accuracy and outcome of the algorithm strictly depend on the spacing between image captures, resulting in a nonuniform result. With a CsI scintillator, a Dantec Dynamics HiSense 620 camera is available for static imaging. The camera offers a sensor resolution of 4 MPx with a pixel size of just 7.4 μm.

The single-particle tracking approach is the most widely accepted technique for proton imaging. Single-particle tracking systems employ a pair of trackers placed before and after the patient to record the spatial coordinates and directions of individual protons.[7] This information, when correlated with proton residual ranges, can be used for correcting multiple Coulomb protons scattering and improving proton image resolution.[8] This superior imaging performance, however, comes with technical challenges, such as bulky and complex instrumentation, the need for expensive high-speed electronics to record individual protons in a clinically feasible time frame, and the need for modifications to the beam transport system to significantly lower proton fluence so that it can be handled by the data-acquisition instrumentation.[9]

In contrast to single-particle tracking, the proton-integrating approach generates proton images by measuring an ensemble of proton energy losses suffered by a proton beam within the patient. The imaging system design is relatively simple, with a single detector placed beyond the patient to collect the dose integral, which can then be calibrated to obtain the equivalent WET map. The proton-integrating approach offers several advantages over the single-particle tracking technique, including faster imaging speeds enabled by the availability of greater proton fluence; compatibility with clinical beam currents, which enables integration into existing proton gantries; and less costly and simpler electronics for data acquisition and processing.

These advantages have led many researchers to further explore the proton-integrating approach to proton imaging. The scientists used beam-range modulation to produce a linearly decreasing detector signal as a function of the depth of penetration for generating water-equivalent thickness maps. Pre-established calibration datasets for time-resolved[11] and energy-resolved[12] dose measurement techniques for passive scattering and scanning pencil beams, respectively, were also shown to generate water-equivalent thickness-equivalent proton maps from the measured dose. A multitude of detector technologies have been used by proton-integrating imaging systems, including multilayer ionization chambers,[12] amorphous silicon,[13] charge coupled device cameras,[14] 2D diode-array detectors,[15] complementary metal oxide semiconductor active pixel sensors,[16] and a single diode.[17] All these systems employ 2D or planar detectors for imaging. The 2D detectors have a thin active cross-section to measure proton energy loss. They therefore need some form of beam range modulation (for passive beams) or energy scanning (for scanning beams) to correlate the measured signal to beam energy.[13] A recent study[18] found higher systematic errors in water-equivalent thickness calculation accuracy when fewer modulated energy layers were used. However, the need to use an adequate number of energy steps for imaging could lead to increased imaging dose and time.

By contrast to proton-integrating devices, proton-tracking radiography and tomography systems consist of a number of PSD modules to infer proton path (typically between one and four) and a RERD to determine its residual energy. This is illustrated in Figure 2. Note that a detector to measure initial proton energy would also be advantageous, although no suitable detector has yet been proposed as a part of any prototype system. A precise determination of proton energy would be required without substantially perturbing the proton’s path or degrading its energy.

PROTON RADIOGRAPHY RESOLUTION

For a long time, the practical applicability of proton imaging remained limited, mostly because of insufficient spatial resolution (a few mm) due to MCS inside an imaged object: When a proton beam traverses matter, the protons are not only slowed down in interactions with atomic electrons, but they are also deflected from their initial direction in electromagnetic interactions with atomic nuclei. The angular spread of a proton beam leaving an object is the result of many such deflections. If the scattered protons fall on a screen behind the object, their spatial distribution is very nearly Gaussian. This makes proton images blurry unless a more sophisticated approach is chosen. Single particle tracking is one way to compensate for blurring induced by MCS, and current research has become focused on this method. We use an alternative method relying on magnetic optics to compensate for the image blur induced by MCS. This method even employs MCS for enhancing the image contrast. Both single particle approaches and our method produce 2-dimensional images of an object. These can be combined to derive tomographic reconstructions of an object.

Many Monte Carlo simulations have been performed,[4] but no precise criterion has ever been introduced to measure the resolution power of proton radiography. In principle, if multiple scattering is negligible, the object will appear transparent on the screen, and the image will be enlarged by a factor M (Equation 16), which corresponds to geometric magnification. In particular, by defining a general distance between two points of the object, δx, the size of the image captured on the detector becomes equal to = Mδx. However, the effect of MCS can never be ignored, and protons are deflected by an average angle θ and consequently by a certain average displacement ξ=Lθ as they pass through the object.

Finally, based on our calculations presented in this section of the article, the following factors affect image blur in proton radiography:

(a) Multiple Coulomb scattering: Protons, being charged particles, experience scattering as they travel through matter, leading to a spread in their trajectories and a corresponding blurring effect. (b) Chromatic aberration: In proton radiography, magnetic lenses are used to focus the proton beam. Chromatic aberrations, where the lens’s focusing power varies with energy, can further blur the image. (c) Object thickness and material: The thickness and density of the object being imaged directly influence the amount of scattering and, therefore, the blurring. (d) Proton energy: Higher proton energies generally result in less scattering and potentially better image resolution, but also increase the risk of noise and other factors that can affect image quality.

How to quantify and mitigate blur?

(a) Numerical simulations: Simulations can predict the level of blur based on the properties of the object and the beamline setup. (b) Experimental measurement: The blurring coefficient can be determined experimentally by comparing the widths of lines or edges in the proton radiographic image to the expected values based on theoretical models. (c) Magnetic lens tuning: By adjusting the currents in the magnetic lenses, the effects of chromatic aberrations can be minimized, improving image sharpness. (d) Scattering reduction: Techniques to reduce MCS, such as using thinner objects or focusing the proton beam to a smaller spot, can also improve image quality.

CONCLUSION

A proton imaging device can improve the accuracy in proton radiation therapy treatment planning and in the alignment of the patient with the proton beam, since it can provide images with exactly the same geometrical conditions encountered during the treatment. The study presented here was designed to deliver qualitative results to demonstrate the potential for imaging of biological objects. A proton imaging device can improve the accuracy in proton radiation therapy treatment planning and in the alignment of the patient with the proton beam, since it can provide images with exactly the same geometrical conditions encountered during the treatment. The poor spatial resolution, compared to X-ray images, is due to multiple scattering of protons in the patient, which makes reconstruction along straight lines inaccurate. Moreover, uncertainties in stopping power or electron density arise from energy loss straggling due to the momentum spread of the beam entering the patient, and from proton range straggling in the patient. However, proton-by-proton track reconstruction techniques based on the most likely path concept promise to improve the spatial resolution of proton computed radiography and pCT images. In addition, the measurement of the energy of each proton provides the best possible density resolution. The first step for developing a pCT device is to construct a proton computed radiography apparatus, capable of measuring a single projection. The full tomography apparatus requires the rotation of the proton computed radiography device and suitable reconstruction algorithms. Unlike images based on photon detection, proton radiographic images contain information on the range of protons passing through the patient. The energy loss is characterized by the proton stopping powers, which depend on the properties of the traversed materials. Their precise knowledge is essential for radiotherapy treatment planning. Therefore, proton radiographs can be used for imaging purposes and treatment verification.

(i) To determine the appropriate dose without risks, researchers in the domains of nuclear medicine and radiotherapy must examine the parameters of proton interactions with different tissues. (ii) The current investigation is highly important in the proton radiotherapy and radiography of different human tissues. (iii) The cortical bone has the highest Zeff values, while the adipose tissue has the lowest values at all proton energies. (vi) CSDA range of protons was higher in high-density tissue until 200 MeV energy protons. (v) Total stopping power of a proton is directly proportional to the energy of protons. (iv) The blurring coefficient, ξy(γ), increases with decreasing A. In the context of quality assurance in proton therapy, proton radiography and tomography images may be used in the future to improve the range accuracy of proton dose calculations compared to X-ray CT and to verify the correct positioning of the patient.
