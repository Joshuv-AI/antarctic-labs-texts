A positive feedback loop between NPR1 and SOG1 reinforces genotoxic tolerance in Arabidopsis
Authors: Soonyoung Kwon, Hongwoo Lee, Pil Joon Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cellular functions and viability of living organisms are constantly challenged by endogenous and environmental agents that induce DNA damage. In plants, DNA-damaging agents, such as salicylic acid (SA), and the double-strand-break-inducing antibiotic Zeocin, trigger the DNA damage response, thereby promoting genotoxic tolerance. We found that the SA-responsive gene NONEXPRESSOR OF PATHOGENESIS-RELATED GENES 1 (NPR1) and the DNA-damage-responsive gene SUPPRESSOR OF GAMMA RESPONSE 1 (SOG1) are both essential for genotoxic tolerance. Mutants lacking either NPR1 or SOG1 exhibit reduced tolerance to genotoxic stress. Furthermore, NPR1 and SOG1 form a positive feedback loop at the transcriptional level, thereby amplifying genotoxic stress responses. Additionally, NPR1 and SOG1 physically interact and co-occupy the NAC085 promoter, synergistically activating its expression and consequently enhancing genotoxic tolerance. Genetic analysis revealed that NAC085 acts downstream of NPR1 and SOG1 to control genotoxic tolerance. Taken together, our results demonstrate that a positive feedback loop between NPR1 and SOG1 promotes NAC085 expression, thereby enhancing genotoxic tolerance and safeguarding genome integrity in plants.

Introduction

Plants, as sessile organisms, are continuously exposed to a wide variety of external and internal stimuli capable of causing DNA damage (Tuteja et al., 2001; Mahajan and Tuteja, 2005; Krasensky and Jonak, 2012). DNA lesions arise not only from exogenous sources, such as UV radiation and chemical mutagens, but also from endogenous biological processes, including reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and DNA replication errors (Preuss and Britt, 2003; Mittler et al., 2004). DNA damage compromises genome integrity and poses a significant threat to plant survival and productivity (Waterworth et al., 2011, 2015; Spampinato, 2017). To cope with these constant challenges, plants have evolved sophisticated DNA damage response (DDR) pathways that detect DNA lesions and orchestrate diverse protective cellular responses (Raina et al., 2021; Szurman-Zubrzycka et al., 2023). Depending on the extent of DNA damage, cellular processes related to DNA repair, the cell cycle, and programmed cell death (PCD) are systematically coordinated (Gentric et al., 2021; Pedroza-Garcia et al., 2022).

A key component of the plant DDR is the plant-specific NAC (NAM, ATAF1/2, or CUC2) transcription factor SUPPRESSOR OF GAMMA RESPONSE 1 (SOG1), which functions analogously to the mammalian tumor suppressor p53 (Yoshiyama et al., 2014; Yoshiyama, 2016; Ogita et al., 2018). In response to DNA damage, the kinases ATAXIA-TELANGIECTASIA MUTATED (ATM) and ATAXIA-TELANGIECTASIA AND RAD3-RELATED (ATR) phosphorylate and activate SOG1 (Yoshiyama et al., 2013). Activated SOG1 then orchestrates a broad spectrum of downstream responses, including DNA repair, cell-cycle arrest, and PCD, thereby functioning as a critical node for maintaining genome stability (Yoshiyama et al., 2009; Bourbousse et al., 2018).

Given that DNA damage is frequently triggered by environmental stresses, the DDR and stress responses are likely to be closely coordinated, supporting plant adaptation under specific environmental conditions (Nisa et al., 2019). For example, DDR and immune responses are known to be interconnected. The plant immune hormone salicylic acid (SA) can induce DNA damage and activate the DDR (Song et al., 2011). Core DDR pathway components, such as the checkpoint clamp component RADIATION SENSITIVE 17 (RAD17) and the kinase ATR, also contribute to plant immune responses beyond their canonical DDR functions (Yan et al., 2013; Yin et al., 2023). Moreover, SUPPRESSOR OF NPR1-1, INDUCIBLE 1 (SNI1), a key regulator of SA-mediated defense, is part of the structural maintenance of chromosomes 5/6 (SMC5/6) complex, which is involved in the DDR and homologous recombination repair (Watanabe et al., 2009). Consistently, mutations in SNI1 lead to the activation of both DNA damage and immune responses, highlighting the close interplay between immune signaling and DDR pathways (Durrant et al., 2007; Rodriguez et al., 2018).

NONEXPRESSOR OF PATHOGENESIS-RELATED GENES 1 (NPR1) is a central regulator of SA-mediated plant immunity (Cao et al., 1997; Dong, 2004; Tada et al., 2008). NPR1 lacks a canonical DNA-binding domain and therefore functions as a transcriptional co-activator by interacting with various transcription factors, including TGA family proteins, to modulate the expression of diverse defense-related genes, including PATHOGENESIS-RELATED (PR) genes, thereby coordinating multiple defense outputs (Powers et al., 2024; Yun et al., 2024; Zavaliev and Dong, 2024). Accumulating evidence shows that NPR1 forms cytosolic condensates enriched with stress-related proteins. Notably, DDR components are significantly enriched within NPR1-containing condensates, further underscoring the close interconnection between SA and DDR signaling pathways (Zavaliev et al., 2020; Wang and Gu, 2022).

Despite growing insights into the crosstalk between SA and DDR signaling (Jeong et al., 2023), the mechanisms that integrate these pathways to elicit robust tolerance to genotoxic stress remain unclear. In this study, we demonstrate that NPR1 and SOG1 mutually activate each other, forming a positive feedback loop that amplifies cellular responses to genotoxic stress. In addition to mutual transcriptional activation, NPR1 and SOG1 proteins physically interact with each other and co-bind to the promoter of NAC085, also known as ANAC085, synergistically promoting its expression. Given that NAC085 promotes G2/M-phase cell-cycle arrest (Takahashi et al., 2019; Zaragoza et al., 2024), the NPR1–SOG1–NAC085 regulatory axis likely contributes to tolerance to genotoxic stress by providing time for DNA repair, thereby safeguarding genome integrity. These results not only reveal a regulatory link between SA and DDR signaling but also provide insights into strategies for enhancing plant resilience against environmental challenges (Vinocur and Altman, 2005; Lukan and Coll, 2022; Elsisi et al., 2024).

To assess the roles of NPR1 and SOG1 in tolerance to genotoxic stress, we evaluated the responses of wild-type, npr1-1, and sog1-1 seedlings to genotoxic stress. Five-day-old seedlings grown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium were transferred to MS medium supplemented with 0.5 mM SA or 100 μg/ml Zeocin. These relatively stringent treatment conditions were selected to enable robust comparisons of genotoxic tolerance among genotypes (Vanacker et al., 2001; Culligan et al., 2006; Mateo et al., 2006; Furukawa et al., 2010; Rivas-San Vicente and Plasencia, 2011; Yoshiyama et al., 2013; Sjogren et al., 2015; Ogita et al., 2018). The detrimental effects of SA and Zeocin on root cells were more pronounced in npr1-1 and sog1-1 mutants. Under genotoxic conditions, npr1-1 and sog1-1 seedlings exhibited a marked reduction in root meristem size, quantified as the distance from the quiescent center to the transition zone where cell elongation initiates (Chen and Umeda, 2015; Sjogren et al., 2015). Consistent with this, propidium iodide (PI) staining revealed extensive cell death and compromised tissue integrity in mutant roots (Figures 1E–1H and Supplemental Figure 1). Although occasional PI-positive cells were detected in or near the quiescent center, cell death frequently extended into the actively dividing cells of the proximal meristem (Fulcher and Sablowski, 2009; Heyman et al., 2013). SA- or Zeocin-induced cell death in wild-type roots was largely restricted to small, localized regions. In contrast, the widespread intracellular PI accumulation observed in npr1-1 and sog1-1 mutants indicates extensive loss of meristematic cell viability, suggesting an impaired capacity to cope with replication-associated DNA damage (Figures 1E–1H and Supplemental Figure 1). These results indicate that npr1-1 and sog1-1 mutants are more sensitive to genotoxic stress than wild-type plants.

To further support the role of NPR1 and SOG1 in tolerance to genotoxic stress, we conducted a recovery assay following genotoxic treatment (Figure 1I and Supplemental Figure 2). Following removal of SA or Zeocin, wild-type seedlings gradually resumed primary root growth, whereas the npr1-1 and sog1-1 mutants failed to recover root growth (Figure 1J). Root measurements revealed that both mutant genotypes displayed shorter roots than wild-type plants after genotoxic treatment (Figures 1K and 1L). Together, these findings indicate that NPR1 and SOG1 are essential for responses to and tolerance of genotoxic stress.

DNA damage is exacerbated in npr1-1 and sog1-1 mutants

To further assess DDR activation, we monitored levels of phosphorylated histone H2A.X (γH2A.X), a marker of DSB-induced DNA damage signaling (Friesner et al., 2005). Histone proteins were extracted from seedlings following a 4-day treatment with either 0.5 mM SA or 100 μg/ml Zeocin. Western blot analysis, using histone H3 as a loading control, revealed elevated γH2A.X levels in wild-type seedlings following treatment with SA or Zeocin (Figure 2E). The accumulation of γH2A.X was even more pronounced in the npr1-1 and sog1-1 mutants compared with the wild type under both treatment conditions (Figure 2E).

To visualize the DDR at cellular resolution, we additionally performed γH2A.X immunostaining in root-derived protoplasts treated with 10 μM SA or 80 nM Zeocin for 4 h. Because isolated protoplasts were substantially more sensitive to genotoxic stress than intact seedlings, milder treatment conditions were used for these assays (Yoo et al., 2007; Rosa and Scheid, 2014; Bourbousse et al., 2018; Fan et al., 2022). In wild-type protoplasts, both SA and Zeocin treatments induced an increase in the number of discrete nuclear γH2A.X foci compared with mock-treated controls (Supplemental Figure 4). Notably, npr1-1 and sog1-1 protoplasts displayed significantly greater accumulation of γH2A.X foci, with stronger and more widespread nuclear signals than those observed in the wild type (Supplemental Figure 4). These results indicate that NPR1 and SOG1 are crucial for limiting DNA damage and maintaining genome stability under genotoxic stress.

NPR1 and SOG1 form a positive feedback loop under genotoxic stress

Given the shared roles of NPR1 and SOG1 in promoting tolerance to genotoxic stress, we hypothesized that these two key transcriptional regulators engage in a mutual regulatory interaction. Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis revealed that SA- and Zeocin-induced upregulation of NPR1 in wild-type plants was significantly attenuated in the sog1-1 mutant (Figure 3A), suggesting that SOG1 positively regulates NPR1 expression. To test whether SOG1 directly binds to the NPR1 promoter, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by qPCR (ChIP-qPCR) on 35S:SOG1-GFP transgenic plants. This analysis revealed significant enrichment of SOG1-GFP at a CGTG-motif-enriched region within the NPR1 promoter (Figure 3B), with enrichment further enhanced following SA or Zeocin treatment (Supplemental Figures 5A and 5B). Consistent with these results, NPR1 expression was elevated by SOG1, particularly following SA or Zeocin treatment (Figures 3C and 3D). Furthermore, transient expression assays using the NPR1 promoter-driven GUS reporter construct (pNPR1:GUS) showed that, compared with the empty-vector control, GUS activity was significantly increased when the reporter construct was co-expressed with the SOG1 effector construct. This transcriptional activation was further amplified by treatment with SA or Zeocin (Supplemental Figures 6A–6C). To investigate whether NPR1 acts reciprocally to upregulate SOG1 expression, we analyzed the accumulation of the SOG1 transcript in npr1-1 seedlings using RT-qPCR. The results showed that the induction of SOG1 expression by SA or Zeocin was significantly diminished in npr1-1 (Figure 3E). ChIP-qPCR assays using 35S:NPR1-HA transgenic plants showed enrichment of NPR1-HA at the SOG1 promoter (Figure 3F), with SA or Zeocin treatment enhancing this promoter association (Supplemental Figures 5C and 5D). Consistently, SOG1 expression was enhanced by NPR1, especially under SA or Zeocin treatment (Figures 3G and 3H). Transient expression assays using the pSOG1:GUS construct showed that NPR1 activated SOG1 transcription, particularly in response to SA or Zeocin treatment (Supplemental Figures 6A, 6D, and 6E). This NPR1-dependent activation was markedly compromised when the core TGAC motifs within the SOG1 promoter, which are recognized by TGA transcription factors that mediate NPR1-dependent transcriptional regulation (Despres et al., 2000; Kumar et al., 2022; Yun et al., 2024), were mutated (pSOG1m; Supplemental Figure 8), indicating that NPR1 associates with the SOG1 promoter via TGA transcription factors under genotoxic stress conditions.

This positive feedback loop likely reinforces genotoxic stress responses, thereby promoting effective DNA damage repair and genome integrity. To substantiate the biological significance of this mutual regulatory mechanism, we examined the genetic relationship between NPR1 and SOG1 using the sog1-101 npr1-1 double mutant. Consistent with the presence of a functional positive feedback circuit, the double mutant exhibited more severe growth retardation, with significantly shorter roots than either single mutant following SA or Zeocin treatment (Figures 3I and 3J). Taken together, these results indicate that NPR1 and SOG1 mutually activate each other by directly binding to their respective promoter regions. This positive feedback loop likely reinforces genotoxic stress responses, thereby facilitating DNA damage repair and helping maintain genome integrity.

NPR1 and SOG1 function interdependently to control genotoxic tolerance

To verify the genetic interdependence between NPR1 and SOG1, we generated 35S:MYC-SOG1/npr1-1 and 35S:HA-NPR1/sog1-1 transgenic plants. Root growth recovery following SA or Zeocin treatment was assessed in these transgenic lines, alongside the corresponding single mutants, sog1-1 and npr1-1, and overexpression lines, 35S:MYC-SOG1/sog1-1 and 35S:HA-NPR1/npr1-1. We normalized the data by calculating the relative change in root growth after genotoxic stress treatment compared with mock conditions for each genotype. Overexpression of SOG1 enhanced root growth recovery after exposure to genotoxic stress; however, this SOG1-mediated enhancement was abolished in the npr1-1 mutant background (Figures 4A–4C). The 35S:MYC-SOG1/npr1-1 plants displayed a root growth phenotype comparable to that of npr1-1 single-mutant plants (Figures 4A–4C), with corresponding changes in PHOSPHOLIPASE A2-ALPHA (PLA2A) expression, which reflects plant responses to cellular damage (Jung et al., 2012) (Figures 4D and 4E). Similarly, although NPR1 overexpression promoted root growth recovery after SA or Zeocin treatment, this effect was abolished in the sog1-1 mutant background (Figures 4F–4H). The 35S:HA-NPR1/sog1-1 lines phenocopied the sog1-1 mutant and exhibited impaired recovery (Figures 4F–4H), with corresponding changes in PLA2A expression (Figures 4I and 4J). We hypothesized that NPR1 and SOG1 target a common set of genes that confer genotoxic tolerance. To identify direct target genes co-regulated by NPR1 and SOG1 in response to SA and Zeocin, we performed an integrative analysis of publicly available transcriptomic and chromatin-binding datasets. First, we identified genes responsive to both SA and DNA damage by intersecting RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets from SA-treated plants (Ding et al., 2018; Skelly et al., 2019; Yun et al., 2024) and γ-irradiation-treated plants (Bourbousse et al., 2018), which yielded 700 co-responsive genes (Figure 5B). To further refine this list and identify direct co-regulated targets, we intersected SOG1 ChIP-seq (Bourbousse et al., 2018) and NPR1 ChIP-seq datasets (Skelly et al., 2019), resulting in a final set of 17 high-confidence candidate genes regulated by both NPR1 and SOG1 (Figure 5B; Supplemental Table 1).

We then examined root growth recovery after SA or Zeocin treatment in available T-DNA insertion mutants for several of the 17 genes identified above (Supplemental Figure 12). Among these, NAC085 (AT1G02220) emerged as a strong candidate. Similar to npr1-1 and sog1-1 seedlings, nac085 mutant seedlings displayed markedly impaired recovery of primary root growth and significantly elevated DNA damage, as indicated by a higher percentage of DNA in the comet tail, following SA or Zeocin treatment compared with wild-type seedlings (Figures 5C and 5D and Supplemental Figure 13). Consistently, RT-qPCR analysis revealed that NAC085 expression was robustly induced by SA and Zeocin in wild-type plants but was significantly attenuated in both npr1-1 and sog1-1 single mutants (Figure 5E). This induction was most severely compromised in the sog1-101 npr1-1 double mutant (Supplemental Figure 14), further supporting NAC085 as a key downstream target co-regulated by the NPR1–SOG1 module in response to genotoxic stress.

ChIP-qPCR analysis showed that both NPR1 and SOG1 were recruited to overlapping regions of the NAC085 promoter containing their respective cis-regulatory elements (Figures 5F–5H). Furthermore, overexpression of either SOG1 or NPR1 increased NAC085 expression, particularly in response to SA or Zeocin (Figures 5I and 5J). Transient expression assays confirmed that co-expression of the pNAC085:GUS reporter construct with SOG1 or NPR1 effector constructs significantly increased GUS activity (Supplemental Figure 15). SA or Zeocin treatment further enhanced the transcriptional activation of NAC085 (Supplemental Figure 15). This transcriptional activation was motif-dependent, as SOG1-induced activation was abolished when the core CGTG motif was mutated (pNAC085mC) (Supplemental Figure 16), and NPR1-mediated activation was significantly compromised upon mutation of the TGAC motifs (pNAC085m1–m3) (Supplemental Figure 17). These results indicate that NPR1 and SOG1 coordinately associate with the NAC085 promoter and activate its transcription, particularly under genotoxic stress conditions.

Next, to determine the genetic hierarchy between NPR1 or SOG1 and NAC085, we crossed 35S:NPR1 or 35S:SOG1 lines with the nac085 mutant and evaluated root growth recovery after SA or Zeocin treatment. The results showed that the enhanced recovery conferred by NPR1 or SOG1 overexpression was largely diminished in the nac085 mutant (Figures 5K and 5L), demonstrating that NAC085 acts downstream of the NPR1–SOG1 feedback loop to mediate genotoxic stress tolerance.

To determine whether coordinated recruitment of NPR1 and SOG1 to the NAC085 promoter results in synergistic transcriptional activation, we performed transient expression assays using Arabidopsis protoplasts. Effector constructs expressing NPR1 and SOG1 were co-transfected with the pNAC085:GUS reporter. Expression of either effector alone resulted in only a modest increase in GUS activity, whereas co-expression of NPR1 and SOG1 led to robust reporter activation that significantly exceeded the additive effects of the individual proteins under both SA and Zeocin treatments (Supplemental Figure 19). Consistent with this, cotranfection of 35S:SOG1 and 35S:NPR1 constructs led to stronger induction of endogeous NAC085 expression than either single construct transfection (Figure 6G), indicating that NPR1 and SOG1 function synergistically to activate NAC085 transcription in response to genotoxic stress.

Discussion

This study uncovers a sophisticated molecular framework by which plants respond to DNA damage and establish genotoxic tolerance. We demonstrate that SOG1, the master regulator of the DDR (Mahapatra and Roy, 2019; 2020), and NPR1, the central mediator of SA-dependent immunity (Zavaliev and Dong, 2024), form a positive feedback loop through mutual transcriptional activation, especially in response to severe DNA damage. This feedback circuitry not only amplifies the cellular response to genotoxic stress but also provides a mechanism by which plants can sensitively perceive and robustly respond to environmental challenges that threaten genome integrity. This system enables the full activation of defense and repair programs once damage reaches a critical threshold. The functional output of the NPR1–SOG1 feedback circuit is further strengthened by the physical association between the two proteins. We show that NPR1 and SOG1 proteins, whose abundance is amplified by the positive feedback loop, physically interact and co-occupy downstream target gene promoters to synergistically activate their transcription. This synergistic regulation becomes most evident under severe DNA-damage conditions, in which cell-cycle arrest alone is insufficient to ensure survival. To specifically interrogate repair-dependent survival rather than cell-cycle checkpoint activation alone, we intentionally used high concentrations of SA and Zeocin to induce extensive DNA damage, thereby requiring active DNA repair for cellular recovery. These experimental conditions likely mimic severe stress conditions, under which basal repair pathways are overwhelmed, necessitating robust amplification of DDR signaling.

While NPR1 and SOG1 have each been studied extensively in the context of SA signaling and the DDR, respectively, their coordinated role in genotoxic tolerance remains unclear. In this study, we demonstrate that the role of the NPR1–SOG1 complex in genotoxic tolerance is largely dependent on NAC085. NAC085 expression was induced by SA and Zeocin treatment in an SOG1- and NPR1-dependent manner, and the nac085 mutant exhibited increased sensitivity to genotoxic stress and impaired root growth recovery, similar to npr1-1 and sog1-1 mutants. NAC085 has been implicated in DNA damage–induced G2/M cell-cycle arrest (Takahashi et al., 2019; Zaragoza et al., 2024), a process that provides time for repair and influences the decision between cell survival and PCD (Lavrik, 2021; Cui et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2025). Although NAC085 appears to be a crucial mediator of this process, additional target genes of NPR1 and/or SOG1 are also likely to contribute to the genotoxic stress response. For instance, our genomic analyses identified putative regulatory targets associated with DDR pathways and genome integrity (Figure 5B; Supplemental Table 1). Moreover, canonical DDR marker genes involved in homologous recombination, including BRCA1 and RAD51B, were strongly induced in 35S:SOG1 and 35S:NPR1 lines under genotoxic stress, whereas their induction was compromised in the corresponding mutant backgrounds (Supplemental Figure 20), supporting the idea that the NPR1–SOG1 module operates as a broad, synergistic regulatory network essential for maintaining genome integrity. Identifying and characterizing these targets will be an important next step toward defining how this regulatory network governs diverse physiological outcomes.

Our findings also shed light on the broader implications of signaling crosstalk between SA and DDR pathways. Members of the TGA bZIP family are well-established DNA-binding partners of NPR1, several of which were co-expressed with NPR1 and SOG1 (Supplemental Figure 21) and mediate the recruitment of NPR1 to promoters containing TGACG cis-elements during immune signaling (Zhang et al., 1999, 2003; Despres et al., 2000; Fan and Dong, 2002; Kesarwani et al., 2007). In this study, mutations in TGA-associated motifs strongly reduced NPR1-dependent activation of the SOG1 and NAC085 promoters, and interaction analyses further suggested that TGAs facilitate the assembly of an NPR1–SOG1-containing transcriptional complex. These findings suggest that TGA factors function not only as canonical immune regulators but also as integrators linking SA signaling with DDR-associated transcriptional control, although how TGA-mediated regulation is modulated in response to genotoxic agents remains unclear. Because multiple TGA family members exhibit partial redundancy and context-dependent activities, defining the specific TGA factors involved in genotoxic stress responses will be an important objective for future studies. Consistent with this, the NPR1–SOG1 positive feedback loop not only reinforces genotoxic stress responses but also enhances both SA-dependent defense and DDR signaling outputs. This integration enables plants to mount a unified response to a wide range of environmental stresses. Plants have evolved efficient and effective strategies that activate common responses required for defense against diverse but related stress stimuli (Atkinson and Urwin, 2012).

In conclusion, we propose a model in which NPR1 and SOG1 form a multilayered, self-reinforcing regulatory mechanism to confer genotoxic tolerance. As a first layer, NPR1 and SOG1 mutually activate each other’s transcription, thereby establishing a positive feedback loop, especially under stress conditions. As a second layer, NPR1 and SOG1 form a complex that binds to the NAC085 promoter. As a third layer, NPR1 and SOG1 synergistically activate NAC085 transcription. The SOG1–NPR1–NAC085 axis serves as a key module that fine-tunes cellular responses, including DNA repair, cell-cycle arrest, and cell death, thereby helping to maintain plant genome integrity and survival in challenging environments. Considering the potentially extensive posttranslational modifications and regulatory mechanisms that shape NPR1 and SOG1 activity, future studies will likely further expand our understanding of complex plant survival strategies.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana ecotype Columbia-0 (Col-0) was used as the wild-type control for all experiments. The sog1-1 mutant, which carries an ethyl methanesulfonate-induced point mutation (G115R; GGA>AGA) in SOG1 (AT1G25580) (Yoshiyama et al., 2009), was kindly provided by Prof. Masaaki Umeda and Dr. Naoki Takahashi (NAIST, Japan). The T-DNA insertion mutants npr1-1 (SALK_203386C), nac085 (SALK_020947), sog1-101 (GABI_602B10), AT5G60250-KO (SALK_143058), AT2G18193-KO (SALK_094544), and AT5G43450-KO (SALK_107615) were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC, Columbus, OH, USA). The npr1-1, nac085, and sog1-101 mutants carry T-DNA insertions in the 5′ UTR of NPR1 (AT1G64280), an exon of NAC085 (AT5G14490), and the SOG1 gene, respectively. The sog1-101 npr1-1 double mutant was generated in this study by crossing sog1-101 with npr1-1. Homozygous mutant lines were confirmed by PCR-based genotyping and/or sequencing. To generate 35S:MYC-SOG1, 35S:GFP-SOG1, 35S:HA-NPR1, and 35S:NPR1-HA transgenic plants, the corresponding fusion constructs were cloned into pEarleyGate203, pB7FWG2, pEarleyGate201, and pEarleyGate301, respectively, and introduced into Arabidopsis plants by Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. Plants were grown under long-day conditions (16 h light/8 h dark) under white fluorescent light (150 μmol photons m−2 s−1) at 22°C–23°C. For root growth analysis, 5-day-old seedlings were treated for 2 days and then allowed to recover for 5 days. Primary root lengths were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Sample sizes are described in the figure legends.

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from plant materials using TRI reagent (Takara Bio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using Moloney murine leukemia virus reverse transcriptase (Enzynomics) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RT-qPCR assays were performed using TOPreal SYBR Green qPCR High-ROX PreMIX (Enzynomics) and sequence-specific primers (Supplemental Table 2). Transcript levels were normalized to EUKARYOTIC TRANSLATION INITIATION FACTOR 4A1 (eIF4A; At3g13920). The comparative ΔΔCt method was used to calculate relative transcript abundance. The threshold cycle (Ct) for each reaction was automatically determined by the analysis software using default parameters. The specificity of the RT-qPCR assays was confirmed by melt-curve analysis of the amplified products.

ChIP assay

ChIP assays were performed as described previously (Lee et al., 2021). Tissue samples from transgenic plants expressing epitope-tagged proteins were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde. Chromatin was resuspended in nuclei lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 8.0], 10 mM EDTA, 1% [w/v] sodium dodecyl sulfate [SDS], 1 mM PMSF, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail [Roche]) and sonicated using a Bioruptor Pico (Diagenode; 15 cycles of 30 s ON and 30 s OFF) to generate fragments of 500 bp or shorter. The sonicated chromatin complexes were immunoprecipitated with Dynabeads Protein G magnetic beads (Invitrogen, 10003D) and anti-HA antibody (1:5000 dilution, Millipore, 05-904; 1:5000 dilution, Invitrogen, 26813), anti-MYC antibody (1:5000 dilution, Millipore, 05-724), or anti-GFP antibody (1:1000 dilution, Roche, 11814460001). The precipitated DNA, in a total volume of 100 μl, was purified using the DNA elution kit (LaboPass, South Korea, CMA0112), and 2 μl of the precipitated DNA was used as a template for qPCR (Supplemental Table 2). Enrichment values were calculated as the percentage of input DNA.

Transient expression assays

Transient expression assays were conducted using Arabidopsis protoplasts and reporter and effector plasmids. To construct reporter plasmids, the promoter and/or coding sequences of NPR1, SOG1, and NAC085 were separately cloned into a modified pCAMBIA1305 vector containing the minimal 35S promoter sequence and GUS coding sequence. To construct effector plasmids, NPR1 and SOG1 cDNAs were cloned into pEarleyGate203 and pEarleyGate201, respectively, under the control of the cauliflower mosaic virus 35S promoter.

Protoplast isolation was performed according to a previously described method (Jeong et al., 2021), with modifications. Briefly, 10-day-old whole seedlings were soaked in 20 ml of enzyme solution containing 2% (w/v) viscozyme, 1% (w/v) cellulase, 1% (w/v) pectinase, 10 mM MES (pH 5.7), 0.47 M D-mannitol, and 10 mM CaCl2, for 6 h at room temperature. The samples were filtered through a 70-μm nylon mesh (Carolina Biologicals, Burlington, NC, USA) and centrifuged at 100× g for 7 min at room temperature. The reporter and effector plasmids were co-transformed into Arabidopsis protoplasts, together with the 35S::Luciferase (35S::LUC) construct as an internal control, by polyethylene glycol-mediated transformation. GUS activity in the transformed protoplasts was measured after 16 h of incubation in the dark at 23°C, and LUC activity was determined with the Luciferase Assay System Kit (Promega, Madison, WI).

Microscopy

Plant tissues were fixed in 4% (w/v) formaldehyde and then cleared with ClearSee solution (031-25151; Wako Chemicals). Cell walls were stained with 1% Calcofluor White solution (4404-43-7; MP Bio) containing 10 μg/ml PI (P1304MP, Thermo Fisher). Fluorescent protein signals were observed using an ECLIPSE Ti2 confocal inverted microscope (Nikon). Calcofluor White was excited at 405 nm with the laser power set to 0.1, and emission was detected at 429/74 nm. PI was excited at 561 nm, and the emitted fluorescence was detected at 600–650 nm.

Co-IP assay

Protoplasts were isolated and transfected as described above. One milliliter of protoplasts (1 × 107 ml−1) was transfected with 100 μg of plasmid DNA. After incubation, the transfected protoplasts were harvested and lysed in 400 μl of IP buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail). The lysate was incubated with gentle rotation at 12 rpm for 1 h at 4°C to ensure complete lysis. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 12,000× g for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant (400 μl) was carefully transferred to a new tube and incubated with 30 μl of anti-MYC magnetic beads (88842; Invitrogen) for 2 h at 4°C with gentle rotation at 10 rpm. The beads were then collected using a magnetic stand and washed three times with IP buffer. The immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted by boiling the beads in 45 μl of 2× SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) loading buffer for 10 min. The eluted proteins were subjected to SDS-PAGE and analyzed by immunoblotting with anti-HA antibody (1:2000 dilution, Millipore, 05-904; 1:2000 dilution, Invitrogen, 26813), anti-MYC antibody (1:2000 dilution, Millipore, 05-724), anti-GFP antibody (1:2000 dilution, Roche, 11814460001), m-IgGκ BP-HRP (1:5000 dilution, Santa Cruz, sc-516102), anti-mouse IgG for IP (1:5000 dilution, Abcam, ab131368), and VeriBLot for IP (1:5000 dilution, Abcam, ab131366). Co-IP assays were independently repeated using separate biological samples, and representative results are shown.

Immunoblot analysis

Plant samples were harvested, ground in liquid nitrogen, and resuspended in 2× SDS loading buffer. After centrifugation at 15,000× g for 10 min at 4°C, total protein extracts were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Roche). Proteins were detected using the following antibodies: anti-HA antibody (1:5,000 dilution, Millipore, 05-904; 1:5,000 dilution, Invitrogen, 26813), anti-MYC antibody (1:5,000 dilution, Millipore, 05-724), anti-H3 antibody (1:5,000 dilution, Abcam, ab1791), m-IgGκ BP-HRP (1:5,000 dilution, Santa Cruz, sc-516102), and mouse anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (1:5,000 dilution, Santa Cruz, sc-2357). Protein bands from at least three independent blots were quantified using ImageJ software (v1.49; http://imagej.nih.gov), and the mean values were calculated.

Immunostaining for γH2A.X

Wild-type (Col-0) and mutant Arabidopsis protoplasts, isolated from 14-day-old plants, were treated with 0.25 mM SA, 10 μg/ml Zeocin, or mock solution for 4 h after isolation. Protoplasts were fixed with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde in WI solution for 10 min at room temperature. After washing with WI solution, the samples were permeabilized with 0.05% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min and then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in WI solution for 30–60 min at room temperature. The samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-phospho-histone H2A.X (Ser139) primary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 9718; 1:500 dilution in blocking buffer). After washing, the samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Santa Cruz, sc-516176; 1:1,000 dilution) for 1–2 h in the dark at room temperature. Nuclei were optionally counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1 μg/ml). Samples were mounted in antifade medium and imaged using an inverted confocal microscope (ECLIPSE Ti2; Nikon). Samples incubated without primary antibody served as controls. Nuclear γH2A.X foci were quantified from multiple independent cells per genotype and treatment group using ImageJ (Schneider et al., 2012). A nucleus was scored as γH2A.X-positive when it contained any detectable foci above the background signal, based on consistent criteria applied uniformly across all images within each experiment. Sample sizes are indicated in the figure legends.

Comet assay (single-cell gel electrophoresis)

DNA strand breaks were assessed using an alkaline comet assay adapted from established protocols (Tice et al., 2000; Olive and Banath, 2006; Collins et al., 2023). Five-day-old wild-type (Col-0) and mutant Arabidopsis seedlings (approximately 0.1–1 g) were treated with 0.5 mM SA, 100 μg/ml Zeocin, or mock solution for 4 days and then finely chopped in 1 ml of ice-cold 1× PBS (pH 7.4, supplemented with 1 mM EDTA when required) to release nuclei. The slurry was filtered through an 80-μm mesh, and nuclei were pelleted by centrifugation (100× g, 5 min, 4°C), washed with PBS, and resuspended in 50–100 μl of PBS. The nuclear suspension (50 μl) was mixed with 50 μl of 0.8% low-melting point agarose at 37°C and immediately spread on pre-coated slides (1.5% normal-melting point agarose). After solidification at 4°C for 5 min, the slides were immersed in ice-cold lysis solution (2.5 M NaCl, 100 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris–HCl [pH 10.0], with 1% Triton X-100 and 10% DMSO added before use) at 4°C for 1 h with gentle shaking. Slides were then rinsed and incubated in freshly prepared denaturation solution (300 mM NaOH, 1 mM EDTA [pH > 13]) at 4°C for 20 min with slow shaking. Electrophoresis was performed in freshly prepared, ice-cold alkaline electrophoresis buffer (300 mM NaOH, 1 mM EDTA [pH > 13]) at 25 V for 3 min. Slides were then washed, fixed in 100% ethanol, dried, and stained with EvaGreen (Biotium, 31000; 1:2 dilution in TE buffer). Comets were visualized under a fluorescence microscope (excitation, ∼488 nm; emission, ∼525 nm), and DNA damage was quantified as the percentage of DNA in the comet tail using the OpenComet plug-in in ImageJ. At least 50 comets were analyzed per sample.

In vitro pull-down assay

To examine the direct interaction between SOG1 and NPR1, their full-length coding sequences were cloned into the pET-28b vector (Novagen) to generate MYC-tagged SOG1 (MYC-SOG1) and HA-tagged NPR1 (HA-NPR1) constructs, respectively, and the constructs were transformed into Escherichia coli strain Rosetta (DE3). Protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG); cultures expressing MYC-SOG1 were incubated at 37°C for 6 h, whereas those expressing HA-NPR1 were incubated at 18°C for 24 h. Recombinant MYC-SOG1 and HA-NPR1 proteins were purified via the vector-encoded His-tag by fast protein liquid chromatography (FPLC) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the in vitro pull-down assays, purified MYC-SOG1 and HA-NPR1 proteins were mixed in binding buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40, and protease inhibitor cocktail) and incubated at 4°C for 2 h with gentle rotation. The protein complexes were immunoprecipitated using anti-MYC magnetic beads (88842; Invitrogen), washed three times with wash buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, and 0.1% NP-40), and eluted by boiling in SDS sample buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 6.8], 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 1% β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.02% bromophenol blue). Eluted proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE and analyzed by immunoblotting with anti-HA and anti-MYC antibodies.

Bioinformatic analysis

Publicly available sequencing datasets were downloaded from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA; http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/) database. RNA-seq datasets for SA treatments (SRP132955, ERP111843, and CRA016010) and γ-irradiation treatments (SRP136812) were used to identify SA- and γ-irradiation-responsive genes, whereas NPR1 (SRP391092) and SOG1 (SRP136812) ChIP-seq datasets were used to identify their direct target genes (Bourbousse et al., 2018; Ding et al., 2018; Skelly et al., 2019; Yun et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). For RNA-seq analysis, raw reads were aligned to the Arabidopsis TAIR10 reference genome using STAR (v2.7.10a) (Dobin et al., 2013) with the options “--pOverlapNbasesMin 12 --peOverlapMMp 0.1 --twopassMode Basic.” Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 (v1.34.0) (Love et al., 2014), with thresholds of log2(fold change) >1 and p < 0.05. For ChIP-seq analysis, reads were trimmed with Trim Galore (v0.6.7) (Martin, 2011) and aligned to TAIR10 using Bowtie2 (v2.4.5) (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012) with default parameters. MACS2 (v2.2.7.1) (Zhang et al., 2008) was used to call ChIP-seq peaks. Direct NPR1 and SOG1 targets were defined as genes containing a ChIP-seq peak within the 2-kb promoter region or gene body.
