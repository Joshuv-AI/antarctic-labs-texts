Background

HIV continues to be a serious public health problem in many regions of the world, with over 40 million individuals estimated to be living with HIV by 2024.1 Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) continues to be the hub of the global HIV/AIDS pandemic, accounting for more than 67% of all people living with HIV globally. Despite several attempts to meet their HIV prevention needs, men continue to have high rates of HIV infection and HIV-related deaths.2 In South Africa, for example, an estimated 8 million people were living with HIV in 2024, of whom approximately 6.2 million (78%) were receiving antiretroviral therapy.3 Among those living with HIV, about 2.6 million were men compared with 5.2 million women. During the same period, HIV-related deaths were higher among men (27 100) than among women (24 200).3

Daily oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is an HIV prevention treatment that, when taken as prescribed, can reduce and prevent the risk of HIV acquisition by 99%.4 5 PrEP use has progressively increased since it was approved by the World Health Organization (WHO) as an additional HIV prevention technique in 2012.6 7 However, PrEP utilisation has been hampered by several challenges that hinder its widespread adoption, including low knowledge of PrEP and its benefits, side effects, stigma, access, cost and long wait times at many healthcare institutions.8,14

HIV self-testing (HIVST) has been demonstrated to be acceptable among men in SSA as it provides a confidential, convenient, and user-controlled method for individuals to assess their HIV status.15 16 Performing an HIVST requires individuals to collect their own sample, execute an HIV test in a private setting and at their own convenient time, and interpret the results themselves.17 The broad acceptance of HIVST has boosted anticipation for the implementation of this testing strategy to enhance rates of HIV testing in men, as well as enhancing the possibility of frequent testing among men at high risk of HIV infection.18

The WHO currently recommends that individuals using PrEP should test for HIV every 3 months to detect potential breakthrough infections.19 PrEP initiation is generally linked to clinic-based HIV testing services, followed by quarterly testing that accompanies PrEP refills. However, PrEP can be costly for clients due to time away from work and travel costs associated with clinic visits.20 21 Regular HIV testing is required both at the initiation of PrEP and quarterly thereafter.12 22 Implementing HIVST to support PrEP delivery offers several potential benefits, including reducing the number of clinic visits through HIVST, saving costs for both healthcare facilities and individuals using PrEP.

HIVST could enable individuals taking PrEP to test routinely by replacing or supplementing existing testing intervals with healthcare providers, potentially increasing access and adherence to PrEP services.23 Integrating PrEP into routine primary care settings can help reach potential PrEP candidates, promote patient-centred care, destigmatise PrEP and disseminate PrEP knowledge to the broader community.24 Combining PrEP dispensing with HIVST could support men at high risk of HIV infection and help overcome a number of barriers, including stigma, discrimination and accessibility issues. The integration of HIVST with PrEP dispensing represents an innovative approach to overcoming these barriers, providing a streamlined, user-friendly and potentially more acceptable means of accessing HIV prevention and care.

The research evidence regarding men’s integration of HIVST and PrEP among men, globally and SSA in particular, remains limited. The primary objective of this review is to explore the existing literature on the integration of PrEP dispensing with HIVST among heterosexual men in SSA. The secondary objective is to identify gaps in the integration of PrEP dispensing with HIVST in SSA. By mapping the current research, identifying key findings and highlighting gaps, this review will inform future research development and programme implementation in the region.

Research question

What is the current evidence on the integration of PrEP dispensing with HIVST among heterosexual men in SSA?

Methods and analyses

To map evidence on the integration of HIVST with PrEP dispensing among men in SSA, we will conduct a scoping review of peer-reviewed published research and grey literature articles (ie, theses, government documents and reports). Our review will be guided by the Arksey and O’Malley framework and Levac et al,25 26 as well as in line with Joanna Briggs Institute scoping review methodology.27 The review results will be reported using the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analysis: extension for scoping reviews guidelines.28 This protocol has been registered on Open Science Framework (link: https://osf.io/8wujz/).

Our review objectives and eligibility criteria will be developed in line with the PCC (population, concept and context) framework, a recommended guide to construct clear and meaningful objectives and eligibility criteria for a scoping review (table 1: PCC framework for determining the research question).

Identifying the research question

Search strategy

Our search strategy aims to retrieve both published and grey literature articles. After conducting a preliminary search of PubMed (online supplemental appendix 1), text phrases found in the titles and abstracts as well as the index terms used to characterise the articles will be examined. This will guide the creation of a search strategy that is specific to each source of information. We will search the following electronic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature Plus, Sociological Abstracts, ProQuest Dissertations and Theses global. We will search the reference lists of all papers that qualify for inclusion for any potential additional articles. The search strategy will include Medical Subject Headings phrases and Boolean terms (AND, OR). We will employ key search terms like men, PrEP and HIVST. To ensure that articles indexed using African nation-specific names or regional phrases are retrieved, African country names and truncated terms like “west Africa” will also be used. The studies that will be considered will provide evidence on the integration of PrEP and HIVST, focusing on men, from 2012 to 2025. Because studies conducted before 2012 are unlikely to reflect important details and information relating to the integration of PrEP and HIVST model in SSA, mostly because of its recent adoption, our search will encompass all studies published from January 2012 to December 2025. The database search will be conducted by one of the co-authors (between October and December 2025), which will include screening titles of retrieved articles. Following the title screening, Rayyan will be used to facilitate the screening process, and duplicates will be subsequently removed.29

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Our review will include articles presenting evidence on the integration of HIVST with PrEP dispensing, in samples consisting of only men, or of both men and women with sex-stratified analyses presented, conducted in SSA, and published between January 2012 and 2025, in English language.

We will exclude studies that are solely focused on men who have sex with men, due to the potentially confounding influences that result from the unique challenges they (and other members of Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS-defined “key population” groups - sex workers, transgender people, people who inject drugs and people who are incarcerated) face in accessing HIV care and related services.

Studies that meet the inclusion criteria will be retrieved in preparation for the full-text article screening phase. The results of the screening, from each screening phase, will be reported in full in the final report and presented in a preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram (figure 1: PRISMA 2020 flow diagram for systematic reviews). Any disagreements arising between the two independent reviewers will be resolved through discussion or with a third reviewer.

Quality appraisal

To assess the methodological quality of the included articles, the mixed methods appraisal tool (MMAT) version 2018 will be used.30 The quality appraisal process will be carried out by two independent reviewers, a principal investigator and a co-screener. Each article will undergo a quality assessment to examine its strengths, weaknesses, potential bias and level of scientific evidence. The MMAT tool will be used to assess the article’s overall quality as well as the applicability of its study aim(s), methodology, study design, data collection, data analysis, presentation of findings and authors’ discussions and conclusions. This quality assessment will allow us to evaluate a wide range of study methodologies, including mixed methods studies, quantitative randomised controlled trials, quantitative non-randomised trials, quantitative descriptive studies and qualitative studies. The following percentages are used to grade the quality of the evidence: (a) ≤50% indicates low quality, (b) 51–75 per cent indicates average quality and (c) 76–100 per cent indicates good quality evidence. If a study receives a low methodological quality assessment rating, it will still be included in the review; however, its limitations will be acknowledged.

Outcomes

In this review, we aim to synthesise the key concepts and outcomes reported in the included studies. Specifically, we sought to identify the types of interventions or phenomena studied, the outcomes assessed, and the contextual, behavioural, psychosocial or implementation-related factors described in the included studies.

Data extraction

The included studies will be analysed to obtain the following characteristics: (a) Study aims, (b) Study population, (c) Gender, (d) Study setting, (e) Study design, (f) Intervention and its results, (g) Key findings, (h) Study limits and implications and (i) The authors’ interpretations and conclusions.

Data synthesis

We will present a narrative account of the findings from the eligible studies, presenting the main concepts from the included articles in line with the research question. We will use NVivo software (V.11, QSR International, Melbourne, Australia) to synthesise data from the included studies using a thematic approach. Three main steps will be taken in this process: (a) Line-by-line coding of the findings from the included studies, (b) Grouping these free codes into comparable regions to create descriptive themes and (c) Creating analytical themes. After re-examining the themes, we will seek any gaps that could suggest a need for future research. We will further analyse and report on the implications of the review findings for future research, policy and practices.

Patient and public involvement

It was not appropriate or possible to involve patients or the public in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Ethics and dissemination

This review does not require ethical approval because no humans nor animals will be involved. The information that will be presented in this evaluation is already publicly accessible. The findings of this review will be published in a peer-reviewed journal, presented at local and international conferences, and shared with stakeholders involved in HIV prevention and treatment in SSA, such as policymakers, healthcare professionals and community-based groups.
