Meta-analysis of surrogate endpoints for overall survival in extensive-stage small-cell lung cancer
Authors: S Durer, P Fu, Z Chen, A Dowlati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOverall response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), and progression-free survival (PFS) are traditionally used as surrogates for overall survival (OS). However, their validity as reliable predictors of OS in small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) remains unclear.Materials and methodsMEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane, and ClinicalTrials.gov were systematically searched between 2014 and 2024. Study selection followed PRISMA guidelines. Data on ORR, DCR, PFS, and OS were manually extracted from phase III randomized trials. Weighted linear regression and Pearson correlation analyses, with logarithmic transformation of odds ratios and hazard ratios, were used to assess associations between endpoints. Correlation coefficients were computed to evaluate the correlation between surrogate endpoints and OS. All tests were two-sided, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.ResultsThe analysis comprised 23 randomized phase III trials, including 10 340 patients. In first-line studies, a strong correlation was observed between PFS and OS (r = 0.77, P < 0.001). In first-line immunotherapy studies, PFS also correlated significantly with OS (r = 0.80, P = 0.006). In contrast, ORR and DCR did not correlate with OS in either first- or second-line settings. In the second-line setting, DCR correlated with PFS (r = −0.84, P = 0.01) but not with OS. These findings were consistent across both the difference model and the odds ratio model.ConclusionsPFS demonstrates strong clinical value as a surrogate endpoint for OS in the first-line SCLC setting, including with chemoimmunotherapy, and may serve as a reliable endpoint for future phase III trials. In contrast, ORR and DCR do not reliably predict long-term outcomes, underscoring the need to prioritize PFS over these measures in trial design and interpretation.Key words: SCLC, surrogate endpoints, clinical trials, survival

Introduction

Small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) is one of the most aggressive forms of lung cancer, characterized by rapid growth and early metastasis.1 Approximately 70%-80% of patients with SCLC have extensive-stage (ES)-SCLC at diagnosis.2 While high response rates are achieved with initial chemotherapy, these responses are often not durable, and significant therapeutic resistance typically emerges upon relapse.3,4 The complexity and heterogeneity of SCLC present significant challenges in developing and validating effective therapeutic strategies.5

Overall survival (OS) is the standard primary endpoint to assess efficacy in randomized trials for SCLC.6 However, obtaining OS data can be delayed due to the longer-term follow-up required. In addition, short-term outcomes [such as response rates, disease control rates (DCRs), and progression-free survival (PFS)] from phase II trials and expansion cohorts of large phase I trials are typically used to determine the worth and risk of proceeding to phase III trials.7, 8, 9 Understanding the relationship between commonly used short-term endpoints, such as overall response rate (ORR), DCR, PFS, and OS, can facilitate earlier interpretation of study results and help investigators make the go/no-go decision toward a large, randomized trial.

Previous studies in solid tumors, including SCLC and non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), have demonstrated the inconsistency of surrogate endpoints as reliable predictors of OS.10,11 In some cases, improvements in ORR or PFS do not lead to improvements in OS. For instance, the RESILIENT trial reported a doubling of ORR with PEGylated liposomal irinotecan in relapsed SCLC compared with the control group; however, this improvement did not translate into better PFS or OS outcomes.12 Conversely, certain immunotherapy trials have demonstrated OS benefits despite similar ORR or PFS outcomes between treatment and control groups.13, 14, 15 This discrepancy warrants further evaluation to optimize resource allocation and ensure accurate prioritization or de-prioritization of emerging therapies based on preliminary data.

This analysis assessed the trial-level correlation of ORR, DCR, and PFS with OS in ES-SCLC. We also carried out a subgroup analysis of first-line immunotherapy trials, as a disconnect between surrogate endpoints and OS may occur with immunotherapies.16

Materials and methods

Data sources

MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane, and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched to identify randomized phase III clinical trials in ES-SCLC in first- and second-line settings between 1 January 2014 and 1 February 2024. This analysis complied with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement. Search terms encompassed disease-related keywords (“small cell lung cancer,” “SCLC,” “small cell lung carcinoma”), trial design (“phase III,” “randomized controlled trial”), and treatment approaches [chemotherapy, immunotherapy, checkpoint inhibitor, programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1, cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein-4, monoclonal antibody, antibody–drug conjugate (ADC), targeted therapy, biologic]. Outcome terms included ORR, DCR, PFS, OS, and surrogate endpoint. Additionally, we manually reviewed abstracts, posters, and presentations from major oncology conferences, including the American Society of Clinical Oncology, European Society for Medical Oncology, and International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer, including the World Conference on Lung Cancer. Conference abstracts and poster presentations were evaluated to capture updated OS results, which were included in this analysis when full peer-reviewed reports were not yet available. Reference lists from relevant systematic reviews and meta-analyses were also screened to identify any further eligible studies. A complete list of the search strategy is present in Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105843.

Selection criteria and data extraction

SD and AD screened the randomized control trials independently and extracted the following data: author, trial phase, sample size, treatment arms, line of therapy, and outcomes (ORR, DCR, PFS, and OS). Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Inclusion was restricted to registered, randomized phase III clinical trials reporting OS results, along with either ORR, DCR, PFS, or all. Studies were required to report the National Clinical Trial number. Protocols and ongoing studies without results and trials primarily focused on radiotherapy were excluded. Additionally, trials predominantly involving limited-stage SCLC were excluded.

Data analysis

Data on ORR, DCR, PFS, and OS were manually extracted from the randomized phase III trials that met the inclusion criteria. Weighted linear regression models investigated the association between treatment effects on ORR, DCR, OS, and PFS. Weighted Pearson correlation analyses were carried out under the logarithmic transformation of ORR and DCR odds ratios (ORs), with weights equal to the inverse of the variance of OR [back-solved from reported 95% confidence intervals (CIs)] associated with the log PFS hazard ratios (HRs) (for the ORR/PFS analyses) and the log OS HRs (for the ORR/OS and PFS/OS analyses). The weighted linear regression (with weights equal to the inverse of variance of OR) takes the sample size of each study into consideration. As the precision (or inverse of variance) of estimate (e.g. OR) when the sample size increases, we put more weights for bigger studies. We used the logarithmic transformation on outcome measures (ORs and HRs) for variance stabilization, so the dependent variables of our regression models are approximately normally distributed. Logistic regression models were used to derive ORR, DCR ORs when not directly reported in the source reference. HRs estimated from Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to express the treatment effects on PFS and OS. HRs were estimated based on reported median PFS and median OS under the assumption of exponential distribution when HRs were not reported in the references. All tests were two-sided; a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. An additional analysis was conducted on first-line immunotherapy trials. To address heterogeneity among the studies, we conducted a stratified analysis by experimental treatment type, grouping studies into three categories: chemotherapy, chemoimmunotherapy, and ‘other’ (the latter combining less common modalities with limited sample sizes). Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the τ2 and the I2. We used a weighted linear regression approach to address the heterogeneity issue.

Results

The study selection process followed the PRISMA guidelines. An initial search identified 2257 publications for screening. After removing duplicates, 840 unique records remained and were subjected to title and abstract screening. Following this, 115 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Ultimately, 25 eligible studies from 23 trials, comprising a total of 10 340 patients, were included in this study. Surrogate endpoints in the first-line setting

As SCLC is a disease characterized by high initial response rates, go/no-go decisions are often made based on ORR. The weighted Pearson correlation coefficient between the ORR difference and the log PFS HR was −0.40 (P = 0.126) (Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105843). Applying the log OR model, the weighted Pearson correlation was −0.48, with a corresponding P value of 0.062, suggesting a potential trend toward correlation with PFS in this model (Supplementary Figure S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105843).

The weighted Pearson correlation coefficient between the ORR difference and the log OS HR was −0.32 (P = 0.23), and the log ORR OR and the log OS HR was −0.37 (P = 0.157), indicating no correlation with OS (Supplementary Figures S5 and S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105843). Therefore, ORR does not correlate with PFS or OS in the first-line setting, although a non-statistically significant trend toward correlation with PFS was seen.

Analysis of the treatment effects between DCR and PFS showed no correlation. The weighted Pearson correlation coefficient between the DCR difference and the log PFS HR was −0.11 (P = 0.686), and between the log DCR OR and the log PFS HR was −0.24 (P = 0.366) (Supplementary Figures S7 and S8, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105843).

Similarly, no significant correlation was observed between the DCR difference and the log OS HR (r = 0.06, P = 0.81) or between the log DCR OR and the log OS HR (r = −0.01, P = 0.97) (Supplementary Figures S9 and S10, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105843). Therefore, DCR in the first-line setting does not correlate with PFS or OS.

On the other hand, a strong correlation was identified between PFS and OS, with a weighted Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.77 (P = 0.0005) between the log PFS HR and the log OS HR (Figure 1).

Subgroup analysis of first-line checkpoint inhibitor studies showed no correlations between ORR and either PFS or OS. The weighted correlation coefficients for the ORR difference and log PFS HR were −0.55 (P = 0.098), and for the log ORR OR and log PFS HR, −0.58 (P = 0.077) (Supplementary Figures S11 and S12, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105843). For OS, the correlation coefficients were −0.34 (P = 0.342) for the ORR difference and −0.34 (P = 0.333) for the log ORR OR (Supplementary Figures S13 and S14, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105843). Similarly, DCR showed no significant correlations with either PFS or OS. The weighted correlation coefficients between the DCR difference and the log PFS HR were −0.3 (P = 0.427) and −0.35 (P = 0.349) for the log DCR OR and log PFS HR, respectively (Supplementary Figures S15 and S16, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105843). For OS, the correlation coefficients were 0.14 (P = 0.728) for the DCR difference and 0.16 (P = 0.688) for the log DCR OR (Supplementary Figures S17 and S18, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105843).

Consistent with the findings from analyses not limited to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in the first-line setting, this additional analysis also demonstrated a statistically significant correlation between PFS and OS, with a weighted Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.8 (P = 0.006) between the log PFS HR and the log OS HR (Figure 2). Sensitivity analyses by treatment class (Supplementary Tables S2-S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105843) further supported this relationship, showing a strong positive correlation between PFS and OS in chemoimmunotherapy (r = 0.85, P = 0.004) and in other biologics (r = 0.82, P = 0.044). In contrast, ORR did not correlate significantly with either PFS or OS in chemotherapy or chemoimmunotherapy subgroups, and no statistically significant associations were observed between DCR and either PFS or OS across any treatment group.

Scatter plot of the relationship of the OS hazard ratio with PFS hazard ratio in first-line immunotherapy trials. OS, overall survival; PFS, progression-free survival.

The first-line immunotherapy subgroup included only 10 studies; nevertheless, the correlation with PFS and OS was strong (r = 0.80, P = 0.006). Stratification by regimen demonstrated that this correlation was significant in chemoimmunotherapy but not in chemotherapy-only trials, explaining why the first-line subgroup results remain consistent with the overall analysis despite the smaller sample size.

Surrogate endpoints in the second-line setting

ORR did not show a correlation with either PFS or OS. The weighted Pearson correlation coefficients for ORR and PFS were −0.5 (P = 0.2) for the ORR difference and −0.57 (P = 0.14) for the log ORR OR (Supplementary Figures S19 and S20, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105843). Similarly, the correlations with OS were −0.2 (P = 0.63) for the ORR difference and −0.25 (P = 0.55) for the log ORR OR (Supplementary Figures S21 and S22, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105843). Therefore, in the second-line setting, the response rate does not predict PFS or OS.

Notably, there was a strong correlation between DCR and PFS, with the correlation coefficient between the DCR difference and the log PFS HR being −0.84 (P = 0.01) (Figure 3). Similarly, the correlation between the log DCR OR and the log PFS HR was also −0.84 (P = 0.01) (Supplementary Figure S23, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105843). While the correlation was observed between DCR and PFS, it did not translate into a statistically significant correlation with OS. The weighted Pearson correlation coefficients were 0.61 (P = 0.11) for the DCR difference and the log OS HR and 0.6 (P = 0.119) for the log DCR OR and the log OS HR (Supplementary Figures S24 and S25, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105843). Unlike in the first-line setting, no correlation was found between PFS and OS, with a weighted Pearson correlation coefficient of −0.14 (P = 0.73) (Supplementary Figure S26, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105843).

Discussion

This study examined the endpoints and outcomes of randomized phase III trials of ES-SCLC conducted between 2014 and 2024. As the number of new drugs in development for SCLC has substantially increased, it is critical that early phase trials, such as phase II or expansion cohorts of phase I trials, are interpreted correctly. Overinterpreting response rates, DCR, and PFS may lead to large phase III studies that ultimately fail. Analyzing phase III studies and correlating primary and secondary outcomes may help identify shorter-term surrogate endpoints.40 To our knowledge, we report for the first time such an analysis of modern clinical trials in ES-SCLC. We specifically decided not to analyze older studies as most studies before 2014 were negative trials, and thus, correlations could not be made.

The value of PFS as a surrogate endpoint has been established in many cancers.41,42 As the number of effective drugs (tarlatamab, lurbinectedin, potential ADCs) in SCLC increases, the OS for SCLC will likely increase, and the gap between PFS and OS will increase. Such a phenomenon has already been seen in limited-stage SCLC in the recently reported ADRIATIC trial, where the PFS was 16.6 months with an OS of 55.9 months in the experimental arm.43

Our findings revealed a strong correlation between PFS and OS in the first-line setting, confirming PFS as a potential surrogate endpoint. The magnitude of the correlation between PFS and OS was similar when comparing all first-line studies to immunotherapy trials, with correlation coefficients of 0.77 and 0.8, respectively. In contrast, DCR and ORR showed no correlations with OS. This may be partly due to the high ORR and DCR seen in the first-line setting. Sensitivity analyses further suggested that the observed PFS and OS correlation was largely driven by studies of chemoimmunotherapy and, to a lesser extent, other biologics, whereas ORR and DCR showed no consistent associations with PFS or OS across treatment subgroups.

In the second-line setting, DCR was strongly correlated with PFS that has limited clinical applicability, as DCR did not correlate with OS. Improvements in disease control that do not translate into OS are unlikely to reflect meaningful clinical benefit. Moreover, these results should be interpreted with caution given the limited number of studies in the second-line setting, which may reduce statistical power. In contrast, there was no correlation between ORR and OS or between PFS and OS. The lack of correlation is likely multifactorial, including treatment heterogeneity, modest OS gains, and limited sample size. When we stratified studies by treatment type (chemotherapy, chemoimmunotherapy, or other therapies such as ADCs and monoclonal antibodies), a correlation became more apparent among other biologics. Notably, the chemotherapy subgroup did not show a significant correlation between PFS and OS in the sensitivity analysis, and three of the seven second-line studies evaluated chemotherapy. This suggests that variability in treatment mechanisms may have masked the relationship in the pooled analysis.

The lack of correlations with survival should be examined by therapeutic class. ORR responses in chemotherapy without corresponding improvements in survival outcomes may be attributable to factors such as toxic side-effects offsetting survival benefits, aggressive tumor rebound44 from treatment-induced selective pressures, and the evolution of resistance.45,46

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 63 ICI trials, including over 30 000 patients across multiple tumor types, demonstrated that ORR is a poor surrogate for OS, highlighting that radiographic tumor shrinkage does not reliably capture the long-term survival benefit of immunotherapy. Possible reasons for observing OS benefit without ORR improvement in immunotherapy trials could be distinct biological mechanisms associated with ICIs that can lead to delayed responses, pseudoprogression caused by T-cell infiltration, and atypical response patterns that differ significantly from those seen with cytotoxic treatments. Durable response patterns to immunotherapy often emerge later in the disease course and are not reliably captured using standard RECIST criteria. In the CASPIAN trial, ORR and median duration of response were similar between the treatment groups. However, the durvalumab arm showed a higher proportion of patients with ongoing responses, suggesting that a subset of patients, including those with stable disease, experienced prolonged tumor control, which contributed to improved OS. There remains a need for new validation efforts (iRECIST, liquid biopsy technologies such as longitudinal circulating tumor DNA, radiomics) to provide earlier information on the burden of disease before scan. Other mechanistic hypotheses include prevention of tumor seeding through micrometastasis eradication,47 metabolic alterations induced by immunotherapy,48 and delayed therapeutic effects due to the time required for immune system education. A trial-level analysis published in 2014, evaluating 66 SCLC trials conducted between 1983 and 2010, reported a statistically significant correlation between response rates and both PFS and OS in ES-SCLC.49 However, despite a positive trend, no statistically significant correlation was found between PFS and OS. This analysis differs in methodology, as it utilized unweighted Pearson correlation and included phase II and phase III studies across first-line, second-line, and maintenance treatment settings. The differences may also be attributable to immunotherapy not being widely used in SCLC treatment before 2010, when chemotherapy was the standard of care. In the current analysis, most first-line trials involved immunotherapy combinations. The distinct mechanisms of action of immunotherapy, including potential for delayed responses, pseudoprogression, and durable benefit in a subset of patients, may alter the relationship between response rates and OS compared with cytotoxic chemotherapy alone. Thus, differences in treatment era, in addition to methodological distinctions (unweighted Pearson correlation in the 2014 analysis versus trial-level weighted correlation in the current study), likely contribute to the discrepancies observed between the two analyses.

A meta-analysis of 77 phase III immunotherapy trials demonstrated weak correlations between ORR, DCR, PFS, and OS.50 This study included 15 different tumor types across various lines of therapy. Further subgroup analyses were carried out by tumor type, line of treatment, and drug target, with weak correlations persisting across all groups. Notably, this analysis grouped NSCLC and SCLC under lung cancer. Furthermore, the study did not separately evaluate the outcomes of first-line immunotherapy studies within this group.

A recent study conducted a pooled analysis of first-line immunotherapy trials in metastatic NSCLC and demonstrated a moderate correlation between ORR and OS (R2 = 0.61).40 Similarly, a moderate correlation between PFS and OS was observed (R2 = 0.7), consistent with our findings in the first-line immunotherapy setting. In our analysis, the correlation with ORR is noticeably weaker, suggesting that the predictive value of ORR may vary across tumor types, irrespective of the therapeutic mechanism or treatment line.

A pooled patient-level data analysis, including 263 patients from the Southwest Oncology Group (SWOG), assessed DCR as a potential surrogate endpoint for OS in patients with relapsed/refractory ES-SCLC receiving investigational therapies in phase II trials.51 The study demonstrated that DCR at 8 weeks was a stronger predictor of subsequent OS than traditional tumor response rates. Multivariable analysis further confirmed DCR as an independent predictor of OS (HR = 0.50, P < 0.0001). Although our study focuses exclusively on phase III trials with a significantly larger patient population receiving chemoimmunotherapy regimens, we propose that DCR may be a valuable endpoint for consideration in future studies of relapsed ES-SCLC.

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, its retrospective nature and the heterogeneity of therapies used in the trials present challenges. Additionally, we did not have access to individual patient data, which limited our ability to examine patient-level correlations and to adjust for confounding factors such as Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status or presence of brain metastases. Access to such data could provide more reliable support for evaluating surrogate endpoints in first-line immunotherapy for SCLC. Potential inconsistencies in ORR/DCR definitions across trials may introduce heterogeneity and affect the observed correlations. There is a small number of second-line trials that potentially limits the statistical power of the correlation analyses in this subgroup.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated no correlation between ORR and OS in both the first- and second-line settings, highlighting the limitations of ORR as a surrogate endpoint and the need for careful consideration in trial design and interpretation. Our findings support the use of PFS as a reliable surrogate for OS in the first-line setting of ES-SCLC, including immunotherapy-based regimens. Additionally, a strong correlation between DCR and PFS was observed in the second-line setting, providing valuable insights for future trial design and interpretation. Further studies, particularly those utilizing patient-level data, are warranted to validate these results and optimize the selection of surrogate endpoints in SCLC clinical trials.
