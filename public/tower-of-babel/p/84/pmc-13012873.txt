Protocol for dissecting and excising the brain of the Manduca sexta (Lepidoptera: Sphingidae) moth
Authors: Sarah Frankenberg, Nidhi Byragoni, Angela R Dixon, Mario Muscedere
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Carolina Sphinx moth, Manduca sexta (L.) is widely used in neurobiological research, particularly those investigating the olfactory system. Due in part to similarities in brain structure and neural processing with mammals, M. sexta is regarded as a valuable model organism in neuroscience. However, despite its increasing relevance in research, detailed protocols for brain dissection in this species remain limited. This protocol provides step-by-step guidance for opening the head capsule of M. sexta and excising the brain. It visually guides the reader using annotated diagrams highlighting key anatomical landmarks, along with microscope images illustrating each major dissection step. Troubleshooting tips and suggested strategies for addressing dissection complications are included to help readers improve their technique. In addition, the protocol emphasizes precise removal of nonneural tissues, including trachea, which is particularly important for downstream applications that require high-quality, brain-specific nucleic acids and proteins. Although this protocol was specifically designed for M. sexta, it may also serve as a useful guide for brain dissections in closely related species.Keywords: insect neuroanatomy, invertebrate brain isolation, model insect nervous system, invertebrate neuroscience, insect biology

Introduction

Insects have long served as valuable model organisms in neurobiology research due to their small size, abundance, accessibility, and relatively simple anatomical organization (Daly and Smith 2000, Dacks et al. 2008, 2013, Heinbockel et al. 2013, Delahunt et al. 2018). These characteristics often make them more practical study systems than vertebrates. Despite their relatively simple anatomy, insects exhibit sophisticated cognitive functions, particularly in learning and memory, making their neuroanatomy a focal point for neurobiology research (Hildebrand and Shepherd 1997, Davis 2005, Raliya et al. 2017).

Among insect models, Manduca sexta (L.), a member of the Sphingidae family and commonly known as the Carolina Sphinx moth, has become a prominent subject in sensory neurobiology, particularly in the study of vision, gustation, and olfaction (Hildebrand and Shepherd 1997, Wang et al. 2003, Dacks et al. 2005, 2008, Riffell 2011). Modern M. sexta neurobiology has been greatly shaped by the foundational work of researchers such as John G. Hildebrand, whose pioneering studies on olfactory processing established the species as a key model organism (Hildebrand and Shepherd 1997). Alongside Hildebrand, Nicholas J. Strausfeld also made significant contributions through his investigations of insect brain architecture and visual processing, further cementing M. sexta’s role in comparative neuroanatomy (Strausfeld 1988).

Although M. sexta is now well-established as a neurobiological model, its foundational anatomical framework was built by earlier classical studies. Early contributions by entomologists such as John. L. Eaton introduced systematic approaches for nervous system excision and preparation, forming the basis for contemporary dissection methodologies for M. sexta and other lepidoptera. Eaton’s comprehensive monograph on lepidopteran anatomy (Eaton 1988) includes detailed illustrations of both the fully excised nervous system and the isolated brain, some of which were originally published in his earlier anatomical studies (eg Eaton and Dickens 1974) (Supplementary Fig. S1). Similarly, studies by Kammer and colleagues in the 1970s and 1980s used isolated nervous system preparations to investigate motor pattern generation and neural control of flight behavior in M. sexta (Kammer 1971, Kammer and Kinnamon 1979). Together, these classical anatomical descriptions serve as essential guides for recognizing key neuropil boundaries, ganglionic connections, and tissue relationships within the moth head capsule (Kammer 1971, Eaton and Dickens 1974, Kammer and Kinnamon 1979, Eaton 1988). This foundational literature informs the present protocol by enabling consistent identification of major brain regions and their precise separation from adjacent thoracic or sensory structures while minimizing damage.

Building on both the classical and modern foundations, subsequent research has used M. sexta extensively to investigate olfactory mechanisms, including odor detection and processing, behavioral responses to diverse odor stimuli, and olfactory neural plasticity (Riffell 2011, Heinbockel et al. 2013). Additional studies have demonstrated the M. sexta’s capacity for associative olfactory learning through exposure to visual and tactile stimuli (Balkenius et al. 2009, Sponberg et al. 2015).

To examine the neural connectivity of the M. sexta olfactory system and the overall structure of the moth brain, researchers have employed a range of imaging techniques, including optical microscopy, magnetic resonance imaging, scanning electron microscopy, and transmission electron microscopy (Homberg et al. 1988, Sun et al. 1993, Michaelis et al. 2005). The brain can be removed from the head capsule and sectioned using various histological techniques, including paraffin embedding (Wang et al. 2003), resin embedding (Wang et al. 2003), or agarose embedding (Dacks et al. 2013), to investigate specific connections involving the antennae and olfactory pathway. These methods have revealed that the antennal lobe, the primary olfactory processing center and termination site of the olfactory nerve, shares structural similarities with the vertebrate olfactory bulb (Dacks et al. 2013), highlighting M. sexta as a valuable model for studying olfactory pathways with potential relevance to vertebrate systems. Many of the neural structures and associated openings investigated using these methods, including olfactory centers such as the antennal lobes and nonolfactory structures like the optic lobes, ventral nerve cord, and esophageal foramen, are readily visible and distinguishable during dissection (Fig. 1). Additionally, due to their close proximity to the antennal lobes and mushroom bodies, preserving the optic lobes during dissection is essential to avoid compromising adjacent neural structures (Homberg et al. 1988).

Antennal lobes (AL), optic lobes (OL), esophageal foramen (EF), suboesophageal ganglion (SEG), and ventral nerve cord (VNC) of the Manduca sexta brain shown in (a) a schematic and isolated microscope images depicting (b) a posterior view and (c) an anterior view.

Despite increasing interest in M. sexta as a model system, protocols for moth brain dissection remain relatively scarce compared to other extensively documented model insects, such as Drosophila (Tito et al. 2016, Toh et al. 2021). For researchers new to insect neuroanatomy, the moth brain can be delicate and difficult to isolate. This protocol provides a clear, accessible method for resecting the adult M. sexta brain from the head capsule, with potential adaptability for use in other large moth species.

Experimental Design and Materials

M. sexta larvae and adult moths were maintained on a 16:8 h light:dark cycle. Breeding adults were housed together and provided with a 75% sucrose solution as a food source until females deposited eggs on tobacco leaves. Eggs were collected and transferred to lidded containers with premade diet cubes specifically formulated for M. sexta. Upon hatching, larvae were reared individually in lidded cups containing diet cubes until they reached the prepupal stage. Each larva was then placed in an individual cylindrical cell within a wooden block until it reached the pharate adult stage. Pharate adults were placed in paper bags with the tops folded down and stored in a cool, dry environment until eclosion. Adults were anesthetized in a refrigerator 4 d posteclosion.

Dissection Hook Assembly

To facilitate removal of the trachea from the Manduca brain, a custom tracheal hook tool was constructed from these materials: the wooden shaft of a cotton swab (or another long, thin wooden dowel), an insect pin (or minuten pin), strong adhesive (e.g., cyanoacrylate), pliers, and wire cutters. The wire cutters were used to carefully remove the cotton tip from the swab. The pointed end of the pin was pressed into a piece of styrofoam to prevent puncture risk, and the pin head was clipped off with the wire cutters (minuten pins are typically headless). The head was then disposed of in a proper sharps container. Gripping the body of the insect pin with pliers, a small amount of strong glue was applied to the blunt end which was subsequently inserted into the wooden shaft of the cotton swab. After the glue completely dried and the pin was securely attached to the wooden stick, the pliers were used to gently bend the pointed end of the pin to form a small hook. Be careful not to apply excessive force, as this may dislodge or break the pin. The bend in the hook was assessed under a dissecting microscope to ensure that the angle was suitable for gently sliding under the tracheal tubes for removal. Refer to Fig. 3 for an example of the finished tool.

Assembly of the custom tracheal removal tool, or dissection hook, requires a wooden stick from cotton swab (or other thin wooden dowel), insect pin, superglue (or other strong adhesive glue), and some pliers/wire cutters. Once assembled, make a slight bend in the tip of the insect pin to facilitate trachea removal around the brain.

Protocol

1. Specimen Preparation and Sedation

Timing: Up to 24 h (slow chilling), 10 to 15 min (rapid chilling)

1.1. Place adult M. sexta moths in a plastic cup (∼8 fl. oz) containing a folded, damp Kimwipe to maintain humidity (Fig. 4a). Cover the cup with a lid that has small holes or openings to allow ventilation (variations in the size and number of openings can alter the efficacy of sedation).

Preparation of Manduca sexta for dissection; a) The moth was placed in a container with a dampened Kimwipe half-sheet (11.15 × 20.83 cm), folded into eighths, and anesthetized in the freezer. b) Restrained by the wings, decapitation was performed by cutting along the dotted blue line at the junction of the head and thorax. c) The head capsule was pinned posteriorly between the vertex (VX) and occiput (OC) and anteriorly at the frontoclypeus (FC), following excision of the antennae and labial palps. Setae were removed prior to imaging. d) Dorsal view of the head capsule showing the labial palps (LP), frontoclypeus (FC), transfrontal suture (TFS), antennal suture (AS), vertex (VX), occipital suture (OS), and occiput (OC).

1.2. Use the slow chilling method to sedate moths by placing them in a refrigerator (2 to 4 °C) for up to 24 h. If the moth remains active at the start of the dissection or was not previously sedated, place it in a freezer (−20 °C) for 10 to 15 min to achieve full sedation.

Note: Sedation timing may vary based on moth size and age. Avoid prolonged freezing, which could euthanize the moth. Extended refrigeration aids immobilization for dissection but may induce physiological stress responses. Therefore, for molecular studies such as transcriptomics, rapid chilling is recommended to minimize stress-related gene activation (Sinclair et al. 2007, Overgaard and MacMillan 2017). Only adult moths were chilled; larvae were not subjected to cold exposure at any stage of rearing or preparation.

2. Decapitation and Initial Cleaning

Timing: ∼5 min

2.1. Transfer the sedated moth to a fume hood to limit scale and setae dispersion.

2.2. Secure the moth by the wings and use straight dissection scissors to decapitate it by cutting through the cervical membrane at the junction with the prothorax (Fig. 4b).

2.3. Hold the head by the antennae or use forceps to grip the mouthparts and gently scrape the vertex (dorsal surface between antennal sockets) with the scalpel blade to remove surface setae. Perform this step over a dish to collect loose debris.

2.4. Roll the head capsule gently between a Kimwipe to remove residual scales/setae.

2.5. (Optional) Use scissors or forceps to remove the labial palps and galea forming the proboscis to improve visibility and access, as these structures can obstruct the dissection area and contribute to setal contamination.

3. Mounting the Head for Dissection

Timing: ∼5 min

3.1. A silicone-coated Petri dish (Living Systems Instrumentation, No. DD-90-S-BLK-3PK) was used to stabilize and secure the cleaned head capsule during dissection. Similar dishes can be prepared by coating a standard Petri dish with a prepared silicone mixture or molten paraffin wax.

3.2. Pin the head securely using 2 insect pins (Size No. 2, length = 4 cm), one inserted through the posterior margin of the vertex near the occiput, and the other through the anterior region of the frontoclypeus (Fig. 4c).

Caution: Avoid impaling the brain, which lies just beneath the vertex.

3.3. Move the dish to a dissecting microscope equipped with gooseneck or base-mounted illumination.

3.4. Use a scalpel to gently scrape away residual setae or rinse them off with Moth Ringer’s solution using a transfer pipette. The head capsule should appear as shown in Fig. 4d.

3.5. (Optional) Use cotton swabs for final cleaning of the capsule to remove any lingering scales or setae.

Timing: ∼10 min

Incision locations and brain exposure in Manduca sexta. a) Posterior incision made near the occipital suture which borders the occiput and the vertex. b) Anterior incision made along the transfrontal suture, which borders the frontoclypeus and the vertex. c) Lateral incisions made along the antenatal sutures. d) Removal of the vertex using forceps and scalpel, revealing the underlying fat body and connective tissues (indicated by the blue arrows). e) Exposed brain (indicated by the blue arrows) following the removal of fat body, trachea, and connective tissues with forceps. f) Incision along the ocular margin to facilitate optic lobe removal. The brain is partially obscured by floating fat body and connective tissues when submerged in Moth Ringer’s solution.

4.2. Gently lift the cuticle of the cut vertex with fine forceps and sever any connective tissues attached before removing it in one piece.

4.3. Apply Moth Ringer’s solution as needed to hydrate the area and clear loose tissues.

4.5. Carefully remove the underlying layers of opaque, off-white or pinkish fat body and connective tissue to reveal the brain. The brain appears as a pale, bilaterally symmetrical structure and may exhibit slight pulsation (Fig. 5d).

5. Isolating the Brain

Timing: ∼10 to 15 min

Note: Minimal pressure should be used throughout. The brain is delicate and prone to tearing.

5.1. Use fine forceps and a scalpel to carefully remove any remaining cuticle that obstructs visibility of the brain near the antennae or compound eyes (Fig. 5f).

6. Severing the Optic Connections

6.1. If necessary, remove additional fragments of cuticle near the compound eye with forceps until the optic nerves become clearly visible.

6.2. Make an incision along the ocular margin (Fig. 5f), and rinse away any released pigment with Moth Ringer’s solution. Alternatively, the pigment can be soaked up with the cotton tip of a swab or a Kimwipe.

6.3. Use forceps to carefully separate the lamina (outermost layer of the optic lobe) from the retina. Trim away any remaining eye tissue attached to the optic lobe to fully isolate the optic lobes during extraction(Fig. 6). The locations of both incisions used to sever the optic lobes are shown in Fig. 7.

Optic lobe (OL), antennal lobe (AL).

Simple diagram of the Manduca sexta brain (adapted from Fig. 1a) indicating the sites of transection at the optic and antennal lobes, illustrated by dotted red lines. Labeled structures: antenatal lobe (AL), antennal nerve (AN), compound eye (CE), esophageal foramen (EF), optic lobe (OL), ventral nerve cord (VNC), suboesophageal ganglion (SEG).

7. Severing Antennal Connections

7.1. Cut the antennal nerves at their base, near the antennal socket (Fig. 5c). Take care not to damage the delicate antennal lobes. Schematic illustrations of cut positions are shown in Fig. 7.

7.2. Apply additional Moth Ringer’s solution during these steps to maintain hydration and visibility.

8. Trachea Removal

8.1. Locate the 2 large tracheal tubes attached to the posterior region of the antennal lobes (Fig. 8a). These large tracheal tubes serve as primary anchor points of the tracheal system, branching into smaller tracheoles that supply oxygen to the brain.

Trachea removal in Manduca sexta. a) Primary tracheal tubes (dotted lines) and tracheal sheath indicated by the black arrows. b) Dissection hook (indicated by the black arrow) inserted beneath the right primary tracheal tube and sheath. c) The same process applied to the left side. d) Removal of the tracheal sheath by gently inserting the dissection hook beneath the primary tracheal tube and sliding it along the brain surface toward the sheath to pry it away. e) Trachea completely removed from the brain in situ. f) Isolated brain (ventral view) with tracheal sheath still attached at the apex of the optic lobe. Inset: Isolated tracheal sheath.

8.2. Carefully insert the tip of the custom dissection hook (or a fine pin), underneath one of the main tracheal tubes as shown in Fig. 8b and c. Do this slowly to avoid puncturing or damaging the brain.

8.3. Gently pry and slide the tracheal tube away from the brain surface until it begins to detach. Repeat for the second main tracheal tube on the opposite side.

8.4. Once the primary tubes are loosened, use the hook tool or fine forceps to gently slide under the attached tracheoles, following their path along the brain. Lift them slowly away from the brain surface (Fig. 8d).

8.5. When several sections are freed, use 2 pairs of forceps to grasp different areas of the large tracheal tubes. Gently pull the tracheae apart from each other, without pulling them away from the brain, to distribute tension and avoid damage to brain tissue.

8.6. If some tracheoles remain attached, gently reinsert the hook tool beneath them and slide along their length to free them from the brain.

8.7. Perform all trachea removal steps while the brain is still within the head capsule. This provides structural support and reduces the risk of damage to brain tissue.

8.8. Once the tracheal network is fully detached, proceed with brain removal (Fig. 8e). Use fine forceps to clean off any remaining tracheal remnants or tissue once the brain has been excised as shown in Fig. 8f.

9. Removing the Brain

9.1. Use microdissection scissors to make a horizontal cut beneath the brain to separate it from any underlying tissue. Sever the ventral nerve cord (VNC) just below the subesophageal ganglion (SEG) using fine scissors (Fig. 7). Only retain the brain and attached SEG for subsequent fixation and processing, as the SEG helps preserve neural integrity during removal. Note: This approach ensures inclusion of the complete brain while excluding the thoracic and abdominal ganglia, which contain distinct populations of motor and sensory interneurons not relevant to central neural processing (Homberg et al. 1988). Consistent identification of this boundary improves comparability across preparations and reduces variability in downstream analysis.

9.2. Once the optic, antennal, and VNC connections have been severed (Fig. 7) and the trachea removed, gently lift the brain from the head capsule by inserting forceps under the brain and applying even upward pressure on both sides until it is free.

Results and Discussion

A successful dissection yields an intact adult M. sexta brain with clearly visible optic and antennal lobes. The isolated brain should be pale, bilaterally symmetrical, and free of surrounding fat body, connective tissue, or cuticular debris (Fig. 1). When properly isolated, the brain remains structurally intact and is suitable for a range of downstream applications, including anatomical imaging and molecular analysis. A typical example of a successfully isolated brain showing gross anatomical features and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole-stained nuclei, is shown in Fig. 9. The full procedure, from sedation to brain removal, typically requires 30 to 45 min depending on user experience. This protocol provides a streamlined, reproducible and accessible method for isolating the intact brain of M. sexta, a widely used model in neuroanatomy, behavior and neural circuit research. Although the procedure becomes more intuitive with practice, consistent results rely on a clear understanding of the head capsule’s anatomy and the identification of key neural structures.

Anterior view of an adult Manduca sexta brain shown in (a) a stereo micrograph highlighting gross anatomical features and (b) a fluorescence micrograph with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining of cell nuclei.

This approach minimizes tissue damage while preserving key sensory structures including the antennal and optic lobes. The use of Moth Ringer’s solution throughout the dissection helps to maintain tissue hydration, reduce glare, and facilitate the removal of fat body and debris, providing visual clarity and reproducibility of the procedure.

While relatively straightforward, this dissection presents several challenges. Isolating the optic lobes remains technically demanding due to their fragility and close proximity to the pigmented eye tissues. Puncturing the compound eye releases pigment which can obscure visibility and increases the risk of damaging the optic lobes. Insufficient hydration during dissection can result in tissue desiccation or optical distortion when viewed under the stereoscope. Additionally, precise placement of the insect pins is critical, as improper positioning can compromise structural integrity or puncture the brain. Table 1 outlines troubleshooting strategies for addressing these and other common difficulties. This protocol is optimized specifically for the adult M. sexta, and modifications may be required for other developmental stages or related moth species. By standardizing this approach, researchers can achieve greater consistency in tissue quality and experimental outcomes, supporting a wide range of future investigations in sensory neurobiology.
