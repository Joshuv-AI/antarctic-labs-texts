Deep learning for automated detection and classification of temporomandibular joint disorders: A scoping review
Authors: Husnul Hatimah, Irfan Sugianto, Barunawaty Yunus, Muhammad Fadil Hidayat, Rifaat Nurrahma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo map the available evidence on the application of deep learning (DL) in the imaging-based detection and classification of temporomandibular joint disorders (TMDs), and to identify research trends, imaging modalities investigated, and gaps in the current literature.Materials and MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, IEEE Xplore, Wiley Online Library, ScienceDirect, and Cochrane, covering publications between January 2015 and January 2025, without restrictions on language. Studies applying any DL algorithm to medical imaging for the detection, segmentation, or classification of TMDs were included.ResultsFifteen studies met the inclusion criteria, comprising 15 retrospective studies. MRI was the most frequently used imaging modality (10 studies), followed by CBCT (3 studies) and panoramic radiography (2 studies). The most commonly applied DL architectures were CNN-based classifiers (9 studies), followed by segmentation frameworks including U-Net and DeepLabV3+ (4 studies), and object-detection models such as YOLO(two studies).ConclusionThis scoping review demonstrates that the available evidence on deep learning applications in TMJ imaging is growing, with CNN-based classifiers and segmentation frameworks applied to MRI and CBCT showing early promise as adjunctive diagnostic tools. However, most studies are retrospective, single-center, and lack external validation. These findings map a rapidly evolving field and highlight the need for multicenter prospective studies, standardized imaging and reporting protocols, and greater integration of explainable AI frameworks to guide future research and support clinical translation.Keywords: Temporomandibular Joint Disorders, Deep Learning, Convolutional Neural Networks, Magnetic Resonance Imaging, Cone-Beam Computed Tomography

Introduction

Temporomandibular joint disorders (TMDs) represent a diverse group of conditions affecting the temporomandibular joint(TMJ), which is a crucial structure for jaw function. These disorders are characterized by a range of clinical manifestations including pain in the jaw, restricted jaw movement, and clicking or popping sounds during jaw movement. TMDs are classified into myofascial pain, internal derangements(e.g., disk displacement), and degenerative conditions such as osteoarthritis. The global prevalence of TMDs is estimated to affect approximately 10% of the population, with a higher incidence in women, particularly between the ages of 20 and 40 years.1,2 The impact of TMDs on an individual’s quality of life is profound, often leading to chronic pain, impaired oral function, and associated psychological distress, thus posing a significant burden on both the individual and the healthcare system.3 TMDs are complex, stemming from multifactorial etiologies including mechanical and psychosocial factors. This complexity complicates accurate and timely diagnosis.

Traditionally, the diagnosis of TMD has relied on clinical examination supplemented by radiographic imaging, including X-rays, MRI, and CT scans. These imaging techniques are essential for assessing the structural integrity of the TMJ, with MRI being the gold standard for evaluating soft-tissue abnormalities, such as disc displacement.1,4 However, traditional diagnostic methods have several limitations. Clinical examination can be highly subjective, requiring substantial expertise from the clinician, whereas imaging modalities, such as X-rays and CT scans, often fail to adequately visualize soft-tissue lesions and may expose patients to radiation. Furthermore, current diagnostic approaches are limited in detecting early-stage TMD or subtle abnormalities that may be indicative of incipient joint degeneration.1 Therefore, there is a growing need for more accurate, efficient, and non-invasive diagnostic tools for TMD detection.

Deep learning, a branch of artificial intelligence (AI), has emerged as a transformative technology for medical image analysis. Deep learning models, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have demonstrated superior performance in various diagnostic imaging tasks such as lesion detection, image segmentation, and disease classification, often surpassing the accuracy of human experts in certain contexts.5,6 These advancements have been made possible by the ability of deep learning algorithms to process large volumes of complex medical data and identify patterns that may be imperceptible to the human eye. In the field of medical diagnostics, deep learning has shown promise in diverse areas, including radiology, pathology, and dermatology, where it has been applied to improve diagnostic accuracy, reduce human error, and increase the efficiency of medical workflow.7,8

The application of deep learning to TMD detection and classification represents a promising approach for overcoming the limitations of traditional diagnostic methods. Given the complexity and heterogeneity of TMDs, deep learning algorithms are well suited to analyze medical imaging data and identify subtle abnormalities in both hard and soft tissues of the TMJ. By training on large, annotated datasets, deep learning models can automatically detect patterns indicative of TMDs, potentially offering higher sensitivity and specificity than traditional methods. Additionally, deep learning models can provide a standardized and reproducible approach to TMD diagnosis, reducing interobserver variability and improving diagnostic consistency. Importantly, the use of deep learning for TMD detection could lead to non-invasive diagnostic methods that eliminate the need for radiation exposure, which is particularly beneficial for long-term monitoring of patients with chronic TMD.9

This scoping review aims to systematically map the current landscape of deep learning applications in TMJ imaging, characterize the range of imaging modalities and architectures studied, identify the types of TMD conditions addressed, and highlight methodological gaps in the existing evidence base to guide future research. As a scoping review, this study does not aim to synthesize evidence for clinical recommendations but rather to provide a comprehensive map of the existing literature to inform research priorities.

Materials and Methods

Protocol and registration

This scoping review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews(PRISMA-ScR) guidelines.10 The review protocol was designed prior to the literature search to ensure methodological transparency and consistency. This review was registered in the Open Science Framework (osf.io/gy2d7).11

Eligibility criteria

Studies were identified and selected using the population-concept-context (PCC) framework (Table 1). The population consisted of individuals diagnosed with TMDs, including disc displacement, osteoarthritis, and other internal derangements. The conceptual scope focused on the application of deep learning algorithms, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs), U-Net, ResNet, VGG, and GAN-based models, and related architectures for the detection, segmentation, or classification of TMDs across various medical imaging modalities, including MRI, computed tomography (CT), cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), panoramic radiography, and ultrasound. The contextual criteria required studies to report quantitative diagnostic performance metrics(accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, Dice coefficient, and area under the curve (AUC)). To ensure contemporary relevance, only peer-reviewed original research articles published between 2015 and 2024 were considered eligible. Eligibility was restricted to full-text English, studies involving human imaging datasets, and applying any deep learning method to TMJ imaging. Articles were excluded if they were reviews, editorials, case reports, animal or in-vitro studies, or if they lacked imaging-based deep learning applications or quantitative performance outcomes.

Information sources

Electronic databases, including PubMed, IEEE Xplore, Wiley Online Library, ScienceDirect, and Cochrane, were systematically searched to identify relevant studies that evaluated deep learning applications in the diagnosis of temporomandibular joint disorders. All searches encompassed publications between January 2015 and January 2025. The final search was completed on January 15, 2025.

Search strategy

The search strategy incorporated Boolean operators and controlled the vocabulary specific to each database. Manual searches were conducted by examining the reference lists of all eligible articles to ensure that potentially relevant studies that were not captured through electronic searches. The search covered articles published between January 2015 and January 2025. All retrieved results were exported into reference management software (Zotero) for duplicate removal and screening.

Selection of sources of evidence

Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers(HH, IS) to identify eligible studies. The full texts of potentially relevant articles were then retrieved and assessed according to the inclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer (BY). A PRISMA-ScR flow diagram was used to document the selection process, including the number of records identified, screened, excluded, and included.

Data charting process

Data were independently extracted by the research team (HH, MFH) using a standardized data extraction sheet to ensure consistency. The following key items were recorded for each study: author(s), year of publication, and country of origin; study design and sample size; imaging modality utilized (such as MRI, CBCT, CT, or panoramic imaging); deep learning model or architecture employed; performance metrics(including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and Dice/AUC scores); and key findings along with any clinical recommendations provided. This systematic approach allowed for the structured collection and analysis of relevant data for the scoping review. The extracted data were cross-checked for consistency and summarized in Table 4.

Data items

The primary variables extracted from the included studies encompassed diagnostic performance metrics, including: accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, which quantify how reliably each deep learning model identifies the presence or absence of TMD disorders. Types of deep learning architecture, including CNNs, ResNet, U-Net, and other advanced frameworks, was documented to capture how differences in network design influence diagnostic capability and computational behavior. The imaging modality was recorded to reflect the structural and functional information available to the algorithm; MRI offered superior soft-tissue visualization, CBCT provided detailed osseous assessment, and panoramic radiography served as a lower-resolution screening tool.

The targeted TMD subtype, such as disc displacement, osteoarthritis, or degenerative joint changes, was noted to clarify the specific clinical phenotype that each model aimed to detect. Secondary variables included dataset size, which directly affects model training stability and generalizability; image quality control measures, which influence noise levels and diagnostic clarity; and the application of explainability tools, such as Grad-CAM, which provide visual insights into model decision-making and enhance transparency and clinical interpretability.

Critical appraisal of individual sources of evidence

To characterize the methodological quality of included studies and inform the interpretation of findings, a descriptive methodological assessment was conducted using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) tool. This assessment was used descriptively—consistent with the scoping review purpose of mapping the evidence base rather than formally appraising study quality for inclusion or exclusion. QUADAS-2 evaluates methodological considerations across four domains: 1) patient selection, 2) index test, 3) reference standard, and 4) flow and timing. Each domain was assessed independently by 2 reviewers(H.H. and I.S.), with discrepancies resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (B.Y.).12

Each domain was assessed using pre-specified signaling questions adapted for artificial intelligence-based diagnostic accuracy studies. Signaling questions are binary prompts that identify specific sources of bias; a negative or unclear answer flags a methodological concern. The key signaling questions applied per domain were as follows. For patient selection: 1) Was a consecutive or random sample enrolled? 2) Was a case-control design avoided? 3) Were inappropriate exclusions avoided? For index test: 1) Were index test results interpreted without knowledge of the reference standard? 2) Were thresholds pre-specified? 3) Were model development steps(preprocessing, augmentation, tuning) fully reported? For reference standard: 1) Is the reference standard likely to correctly classify the target condition? 2) Were reference standard results interpreted without knowledge of the index test? For flow and timing: 1) Was the interval between index and reference test appropriate? 2) Were all patients included in the analysis? Based on these questions, each domain was rated as Low, Moderate, or High risk of bias, as defined in the legend of Table 3.

The QUADAS-2 appraisal revealed notable variability in methodological quality across the 15 included studies. Most studies demonstrated a moderate-to-high risk of bias in the patient selection domain, largely due to single-center retrospective sampling, class-balancing resembling a case-control design, and insufficient reporting of the exclusion criteria. These factors limit the representativeness and may inflate model performance.

The Index Test domain exhibited the highest methodological vulnerability. Only a minority of studies described blinded interpretation or pre-specified thresholds, whereas most provided incomplete reporting of image preprocessing, augmentation, and model tuning steps. This raise concerns related to overfitting and significant limitations for reproducibility in clinical translation.

The reference standard domain performed comparatively better, with MRI-based studies demonstrating a low risk of bias owing to established diagnostic accuracy for disc displacement. However, panoramic and CBCT-based studies displayed higher concerns owing to variability in OA grading and lack of blinding between assessors and model outputs.

The results for the Flow and Timing domains were mixed. Some studies retained all samples with consistent MRI/CBCT protocols, whereas others excluded low-quality images or did not report the timing between the index and reference tests, introducing potential attrition bias. Overall, despite the high internal accuracy metrics, the QUADAS-2 assessment highlights limitations in patient representativeness, threshold specification, blinding, transparency of model development, and external validation factors that restrict generalizability and require attention in future AI-based TMJ research.

Synthesis of results

A narrative synthesis was performed to integrate and interpret the findings across the included studies by examining three principal dimensions: the imaging modalities employed, the deep learning architectures utilized, and the specific diagnostic targets addressed. The studies incorporated a range of imaging techniques, including MRI, CBCT, CT, and panoramic radiography, each contributing different levels of anatomical detail and influencing model performance accordingly. Various deep learning architectures were applied, such as CNN-based classifiers, U-Net and other segmentation frameworks, ResNet and VGG feature extractors, and object-detection models like YOLO and MobileNet, reflecting substantial methodological diversity in algorithm design.

The diagnostic objectives of these models also varied, encompassing the detection of disc displacement, identification of osteoarthritic changes, and evaluation of degenerative joint alterations. While quantitative outcomes are systematically compiled in Table 4, the qualitative synthesis focused on identifying overarching trends, evaluating clinical implications, and highlighting persistent gaps in methodological rigor and model generalizability. Owing to considerable heterogeneity in study design, dataset characteristics, performance metrics, and reporting standards, a formal meta-analysis was deemed inappropriate and therefore not conducted.

Results

Selection of studies

Figure 1 presents the flowchart of the study selection process, prepared according to the PRISMA-ScR guidelines.10 The initial search across five electronic databases PubMed, IEEE Xplore, Wiley Online Library, ScienceDirect, and Cochrane Library identified a total of 5,115 records. Following the removal of 4,863 duplicate records, 252 articles were screened based on their titles and abstracts. Of these, 98 records were excluded at this stage, and 154 reports were sought for full-text retrieval. However, 102 reports could not be retrieved, leaving 52 reports assessed for eligibility. During full-text assessment, 37 reports were excluded for the following reasons: poor data quality or too small a sample size (n=8), studies that did not use deep learning or relied solely on traditional methods(n=15), and full-text articles that were not available (n=14). Characteristics of the articles included

All studies were published within the last six years, with the earliest published in 2020 and the most recent in 2025. The studies were conducted in the following countries: South Korea,9,16,18,19,20 China,8,17,23,25 Türkiye,21,22 Japan,15 Taiwan,14 Romania,13 and the United Arab Emirates.24 All 15 included studies employed a retrospective design.

The 15 studies collectively involved a substantial number of imaging datasets, ranging from 50 patients to 9,009 patients. Regarding imaging modality, MRI was the most frequently employed (n=10),8,9,13,14,15,16,17,20,21,25 followed by CBCT (n=3),18,23,24 and panoramic radiography (n=2).19,22

The deep learning architectures applied across the included studies were diverse. CNN-based classifiers were the most commonly used, including VGG16,9,16,19 ResNet variants,17,20,25 InceptionV3,19,21 MobileNetV2,13,21 and Dense Net.21,22 Segmentation-based frameworks were applied in four studies, including 3D U-Net,18 DeepLabV3+,17,20 and a modified U-Net architecture.15 Object-detection models were employed in two studies, both utilizing YOLO-based pipelines YOLOv314 and YOLOv10.23 One study incorporated Grad-CAM to provide visual explainability of model outputs.17 The TMD conditions most frequently targeted were anterior disc displacement,8,9,13,14,15,17,20,25 followed by osteoarthritis and degenerative joint changes,19,22,23,24 joint effusion,16,21 and multi-stage TMJD classification.14 Several studies addressed more than one TMD condition simultaneously.8,20,21

Imaging modalities: MRI, CBCT, and panoramic imaging

Table 4 shows that soft-tissue assessment in AI-based TMJ diagnostics was performed exclusively using MRI and focused predominantly on the articular disc. Most included studies evaluated anterior disc displacement through disc position classification or segmentation.8,9,15,19,20,21,25 This highlights the articular disc’s central role as the primary soft-tissue target. Only a limited number of investigations extended soft-tissue analysis to joint effusion or inflammatory changes,16,21 while disease-staging models incorporated composite soft-tissue features without isolating specific structures. Notably, no CBCT or panoramic-based study has evaluated soft-tissue components, underscoring MRI as the sole imaging modality supporting AI-assisted soft-tissue assessment in the current literature.

In contrast, models utilizing CBCT and panoramic radiographs showed greater performance variability. While effective for identifying osseous changes such as temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis, accuracy in two-dimensional panoramic imaging remained lower, at approximately 84%.19 A limited number of CBCT-based studies(n=3)18,23,24 reported agreement with expert evaluation for osseous morphology and degenerative joint changes. While these preliminary findings suggest potential for diagnostic support, the small evidence base and lack of external validation preclude broader conclusions about clinical applicability.

Performance of DL architectures

Conventional CNN architectures, including VGG16, ResNet, and InceptionV3, reported a wide range of sensitivity values in MRI-based classification of disc displacement, with some studies reaching values approaching 98% under internally validated, single-center conditions.8,9,13,14,16,17,21,23,25 However, for anatomically detailed tasks such as articular disc localization, segmentation-based models particularly U-Net and its derivatives have emerged as the preferred approach in MRI studies.15,20 In contrast, CBCT-based segmentation studies primarily targeted osseous structures, such as the mandibular condyle, achieving Dice coefficients ranging from 0.58 to 0.73.18 Collectively, these findings indicate a growing emphasis on spatially explicit outputs to support anatomical interpretation and clinical planning, while soft-tissue segmentation remains confined to MRI-based applications.

Clinical applicability and diagnostic efficiency

Beyond achieving high accuracy for anterior disc displacement detection, several studies described AI systems as adjunctive tools capable of assisting radiologists in image interpretation and clinical decision-making.8,9,13,15,17,25 MRI-based classifiers and hybrid pipelines including CNN, ResNet-based, and segmentation-assisted frameworks demonstrate consistent value for anterior disc displacement detection and disc reduction assessment, supporting their use as second readers or triage aids in routine MRI workflows. Multi-class and segmentation-based MRI models have demonstrated the capacity to address multiple diagnostic targets simultaneously including disc position, osteoarthritic changes, and joint effusion which may represent a direction for future workflow integration. Beyond MRI, CBCT and panoramic-based models offer modality-specific clinical roles. CBCT-based CNNs, segmentation models, and detection frameworks such as 3D U-Net and YOLO-based pipelines demonstrate sufficient agreement with expert evaluation for osseous morphology and degenerative joint disease, supporting their application in morphological assessment and diagnostic assistance rather than primary diagnosis. Panoramic-based models were reported in only two studies,19,22 with variable performance ranging from 84% to 95.23% accuracy. While these findings indicate exploratory potential as screening aids, the low sensitivity reported in one study (54%)19 underscores the current limitations of panoramic radiography for soft-tissue conditions such as TMD, and suggests panoramic-based AI models should be considered supplementary rather than stand alone diagnostic tools.

Research trends based on deep learning tasks

When categorized according to deep learning task, most studies focused on classification models (n=9), followed by segmentation approaches (n=4) and object detection frameworks (n=2). This trend indicates that automated diagnosis remains the primary focus of AI development in TMJ imaging, whereas anatomically detailed segmentation and real-time detection models represent emerging research directions. Segmentation approaches were predominantly applied in MRI and CBCT datasets to improve anatomical localization, while object detection models such as YOLO were mainly used for degenerative bone changes.

Research gaps and future directions

Despite promising diagnostic performance, several important research gaps remain. Most studies relied on single-center retrospective datasets with limited external validation, which may restrict generalizability. Few studies incorporated explainable AI frameworks or multimodal datasets combining imaging and clinical variables. Furthermore, prospective validation studies remain scarce. These gaps highlight important opportunities for future research to improve the robustness and clinical translation of AI-based TMJ diagnostic systems.

Discussion

The included studies suggest that deep learning models may support the detection of disc displacement and degenerative joint conditions as adjunctive tools in radiologic assessment. Among MRI-based studies, CNN-based classifiers reported a wide range of performance, with several studies achieving accuracy values above 90% under internally validated conditions.8,9,17,20,25 For instance, Lee et al.9 reported promising performance of fine-tuned CNNs for ADD detection across two hospital datasets, while Lin et al.25 reported accuracy of 97% using a ResNet architecture on a large MRI dataset. For segmentation tasks, Choi et al.18 applied a 3D U-Net to CBCT images and achieved Dice coefficients of 0.58–0.73 for mandibular condyle segmentation, suggesting potential utility for morphological evaluation, though the small sample of 99 patients limits interpretation. Almăşan et al.13 applied MobileNetV2 with transfer learning to 50 MRI cases and reported 81% accuracy for ADD detection, with proton density-weighted sequences used as the primary input, consistent with the approach of Lee et al.16

Among studies evaluating multiple architectures, Ozsari et al.21 compared seven CNN models on MRI data and found that Xception and MobileNetV2 showed relatively stronger performance for closed-mouth disc position classification. Pekince et al.22 compared six architectures on panoramic images for condylar osteoarthritis grading, illustrating considerable variability even within a single study. Yoon et al.20 applied ensemble segmentation models including DeepLabV3+, SegFormer, and UPerNet variants to MRI data for simultaneous ADD and osteoarthritis evaluation, reporting accuracy of 93.75%. In the context of panoramic imaging, Kim et al.19 demonstrated that R-CNN and VGG16 models could identify condylar osteoarthritic changes with 84% accuracy, though sensitivity was only 54%, and the authors acknowledged that panoramic radiography is not the reference standard for this condition. These findings collectively suggest exploratory potential across modalities, though direct comparison across studies is limited by substantial heterogeneity in design and reporting.

In contrast, AI-powered models such as ResNet and MobileNetV2 have shown comparatively higher sensitivity and specificity than conventional reading under specific experimental conditions in detecting TMDs. For example, ResNet101-vd achieved 96.91% accuracy for disc displacement detection.16 Recently, Ozsari et al.21 conducted a series of experiments to evaluate and confirm the performance of a CNN structure, different AI models(Xception, ResNet-101, MobileNetV2, InceptionV3, DenseNet-121, and ConvNeXt), and ViT in terms of inferencing TMJ disorders automatically; this study showed that models like Xception and MobileNet V2 were particularly effective in diagnosing closed-mouth disc positions, achieving 97% accuracy. Accordingly, following the performance findings by Azlağ Pekince et al.,22 the 121-layer model of DenseNet showed the worst result with 64.85% accuracy for the 70 : 30 distribution. In comparison, GoogleNet showed the best result with 95.23% accuracy for the 80 : 20 distribution. Yoon et al.20 introduced AI models combined with the Swin Transformer, and Seg Former to diagnose TMJ ADD and osteoarthritis suggest that AI-assisted imaging interpretation may substantially augment clinician performance, reduce diagnostic variability, and support more timely and accurate interventions in the management of temporomandibular disorders.

Study by Kim et al.19 have shown that artificial intelligence extends beyond CBCT and MRI modalities in the detection of TMJ disorders, and panoramic radiographs can also benefit from deep learning techniques for identifying TMJ osteoarthritis. Some deep learning models have shown performance advantages over conventional reading methods under specific experimental conditions. Traditional techniques, such as manual image interpretation and clinical assessments, rely on clinician expertise and may introduce subjectivity.4 In the present study by Mao et al.,23 the AI model was based on YOLOv10, which places structural constraints on the grid cell proposals, supporting multiple simultaneous recognitions of the same object. The results showed that the diagnostic performance measures of the AI model were more consistent with the gold standard than those of the oral radiologists. Moreover, traditional MRI and CT scans can be time-consuming and may not provide sufficient clarity for early-stage disc displacement or soft-tissue abnormalities.23 Several studies suggest that AI models may reduce diagnostic time in certain settings. For instance, Bai et al.8 reported that MSANet reduced diagnostic time from 21.01 min to 16.15 min, while Mao et al.23 reported that a YOLOv10-based model diagnosed degenerative joint disease in 0.2 s compared to 6.7 s for human experts. These efficiency gains may potentially benefit clinical workflows, particularly in high-volume settings, though prospective evidence in routine practice is still limited. Lee et al.16 stated that the fine-tuning model and DNN were optimal for judging TMJ effusion and may be used to prevent true negative cases and aid in human diagnostic performance.

The progressive integration of deep learning models into clinical practice may gradually improve the diagnosis and management of TMDs. Early and accurate detection of disc displacement and other TMD conditions may contribute to preventing disease progression and improving patient outcomes. In addition, deep learning has been explored for identifying severe stages of temporomandibular joint disorders, as shown by the study by Su et al.,14 emphasizing its potential role in both early detection and comprehensive evaluation of disease severity, with a CNN model capable of classifying multiple TMJD stages and achieving an accuracy of 84.73% under internal validation. The use of AI models may also support improved diagnostic standardization and reduced inter-rater variability. For example, ResNet and U-Net have demonstrated the potential to detect disc displacement with reduced clinician bias, contributing to more consistent decision-making.20 The precision of AI models in segmenting TMJ components on imaging, particularly MRI and CBCT, may support diagnostic confidence and individualized treatment planning. If validated in prospective clinical settings, this could contribute to reduced misdiagnosis rates, fewer unnecessary procedures, and more targeted therapeutic strategies.25 Moreover, the ability of AI tools to standardize diagnostic interpretation across practitioners helps mitigate variability in decision-making, ultimately contributing to more consistent and evidence-based care. As a result, patients may benefit from earlier symptom relief and improved functional outcomes.20,26

Most included studies were retrospective and conducted in single-center settings, limiting the generalizability of findings to broader and more diverse populations.8,9,13,14,19,20,21,22,23,24,25,27 Retrospective designs are susceptible to selection bias, as cases may be selected based on image quality or diagnostic certainty, which can artificially inflate model performance metrics. Most included studies were based on relatively small or narrowly defined datasets, which may limit model robustness and increase the risk of overfitting particularly evident in studies such as Almăşan et al.13 with only 50 patients and Choi et al.18 with 99 patients.

Although several studies reported diagnostic accuracies exceeding 90%, these results should be interpreted cautiously because many models were trained and tested on highly curated, homogeneous datasets. High performance in experimental settings does not necessarily indicate equivalent performance in routine clinical environments. The performance variation observed between internal and external test sets in Nozawa et al.15 where overall accuracy decreased from 91.6% to 87.9% and sensitivity dropped from 95% to 80% across hospitals illustrates how model performance can degrade outside the training environment. Only a limited number of studies performed external validation using independent datasets,15,17 making it difficult to determine real-world clinical applicability. Considerable heterogeneity existed among studies regarding imaging modalities, preprocessing methods, AI architectures, and outcome measures, making direct comparison difficult. Some studies reported AUC values, others reported raw accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, while segmentation studies reported Dice coefficients creating an inconsistent evidence base that resists meaningful synthesis.

Future research should address the generalizability and validation of AI models across different institutions and diverse patient populations. Focusing on multi-center collaborations is essential to develop robust models that can perform well in varied healthcare environments. Advancing hybrid models that CNNs and GANs could improve segmentation and classification accuracy. Hybrid models could take advantage of the strengths of different architectures to handle complex cases, improving model robustness and adaptability.27 With the increasing adoption of AI in healthcare, several ethical and regulatory challenges need to be addressed. Transparency and interpretability of deep learning models are crucial to ensure clinician trust. For example, Grad-CAM visualizations can help clinicians understand how models make decisions by visualizing the important areas of the image, such as the articular disc.28

Strict regulatory frameworks, such as GDPR or HIPAA, are needed to protect patient confidentiality and ensure ethical AI usage in healthcare.15 Finally, human oversight should always be central to clinical decision-making. AI models should be viewed as supportive tools and not replacements for clinicians, ensuring that AI assists but does not replace human judgment.27,28
