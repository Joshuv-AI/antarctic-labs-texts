Communication Strategies for Children With Chronic Pain Across the Ages: A Qualitative Study of Physiotherapists Using Vignette‐Based Focus Groups
Authors: Jessica Coventry, Rhianydd Thomas, Luke M. Davies, Cylie M. Williams, Binh Ta, Verity Pacey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGood communication strategies are essential in the management of chronic pain in children. While physiotherapists play a key role in pain management, there is limited guidance on adapting communication strategies for children of different ages. This study describes the communication approaches physiotherapists consider when working with children experiencing chronic lower limb pain and how they adapt these strategies across developmental stages. This study incorporated a qualitative design with three case vignettes and semi‐structured focus groups with 20 physiotherapists with experience in pediatrics (mean 11 years physiotherapy experience, 70% with 80%–100% pediatric caseload). Data were analyzed using thematic analysis. Three key themes were generated: (1) Understanding the child and their needs, tailored to the child's developmental level, interests, and communication style; (2) Reassuring and empowering the child, highlighting strategies to build trust, validate pain experiences, and foster autonomy; and (3) Educating the child and their family, addressing pain‐related misconceptions and supporting pain management. Additionally, four communication strategies were described by physiotherapists as methods they employ during consultations to support their approaches. These were (1) visual aids, (2) mirror child's language, (3) storytelling and analogies, and (4) age‐appropriate resources. Physiotherapists describe understanding, empowering, and educating children with chronic pain through diverse communication strategies tailored to the child's age. These findings highlight the different approaches that may be helpful for physiotherapists who are new to pediatrics or chronic pain management and may enhance physiotherapy practice and improve outcomes for children with chronic pain.

Introduction

Physiotherapists play an important role in the assessment and management of chronic lower limb pain in children and adolescents. For children and adolescents who live with chronic pain, the impact is debilitating and often results in significant disruption to schooling and physical activity. This can lead to high levels of depression and anxiety [1, 2, 3]. Children with chronic pain often feel they are not listened to or respected in healthcare settings [4]. These experiences suggest that communication with children and their families remains a challenging area for healthcare professionals. These difficulties are further compounded by the broader disruptions caused by chronic pain. Physiotherapists need a diverse set of skills to navigate these challenges. Their role extends beyond providing effective physical interventions. They must also use age‐appropriate, empathetic communication strategies to address the complex and multifaceted impacts of chronic pain.

Effective communication is a fundamental competency for physiotherapists [5, 6]. Effective communication plays a pivotal role in shaping children's health beliefs, understanding health concerns, and engaging children and their families with meaningful treatment strategies [7, 8, 9]. For communication to be considered effective, information must be exchanged in a way that the recipient understands [5, 10]. Physiotherapists should adapt their communication style significantly depending on the child's age and cognitive ability to communicate meaningfully. Despite the inclusion of pediatric content in most physiotherapy curricula, dedicated time and learning opportunities for developing communication skills with children and adolescents remain limited [11, 12]. Physiotherapists may also seek additional training in children's healthcare, of which communication may be just one part [13]. This highlights the need to train physiotherapists in age‐appropriate communication and collaboration with children and their families.

Children and their families report mixed experiences of healthcare services; however, positive interactions are described as ones where the child feels listened to and believed about their pain, and where they feel their questions and concerns are heard and addressed [4]. International evaluation of pediatric physiotherapy services found a need for further overall guidance for physiotherapists on working with children [12]. Effective communication between health professionals and children with chronic pain can improve adherence to management recommendations [14], and children report increased trust and decreased stress when they feel listened to and supported [4].

Despite the importance of communication, there is limited guidance for physiotherapists about the practicalities of adjusting their language and communication approaches for children with chronic pain of different ages. In adults, communication strategies such as active listening, shared decision making, and nonverbal communication have been studied. Actions such as maintaining positive body language have been shown to improve patient satisfaction, increase treatment adherence, and reduce medical errors [15, 16]. The International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) pain curriculum for physiotherapists provides a broad evidence‐based foundation; however, it does not provide guidance on the specifics of how physiotherapists should adapt their pain management strategies for children [17].

This study aimed to explore the communication approaches and strategies considered by physiotherapists to address the pain experience of children with chronic lower limb pain and to determine if and how they adapt these for children of different ages. Understanding how physiotherapists currently use communication strategies for children with chronic pain is a crucial step toward developing evidence‐based guidance. By doing so, this study may contribute to the development of communication guidelines that can be used to guide physiotherapists and other healthcare professionals in the assessment and management of children's chronic lower limb pain.

Method

Study Design

We used a qualitative research design using reflexive thematic analysis employing vignette‐based focus group discussions [18]. This design was selected to facilitate discussion and knowledge sharing between diverse participant perspectives [19, 20]. The reporting of this study was guided by the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [21].

A social constructivist theoretical orientation was used throughout this study. This is underpinned by the idea that knowledge is developed through interactions individuals have with one another [22]. Social constructivism is both an ontology and epistemological positionality, where multiple realities exist and are constructed through lived experiences and interactions with others [22].

Ethical Considerations

Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee approved this research (MUHREC: 39324). Written informed consent was obtained for participation and audio recording of the group discussion.

Participants and Recruitment

Participants were recruited during the 2023 Queensland Pediatric Physiotherapy Clinical Network Conference in Brisbane, Australia. The focus groups were held as part of a workshop at the conference. During the conference, this research and the opportunity to pre‐read the explanatory research statement were promoted in the conference program app and during plenary sessions via a QR code displayed on the screen for conference delegates. All attendees were eligible to participate.

Procedure for Data Collection

This study was conducted as a 90‐min workshop on the final day of the conference. Three researchers facilitated the workshop (V.P., J.C., L.M.D.). J.C. is a registered physiotherapist and PhD student; V.P. has 20 years' experience as a pediatric physiotherapist and researcher, and L.M.D., a registered physiotherapist and experienced qualitative researcher, assisted with participant management. At the workshop commencement, the research was explained, and participants were given time to review the explanatory statement and provide written informed consent. Following consent, participants also responded to questions about age, gender identity, qualifications, and pediatric experience.

The workshop room was set up with both recording and non‐recording tables, ensuring that participants who chose not to engage in the research still had the opportunity to fully participate in the workshop. The workshop commenced with a 10‐min introduction to the concept and impact of children's chronic lower limb pain. Data were collected through a semi‐structured focus group format with three specific questions aligned to three case study vignettes. The three questions asked were the same for each vignette; examples of these questions are provided in Table 1.

A graphical artist was present during the workshop to create an initial concept map and real‐time feedback to participants as the workshop progressed. The facilitating researchers (V.P., J.C., L.M.D.) walked around the room during discussion periods and recorded key phrases and words from tables onto a shared Padlet, to inform the initial concept map. Additionally, one participant at each focus group table was provided access to the Padlet to add phrases from their table discussion in real‐time. This concept map was sent out as a resource to all workshop attendees and was reviewed by the research team before commencing coding of the focus group transcripts.

Sample adequacy was considered using the concept of information power [23]. The study's aim was narrowly focused, the sample was highly specific to the phenomenon of interest, and the data were generated through rich, collegial discussion in focus groups underpinned by a social constructivist approach. The context of data collection, during an academic conference where participants had been exposed to current best practice and peer exchange, further enhanced data quality. Given these factors, the authors determined that the existing data set held sufficient information power to address the research question without further data collection [23].

Data Analysis

Demographic data were entered into an Excel spreadsheet by the first author (J.C.) and standard descriptive statistics were calculated. This information was used holistically, without participants' demographic information or contributions to the focus group discussion. Recordings of tables were uploaded into the auto‐transcribing software Otter.ai (AlSense 2016). Transcriptions were then manually checked, and identifying information of participants, such as names, was removed by the first author (J.C.). Due to the nature of the recording of multiple voices without identifiers, demographic data was not linked to responses or tables.

Reflexive thematic analysis, as outlined by Braun and Clarke [18] was used to analyze the focus group data. The research question explored how physiotherapists explain pain to children with chronic musculoskeletal pain and their families, and how they adapt their communication approaches across different age groups. The process of transcription correction allowed the first author (J.C.) to become familiar with the data. Corrected transcripts were read and re‐read by the first author. Two authors independently coded all transcripts (J.C., R.T.). The coding process was primarily inductive; however, where participants described specific strategies such as using visual aids, these were coded descriptively as “visual aid” as well as interpretively “educating the child about the condition.” This approach aligns more closely with a codebook or content analysis approach. All coded quotes were reviewed individually and iteratively consistent with reflexive thematic analysis [22]. Codes were tentatively clustered into potential themes in relation to the research question by one author (J.C.). J.C. and R.T., a physiotherapist with 10 years clinical and research experience in pediatrics, discussed these themes before being reviewed by the full authorship team. In an iterative discussion with the full authorship team, the themes and subthemes were revised. The final themes were further refined during the writing process by two authors (V.P., J.C.). The final themes were then reviewed by the full authorship team.

Illustrative data for themes.

The three main themes: Understanding the child and their needs, reassuring and empowering the child and educating the child and their family, represent distinct but interconnected approaches that physiotherapists discussed using when communicating with children and their families about chronic pain. Each theme and its associated subthemes were created to capture an underlying rationale or approach.

In “Understanding the child and their needs,” physiotherapists emphasized the importance of tailoring communication based on the child's cognitive ability, pain experience, contributing factors, and the relationship built with the child and family. The “Reassuring and empowering” theme focused on validating the child's pain experience, providing reassurance, and fostering autonomy through choice. Meanwhile, “Educating the child and their family” centered on correcting misconceptions, delivering pain education, and collaboratively creating a supportive management plan.

These approaches were often used in combination and influenced one another. For example, gaining insight into the child facilitated more targeted education, while reassurance helped build rapport and deepen understanding. Education, in turn, could validate and empower the child, enhancing their agency. Physiotherapists recognized these components as interconnected and context‐dependent, with the specifics of each case guiding how the approaches were applied, as reflected in the subthemes, which capture age‐related communication adaptations.

A reflexive interpretation of the data highlights potential points of challenge in balancing these three approaches [18]. For example, prioritizing “Understanding the child and their needs” might, at times, lead to prolonged assessment delaying physiotherapists in providing reassurance or education. Conversely, focusing heavily on “Educating the child and their family” without first establishing a thorough understanding of the child's context risks delivering generic or less meaningful information. These inferred tensions highlight the complex and dynamic nature of clinical communication, where physiotherapists must continuously adapt and integrate their approaches to meet the unique needs of each child and family.

Theme 1—Understanding the Child and Their Needs

In this theme, physiotherapists emphasized the need to understand the child's cognitive ability and experience of pain, exploring contributing factors and building rapport with the child and their family.

Understand and Consider the Child's Cognitive Ability

Physiotherapists acknowledged the need to consider the child's cognitive ability throughout the consultation. For Lily (Age 7), physiotherapists anticipated that she may struggle to retain information and may be unable to understand complex concepts. For Jasmine (Age 12), physiotherapists recognized that she would likely have a higher cognitive ability, allowing for more complex ideas and slightly more advanced explanations about her pain. Across groups, there was some disagreement about whether at Age 16, Ashcan shared the cognitive abilities of an adult. Overall, they agreed that as an adolescent he may be able to engage with more complex concepts but may not fully understand the long‐term perspective they associated with adult understanding.

Understand the Child's Concept and Experience of Pain

Physiotherapists discussed how they would adapt their approach to understand the child's concept and experience of their pain. For Lily (Age 7), physiotherapists focused on simple, direct, open‐ended questions, particularly relating to the child's experience of their pain. When discussing their approach to Jasmine (Age 12), physiotherapists began to consider the individual pain experience, exploring other facets and impacts of the child's pain. Questions about how their pain makes them feel and what they believe triggers their pain provided an understanding of the broader impacts of the pain. In discussion around Ashcan (Age 16), physiotherapists recognized that as an adolescent he likely has already developed an understanding of his pain from his own experiences and previous exposure to information. Physiotherapists highlighted the need to uncover the preexisting beliefs about pain, which may include misconceptions, in order to adjust their pain education.

Understand the Pattern of the Child's Pain

Particularly with older children (Ages 12 and 16), physiotherapists described asking further questions to better understand the pattern of the child's pain, including aggravating and easing factors, 24‐h behavior patterns, and establishing a functional baseline. A few groups suggested that many children originally report their pain as constant and all over their body, but careful questioning may provide a clearer picture of the location and nature of their pain. Some groups discussed how, by using everyday relatable scenarios such as lying on the couch or watching TV, they can better understand whether distraction may be an alleviating technique for the child's pain. For Ashcan (Age 16), physiotherapists focused on how his pain affects his daily life, including everyday tasks and leisure activities.

Previous Pain Management Strategies

Physiotherapists were aware that children with chronic pain and their families may have tried several pain management strategies and sought treatment from other clinicians in their journey. Many groups discussed the need to better understand previously used strategies and whether these strategies had been effective before adding additional strategies. While overall questioning approaches were similar across all three vignettes, for Lily (Age 7) the questions discussed were mainly directed at her parents.

Get to Know the Child and Their Family

Physiotherapists prioritized building rapport and better understanding the child and their family to adapt their treatment approach. For Lily (Age 7) physiotherapists discussed exploring Lily's interests to incorporate these into discussion and help simplify complex concepts. Across all vignettes, physiotherapists maintained an explicit focus on child‐centered care. For older children, physiotherapists shifted more toward the individual, understanding their personal goals and motivators to inform their personalized management plans.

Explore Whether There Are Any Psychosocial Contributors to the Child's Pain

Particularly with older children (Ages 12 and 16), many groups flagged that they would try to ascertain whether there were other psychosocial contributors to the child's pain experience, such as family dynamics, stress, or mental health conditions. For both Jasmine (Age 12) and Ashcan (Age 16), physiotherapists provided examples of their considerations, such as how past events may contribute to the child's current pain experience and the presence of mental stressors.

Theme 2—Reassuring and Empowering the Child

In this theme, physiotherapists focused on providing the child with reassurance and validating their experience of pain, while also providing them with choice and autonomy in both the consultation and long‐term management and goals.

Reassure, Acknowledge, and Validate the Child's Pain and Experience

Before moving on to educating the child and their family, physiotherapists discussed the need to reassure and acknowledge the child's experience. Multiple groups noted that without this validation and acknowledgment, the child may feel disbelieved and unsupported. For Lily (Age 7), physiotherapists described using simple language to affirm the child's pain experience. Across all ages, physiotherapists described reassuring the child to ensure they felt heard and that their concern was important, as well as acknowledging the effort of the child and their family in managing the child's pain.

Provide the Child With Agency and Autonomy

Additionally, when discussing the management of the child's pain, physiotherapists reiterated the need to maintain the child's autonomy, providing them with choice and independence in their pain management. Some groups discussed that they may have separate conversations with the child and the family to ensure that the child's voice and wants were heard and acknowledged. For Lily (Age 7), many groups focused on simple open‐ended questions inviting her to contribute to the treatment planning and discussion about her pain. For the older children (Ages 12 and 16), questioning and invitations for shared decision‐making became more nuanced and focused on the individual's preferences for tools and tracking diaries to encourage self‐management.

Theme 3—Educating the Child and Their Family

In Theme 3, physiotherapists focused on pain education both for the child and for their family while also shifting the narrative toward a focus on function rather than on pain.

Addressing Misconceptions

For the older children (Ages 12 and 16), physiotherapists focused on addressing key misconceptions, particularly around how pain does not necessarily indicate damage and challenging absolute statements, encouraging acknowledgement of the fluctuating nature of pain. For Ashcan (Age 16), many physiotherapists suggested that significant discussion may be required to identify and deconstruct deep‐rooted pain beliefs.

Focus on Function, Not Pain

Redirecting the focus from the child's pain to what the child can do was a discussion topic across all age groups. After ascertaining the pain patterns and experience of the child, many physiotherapists discussed intentionally shifting to focus on what the child can do rather than what they cannot. For Lily (Age 7), physiotherapists focused on education around pacing and checking in with herself, with an emphasis on function rather than pain. However, for Jasmine (Age 12), this redirection tied closely to acknowledging the impact and experience of Jasminder's pain before firmly redirecting the conversation toward what Jasmine can do. Similarly, for Ashcan (Age 16), physiotherapists discussed ways that they could encourage him to try movements and activities that he may have been avoiding, with a focus on getting him to do “something” rather than “nothing.”

Age‐Appropriate Pain Education

Physiotherapists were mindful of adapting their pain education for children of different ages. During the discussion about Lily (Age 7), many physiotherapists discussed using simple, familiar words to frame the child's pain in a way that avoided harmful connotations, explaining the role of the brain and the body and how these connect during the experience of pain. Overall, physiotherapists foregrounded anatomy and physiology in their explanations about pain, building on the concept of brain and body connection to discuss more details with Jasmine (Age 12). Similar discussions were had around Ashcan (Age 16), with further emphasis on independence and autonomy to engage Ashcan as an active participant in his pain management journey.

Educate Family to Help Them Support Their Child

Physiotherapists highlighted the significant impact of family on the child's pain and pain beliefs. For Lily (Age 7), the discussion focused on reassuring her parents and re‐educating them around fears about the nature of Lily's pain. Additionally, they were mindful that the language they may use to describe pain and its management to Lily may need to be quite different from the language used in the conversation with her family. As a result, some physiotherapists suggested they may have two separate conversations. For older children, physiotherapists continued to acknowledge the impact that negative pain beliefs can have on the child, focusing on educating the family. This education centered around avoiding reinforcing unhelpful pain behaviors and promoting function, providing support, and particularly with Ashcan (Age 16), encouraging independence.

In addition to the overall approaches, physiotherapists described a variety of communication strategies they use consistently across different age groups. We intentionally distinguished between the physiotherapists' overarching approaches and specific communication strategies. The latter represents key tools or skills that physiotherapists drew upon across all themes, integrated flexibly to support their approaches rather than confined to any single one. Physiotherapists discussed several communication strategies that they would use during their consultations, particularly in understanding the child's needs and in educating the child and their family.

These strategies included “Visual aids,” “Mirroring the child's language,” “Using analogies and storytelling” and, where appropriate, supplement their approaches and education with recommendations to access other ‘age‐appropriate resources’ (Figure 1). Visual Aids

Most groups discussed using visual aids to assist children in understanding and communicating about their pain experience. Visual aids were featured as a topic across all vignettes, with increasing levels of structure and complexity as children got older. Crayons and body diagrams were described as a creative way to help children describe their pain, particularly for Lily (Age 7). These evolved into more structured diagrams used to describe and explore basic anatomy for Jasmine (Age 12) and even more detailed diagrams describing the brain–body connection or biopsychosocial contributors of pain.

Mirror Child's Language

In getting to know the child and understanding their language around pain, physiotherapists were mindful to incorporate this language into their pain education and management. For Lily (Age 7) this may be simply using the same words she uses during the consultation. Meanwhile, for Ashcan (Age 16), physiotherapists considered how using language that he found relatable and engaging may also increase his engagement with the consultation and pain education being provided.

Storytelling and Analogies

Physiotherapists incorporated storytelling (Age 7) and analogies (Age 12) into their approaches. For Lily (Age 7), one group described using a “pain monster” to understand and even explain aspects of children's pain. For Jasmine (Age 12), physiotherapists described abstract analogies, which encouraged children to reflect on and visualize their pain in different ways.

Age‐Appropriate Resources

Across all groups, for older children like Jasmine (Age 12) and Ashcan (Age 16), physiotherapists noted the benefit of providing additional resources to children to continue to build their understanding of chronic pain. Many groups noted that resources would need to be at an appropriate level for the child's cognitive ability. For Jasmine (Age 12), some groups felt that she might benefit from shared resources with her parents, something they could read through and gain understanding from together. Overall, they emphasized age‐appropriate reading material. Conversely, for Ashcan (Age 16) physiotherapists recognized that many resources aimed at younger children may not be appropriate, instead suggesting that online resources and peer perspectives may be more engaging for Ashcan.

Discussion

This study offers novel insights into how physiotherapists describe how they modify their communication approaches and employ communication strategies when working with children who experience chronic pain across different ages. The physiotherapists provided direct quotes and examples that illustrate how communication may occur in clinical practice. Physiotherapists used a core approach of “understanding the child and their needs,” “reassuring and empowering the child,” and “educating the child and their family” with all three age groups. However, the way these approaches were used and the communication strategies applied differed across ages. Current literature suggests that health professionals find it challenging to adjust their communication for children of different ages, particularly for children under 12 [24]. Additionally, poor communication is often reported by children as a feature of negative healthcare experiences [4]. Findings from our study provide practical examples of how physiotherapists approach children's chronic pain consultations and how key communication strategies, such as visual aids, are implemented across children of different ages; to provide communication tailored to the individual.

Within physiotherapy, being able to adapt communication styles appropriately is recognized as a core competency by professional accreditation bodies [5, 6]. However, across chronic pain literature, clinicians of all disciplines frequently report challenges with communicating about the complexities of chronic pain [25, 26]. In healthcare settings, communication is often more complex with children, as clinicians must consider both the child and their parent. Health professionals must adapt their communication significantly between the dyad, and then alter it across the ages, particularly for younger children. Despite health professionals' best intentions, children and families frequently report that they feel dismissed, uncertain, and unsatisfied in healthcare consultations, reinforcing the need for improved communication [4]. Our study provides insight into the application of approaches and communication strategies, presenting examples of how these may be implemented and adjusted for children of different ages.

A key finding from our study is the role of the physiotherapist as both an educator and an advocate. Physiotherapists described actively reassuring children and seeking to empower them by validating their pain experiences and involving them in shared decision‐making irrespective of their age. This approach aligns with existing literature, which emphasizes that supported children are better empowered in their independence and self‐efficacy [4]. Furthermore, physiotherapists recognized the important influence of pain beliefs on children's pain experiences, adapting their education to address these, with an emphasis on family beliefs and older children's preconceptions. Given the high prevalence of misconceptions around pain and pain catastrophizing (often from families rather than children) [27, 28] our findings highlight how physiotherapists use evidence‐based education approaches to address these misconceptions and maintain a focus on function rather than pain [29, 30]. Importantly, our study provides insight into how physiotherapists may also implement these approaches as emphasizing reassurance to build rapport or focusing parent education on how best to support the child's own understanding of their pain. This situational application of communication approaches provides practical insight that is often missing from current guidelines. Overall, our findings are consistent with the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) pain curriculum for physiotherapists, reinforcing that their approaches to chronic pain management in children are grounded in evidence‐based practice while also offering valuable guidance on the integration of these approaches in clinical consultations [17].

Lastly, our findings reinforce the importance of child‐centered communication in physiotherapy for children with chronic pain. We provide further insight into how physiotherapists consider tailoring their approaches across age groups. In line with existing research [31, 32, 33], physiotherapists in our study emphasized the child's interests and developmental stage to enhance engagement and understanding. Similar to our findings, a recently published guide for communicating with children and young people recommended strategies to improve communication by including play‐based strategies and visual tools, reinforcing their use when assessing and treating children [34]. Our study builds on the IASP pain curriculum for physiotherapists [17] by providing practical examples of how physiotherapists integrate these strategies into their overall approach, emphasizing various ways that physiotherapists may find out about the child's experience of pain and providing pain education and reassurance. In this study, physiotherapists used visual aids to explore children's concepts of pain and provide further understanding of their pain. Visual aids are widely accepted within the literature as a helpful way of explaining complex health concepts to both adults and children and can assist with making abstract pain concepts more tangible and less intimidating [34, 35, 36]. Storytelling and analogies are frequently used in pain education [7]; however, within our study, physiotherapists also used them to learn more about younger children's pain experience. Overall, the communication strategies are supported by existing literature and highlight the need for creativity, particularly when working with young people.

Of the physiotherapist participants in this study, 70% reported a caseload of between 80% and 100% of pediatric patients. Given the significant experience of the participants involved in this study, the examples provided are likely a reflection of what these physiotherapists do day to day rather than merely theoretical suggestions. Therefore, the framework developed from this study may serve as a resource that illustrates flexible communication approaches and demonstrates how these approaches are adapted across different ages. It also provides examples of specific communication strategies, such as using visual aids or mirroring the child's language, showing how these strategies can be integrated effectively within the broader approaches. Additionally, the framework provides a conceptual foundation that could anchor future research. We recognize that the pool of participants for this study is limited to Australian physiotherapists seeking professional development through conference attendance, and therefore the findings may not represent physiotherapy practice nationally or globally. Another limitation of this study is the focus group methodology, where findings may have been influenced by dominant voices within each group. Furthermore, the vignette‐based model involved physiotherapists in what they would do rather than evaluating what they do in practice; therefore, it is possible that elements of reporting bias were present. Further research could involve video analysis of real‐life consultations and evaluation of the effectiveness of the communication approaches and strategies framework identified in this study. Additionally, there was no discussion in our study about the influence of cultural or socio‐economic factors which may impact these approaches or the topics discussed. Future work could explore the perspectives of children or parents around communication during consultations for treatment for chronic pain, as well as the effectiveness of the specific approaches and strategies identified here.

Conclusion

This study provides practical insight into how physiotherapists approach consultations with children and adolescents and implement communication strategies to improve their engagement and effectiveness. Overall, physiotherapist approaches align with the child‐centered care model and evidence‐based practice for the management of children's chronic pain. These findings emphasize the importance of adapting communication to developmental stages, particularly for younger children, to enhance engagement and pain management. By bridging theory and practice, this study offers valuable guidance for clinicians with the potential to inform communication guidelines.
