Clinical and literature insights into the frontotemporal dementia and motor neuron disease spectrum
Authors: Faezeh Khorshidian, Zahra Vahabi, Setareh Rassa, Mohammadreza Mousavipour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrontotemporal dementia represents a heterogeneous group of neurodegenerative disorders primarily affecting the frontal and temporal lobes. The overlap between FTD and motor neuron disease is increasingly recognized, presenting a complex clinical syndrome characterized by progressive cognitive, behavioral, and motor decline.Case presentationWe describe a 69-year-old patient with a 4-year history of excessive ambulation. Over the last year, behavioral changes including disorganized conduct, irritability, spitting, and cold water foot immersion developed. The patient experienced compelling auditory hallucinations driving her to walk continuously for up to 10 h per day. Four months prior to admission, gait impairment with frequent falls, along with hyperorality developed. Neurological examination revealed asymmetric mild weakness, marked muscle atrophy of facial and limb muscles, hyperreflexia, and impaired postural control. Brain MRI showed diffuse cerebral atrophy; electrophysiological studies indicated probable motor neuron disease; and TRODAT SPECT demonstrated impaired presynaptic dopaminergic function bilaterally, consistent with parkinsonism. Final diagnosis was frontotemporal dementia with probable motor neuron disease.Literature reviewA review of the literature highlights the clinical, radiological, and molecular features of FTD-MND overlap, emphasizing the role of TDP-43 pathology, C9orf72 mutations, and the need for multidisciplinary management. Current strategies are symptomatic, though novel therapies such as antisense oligonucleotides and biomarkers like neurofilament light chain (NfL) show promise.ConclusionsThis case highlights the diagnostic complexity of FTD with MND overlap syndrome, emphasizing the need for comprehensive clinical, neuroimaging, and electrophysiological evaluation. Multimodal treatment approaches focusing on behavioral symptoms and functional support are essential for optimizing patient outcomes.

Introduction

Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) encompasses a heterogeneous group of progressive neurodegenerative disorders characterized by selective atrophy of the frontal and temporal lobes, leading to profound impairments in behavior, executive functioning, and language [1, 2]. It comprises a spectrum of clinical phenotypes such as behavioral variant FTD (bvFTD), primary progressive aphasia (PPA), and motor syndromes like corticobasal syndrome (CBS) and progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) [3–6].

A particularly challenging and clinically significant subset of FTD involves its overlap with motor neuron disease (MND), most frequently in the form of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [7–9]. This intersection, often referred to as FTD-MND, is increasingly recognized as a clinicopathological continuum rather than two distinct entities, unified by shared molecular pathology—most notably, cytoplasmic aggregation of TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43) [10, 11].

The coexistence of cognitive, behavioral, and motor deficits presents unique diagnostic difficulties, often leading to delayed recognition and suboptimal management [12, 13]. Moreover, this overlap syndrome has important implications for prognosis, patient care planning, and genetic counseling, particularly in the context of familial forms involving C9orf72 hexanucleotide repeat expansions [11–13].

In this report, we describe an atypical case of an elderly patient presenting with a progressive constellation of neuropsychiatric symptoms, parkinsonian features, and electrophysiological evidence of lower motor neuron involvement. Comprehensive clinical, radiological, and neurophysiological assessments ultimately led to a diagnosis of FTD overlapping with probable MND. Through this case, we aim to highlight the diagnostic complexities and therapeutic challenges inherent in the FTD-MND spectrum and review the relevant literature to contextualize current understanding and emerging strategies for management.

Case presentation

Patient history

A 69-year-old patient with no formal education was brought by family members due to progressive behavioral and motor abnormalities spanning 4 years. The initial symptom was a marked increase in walking activity, described as “excessive ambulation,” which family members found unusual but not distressing at first. Over the course of a year, the patient developed prominent behavioral changes characterized by disorganized actions such as repetitive spitting, increased irritability, and an unusual compulsion to immerse feet in cold water during winter despite discomfort.

The patient reported auditory hallucinations consisting of imperative voices commanding her to keep walking continuously, often for 8–10 h per day. These symptoms were accompanied by progressive social withdrawal, decreased insight, and neglect of personal hygiene, consistent with disorganized behavior.

Four months prior to admission, a gait disturbance developed, marked by impaired balance, postural instability, and recurrent falls. Concomitantly, hyperorality became evident with excessive oral intake of large food volumes, resulting in frequent choking and coughing episodes. There was no reported history of obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD), hoarding, psychosis aside from hallucinations, or prior psychiatric illness. No substance abuse was noted. Family history was positive for Parkinson’s disease in the patient’s maternal aunt(Flowchart 1).

Treatment and clinical course

The patient had previously received donepezil 5 mg/day and levodopa/benserazide (100/25 mg) twice daily for suspected Parkinsonism and an initial diagnosis of PDD. After 4 months of treatment at a total daily levodopa dose below 300 mg, no clinically meaningful improvement in motor or behavioral symptoms was observed. During hospitalization, dopaminergic therapy was subsequently discontinued. Behavioral symptoms including disorganized conduct, irritability, and hallucinations responded markedly to combination pharmacotherapy with Sertraline 100 mg daily, trazodone 50 mg twice daily, and low-dose olanzapine 2.5 mg daily. The prescribed medications were well tolerated throughout hospitalization.No treatment-related adverse events were observed.

Literature review: current understanding and emerging strategies for management of frontotemporal dementia and motor neuron disease overlap syndrome

Pathophysiology and Molecular Insights

Pathophysiology and Molecular Insights

Pathophysiology and Molecular Insights

A defining pathological feature shared between Frontotemporal Dementia (FTD) and Motor Neuron Disease (MND), particularly Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS), is the cytoplasmic mislocalization and aggregation of TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43) [38, 39]. TDP-43 pathology is observed in approximately 95% of ALS cases and about 50% of FTD cases, suggesting a common molecular mechanism underlying these disorders [37, 38]. While other pathological proteins—such as fused in sarcoma (FUS) and tau—may appear in specific FTD variants, they are less frequently involved in FTD-MND overlap cases [39, 40]. Genetically, the discovery of a hexanucleotide (GGGGCC) repeat expansion in the C9orf72 gene has significantly advanced understanding of the FTD-MND continuum. This expansion is now recognized as the most prevalent genetic cause of familial FTD, familial ALS, and FTD-MND overlap [40, 41]. The pathogenic mechanisms associated with C9orf72 include RNA foci formation, dipeptide repeat protein accumulation, nucleocytoplasmic transport disruption, and broader defects in protein homeostasis [41–43].

2.Clinical presentation and diagnostic challenges

2.Clinical presentation and diagnostic challenges

Clinical presentation and diagnostic challenges

FTD-MND overlap syndrome presents with a blend of cognitive, behavioral, language, and motor disturbances. Typical cognitive features include progressive executive dysfunction, apathy, disinhibition, and impaired social cognition. Behavioral variant FTD (bvFTD) represents the most commonly observed clinical phenotype in this overlap, occurring in approximately 15% of patients with ALS, while up to 50% may exhibit subclinical or milder cognitive and behavioral impairments without meeting full diagnostic criteria [18, 44].

Motor symptoms—ranging from muscle weakness, fasciculations, and atrophy to spasticity and hyperreflexia—reflect lower and upper motor neuron involvement akin to ALS [45, 46]. Importantly, early upper motor neuron signs may be subtle and easily overlooked, contributing to the clinical heterogeneity and gradual onset that often delay diagnosis [45, 46].

Neuroimaging using structural MRI frequently demonstrates bilateral or asymmetric frontotemporal atrophy [47, 48]. Electrophysiological evaluations such as EMG are essential for confirming lower motor neuron degeneration. In cases with overlapping parkinsonian symptoms, dopamine transporter imaging (e.g., TRODAT SPECT) can reveal presynaptic dopaminergic deficits [48–50].

3.Current management strategies

3.Current management strategies

Current management strategies

To date, no disease-modifying therapies are approved for FTD-MND. Management is largely symptomatic and involves multidisciplinary coordination.

3.1Pharmacological interventions

3.1Pharmacological interventions

Pharmacological interventions

Behavioral and psychiatric symptoms often require pharmacological intervention. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), such as sertraline and citalopram, have demonstrated efficacy in managing compulsivity and mood disturbances [49, 50]. Atypical antipsychotics (e.g., olanzapine, quetiapine) may be used cautiously for agitation or psychosis, with careful monitoring for adverse effects [17, 47].

Discussion

This case illustrates the diagnostic complexity encountered in patients presenting with overlapping neurodegenerative syndromes, specifically FTD and probable MND. The progressive behavioral abnormalities, including disorganized conduct, auditory hallucinations, and compulsive walking, are characteristic of the behavioral variant of FTD (bvFTD) [5, 9]. Auditory hallucinations are uncommon in typical bvFTD and represent a notable feature of this case. Their presence, together with motor neuron involvement and family history, further supports the possibility of a genetic FTD-MND spectrum disorder, particularly associated with *C9orf72* expansions [47, 48]. Although language impairment can be part of the FTD spectrum, the clinical presentation in this case was not consistent with primary progressive aphasia, as early and prominent behavioral changes predominated and speech deficits were secondary to executive and motor dysfunction rather than a primary language syndrome [3, 44]. The presence of muscle atrophy with electrophysiological evidence of denervation supports concomitant lower motor neuron involvement, suggesting overlap with MND. The combination of cognitive-behavioral symptoms and motor neuron disease has been described in approximately 15% of ALS cases and is attributed to shared TDP-43 protein pathology [9, 11, 23].

Parkinsonian features and a dopaminergic deficit identified on TRODAT SPECT CT added a further layer of complexity to the clinical picture. However, dopaminergic imaging was considered supportive rather than diagnostic, and the overall interpretation was guided primarily by clinical and neuropsychological findings. Parkinsonism in the context of FTD-MND overlap may reflect basal ganglia involvement or coexisting degenerative pathology. The lack of response to dopaminergic therapy in this case favored a parkinsonian syndrome secondary to FTD-MND pathology rather than idiopathic Parkinson’s disease [42, 45].

Differential diagnoses considered included Alzheimer’s disease, corticobasal syndrome, progressive supranuclear palsy, and primary psychiatric disorders. However, the combination of early behavioral changes, motor neuron signs, and neuroimaging findings strongly favored FTD-MND overlap.

Behavioral symptom management in FTD remains challenging, with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and antipsychotics often employed to mitigate disinhibition, irritability, and psychosis [48, 49]. The remarkable response to Sertraline, trazodone, and olanzapine in our patient emphasizes the importance of tailored psychopharmacological strategies alongside non-pharmacological interventions. Speech and swallowing dysfunction are common in FTD-MND and contribute significantly to morbidity, necessitating early involvement of speech therapy and nutritional support to prevent aspiration and malnutrition [3, 5, 44].

Limitations

This case report, while comprehensive, has inherent limitations that should be acknowledged. First, Genetic testing, including screening for *C9orf72* hexanucleotide repeat expansion, was not performed due to limited availability and financial constraints. This represents a critical limitation, particularly given the family history of Parkinsonism and the presence of motor neuron involvement. Additionally, although neuroimaging and electrophysiological assessments provided valuable diagnostic insights, the lack of advanced biomarkers—such as cerebrospinal fluid neurofilament light chain (NfL) or TDP-43 assays—precludes a more precise correlation between clinical presentation and pathological processes. Cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers for Alzheimer’s disease (Aβ42, total tau, and phosphorylated tau) were not obtained. The absence of Alzheimer’s disease biomarkers represents a major limitation, as frontal variants of Alzheimer’s disease could not be definitively excluded. However, the overall clinical presentation, behavioral profile, and neuropsychological findings were more consistent with behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia. In addition, advanced molecular imaging, such as amyloid or tau PET, was not available, which further limits pathological confirmation of the underlying neurodegenerative process. Longitudinal follow-up and clinicopathological correlation would be required to strengthen diagnostic certainty. The single-case nature of this report limits generalizability, and longitudinal follow-up data are necessary to fully understand disease progression and response to multidisciplinary interventions. Finally, therapeutic responses were assessed over a relatively short period, limiting conclusions regarding long-term efficacy and safety of the pharmacological regimen.

Future considerations

Future research should prioritize the integration of genetic and molecular diagnostics into clinical evaluation protocols for patients presenting with overlapping FTD and MND syndromes, to enhance diagnostic accuracy and enable personalized management strategies. The development and validation of accessible biomarkers for early detection and monitoring—such as plasma or CSF neurofilament light chains and TDP-43 species—are critical to advance both clinical care and therapeutic trials. Longitudinal, multicenter studies with larger cohorts are warranted to better characterize the natural history, prognostic indicators, and optimal symptomatic treatments of the FTD-MND spectrum. Investigating the efficacy and safety of emerging disease-modifying therapies, including antisense oligonucleotides targeting C9orf72 and immunotherapies against pathological protein aggregates, represents a promising frontier. Lastly, greater emphasis on multidisciplinary care models incorporating neurology, psychiatry, rehabilitation, speech therapy, and nutritional support will be essential to improve quality of life and functional outcomes in this challenging patient population.

Conclusion

Frontotemporal dementia overlapping with probable motor neuron disease constitutes a diagnostically challenging and pathophysiologically intricate neurodegenerative continuum marked by the convergence of profound behavioral, cognitive, and motor dysfunctions. This case exemplifies the imperative for meticulous, multidisciplinary diagnostic frameworks integrating comprehensive clinical evaluation, advanced neuroimaging modalities, and electrophysiological confirmation to delineate this overlap syndrome accurately. In the absence of curative treatments, therapeutic strategies remain predominantly supportive and symptom-targeted, necessitating a more coordinated, multidisciplinary approach that encompasses neurologic, psychiatric, rehabilitative, and nutritional expertise to optimize patient outcomes. Ongoing elucidation of the molecular and genetic substrates—particularly the role of TDP-43 pathology and C9orf72 expansions—promises to inform future disease-modifying interventions. Furthermore, proactive involvement of caregivers and tailored supportive care pathways are indispensable to enhance quality of life and mitigate the multifaceted burdens associated with this complex clinical entity.
