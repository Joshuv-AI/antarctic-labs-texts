Prospective associations of long COVID with sleep health nearly 3 years after SARS-CoV-2 infection: a statewide representative cohort study
Authors: Soomin Ryu, Elizabeth M Slocum, Blair Whittington, Luis Zavala Arciniega, Sameera Ahmed, Nancy L Fleischer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesWhile many adults with long COVID experience sleep problems, the long-term relationship between long COVID and sleep remains poorly understood. We investigated how long COVID is prospectively associated with sleep duration, sleep quality, and sleep disturbance using a population-based cohort of Michigan adults with COVID-19 (N = 2406).MethodsLong COVID was defined at baseline as reporting a recovery time of 90 days or more after the initial infection and sleep outcomes were assessed at follow-up 1 and 2, approximately 1.5 and 3 years after the initial infection. We estimated linear and multinomial logistic regression models with sleep duration as continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Then, we conducted multinomial logistic regression models for sleep quality and modified Poisson regression for moderate-to-severe sleep disturbance.ResultsLong COVID was prospectively associated with a shorter sleep duration by 0.35 hours (95% CI = −0.53% to −0.17%) at follow-up 1. Relative to sleeping 6−9 hours, long COVID was associated with sleeping less than 6 hours at follow-up 1 (adjusted relative risk ratio [aRRR]: 3.27; 95% CI = 2.16% to 4.96%) and follow-up 2 (aRRR: 1.91; 95% CI = 1.28% to 2.85%). Additionally, long COVID had a strong association with poor-to-very poor sleep quality at both follow-up periods relative to very good-to-fair sleep quality at both follow-up periods. Long COVID was also associated with a 1.53 times higher risk of moderate-to-severe sleep disturbance (95% CI = 1.23% to 1.91%).ConclusionLong COVID was prospectively associated with unhealthy sleep outcomes 3 years after onset. There is a need to enhance sleep health among individuals with long COVID.Keywords: long COVID, sleep duration, sleep disturbance, sleep quality, cohort, COVID-19

Introduction

Nearly 3 in 10 US adults who have had COVID have experienced post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection (PASC or long COVID) [1], which is defined as having a chronic condition after SARS-CoV-2 infection that is present for at least 3 months [2]. Long COVID has negatively affected individuals’ daily activities, mental and physical health, and sleep behavior [3–6]. Sleep health is particularly crucial as it is closely related to metabolic and emotional regulation, immune defense, and memory and brain function [7, 8]. Individuals with sleep disorders, including sleep disturbance and short or long sleeping, are likely to experience cognitive deficits and a reduced quality of life in the short term, as well as chronic diseases and mortality in the long term [9–15]. Therefore, it is critical to investigate the prospective relationship between long COVID and sleep health in the general population using a probability-based cohort study.

A growing body of research suggests that individuals with long COVID report several sleep problems, including sleep disorders and insomnia [3, 4, 16–21]. Using data from 27 studies, a systematic review found that the pooled prevalence of sleep disorder was 45% among individuals with long COVID (though definitions were inconsistent across studies) [4]. An international study of 13 628 adults from 16 countries, including the USA, showed that fatigue, insomnia, and excessive daytime sleepiness were the most common symptoms of long COVID lasting for at least 3 months, regardless of the severity of acute COVID-19 illness [16]. Moreover, a small cross-sectional study of adults with COVID-19 in Italy suggested that individuals with long COVID lasting for at least 3 months reported worse sleep quality, more frequent naps, greater daytime sleepiness, sleep inertia, insomnia, sleep apnea, and nightmares than those without long COVID [22]. However, current evidence on the relationship between long COVID and sleep is limited by inconsistent definitions of long COVID [3, 4], a small number of study participants [17–20, 22, 23], non-probability-based samples [17–23], or short follow-up durations [4, 18, 22].

To address these gaps in the literature, we examined the prospective associations of long COVID with sleep duration, sleep quality, and sleep disturbance nearly 3 years after initial COVID-19 onset using a population-based cohort study of Michigan adults who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2. We hypothesized that long COVID would be prospectively associated with unhealthy sleep duration, poor sleep quality, and disrupted sleep. This study contributes to our knowledge of the long-term effects of COVID-19 illness on population sleep health by using a population-based probability cohort sample.

Methods

Data

This study used baseline and follow-up data from the Michigan COVID-19 Recovery Surveillance Study (MI CReSS), a statewide representative survey of noninstitutionalized adults with a polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 test in Michigan. The eligibility criteria included (1) having a PCR-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection reported in the Michigan Disease Surveillance System (MDSS) between March 2020 and May 2022, (2) having a valid phone number and geographic information (county and/or zip code) in the MDSS, and (3) being alive at the time of the survey. All the respondents were invited to the study via mailed letters and completed the survey over the phone with a trained interviewer in English, Spanish, or Arabic, or online in English. Informed consent was obtained from all the respondents. The survey methods are described in detail elsewhere [24].

Respondents completed baseline surveys between June 2020 and December 2022, with a median time from COVID-19 illness onset to survey completion of 4.4 months (interquartile range [IQR]: 3.4–5.7 months). The first follow-up surveys were completed between January 2022 and October 2023, with a median time from illness onset to survey completion of 18.4 months (IQR: 15.0–21.3 months), and the second follow-up surveys were conducted between September 2023 and February 2025 (median time: 34.9 months; IQR: 31.3–39.4 months). A total of 5521 adults completed the baseline survey (response rate: 32.1%). Of these, 4100 adults completed the first follow-up survey (response rate: 80.5%), and 3667 completed the second follow-up survey (response rate: 78.0%) (American Association for Public Opinion Research response rate #6) [25].

The data analysis included respondents who completed baseline and both follow-up surveys. Of the 3243 individuals who completed all three surveys, we excluded those with missing information on long COVID at baseline (n = 158), sleep health outcomes at either follow-up (n = 536), vaccination uptake at the first follow-up (n = 16), and other covariates at baseline (n = 120). We additionally excluded seven surveys completed by proxy respondents at any survey due to mental capacity issues or other reasons. Our analytic sample was N = 2406 (Figure 1). The University of Michigan Institutional Review Board deemed this study exempt due to the use of secondary deidentified data.

Long COVID

We measured long COVID at baseline, which was defined as reporting a COVID-19 recovery time of at least 90 days or more after initial COVID-19 onset consistent with the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM)’s long COVID definition [2]. To measure illness duration, respondents were asked, “Have you recovered from COVID-19 to your usual state of health?” If respondents had recovered, they were further asked, “How long did it take you to recover?” For respondents who had not yet recovered or had missing information on recovery duration, we calculated the time elapsed between their COVID-19 onset date and the baseline survey completion date to approximate their minimum illness duration. Respondents were then categorized into two groups based on a 90-day recovery threshold: those who recovered within 90 days were considered not to have long COVID, while those who either had not recovered or took 90 days or more to recover were considered to have long COVID. Respondents who had not yet recovered but had a minimum illness duration of less than 90 days were coded as missing.

Sleep health

The main sleep outcomes are daily sleep duration, sleep quality, and sleep disturbance. We measured daily sleep duration at the first follow-up (median 18 months after COVID-19 onset) and at the second follow-up (median 35 months after COVID-19 onset) by asking respondents how many hours of sleep they get in a 24-hour period on average [26]. Using responses ranging from 2 to 18 hours, we created a continuous and a categorical variable of sleep duration (<6 hours, 6–9 hours [reference], >9 hours) based on prior studies that have found that sleeping less than 6 hours or more than 9 hours is considered unhealthy [27, 28].

We assessed sleep quality at the first and second follow-up. We generated a binary variable of sleep quality (1 = poor or very poor, 0 = fair, good, or very good), using the first question from the Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Sleep Disturbance short form, “In the past 7 days, was your sleep quality very poor, poor, fair, good, or very good?” Then, we created a four-category variable to measure sleep quality across different two follow-up points: (1) very good-to-fair sleep quality at both follow-ups (reference), (2) poor-to-very poor sleep quality only at the first follow-up, (3) poor-to-very poor sleep quality only at the second follow-up, and (4) poor-to-very poor sleep quality at both follow-ups.

Finally, sleep disturbance was assessed only at the second follow-up as this measure was not included in the first follow-up survey questionnaire. We used the four-item scale of the PROMIS Sleep Disturbance short form, which has good reliability and construct validity [29, 30]. The first item measures sleep quality, “In the past 7 days, was your sleep quality very poor, poor, fair, good, or very good?” The other items are as follows: “My sleep was refreshing”, with response options ranging from 1 (very much) to 5 (not at all), and “I had a problem with my sleep” and “I had difficulty falling sleep”, both with response options ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very much). Higher scores indicate more disrupted sleep, with the total scores ranging from 4 to 20 (α = 0.85). PROMIS recommends converting the total raw score into a standardized T-score using conversion tables and classifying these T-scores into four categories (none to slight, mild, moderate, and severe) [31, 32]. We further categorized sleep disturbance into a binary variable (0 = none to mild, 1 = moderate to severe).

Covariates

We included several sociodemographic and clinical factors measured at baseline as covariates: age (18–34, 35–54, 55–64, ≥65), sex at birth (male, female), race and ethnicity (Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, another non-Hispanic race or ethnicity), marital status (married/cohabiting, not married/cohabiting), educational attainment (high school or less, some college, college graduate), annual household income (<$35 000, $35 000–74 999, ≥$75000), health insurance status (private insurance, Medicare/Medicaid/another type, none), an indicator for pre-existing diagnosed physical comorbidities (presence of any chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, hypertension, liver disease, kidney disease, cerebrovascular disease, cancer, immunosuppressive condition, autoimmune condition, and/or physical disability), an indicator for pre-existing diagnosed psychological or psychiatric condition, and hospitalization for COVID-19. Additionally, we included census-tract-level residential rurality (metropolitan, nonmetropolitan) at baseline based on the Rural–Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes [33]. We also adjusted for COVID-19 vaccination uptake (yes, no) using the question asked at the first follow-up, “Have you ever had the COVID-19 vaccine?” We further controlled for survey-related factors at baseline, such as mode of completion (phone, online) and pandemic phase based on the COVID-19 onset date (March 2020–September 2020, October 2020–February 2021, March 2021–September 2021, and October 2021–May 2022).

Statistical analysis

First, we presented demographic and health characteristics of the analytic sample overall. Second, we conducted linear and multinomial logistic regression models using sleep duration at the first and second follow-ups as a continuous and categorical variable, respectively. Third, we conducted multinomial logistic regression models using poor-to-very poor sleep quality as a four-category variable measured at different follow-up points. Finally, we used modified Poisson regression models with robust standard errors to estimate the prospective association between long COVID and moderate-to-severe sleep disturbance at the second follow-up. For all the analyses, we used longitudinal sampling weights to match the weighted distribution of our sample to the age and sex distribution of the sampling frame in each geographic region at baseline and to account for attrition and nonresponse at both follow-ups [34]. As missingness of household income was greater than 10%, we imputed missing household income under a missing at random assumption using a weighted sequential hot-deck method [35] and hot deck propensity score imputation [36]. All the statistical analyses were completed using Stata, version 18 [37]. The study method and results are reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cohort studies.

Results

In our analytic sample, approximately 15% of all the respondents had long COVID at baseline (Table 1). The average sleep duration was 7.0 hours at the first follow-up and 6.9 hours at the second follow-up. Additionally, 8.2%–10.5% of the respondents reported sleeping fewer than 6 hours, and 6.8%–7% reported sleeping more than 9 hours. By categories of sleep quality, 70.6% of the respondents reported very good-to-fair sleep quality at both follow-ups, 10% reported poor-to-very poor sleep quality only at the first follow-up, 10% reported poor-to-very poor sleep quality only at the second follow-up, and 9.5% reported poor-to-very poor at both follow-ups. At the second follow-up, 78.5% of the respondents reported experiencing either no sleep disturbance or only slight disturbance, while 13.2% reported experiencing mild sleep disturbance, 7.4% moderate disturbance, and 0.9% severe disturbance.

With adjustment for covariates, long COVID was also associated with a shorter sleep duration by 0.35 hours (21 minutes) per day (95% CI = −0.53% to −0.17%) at the first follow-up (Table 2). However, the negative association was not statistically significant at the second follow-up (−0.16 hours, CI: −0.38 to 0.06). Using a categorical sleep duration variable, we found that long COVID was associated with a higher risk of sleeping fewer than 6 hours relative to sleeping 6–9 hours (Table 3). Compared to sleeping 6–9 hours, individuals with long COVID had a 3.27 times higher risk of sleeping fewer than 6 hours (95% CI = 2.16% to 4.96%) at the first follow-up in the adjusted model. At the second follow-up, long COVID was associated with a 1.91 times higher risk of sleeping fewer than 6 hours (95% CI = 1.28% to 2.85%). There was no statistically significant association between long COVID and sleeping more than 9 hours relative to sleeping 6–9 hours when adjusting for covariates.

Discussion

This study examined prospective associations between long COVID and sleep health at two time points, approximately 1.5 and 3 years after initial COVID-19 onset, using a population-based cohort study of Michigan adults with PCR-confirmed COVID-19. Using a continuous sleep duration variable, we found that long COVID was associated with a shorter sleep duration at the first follow-up; however, this association was not maintained at the second follow-up. When using a three-category sleep duration variable, long COVID was prospectively associated with a higher risk of sleeping fewer than 6 hours at both follow-ups relative to sleeping 6–9 hours. Additionally, long COVID at baseline was associated with a higher risk of reporting poor-to-very poor sleep quality at both follow-up points relative to reporting very good-to-fair sleep quality at both follow-ups. Furthermore, relative to very good-to-fair sleep quality, the magnitude of the association between long COVID and poor-to-very poor sleep at both follow-ups was stronger compared with the associations between long COVID and poor-to-very poor sleep only at the first follow-up or only at the second follow-up. This finding may suggest a persistent relationship between long COVID and poor sleep quality over time. Finally, we found that long COVID was prospectively associated with a higher risk of reporting moderate-to-severe sleep disturbance nearly 3 years after initial COVID-19 onset.

Our findings align with previous literature that examined the negative impact of long COVID on sleep health. Some studies showed that individuals with long COVID reported a range of sleep-related symptoms, including fatigue, insomnia, excessive daytime sleepiness [16], and poor sleep quality [18]. Our results for sleep disturbance and sleep quality are consistent with a small study of Italian adults with COVID-19 surveyed during May–November 2021: individuals with long COVID, defined as having at least two symptoms lasting at least 3 months, experienced worse sleep quality, greater daytime sleepiness, sleep inertia, insomnia, and nightmares compared to individuals without long COVID (with none or one long-lasting symptom) [22]. Another retrospective study among Hungarian adults who had COVID-19 at least 4 weeks prior found that those with long COVID (experiencing at least one COVID-19 associated symptom) reported poorer sleep quality compared to those without long COVID [23]. Our result for sleep duration aligns with an observational study in the USA that found adults with long COVID had lower total sleep duration compared to matched controls who never had COVID-19 [19]. The current study contributes to the literature by using a population-based probability cohort sample of adults who were followed for almost 3 years and examining several sleep health outcomes.

Multiple mechanisms may connect long COVID and sleep health. For example, psychosocial factors, such as social isolation or stigma due to COVID-19 infection, have been linked to increased vulnerability to stress and may result in poor sleep outcomes [22]. Having persistent symptoms for at least 3 months may be considered a stressful event for some adults, which may activate the sympathetic nervous system and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, leading to hyperarousal and dysfunctional sleep behaviors [38, 39]. Furthermore, it has been documented that physical complications from chronic medical conditions are possible causes of poor sleep health [40–42]. Individuals with long COVID experience diverse symptoms, with fatigue, dyspnea, pain, and headache being the most commonly reported [43]. Having these symptoms for more than 90 days might have disrupted sleep among individuals with long COVID.

The association between long COVID and unhealthy sleep duration raises a public health concern as short sleepers are at increased risk of morbidity and mortality. Sleeping fewer than 6 hours is associated with a higher risk of diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, coronary heart diseases, obesity, and all-cause mortality [9, 10, 12, 13, 15, 44]. Furthermore, prior literature has demonstrated poor sleep quality and sleep disturbance are risk factors for worse mental health, diabetes, coronary heart disease, and mortality [45–48]. Therefore, it is essential to promote healthy sleep duration and sleep quality among adults with long COVID.

This study has limitations to be acknowledged. First, our sample may have been subject to selection bias given that the analysis is restricted to adults with a positive PCR test for SARS-CoV-2 recorded in the MDSS with valid contact and geographic information, and who were alive when the surveys were conducted. Second, causal inference is limited due to the nature of the observational study. Third, both long COVID and sleep health outcomes were self-reported. Self-reported sleep duration may not align with objectively measured sleep duration [49]. Fourth, we were not able to adjust for pre-existing sleep problems before COVID-19 onset or at baseline. Prior studies indicate that sleep disturbances, insomnia, poor sleep quality, and short sleep duration are potential risk factors for long COVID [50–52]. Therefore, pre-existing sleep disorders could have influenced the observed associations between long COVID and sleep outcomes, potentially leading to an overestimation of these relationships. Fifth, the study sample may have response bias. For example, adults who had serious sleep problems or severe ongoing long COVID might not have been able to provide accurate responses. Finally, the response rate at the baseline survey was 32.1%. However, a nonresponse bias analysis suggested minimal bias in our weighted baseline sample [24].

This study provides evidence on the prospective associations between long COVID and sleep outcomes using a population-based cohort study. Long COVID was prospectively associated with unhealthy sleep duration, poor sleep quality, and sleep disturbance nearly 3 years after initial COVID-19 onset. Future prospective studies should use objective sleep measurements such as polysomnography or actigraphy to continuously examine the longer-term relationship between long COVID and sleep health. In addition, future research should investigate how specific long COVID symptoms—such as fatigue, cognitive dysfunction, or respiratory difficulties—are differentially associated with sleep outcomes. Furthermore, researchers need to identify protective factors to improve sleep duration, sleep quality, and sleep disturbance following experiences of long COVID. As poor sleep is a risk factor for multiple chronic conditions and mortality [53–56], medical professionals need to monitor sleep outcomes among individuals who experience long COVID and find strategies to enhance their sleep health.
