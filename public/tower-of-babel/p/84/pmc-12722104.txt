Evaluation of GABAergic and Glutamatergic Levels in the Dorsal Anterior Cingulate Cortex of Patients With Bipolar Disorder: A Cross‐Sectional 1H‐MRS Study
Authors: Sotaro Moriyama, Yoshihiro Noda, Shiori Honda, Richard A. E. Edden, Koki Takahashi, Keisuke Saito, Keita Taniguchi, Masataka Wada, Sakiko Tsugawa, Yui Tobari, Sunjun Huh, Yumina Nakane, Saki Homma, Mie Matsui, Shinsuke Koike, Masaru Mimura, Hiroyuki Uchida, Shinichiro Nakajima
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundImbalances between excitatory and inhibitory (E/I) neurotransmitters in the brain may contribute to the pathophysiology of bipolar disorder (BD). Proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H‐MRS) measures glutamatergic neurometabolites and gamma‐aminobutyric acid (GABA) as indices of E/I neurotransmission. Previous meta‐analyses reported higher glutamatergic neurometabolite levels in the medial prefrontal cortex of BD, but findings on GABA levels in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) of BD patients remain inconsistent.MethodsWe conducted a cross‐sectional study using 1H‐MRS to compare GABA and glutamate plus glutamine (Glx) levels in the dorsal ACC (dACC) of 27 patients with BD and 27 age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls (HCs). We used the 1H‐MRS (3 T MRI, MEGAPRESS, 256 averages, TR = 1500 ms, TE = 68 ms). Clinical symptoms were evaluated using standardized rating scales. Statistical analyses were conducted to assess group differences in neurometabolite levels and to explore the associations between these neurometabolite levels and clinical symptom severity within the BD group.ResultsNo significant differences in dACC GABA levels, Glx levels, or Glx/GABA ratio were observed between the BD and HC groups. There were no significant differences within the BD group based on BD subtype (I or II) or clinical phase (depression or euthymic). Patients with lithium treatment had higher GABA levels compared to those without.DiscussionOur results suggest that GABAergic function in the dACC may not play a pivotal role in the pathophysiology of BD. Given possible GABAergic dysregulation in BD, further research using alternative methodologies is warranted to elucidate the pathological basis.

Introduction

Bipolar disorder (BD) affects approximately 1% of the global population and is characterized by recurrent manic, hypomanic, and depressive episodes. BD is also associated with a 10–20 year reduction in life expectancy [1], with a suicide rate 20–30 times higher than that of the general population [2]. Diagnosis typically takes 6–10 years [3]. Conventional treatments see only 58.4% of patients achieve remission within 2 years, with around 50% experiencing recurrence [4]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to uncover the pathophysiology of BD to facilitate early diagnosis and the development of novel therapies [5, 6, 7].

The excitatory and inhibitory (E/I) imbalance in the brain may be implicated in the pathophysiology of BD [6, 7]. Neurons are broadly classified into excitatory and inhibitory types, which control the flow of information within neural networks [8, 9]. Glutamate serves as the primary excitatory neurotransmitter, while gamma‐aminobutyric acid (GABA) serves as the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter. For example, a recent systematic review noted neurophysiological impairments in cortical E/I indices measured with positron emission tomography [10], transcranial magnetic stimulation neurophysiology [6], postmortem samples [11], and genetics [12] in patients with BD compared to healthy controls (HCs).

Among the various methods for assessing neurometabolites, proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H‐MRS) is the only neuroimaging technique that can directly and non‐invasively measure glutamate and GABA levels in the brain in vivo [13]. A host of studies have suggested glutamatergic system dysfunction in BD [14]. Of note, a meta‐analysis (40 studies) demonstrated that glutamate plus glutamine (Glx) and glutamine levels in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) were elevated in patients with BD in comparison with HCs [15]. On the contrary, there is limited evidence on GABA levels measured with 1H‐MRS in patients with BD. A few MRS studies (k = 8) have specifically measured GABA levels in the brain of patients with BD [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. These studies targeted different brain regions, mainly the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), but yielded inconsistent findings. For example, some studies focusing on the dorsal ACC (dACC) reported no significant differences in GABA levels between BD patients and healthy controls [18, 20, 21]. In contrast, other studies observed altered GABA levels in BD patients, such as an increased GABA/creatine ratio in the ACC and significantly decreased absolute GABA concentrations in the medial frontal cortex [16]. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in measurement techniques, patient characteristics, and voxel placement within the ACC region (Figure 1).

Previous studies have reported that Glx levels may vary across different mood states in BD, such as during depressive or euthymic episodes [15, 24]. These findings raise the possibility that Glx might reflect mood‐related changes; however, it remains unclear whether such changes indicate state‐ or trait‐dependent characteristics. Similarly, the interpretation of GABA levels as either state or trait markers is limited due to the scarcity and inconsistency of existing evidence. Notably, GABA concentrations are known to be influenced by various biological and demographic factors, including sex, age, and medication. Given these complexities, this study does not aim to classify GABA or Glx as state or trait markers, but rather to explore how these neurometabolite levels vary according to clinical and demographic characteristics within the BD population.

This cross‐sectional study aimed to compare GABA and Glx levels in the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC) measured with 1H‐MRS between patients with BD and HCs. The dACC is a subregion of the ACC located in the medial frontal lobe. It is involved in cognitive control, attention, decision‐making, error detection, and reward processing, and plays an important role in the integration of emotion and cognition [25]. We selected the dACC as a region of interest because these functions are highly relevant to the symptoms of BD, and functional and structural abnormalities of the dACC have been suggested to contribute to the pathophysiology of BD [21]. We were particularly motivated by a prior meta‐analysis [15] that reported elevated Glx levels in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) of patients with bipolar disorder (BD). Building on these findings, we focused specifically on the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), a subregion of the ACC implicated in emotional integration [26], and investigated the possibility that GABA levels in the dACC may serve as a sensitive marker of BD pathophysiology. To this end, we broadly recruited patients with bipolar disorder to explore their neurophysiological characteristics using GABA measurements. However, because the study was conducted in a university hospital setting where patients presenting with manic symptoms are relatively rare, individuals with manic episodes were not included. Consequently, our sample consisted of patients in either depressive or euthymic states.

To assess GABA concentrations, we employed the MEGA‐PRESS technique, which is widely recognized for its high reliability in measuring GABA. To our knowledge, this is the first study to measure GABA in the ACC of BD patients using MEGA‐PRESS. Notably, previous MRS studies targeting the dACC have primarily examined patients in a euthymic state, with individuals experiencing bipolar depression largely excluded. By including patients with bipolar depression, our study expands the clinical spectrum under investigation.

In addition, we conducted a comprehensive assessment of baseline clinical characteristics, including neuropsychological functioning and detailed medication history. As commonly prescribed pharmacological treatments for BD—such as lithium and anticonvulsants—are believed to modulate GABAergic neurotransmission, we hypothesized that GABA levels may reflect state‐dependent neurochemical changes influenced by both clinical and pharmacological factors. Based on this hypothesis, we also explored the associations between GABA and Glx levels and the severity of clinical symptoms within the BD group. Previous studies have suggested that Glx levels may be elevated in individuals with bipolar depression and may correlate with depressive symptom severity [15, 24]. However, evidence regarding the association between GABA levels and symptom severity in BD remains limited, with only a few studies addressing this relationship and reporting inconsistent findings. Therefore, we aimed to contribute to this literature by examining these associations using a robust MRS protocol and detailed clinical characterization.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

The present cross‐sectional study was conducted at Keio University Hospital between 2017 and 2024. The study protocol (UMIN000028863) was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Keio University School of Medicine. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were also approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Tokyo for MRI scans (Approval No. 23–567). All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients with BD were recruited from Keio University Hospital, Tokyo, Japan, and through online advertisements. Age‐ and sex‐matched HCs were recruited from the general population. The study included patients with BD if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) a diagnosis of BD as defined by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‐5) [27], (2) aged 18 years or older, and (3) receiving regular clinical treatment at Keio University Hospital. They were excluded if they had: (1) a history of substance use disorders within the previous 6 months, (2) any contraindication for MRI scan, (3) an unstable physical illness or neurological condition, or (4) a history of convulsive seizures or epilepsy. The screening of HCs was carried out by three certified psychiatrists (Y.N., S.N., and M.W.) to confirm the absence of a history of psychiatric disorders through the administration of the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM Disorders, which served as the inclusion criterion for this cohort. The participants in each group were matched for age and sex. In this study, the sample size was provisionally set at n = 60, based on similar prior studies, as few studies have previously tested the same hypotheses as this study [28].

Clinico‐Demographic Assessments

The medical history, years of education, and other relevant clinical data were obtained through the administration of structured interviews. Clinical symptoms were assessed using the Montgomery Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) [29] for depressive symptoms, the Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) [30] for manic symptoms, the Japanese Adult Reading Test (JART) [31] for premorbid IQ, and the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS) [32, 33] for cognitive functioning, and the Executive Interview 25 (EXIT25) [34] for assessing executive functioning.

MRI Processing

All participants were scanned using a 3 T Siemens Prisma scanner equipped with a 32‐channel head coil (Siemens Healthineers AG, Erlangen, Germany) at the Center for Evolutionary Cognitive Sciences, the University of Tokyo. For anatomical referencing and tissue segmentation, a T1‐weighted magnetization‐prepared MRI scan and gradient echo images were acquired with the following parameters (echo time [TE] = 2.22 ms, repetition time [TR] = 2400 ms, inversion time = 1000 ms, flip angle = 8°, field of view = 240 mm, 320 × 320 matrix, slice thickness = 0.80 mm, voxel matrix = 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 mm).

1H‐MRS Acquisition, Quantification, and Quality

1H‐MRS data were acquired using the MEGA‐PRESS technique (TE = 68 ms, TR = 1500 ms, data points = 2048, 256 water‐suppressed and 16 water‐unsuppressed averages, editing RF pulse width = 14.4 ms applied at 1.9 [on scans] and 7.5 ppm [off scans], VAriable Power and Optimized Relaxations delays [VAPOR] water suppression, and interleaved unsuppressed water reference) [35, 36, 37, 38]. A 1H‐MRS voxel was positioned in bilateral dACC with a voxel size of 24 mL (20 × 40 × 30 mm), and GABA + macromolecule levels were fitted in the spectral range between 2.79 and 3.55 ppm and quantified as water‐scaled values using Gannet version 3.2.0 (The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA) [39]. T1‐weighted images were segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Gannet was utilized to create a voxel mask on the segmented T1‐weighted image, using the scanner's spatial coordinates. A partial‐volume correction was implemented to quantify the metabolite concentration relative to the fully relaxed water peak, considering the volume fractions, water relaxation times, and water concentrations of the three compartments (GM, WM, and CSF). The neurometabolite levels of interest in this study were GABA+ and Glx, expressed in institutional units. Quality assessment was done by visual inspection and by using a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the unsuppressed water resonance. GABA and Glx fit errors were used for assessing modeling errors, with values > 15% identified as substandard and hence excluded from subsequent analyses. The FWHM and GABA and Glx fit errors of the spectra (mean (±SD) [range]) included in this analysis were as follows: FWHM (Hz), 8.1 (2.1) [6.9–11.4]; GABA fit error (%), 6.5 (2.3) [3.5–10.8]; Glx fit error (%), 6.1 (2.4) [2.9–11.4].

Statistical Analyses

R version 4.2.2 was used as statistical analysis software. For the characteristics, we applied chi‐square tests for categorical variables and t‐tests for continuous variables between the BD and HC groups. Smoking history was the only factor showing a significant difference between the groups (p < 0.05).

We conducted the Shapiro–Wilk tests to assess the normality of the data distribution for GABA levels, Glx levels, and Glx/GABA ratios. The GABA and Glx levels were normally distributed, whereas the Glx/GABA ratio was non‐normally distributed. A t‐test was conducted to compare GABA and Glx levels between the BD and HC groups. The Mann–Whitney U‐test was used to compare Glx/GABA ratios between the two groups. Common covariates from prior studies included age, sex, and years of education [40, 41, 42]. Based on the intergroup comparison, smoking history was added to these covariates, resulting in a total of four covariates. The analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was subsequently performed on the normally distributed GABA and Glx levels, and Quade's ANCOVA was used for the non‐normally distributed Glx/GABA ratio. Covariates were incorporated both individually and collectively in the analysis.

As a secondary analysis, t‐tests were used to assess group differences in GABA and Glx levels within the BD group based on clinical characteristics. Likewise, the Glx/GABA ratio was compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Subgroup comparisons within the BD group revealed significant differences in neurometabolite levels when stratified by sex and lithium treatment status. Using these variables as covariates, ANCOVA was applied to GABA and Glx levels, and Quade's ANCOVA was applied to the Glx/GABA ratio, with covariates included both individually and collectively.

Additionally, correlation analyses were conducted within the patient group to investigate associations between neurometabolite levels, symptom severity, and cognitive function. Symptom severity was evaluated using the Montgomery‐Åsberg Depression Rating Scale and the Young Mania Rating Scale, while cognitive function was assessed through the Japanese Adult Reading Test for premorbid IQ and the Executive Interview 25 for executive functioning. Pearson's correlation coefficient was used for variables with a normal distribution, and Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was applied for non‐normally distributed variables. The significance level for all statistical tests was set at 0.05, and Bonferroni correction was applied in post hoc analyses to account for multiple comparisons.

Results

Participants

This study included 60 participants: 30 patients with BD and 30 age‐ and sex‐matched HCs. Due to low spectral quality, three patients with BD and three HCs were excluded. Data from the remaining 54 participants (27 patients with BD and 27 HCs) were analyzed (Table 1). One patient failed to undergo the psychological tests; however, their MRS data, age, sex, and diagnosis were included in the analysis. As a result, we analyzed data from 27 patients with BD (average age: 50.2 ± 16.1 years, females: n = 10 [37%]; type 1: n = 12 [44%]; depression: n = 7 [26%]; and euthymic: n = 20 [74%]; and lithium intake: n = 20 [74%]) and 27 age and sex‐matched HCs. Smoking history was more prevalent in the BD group compared to the HC groups.

In addition, we compared GABA and Glx levels between patients in depressive (n = 8) and euthymic (n = 19) states within the BD group. No significant differences were found in GABA or Glx levels between the two mood states.

Correlation Analyses

No significant correlations were found between GABA levels, Glx levels, or Glx/GABA ratio and MADRS or YMRS total scores. To account for potential confounding effects of sex and lithium treatment, we additionally conducted partial correlation analyses between neurometabolite levels and MADRS scores. These analyses also showed no significant associations (Glx: r = 0.104, p = 0.469; GABA: r = 0.007, p = 0.959; Glx/GABA: r = 0.069, p = 0.629) (Figure 4). Glx levels showed a positive correlation with the JART Full Scale Intelligence Quotient (FSIQ) score (r = 0.392, p = 0.004), while Glx (r = −0.441, p = 0.024) and GABA (r = −0.577, p = 0.002) levels presented with a negative correlation with the EXIT25 score. After Bonferroni correction, the positive correlation between Glx levels and JART FSIQ scores, as well as the negative correlation between GABA levels and EXIT25 scores, remained significant.

Discussion

This study compared GABA levels in the dACC of the BD and HC groups using the MEGAPRESS method with a 3T MRI. The participants included patients in depressive or euthymic states and those with BD type I or II. The results showed no significant group differences in dACC Glx levels, GABA levels, or the Glx/GABA ratio. Additionally, secondary analyses found that no significant differences were observed between those with bipolar depression and those with euthymic BD, or between those with BD type I and II. On the other hand, male sex and lithium intake might be associated with higher dACC GABA levels within the BD group. Furthermore, exploratory correlation analyses demonstrated a positive correlation between dACC Glx levels and premorbid IQ, as well as between dACC Glx and GABA levels and executive function. These results suggest that GABAergic function in the dACC may not play a key role in the pathophysiology of BD.

Among the three previous studies focusing on dACC GABA levels in BD, two studies explicitly targeted the dACC [20, 21], while one focused on the ACC but placed the voxel on the dACC upon visual inspection [18]. These studies employed different quantification methods: one study reported uncorrected GABA levels [20], one applied water scaling [21], and the other used creatine normalization [18]. Although creatine correction is considered relatively robust due to its presumed uniform distribution in the brain, some reports have suggested that this uniformity may be compromised in certain conditions, such as psychiatric disorders or aging. Furthermore, creatine correction includes signals from cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) within the voxel, which can affect the accuracy of metabolite estimation. In recent years, water referencing with CSF exclusion has become more widely adopted, as it provides concentrations closer to absolute quantification. Given these methodological considerations, we adopted water‐scaled quantification with partial volume correction to minimize the influence of CSF. In previous studies, different correction methods have been applied, including no correction, water referencing, and creatine referencing. Comparative studies of these approaches have not reached a clear conclusion as to which correction method is preferable [43]. While MRS results can be reliably compared within the same facility, our study was not intended to directly compare absolute values across studies. Therefore, despite methodological heterogeneity, we included three relevant prior studies in our discussion. Importantly, these quantification methods differ in several technical respects, including assumptions about internal reference substances and tissue composition. Thus, direct comparisons between GABA levels measured using creatine normalization and those obtained using CSF‐corrected water referencing, as in our study, should be interpreted with caution. On the other hand, it was reported that results for Glx levels were not consistent between the PRESS and MEGAPRESS methods [44, 45]. This disagreement may be due to the phase evolution of glutamine at the different echo times. A recent meta‐analysis mainly included previous studies employing the PRESS method to examine glutamatergic metabolite levels in patients with BD [15], while we measured Glx levels with MEGAPRESS since a neurometabolite of our primary interest was GABA, which may account for the discrepancy of glutamatergic neurometabolite levels between our study and the result of the meta‐analysis.

In our study, no significant relationships were found between Glx or GABA levels in the dACC and depressive symptoms severity in patients with BD. However, one meta‐analysis reported decreased glutamatergic neurometabolite levels in the medial prefrontal cortex in patients with major depressive disorder [46], while the other meta‐analysis noted an increase in glutamatergic neurometabolite levels in the ACC of patients with BD [15]. Given these findings, the small number of patients with bipolar depression in our study (n = 8, 30%) may have contributed to the no significant correlation observed in this study. To further account for potential confounding factors, we conducted partial correlation analyses controlling for sex and lithium treatment. These analyses also revealed no significant associations between neurometabolite levels and depressive symptom severity, as measured by the MADRS score. This finding suggests that the observed lack of correlation is unlikely to be due to the influence of these demographic or clinical variables.

Although no significant difference in dACC GABA levels was observed between the BD and HC groups, secondary analyses suggested that factors such as lithium intake and sex may influence dACC GABA levels in patients with BD. Previous MRS studies have indicated that lithium treatment may modulate GABAergic neurotransmission and increase brain GABA levels [47]. Although the precise mechanisms remain unclear, several hypotheses have been proposed. Specifically, lithium has been reported to enhance the activity of glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD), thereby promoting the conversion of glutamate to GABA, and to inhibit GABA transaminase (GABA‐T), resulting in reduced GABA degradation [17, 47]. In addition, lithium may alter the excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) balance by attenuating glutamatergic transmission, which indirectly enhances inhibitory tone [48]. In our study, patients receiving lithium also exhibited higher dACC GABA levels. However, as serum lithium levels were not measured and the sample size was relatively small, definitive conclusions cannot be drawn. These findings suggest that GABA levels may reflect lithium‐associated neurochemical changes in BD; nevertheless, further research is needed to establish their potential role as a biomarker for lithium response. It has been noted that GABA levels are different between males and females [49], a finding replicated in our study. Moreover, previous studies have indicated that GABA concentrations fluctuate with the menstrual cycle, with some studies reporting elevated GABA levels during the follicular phase, particularly around ovulation, and decreased levels during the luteal phase [50, 51, 52]. Thus, the fluctuation of GABA concentration within the menstrual cycle may, in part, be attributable to this sex‐related difference in GABA concentrations.

In this study, exploratory correlation analyses revealed a positive association between Glx levels in the dACC and premorbid IQ, as well as positive correlations between both Glx and GABA levels in the dACC and executive function. These findings align with previous studies that have reported associations between neurometabolites in the anterior cingulate cortex and prefrontal cortex and cognitive performance in patients with bipolar disorder (BD). Specifically, higher ACC GABA levels have been linked to better performance on the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST), a measure of executive function, in BD patients [53], and a higher Glx/GABA ratio in the mPFC has been associated with poorer executive performance [22]. Moreover, reduced ACC GABA levels were associated with executive dysfunction in patients with mild cognitive impairment [54], supporting our finding that decreased GABA may contribute to cognitive decline. In contrast, a negative correlation has been reported in healthy individuals, where higher ACC GABA levels were associated with poorer executive performance on the Stroop task [55], suggesting that the relationship between E/I balance and executive function may differ between clinical populations and healthy controls.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the 1H‐MRS measurements employed in this study did not distinguish between GABA and macromolecules, potentially confounding the results and making it difficult to isolate the precise GABA signal. MRS technology cannot differentiate between intracellular and extracellular GABA within the measured voxel, leading to ambiguity in the localization of GABA signals. In addition, Glx concentrations were estimated using the difference (DIFF) spectrum from the MEGA‐PRESS sequence, which has been reported to show lower consistency with standard PRESS‐based Glx quantification [44, 45]. However, other studies have reported minimal differences in Glx quantification between MEGA‐PRESS and PRESS, suggesting that sequence choice alone may not fully explain discrepancies [56]. Second, the proportion of smokers in the HC groups was lower than in the patient group, introducing a potential bias since smoking is known to affect the measured neurometabolite levels [57]. Third, patients in this study were prescribed different psychotropic medications, which could have had varying effects on dACC GABA and Glx levels in the BD group. Fourth, although our facility typically monitors blood lithium levels in patients on lithium therapy, we did not include such monitoring in this study. Moreover, current use of lamotrigine and other mood stabilizers may potentially exert glutamate modulating properties [58, 59]. The lack of detailed data on mood stabilizers limited our interpretation of the effect of lithium intake on GABA and Glx concentrations in dACC in the BD group. Fifth, the cross‐sectional nature of this study prevents us from drawing causal inferences regarding the relationship between GABA levels and lithium intake. Finally, this study focused exclusively on the dACC, which limited the generalizability of the findings to other brain regions that may also play a role in the pathophysiology of BD. Additionally, while we conducted exploratory comparisons within the BD group across mood states (euthymia vs. depression) and diagnostic subtypes (BD‐I vs. BD‐II), the limited sample size may have reduced the statistical power to detect meaningful differences. Future studies with larger and diagnostically stratified cohorts will be necessary to clarify whether distinct neurometabolic patterns exist between BD subtypes or across mood states.

From a clinical perspective, the findings of this study are unlikely to lead directly to clinical applications. This study aimed to examine neurophysiological characteristics of bipolar disorder (BD). Although no significant differences were observed between groups, we identified neurophysiological features related to medication use, sex, and cognitive function. We believe that considering these characteristics can inform and enhance future research.

The observed associations between dACC GABA levels and clinical features such as lithium intake and sex suggest the presence of individual variability in neurochemical profiles. These findings may provide implications for future personalized medicine approaches, where patient subtyping based on neurometabolic characteristics could help tailor treatment strategies. Ultimately, expanding MRS studies with larger, diagnostically stratified samples may contribute to the development of biologically grounded diagnostic tools and the optimization of pharmacotherapy for BD. It is also important to note that this study was conducted during the COVID‐19 pandemic, which considerably limited our ability to increase the sample size as originally planned. In future research, we aim to recruit larger cohorts to enable more robust analyses and allow for stratification by diagnostic subtype and mood state.

Importantly, to our knowledge, this is the first study to apply the MEGA‐PRESS technique specifically to the dACC in patients with bipolar disorder. This novel methodological approach adds value to the existing literature, as it allows for a more targeted investigation of GABAergic dysfunction in a brain region that is central to emotion regulation and cognitive control.

Despite these limitations, this study thoroughly examined GABA and Glx levels within the dACC of patients with BD and HCs using 1H‐MRS measurement. The primary finding revealed no significant differences in dACC GABA or Glx levels between the two groups. Collectively, taken together with previous studies, this study suggests that GABA levels in the dACC may not serve as a distinguishing feature of BD. Given possible GABA dysregulation in BD, further research is required employing other methodologies to examine in vivo GABAergic functions in a non‐invasive manner, such as the combined TMS‐EEG technique [60] as well as positron emission tomography [61].
