Trends in prescription opioid use for pain in Canada: a population-based repeated cross-sectional study of 6 provinces
Authors: Nevena Rebić, Bisola Hamzat, Grace Cheung, Dean T. Eurich, Jean-Luc Kaboré, Louis de Léséleuc, Zhaoyu Liu, Houssem Missaoui, Jason R. Randall, Dana Shearer, David Stock, Joanna Yang, Tara Gomes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:National information on prescription opioid use in Canada is lacking. We sought to examine trends in the incidence and prevalence of prescription opioid dispensing for pain in Canada.Methods:We conducted a population-based repeated cross-sectional analysis of pharmacy dispensing data from 6 provinces, which captured data from around 80% of Canada’s population, between Jan. 1, 2018, and Dec. 31, 2022. We reported monthly population-adjusted rates of new and prevalent recipients of prescription opioids by province and annual provincial population-adjusted rates overall and by age, sex, neighbourhood income quintile, and location of residence. We calculated the proportion of incident recipients receiving guideline-recommended initial opioid doses and prevalent recipients receiving specific opioids indicated for pain (i.e., codeine, hydromorphone, oxycodone, morphine, and fentanyl).Results:Between 2018 and 2022, the annual incidence and prevalence of prescription opioid dispensing declined by 7.9% and 11.1%, respectively, across all provinces, with monthly trends showing that Manitoba consistently had the highest, and British Columbia the lowest, rates of prevalent opioid use. In 2022, we found 1 818 680 incident and 2 770 268 prevalent recipients, with incidence ranging from 55.2 (Ontario) to 63.0 (Alberta) per 1000 population and prevalence ranging from 85.1 (Saskatchewan) to 96.3 (Alberta) per 1000 population. Annual rates were higher among females, older adults, and people living in lower-income neighbourhoods and rural regions of Canada. Initial daily doses greater than 50 mg morphine equivalents declined over time, with provincial and temporal differences in the types of opioids prescribed.Interpretation:Declines in initiation and overall dispensing of prescription opioids for pain between 2018 and 2022 aligned with national efforts to promote appropriate opioid prescribing for acute and chronic noncancer pain in Canada.

Global consumption of prescription opioid analgesics has changed drastically in the last few decades.1,2 In Canada, the use of prescription opioids to treat acute and chronic noncancer pain increased sharply after the turn of the century, with the availability of more potent opioid products and aggressive marketing by pharmaceutical companies influencing prescription practices.3–5 The ensuing rise in opioid-related harms, including nonmedical use and acute toxicities,6–8 led to clinical and policy interventions aimed at regulating prescription opioid access, including national Canadian guidelines for opioid prescribing in 2017.9,10 Other interventions have included reimbursement restrictions under public drug programs, such as those implemented in Ontario in 2017 for high-strength opioid formulations; 11 rescheduling of drug products, such as Manitoba’s 2016 change requiring a prescription for low-dose codeine, unlike its availability over the counter in other provinces;12 and introduction of a tamper-deterrent formulation of long-acting oxycodone (i.e., OxyNeo) in Canada in 2012.13,14 More broadly, prescription monitoring programs across Canada have been strengthened,15,16 and educational initiatives by various agencies — including provincial medical colleges, the College of Family Physicians of Canada, and academic continuing medical education programs — have promoted evidence-informed opioid prescribing and deprescribing practices.17 Together, these initiatives have led to persistent declines in prescription opioid consumption,18–22 including a national decline of 36.8% per 1000 population per day between 2009 and 2019.23 Nonetheless, opioid-related harms have continued to rise across most provinces since the mid-2010s, primarily driven by the introduction of fentanyl and other potent opioids to the unregulated drug supply.24

Although national Canadian data are available on opioid-related harms (e.g., hospital visits for opioid toxicities, opioid-related deaths),24 information on prescription opioid dispensing for pain has been limited to regional analyses.25–27 National estimates have relied on pharmaceutical sales data up to 2020,18,19,21 meaning there is limited information on population exposure to opioid analgesics, patterns of initiation, or variations by geography, demographic characteristics, and time. We aimed to estimate the rate of prescription opioid use for pain in Canada before and during the COVID-19 pandemic and to assess differences by region and recipient characteristics.

Methods

Data sources

We conducted a population-based repeated cross-sectional study examining the incidence and prevalence of prescription opioid use for pain in Canada between Jan. 1, 2018, and Dec. 31, 2022, using pharmacy dispensing data from 6 provinces. Our data sources included the Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI) National Prescription Drug Utilization Information System for British Columbia, Saskatchewan, and Manitoba data; the Alberta Pharmaceutical Information Network for Alberta data; the Narcotics Monitoring System for Ontario data; and the Régie de l’assurance maladie du Québec (RAMQ) for Quebec data. Ontario data sets were linked using unique encoded identifiers and analyzed at ICES, an independent, nonprofit research institute whose legal status under Ontario’s health information privacy law allows it to collect and analyze health care and demographic data, without consent, for health system evaluation and improvement. Apart from RAMQ, these databases capture all provincial claims for prescription opioids dispensed from community pharmacies, regardless of payer. In contrast, RAMQ contains only claims for people covered by Quebec’s public drug insurance plan (i.e., those aged ≥ 65 yr, on social assistance, or without a private insurance plan), representing around 46% of the provincial population.28 Collectively, our data sources captured an estimated 80% of Canada’s population based on Statistics Canada’s provincial population estimates at the study midpoint.29

Opioid prescribing for pain

We used drug identification numbers and province-specific product identification numbers to identify prescription opioids indicated for pain (Appendix 1, eTable 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.250670/tab-related-content). We excluded methadone and buprenorphine-containing products indicated for opioid agonist treatment (i.e., methadone liquid, buprenorphine–naloxone, subcutaneous or implantable buprenorphine)30 and opioids used as antitussives or antidiarrheals. We excluded low-dose codeine products (i.e., ≤ 8 mg of codeine per tablet or ≤ 20 mg of codeine per 30 mL of liquid) to ensure comparable reporting between Manitoba, where these products require a prescription, and other provinces.12 Slow-release oral morphine (i.e., Kadian) was reported separately, as it is used for both pain and opioid agonist treatment.30

Study population

We defined incident recipients as individuals without a claim for any opioids, regardless of indication, dispensed in or having a days’ supply overlapping the 365 days before initial dispensing date (i.e., index date). We defined prevalent recipients as individuals with any claim for an opioid indicated for pain in the period of interest. For all provincial data sets, we excluded individuals with invalid patient identifiers, those with missing or invalid data on age or sex, those older than 105 years, and those residing outside of the province at index. In Quebec, as we had data only for publicly funded medications, we excluded individuals who were not continuously enrolled in the public drug insurance plan 455 days (i.e., 365-day washout and a maximum 90 days’ supply of a previous prescription) before the first day of the period of interest (i.e., month, year) until the end of the period or date of death.

Statistical analysis

We calculated monthly population-adjusted rates of incident and prevalent opioid recipients per 1000 population, stratified by province. Then, we constructed annual cohorts of incident and prevalent recipients in 2018, 2020, and 2022, and calculated provincial population-adjusted rates stratified by age, sex, neighbourhood income quintile, and location of residence. We defined provincial and stratum-specific denominators using Statistics Canada’s publicly posted population estimates29 for provinces with complete dispensing data; for Quebec, we determined denominators by the population enrolled in the province’s public drug insurance plan. Rates were not age- or sex-standardized across provinces. We used the Postal Code Conversion File Plus (PCCF+),31 which matches 6-digit postal codes to census geographies to determine neighbourhood income quintile and location of residence for all provinces except Quebec, where data were not available. As we used population-level administrative data that captured all opioid dispensations, we did not conduct any inferential statistics.

To determine whether newly initiated opioid prescriptions aligned with national guidelines,9,10,32,33 we converted initial daily doses to milligram morphine equivalents (MME; Appendix 1, eTable 2) and calculated the proportion of recipients given an initial daily dose above the maximum recommended dose of 50 MME. Among prevalent recipients, we reported the proportion of specific types of opioids received (i.e., codeine, hydromorphone, oxycodone, morphine, and fentanyl). This study is reported in accordance with the Reporting of Studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-Collected Data (RECORD) checklist.34

Ethics approval

The study protocol and use of CIHI data was approved by the Unity Health Toronto’s Research Ethics Board (no. 23-275). The use of Alberta data was authorized by the University of Alberta Research Ethics Board (no. Pro00083807). The use of Ontario data was authorized under section 45 of Ontario’s Personal Health Information Protection Act, which does not require review by a research ethics board. The use of Quebec data was authorized through a tripartite agreement between the Ministère de la santé et des services sociaux of Québec, the RAMQ, and the Institut national d’excellence en santé et services sociaux, which was approved by the Commission d’accès à l’information du Québec.

Results

Between January 2018 and December 2022, monthly rates of incident opioid recipients declined overall, following relatively similar trends across all studied provinces (Figure 1). A marked short-term decline in opioid initiation occurred between March 2020 and June 2020, reflecting the start of pandemic mitigation measures for COVID-19 and related disruptions in prescription medication dispensing that occurred across Canada. In December 2022, the monthly incidence ranged from 4.7 (Ontario and Saskatchewan) to 5.3 (Alberta) per 1000 population.

Monthly rate of incident use of prescribed opioids for pain in Canada from 2018 to 2022, stratified by province.

Although monthly rates of prevalent opioid recipients declined across all provinces, they were consistently highest in Manitoba and lowest in BC (Figure 2). The short-term decrease early in the COVID-19 pandemic was less pronounced among prevalent recipients than incident recipients. By December 2022, the monthly prevalence ranged from 18.3 (BC) to 26.2 (Manitoba) per 1000 population.

Monthly rate of prevalent use of prescribed opioids for pain in Canada from 2018 to 2022, stratified by province.

We captured data from 1 975 327 incident prescription opioid recipients in 2018, which fell 7.9% to 1 818 680 incident recipients in 2022 (Appendix 1, eTable 3). In 2022, the annual incidence ranged from 55.2 (Ontario) to 63.0 (Alberta) per 1000 population. We observed an 11.1% reduction in prevalent recipients, from 3 114 945 to 2 770 268 recipients between 2018 and 2022. The annual prevalence ranged from 85.1 (Saskatchewan) to 96.3 (Alberta) per 1000 population in 2022. Finally, we found an 86.4% increase in prevalent recipients of slow-release oral morphine, rising from 12 157 to 22 660 recipients between 2018 and 2022 (Appendix 1, eTable 4). Annual rates of prevalent slow-release oral morphine use remained low, ranging from 0.07 (Saskatchewan) to 1.1 (BC) per 1000 population in 2022.

The annual incidence was consistently higher among females (Appendix 1, eFigure 1) and older adults (Figure 3) among all provinces and years studied. In 2022, the incidence ranged from 60.3 (Ontario) to 67.9 (Alberta) per 1000 females, and 50.1 (Ontario) to 58.5 (BC) per 1000 males. Incidence was highest among adults aged 75 years or older, ranging from 92.7 (Manitoba) to 112.1 (BC) per 1000 population in 2022, and among those residing in neighbourhoods in the lowest income quintile, with a right-skewed distribution that was most prominent in 2018 (Table 1). By 2022, rates were similar across income quintiles in Manitoba and Ontario, while higher rates persisted in the lowest income quintiles in other provinces, particularly Alberta, where incident use was more than double in neighbourhoods in the lowest (90.2 per 1000) compared with those in the highest (41.9 per 1000) income quintile. The annual incidence was consistently higher in rural regions, with Alberta having the greatest regional differences (73.8 v. 60.5 per 1000 population in rural v. urban areas, respectively, in 2022). Stratified rates of annual prevalence exhibited similar patterns by sex, age, neighbourhood income quintile, and location of residence over time and across all provinces (Appendix 1, eTable 5, eFigure 2, and eFigure 3).

Interpretation

Our population-based repeated cross-sectional study of data from 6 Canadian provinces showed that annual incident and prevalent prescription opioid dispensation for pain declined by 7.9% and 11.1%, respectively, between 2018 and 2022. Manitoba consistently had the highest, and BC the lowest, monthly prevalence of prescription opioids for pain. Incident and prevalent opioid dispensations were higher among females, older adults, and those residing in lower-income quintile neighbourhoods and rural regions. The proportion of new opioid recipients prescribed a daily dose greater than 50 MME varied among the provinces studied but declined over time, with important variations in the types of opioids prescribed, including higher proportions of codeine, hydromorphone, and oxycodone dispensed in Manitoba, Quebec, and Ontario, respectively, compared with other provinces.

Our study provides population-based measures of opioid prescribing across Canada, supporting previous studies that described a national decline in prescription opioid consumption before the COVID-19 pandemic.18–22 Consistent with our findings, previous Canadian research reported an acute disruption in community pharmacy dispensing at the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, 35–39 and an Ontario study40 of prescription opioid dispensing demonstrated the substantial effects of the pandemic on rates of new opioid recipients, with incident and prevalent opioid users decreasing by 27.8% and 6.2%, respectively, after the initial COVID-19 provincial lockdown.

Our stratified analysis found that females and older adults had higher rates of prescription opioid use for pain, in line with previous research using North American41–43 and Canadian44–46 data that reported that females are more likely to use prescription opioids, particularly as they age. Prior studies have indicated that incident opioid exposure, high-dose prescribing, and prescription duration have been on the decline in North America since 2012,19,27,47–49 particularly following the release of guidelines for appropriate opioid prescribing for pain in the United States50 and Canada10 in 2016 and 2017, respectively. However, the inter-provincial variations in our findings may indicate differences in the implementation of prescribing guidelines, underscoring the need for coordinated national strategies and ongoing evaluation of their effects on patient outcomes.

High-dose initiations — which have been associated with elevated risks of continued opioid use, opioid use disorder, opioid toxicity, and opioid-related death51,52 — became increasingly uncommon, providing contemporary data demonstrating a shift in clinical practice in Canada consistent with national guideline recommendations.10 Prior research from Ontario on the impact of Canadian national guidelines on prescribing practices found a small reduction in people initiated on doses greater than 50 MME between 2015 (23.9%) and 2019 (20.1%).27 We observed a decline between 2018 (17.1%) and 2022 (11.9%) in Ontario and similar declines in high-dose opioid initiation across other Canadian provinces. Importantly, although these recommendations intended to promote safer opioid prescribing, improper implementation can lead to rapid dose tapering, abrupt opioid discontinuation, and reluctance to initiate patients on opioids when clinically indicated.53,54 In some cases, these changes have led to patients seeking access to opioids from the unregulated drug supply, which are inherently more harmful.53–56 Although opioid-related mortality continues to rise in Canada, prescription opioids have become less frequently involved, with rising mortality rates largely attributed to the growing toxicity of the unregulated drug supply.24,57 Declining trends in opioid prescribing and high-dose initiation have likely mitigated iatrogenic harm from pharmaceutical opioid use, but even so, our findings highlight the need for clinical practice guidelines and policies to balance these efforts with the pain management needs of specific populations (i.e., females, older adults) and the risk of unintended consequences of restricting access to prescription opioids. Prescribers should undertake opioid stewardship activities through shared decision-making with patients, regularly reassessing pain and its effect on function, and applying compassionate tapering strategies to optimize outcomes while minimizing harms from regulated and unregulated drug supplies.58

Although the type of opioids dispensed varied among provinces, several patterns were consistent across provinces. Decreased dispensing of oxycodone was likely influenced by changing formulation availability and listing status on public drug formularies. A long-acting oxycodone formulation (OxyContin) was introduced to the Canadian market in 2000 and subsequently implicated in a disproportionate number of opioid-related harms,3,6,59 culminating in its delisting from public drug plans in all but 3 provinces, including Alberta, in 2012.18,60 This resulted in a 46.4% national decline in oxycodone dispensing in the following 5 years,13 and a shift toward prescribing of other potent opioids, mainly hydromorphone.3,18 Interestingly, we observed a high percentage of people in BC and Alberta who were dispensed other opioids, which likely reflects historically high rates of tramadol prescribing in these provinces.61–63 This, in addition to higher use of codeine across all provinces, paralleled patterns of consumption observed in other economically developed countries, whereas higher hydromorphone use was unique to Canada,64 highlighting the distinct and evolving nature of opioid selection in clinical practice nationally.

Limitations

Population-level data were not accessible for some regions of Canada (i.e., the territories, Atlantic provinces, and parts of Quebec), where distinct dynamics of opioid prescribing may exist. As the Quebec data were limited to individuals continuously insured by their public drug plan, interprovincial comparisons with other provinces should be undertaken cautiously. Although our findings capture community pharmacy opioid dispensing, we cannot verify whether patients took their prescribed medication. Finally, as we did not have information on prescription indication, some opioid dispensations may not have been indicated for pain (e.g., immediate-release hydromorphone for safer opioid supply); however, we expect these numbers to be relatively small overall (e.g., < 3000 recipients in Ontario in 2023),65 with potential variations across provinces related to differences in safer supply program implementation and prescribing practices.66

Conclusion

Our study provides valuable information on the progressive decline of prescription opioid dispensing for pain and high-dose initiations among new users, as well as the types of opioids prescribed and important geographic differences in these trends across Canada. Our findings suggest that national efforts to promote opioid stewardship and safer opioid prescribing for acute and chronic noncancer pain over the past decade have had an impact. Ongoing monitoring is needed to ensure that policies and guidelines promote safe and appropriate opioid prescribing without leaving patients with inadequate pain management that may increase reliance on unregulated drug markets.
