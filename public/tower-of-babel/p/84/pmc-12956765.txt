Analysis of uroflowmetry results in community-dwelling adult males: Results of 24 years of Korean population-based screening data
Authors: Dae Hyun Kim, Chan Ho Park, Chang Seok Kang, Jae Whi Choi, Seong Uk Jeh, See Min Choi, Sung Chul Kam, Jeong Seok Hwa, Seongwon Kwon, Saecheol Kim, Dongdeuk Kwon, Taekyun Kwon, Seonjin Kim, Younggon Kim, Taehyung Kim, Yonggil Na, Dongsoo Park, Hyun Jun Park, Rakhee Seong, Sangguk Yang, Gyeongseop Lee, Donghyun Lee, Sangeun Lee, Hyunchul Jung, Yunsoo Lee, ChangWan Kang, Jaeseog Hyun

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIn this study, we aimed to analyze the uroflowmetry findings in community-dwelling adult males and examine the relationship between maximum urine flow rate (Qmax) and factors such as age, prostate size, International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS), prostate-specific antigen (PSA), and voided volume.Materials and MethodsData were collected from 75,199 males aged >24 years through community health screenings conducted by the Korea Prostate & Voiding Health Association. After applying the exclusion criteria, 12,891 males with complete data were included in this analysis. Uroflowmetry, IPSS, transrectal ultrasonography, and PSA tests were performed.ResultsThe mean patient age was 74.66±9.10 years, mean IPSS was 14.59±8.11, mean prostate size was 30.50±14.44 mL, mean PSA was 1.79±3.94 ng/mL, and mean Qmax was 14.21±6.34 mL/s. Significant associations were found between Qmax and age (p<0.001), IPSS (p<0.001), prostate size (p<0.001), PSA level (p<0.001), and voided volume (p<0.001). Qmax decreased with increasing age, IPSS, prostate size, and PSA, but increased with larger voided volumes.ConclusionsThis large-scale community-based study provides valuable insights into the relationship between uroflowmetry results and various factors in adult males. These findings can serve as a reference for evaluating uroflowmetry results in clinical settings and contribute to a better understanding of lower urinary tract symptoms in the general male population.Keywords: Age groups, Men, Prostate, Prostate-specific antigen, Prostatic hyperplasia

INTRODUCTION

Uroflowmetry is a noninvasive and objective test that plays a crucial role in evaluating lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), including benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) [1]. Several important parameters must be considered when interpreting uroflowmetry results, including the maximum urine flow rate (Qmax). The normal Qmax range varies depending on age and sex. For males under the age of 40 years, a Qmax of over 22 mL/s is generally considered normal. For males aged 40–60 years, a Qmax of 18 mL/s or higher is typical, whereas for males aged >60 years, a Qmax of >13 mL/s is considered normal [2]. Regarding females, the normal Qmax is >25 mL/s for those aged <50 years and >18 mL/s for those aged >50 years [3].

Another key parameter is the total urine volume. A meaningful result requires a total urine volume of at least 150 mL; too little or too much urine can compromise the accuracy of the results [4,5]. The main advantage of uroflowmetry is that it is noninvasive. Patients undergoing uroflowmetry are required to urinate naturally without the need to undergo any special procedures or surgeries, minimizing patients’ physical discomfort while providing valuable diagnostic information.

Uroflowmetry is useful for diagnosing various urinary disorders. BPH and urethral strictures are common conditions that affect urine flow, and by analyzing voiding patterns and flow rates, the severity and progression of these conditions can be assessed [6]. Functional issues, such as neurogenic bladder and overactive bladder, can also be evaluated through uroflowmetry [7].

Additionally, uroflowmetry is important for assessing treatment outcomes [8]. For instance, after prostate surgery or medical treatment for BPH, evaluation of changes in urine flow can help determine treatment progress [9,10,11].

However, defining “normal” flow rates across age groups is complex because of the interplay of various factors, including changes in bladder function and voiding difficulties associated with BPH in aging males. While a voided volume of at least 150 mL is required for accurate testing, ensuring that patients have the appropriate amount of urine in their bladders at the time of testing can be challenging [12,13].

Although uroflowmetry has been used in clinical practice for diagnosing LUTS and BPH for over 40–50 years, large-scale epidemiological studies on mean flow rates in healthy males are lacking. Most large-scale studies have focused on males with LUTS or BPH, which makes it difficult to apply these results to the general male population. Notably, large-scale epidemiological studies of mean flow rates in the general population are scarce because recruiting sufficient participants and ensuring that collected samples meet the voided volume requirement can be logistically challenging [14]. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to analyze data collected over a 24-year period from community health screenings to investigate the mean flow rates of adult males according to age, prostate size, and LUTS.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The Korea Prostate & Voiding Health Association undertook a comprehensive initiative and conducted prostate examinations and surveys across all regions of South Korea over a 24-year period, from 2001 to 2024. This initiative was executed in collaboration with local public health centers to facilitate prostate health assessment. The target demographics for these screenings did not include patients with LUTS, such as BPH, but instead included general adult males because the primary aim of this initiative was to conduct health screenings to confirm the presence or absence of prostate cancer or BPH among otherwise healthy individuals.

During the screening process, all male participants underwent a series of evaluations, including a thorough medical history assessment, International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS), transrectal ultrasonography, prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing, uroflowmetry, and residual urine measurement. Overall, 75,199 males were screened. Subsequently, data from participants who met the following exclusion criteria were excluded from the analysis because they were classified as outliers: an IPSS exceeding 36, a Qmax >60 mL/s, a voided volume exceeding 1,000 mL, a post-void residual urine volume greater than 600 mL, a prostate size of <10 mL or >400 mL, and a patient age of <40 years.

The study specifically focused on males who completed all three tests: the IPSS, transrectal ultrasonography, and uroflowmetry with residual urine measurements. Uroflowmetry has a minimal total urine volume requirement; any sample of total urine volume (the sum of voided volume and post-void residual urine) of <150 mL is deemed inadequate. Therefore, after excluding participants who met the exclusion criteria and participants with inadequate urine samples (total urine volume less than 150 mL), data from 12,891 males were included in the final analysis.

The following analytical approaches were employed to elucidate the characteristics of the uroflowmetry results. First, examination of changes in the uroflowmetry results according to age groups (<50s, 50s, 60s, 70s, 80s, and 90s). Second, assessment of uroflowmetry variations in relation to prostate size. Third, analysis of uroflowmetry changes corresponding to the IPSS total and subscores. Fourth, evaluation of changes in uroflowmetry based on PSA levels. Fifth, comparison of the uroflowmetry results according to urine volume.

This study was conducted in compliance with relevant ethical standards and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Gyeongsang National University Hospital (approval number: GNUH 2025-02-014). The written informed consent is waived by the IRB due to the retrospective nature of study.

Statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 (IBM Corp.). For descriptive statistics, continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. A p-value <0.05 is considered statistically significant.

ANOVA was used to analyze the differences in LUTS-related variables across age groups. Additionally, cross-tabulations were used to explore associations between the Qmax groups and age, IPSS, prostate size, PSA, and urine volume groups, with significance assessed using chi-square test. ANOVA was also applied to compare the mean Qmax differences among the various LUTS-related variable groups.

RESULTS

Of the 75,199 males, data from 12,891 males who met the inclusion criteria were included in the analysis. These participants had valid results for all three tests (IPSS, transrectal ultrasonography, and uroflowmetry with residual urine measurement) and a total urine volume (voided volume+post-void residual urine) of at least 150 mL. The mean age of the participants was 74.66±9.10 years, with a mean IPSS of 14.59±8.11, a mean prostate size of 30.50±14.44 mL, a mean PSA of 1.79±3.94 ng/mL, and a mean Qmax of 14.21±6.34 mL/s (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

Uroflowmetry is a noninvasive and objective diagnostic modality that plays a critical role in evaluating LUTS, including BPH [15,16]. Uroflowmetry dates back to the early 1950s. In 1956, Drake introduced the first uroflowmetry device that used the principle of weight measurement to record urine flow. Uroflowmetry technology has significantly advanced over the years, particularly during the 1960s and 1970s. By the 1970s, electronic uroflowmeters were developed, which allowed for more accurate and reliable measurements. In the 1980s, uroflowmetry became a fundamental tool for evaluating LUTS, particularly BPH, and a core element of BPH diagnosis alongside the IPSS. By the 1990s, with advancements in computer technology, uroflowmetry devices became more sophisticated, facilitating data storage and analysis. Since the 2000s, the measurement of post-void residual urine using ultrasound has become commonplace, allowing for a more comprehensive evaluation of voiding function. Portable uroflowmeters enabling uroflowmetry testing at home have recently been developed [17,18,19,20].

Uroflowmetry, a simple and non-invasive test, places minimal burden on the patient, who needs to only urinate normally. Moreover, uroflowmetry results can be immediately viewed after the test, allowing for quick diagnosis and treatment planning. The test is also relatively inexpensive, as it does not require complex or costly equipment, and reliable results can be obtained using simple devices. However, in a hospital setting, patients undergoing uroflowmetry may feel anxious or uncomfortable, which can affect test results. Moreover, environmental factors, such as prolonged voiding time or temporarily reduced urine flow, may lower test reliability. Achieving consistent results, even in the same patient, can be challenging, as the results may vary depending on the time of the test, bladder volume, and hydration status. Therefore, uroflowmetry often needs to be repeated to obtain reliable data. Moreover, although uroflowmetry provides information about urine flow, it is not sufficient to accurately diagnose all urological conditions. For example, a reduced urine flow does not definitively indicate whether the underlying cause is BPH, urethral stricture, or another factor [6,7]. As such, the uroflowmetry results must be interpreted in conjunction with other diagnostic tests.

Although uroflowmetry has been clinically used for over 50 years, there is still no clear consensus on the “normal” reference values for the test. Most epidemiological studies on uroflowmetry involve patients with BPH or LUTS, and unfortunately, the studies focused on healthy adult males often have small sample sizes, typically in hundreds, limiting the generalizability of the results [21]. However, in this study, we screened data from 75,199 participants >24 years of age and used complete data from 12,891 individuals for final analysis. Unlike most hospital-based studies, this large-scale study focused on the general adult male population, which adds significance to its findings. The results of this study, by comprehensively analyzing factors such as age, prostate size, IPSS, and QoL and their relationship with urine flow rates, can help deepen our understanding of the complex characteristics of LUTS. These findings provide valuable reference data for clinicians assessing urine flow in patients.

The results of this study clearly showed that as age increased, IPSS, prostate size, and PSA levels tended to increase, while Qmax decreased [22]. This finding reinforces the notion that age is a significant risk factor for LUTS and BPH. Notably, there was a marked worsening of LUTS-related indicators among patients in their 70s and older, highlighting the importance of managing LUTS in the aging population [23,24]. This finding is consistent with that of other studies that showed a significant negative correlation between age and Qmax, with a decreasing trend in Qmax as age increased. Studies have reported that, in males, Qmax increases up to 15 years of age, gradually decreases until 50 years of age, and then sharply declines after 50 years of age.

The significant association between IPSS and Qmax observed in this study demonstrates the consistency between subjective symptoms and objective test results [25]. This study showed a statistically significant relationship between the severity of IPSS (mild to severe) and urine flow rate. Specifically, the severe symptom group (IPSS 20–35) had lower Qmax values, whereas the mild symptom group had higher Qmax values. Similar findings have been reported previously. Multiple studies have consistently demonstrated a negative correlation between the IPSS and Qmax, indicating that as the IPSS increases (indicating more severe symptoms), Qmax decreases (indicating worsening urine flow).

The decreasing trend in Qmax with increasing prostate size reflects the impact of BPH-induced urethral obstruction caused by BPH. Generally, there is a negative correlation between Qmax and prostate size, with a larger prostate size associated with a lower Qmax [26]. This is a natural phenomenon, as prostate enlargement increases the degree of urethral obstruction.

The relationship between PSA levels and Qmax suggests that PSA levels can serve as indirect markers for prostate size and LUTS severity. PSA is primarily used to evaluate the presence and severity of prostate-related conditions such as BPH, prostatitis, and prostate cancer [21]. There was an indirect correlation between PSA levels and Qmax. Elevated PSA levels may indicate the presence of BPH, inflammation, or malignancy, which can lead to urethral compression and a subsequent reduction in Qmax. In patients with BPH, higher PSA levels are generally associated with larger prostates. As the prostate enlarges, the urethra is compressed, leading to a decrease in Qmax. Thus, as the prostate size increases, Qmax tends to decrease, and PSA levels are more likely to increase. However, elevated PSA levels do not always correspond to low Qmax because PSA levels reflect the overall condition of the prostate (inflammation, hypertrophy, and malignancy), which may not always align with Qmax [27].

The observation that a higher voided volume is associated with a higher Qmax reflects the fundamental urodynamic principles. There is a strong positive correlation between voided volume and Qmax, as voided volume increases, Qmax also tends to increase [13]. In most cases, a minimum voided volume of 150 mL is recommended for accurate assessment. Voided volumes <100 mL may not produce sufficient flow for accurate measurement, whereas excessively large volumes can overstretch the bladder muscle, leading to inefficient contractions and abnormal results, even in patients without an underlying pathology.

As this was a cross-sectional study, changes were not observed over time. Future longitudinal studies are needed to track changes in urine flow over time. Additionally, as this study involved participants who voluntarily underwent health check-ups, it may not fully represent the general population. Analysis of factors such as medication use or comorbidities that could affect urine flow was also limited. Although most participants were older adults and comorbidity data were limited, this study uniquely represents a large-scale cross-sectional snapshot of voiding characteristics among community-dwelling older males. Thus, these findings provide valuable baseline information for future longitudinal or disease-specific studies designed to clarify the causal pathways that influence Qmax and the severity of LUTS.

CONCLUSIONS

This study analyzed a large-scale community-based dataset collected over a 24-year period to investigate the relationships between age, prostate size, IPSS, PSA levels, voided volume, and Qmax. The findings clearly showed that Qmax tended to decrease proportionally with increasing age, IPSS, prostate size, and PSA level, whereas higher voided volumes were associated with an increase in Qmax. This large-scale community-based study provides valuable insights into the relationship between uroflowmetry results and various factors in adult males. These findings can serve as reference data for interpreting uroflowmetry results in clinical settings and contribute to a better understanding of LUTS in the general male population.
