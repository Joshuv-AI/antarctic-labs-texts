Detection of submicron titanium debris associated with peri-implantitis
Authors: Françoise Kleinsorgen, Orlanda Torres, Fernando Ferreira, Bruno Henriques, António Ramos, Júlio C M Souza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe main aim of this study was to perform a systematic review on the detection of submicron-scale titanium debris at peri-implant tissues focusing on the analytical methods.MethodsA PRISMA-oriented systematic search was carried out on PubMed and Scopus using the following search items: titanium debris, submicron particles, peri-implantitis, analytical methods, inflammation, and implant failure. The inclusion criteria involved in vivo studies published in the English language, until November 26, 2025. Risk of bias was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool.ResultsPrevious studies reveal submicron-scale titanium debris at peri-implant tissues although the particles' detection depend on the microscopic method. High-resolution SEM and TEM images clearly showed titanium debris’ dimensions below 1 µm although the heterogeneity of the studies revealed limitations on the particles’ measurements. Some titanium particles were associated with oxidative stress and activation of pro-inflammatory cytokines to osteoclastogenesis (i.e., RANKL/RANK/OPG) and other inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, PGE2.ConclusionThus, submicron titanium debris can be detected at peri-implant tissues that indicate the degradation of the materials and potential adverse biological effects in the human body. However, the analytical methods should involve high-resolution microscopy and a careful analysis of the images.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04741-0.Keywords: Titanium debris, Submicron particles, Peri-implantitis, Microscopy, Failure

Introduction

In implant dentistry, the prevalence of peri-implantitis has been consistently reported at approximately 20–22% at the patient level [1, 2] and 20% at the implant level [2, 3], underscoring its epidemiological relevance. Histopathological findings reveal that peri-implantitis lesions are larger and more aggressive, with denser inflammatory infiltrates than those noticed in periodontitis or peri-implant mucositis [2, 4–7]. Although oral biofilms remain the primary etiological factor, cumulative evidence indicates that titanium degradation by corrosion and wear can release particles and metal ions capable of interacting with peri-implant tissues [8–10]. The presence of titanium debris at different size, chemical composition, and content stimulate the immune cells triggering an inflammatory reaction around implants [11–16].

Dental implants are currently manufactured from commercially pure titanium (cp Ti) or Ti6Al4V alloy due to their physical properties (e.g., elastic modulus, strength) and biocompatibility [10, 11]. Clinical performance relies on the spontaneous formation of a thin passive titanium dioxide (TiO2) layer that provides electrochemical protection against the oral environment [12, 13]. However, this passive film is not fully stable since acidogenic environments, fluoride exposure, cyclic masticatory loads, and prosthetic mismatch, can disrupt its integrity, exposing the underlying metal substrate and promoting corrosion [10, 14–17].

The implant–abutment connections represent one of the most structurally vulnerable regions to degradation owing to the combined effects of biofilms, acidic environment, microgaps, cyclic loading, micromovements, and wear [11, 14, 15]. Experimental studies and systematic reviews demonstrate that different connections' design (e.g., Morse taper, internal hexagon, or hybrid connections) exhibit distinct wear and corrosion (tribocorrosion) profiles, with marked variations in wear patterns, passive film rupture, and metallic debris release [8, 13, 18]. Experimental analyses reveal that cyclic loading and wear intensify surface damage, promoting the formation of micro- and submicron-scale debris during repeated functional cycles [11, 19]. Consequently, titanium surface degradation alters the implant–tissue interface's physicochemical properties, increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, metal ion release, and debris formation across multiple size scales [8, 9, 20].

Degradation of titanium gives rise to debris spanning a broad dimensional spectrum ranging from macro- (1–50 µm) to nano-scale particles and ionic species derived from the TiO2 thin layer [20–24]. In vivo studies have reported micro-scale titanium although there is a lack of data on the presence of submicron- (< 1 µm) and nano-scale (1–100 nm) particles [8–10, 25, 26]. The major reason for the lack of information on submicron debris is related to the limitations of the analytical methods and therefore high-resolution microscopic analyses are required for adequate measurements. Nevertheless, titanium debris from implants have been associated with chronic inflammatory infiltrates and increased expression of IL-1β, IL-8, IL-18, TNF-α, IL-6, and VEGF [20, 21, 24, 27]. IL-1β plays a central role in initiating osteolytic processes and contributes to TNF-α–mediated RANK-L production, promoting bone resorption [28, 29]. Some studies showed that submicron and nano-scale particles exhibit high chemical reactivity enabling cell internalization, oxidative stress induction, macrophage polarisation toward an M1 phenotype, and cells' dysfunction such as mitochondrial damage and DNA injury [8, 9, 20, 25, 30].

The main aim of this study was to perform a systematic review on the detection of submicron-scale titanium debris (< 1 µm) at peri-implant tissues focusing on the analytical methods. It was hypothesized that most of clinical studies report the presence of micro-scale titanium debris instead of submicron-scale debris.

Materials and methods

Information sources and search strategy

This study is in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) [31] and it was also registered in the PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) database (CRD420251145047). Also, the method was adapted from search strategies applied in previous systematic, scoping, or integrative reviews [8, 9, 31].

A systematic literature search was performed on the PubMed (via the National Library of Medicine) and Scopus database using structured Boolean search strategies. Search terms were combined using Boolean operators (AND/OR) and the TITLE-ABS-KEY syntax to capture all the relevant studies as follow: titanium OR cp titanium OR cp-Ti OR cp-Ti OR Ti6Al4V or Ti–6Al–4V OR Ti15r10Nb OR Ti13Zr13Nb OR Ti13Zr OR Ti15Zr OR roxolid OR titanium alloy AND debris OR nanoparticles OR ions OR particles AND peri-implant OR perimplant OR peri implant OR dental implant OR implant-abutment AND periimplantitis OR peri implantitis OR peri-implantitis OR mucositis OR bone loss OR bone remodeling OR bone resorption OR inflammation OR osteolysis OR failure.

The inclusion criteria comprised in vivo studies carried out in human participants and reporting the detection and characterization of submicron-scale titanium particles (< 1 µm) at peri-implant tissues. The studies were published in the English language up to November 26, 2025. Ongoing studies were searched in the following clinical trial registries: Current Controlled Trials, International Clinical trials registry platform, ClinicalTrials.gov, ReBEC, and EU Clinical Trials Register. Also, a hand-held search was carried out on the reference lists of all main sources and eligible studies of this review for further relevant publications. Exclusion criteria included in vitro studies, investigations involving other metal alloys, ceramic implants, or detecting exclusively titanium dioxide (TiO2) particles. Also, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and narrative reviews were excluded. The search results were imported into Microsoft Excel™ to support the visualization, selection of studies, data retrieval, to evaluate the methodological quality of the studies.

Study selection and data collection

A structured selection process was implemented following the search. The PICO (population, intervention, comparison, and outcome) method was followed as a framework to address the following research questions: “Can submicron-scale titanium debris be detected at the peri-implant tissues and which analytical methods have been used for their localization, characterization, and quantification?”.

Considering the PICO question, the following parameters were taken into consideration: (i) population: peri-implant tissues samples; (ii) intervention: histomorphometry assays, microscopy, chemical analyses; (iii) comparison: dimensions and content of particles, types of materials; healthy state of the peri-implant tissues (iv) outcome: Major findings on the detection of particles and their dimensions and location.

At first, studies were examined for relevance by title and then the abstracts were evaluated. Abstracts of the articles were independently reviewed by two authors (JCMS, FK) regarding the predefined eligibility criteria and PICO question. A third author (OT) accomplished a final evaluation in case of disagreement. The remaining studies were exported to the Zotero citation manager [32], supporting the compilation and removal of duplicate entries. After screening the abstracts, studies consistent with the objectives of this review were selected for full-reading and individual evaluation. Eligible articles were then given an identification code that combined the name of the first author and the year of publication. The following factors were retrieved for this review: authors’ names, publication year, journal, purpose, study design, physicochemical characterization, particles’ dimensions, biological response of peri-implant tissues.

The data was compiled in Microsoft Excel™ and analyzed descriptively in IBM SPSS Statistics™ 30.0 [33–38]. Considering the dataset was composed mainly of categorical and qualitative evidence, inferential tests were not applicable. A structured semi-quantitative coding scheme was applied to standardize extraction across six domains (physicochemical characterization, particle-size evidence, titanium detection, analytical methods, biological responses, and contamination sources). Each domain was coded using predefined numeric categories to generate frequencies, percentages, and cross-tabulations. These outputs were used to describe distribution and patterns of overlapping across domains.

Risk of bias assessment

The Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool was applied to assess the risk of bias (RoB) across the selected in vivo studies to promote a critical appraisal of the findings [39]. ROBINS-I was selected due to its structured domain-based framework, which enables systematic appraisal of key sources of bias relevant to observational in vivo research, particularly confounding, participant selection, outcome measurement, missing data, and selective reporting. The intervention-related domains were interpreted conservatively within an exposure-based framework since the included studies were non-randomized human investigations evaluating exposure to titanium particles/debris and related biological outcomes.

Each study was independently assessed across seven bias domains regarding: confounding (D1); selection of participants into the study (D2); classification of interventions (D3); deviations from intended interventions (D4); missing data (D5); measurement of outcomes (D6); and selection of the reported result (D7). Domains originally framed around interventions (D3–D4) were interpreted conservatively in the context of exposure classification and procedural consistency, with D3 referring to exposure to titanium particles/debris and D4 to consistency in sampling, tissue handling, and analytical procedures.

Thus, the resulting judgments were interpreted as a conservative, structured appraisal of methodological limitations rather than as a direct assessment of intervention effects. The certainty of evidence for the main outcomes was also assessed using the GRADE approach, which considered risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and potential publication bias to determine the overall level of confidence in the evidence synthesized in this review.

Results

The bibliographic search identified 241 studies on Scopus and 244 studies on PubMed as seen in Fig. 1. After excluding 92 non-original publications, a total of 393 articles were exported to the Zotero™ citation manager [32] and then 39 duplicates were removed. After the preliminary appraisal of the abstracts, 253 studies were removed leading to the selection of 25 studies for full-text reading. Of these, 18 studies were excluded due to the lack of data on submicron titanium particles in peri-implant tissues. At last, seven studies were selected for this review. All the selected studies were conducted in humans through tissue biopsies or exfoliative cytology [6, 20–24, 27]. Two studies (28.6%) utilized exfoliative cytology by smear [22, 23], while five studies (71.4%) analyzed surgically retrived peri-implant soft or granulation tissue biopsies [6, 20, 21, 24, 27].

The analytical methods varied across the selected studies. Light microscopy on samples stained by H&E or polarized light was used in four studies (57.1%) [21–23, 27]. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) was applied in three studies (42.8%) [6, 21, 24], while transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used in two studies (28.6%) [6, 20]. Chemical quantification techniques, such as ICP-MS, were reported in two studies (28.6%) [20, 22], while μ-PIXE was used in one study [6]. Elemental confirmation of titanium was performed in five studies (71.4%) using ICP-MS [20, 22], μ-PIXE [6], or SEM–EDS/EDX [21, 24]. In two studies (28.6%) [23, 27], titanium-based particles identification was based solely on morphological criteria by optical microscopy.

Titanium particles were detected at peri-implant tissues irrespective of clinical status. Spatial analysis demonstrated non-uniform particle distribution, with higher density near the implant–tissue interface and progressive reduction in deeper regions [6]. A few studies showed high-resolution images but did not describe the particles’ dimensions. Six articles (85.7%) reported particles predominantly ranging from 0.6 up to 1.3 μm [6, 21–24, 27]. Smaller particles (< 0.2 μm) were reported in one study [20] while two studies [22, 24] recorded intermediate dimensions between 0.2 and 0.7 μm. One study reported particle size both as an average area (8.9 ±24.8 μm2) with an estimated range of ~ 0.4–1.0 μm and a titanium mass composition of 0.38% ±0.16% in peri-implantitis specimens [21].

All seven studies attributed the release of titanium debris due to a simultaneous degradation by corrosion and wear of the implant surface. Chemical factors (e.g., lactic acid) and electrochemical dissolution of the TiO2 layer were described as contributing corrosion pathways in one study [23]. Five studies (71.4%) also described wear or tribocorrosion (wear and corrosion) mechanisms at the implant–abutment connection under functional conditions [20, 21, 23, 24, 27]. One study reported influences related to implant system characteristics or manufacturing processes [6].

Particles were identified in sites diagnosed with peri-implantitis as well as in clinically healthy peri-implant tissues within the same individuals [6, 22, 23]. In one study, titanium concentration measured by ICP-MS was significantly higher in peri-implantitis sites (2.02–2.44 ppb) compared with non-diseased peri-implant sites (0.41–0.88 ppb) [22]. In another study, particles were identified in 60% of patients with moderate to severe mucositis, whereas no particles were detected in cases of mild mucositis [23]. Quantitative assessments were reported in four studies using different metrics. One study reported a mean volumetric density of 190 particles/mm3 in peri-implantitis sites and 210 particles/mm3 in clinically healthy reference sites within the same individuals, with particles occupying 0.11–0.15% of the analyzed tissue area [6].

Particle detection was reported across a wide range of implant service times, from 3 months up to 12 years. One study reported no association between implant time in function (meaning 12.6 years) and particle density [6]. Titanium particles were detected as early as 3–5 months after implant placement or during second-stage surgery (3–5 months after placement) in clinically successful implants [27]. Other studies included implants with service times ranging from 3 up to 63 months [22].

Chronic inflammatory infiltrate was reported in six studies (85.7%), characterized by lymphocytes, plasma cells, or macrophages [6, 20, 21, 23, 24, 27]. Particles were detected within epithelial layers, macrophages, and occasionally vascular compartments [20, 22]. Increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-8, and IL-18) was described in three studies (42.8%) [20, 21, 27]. Four studies (57.1%) reported involvement of the RANKL/RANK pathway in peri-implant tissues containing titanium particles [20, 23, 24, 27]. Neovascularization with increased VEGF expression was noticed in two studies (28.6%) [21, 24]. Increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and adipocytes differentiation of mesenchymal cells were reported in one study (14.3%) [20]. Positive expressions of CD68 (macrophages) and CD3 (lymphocytes) were documented in three studies (42.8%) [21, 24, 27]. Cell internalization of titanium particles within epithelial or phagocytic cells was described in two studies (28.6%) [22, 23]. Absence of multinucleated giant cells in peri-implant tissues containing titanium particles was reported in two studies (28.6%) [21, 24].

Two studies [21, 24] exhibited an overall moderate RoB, while the remaining five [6, 20, 22, 23, 27] were classified as having a serious overall risk (Fig. 2).

Risk of Bias (RoB) in Non-Randomized Studies of Interventions

Domains D3–D5 were consistently rated as low risk, reflecting methodological consistency in exposure classification, laboratory procedures, and sample handling. Conversely, higher-risk ratings in D1, D2, D6, and D7 reflected common constraints of observational evidence stemming from the inherent limitations of observational designs, variability in participant selection, absence of preregistered protocols, and heterogeneity in outcome measurement techniques.

The GRADE approach was used to assess the overall certainty of evidence for the main outcomes of this review (Table 2). This complementary assessment was undertaken to clarify the level of confidence that can be placed in the correlations between titanium debris and peri-implant biological responses. The certainty of evidence was rated as moderate for the detection of titanium particles at peri-implant tissues, reflecting the consistent identification of the particles across all included studies using multiple analytical techniques. In contrast, the certainty of evidence was judged as low for histopathological inflammatory changes and inflammatory cytokine expression, and very low for osteoclastogenic signaling, including RANKL/OPG-related pathways. The rating for cytokine expression reflects generally consistent trends in pro-inflammatory mediator upregulation across studies, but with limitations related to serious RoB, methodological heterogeneity, and imprecision.

Discussion

The results of the selected studies validate the hypothesis of the present study once titanium debris on a submicron scale can be detected in peri-implant tissues. The previous selected studies indicates that the titanium identification depends mostly on high-resolution techniques associated with secondary morphometric measurements, since ordinary methods (e.g., light microscopy) rarely allow the precise dimensional delimitation of submicron-scale particles. Furthermore, the size, tissue distribution, and localized amount of debris released from implants were described in association with biological pathways related to cell internalization, phagocytosis, macrophage polarization, immunomodulatory markers, osteoblastic–osteoclastic balance, as well as lymphovascular translocation and systemic dissemination. Methodological parameters applied for the identification of submicron-scale debris, the biological responses observed in peri-implant tissues, and the potential sources of contamination associated with debris release are discussed as follow.

Release and spatial distribution of titanium debris in peri-implant tissues

The presence of titanium debris at peri-implant tissues results from the interaction between mechanical, chemical and electrochemical processes associated with dental implants since the surgical placement. The placement of titanium implants, particularly with rough or coated surfaces, into the bone site promotes the detachment of titanium particles to the surrounding tissues, which can lead to adverse clinical effects [11, 12, 40].

Previous studies have described corrosion and wear are processes inherent to the oral cavity, whereas the titanium reactivity increases under acidic or oxidizing conditions, such as exposure to lactic acid, hydrogen peroxide, citric acid, saliva, and fluoride-containing solutions, leading to degradation of the protective titanium oxide layer (TiO2) [10, 15] (Fig. 3). The thin oxide film which forms spontaneously on titanium implant surfaces can be destroyed under corrosive conditions associated or not with wear pathways [12, 41].

In addition to the general processes of corrosion and wear, material-related factors and implant–prosthetic system characteristics may influence the elemental profile and the patterns of debris release in peri-implant tissues. In two of the selected studies, minor elemental components associated with titanium particles, such as zinc, copper and rubidium, were identified in peri-implant tissues [20, 21]. Those findings are consistent with the well-documented capability of the titanium oxide film to incorporate small amounts of alloying elements or absorbed species during surface modification and corrosion processes [9, 42–44]. The detected elements were present only in trace amounts and were interpreted as secondary constituents related to alloy composition, surface adsorption, corrosion by-products or environmental contamination. Importantly, the presence of residual elements did not alter the interpretation of titanium as the predominant constituent of the detected debris. Considering mechanical pathways, adhesive abrasion and material transfer from the titanium implant surface to adjacent tissues were described as relevant wear routes, particularly during insertion and functional loading. Micro-movements and frictional wear (fretting) at the implant–abutment connections were also reported as continuous sources of ion and particle release into the biological environment (Fig. 3) [19]. Thus, the mechanical and chemical stability of titanium is challenged both by the physical force of friction and the hostile chemical microenvironment of the oral cavity [20, 21, 23, 24]

The release of titanium debris was previously reported on both healthy and inflamed peri-implant sites, indicating that particle release can occur independently of inflammatory reactions [6, 22]. However, higher titanium concentrations were consistently reported in sites diagnosed with peri-implantitis compared to healthy control groups. The amount of particles increased proportionally to clinical inflammatory severity in peri-implant mucositis [23]. Although titanium is biocompatible, the presence of debris in 85% sites (including healthy ones) indicates that the body is constantly exposed to those debris. Inflammatory processes decrease the local pH (acidic environment), that promote the corrosion of titanium, releasing even more particles and ions into the tissue. The immune system can tolerate a low content of titanium debris at peri-implant tissues. However, a high content of titanium particles exacerbates the inflammatory response, activating macrophages and cytokines leading ultimately to bone resorption [18, 20, 41, 45].

The analyzed studies demonstrated a relatively consistent pattern regarding tissue distribution and localization. The highest particle density was reported within a perimeter zone adjacent to the implant surface, generally within approximately 2 mm from the implant–tissue interface [6, 25]. Since these microparticles are often larger (frequently ≥ 1.5 μm), they remain predominantly in the extracellular compartment near the interface rather than migrating freely (Fig. 4). Most of those metallic particles reside up to 2 mm, with a gradual decrease in density as one move deeper into the tissue (beyond 2 mm). That reinforces the idea the debris load in this peripheral region is established almost entirely at the time of implant placement and by the geometry/system of the implant, performing as long-term“installation residue” in the peri-implant microenvironment [6, 12, 46].

Titanium debris was identified both extracellularly and intracellularly, predominantly within macrophages, but also in epithelial cells and occasionally associated with erythrocytes, indicating multiple interaction pathways [21, 25]. A size-dependent histomorphological pattern was also described, while smaller particles were predominantly noticed in boundary zones close to the implant surface and larger particles were more frequently located in central regions of peri-implant granulation tissue [21, 24]. Such finding supports the firm belief that the detection of submicron-scale titanium debris is an inevitable biological reality as illustrated in Fig. 4.

The adverse reaction magnitude is also dependent on particle content and exposure time. Indeed, the increase in the content of submicron- and nano-scale particles negatively affect the immune response [8, 14, 15]. The success or failure of the implant seems to depend not on the mere existence of the reactive debris, but on the intensity of the related inflammatory response and the corrosive environment [8, 14, 15]. Further studies are mandatory to assess the synergistic effects of the size, content, and chemical composition of titanium particles. Also, in vivo and in vitro studies are needed concerning different implant systems, anatomical sites and clinical procedures to clarify the pathways involved in the cytotoxicity and genotoxicity of titanium debris.

Analytical methods and morphometric criteria for identification of submicron particles

The reliable identification of titanium debris at peri-implant tissues, particularly within the submicron- and nano-scale range, is strongly dependent on the analytical methods. The available literature indicates that conventional histological and imaging approaches are often insufficient to reliably identify and delimit particles at such dimensional scale, which may lead to systematic underestimation of their occurrence [6, 21, 22, 24, 25, 33].

A broad spectrum of techniques was reported in the selected studies, including optical microscopy, exfoliative cytology, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and elemental quantification approaches [6, 21–25, 27]. However, none of those solely methods, provide comprehensive information regarding particle size, spatial distribution, and chemical composition.

High-resolution imaging techniques play a central role in the detection of submicron-scale particles. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was the only method capable of resolving nano-scale and fine submicron structures showing cell internalization within endocytic vesicles and cytoplasmic compartments [6, 20]. Despite its unmatched spatial resolution, TEM analysis was inherently limited to small tissue areas and selected fields, restricting its representativeness for overall particle burden and precluding quantitative tissue-wide assessments [6, 20, 21, 46].

Elemental quantification techniques, particularly inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), has been utilized to detect titanium at trace levels at peri-implant tissues, blood components, and plasma [13, 22, 25]. Such approach provided a highly sensitive chemical confirmation of the titanium debris; however, ICP-MS quantifies total elemental content rather than discrete particles. As a result, ICP-MS did not allow discrimination between ionic titanium, nanometric aggregates, or larger particulate debris, limiting its interpretative value for size-dependent biological interactions [9, 12, 22, 47].

Other analytical approaches, such as scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (SEM–EDS) and micro proton-induced X-ray emission (µ-PIXE), were applied to confirm elemental composition and map titanium distribution within peri-implant tissues [6, 21, 24]. Even though their effectiveness for the identification and characterization of micrometric particles, the sensitivity of those techniques decreased markedly for structures below approximately 1–1.5 μm. Thus, submicron-scale particles cannot be easily detected that reinforces the methodological bias when compared with larger debris.

Simplified approaches, including exfoliative cytology combined with optical microscopy, were applied in clinical contexts to identify particulate material within epithelial cells and macrophages [22, 23]. Although minimally invasive and suitable for screening purposes, such methods relied primarily on visual recognition of opaque or pigmented aggregates and lacked the resolution required for precise size determination or chemical confirmation of submicron-scale particles.

According to the selected studies, the dimensional characterization of titanium debris revealed a consistent predominance of particles within the upper submicron-scale range. Most studies reported particle dimensions mainly between approximately 0.6 and 1.3 µm, representing the most frequently size interval found at peri-implant tissues and forming the core dimensional pattern identified in this review. Those particles were detected through different analytical approaches, including SEM–based techniques and exfoliative cytology, with dimensional confirmation relying systematically on secondary digital morphometric analysis of micrographs. In contrast, finer particles below 0.2 µm were described exclusively by Bressan et al. [20], using TEM, reflecting the superior resolving capacity of such technique for submicron- and nano-scale structures. Intermediate size ranges between 0.2 and 0.7 µm were reported in a few studies [21, 22, 24], and therefore they do not represent the predominant dimensional fraction across the literature. Those differences in reported particle size distributions appear to be closely related to the methodological sensitivity rather than to true biological discrepancies. Indeed, high-resolution techniques such as TEM enable the detection of smaller particles but are limited to restricted tissue areas, while SEM-based approaches, elemental mapping methods, and optical microscopy favor the identification of larger debris. Those findings indicate that the dimensional spectrum of titanium debris reported in human peri-implant tissues reflects both a genuine predominance of submicron-scale particles and inherent constraints to the analytical methods used for their detection.

Biological response of peri-implant tissues to titanium debris across size scales

Beyond the methodological challenges associated with the detection and characterization of titanium debris, increasing attention has been directed toward understanding the biological responses elicited by the debris across different dimensional scales (Fig. 4). The interaction between titanium debris and peri-implant tissues is not uniform, but rather strongly dependent on particle size, localized amount, physicochemical characteristics, and cell viability. Across the included human studies and the broader experimental literature, titanium debris have been consistently associated with distinct patterns of tissue distribution, cell interaction, and inflammatory signaling, ultimately influencing peri-implant tissue homeostasis and bone remodeling [9, 12, 47].

Taken together, such findings support a size-dependent pattern of tissue interaction, in which cytokine expression represents a shared biological response across particle dimensions, while the predominant immune pathways vary according to particle size [6, 20, 21, 23, 24, 27]. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 4, submicron- and nano-scale titanium particles were more frequently associated with cell internalization and macrophage-driven responses, including endocytic uptake, macrophage polarization, and cytokine release [20, 22, 27]. In contrast, micro-scale particles persist predominantly in the extracellular compartment, promoting sustained tissue-level inflammation, impaired osteoblast–osteoclast balance, and alterations in bone remodeling. [6, 21, 23, 24].

The deterioration of implant surfaces results in the progressive loss of material and the release of metal ions (Ti, Al, V), as well as metal debris that presents a wide dimensional spectrum, approximately between 100 nm and 54 μm, encompassing nano-, submicron- and micro-scale fractions that may accumulate in the surrounding tissues [9, 43]. Studies indicate that, once released, metallic debris interact with the peri-implant microenvironment, presenting specific distribution patterns and biological responses that depend largely on the size, local quantity and density of the deposited material, conditioning their internalization by macrophages and other inflammatory cells and influencing in a distinct way the cells and tissue responses.[20, 21, 23, 24, 27].In general, smaller particles tend to exhibit higher biological activity and cytotoxic potential (Figs. 3 and 4) [9, 12, 20, 28, 43].

At the nano- and submicron- scale, titanium particles exhibit a higher degree of biological reactivity when compared with larger debris. Bressan et al. [20] demonstrated that particles within the submicron-scale range, approximately 0.2–0.8 μm, are readily internalized by surrounding cells through endocytic mechanisms, accumulating within cytoplasmic vesicles and interacting with intracellular compartments. Such cell internalization was associated with increased production of ROS, mitochondrial dysfunction, and reduced cell viability, supporting the interpretation that submicron-scale titanium particles may act as intracellular stressors. In parallel, Kheder et al. [27] reported that nano- and submicron-scale titanium particles modulate macrophage behavior in peri-implant tissues, favoring a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and amplifying cytokine-mediated immune signaling in a size- and dose-dependent manner.

Some studies reported micro-scale debris at peri-implant tissues irrespective of inflammatory status, frequently appearing as free intercellular material without associated with macrophage engulfment or multinucleated giant cell formation [6, 21, 24]. Those observations support the concept that micro-scale titanium debris, while biologically active, does not invariably induce a classic foreign body reaction in human peri-implant tissues, reinforcing the recognized biocompatibility of titanium at the tissue level [2, 8–10].

Despite those size-dependent differences in localization and cell interaction, titanium debris across all dimensional scales has been associated with the release of pro-inflammatory mediators. Several studies reported increased expression of cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-18, and TNF-α in tissues containing titanium particles [21, 23, 25, 40, 45]. However, the magnitude, persistence, and biological consequences of this response appear to vary according to particle size and localization. Submicron- and nano-scale particles are more frequently associated with sustained oxidative stress, abnormal macrophage activation, and dysregulation of innate immune responses, while larger particles tend to elicit predominantly extracellular and lower-grade inflammatory reactions [6, 20, 25].

Those inflammatory pathways converge on mechanisms involved in peri-implant bone remodeling. Multiple authors have identified an association between titanium debris related inflammation and the up-regulation of osteoclastogenic signaling, particularly through activation of the RANKL/RANK/OPG axis (Fig. 5) [20, 23, 27]. Such findings do not support a direct osteolytic effect of titanium particles themselves but rather suggest an indirect amplification of inflammatory cascades that promote bone resorption in susceptible peri-implant microenvironments [8, 29, 41].

An additional biologically relevant aspect concerns the response of mesenchymal stem cells. Experimental evidence indicates that prolonged exposure to titanium particles, especially at nano- and submicron- scales, may reduce mesenchymal stem cell proliferation and divert differentiation away from an osteogenic phenotype toward adipogenic lineages, potentially compromising peri-implant bone stability [9, 25, 46].

Interpretative perspectives on the biological significance of titanium debris

From an interpretative standpoint, the integrated synthesis of the available evidence highlights the multifactorial nature of titanium particle release and the interaction with peri-implant tissues. [6, 13, 25, 27, 30, 47]. The included studies consistently report the presence of titanium debris as a frequent finding in peri-implant environments; however, they diverge in the interpretation of the biological relevance [6, 14, 17, 22, 23, 25]. While micro-scale particles remain the most reported fraction, largely due to methodological accessibility, submicron- and nano-scale debris (less frequently detected) appear to be associated with more pronounced biological activity, particularly in inflammatory and bone remodeling contexts (Figs. 4 and 5) [6, 21, 23].

In two studies, titanium particles were detected in all peri-implantitis specimens examined and described a consistent histomorphological pattern, in which larger particles were typically located within central regions of granulation tissue, whereas smaller structures accumulated near the implant–tissue interface [21, 24]. Notably, those studies also reported the absence of multinucleated giant cells, supporting the view that titanium debris does not necessarily elicit a classic foreign body reaction and reinforcing the recognized biocompatibility of titanium. In contrast, other studies propose a closer association between titanium debris and inflammatory burden. Olmedo et al. [22] identified titanium particles through exfoliative cytology both extracellularly and intracellularly within epithelial cells and macrophages at implant sitess associated or not with peri-implantitis; however, particle concentration was significantly higher in diseased sites. A previous study further demonstrated that metal particles were detectable in approximately 60% patients with moderate to severe peri-implant mucositis, while being absent in cases of mild inflammation, suggesting a relationship between particle burden and clinical severity [23].

Another study reported titanium particles in approximately 85% peri-implant tissue samples and noticed a more intense inflammatory infiltrate surrounding debris in failed implants compared with successful ones [27]. Those findings suggest a potential association between titanium debris accumulation and peri-implant inflammatory severity, although causality cannot be inferred from observational data. Implant failures do not depend solely on the presence of particles (since they are present in most cases), but on the intensity of the immune response that those particles evoke in the surrounding tissue. In failing implants, titanium ceases to be a mechanical support and can become the epicenter of a foreign body reaction that aggressively recruits inflammatory cells, culminating in osteolysis and loss of the device [12, 41, 43].

Although most biological effects of titanium debris appear to remain localized to peri-implant tissues, several studies have reported the detection of titanium in blood components and other organs, suggesting the possibility of systemic translocation of smaller particles via vascular or lymphatic routes [8, 9, 20, 23]. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 6, submicron- and nano-scale particles entering in the blood streem may follow size-dependent biodistribution and clearance pathways mediated by the mononuclear phagocyte system, involving hepatic, splenic, and pulmonary sequestration, with renal elimination likely restricted to the smallest fractions. While the clinical significance of the systemic distribution in implant dentistry remains uncertain, such pathways provide a biologically plausible framework omce fine titanium debris may persist beyond the peri-implant environment and act as long-term biological stressors rather than inert by-products [8, 11, 20, 22, 25].

Despite the consistency of the findings synthesized in this review, several methodological limitations must be acknowledged. A critical and unifying limitation across all selected studies is the absence of direct primary dimensional measurements of titanium particles derived from analytical instrumentation, particularly within the submicron-cale range. The absence of standardized morphometric protocols and reporting criteria further contributes to the heterogeneity among studies. Those methodological constraints have important interpretative implications, as the micro- scale particles are more readily detected by conventional imaging techniques and are overrepresented in the literature, while submicron-scale debris despite growing evidence of biological relevance, remain susceptible to underreporting due to analytical limitations rather than true absence at peri-implant tissues [33, 43].

In this review, particle size characterization relied predominantly on the interpretation of published findings and descriptive morphological criteria. Factors related to image resolution, magnification calibration, threshold definition, particle segmentation, and observer-dependent selection of regions of interest may influence dimensional estimates. In this context, the convergence of physicochemical and biological variables detected across the selected studies underscores the central role of analytical methodology in shaping current interpretations and may partly explain the apparent inconsistencies reported in the literature regarding the presence, distribution, and biological significance of submicron-scale titanium debris [33, 43].
