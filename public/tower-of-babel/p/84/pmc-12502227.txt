Impact of treatment modalities on stress distribution in maxillary incisors with varying levels of external cervical resorption: a finite element analysis
Authors: Ayca Yilmaz, Alper Kabakci, Dilek Helvacioglu Yigit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of various treatment modalities on the mechanical behaviour of a simulated maxillary central incisor with external cervical resorption (ECR) at different progression levels using finite element analysis (FEA).MethodsA three-dimensional (3D) model of a maxillary central incisor and surrounding tissues was created. The model was modified to include vestibular or palatal ECR defects of varying heights and circumferential angles. The root canal and the resorption defect were restored using gutta-percha and various repair materials. The models underwent simulated oblique loading of 100 N on the palatal surface. Von Mises stresses within the dentin were evaluated through FEA.ResultsThe mean maximum stress for models with vestibular ECR defects was greater than that for palatinal defects. Complete filling of both the root canal and defect with BioAggregate resulted in the lowest average dentin stresses (5.79 ± 0.11 MPa), while filling the root canal with gutta-percha and the ECR cavity with glass ionomer cement resulted in the highest (6.19 ± 0.05 MPa).ConclusionsFrom a mechanical standpoint, filling the root canal with gutta-percha and repairing the resorption defect with Biodentine is the appropriate management option when ECR occurs on the vestibular surface.Keywords: Finite element analysis, Stress distribution, External cervical resorption, Root resorption

Background

Root resorption is classified as either physiological or pathological. Physiological resorption is desirable and occurs during the eruption of primary teeth, while pathological resorption affects permanent teeth and is categorized based on location, pathogenesis, or aetiology [1]. Pathological root resorption may occur within the root canal, known as internal root resorption, or on the outer surface of the root, referred to as external root resorption. In severe cases, the resorptive damage can extend into the crown of the tooth [2]. External cervical resorption (ECR) refers to the degradation of mineralized tooth structure caused by odontoclastic activities, typically originating in the cervical aspect of the root. While the main cause of ECR remains uncertain, it is often associated with a breakdown or absence of the protective cementum layer. These resorptive lesions can extend either circumferentially or horizontally in a coronal-apical direction, with advanced cases potentially perforating into the root canal [3]. As the resorptive cavity progresses, the structural integrity of the tooth weakens, particularly under oblique forces, which can generate high lateral forces and increase the fracture risk.

Restoration of the affected teeth with materials capable of withstanding occlusal stresses is imperative. Reviewing the current literature reveals different repair protocols in teeth that require root canal treatment: Obturation of the entire root canal system followed by restoring the ECR cavity, or obturation of the root canal apical to the ECR cavity followed by filling the ECR cavity and the rest of the root canal using repair materials [4, 5]. Common repair materials include resin composites, glass ionomer cement (GIC), mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA), Biodentine, and BioAggregate [6–8].

Finite element analysis (FEA) is a valuable tool for predicting mechanical behavior in complex geometric arrangements. Particularly in dentistry, FEA aids in assessing the mechanical properties of biomaterials and oral tissue that may be challenging to measure in clinical setting [9]. It is widely considered a reliable method for simulating different dental procedures for stress analyses [10, 11]. While previous studies have explored the effects of ECR cavity restoration techniques using FEA, they often exhibit significant limitations due to their narrow focus. Many assess only specific types of defects or utilize models without pulpal involvement, leading to a lack of comprehensive insights [12–15]. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the influence of different treatment modalities on the mechanical behaviour of the maxillary central incisor affected by ECR defects with varying characteristics using FEA.

The following null hypotheses were tested;

ECR defect heights, angles, and locations did not affect stress distribution on dentinal walls.

ECR defect heights, angles, and locations did not affect stress distribution on dentinal walls.

The mechanical behaviour of the simulated tooth was not affected by different treatment modalities utilizing gutta-percha, resin composite, glass ionomer cement, MTA, Biodentine, and BioAggregate.

Methods

Ethical approval was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Istanbul University (approval no. 2022/18). After providing informed consent, a patient donated an intact maxillary central incisor extracted for periodontal reasons. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines. Following access cavity preparation, the working length was determined using a 10# K hand file, and the root canal was subsequently prepared to X3 with the ProTaper Next (Dentsply, Sirona, Ballaigues, Switzerland) rotary system according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Micro-computed tomographic (Micro-CT) imaging using the Skyscan 1275 (Bruker, Kontich, Belgium) system was then performed on the tooth at a pixel size of 8 m x 8 m, from which an STL file was obtained. Additionally, the patient’s cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) scan, obtained for another dental procedure prior to the study, was processed using InVesalius 3.1 and exported as an STL file to generate a bone model of the relevant region. (Figure 1A and B) Fusion 360 (Autodesk Inc., San Rafael, CA, USA) software was utilized to create tooth layers and surrounding tissues based on the STL files. To ensure accurate circumferential spread angles, the center of masses of horizontal slices of the pulp solid at the top and bottom of the desired resorption cavity design was used as the center point. Models featuring a 45-degree circumferential spread had a proportional design with a height of 4.66 for accuracy. Sharp edges of the resorption were rounded in all models to minimize stress concentration. Resorption defects were designed to simulate defects. (Fig. 1C) Fourteen models with different ECR specifications with possible pulpal involvement were modified using three-dimensional (3D) classification based upon ECR height and circumferential spread [16]. (Fig. 1D), and 11 treatment modalities (Table 1) were applied to every ECR model. A total of 155 models were formed with various specifications, including one control tooth model representing an endodontically treated tooth without ECR.

All materials were considered to be homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic. Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio were obtained from the literature and presented in Table 2. Models were imported to ANSYS (Ansys, Inc., Pennsylvania, USA) software program, and meshes were created through 10-node tetrahedral elements with mesh body element size of 0,2 mm. These models were created using approximately 1,836,942 nodes and 1,256,847 tetrahedral solid elements. The most commonly used value, an oblique load of 100 N, was applied at the palatal surface of the tooth angled at 135° with the longitudinal axis of the tooth as a representation of bite force [17–19]. (Fig. 1B) Fixed support was applied to split bone surfaces. The linear analysis was performed under defined loading conditions. The numeric values for average and maximum von Mises stresses (MPa) of dentin were obtained (Fig. 2).

Results

The Von Mises stress failure criterion was utilised to indicate potential tissue damage to the teeth. The results were presented in terms of Von Mises stresses in Figs. 3 and 4. The simulated model without ECR, where root canal treatment was the only treatment performed, exhibited the lowest stress (20.66 MPa) on dentin in terms of maximum stress analysis (Fig. 5).

Among models with the same ECR height, circumferential spread angle, and repair technique, 87% of ECRs had higher maximum stress values when they were located on the vestibular side. The mean maximum stress for vestibular defects was 56.29 MPa with a 95% confidence interval of (46.71 MPA–69.85 MPa) and 35.35 MPa with a 95% confidence interval of (24.82 MPA– 57.18 MPa) for palatally located defects. A higher standard deviation was observed between treatment protocols in teeth with vestibular ECRs (0.48–29.96 MPa) compared to palatal ones (0.9–2.59 MPa), as depicted in Fig. 6. In models with equivalent resorption parameters, ECRs located on the palatal side generally resulted in higher average stresses than those on the vestibular side. The mean stress level on dentin was 6.20 MPa for palatal locations, compared to 5.67 MPa for vestibular locations.

Complete filling of both root canal and the defect with BioAggregate (Model 11) yielded the lowest average stresses (5.79 ± 0.11 MPa) on dentin across all models with consistent location, height, and circumferential spread angle. Conversely, filling the entire root canal system with gutta-percha and restoring the resorptive defect with glass ionomer cement (Model 5) resulted in the highest average dentin stress (6.19 ± 0.05 MPa) in all models with consistent location, height, and circumferential spread angle.

Discussion

Teeth affected by ECR lack a protective wall of intact root dentin, rendering them prone to fractures. Consequently, such teeth heavily rely on external reinforcements due to the absence of dentin walls [3, 15]. In the present study, an in silico model for ECR with different circumferential spread angles, heights, and locations was constructed, and various treatment modalities with different repair materials were compared. A 3D FEA facilitated the exploration of these variations under diverse circumstances, impractical to replicate in vitro. FEA also enabled more precise resorption defect simulations than other methodologies [20, 21]. The von Mises stress is predicated on the total stress field and is a widely used indicator of the possible tissue damage under stress loading [15]. Therefore, the results were presented using the von Mises stress criteria.

Materials used for restoring resorptive defects are frequently in close proximity to the periodontium. Therefore, repair materials having not only good mechanical properties but also biocompatibility and sealing capability are preferred to promote periodontal regeneration and bone repair during the healing process [22]. Recently, Calcium silicate repair materials have emerged as the preferred choice for restoring radicular resorptive defects with their optimal strength, biocompatibility and excellent sealing ability in the presence of moisture. Studies have indicated that the utilization of materials like MTA and Biodentine is highly favourable for restoring resorptive cavities due to their superior properties [23].

The models were designed to mimic routine clinical cases rather than complex ones, limiting height and circumferential spread angle options. Resorption cavities were outlined with edges for efficient classification and better variable observation, with rounded corners for enhanced stress distribution. Consequently, the resorption defects may appear somewhat artificial. An unsupported dentin bridge below the access cavity increased stress levels. Combining the resorption cavity with the access cavity may complicate identifying the upper boundary of the vestibular lesion, leading to sharp palatal surface corners.

Linear static models can’t predict the stress and strain within natural structures in realistic situations. Moreover, the anisotropic and non-homogeneous nature of most dental material must be considered to understand how stresses are distributed within the tooth and how they interact with restorative materials. For example, the anisotropic arrangement of enamel prisms plays a crucial role in effectively dissipating occlusal loads, thereby reducing both tensile and compressive stresses that could lead to enamel fracture, particularly in the cervical region [24, 25]. The dentin structure, however, demonstrates an acceptable response when regarded as an isotropic structure [25, 26]. Nevertheless, in the present study, all materials were considered as linear, isotropic, and homogeneous. This simplification may not accurately reflect the complex mechanical behaviour of dental hard tissues and restorative materials. However, this method can still offer valuable insights into the functionality of dental materials in the oral environment when the limitations and benefits of FEA are understood. Additionally, the complexity of functional loading in patients was not accounted for in the loading scenarios analyzed. This limitation arose from analyzing only one loading condition, leading to the loading application’s nonlinear effects [27]. An oblique load of 100 N was applied to simulate functional loading, approximating axial and horizontal occlusal forces; however, it does not fully capture the complexity of masticatory dynamics. Masticatory forces are dynamic and complex loads with varying direction and magnitude influenced by multiple factors, including the location in the mouth, muscle size, bone shape, and patient-specific characteristics such as age and gender. They are difficult to numerically model in FEA studies. Therefore, using static loading conditions is considered one of the limitations of the present study. In all FEA models, the stresses on the vestibular sides of the teeth were higher than those on the palatal sides. This observation may be attributed to an oblique force acting on the occlusal plane. The force is applied to the palatal surface and directed toward the vestibular direction, generating flexion in that direction.

Our study’s findings align with previous research that highlights the critical role of specific repair material selection in maintaining the strength of the tooth with an ECR defect. The present study findings demonstrated that filling the entire root canal system with BioAggregate resulted in the lowest maximum dentin stresses (5.79 MPa) compared to Gutta-percha and GIC (6.19 MPa), indicating BioAggregate’s superior biomechanical stability. In line with this, Akgun & Kalyoncuoglu [15] reported that their FEA revealed the highest von Mises stress concentrations when GIC was used as the repair material. In contrast, both Biodentine and MTA produced significantly lower stress values within the dentin, with Biodentine exhibiting the lowest stresses, with the ERR defect located in various regions of the root. Their results support the conclusion that these calcium silicate materials are more effective at reducing stress transmission and potential safety factors compared to GIC. Manaktala et al. [11] reported higher stress concentrations in models restored with MTA or Biodentine compared to intact teeth, with subcrestal lesions generating greater stresses than supracrestal ones. Overall stress levels in their models of MTA and Biodentine restorations were similar, due to the similar structure and composition. Additionally, Rajawat & Kaushik [13] compared the stress distributions in teeth with varying sizes of ECR defects and found that BioAggregate resulted in the least dentinal stresses among the tested materials, particularly in models with larger lesions. This reinforces the idea that calcium silicate materials with elastic moduli similar to dentin improve stress mitigation. In another study, Biodentine was found to have superior biomechanical properties compared to highly viscous GIC, achieving lower stress values and optimal stress distribution, especially for lesions in close proximity to the pulp, regardless of the root canal being treated [12]. Coban Oksuzer & Sanal Cikman [28] showed that Biodentine, BioAggregate, and calcium-enriched mixture (CEM) produced lower von Mises stresses in dentin compared to MTA, GIC, and resin-modified GIC, further supporting these findings. Our study findings highlight the importance of using calcium silicate materials in decreasing stress values in the dentin. Materials with a modulus of elasticity closest to dentin should be preferred for the repair of ECR defects. The strength of our study lies in its comprehensive evaluation of both defect location (vestibular vs. palatal) and various treatment strategies, as well as a range of materials, within a single, systematic model. This approach provides for a detailed understanding of how these parameters influence stress distribution, providing insights into ECR treatment.

Our study’s findings also highlight the importance of the ECR defect site. Filling the entire root canal and resorption defect with BioAggregate resulted in the lowest average dentinal stress across all models. However, it also led to the highest maximum stress in specific vestibular models, raising concerns about its suitability for this region. Filling the root canal either fully or partially with biomaterial caused higher maximum stresses on dentin in vestibular models due to its conduction to the wall of the root canal surface in that direction. Based on our analysis, filling the root canal with gutta-percha and using Biodentine to repair resorptive defects seems to be the most appropriate choice for managing vestibular resorptions. Averaged stress provides insight into quantitative results of the overall values of von Mises equivalent in dentin and potential long-term behaviour of the material under physiological conditions. The maximum stress indicates the peak value of von Mises stress, which indicates the areas more susceptible to fracture under the applied load. Clinicians should consider both values when selecting suitable restorative materials.

In palatal models, maximum stress values on dentin were largely similar except for one model. Filling the entire root canal with gutta-percha and repairing resorptive defects with glass ionomer cement significantly reduced maximum stress on dentin in palatal models. In another simulation of our experiment, we attempted to repair a radicular defect using GIC, while filling the supragingival portion with composite resin. This approach resulted in an increase in maximum stress to a level comparable to that of other models. This highlights the importance of choosing the appropriate filling material in the cervical region [27]. The superior mechanical properties of glass ionomer cement over composite resin play a role in this outcome. However, these materials have inferior aesthetic properties due to limited color options [27, 29, 30]. Additionally, GICs are less resistant to abrasion, leading to increased surface roughness over time. Composite resins, on the other hand, have smoother surfaces due to reduced particle size. In an effort to enhance the properties of glass ionomer cement, resin-modified glass ionomer cement has been developed, which may be beneficial in such scenarios.

While the immediate mechanical processes under stress loading can be effectively simulated with FEA models, they do not reflect the performance of materials over time. These models focus solely on stress distribution on the modelled teeth at the time of application and do not consider oral environmental factors, such as bonding failures, microleakage and microbial contamination. Establishing a direct clinical interpretation between the models and treatment outcomes is challenging. However, they enable comparison between materials under standard conditions and are helpful for explorative preclinical investigations.

Conclusions

Vestibular ECRs tend to cause higher maximum stress on dentin compared to palatal ECRs, making them more susceptible to fractures. The selection of an appropriate repair protocol for ECR is crucial, particularly when it is located in the vestibular aspect of the tooth. A combined treatment approach using gutta-percha to fill the root canal and Biodentine to repair the resorption defect is the most suitable option for managing vestibular ECRs.
