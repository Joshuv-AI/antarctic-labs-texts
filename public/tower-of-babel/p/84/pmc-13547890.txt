Accuracy of data routinely collected by Australian hospitals for identifying infective endocarditis, causative organisms and clinical outcomes: a diagnostic accuracy study
Authors: Ruturaj Vaidya, Nalin Kataria, Jason Zou, Quan Li, Sunnya Khawaja, Trang Dang, Susan Smith, Katherine Lau, Russell Denman, Yong Wee, Bronwyn Learmouth, Bo Janoschka, John Sedgwick, Robert Horvath, Isuru Ranasinghe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfective endocarditis (IE) is a life‐threatening condition, and the surveillance of the incidence is important for monitoring disease trends. Administrative data routinely collected by hospitals offer a feasible and inexpensive method for surveillance of patients with IE, yet the accuracy of coding of IE, causative organisms and outcomes is uncertain.AimTo evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of routinely collected Australian hospital administrative data for identifying IE, causative organisms and selected clinical outcomes.MethodsUsing a cross‐sectional design, we included all hospitalisations with International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision, Australian Modification (ICD10‐AM) codes for IE from 1 January 2020 to 30 June 2023 at a quaternary referral centre. IE cases not captured by coding were ascertained by reviewing clinical data sources (echocardiogram reports, cardiothoracic surgical and device implantation registries and an IE biobank). We used a definite, possible or pathological diagnosis of IE as per the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Modified criteria for IE as the reference standard for diagnosing IE.ResultsWe identified 353 hospitalisations with principal or secondary ICD10‐AM diagnosis codes at discharge. Of these, 313 patients had IE as per the ESC Modified criteria. A further 51 patients (14%) with IE meeting the ESC modified criteria = were found not captured by coding during the same period. ICD10‐AM codes for IE had an overall sensitivity of 0.86 (95% confidence interval (CI) 0.82–0.90), specificity of 1.00 (95% CI 1.00–1.00), a positive predictive value of 0.89 (95% CI 0.85–0.92) and a negative predictive value of 1.00 (95% CI 0.99–1.00). Coding identified the causative organism with an overall sensitivity of 0.77 (95% CI 0.72–0.81) and specificity of 1.00 (95% CI 1.00–1.00), with high accuracy for the most common organism, Staphylococcus aureus (sensitivity 0.88, 95% CI 0.80–0.93; specificity 0.95, 95% CI 0.89–0.98). Coding demonstrated high accuracy for detection of in‐hospital deaths (sensitivity 0.91, 95% CI 0.75–0.98; specificity 1.00, 95% CI 1.00–1.00) and surgical management (sensitivity 0.91, 95% CI 0.86–0.94; specificity 1.00, 95% CI 1.00–1.00) with lower sensitivity and specificity for other outcomes.ConclusionICD10‐AM codes offer reasonable sensitivity and excellent specificity for the diagnosis of IE, common organisms and selected clinical outcomes and can be utilised for surveillance of IE.

Introduction

Infective endocarditis (IE) is a rare life‐threatening condition involving infection and inflammation of the heart chambers, valves and intracardiac prosthetic devices. IE has a changing disease epidemiology with an increasing incidence of healthcare‐associated IE 1 and Staphylococcus aureus, a part of the normal skin flora, now the most prevalent causative agent in Australia and internationally. 2 , 3 Obfuscating the rarity of IE is its varied clinical presentation. IE can present as an acute or subacute pathology depending on the causative organism with a variety of vascular, neurological and immunological phenomena. 4 , 5 Only 25% of patients have clinical evidence of embolic phenomena, and most patients present with non‐specific symptoms such as fevers and lethargy. 5 Patients also often present with complications of IE such as stroke, valvular disease or sepsis. 4 , 5 Due to the life‐threatening complications and high mortality associated with IE, as well as its increasing association with prosthetic cardiac valves, implants and preventable healthcare‐associated infections, surveillance of the incidence and outcomes is important to improve care. However, determining the true incidence and outcomes of IE remains challenging, as very few countries have surveillance systems for IE case identification. 6

In this context, routinely collected healthcare data offer a time‐ and labour‐efficient method of identifying patients with IE, the causative organisms involved and potential complications. 7 Prior research from a Canadian hospital found hospital administrative data coded using International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD10) coding as a sensitive (0.90) and specific (1.0) predictor of IE diagnosis when compared to definite or possible Duke criteria, 8 the clinical reference standard for diagnosis of IE. 9 Similarly, a French study reported a good positive predictive value (PPV) of 0.87 when using ICD10 coding for detecting IE and an excellent sensitivity (0.90) and specificity (1) for detecting the surgical management of IE. 10 A similar analysis from two large UK hospitals reported a good PPV of 0.82 when utilising the ICD10 code I33 (acute and subacute endocarditis) 11 but lower accuracy for other potential ICD10 codes. Work in drug‐use‐associated IE has shown mixed results. 12 , 13

In Australia, all hospitals routinely collect data on episodes of care using nationally standardised data definitions. In these administrative datasets, diagnoses are coded based on the ICD10, Australian Modification (ICD10‐AM). Multicentre validation of coding against clinical records in Australia has shown good accuracy of ICD10‐AM‐coded data in general for most common conditions, with cardiac conditions and procedures generally well coded, 14 , 15 yet none have tested the accuracy of ICD10‐AM coding of IE. Additionally, ICD10‐AM codes capture the causative organism, and interventions, including surgery, along with the complications of IE, are captured using procedure codes that are reported alongside diagnoses codes; however, whether these measures can accurately be identified using coding alone is uncertain.

Accordingly, in this study, we sought to determine the diagnostic accuracy of ICD10‐AM coding for identifying possible or definitive cases of IE. We also sought to assess whether causative organisms and outcomes of IE, including common complications, can be accurately identified from coded data.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a cross‐sectional diagnostic accuracy study utilising existing data sources on IE located at The Prince Charles Hospital (TPCH), a quaternary referral centre for the state of Queensland and Northern New South Wales in Brisbane, Australia with cardiovascular and cardiothoracic surgical facilities.

Study cohort

The study cohort consisted of consecutive adult patients aged ≥18 years discharged with an administrative diagnostic code of IE or a clinical diagnosis of IE between 1 January 2020 and 30 June 2023. We included patients who were diagnosed with IE at a different hospital but were subsequently transferred to TPCH to receive further diagnostic testing and intervention such as cardiac surgery. Only patients admitted as inpatients for acute IE were included. Patients who had a remote history of IE or those who were admitted electively for cardiac valvular surgery following completed treatment of a prior episode of acute IE were excluded. Cases of non‐bacterial thrombotic endocarditis, such as Libman‐Sacks endocarditis, were also excluded. Patients with multiple re‐admissions for the same clinical episode of IE during the study period were included only once. However, if the patients were re‐admitted for distinct, separate episodes of IE, then each episode was included as an independent entry.

Hospital‐coded data

All hospital encounters in Australia are coded using a standardised set of variables including patient demographic characteristics, admission and separation dates, admission status (acute or elective), principal and secondary diagnoses, all procedures performed and patient status at discharge according to the Australian National Coding Standards. All diagnoses and procedures are coded respectively as per the ICD10‐AM and the Australian Classification of Healthcare Interventions (ACHI) systems. Coding is performed at the conclusion of a patient's episode of medical care by professional medical coders, translating clinical information, which consists of diagnoses, interventions and complications from patient health records into alphanumeric ICD10‐AM and ACHI codes.

The ICD10‐AM coding data were obtained from the hospital's clinical health information services to identify all cases that were coded as IE. The following ICD10‐AM codes (including all subcategories) for a principal or secondary diagnosis were used to identify patients with IE: I33 (Acute and subacute endocarditis), I38 (Endocarditis, valve unspecified) and I39 (Endocarditis and heart valve disorders in diseases classified elsewhere).

We identified common organisms from coded data using ICD10‐AM disease codes provided in Appendix S1. Similarly, we identified a range of common outcomes and complications of IE using ICD10‐AM and ACHI procedure codes (see Appendix S2 for codes used to define these outcomes). Inpatient mortality was identified using the separation mode variable, which records discharge disposition (e.g. discharged home, died in hospital, transferred).

During a single inpatient admission, patients often undergo multiple coding episode changes, especially when transferred between different medical teams. Despite belonging to the same admission, these episodes may be assigned different ICD10‐AM and ACHI codes. To ensure comprehensive and accurate coding capture, all episodes within the same admission were collated, allowing us to consolidate all relevant codes associated with each admission.

Identifying patients not captured by coding

For full IE case ascertainment, we reviewed the hospital's existing clinical data sources to identify patients with a diagnosis of IE over the same study period. These include: (i) Queensland Cardiac Outcomes Registry – Cardiothoracic Surgery Database, which records all patients who have undergone cardiac surgery including for IE at the hospital using standardised data definitions; (ii) cardiovascular implantable electronic devices (CIEDs; pacemakers and defibrillators) lead extraction dataset, which records patients undergoing lead extraction and device removal for patients with suspected device infection including those with IE; (iii) hospital echocardiographic database for echocardiogram reports using the search term ‘infective endocarditis’; and (iv) Infective Endocarditis Queensland Biobank, which consists of patients who have consented to providing blood and tissue samples for future IE research.

Clinical reference standard

We used the 2023 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Modified Criteria for Infective Endocarditis as the clinical reference standard for diagnosis of IE. A patient was deemed to have a diagnosis of IE if they had definite, possible or pathological diagnosis of IE based on the 2023 ESC Modified criteria. 16 The ESC Modified Criteria were determined by searching the patient's clinical and electronic health records, admission notes, electronic discharge summaries, blood test results, echocardiogram reports and other imaging findings.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as means ± standard deviation and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. The ability of ICD10‐AM coding to discriminate cases of IE versus those without IE were assessed using the 2 × 2 contingency table analysis method. We calculated the sensitivity, specificity, PPV and negative predictive value (NPV) using this approach by comparing the ICD10‐AM coding with the clinical reference standard. Further sensitivity analyses were undertaken for subgroups such as definite versus possible IE as per the ESC Modified Criteria for IE, principal versus secondary IE coding diagnosis, and specific codes (I33 alone versus I33, I38 and I39 in combination). The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV were calculated using normal approximation (Wald method). Additionally, the Cohen kappa method was used to calculate the agreement between ICD10‐AM codes and the ESC Modified Criteria for the diagnosis of IE. A two‐sided P‐value <0.05 was indicative of statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS Software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The study findings were reported using the Standards for Reporting Diagnostic accuracy studies guidelines. 17

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Metro North Hospital and Health Service Human Research Ethics Committee (102516).

Results

From 1 January 2020 to 30 June 2023, we identified 353 hospitalisations with a discharge diagnosis of IE based on ICD10‐AM codes specific for IE. The most common code was I33.0 with 326 patients, followed by the I38.0 and I39.0 codes with 26 and 1 patient(s) respectively. Of these, 211 hospitalisations had IE coded as a principal diagnosis, with the remainder having IE coded as a secondary diagnosis. Of the coded cases, 313 patients (86%) had IE as per the ESC Modified Criteria.

During the same period, we identified an additional 51 (14%) patients who had a diagnosis of IE based on our search of clinical records and datasets that were not captured by coding. There were 1145 echocardiogram reports with the term IE from which we found 31 unique additional cases of IE. There were 133 patients that underwent surgery for active IE during the study period, from which we found seven additional cases of IE. The lead extraction dataset provided a further nine additional cases, and the ieQ biobank revealed four more cases, as seen in Figure 1.

Diagnostic performance of ICD10‐AM codes for IE

Of the 353 patients that had a diagnosis of IE based on ICD10‐AM coding, 313 of these patients had definite, possible or pathological diagnosis of IE based on the ESC Modified Criteria. Forty patients did not meet the ESC Modified Criteria for diagnosis of IE and were identified as false positives according to the study criteria (i.e. not considered IE). All 51 additional cases of IE that were identified from other clinical datasets (and were not captured based on ICD10‐AM coding) met criteria for IE based on the ESC Modified Criteria.

Using the ESC Modified Criteria as a reference standard for a diagnosis of IE, the ICD10‐AM codes for IE had a sensitivity of 0.86 (95% CI 0.82–0.90), specificity of 1.00 (95% CI 1.00–1.00), PPV of 0.89 (95% CI 0.85–0.92), NPV of 1.00 (95% CI 0.99–1.00) and a Cohen kappa (κ) value of 0.87 (Table 2).

Sensitivity or subgroup analysis

Of the three ICD10‐AM codes for IE (I33, I38 or I39), the I33 code was significantly more sensitive, 0.84 (95% CI 0.80–0.87), and had a higher PPV, 0.94 (95% CI 0.90–0.96), compared to the I38 code, which had a sensitivity and PPV of 0.06 (95% CI 0.04–0.09) and 0.88 (95% CI 0.71–0.96) respectively. Both the I33 and I38 codes had similar specificity and NPV (Table 2).

When IE was coded as a principal diagnosis only, the ICD10‐AM codes demonstrated a reduction in sensitivity to 0.54 (95% CI 0.49–0.59) while maintaining high specificity, PPV and NPV (Table 2).

The ICD10‐AM coding demonstrated good accuracy for identifying cases classified as definite/pathological IE or possible IE according to the ESC Modified Criteria, with similar sensitivity, specificity and NPV across both groups. However, the PPV was significantly higher for cases meeting the definite or pathological IE criteria, 0.67 (95% CI 0.62–0.72), compared to the cases meeting the possible IE criteria alone, 0.20 (95% CI 0.17–0.25) (Table 2).

Diagnostic performance of ICD10‐AM codes for identifying the causative organism

As expected, the organisms most typically associated with IE in the literature were also the predominant organisms identified in our study. Staphylococcus spp. accounted for 161 cases of IE; of these, S. aureus was associated with 113 cases. Other commonly identified organisms included Streptococcal spp., which accounted for 83 patients, and Enterococcus spp., which caused IE in 45 patients.

Overall, the ICD10‐AM codes were accurate for detecting the most common causative pathogens of IE, with an overall sensitivity of 0.77 (95% CI 0.72–0.81), specificity of 1.00 (95% CI 1.00–1.00), PPV of 0.70 (95% CI 0.66–0.75), NPV of 1.00 (95% CI 1.00–1.00) and a Cohen kappa (κ) value of 0.74 (Table 3).

Diagnostic performance of ICD10‐AM and ACHI codes for identifying outcomes of endocarditis

Overall, 29 patients (8%) died during their hospital admission with a sensitivity of 0.91 (95% CI 0.75–0.98), specificity of 1.00 (95% CI 1.00–1.00), PPV of 1.00 (95% CI 0.88–1.00), NPV of 1.00 (95% CI 1.00–1.00) and a Cohen kappa (κ) value of 0.95 for the detection of in‐hospital death.

Acute heart failure was the most frequently observed IE complication, occurring in 128 patients. ICD10‐AM codes were associated with a poor sensitivity for the detection of heart failure, 0.45 (95% CI 0.36–0.54); however, it had excellent specificity – 1.00 (95% CI 1.00–1.00), PPV – 0.89 (95% CI 0.79–0.95) and NPV – 1.00 (95% CI 1.00–1.00). Similarly, the ICD10‐AM codes demonstrated poor sensitivities for the detection of other complications, such as cardiogenic or septic shock, stroke, pulmonary embolism and heart block (Table 5).

Discussion

We performed a cross‐sectional study to determine the diagnostic accuracy of ICD10‐AM and ACHI coding for identifying cases, causative organisms and clinical outcomes of IE within a large quaternary cardiothoracic referral centre in Australia. Overall, the agreement between the ICD10‐AM codes for IE and the clinical reference standard for IE was high, supported by a Cohen kappa (κ) value of 0.87. Similarly, coding accuracy was high for detecting the most common organisms – the Staphylococcus spp., for identifying surgical management and inpatient mortality. However, the accuracy of the coding to identify other organisms and complications was more modest. The excellent specificity and NPV across all our analyses reflect the large number of true negatives in our cohort, which is typical of a high‐volume referral centre where IE remains rare. Overall, the study supports the use of ICD10‐AM coding for surveillance of the incidence of IE, in particular IE caused by S. aureus, as well as for monitoring of selected clinical outcomes of surgical intervention and deaths.

Prior studies validated the use of administrative coding against a clinical diagnosis of IE using the Modified Criteria. 8 , 10 , 11 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Of these, four studies employed the ICD10 coding system, reporting sensitivities between 0.74 and 0.93 and specificities between 0.47 and 1.0, confirming that coding systems can identify IE with moderate to high accuracy. 8 , 10 , 11 , 22 However, these studies were limited to European and North American cohorts and primarily assessed case detection alone. Our study extends this literature in three important ways. It provides the first validation of ICD10‐AM and ACHI codes in an Australian setting, demonstrating that routinely collected administrative data can be a reliable foundation for national IE surveillance and research. To our knowledge, it is also the first study worldwide to systematically assess the accuracy of coding for clinical outcomes of IE, including complications, thereby broadening the scope of coding validation beyond simple case identification. Finally, by evaluating coding accuracy for both surgical management and microbiological aetiology, we demonstrate that the ICD10‐AM codes can capture valvular surgery, device extraction and common pathogens such as S. aureus with high sensitivity. Taken together, these findings extend the current literature regarding the utility of administrative data for IE and suggest that it can inform not only disease surveillance but also microbiological trends and selected clinical outcomes such as incidence of surgical intervention for management.

Our findings also shed light on an important consideration when using and interpreting coded data to estimate IE incidence. We found that limiting case ascertainment to only patients with a principal diagnosis code of IE markedly reduced case detection, with a poor sensitivity of 0.54, meaning that this will underestimate the true incidence of IE by almost half. This underestimation may occur because ICD coding defines the principal diagnosis as the condition established to be chiefly responsible for the admission or resource use. For example, in cases with sepsis, acute heart failure or stroke related to IE, the complication may be coded as the principal diagnosis because this was the immediate reason for clinical intervention or extended care. The accurate identification of IE cannot rely on the principal diagnosis codes alone, and inclusion of secondary diagnosis codes is essential to ensure complete case capture. Recognising and addressing this limitation is crucial to strengthening the validity of administrative datasets for the surveillance of IE.

Furthermore, our study identified a small number of false negatives and false positives, largely attributable to discrepancies between coded data and clinical documentation. False negatives were most linked to incomplete or ambiguous documentation of IE diagnosis, causative organisms or complications in clinical records and discharge summaries. This was particularly evident in procedural admissions, where documentation often centred on the procedure itself without explicitly noting IE. Outcomes that are objective and discrete, such as surgical management and inpatient mortality, were reliably documented and accurately coded, whereas conditions with less distinct clinical thresholds or variable presentations, such as heart failure or stroke, were frequently under‐recognised or described in vague terms, leading to missed diagnoses. Furthermore, diagnostic ambiguity also contributed to the false negatives, as cases of bacteraemia or sepsis were coded under these diagnoses despite meeting the ESC Modified Criteria for IE. In contrast, false positives most frequently occurred when IE codes were assigned to patients with a remote history of the disease, alternative valvular pathology or suspected IE that was subsequently excluded after investigation. Collectively, these findings underscore how limitations in clinical documentation and diagnostic uncertainty constrain the accuracy of administrative coding for IE.

Our findings have important implications for hospitals, health services and policymakers. For hospitals, the validated use of ICD10‐AM and ACHI coding enables reliable identification of IE cases, facilitating internal audits, and supports benchmarking of clinical outcomes. At the health service level, accurate case capture across multiple hospitals enables potentially precise estimates of disease burden, surgical caseloads, pathogen distribution and mortality rates, providing important data for health system capacity planning and resource allocation. For policymakers, use of administrative coding provides a feasible solution to determine national estimates of incidence and mortality, monitor surgical activity and evaluate geographic variation in disease burden. Importantly, our finding of high coding accuracy for S. aureus, a common and preventable cause of hospital acquired IE, 23 underscores the value of administrative data in infection surveillance, which may inform targeted infection control strategies.

Our study was a single‐centre study at a tertiary referral centre in Australia. Because of this, there may be a degree of selection bias, as this centre receives a high volume of transfers for the surgical management of IE, which results in a higher prevalence of IE and higher surgical intervention rates compared to the general population. Additionally, the presence of dedicated cardiac stream coders at this centre may have contributed to higher coding accuracy than would be expected at non‐specialist centres. Thus, coding validation across multiple centres with a broader case mix would improve the generalisability of our results. Furthermore, we recognise that the ESC (and other) diagnostic criteria are not without limitations and that the ‘Rejected IE’ category may be susceptible to misclassification in borderline cases; however, the high specificity and NPV of the 2023 ESC Modified Criteria 24 justify its use in this context. Additionally, despite our rigorous case ascertainment process, some cases of IE may have been missed, particularly among patients without echocardiographic evidence and those who did not undergo surgical intervention. Moreover, patients who died in the emergency department or were palliated due to severe comorbidities may not have received a diagnosis of IE, leading to further underestimation of true IE case burden. Finally, our study excluded certain codes that may be indicative of an IE diagnosis – such as T82.6 and T82.7 (infection of cardiac prosthesis, devices and/or implants) and B37.6 (candidal endocarditis). These codes were omitted because they are not specific for IE and thus could have led to the overestimation of true IE cases or because they were exceedingly rare, although this may also have led to the omission of a small number of relevant patients.

Conclusion

In this single‐centre study, ICD10‐AM and ACHI procedure codes demonstrated reasonable sensitivity and specificity for the identification of IE, common causative organisms and selected clinical outcomes. These findings support the potential utility of administrative coding data for the surveillance of IE.
