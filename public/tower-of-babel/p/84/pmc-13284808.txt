Linking neurogenesis, oligodendrogenesis, and myelination defects to neurodevelopmental disruption in primary mitochondrial disorders
Authors: Sahitya Ranjan Biswas, Porter L Tomsick, Alicia M Pickrell, Paul D Morton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary mitochondrial disorders (PMDs) are inherited metabolic diseases that most often present with neurological symptoms in infancy or adolescence, underscoring the central importance of mitochondrial function to brain health. Historically, the field has emphasized neurodegeneration—consistent with the high energetic demands of postmitotic neurons. However, neurodevelopmental manifestations are now recognized as common early phenotypes, frequently preceding clinical regression in many PMDs. Given the pivotal role of mitochondria in neural stem/progenitor cell maintenance and cell fate decisions, defects in the respiratory chain are poised to disrupt neurogenesis and gliogenesis. Evidence for such developmental vulnerabilities is reviewed here. Likewise, because mitochondrial metabolism and dynamics shift across the oligodendrocyte lineage—from oligodendrocyte precursor cell expansion to differentiation and the energetically intensive phase of myelin synthesis—callosal atrophy in mitochondrial leukoencephalopathies may, at least in part, reflect developmental shortcomings in oligodendrogenesis and myelination. This possibility warrants focused investigation in cellular and in vivo models.Keywords: mitochondria, mitochondria disorders, neural stem cells, oligodendrocytes, white matter

The brain is the most energy‐demanding organ in the human body, relying heavily on mitochondria not only for ATP production but also for metabolite shuttling, redox and calcium homeostasis, and signaling pathways that influence cell fate [1, 2, 3]. This dependency makes the brain particularly vulnerable to mitochondrial dysfunction, whether due to aging or genetic mutations, with dysfunction often resulting in neurodegenerative disorders or disease. Although neurodegeneration dominates the clinical presentation of many mitochondrial disorders, mitochondria are equally critical for multipotent neural stem and progenitor cells (NSPCs), which are the precursors for most cells in the brain [4, 5, 6]. Recent research has revealed that mitochondrial metabolism plays a pivotal role in stem cell reprogramming and lineage specification and that disruptions in these pathways can derail normal brain development [7, 8, 9, 10].

While neurogenesis is predominantly an embryonic process, it extends into the postnatal period in mammals, supporting circuit growth and refinement. Embryonic neural stem cells (NSCs) primarily rely on glycolysis, consistent with the hypoxic fetal environment [11]. However, recent evidence suggests that oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) play important roles in NSC proliferation, maintenance, and their transition into intermediate progenitors and neurons [4, 5]. Moreover, the perinatal transition introduces a shift toward OXPHOS, which becomes essential for NSC activation and differentiation [11]. Consequently, defects in respiratory chain function—such as those observed in primary mitochondrial disorders (PMDs) that present in childhood with features such as developmental delays and regression—are likely to disrupt postnatal neural development.

Oligodendrocyte lineage progression offers a complementary example that mitochondrial function is tightly linked to cell fate. Oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) proliferate early in life, while their differentiation and myelination continue over an extended postnatal period [12, 13]. Mitochondrial dynamics and bioenergetics evolve throughout this trajectory: differentiating oligodendrocytes exhibit increased morphological remodeling and motility, which is inferred to influence OXPHOS and ATP demand to support lipid synthesis and myelin sheath formation [14]. Disruptions in respiratory chain activity or mitochondrial dynamics (fission and fusion) impair oligodendrocyte maturation and myelination, providing a mechanistic link between mitochondrial dysfunction and the white matter abnormalities observed in PMDs and secondary mitochondrial disorders [15, 16, 17, 18]. In this review, we have explored the role of mitochondria in neurogenesis and oligodendrogenesis, highlighting evidence of impaired neurogenesis and oligodendrogenesis/myelination in both in vivo and in vitro models of PMDs, as well as commenting on evidence in affected human patients.

Embryonic, postnatal, and adult neurogenesis

The cellular heterogeneity observed in the adult mammalian brain is primarily established during embryonic and early postnatal development [19, 20]. This vastness in diversity arises from a pool of neural progenitor and stem cells, which span the apical–basal axis of the developing mammalian brain. These progenitors have been extensively studied in the developing neocortex [21]. Neural epithelial (NE) cells are the earliest progenitors residing in the developing neural tube with the capacity to self‐renew [21]. Initially, NE cells undergo symmetric self‐renewing divisions to expand the stem cell pool. Subsequently, they switch to asymmetric division to transition into more fate‐restricted radial glial cells [22]. Radial glial (RG) cells, which reside in the ventricular zone (VZ) and display glial features, continue to divide for expansion and also undergo asymmetric divisions to produce both self‐renewing stem cells and more differentiated daughter cells termed basal progenitors [23, 24]. These intermediate progenitors constituting the subventricular zone (SVZ) subsequently undergo symmetric divisions to generate neurons, which then populate the cortical layers [25, 26]. This process is protracted in human brains undergoing long gestational periods and is considered a key evolutionary feature affording humans with an enlarged neocortex. There is also a more direct conversion to neurons, bypassing the intermediate basal progenitor stage, which appears to supply neurons to the olfactory bulb (OB) [27].

The newly generated intermediate progenitors and immature neurons follow two major migration modes to assemble the cortical architecture: radial and tangential migration. In radial migration, which is the primary mechanism responsible for cerebrum formation, neurons derived from the pallial regions of the SVZ migrate perpendicularly toward the cortical surface [28, 29]. During this process, newly generated neurons utilize radial glial fibers as scaffolds to reach their final destinations in the upper cortical layers [30]. Conversely, tangential migration is predominantly employed by inhibitory GABAergic interneurons originating from the ganglionic eminences. These interneurons migrate parallel to the cortical surface through the cortical plate and eventually extend their axons to establish appropriate synaptic connections with their targets [31]. Progenitors residing in the medial and lateral ganglionic eminences also supply medium spiny neurons to the striatum [32, 33, 34].

To support the functional and cellular complexity of distinct brain regions, neurodevelopment continues beyond birth, particularly in higher‐order mammals [35, 36]. Neurogenesis continues in the ventricular–subventricular zone (V‐SVZ) and the subgranular zone (SGZ) of the hippocampus during infancy (Fig. 1 ) [36, 37, 38, 39, 40]. In humans, the migration of progenitors from the SVZ to the olfactory bulb declines significantly after early childhood possibly reflecting an evolutionary shift as humans are more reliant on vision [41]. Adult hippocampal neurogenesis in humans remains a subject of active debate: While some studies, using meticulous tissue processing, report low‐level persistence, others find it to be rare or virtually absent [37, 42, 43, 44]. In adult rodents, neurogenesis in the hippocampus continues throughout life, and its disruption impairs pattern separation, namely the ability to accurately discriminate between similar experiences [45].

Postnatal neurogenesis is driven by a specialized subset of radial glial cells that diverge from the rapidly proliferating embryonic radial glia by mid‐gestation, transitioning into a relatively quiescent state within adult neurogenic niches [46, 47]. NSCs in SVZ were thought to follow the proliferation pattern of embryonic radial glia, initially maintaining the stem cell pool by asymmetric division while maintaining differentiated progeny, ultimately resorting to exhaustive symmetric division to generate immature neurons [48]. However, Obernier et al. demonstrated that in mice, postnatal NSCs follow a pattern of division that uncouples self‐renewal from generation of progeny. In this pattern, 20–30% of postnatal NSCs maintain the stem cell pool by symmetric renewal and can remain in SVZ for several months, whereas the rest undergo consuming division to generate progeny, leading to the eventual depletion of NSCs over time [49].

The regional patterning of the embryonic ganglionic eminences surrounding the lateral ventricle is preserved in the adult SVZ: the dorsal pallium gives rise to the dorsal roof, while the lateral and medial ganglionic eminences contribute to the formation of the lateral wall and ventral tip [50, 51]. This spatial heterogeneity is linked to the differential expression of transcription factors such as EMX1 and PAX6 in dorsal domains; DLX2, GSH2, and NKX2.1 in lateral and ventral domains; and ZIC1/2 in medial domains [52, 53, 54, 55] (Fig. 2). Distinct progenitor domains continue to contribute to the generation of diverse interneuron subtypes, mainly supplying neurons in the OB of rodents throughout adulthood [56, 57]. However, studies using newborn neuron markers and the 5‐HT3A fluorescent reporter mice to track adolescent neuronal migration have reported the movement of newly generated neurons from the SVZ to the ventral striatum [58]. Additionally, postnatal neuronal migration has been observed in the olfactory tubercle and the islands of Calleja, suggesting ongoing neurogenesis of a small population of striatal medium spiny neurons after birth [59, 60]. Using histological techniques and carbon‐14 birthdating, Ernst et al. demonstrated the integration of newly generated neurons into the striatum of the adult human brain and observed a preferential decline in these postnatally born neurons in patients with Huntington's disease [61]. Given the presence of distinct neuronal migratory streams in both the postnatal and adult brain, it would be valuable to investigate the extent and regulation of neurogenesis along these routes [41, 62].

In contrast, the SGZ originates from the embryonic medial pallium over a more extended period. Progenitor cells migrate from the embryonic dentate neuroepithelium via the dentate migratory stream and eventually settle in the SGZ, where they transition to quiescent NSCs, primarily during the postnatal period [63, 64, 65]. SGZ stem and progenitor cells are classified as type I–III, encompassing radial glia‐like cells, intermediate progenitors, and immature neurons, respectively [26]. Live imaging studies suggest that radial glia‐like NSCs in SGZ undergo an initial proliferative phase characterized by symmetric divisions, followed by a neurogenic phase involving asymmetric divisions, generating excitatory neurons—the dentate gyrus granule cell—and, to a lesser extent, astrocytes [66]. In rodents, granule cell neurogenesis peaks during the first postnatal week, and around this time, the SGZ becomes morphologically distinct [67]. Neurogenesis in the SGZ has also been suggested to support both episodic memory formation and mood regulation [68, 69].

The dynamic shifts of cells through the neurogenic lineage are accompanied by continuous changes in energy requirements. Mitochondrial metabolism helps these cells to meet these changing needs by adjusting both the electron transport chain activity and overall mitochondrial content, supporting the energy requirements of each developmental stage.

Mitochondrial bioenergetics in neurogenesis

Embryonic NSCs, like other somatic stem cells, rely primarily on glycolysis to maintain their stemness [11, 70]. Glycolysis provides the ATP, reducing equivalents, and biosynthetic intermediates necessary to maintain the self‐renewal capacity of stem cells [8, 71, 72, 73]. By manipulating the rate‐limiting glycolytic enzyme phosphofructokinase 1, Lang et al. have demonstrated that reduced glycolysis results in decreased expansion and maintenance of neural progenitors in the embryonic mouse cortex [11]. Furthermore, proliferative NSCs in culture exhibited enrichment of hypoxia‐inducible factor 1 (HIF1)‐dependent glycolytic genes, underscoring the critical role of glycolysis during the hypoxic embryonic stage [11].

Despite the strong reliance on glycolysis, studies have shown that OXPHOS remains essential for embryonic NSCs' function. For instance, genetic deletion of mitochondrial apoptosis‐inducing factor (AIF), an FAD‐dependent NADH oxidase, led to mitochondrial dysfunction and disrupted NSC self‐renewal, neural progenitor cell proliferation, early cell cycle exit, and neuronal differentiation [74]. Loss of AIF results in the improper import of NDUFS5 and, to a lesser extent, other complex I subunits, affecting complex I assembly [75]. Mode of division has been found to dictate mitochondrial metabolism in embryonic NSCs. During earlier embryonic days, NE cells undergoing symmetric division have been shown to support rapid proliferation by utilizing pyruvate for lactate production in aerobic glycolysis [70]. In contrast, during later stages of embryonic development, RG cells undergoing asymmetric divisions shift their metabolic strategy—shuttling pyruvate for oxidative metabolism via the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle [70]. This metabolic transition is further supported by single‐cell RNA sequencing data from neurogenic, asymmetrically dividing NSCs, which show upregulation of genes involved in the TCA cycle and fatty acid β‐oxidation pathways, indicating a reliance on mitochondrial oxidative metabolism during embryonic neurogenesis [4, 5].

A rapid metabolic shift toward OXPHOS is observed at the onset of NSCs fate transition, marked by the upregulation of genes associated with mitochondrial respiration [4] (Fig. 3). As NSCs differentiate into intermediate progenitors, there is a notable increase in the expression of TCA cycle enzymes, which supply electrons to the reduced carrier of the electron transport chain (ETC), along with enhanced synthesis of respiratory chain subunits [5, 76]. To meet the rising energy demands, mitochondrial biogenesis becomes essential at this stage. The structural organization of the mitochondrial network also plays a critical role in supporting OXPHOS during lineage progression. Mitochondrial dynamics—regulated through fusion and fission of the mitochondrial membranes—provide the structural flexibility required for metabolic adaptation (Fig. 3). Elongated and fused mitochondria are associated with higher OXPHOS activity, a process largely governed by mitofusin 1 and 2 (MFN1/2) and optic atrophy 1 (OPA1) [77]. Conversely, mitochondrial fission is mediated by dynamin‐related protein 1 (DRP1), which promotes mitochondrial fragmentation. While mitochondrial morphology often correlates with metabolic state, the coupling between fission–fusion balance and metabolic reprogramming is highly context‐dependent (e.g., cell type, developmental stage, and stress conditions) [6]. Mitochondrial morphology is dynamically regulated throughout neurodevelopment. Embryonic NSCs typically exhibit elongated mitochondria, which become fragmented in intermediate progenitor cells (IPCs) and re‐elongate in postmitotic neurons [6, 10]. Disruption of fusion and fission has functional consequences: deletion of MFN1/2 leads to premature mitochondrial fragmentation and a shift from symmetric self‐renewing division to asymmetric division that generate committed progenitors [6, 78]. In contrast, deletion of DRP1 results in an expanded pool of symmetrically dividing NSCs [6]. Moreover, Iwata et al. demonstrated that inducing mitochondrial fusion promotes the self‐renewal capacity of NSCs while suppressing their differentiation into intermediate progenitors and, subsequently, neurons [10].

In adult NSCs, glycolysis plays a critical role in maintaining quiescence and preventing premature activation or depletion of the stem cell pool. This reliance on glycolysis helps minimize the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby preserving stem cell integrity [79, 80]. However, the transition from quiescence to an active state is heavily influenced by mitochondrial metabolism (Fig. 3). Pharmacological inhibition of OXPHOS, blocking complex I with rotenone or complex V with oligomycin, in cultured adult NSCs leads to a marked reduction in cell proliferation and an increase in cell death, demonstrating the essential role of mitochondrial respiration in NSC activation and survival [81]. Moreover, quiescent adult NSCs have been shown to utilize FAO as an alternative carbon source, indicating that their metabolic profile is not exclusively glycolytic [82]. This metabolic flexibility allows adult NSCs to adapt to varying energetic and biosynthetic demands during different stages of activation and differentiation. Interestingly, in adult neurogenesis, intermediate progenitors exhibit a more fused mitochondrial morphology compared to the relatively fragmented mitochondria observed in undifferentiated adult NSCs [83].

Beyond the balance between glycolysis and OXPHOS, emerging evidence indicates that stem cell fate is also shaped by metabolites generated by the TCA cycle. These metabolites can modulate the activity of epigenetic enzymes and thereby influence gene expression [84]. In one study, Iwata et al. reported that pharmacologic mitochondrial uncoupling with carbonyl cyanide m‐chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP), a protonophore, increased electron transport flux and shifted redox balance toward a higher NAD+/NADH ratio. This, in turn, activated NAD+‐dependent sirtuin deacetylases and promoted NSPC self‐renewal/proliferation through selective histone deacetylation and repression of differentiation‐associated gene programs [10]. Furthermore, Liu et al. showed that the aberrant accumulation of the mitochondrial metabolite D‐2‐hydroxyglutarate (D‐2‐HG) impaired NSC activation by reducing intracellular acetyl‐CoA levels and consequently diminishing histone acetylation [85]. This more recent finding underscores the importance of mitochondrial function's influence over neural stem biology beyond OXPHOS.

Oligodendrogenesis and myelination

Myelination is a critical developmental process that enhances the speed and efficiency of electrical signal transmission along axons. This process involves the formation of a myelin sheath, a lipid‐rich insulating layer, around axons [18, 86, 87]. Mature oligodendrocytes are responsible for executing this function, deriving from a lineage of cells known as OPCs. These OPCs originate from radial glial cells or their intermediate progenitors, which reside initially in the neural tube and later in specific regions of the VZ during embryonic development [88, 89, 90].

In the developing forebrain, OPCs are originated in three sequential waves that follow a ventral‐to‐dorsal gradient within the telencephalon. Beginning at embryonic day (E) 12.5 in mice, the first wave originates from Nkx2.1+ precursors located in the VZ of the medial ganglionic eminence (MGE) and anterior entopeduncular area (AEP) [90, 91]. In humans, OPCs are first detected in the MGE VZ around 7.5 weeks of gestation. These early OPCs migrate extensively throughout the embryonic telencephalon, including the cerebral cortex [92]. The second wave arises at E15.5 in mice from Gsh2+ progenitors in the lateral ganglionic eminence (LGE) and MGE. These OPCs also migrate into the developing cortex, contributing to the expanding glial population [90, 91]. The final wave occurs postnatally in mice, originating from Emx1+ progenitors in the cortical VZ. This wave is considered the main source of OPCs in the mature neocortex (Fig. 4) [90, 91]. It was previously recognized that Nkx2.1‐derived OPCs from the first wave were gradually eliminated and replaced by later populations. However, recent findings by Orduz et al. challenge this view, showing that a subset of these early OPCs survive into postnatal stages and exhibit preferential synaptic connectivity with interneurons of shared ontogeny [93]. The mechanisms underlying their selective survival and integration remain incompletely understood but suggest a developmentally programmed interaction between OPCs and specific neuronal subtypes.

Following their temporal and spatial distribution, OPCs populate both white and gray matter (WM or GM), with a slight predominance in white matter [94, 95]. Myelinating oligodendrocytes are generated gradually, and that process begins with OPC migration to WM or GM, where they proliferate before exiting the cell cycle and differentiating into postmitotic premyelinating oligodendrocytes. In the developing brain, OPCs either mature into oligodendrocytes or undergo apoptosis, a regulated process essential for refining neural networks [96, 97]. Time‐lapse imaging targeting premyelinating oligodendrocytes in mouse cortex shows cells which fail to merge into neural circuits undergo apoptosis [98]. The survival and maturation of these cells depend on extrinsic and intrinsic factors, including extracellular ligands, neuronal activity, microRNAs, transcriptional regulation, and interactions with astrocytes and microglia [98, 99, 100, 101, 102]. Oligodendrocytes initiate myelination by extending filopodia‐like processes to engage axons. Once contact is stabilized, proteins and lipids are delivered to form compact myelin. Mature oligodendrocytes express specific markers such as OLIG2, MBP, PLP, MAG, GalC, and MOG. MBP and MAG appear between postnatal days 5–7, while MOG emerges slightly later. Interestingly, PLP is also expressed in early OPCs, indicating a role in process extension [103, 104, 105].

In the adult mouse CNS, oligodendrocyte generation slows, with white matter OPCs contributing ~20% of new myelinating oligodendrocytes in the corpus callosum, compared to ~5% in the cortex [104, 106]. Longitudinal imaging studies show that oligodendrocyte numbers in the mouse cortex increase into middle age (P650) and decline in old age (P950), accompanied by reduced myelin coverage [107]. This age‐related decline in oligodendrocyte number and myelination possibly contributes to cognitive decline, as white matter microstructure correlates with intelligence and deteriorates with age [108, 109]. Notably, neuronal activity influences oligodendrogenesis: sensory deprivation reduces OPC differentiation, while sensory stimulation (e.g., whisker input) enhances oligodendrocyte survival and myelination [110, 111].

Mitochondrial bioenergetics in oligodendrogenesis and myelination

The transition from OPCs to mature myelinating oligodendrocytes involves a distinct metabolic shift that contrasts with the NSC‐to‐neuron transition [112, 113]. A recent study looking into mitochondrial dynamics observed an increase in mitochondrial content and elongated mitochondria in the processes during OPC differentiation [14]. This dynamic conforms with the high rates of OXPHOS in OPCs and elevated oxidative metabolism with higher ATP levels in newly formed oligodendrocytes likely to support the increased need for energy for myelination [114, 115, 116]. The substrates required for ATP and myelination must be supplied either as glucose from the bloodstream or via glycogen reserves in gap junction‐coupled astrocytes [117, 118, 119, 120]. Insufficient energy availability, such as under hypoglycemic conditions, can impair OPC migration and differentiation, reduce the overall oligodendroglial populations, and even lead to axonal degeneration [121]. Oligodendrocytes can also use lactate—not only for ATP synthesis but also as a source of carbon for myelin lipid production [122]. Lactate has been shown to promote OPC proliferation and differentiation and can support myelination under hypoglycemic conditions [113, 123]. However, the energy cost of maintaining the myelin sheath appears to be lower, as evidenced by the absence of mitochondria in the compacted sheath and the presence of more fragmented mitochondria in mature myelinating oligodendrocytes [14]. In cultured OPCs, mitophagy (the turnover of mitochondria) increases during differentiation, and its inhibition arrests this process [16]. These findings suggest that the reduction in mitochondrial number and size may be a necessary step in oligodendrocyte maturation. In adult rodents, white matter appears to rely more heavily on glycolysis than gray matter, suggesting a lower energy demand once myelination is complete [124].

Neurodevelopmental outcomes in primary mitochondrial disorders

PMDs represent a clinically and genetically heterogeneous group of syndromes resulting from defects in OXPHOS. Defective OXPHOS in PMDs is a result of inherited mutations in either nuclear or mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), which directly or indirectly disrupt the synthesis, stability, or assembly of respiratory chain subunits essential for ATP generation [125, 126]. The minimal prevalence of PMDs is estimated at approximately 1 in 4300 individuals, based on clinical diagnoses involving pathogenic mutations in either nuclear or mitochondrial DNA [127, 128, 129]. However, pathogenic mtDNA variants may be far more common in the general population [127, 130].

PMDs are typically classified based on characteristic clinical syndromes, as many of these disorders were clinically recognized before their underlying genetic causes were identified. Commonly described syndromic presentations include Pearson syndrome, mitochondrial DNA depletion syndromes (MDDS), Leigh syndrome (LS), Alpers syndrome, Kearns–Sayre syndrome, Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON), progressive external ophthalmoplegia (PEO), mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke‐like episodes (MELAS), and myoclonic epilepsy with ragged red fibers (MERRF). Among the various clinical manifestations of PMDs, neuromuscular abnormalities are the most prevalent, reflecting the high energy demands of these tissues innervated by myelinated axons extending long physical distances from the nervous system [130, 131, 132]. Due to the inherited nature of these disorders, symptoms involving multiple systems often emerge early in life, during infancy, childhood, or early adolescence.

In recent years, growing evidence has highlighted that children with certain PMDs often experience delays in achieving key neurodevelopmental milestones [133]. A recent study conducted at the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia found that among 69 children with LS, a majority exhibited primary developmental delays—such as delayed head control, rolling over, sitting independently, ambulation, and hand coordination—even before the onset of metabolic strokes or neurodevelopmental regression indicative of neurodegeneration [134]. A recent study by Shi et al. provides strong evidence from both human and murine models that coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) deficiency, which affects electron transport, specifically due to HPDL mutations, leads to neurodevelopmental delay [135]. A longitudinal study of 53 children with various pediatric mitochondrial disorders assessed developmental trajectories over time, noting early global developmental delays, motor regression, and later intellectual disability—with severity correlating with age of onset and genetic subtype [136].

Patients with Pearson syndrome, a rare mitochondrial DNA deletion disorder, have also been reported to display developmental abnormalities, including delayed motor milestones, severe hypotonia, speech delay, and, in some cases, ataxia [137]. However, the very early onset of severe anemia in these patients complicates interpretation of the primary cause of these developmental deficits and whether these defects are intrinsic to NSCs. PMDs are often diagnosed only after severe clinical symptoms emerge; consequently, prospective documentation of early developmental delay is limited, and many developmental milestone data are derived retrospectively in review‐based summaries. However, existing evidence suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction can impair developmental trajectories from an early stage, independent of acute neurologic events.

Neurogenesis, oligodendrogenesis, and myelination defects in PMDs

The motor and cognitive impairments observed in many PMDs are traditionally attributed to the degeneration of postmitotic neurons, which are highly dependent on mitochondrial energy production. However, emerging evidence suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction also affects NSPCs. Given the critical role of mitochondrial energetics and metabolism in regulating proliferation, differentiation, and fate decisions in these progenitor populations, impaired OXPHOS also could disrupt early neurodevelopmental processes.

Recent studies using brain organoid models derived from patient induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) and murine models of mitochondrial dysfunction have provided compelling evidence of disrupted neurogenesis in mitochondrial diseases. NPCs derived from LS patient iPSCs carrying mutations in mt‐ATP6—a gene essential for ATP synthase/complex V function—and PDH (pyruvate dehydrogenase)—critical for linking glycolysis to the TCA cycle—exhibited several mitochondrial abnormalities. These included abnormal mitochondrial morphology, reduced ATP production, elevated mitochondrial membrane potential, and disrupted calcium homeostasis [138, 139]. 3D cerebral organoids generated from these LS‐associated lines were significantly smaller than controls and displayed disorganized and thinner cortical layering. They also had fewer and morphologically abnormal neuroepithelial buds and rosette structures, indicating impaired early corticogenesis [138]. In another organoid model for LS with a mutation in SURF1, a nuclear‐encoded factor essential for complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase) assembly—researchers observed defective neuronal morphogenesis. Inak et al. demonstrated that these defects originated at the level of NPCs, as evidenced by the absence of organized neural progenitor zones in SURF1‐mutant organoids [140]. This morphogenetic disruption was also replicated in NDUFS4 knockout (KO) brain organoids, where complex I assembly is impaired [140]. Supporting these in vitro findings, SURF1 KO piglets displayed reduced cortical gray matter thickness during early postnatal development [141]. A spinal cord organoid system derived from patient‐specific iPSCs carrying the A3243G mutation in the mitochondrial DNA gene mt‐TL1 (which encodes mitochondrial tRNA‐Leu)—the most common mutation associated with MELAS syndrome—has revealed significant neurodevelopmental abnormalities. These organoids exhibited delayed neurogenesis and neurite outgrowth defects, driven by hyperactive NOTCH signaling [142].

Mice harboring a deletion of mitochondrial oxidoreductase AIF serve as an established model of mitochondrial dysfunction. Specifically, deletion of AIF in uncommitted progenitor cells within the developing telencephalon has been demonstrated to recapitulate hallmark mitochondrial defects, including elevated ROS generation, mitochondrial fragmentation, translational impairment of complexes I and III, and an overall reduction in ATP production [143]. Using this model, Khacho et al. identified distinct neurodevelopmental abnormalities characterized by significant cortical layer thinning and ventricular enlargement. Interestingly, their findings indicated that these neuroanatomical changes were not attributable to increased cell death. Instead, they resulted from a shift in neural progenitor cell behavior: progenitors prematurely exited the cell cycle and transitioned from symmetric proliferative divisions (which maintain progenitor pools) to asymmetric divisions, thereby limiting progenitor self‐renewal and compromising cortical development [74]. Cabello‐Rivera et al. used a conditional knockout model in which NDUFS2—encoding a critical subunit of complex I—was deleted in radial glial cells using the hGFAP‐Cre system. This model revealed that loss of NDUFS2 led to markedly reduced NSC proliferation and severely impaired neuronal and oligodendroglial differentiation, both in vivo and in vitro [144].

White matter abnormalities are well documented in the neurodegenerative disease multiple sclerosis (MS), in several mitochondrial and nonmitochondrial leukodystrophies, and in preterm white‐matter injury (WMI) [145, 146, 147]. Although there is evidence of callosal atrophy, hypogenesis of the corpus callosum, and other WM degeneration in PMDs that display leukoencephalopathy, mechanistic studies that directly link oligodendrogenesis and myelination defects to mitochondrial dysfunction in PMDs—using cellular or in vivo models—remain relatively limited with a few notable exceptions [148, 149, 150, 151, 152, 153, 154]. For example, Fünfschilling et al. demonstrated that inhibition of COX10 (essential for complex IV activity) specifically in oligodendrocytes during their development resulted in severe dysmyelination. However, when OXPHOS was disrupted after myelination had concluded, there were no observable effects on either myelin integrity or axonal function [17]. Moreover, Schoenfeld et al. demonstrated that differentiating oligodendrocytes were more sensitive to inhibition of complex I function by rotenone than undifferentiated OPCs and differentiated oligos. This heightened sensitivity was reflected in reduced expression of myelin basic protein (MBP) and cyclic nucleotide phosphodiesterase (CNPase) in differentiating cells [15]. These developmental disruptions in neurogenesis, morphogenesis, and WM development may provide a mechanistic basis for the cognitive and motor impairments frequently observed in patients with PMDs.

Conclusive remarks

Prevalence of neurodevelopmental features in PMDs argues strongly against viewing these diseases solely through a neurodegenerative lens. Growing evidence implicates disrupted neurogenesis across multiple levels: impaired neural stem/progenitor cell proliferation, abnormalities in neuronal morphogenesis and circuit assembly, and downstream structural consequences such as cortical atrophy. On the glial side, defects in OPC lineage progression and myelination are increasingly recognized, with callosal atrophy serving as a readout of white‐matter compromise. In our work using a well‐characterized rodent model of LS—a prototypical pediatric PMD—we observe precisely these neurodevelopmental shortfalls [155]. These findings reinforce a broader hypothesis: OXPHOS dysfunction can derail brain development by altering the energetic, redox, and signaling milieu that governs stem cell maintenance and lineage progression.

However, there are many wide‐open questions remaining. For example, how does specific inhibition of certain complexes derail neurogenesis or oligogenesis? In this review, we limited our discussion to oligodendrocytes and neurogenesis, but astrogenesis is associated with increased OXPHOS [156], and mitochondrial fission in astrocytes has just recently been implicated as an important process during postnatal cortical development [157]. Considering the prevailing viewpoint that astrocytes are glycolytic [158], the understanding of mitochondrial function in astrocytes during development is even more so understudied. PMDs are rare, but if these genetic disorders truly have neurodevelopmental aspects to their pathogenesis, can we better identify and diagnose these patients with better imaging modalities like diffuse tensor imaging? Do degenerative phenotypes worsen in a background when developmental trajectories are altered? Moving forward, mechanistic dissection of how respiratory chain impairment and specific complex inhibition perturbs NSC proliferation, fate choice, maturation, OPC lineage progression, and myelination is essential if we are to begin to understand how mitochondrial function and dysfunction support neurodevelopmental processes.
