The effect of arch supports on foot alignment in patients with metatarsalgia based on weight-bearing computed tomography and radiographic parameters
Authors: Atta Taseh, Samir Ghandour, Bedri Karaismailoglu, Kendal Toy, Soheil Ashkani-Esfahani, Adam Landsman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:Arch support insoles are among the first-line conservative treatments for patients with metatarsalgia. This study aims to examine the clinical effect, along with the associated radiologic changes in foot alignment, among metatarsalgia patients with and without the use of arch support insoles.Methods:In this clinical trial, 14 patients with metatarsalgia aged 18 to 65 years were recruited. Pain intensity was obtained at the first visit and after 4, 8, and 12 weeks. All participants underwent bilateral weight-bearing computed tomography (WBCT) and weight-bearing (WB) radiographs of their feet while standing barefoot and on non-custom-made insoles. Several linear and angular radiological measurements were conducted and compared between the barefoot and insole conditions. Interobserver reliability was calculated (intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC]), and the groups were compared using P < .05 as the statistical significance threshold.Results:A total of 14 patients, consisting of 6 females and 8 males, participated in the study. Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Pain Intensity scores showed significant improvement starting from the 8th week (P = .04). Weight-bearing radiographic measurements showed significant decreases in the M2-M3 and M2-M5 tangent angles (P = .04 and .03, respectively). Similarly, the M4-M5 intermetatarsal angle decreased when wearing insoles (P = .01). There were no significant changes in the WBCT measurements.Conclusion:Arch support insoles significantly improved pain in patients with metatarsalgia. This symptomatic relief coincides with immediate radiologic changes, mainly in the forefoot area. Future prospective studies with larger populations are suggested to investigate the effects of these insoles on metatarsalgia with various foot types.Level of Evidence:Level IV, Therapeutic study.

Introduction

Metatarsalgia is a common foot ailment affecting the forefoot area, with symptoms typically occurring under the metatarsal heads. Nearly 80% of the population develops soreness in this region at some point during their lifetime.1 This pain can result from primary structural changes in the foot, particularly those that alter the relationship between the metatarsal bones.2 Such changes can arise from congenital conditions, such as a cavus foot, or acquired deformities, like hallux valgus.3,4 Additionally, metatarsalgia can be secondary to other conditions, such as interdigital neuromas, or can occur as a complication of surgical interventions like metatarsal osteotomies.5,6 Despite the diversity of etiologies, most of these conditions lead to anatomical malalignment and subsequent redistribution of plantar pressure, overloading specific regions in the forefoot.6

Insoles are among the most frequently prescribed treatments for patients with metatarsalgia.7 Various insole design elements have been identified for alleviating pain in this patient population.2,8 Previous reports have demonstrated that metatarsal pads decreased maximal plantar pressure under the second metatarsal head.6 These findings were supported by significant pain relief, underscoring the efficacy of forefoot offloading in pain reduction. Arch support has been shown to reduce peak pressure in the medial forefoot in patients with a subtle Cavus foot.9 Similarly, contoured insoles have been shown to significantly decrease peak pressures and increase contact area under the metatarsal heads compared to flat insoles.10,11 Despite these findings, the underlying anatomical changes associated with each insole design element remain unclear. While some studies have reported a correlation between radiographic measurements like the talus-first metatarsal angle (Meary’s angle) and plantar pressures, others found no significant correlations.12,13 These conflicting results preclude definitive conclusions about the anatomical changes inferred from plantar pressure alterations.

There exists a gap in the understanding of the anatomical changes due to arch supports. This gap is particularly pertinent given the broad etiology of metatarsalgia and the necessity for tailored insole designs for different etiologies. Understanding these anatomical changes is crucial for developing more effective, patient-specific treatments. In this study, the aim was to evaluate the clinical effect along with the immediate anatomical changes of the arch support insoles in patients with metatarsalgia.

Materials and methods

Study design

The present study was conducted in an academic hospital in Boston, Massachusetts, USA between June 2022 and March 2024 after obtaining approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 2022P000291). The study was part of a larger single-arm clinical trial that included common pathologies in the foot and ankle that could benefit from arch support insoles, including metatarsalgia. In the current manuscript, the focus was on patients with metatarsalgia, with the primary aim of measuring clinical improvements in pain and the secondary aim of assessing radiological alignment changes.

Study population

After obtaining written informed consent, individuals aged between 18 and 65 years with a diagnosis of metatarsalgia who were able to walk up to 0.5 miles without difficulty in a 24-hour period were included in the study. Those with a shoe size difference of more than 2, a body mass index (BMI) of more than 35, prior partial/total foot amputation, foot deformity that would hinder insole fitting, or ulcers, along with those who were using custom shoes/insoles, assistive devices for walking, or ankle/foot braces were excluded from the study. Moreover, pregnant subjects and those with a computed tomography scan performed within the last 30 days before enrollment were excluded. The study was publicly advertised, and interested volunteers reached out to the study team for further information.

Study procedures

An experienced surgeon (A.L.) confirmed the eligibility of patients, and they were fitted for an arch support insole (Maintainer insoles by Good Feet™, Dr.’s Own, LLC., San Diego, California, United States) suitable for their shoe size. These were prefabricated partial-length insoles providing longitudinal arch and metatarsal support. Then, weight-bearing computed tomography (WBCT; tube voltage: 120 kV, field of view: 352 × 352 mm2, slice thickness 0.37 mm) and weight-bearing (WB) radiographs (anteroposterior and lateral views) were obtained during the baseline visit. This was conducted in barefoot conditions, both with and without wearing the insoles. The changes in pain were also recorded using Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Pain Intensity scores at the baselines, 4th-week, 8th-week, and 12th-week visits.

In the current study, the acquired images were meticulously analyzed and compared to investigate possible structural changes. All measurements were conducted by expert orthopedic researchers (2 observers per imaging modality), A.T., S.G., and B.K., who have significant experience in radiologic assessments. (A.T. was also involved in recruitment and data collection phases of the study.) Each observer conducted these measurements twice for each patient, once with images taken with the insole and once with images taken without the insole.

A comprehensive set of linear and angular measurements with established methodologies was selected to address changes across all parts of the foot, including the hindfoot, midfoot, and forefoot, as shown in Tables 1 and 2. Measurements on WB radiographs were performed using eUnity software (version 6.10.2.489) and included M1-M2 and M4-M5 intermetatarsal angles (IMA), metatarsal tangent angles, M1 length, hallux valgus angle, talus-first metatarsal angle, and kite’s angle in the anteroposterior view.14-16 Lateral view measurements included M1 length and declination angle, talus-first metatarsal angle (Meary’s angle), and calcaneal pitch.16-19 Similarly, WBCT measurements, performed in Visage software (Visage Imaging, Version 7.1.18), comprised foot and ankle offset, navicular height, forefoot arch angle, alpha angle, and first metatarsal pronation angle.20-22

Statistical analysis

The intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), along with their 95% confidence intervals, were used to determine the interobserver reliability. The ICC values were categorized as follows: 1 indicates perfect agreement, values between 0.81 and 0.99 signify excellent agreement, and values from 0.61 to 0.80 represent substantial agreement.23 The Shapiro-Wilk test was conducted to assess the distribution of the data. For data that followed a normal distribution, differences between pre- and post-intervention values were analyzed using the paired t-test, while the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to data that did not follow a normal distribution. Normally distributed variables were reported as mean ± SD versus nonnormal data that were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). The study power was determined to be 87% with a sample size of 14 patients, an effect size of 0.8, and a type 1 error probability of .05. A P-value of less than .05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

A total of 14 patients, consisting of 6 females and 8 males, participated in the study. The participants had a mean age of 45.21 ± 13.19 years and a mean BMI of 23.5 ± 3.29 kg/m². Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Pain Intensity scores showed significant improvement starting from the 8th week (P = .04) and continued to improve through the 12th week compared to baseline values (P = .01; Figure 1).

Weight-bearing radiographic measurements showed significant decreases in the M2-M3 and M2-M5 tangent angles (P = .04 and P = .03, respectively). Similarly, the M4-M5 IMA decreased when wearing insoles (P = .01). Additionally, the M1 declination angle increased significantly (P = .001; Table 1). In contrast, WBCT measurements did not reveal any significant changes in the hindfoot, midfoot, or forefoot areas (Table 2).

Discussion

This study highlights the anatomical changes in feet following the use of prefabricated arch support insoles in patients experiencing metatarsalgia symptoms. On WB radiography, participants displayed a significant reduction in M2-M3 tangent, M2-M5 tangent, and M4-M5 IMA, as well as an increase in the first metatarsal declination angle. These anatomical changes represent a repositioning of the metatarsal bones, resulting in a redistribution of plantar pressure. The efficacy of these changes was supported by clinical improvements demonstrated by Pain Intensity scores, where patients began experiencing significant symptom reduction following the eighth week of insole use.

The first metatarsophalangeal joint bears the maximum axial load during walking.24 Many authors believe that a shorter first ray or a discrepancy between the length of the metatarsal bones can cause this load to be transferred to other metatarsal bones.25 In a study comparing patients with transfer metatarsalgia versus healthy individuals, Greisberg et al26 reported a higher first ray elevation in metatarsalgia patients (5 mm versus 3 mm, P < .001). This finding was regarded as a potential explanation for transfer metatarsalgia. In this study, a higher first MT declination angle was found while using insoles. However, the M1-M2 tangent angle did not change significantly, suggesting a simultaneous elevation in M2 because of the medial arch support. This M2 elevation was further supported by the observed decrease in the M2-M3 and M2-M5 tangent angles. The decrease in tangent angles and the better-aligned parabola appear to be clinically effective, as evidenced by the improvements in pain scores. These findings are consistent with those of Fleischer et al,27 who demonstrated a direct correlation between the lengths of the second and third metatarsals and plantar peak pressures, as well as pressure-time integrals.

Metatarsalgia and its manifestation are closely linked to foot alignment. Therefore, when designing insoles for this patient population, it is crucial to focus on design elements facilitating metatarsal offloading and medial longitudinal arch support.28 In a study by Rao et al,29 the authors examined pedobarographic plantar loading in individuals with high, normal, or low arch structures, which were classified using the malleolar valgus index and arch height index. They noticed individuals with low arch structure sustained significantly higher second metatarsal peak pressure (up to 30% more) and increased first metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint flexibility compared to individuals with normal or high arch structure. Moreover, the first MTP joint flexibility and foot arch structure explained up to 20% of the variance in peak pressure under the great toe. Therefore, the integrity of the medial longitudinal arch is paramount for proper plantar load distribution, and, by extension, supporting the arch using an insole promotes pressure loading.

While custom-made foot orthoses are often regarded as the preferred arch support option, the precise mechanisms underpinning their effectiveness remain unclear.30 Some research indicates custom orthoses might yield superior biomechanical outcomes, including improvements in balance and optimized distribution of foot loading compared to prefabricated options.31,32 Nonetheless, evidence suggests minimal or no substantial differences between custom-made and prefabricated orthoses in terms of pain relief or functional enhancement across various treatment durations (6 weeks, 12 weeks, and 12 months) for adults experiencing plantar heel pain.33 This study utilized prefabricated insoles with individualized fittings, combining practical affordability with tailored biomechanical support, which aligns well with current literature findings. Further comparative research may clarify the clinical effectiveness of these approaches in specific patient populations.

Surgical treatment options such as Lapidus surgery and distal metatarsal osteotomies may alleviate metatarsalgia.34 The basic biomechanical principle behind these procedures includes realigning metatarsal heads by changing the declination angle and/or shortening the metatarsal bones.35,36 Vandeputte et al37 performed a Weil osteotomy on 32 patients with plantar keratosis or dislocated MTP joints. After 30 months of follow-up, they reported a significant offloading effect under the metatarsal heads, with 75% of the operations resulting in complete resolution of the calluses. In this study, a change in the relational position of the metatarsal heads, as demonstrated by the change in tangent angles, was found. The higher M1 declination angle supported this change, although a significant change in the metatarsal length was not observed on imaging. Similarly, Busch et al38 examined the effect of M1 plantar flexion on complications of Lapidus surgery. They reported a lower chance of developing post-operative transfer metatarsalgia in those with the first ray fixed in a slightly plantarflexed position. In summary, supporting the medial tarsometatarsal joints when using insoles seems to simulate the alterations in the anatomical alignment of the foot seen with common surgical procedures and, hence, mitigates metatarsalgia.

This study is pioneering in its detailed analysis of foot architecture using advanced technologies like WBCT. Although these WBCT measurements did not reveal significant changes, their inclusion was important as it demonstrated that subtle positional alterations observed radiographically do not necessarily correspond to broader 3-dimensional biomechanical adjustments. However, the study also had its limitations. The small sample size, comprising 14 patients, may limit the generalizability of the findings. Also, the exclusion criteria, which, while necessary for maintaining a controlled study environment, may exclude a portion of the metatarsalgia population, such as those with higher BMI or significant foot deformities. This limits the applicability of the findings to broader patient demographics.

Arch support insoles significantly improved pain intensity in patients with metatarsalgia. This symptomatic relief is supported by alterations in the position of the metatarsals, which are demonstrated by immediate radiologic changes, mostly in the forefoot area. Further studies with larger populations, including subgroup analyses, are recommended to investigate the effects of these insoles on metatarsalgia patients with various foot types.
