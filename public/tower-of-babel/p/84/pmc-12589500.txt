Perception based investigation exploring recurrence of violations by motorist and non-motorist at rail road grade crossings
Authors: Adheesh Kumar Vivek, Smruti Sourava Mohapatra, Tathagatha Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA high proportion of rail road grade crossing (RRGC) crashes are fatal, making road user safety at RRGCs one of the most serious concerns. These crashes are primarily the result of collisions between road users (both motorists and non-motorist) and trains, which are primarily brought on by violations. Road users usually break the law in a predictable pattern. A portion of them may not violate the RRGC, while others do so occasionally, while some of them regularly violate the RRGC. Hence, this study aims to figure out the contributing factors that significantly influence the recurrence of violations (ROV) committed by motorized and non-motorized users. In this regard, a detailed questionnaire-based survey was conducted at 21 distinct RRGCs located across nine Indian states, generating 5243 responses from motorists and 1965 responses from non-motorists (n = 7208). This database was first used for descriptive statistical analysis. The results showed that, compared to motorists, non-motorists account for the highest percentage of regular violations, and a significant proportion of pedestrians and bicyclists attempt to beat the incoming train, they position themselves in the most perilous situation. After that, separate violation models were developed for motorized and non-motorized road users. The results showed that several socio-economic attributes, travel-related factors, and road users’ behavior influence the ROV significantly at RRGCs for both motorists and non-motorists. However, age, marital status, times of the day & checking for incoming trains were found insignificant for motorists’ ROV. In contrast, for non-motorists, driving experience, marital status, times of the day & unintentional trespassing were statistically insignificant. Additionally, compared to all the factors, engagement in secondary activities emerges as the most significant one. Planners and policymakers can use the findings mentioned above to choose suitable interventions to improve safety for motorists and non-motorists at RRGCs.Keywords: Road users’ safety, Rail road grade crossings, Policy implications, Recurrence of violations, Ordered probit modelSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

An intersection where a rail line crosses a road at the same grade is referred to as a rail road grade crossing (RRGC). Despite continued emphasis being directed to the design and implementation practices for RRGCs, the safety of road users is among the most critical aspects1. Gates at RRGCs operate as physical barriers segregating road users from approaching trains and preventing road users from entering the intersecting zone illegally2. RRGCs protected with gates cause conflict between road and rail users despite being safer than unprotected crossings, and fatal incidents at these intersections are documented annually around the world3. Undisciplined and risky behavior by road users (specifically, engagement in deliberate gate violations) is the main cause of the inflated likelihood of crashes at RRGC4,5, resulting in injuries of varying degrees of severity, including even fatal ones6,7.

The probability of mortality in crashes between motorized vehicles and trains is nearly 50%, between pedestrians and trains, it is almost 70%, and the death probability in collisions among automobiles is 5%8. This demonstrates unequivocally that the probability of fatality is higher for non-motorists than those for motorists. A report by the Ministry of Home Affairs’ National Crime Records Bureau9 concludes that 1,185 people were killed and 71 injured in accidents involving 1,014 road users at RRGCs in India. Moreover, 4012 cyclists and 20,349 pedestrians perish in road accidents in India on annual basis, with men alone contributing for 81.6% of the fatal instances10. The Indian railway network is the world’s third-largest, spanning 68,442 km. It has 7,172 stations and transports over 13,500 passengers in 9,100 freight trains everyday11. Between 2006 and 2021, Indian railways reported 685 level crossing accidents, with 611 at unmanned crossings and 74 at staffed crossings. These accidents caused 2,639 deaths and 4,991 non-fatal injuries (Ministry of Railways (Railway Board)). Traffic accidents account for 43% of all accidents in India12,13. Since the majority of incidents were brought on by drivers acting recklessly, the Indian government strictly announced to close the unprotected RRGCs due to safety concerns11. There are currently 863 (4.2%) unmanned/passive RRGCs and 19,532 (95.8%) gated/active RRGCs in India. Furthermore, Indian Railways installed interlocked sliding booms (together with gates) at 5897 RRGCs and gate integrated with signals at 11,710 RRGCs by the end of year 202112,15]. When compared to passive RRGCs, RRGCs with gates provide at least 17% better safety13. Nevertheless, risk-taking activity on the part of other road users, such as gate violations, raises the likelihood of serious injuries and fatalities14,17].

In India, due to the high level of vehicle traffic heterogeneity, the varying motorist and non-motorist speed, the absence of lane discipline, and the diverse judgement abilities of motorist and non-motorist18, the traffic operation becomes complicated and unsafe. Therefore, predicting safety concerns and proposing feasible remedies relies on identifying the causes and relevant factors of risk-taking behavior among motorists and non-motorists at RRGCs. In contrast with automotive crashes (motorists), pedestrian and bicycle crashes (non-motorists) at RRGCs have received less attention in past investigations5,18. Additionally, relatively little research has been carried out to date on the variables influencing non-motorists’ behaviour for non-crash situations, such as violations5,19,20. Moreover, the research in lights of safety at Indian RRGC is not explored to date5. Also, research pertaining to the recurrence of violations by motorists and non-motorists is underexplored. Road users usually break the law in a predictable pattern. A portion of them may not violate the RRGC, while others do so occasionally, while some of them regularly violate the RRGC. Hence, it is the need of the hour to figure out the factors associated with regular violators, occasional violators and non-violators. Hence, in this study, utilizing the motorists’ and non-motorists’ perceptions, two separate violation models have been developed to examine the effect of significant variables on the recurrence of violations (ROV) for motorists and non-motorists. Review of literature

This section reviews the literature on road user behavior, accident analysis, operational aspects, and mitigation techniques for RRGCs. We identified relevant articles using relevant keywords such as “level crossings,” “at grade railroad crossings,” “railroad crossings,” “railroad highway grade crossings,” & “railroad grade crossings.” We gathered pertinent articles from a number of scientific databases, such as research reports, Google Scholar, Science Direct, Scopus, and ProQuest. Citing multiple publications, the snowball technique was used to acquire more articles. The data from 459 investigations on RRGCs undertaken by researchers worldwide from 1975 to date was examined using the VOS viewer software tool, resulting in Fig. 1. It summarizes the themes of the investigation and their relevance to the study. Themes are represented by inscribed circles, with the size of the circle reflecting their frequency of occurrence. The area of a theme’s circle correlates with its frequency in the research, and vice versa. The VOS viewer software package was used to create a figure. Most of the study has focused on safety problems and violations. However, there has been little research into the recurrence of violations by both motorists and non-motorists. Road users typically break the law in usual manner. Some might not transgress the RRGC at all, while others do so on occasion, and still others violate it on every day of their lives. As a result, it is critical to investigate the numerous variables impacting the recurrence of violations by motorists and non-motorists at different levels.

Thematic analysis based on RRGC investigation carried out globally.

Gated RRGCs are thought to substantially reduce collision rates compared to RRGCs employing alternative protective measures, such as “pavement markings, audible bells, warning signs, and flashing lights”2. However, violations at gated railroad grade crossings may constitute the most perilous conduct, as road users are subjected to a high-risk accident zone as they navigate through below and around the gates during their operation1. Investigations of motor vehicle violations and violations by pedestrians and bicyclists are delineated in two distinct sections below.

Motorists’ violation

According to investigations, disobeying the traffic rules is evident across all modes of transportation6. According to Meeker et al.22, around 67% of motorists violate the RRGC configured with flashers and 38% of them break the rule at installed gates before an approaching train. When waiting longer than a threshold limit, road users become impatient and engage in risky behavior like crossing the RRGC illegally during different gate operation phases16,23. Additionally, the percentage of violations increases for various types of road users when drivers are distracted by activities like conversing on phones or with companions, engaging in music, or dealing with other activities19,24. Researchers from all around the world have identified a variety of crucial variables that serve as catalysts for violators. Arriving train speed, rail tracks, sight distance adequacy, warning time, roadway lanes, waiting time, heavy traffic, distractions, nearest railway station, and checks for arriving trains were found to be contributing variables in forecasting the violation3,5,6,18. By advancing certain aspects, such as warning devices and treatments, developing trustworthy methods of enhancing motorists’ safety, and periodically committing to training, engineering, and execution, accidents at RRGC could be lowered, as per Metaxatos & Sriraj26. The elastic centerline barrier is also claimed to be efficient, affordable, and promising in deterring motorists from violating the law at RRGCs27. The results of our recent research18 showed that a variety of trip-related, behavioral, and socioeconomic characteristics have a major influence on the infractions committed by individual and group road users at RRGCs. However, it was discovered that trip type, gender, and educational attainment were not significant predictors of group infractions. The inclination to cross at a high pace after engaging in secondary activities indicates a substantial link with violations across all the criteria. It is discovered that excessive waiting times are the primary cause of this risk-taking behavior, followed by habits, social influence, a lack of understanding, and attitude. Some of the most important variables that are positively correlated with risky behavior of motorist were found in our latest study4. These variables include traveling alone or in a group, engagment in distractions, infringing at RRGC because of lengthy wait times, and accelerating while crossing the RRGC.

Non-motorists’ (Pedestrian and Bicyclist) violation

Recently, the safety of non-motorists (especially pedestrians and bicyclists) has attracted a lot of attention due to their high vulnerability28. According to studies, pedestrians and cyclists are more likely to die or incur serious injuries when they crash with cars or trains than drivers or passengers8,20,28. At RRGC, unintentional error and a deliberate violation could be the leading reasons of this collision. Pedestrian and bicycle violations were the subject of a recent observational research by Vivek and Mohapatra18. The likelihood of pedestrian and bicycle violations was found to be significantly influenced by a number of factors, including train speed and occupancy time, gate operational characteristics (“gate blockage duration and warning time”), and individual-specific factors (“looking both ways, gender, age, and crossing speed”). In addition, pedestrian and cyclist behavior was found to be highly affected by geometrical features and the train vehicle unit, among other aspects. Freeman & Rakotonirainy29 carried out a user perception study and found that while 3.5% of pedestrians crossed the crossing accidentally, 25% of them did so on purpose. According to several experts, it is more beneficial to study violent behaviors in tandem with multiple parameters30,31. Younger men, peak hours, and additional elements like a crowd of individuals travelling fast, distractions, alcohol use, travelling speed, illumination, quality of road surface, warning devices type, and lateral visibility are major contributors to such risky behaviour32–39. Research by Lobb et al.8 found that while education initiatives aid in lowering dangerous pedestrian trespassing at RRGCs, fining drivers for unauthorized crossings has a greater impact on lowering violations. Moreover, according to Siques40, pedestrian treatments at RRGCs, including automatic gates, warning systems, and educational signage, can lower the violation rate significantly. Additionally, Baric et al.20 revealed the existence of law enforcers and cameras at RRGCs as negative catalyst to lower pedestrian and bicycle violation rates. Furthermore, a simplified questionnaire was constructed with respondents’ easiness in mind, embracing all of the crucial components needed to accomplish the objective of the present study. The questions were designed to elicit information about road users’ behaviour during both legal and illegal crossings at the RRGC. The study began with a literature survey, which aided in the filtration of common practicing behaviour of road users at RRGCs. The questionnaire was split into four segments: (a) social-economic characteristics, (b) trip-related information, (c) data regarding motorists’ and non-motorists’ behaviour, and (d) probable reasons for RRGC violations. Trip Duration measures the length of the journey and is divided into several time slots that range from less than 15 min to over an hour. Trip frequency, which can range from daily to monthly, describes how frequently a person makes these kinds of trips. The trip’s time of day, such as early morning, morning, afternoon, evening, or night, is indicated by the Times of the Day. The term “inadvertent illegal crossing” refers to whether a person has ever crossed the RRGC without intending to, frequently as a result of mistake or ignorance. Participation in Secondary Activities refers to the frequency with which an individual engages in additional tasks while travelling, such as talking or using a phone, which may affect safety and attentiveness. Checking for Incoming Trains indicates how frequently a person listens for or watches for approaching trains before crossing, demonstrating their awareness of safety. violation following Hearing the Train’s Horn highlights a dangerous behavior by measuring the frequency with which a person crosses the street after hearing a train’s warning horn. Violation after sighting the approaching train shows how often people intentionally take risks by crossing the tracks even after spotting an approaching train. When crossing tracks, a person’s crossing speed tendency indicates whether they increase, decrease, or stay at the same speed. Ultimately, the term “recurrence of violations” refers to how frequently someone engages in risky or unlawful crossing behaviors; it might range from frequently to never. In the vicinity of the RRGCs, residences, shopping complexes, and fuel stations were finalized as survey locations. Eighteen trained individuals with strong interpersonal skills conducted the survey. The team reassured participants and outlined the importance of safety at RRGC before starting the survey. Following that, interviews were conducted with the participants to collect the data about their sociodemographics, travel experiences, and behaviours. Lastly, they were asked to select the most plausible explanations for their illegal crossings at RRGC. A total of 7451 road users (5389 motorists & 2062 non-motorists) participated in the questionnaire survey and accomplished it.

Analysis methods

The principal objective of carrying out the statistical analysis was to quantify various factors influencing the motorists’ and non-motorists’ recurrence of violations (ROV) and to determine the most likely causes of violations. The first step was to perform a descriptive statistical analysis. The direction and intensity of the association between the dependent variables (ROV) and the independent variables were later determined using Spearman’s correlation analysis. Ultimately, using ordinal regression, separate violation models (ordered probit models) for motorists and non-motorists were developed. Road users’ engagement in various types of violation

Road users of various vehicular classes, including pedestrians and bicyclists, may have a propensity to violate regularly, while others may be occasional violators and a few non-violators. In addition, violators, both frequent and occasional, commonly commit various violations during various gate operation phases. In this regard, the study also sought to determine the engagement of violators in multiple types of violations and the proportion of regular and occasional violators. The present study has segregated the various forms of violations into four different types, namely; after initiation of a siren but before gate descending (V1); Gates descending (V2); Gates completely down and before the train arrived (V3); after the train left but before the gates were fully lifted (V4). The proportion of road users involved in several types of violations is disaggregated by vehicle class, and the outcome is presented in Fig. 5. A high percentage (68.6%) of road users (n = 4945) of all modes are observed under V1 violation, followed by V2 (n = 3337), V4 (n = 3251), while only 25.8% of them (n = 1860) are engaged in V3. Interestingly, motorized two-wheelers (n = 652) account for a considerable share (19.9%) of the motorized modes used in V3, while other motorized modes are absent. The main reason for this observation is that bigger vehicles cannot drive under the gates unless they forcefully penetrate through the gates. However, motorized two-wheelers can somehow manage to pass under the gates by tilting their bikes. Over 60% of pedestrians and bicyclists (n = 1199), on the other hand, commit V3 violations because of the relative ease to pass under the gates. The proportion of road users’ engagement in various types of violations is shown in Fig. 5. It is also evident from the figure that non-motorized road users (pedestrians and bicyclists) are observed to commit violations in all types of gate operation phases, which makes them even more vulnerable. Also, studies undertaken the past have reported similar issue for non-motorists. In comparison to vehicle crashes (motorists), pedestrian and bicycle crashes (non-motorists) at RRGCs have received less attention in previous investigations5,18. Furthermore, relatively little study has been conducted to date on the elements that influence non-motorists’ behavior in non-crash scenarios, such as infractions5,19,20. Hence, in this research we entirely focused to investigate the significant variables associated with recurrence of violation of pedestrians and bicyclists.

The most hazardous behavior of non-motorized violators

The increased number of pedestrian and bicyclist deaths at RRGC is due to several factors, with violations being one of the most important. At RRGC, risk-taking behavior by pedestrians and bicyclists can be monitored during various gate operation phases. However, the scenario gets even more critical if they continue to violate even after hearing the horn and seeing the approaching train. Consequently, while they attempt to beat the incoming train, they position themselves in the most perilous situation. It can be well inferred from Fig. 7 that while 51.2% of pedestrians do not violate after hearing the horn of an approaching train, 43.1% of them violate occasionally, and 5.7% do it on a regular basis. On the other hand, 36.4% of bicyclists put themselves in such hazardous situations occasionally or regularly. While only 2.3% of pedestrians violate at RRGC regularly when they see an approaching train, over 30% of them do so on occasion. When bicyclists see an approaching train, around 21% of them try to beat it.

Ordered probit model results for recurrence of motorists and non-motorists’ violation

Before the models were developed, Spearman’s correlation analysis was carried out separately using the data sets of motorists and non-motorists to determine the contributions of the independent variables to the dependent variable (ROV). The analysis was carried out at a 5% level of significance. According to the results, the variables Age, MS, TOD & CIT were found insignificant for motorists’ ROV, while for non-motorists, DE, MS, TOD & IIC was observed to be statistically insignificant. Hence, these variables were not further taken into account for the model’s development. Additionally, it was observed that none of the independent variables have a significant correlation with each other.

The factors influencing the recurrence of violations may differ for motorized and non-motorized road users. Hence, separate violation models (ordered probit) for motorized and non-motorized road users were developed using NLOGIT 6.0 software package. These models project the effects of contributing factors related to the recurrence of violations by motorists and non-motorists. Approximately, 82% (n = 5944) of the dataset (representing data from 18 RRGCs from nine different Indian states) were used to develop the model. The socio-economic variables (gender and EL) project a positive correlation with ROV for both motorized and non-motorized road users. The positive coefficient of the gender indicates that males (both motorist and non-motorist) are less patient and more inclined to commit violation on a regular basis than females, which is consistent with the findings of earlier research18,29. Males may be less patient and more prone to commit violations due to a greater proclivity for risk-taking and impulsive behaviour, which is affected by both biological characteristics and social expectations that promote aggressiveness and competitiveness55. These elements combine to make males more likely to break rules than females. In contrast to less educated users, highly educated pedestrians, cyclists, and motorists rarely violate the rules at RRGC. Highly educated pedestrians, cyclists, and motorists are less likely to break railway crossing restrictions because they are more aware of the risks and eventual consequences56. However, the variable “age” bears a positive coefficient for non-motorists. In line with previous research, it shows that younger cyclists and pedestrians are more likely to commit infractions on a frequent basis19. But it is insignificant for the motorist. Due to their risk-taking tendencies and insufficient understanding of traffic laws, younger cyclists and pedestrians may commit infractions more frequently. This problem is made worse by peer pressure and inexperience57. Reckless behavior is also influenced by their developmental stage and impulsivity58. On the other hand, the variable AI positively influences the motorists’ ROV. It suggests that high-income drivers are less likely to break the traffic laws at RRGC and vice versa. In comparison, it is statistically insignificant for non-motorist. Because better socioeconomic position is frequently accompanied by more knowledge and education, high-income road users may be less likely to violate traffic laws at RRGCs. If they are detected breaking traffic laws, they may also lose more, like their reputation, jobs, or licenses59. On the other hand, road users with low incomes may experience additional stressors, such financial or time restrictions, which may make them more prone to take chances. Moreover, positive coefficient of DE for motorists reflects that novice drivers tend to violate the RRGC regularly, while experienced drivers rarely commit it. However, DE does not influence the ROV for pedestrians and bicyclists.

All trip-related variables are observed to impact ROV for both motorists and non-motorists significantly. TD and TF are positively correlated with ROV, while TT is negatively correlated. Road users making short trips are more likely to commit violations on a regular basis as they crave to finish the journey in a quick time with minimal delay. This reflects the innate desire of drivers on short trips to arrive at their destination as quickly as possible without wasting time, as opposed to extended travel. The same is the case with regular users, they are regular violators.

Regular travel pattern imparts great familiarity with the subject RRGC, which inflates the probability of regular violations by motorists and non-motorists. The combination of commuters’ high trip frequency, high priority of their trip purpose, and familiarity with their target RRGC may be the likely cause of their relatively frequent violations25. Also, users travelling for important trips like education or work tend to violate regularly when compared to users going for shopping or recreational trips. This notion stems from the fact that people who plan trips for work or school may be more concerned with cutting down on travel time, and users on these trips are more susceptible to the current traffic situation, which affects how long their trip takes.

The behavioral traits like ESA and CST are statistically significant and positively correlated with ROV for both motorized and non-motorized road users. Distracted users are more intended to regularly break the law than those who are focused. Thus, regular violators spend more time dabbling in secondary pursuits like text messaging, making phone calls, engaging in peer gossip, etc. Non-violent people, however, rarely take part in such activities18. Furthermore, the regular violators speed up quickly to cross the RRGC. Due to impatience, a desire to save time, and a propensity to underestimate the risks involved, frequent violators at RRGC frequently speed up to cross. Road users that are impatient may try to “beat” the train in the hopes of safely crossing before it arrives21,23. Non-violators, on contrary, approach the RRGC with conviction and reduce their speed as they move through the RRGC. It reflects the driving styles of both regular violators and non-violators. Moreover, the variable IIC negatively correlates with ROV for motorists and is statistically insignificant for pedestrians and bicyclists. It suggests that drivers who accidentally cross the RRGC during gate operating stages are least prone to violate deliberately. People can learn from their mistakes and modify their behavior accordingly, according to research. When it comes to road user violations at railway crossings, a bad consequence or near-miss brought on by an error may act as a teaching moment, making people more watchful and obedient to safety regulations going forward. This demonstrates unequivocally that the majority of motorized violators intentionally break traffic laws, whether they do so regularly or occasionally. Ultimately, the variable CIT projects a negative correlation with ROV for non-motorists. Non-violators (pedestrians and cyclists) have a habitual propensity to regularly monitor for approaching trains on traversing the RRGC, ensuring that the movement is now secure. However, such routine habitual behavior is less common among violators. On the contrary, CIT is emerged to be insignificant variable for motorized users. The most likely reason could be that drivers only have the chance to break the rules during the gate’s descending and ascending phases which reassures them that the train is not arriving soon18. Hence, they would prefer to cross the RRGC quickly rather than check for the incoming train.

Marginal effects

Marginal effects show the relative contribution of each independent variable to the probability of selecting a specific ROV (regular, occasional, never). The main purpose of it is to monitor how altering the factor that is independent by a single unit impacts the variable that is dependent. The positive sign denotes that the probability of a specific ROV increases with an increment in the variable while all other variables stay unchanged, and vice versa. As shown in Table 6, the marginal effects demonstrate that ESA has the most decisive impact on ROV for both motorists and non-motorists. When all other factors are held constant, a unit increase in ESA (regular to occasional or occasional to never) decreases the chance of occurrence of ROV by the motorist (regular and occasional) by 0.1512 and 0.2418 units, respectively, while inflating the chance of not violating the law by 0.3929 units. Likewise, the likelihood of non-motorists violating the law regularly and occasionally is decreased by 0.5687 and 0.4978 units, respectively, while the likelihood of violating the law never increases by 0.0708 units when this ESA is increased. Distraction is becoming a major safety risk for RRGCs, much like it has been at roadway junctions54. Distracted driving can lead someone to accept reckless behavior, which may lead to a catastrophic outcome, and may be fatal sometimes. Therefore, distracted road users must receive interventions. One strategy is to implement creative solutions that are meant to effectively draw the attention of distracted users, such as voice announcements or flashing lights projected on road surface54. Policy implications

The outcomes of the current study emphasize the need for quick consideration of a few factors in order to improve RRGC safety by discouraging violations and so reducing the risk of accidents. The results show that most of the violators are less educated and engaged in secondary activities. Excessive waiting times are the most common cause of violations, followed by time-saving habits, social impact (following around violators), a lack of awareness of crossing controls, and a negative attitude toward accidents. Moreover, a considerable quantity of bicyclists and pedestrians have been observed participating in risky behaviors. They are the most susceptible road users because they attempt to outrun the train even after they hear and see it. Conclusion

The majority of RRGC accidents involve crashes between road users and trains, with careless driving by motorists and non-motorists being the principal contributing factor. Hence, this perception-based study investigated several contributing factors that cause violations to occur frequently, occasionally, and never among motorists and non-motorists at RRGCs. The evaluation is based on 7208 responses that were collected through a questionnaire survey from twenty-one study locations across nine distinct states of India.

Initially, a descriptive statistical analysis is conducted to investigate the road users’ engagement in various types of violations, the recurrence of violations by motorists and non-motorist, and the most hazardous behavior of non-motorized violators. The findings revealed that a higher proportion of non-motorist are involved in violations during various gate operation phases than motorists. Also, irrespective of the type of violation, pedestrians and bicyclists (non-motorists), compared to motorists, account for the highest portion of regular violations, 38.5% violators (n = 757). Moreover, 43.1% of pedestrians occasionally violate at RRGC after hearing the horn of an approaching train and 5.7% do it regularly. On the other hand, 36.4% of bicyclists put themselves in such hazardous situations occasionally or on regular basis. While only 2.3% of pedestrians violate at RRGC regularly when they see an approaching train, over 30% do so on occasion. When bicyclists see an approaching train, around 21% try to beat it.

Spearman’s correlation analysis was then carried out separately using the data sets of motorists and non-motorists to determine the contributions of the independent variables to the dependent variable (ROV). According to the results, the variables Age, MS, TOD & CIT were found insignificant for motorists’ ROV. At the same time, for non-motorists, DE, MS, TOD & IIC was observed to be statistically insignificant. Hence, discarding these variables, separate violation models for motorized and non-motorized users were constructed to project the effects of significant factors related to the recurrence of their violations. The remaining significant variables project an identical correlation with ROV for both motorized and non-motorized users, with an exception that AI is not significant at 5% level of significance.

The behavioral traits like ESA and CST are statistically significant and positively correlated with ROV for both motorists and non-motorists. Distracted users are more likely to regularly break the law than those who are focused. Furthermore, the regular violators speed up in an effort to quickly cross the RRGC. Non-violators, on the other hand, approach the RRGC with conviction and reduce their speed as they move through the RRGC. Moreover, the variable IIC is negatively correlated with ROV for motorists. This demonstrates unequivocally that the majority of motorized violators intentionally break traffic laws, whether they do so regularly or occasionally. The variable CIT projects a negative correlation with ROV for non-motorists. Non-violators (pedestrians and cyclists) have a habitual propensity to regularly monitor for approaching trains on traversing the RRGC, ensuring that the movement is now secure.

All the trip-related variables are observed to significantly influence ROV for both motorists and non-motorists. TD and TF are positively correlated with ROV, while TT is negatively correlated with ROV. Road users making short trips, regular road users, and commuters travelling for important trips like education or work are more likely to commit violations on a regular basis. Furthermore, socio-economic variables like gender and EL positively correlate with ROV for both motorists and non-motorists. This suggests that men tend to be less tolerant and more likely to commit violations on a frequent basis than women. Also, in contrast to less educated users, highly educated pedestrians, cyclists, and motorists rarely violate the rules at RRGC. Moreover, the variable age bears a positive coefficient for non-motorists revealing that younger walkers and cyclists are more prone to commit repeated offenses than older pedestrians and bicyclists. Furthermore, the variable AI and DE positively influence the motorists’ ROV. It suggests that high-income drivers are less likely to break the traffic laws at RRGC and vice versa. Also, novice drivers tend to violate the RRGC regularly, while experienced drivers rarely commit it.

Limitations and future scope

Planners and policymakers can use the findings mentioned above from developed models and marginal effects analysis to choose suitable interventions to improve safety for motorized and non-motorized users at RRGCs. Furthermore, there are some limitations to the current study. The driving circumstances of other road users, such as psychological stress levels and alcohol consumption, were not considered. Consequently, these factors might be considered in subsequent research. The effect of land use patterns on the recurrence of violations by motorists and non-motorists could also be monitored by developing location-based models.
