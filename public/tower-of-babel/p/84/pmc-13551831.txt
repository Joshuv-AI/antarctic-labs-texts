Metagenomic next-generation sequencing of cerebrospinal fluid reveals pathogen spectrum and mortality predictors among patients with advanced HIV-1 disease at a tertiary hospital in China
Authors: Lin-Yu Wan, Jun Zou, Xue-Meng Li, Rui Zhao, Guang Yang, Meng-Yuan Zhang, Qiu-Ye Xiao, Yin-Duo Wei, Jia-Min Gao, Bao-Peng Yang, Chao Zhang, Yan-Mei Jiao, Fu-Sheng Wang, Jin-Wen Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCentral nervous system (CNS) infections remain the major causes of morbidity and mortality among people living with HIV-1 (PLWH), particularly in resource-limited settings. However, the clinical characteristics and prognostic indicators of PLWH with suspected CNS infections are not well defined. In this study, we aim to characterize the spectrum of CNS pathogens, clinical characteristics, in-hospital mortality, and factors associated with death among people with advanced HIV-1 disease (AHD) in Guangxi, China.MethodsMetagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) was performed to analyze types of infection in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from 61 treatment-naive PLWH with suspected CNS infections. Clinical data, routine laboratory tests, and biochemical tests were collected and analyzed.ResultsAmong the 61 CSF samples, primarily with AHD, a total of 206 pathogens were identified. Viral pathogens predominated, with Epstein-Barr virus being the most frequently identified, followed by cytomegalovirus. Compared with patients with single-pathogen infection, those with multiple infections (viral, bacterial, and fungal) exhibited significantly lower CD4 T cell counts, higher C-reactive protein levels, and markedly reduced lipid metabolism parameters. However, infection types were not significantly associated with in-hospital death. Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified plasma low density lipoprotein (LDL) and CSF lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) as independent predictors of in-hospital death.ConclusionIn PLWH with AHD and suspected CNS infections, multiple pathogens frequently coexist in the CSF. Plasma LDL and CSF LDH levels were independent predictors of death, indicating their potential value as early risk stratification in AHD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12985-026-03234-x.Keywords: HIV-1, Central nervous system infection, Pathogens, Metagenomic next-generation sequencing, Cerebrospinal fluid

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection remains a major global public health issue. Currently, approximately 40.8 million people worldwide are living with HIV, and about 30% of them have progressed to the advanced HIV-1 disease (AHD) [1]. The prevalence of AHD is particularly high in resource-limited settings, mainly due to delayed diagnosis, treatment interruption or poor adherence to antiretroviral therapy (ART), and drug resistance [2]. Because of severe immunodeficiency, patients with AHD (defined by CD4 T cell counts less than 200 cells/µL) are at increased risk of severe opportunistic infections, which may result in poor clinical outcomes and high mortality [3].

HIV-1 can invade multiple organ systems, including the central nervous system (CNS). Approximately 40%–70% of people living with HIV-1 (PLWH) develop CNS-related symptoms during disease progression, and 10%–20% initially present with neurological manifestations at disease onset [4, 5]. A previous study has demonstrated that HIV-1 can be detected in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) as early as approximately eight days after infection [6]. Within the CNS, HIV-1 has been shown to infect multiple cell types, including CD4 T cells, monocytes, microglia, perivascular macrophages, and astrocytes [7, 8]. Due to severe immunodeficiency, patients with AHD are at an increased risk of CNS infections, which can lead to significant neurological complications and increased mortality [9]. Early diagnosis of CNS infections in PLWH enables timely intervention, reduces morbidity and mortality, and prevents long-term neurological sequelae. However, in resource-limited settings, the lack of adequate microbiological diagnostic tools often prompts clinicians to initiate empiric antimicrobial therapy, despite the unclear spectrum of CNS pathogens among PLWH. This practice not only limits precision therapy but may also result in delayed or inappropriate treatment. Therefore, defining the regional distribution of CNS pathogens is important for guiding the formulation of rational empiric treatment strategies.

CSF is commonly collected for PLWH with suspected CNS infection. However, current diagnostic approaches are limited to conventional CSF analyses, including routine and biochemical tests, Gram staining, India ink staining, cryptococcal antigen testing, and bacterial or fungal cultures. These traditional methods often have suboptimal sensitivity and specificity and require a prolonged turnaround time. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) enables unbiased, high-throughput sequencing of all microbial DNA or RNA in clinical samples, including bacteria, fungi, viruses, and parasites without the need for prior assumptions about the causative agent. Compared with conventional methods, mNGS offers higher sensitivity, shorter turnaround time, and broader pathogen coverage, making it particularly advantageous for etiological diagnosis of complex infectious disease. Clinically, mNGS has been successfully applied to CSF [10, 11], bronchoalveolar lavage fluid [12], and specimens from spinal infections [13]. Notably, mNGS shows distinct advantages in PLWH for suspected CNS infection. Chen et al. analyzed CSF from 88 PLWH with negative routine CSF tests, CSF detected Epstein-Barr virus (EBV, 31.8%) and cytomegalovirus (CMV, 17.0%), and other pathogens JC virus, varicella-zoster virus, and Toxoplasma gondii (T. gondii) [10]. Another study reported a 75.7% positive detection rate in CSF from PLWH with suspected CNS infections, with EBV (33.6%), CMV (20.7%), and torque teno virus (TTV, 13.8%) being the most frequent pathogens [11]. The Variability in reported infection rates depends on geographic region, ART use, prophylactic medication, immune status, and gender [14]. Overall, mNGS can effectively reveal the complex and diverse pathogens in PLWH with suspected CNS infections, providing critical support for precision diagnosis and clinical management. Despite the improved pathogen detection achieved by mNGS, its use in resource-limited settings remains constrained by high costs and inadequate infrastructure, and patient prognosis remains poor. This underscores the need for simple prognostic tools based on routine laboratory parameters to identify high-risk individuals. In resource-limited settings, CSF biochemical indicators are also frequently used to help distinguish types of CNS infections [15–17], and PLWH show distinct CSF profiles across different CNS etiologies [18]. However, the prognostic relevance of these CSF biochemical markers in PLWH with CNS infections remains unclear. Identifying clinically meaningful indicators and developing practical risk-stratification strategies are therefore essential for improving outcomes in these settings.

In our study, CSF samples were collected from 61 PLWH with suspected CNS infection in Guangxi, China. We analyzed the types of CNS infection in these patients by using mNGS, while simultaneously collecting clinical data and laboratory indicators. Clinical characteristics were compared across different infection types (including viral, bacterial, fungal, parasitic, and mixed infections), and key predictors associated with in-hospital mortality were assessed. We found that CNS infections were common in AHD and were predominantly viral in origin. Infection type was not significantly associated with in-hospital mortality. In contrast, plasma LDL and CSF LDH levels were independent predictors of in-hospital mortality, suggesting that these two biomarkers may assist in early stratification for PLWH with suspected CNS infections.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

Our study included 61 ART-naïve PLWH who had clinically suspected CNS infection at the Fourth People’s Hospital of Nanning, enrolled between May 2023 and May 2025. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≤ 18 years; (2) pregnant or lactating women; (3) patients with a documented immunosuppressive condition except for HIV-1 infection; (4) inability to obtain CSF. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fourth People’s Hospital of Nanning (Approval No. [2023]64), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cerebrospinal fluid sample collection

A total of 10 mL of CSF was collected via lumbar puncture. Approximately 2 mL of each sample was placed in sterile centrifuge tubes and transported under cold-chain conditions to the genomics laboratory for mNGS. The remaining sample was promptly sent to the clinical laboratory within 2 h for routine cell counts; biochemical tests, including LDH, adenosine deaminase (ADA), proteins, glucose, and chloride; CSF smears; pathogen cultures; as well as molecular diagnosis testing.

Clinical data collection

We collected sociodemographic data, clinical characteristics, and laboratory data for all participants. The variables included signs and symptoms, infection types, HIV-1 viral load, CD4 and CD8 T lymphocyte counts, hematological and liver function parameters, as well as lipid metabolism parameters.

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing

Samples were collected in sterile centrifuge tubes and sent to HugoBiotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) for mNGS detection. DNA was extracted from CSF samples using QIAamp DNA Micro Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA libraries were constructed with the QIAseq™ Ultralow Input Library Kit for Illumina (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA of the samples was extracted using the PACEseq RNA Extraction Reagent Kit (YGZZ009; HugoBiotech, Beijing, China). Subsequently, the RNA sequencing libraries were constructed using the PACEseq Universal RNA Sequencing Library Prep Kit (YGZZ010; HugoBiotech, Beijing, China). The Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, USA) were utilized to assess the quality and quantity of each library. Only those libraries that met the quality standards were sequenced on the NextSeq 550 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA), targeting approximately 20 million 75 bp single-end reads per library.

For bioinformatics analysis, raw data were filtered to remove adapter sequences, short reads (< 35 bp), low-quality reads (Q < 30), and low-complexity reads. Human reads were excluded by mapping to the human reference genome (hg38). The remaining reads were aligned to Microbial Genome Databases using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner. Negative controls (NTC, sterile deionized water) and positive controls (known quantities of synthetic fragments) were included in each batch of experiments, following the same wet lab and bioinformatics procedures. For detected bacteria (excluding Mycobacterium tuberculosis), fungi (excluding Cryptococcus), and parasites, a positive result was considered if the coverage ranked in the top 10 of similar species/genera and was absent in NTC, or if the reads per million (RPM) ratios between the sample and NTC (RPM sample/RPM NTC) was greater than 10 when RPM NTC was not zero. For viruses, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Cryptococcus, a positive mNGS result was indicated by the detection of at least one species-specific read and its absence in NTC, or an RPM sample/RPM NTC ratio greater than 5 when RPM NTC was not zero.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), SPSS version 22.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA), and R Studio version 3.6.4 (R Studio, Boston, MA, USA) were used to analyze the data. Continuous variables are expressed as the median interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables are expressed as count (%). The χ2 test was used to compare categorical data between groups. The Mann–Whitney U test between two groups was used to compare continuous data. Correlations were identified using Spearman rank correlation test between two continuous variables. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were utilized to assess predictors of death. A significance level of < 0.10 was used to select variables for the multivariate analyses. Maximum likelihood method was used to estimate model parameters, and significance was tested using the Wald test statistic. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of study participants

A total of 61 ART-naïve PLWH with suspected CNS infection were included in the study. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the included patients are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 47.5 years (IQR: 41.0–56.3), and the majority were male (52/61, 85.3%). The most common clinical symptom was fever (32/61, 52.5%). Overall, 14 (23.0%) died during hospitalization. Most patients had AHD, with a median CD4 T cell count of 65 cells/µL (IQR: 28.0–131.5). Specifically, a total of 24 patients (39.3%) had CD4 T cell counts less than 50 cells/µL, 29 patients (47.5%) had CD4 T cell counts between 50 and 200 cells/µL, 3 patients (4.9%) had CD4 T cell counts between 200 and 350 cells/µL, and 5 patients (8.2%) had CD4 T cell counts more than 350 cells/µL. Comorbidities were common and present in 54 patients (88.5%), with liver disease (39.3%), respiratory disease (32.8%), and hematologic disease (52.5%) being the most frequently reported.

Distribution of pathogens detected by mNGS in CSF

To characterize the spectrum of CNS pathogens in PLWH with suspected CNS infections, we performed mNGS on CSF samples from all 61 participants. A total of 206 pathogens were identified, with viruses accounting for the largest proportion (137/206, 66.5%), followed by bacteria (50/206, 24.3%), fungi (17/206, 8.3%), and parasites (2/206, 1.0%) (Table 2). Viral infections were predominantly caused by DNA viruses. Among these, EBV was the most frequently detected DNA virus in CSF of PLWH (33/76, 43.4%), followed by CMV (21/76, 27.6%), and TTV (11/76, 14.5%). Notably, HIV-1 itself, an RNA virus, was detected in all 61 participants. Among bacterial infections, 31 Gram-positive species were identified, with Staphylococcus epidermidis being the most common (8/31, 25.8%). Seventeen fungal species were detected, with Talaromyces marneffei (T. marneffei, 7/17, 41.2%) and Cryptococcus neoformans (C. neoformans, 6/17, 35.3%) being the predominant pathogens. Parasitic infections were rare, observed in only two participants, both caused by T. gondii.

Clinical characteristics in PLWH with different types of CNS infection

To compare the clinical characteristics of PLWH with different CNS infection patterns, study participants were categorized according to the types of non-HIV pathogen detected in CSF: negative group (Neg, 4/61, 6.6%), viral infection group (V, 17/61, 27.9%), bacterial infection group (B, 10/61, 16.4%), viral–bacterial co-infection group (V + B, 11/61, 18.0%), viral–fungal co-infection group (V + F, 9/61, 14.8%), viral–parasitic co-infection group (V + P, 1/61, 1.6%), viral–bacterial–fungal co-infection group (V + B+F, 8/61, 13.1%), and viral–bacterial–parasitic co-infection group (V + B+P, 1/61, 1.6%) (Fig. 1). After excluding the groups with very small sample sizes (Neg, V + P, and V + B+P), comparative analysis showed that patients in the V + B+F group exhibited a significantly lower or decreasing trend in the proportion of lymphocytes, CD4 T cell counts, and CD8 T cell counts compared with other groups. Conversely, CRP levels exhibited a significant elevation or an upward trend. In addition, lipid metabolism parameters, including apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1), high density lipoprotein (HDL), and LDL, were markedly decreased, accompanied by a lower ALB/GLO and decreased CSF protein levels (Fig. 2A, B). Factors associated with the in-hospital outcomes

Participants were categorized into survival and death groups based on in-hospital outcomes. The overall in-hospital mortality rate was 23.0% (14/61), including 9 males and 5 females, with a significant difference in gender distribution between groups (P < 0.05); however, no significant differences were observed in infection types. Further analysis of clinical parameters revealed a trend toward older age in the death group compared with the survival group (P < 0.1). Inflammation-related markers, including CRP, WBC, and neutrophil counts, were higher in the death group, whereas RBC counts were lower. Regarding liver function, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were lower, but the AST/ALT ratio was relatively higher in the death group. Lipid-related markers, including total cholesterol (CHOL), LDL, HDL, and apolipoprotein B (ApoB), were all elevated in the death group compared with the survival group. CSF biochemical indicators, including LDH, ADA, protein, and chloride, were also higher in the death group (Fig. 4, and Figure S2).

To identify clinical indicators capable of distinguishing in-hospital outcomes, variables with P < 0.1 were first included in the candidate pool, excluding those with more than 10% missing data. Univariate regression analysis was then used to remove variables with P > 0.05 (Fig. 5A). Ten prognostic factors associated with in-hospital mortality, including gender, RBC counts, ALT, CHOL, HDL, LDL, and ApoB levels, as well as CSF biochemical indicators such as LDH, ADA, and protein, were identified and included for further analysis (Table S2). Given the relatively small sample size, we included the four variables with the smallest P values (LDL, CSF ADA, CSF protein, and CSF LDH) in the multivariate regression model (Fig. 5B). Notably, all four belonged to cluster 7, and among them, plasma LDL (OR = 7.353, 95% CI: 2.008–27.027, P = 0.003) and CSF LDH (OR = 1.014, 95% CI: 1.002–1.026, P = 0.019) were the most robust independent predictors of in-hospital mortality (Fig. 5C and E). Receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 96.12%, and the model’s goodness of fit was confirmed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (Fig. 5C).

Discussion

AHD remains concentrated in resource-limited settings such as West and Central Africa, South-East Asia and the Eastern Mediterranean region [19]. In China, Guangxi is among the provinces with the highest burden of AHD. Owing to severe immunosuppression, PLWH with AHD face a markedly increased risk of CNS infections, which represent a major cause of hospitalization and mortality in this population [10, 20]. This study focused on ART-naïve PLWH with suspected CNS infections in Guangxi, most of whom had AHD. We systematically analyzed the microbiological characteristics and clinical manifestations of CNS infection and further examined the key factors associated with in-hospital prognosis. The findings demonstrated that CNS infection was relatively common in PLWH, predominantly caused by viral pathogens. Additionally, the coexistence of multiple pathogens in CSF was highly prevalent. Importantly, we identified elevated plasma LDL and CSF LDH levels as independent risk factors for poor prognosis, suggesting their potential as therapeutic targets for improving prognosis in PLWH with suspected CNS infection.

Our study showed an in-hospital mortality rate of 23.0% (14/61), with a higher proportion of females in the death group (5/14, 35.7%) compared to the survival group (4/47, 8.5%). This mortality rate is very close to that reported in another Chinese study [21]. It is significantly lower than the mortality rate of 27.8% (67/178) observed by de Melo et al. in Manaus, Amazonas [22] and the 35.3% reported by Siddiqi et al. in Zambia [23]. This discrepancy may be attributed to relatively limited healthcare resources in those regions, and the higher proportion of female participants in their study populations. Although infection types were not significantly different between the two groups, patients in the death group tended to be older than the survival group, indicating that age-related immunosenescence may exacerbate disease severity in HIV-related CNS infections.

Regarding lipid metabolism, our study found that CHOL, LDL, and HDL levels were higher in the death group than in the survival group, with elevated LDL being an independent risk factor for mortality in PLWH. Among PLWH with multiple pathogens infections (viral, bacterial, and fungal), HDL and LDL levels were significantly lower than in those with single or dual infections. PLWH with AHD often exhibit cachexia, accompanied by significant malnutrition and metabolic disorders, which may explain the generally lower lipid levels in patients with multiple infections. Elevated LDL is a well-established pathogenic risk factor for the development of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease [24, 25]. It has also been reported as a risk factor for readmission after the first hospitalization in T. marneffei infection [26]. Conversely, low LDL levels have been associated with poor prognosis in COVID-19 and sepsis patients [27, 28]. Additionally, epidemiological studies have shown that in the general population, both low and high LDL levels are associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality [29]. These seemingly contradictory findings suggest that lipid molecules, particularly in AHD, not only reflect host nutritional status but may also be related to inflammatory response and immune regulatory functions. Their mechanisms of action may be far more complex than the traditional lipid interpretation framework in cardiovascular disease. Therefore, lipid molecules warrant further investigation in the assessment of disease progression and prognosis in AHD.

In the CSF biochemical analysis, we found that levels of LDH, ADA, protein, and chloride were significantly higher in the death group than in the survival group. These indicators are commonly used in resource-limited settings to assist in determining the type of CNS infection and to provide important clues for the preliminary differentiation of various pathogens [15]. ADA, a key enzyme involved in purine metabolism, is typically elevated in patients with tuberculous meningitis [30, 31], and its diagnostic value has also been validated in PLWH complicated by tuberculous meningitis [32]. Moreover, elevated CSF ADA has been closely associated with poor outcomes in tuberculous meningitis, underscoring its significance in reflecting disease severity [33].

One of the key features of HIV-1 infection is chronic immune activation and inflammation [34]. In our cohort, the marked elevation of CRP, WBC, and neutrophil counts in the death group indicates a substantially higher inflammatory burden. Our previous study demonstrated that chronic inflammation drives CD4 T cell pyroptosis in the peripheral blood of PLWH, and inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome reduces CD4 T cell pyroptosis and the associated release of LDH [35]. In this study, we further identified elevated CSF LDH as an independent predictor of mortality, suggesting that CNS inflammation or cell injury may contribute critically to disease deterioration. LDH, an intracellular enzyme catalyzing the interconversion of lactate and pyruvate, also serves as a biochemical marker of cell death; its elevation in CSF may reflect pyroptosis of infiltrating T cells, macrophages, microglia, or neurons within the CNS. Previous studies have consistently supported the diagnostic or prognostic utility of LDH across multiple CNS infections, including refractory bacterial meningitis in children [36], tuberculous meningitis [16], bacterial meningitis [15], and early diagnosis of bacterial meningitis in PLWH [17]. Elevated LDH has also been linked to poorer outcomes in cryptococcal meningitis [37], disease activity in primary CNS lymphoma [38], and neurological recovery in cardiac arrest survivors [39]. Taken together, these findings highlight LDH as a readily accessible marker that reflects CNS tissue injury and inflammatory severity. In resource-limited settings, CSF LDH may serve as a practical indicator for assessing disease severity and stratifying early mortality risk in PLWH with suspected CNS infection.

Among the identified pathogens, this study demonstrates that viruses constitute the predominant pathogens causing CNS infections among PLWH in Guangxi, with EBV and CMV being the most frequently detected. Notably, the detection rate of EBV reached 54.1% (33/61) among all enrolled patients, which is substantially higher than the 5.3%−45.2% reported in previous studies from African regions such as Malawi and from Manaus in South America [40–42]. This discrepancy is likely attributable to the fact that participants in our study were ART-naïve and exhibited severe immunosuppression. Prior studies have shown that PLWH receiving ART exhibit lower EBV detection rates in CSF [43]. From a mechanistic perspective, EBV infection in the CNS carries important clinical implications. EBV has been firmly linked to primary CNS lymphoma in PLWH [44], and thus its detection in patients with AHD warrants heightened clinical vigilance. More importantly, CSF EBV DNA positivity has been associated with elevated levels of neuronal injury biomarkers and an increased risk of mortality [43]. EBV may mediate neural injury through multiple pathways, including direct viral infection of brain-resident cells, activation of glial cells, and induction of CNS inflammatory responses [45, 46]. In addition, findings from immune-mediated disease, multiple sclerosis, further suggest that EBV can drive the expansion of peripheral B cells and promote their trafficking into the CNS, while concurrently recruiting inflammatory T cells into brain tissue. These processes collectively create a persistent inflammatory microenvironment that contributes to demyelination and neuronal damage [46]. Similar mechanisms may also operate in PLWH, leading to sustained CNS inflammation and tissue injury and ultimately contributing to HIV-associated neurocognitive impairment. CMV is a highly prevalent pathogen among PLWH, with seropositivity rates exceeding 90% [47], and remains one of the most common causes of CNS complications in PLWH with AHD [48]. In our study, CMV was detected in 34.4% (21/61) of CSF from PLWH. This proportion exceeds those reported in several earlier studies, such as 22.2% in ART-naïve PLWH [21] and 6.3% among individuals with detectable plasma HIV-1 RNA [43], differences that may arise from variations in host immune status, HIV-1 viral load, diagnostic approaches, and regional epidemiology. Importantly, accumulating evidence shows that CMV persistence in the CNS is associated with neuronal injury and heightened systemic inflammation in PLWH [43], suggesting that its detection is not merely an epiphenomenon of immunosuppression but may represent an active contributor to neuropathogenesis. These observations underscore the need for timely identification of CMV-related CNS involvement in patients with AHD to prevent irreversible neurological damage.

In this study, T. marneffei and C. neoformans were the most frequently identified fungal infection types. T. marneffei is a major opportunistic fungal pathogen that causes life-threatening disseminated infections in PLWH, particularly those with AHD in Southeast Asia and southern China. The prevalence of T. marneffei infection can reach approximately 15.0% in southern regions of China, especially in Guangxi, where it contributes to high in-hospital mortality rates exceeding 30%, highlighting its substantial disease burden in PLWH [49, 50]. In our study, T. marneffei was detected in the CSF of 11.48% (7/61) of patients, suggesting that this pathogen not only causes systemic dissemination but may also involve CNS. Previous study has confirmed that T. marneffei can invade the CSF [51]. Moreover, in PLWH with T. marneffei-associated CNS infection, CD4 T cell counts in non-survivors were significantly lower than those in survivors, indicating that severe immunosuppression is strongly associated with poor outcomes [51]. Cryptococcosis remains the second leading cause of AIDS-related deaths worldwide [52]. CNS is the organ most frequently affected by C. neoformans infection, typically manifesting as cryptococcal meningitis. In our study, C. neoformans was detected in 9.8% (6/61) of CSF from PLWH, a prevalence comparable to findings reported in other resource-limited settings. For instance, de Melo et al. observed a CSF cryptococcal positivity rate of 7.7% (19/245) among PLWH in Manaus, Amazonas [22]. Similarly, a 2024 study by Zono et al. in the Republic of the Congo reported a 7.2% prevalence among PLWH with AHD [53]. These consistent findings across geographically diverse regions highlight the sustained burden of cryptococcal infection in PLWH with AHD.

This high diagnostic yield of pathogens underscores the robust performance of mNGS in our study. The substantially higher positivity rate of mNGS compared with conventional CSF culture and pathogen-specific PCR may be explained by both methodological differences and clinical factors. Pathogen-specific PCR is inherently restricted to a limited number of predefined organisms and thus cannot detect unexpected or uncommon pathogens, while conventional culture depends on the presence of viable microorganisms and may show reduced sensitivity for CSF samples with low pathogen burden or after prior antimicrobial exposure. In contrast, mNGS provides a relatively unbiased approach for detecting microbial nucleic acids across a broad spectrum of bacteria, fungi, viruses, and parasites without requiring prior assumptions about the etiologic agent, which may account for its markedly higher detection yield in this cohort. From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that mNGS may be particularly valuable as an adjunctive or second-line test in patients with suspected CNS infection whose routine CSF culture and targeted PCR results are negative despite persistent clinical suspicion. However, mNGS results should still be interpreted in conjunction with clinical presentation, neuroimaging, routine CSF findings, and other microbiological evidence to distinguish true causative pathogens from background signals or clinically insignificant detections.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, and the findings lack external validation in independent cohorts, which limits the generalizability and statistical power of the findings. Larger multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate these results. Second, while mNGS enables unbiased detection of microbial nucleic acids, the distinction between true infection and background colonization or residual nucleic acid from dead microbes remains challenging. Third, the performance of mNGS is influenced by multiple technical factors, such as nucleic acid extraction, library preparation, and platform-specific workflows, that may affect pathogen detection sensitivity. Although standardized procedures and negative controls were applied, mNGS remains vulnerable to background microbial nucleic acids, making it difficult to distinguish true low-abundance pathogens from noise. These technical and interpretive challenges may lead to missed or spurious detections.

Conclusion

CNS infection of PLWH is predominantly caused by viral pathogens, and co-infections are relatively common. Patients with multiple pathogen infections are characterized by severe immunosuppression, markedly elevated inflammatory markers, and substantially reduced lipid metabolites, suggesting a more complex disease state. Importantly, plasma LDL and CSF LDH were identified as independent risk factors for mortality in PLWH with suspected CNS infection. Incorporating these readily accessible biomarkers into early risk-stratification frameworks may facilitate the timely identification of high-risk patients, optimize therapeutic decision-making, and ultimately improve clinical outcomes.
