Educational inequalities in site-specific cancer mortality: a Japanese census-linked study
Authors: Hirokazu Tanaka, Kota Katanoda, Tomoki Nakaya, Kayo Togawa, Yasuki Kobayashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReports on socioeconomic inequalities in cancer mortality are limited in East Asia. We investigated educational inequalities in cancer mortality in Japan, serving as an advanced example of a nationwide census-based surveillance.MethodsWe developed a Japanese census-linked mortality dataset, using our unique linkage method. The dataset encompassed approximately 0.48 million cancer deaths linked to individual-level census data of 80 million Japanese aged 25–84 years in October 2020. We calculated age-standardised all-cancer and 23 site-specific cancer mortality rates (ASMRs) by educational level. Educational inequalities were quantified using the Relative Index of Inequality (RII) and Slope Index of Inequality (SII) by educational level (high, middle and low). Site-specific cancer contribution to absolute educational inequalities in all cancers was evaluated using the proportions of SII (%: SIICancer-site/SIIAll-cancer×100).ResultsAll cancers’ RIIs were 1.58 (95% CI 1.56 to 1.60) and 1.43 (1.40 to 1.46) in men and women, respectively. Among men, the rectum, stomach, liver and lung were the leading sites based on site-specific cancer RIIs, while the larynx, cervix uteri, liver and lung were the leading sites among women. In absolute terms, lung cancer contributed the most to educational inequalities, followed by stomach, colorectal and liver cancers. No educational inequality was found for pancreatic cancer. Breast cancer showed higher ASMRs among women with low education levels than among those with high education levels, which differed from the previous inequality pattern.ConclusionsEquity-focused strategies to enhance primary and secondary prevention are essential and should be supported by comprehensive nationwide monitoring.

Introduction

Reducing socioeconomic inequalities in cancer is a priority for public health worldwide. Health policy includes reducing socioeconomic inequalities as an overall goal and key element in healthcare or cancer control plans in countries such as the US,1 the UK,2 Australia3 and Canada.4 Although national or regional average values of cancer outcomes (eg, mortality and incidence) have been continuously monitored worldwide,5 evaluating socioeconomic inequalities within countries remains a major challenge,6 even amid the progress of digital health transformation, which is still unclear in East Asia, including Japan.

To monitor socioeconomic inequalities in cancer mortality, educational inequalities have commonly been assessed internationally using nationwide health statistics.7–14 A novel, systematic assessment of socioeconomic inequalities in cancer was previously conducted across many European countries and time periods, using census-linked, population-wide, cancer-specific mortality data.7 The study mentioned above concluded that educational inequalities in cancer mortality in Europe were mainly attributable to higher mortality rates among groups with low education levels.7 Notably, it also reported that even in Nordic countries—with a longstanding tradition of equitable welfare—cancer inequalities had increased among women.7 However, reports on educational inequalities in cancer mortality are limited, both between and within countries in East Asia, where stomach and liver cancer mortality rates are generally higher than those in other high-income countries.15 16 In this context, elucidating the overall perspective of these findings, including broad types of cancer, is essential for cross-country comparisons to inform both international and national cancer control efforts.

Although nationwide cancer statistics have been available from vital statistics since 1899 and the National Cancer Registry since 2016,17 reports on socioeconomic inequalities in cancer mortality in Japan are limited. Reducing inequalities was mentioned in the Basic Plan to Promote Cancer Control in Japan; however, no detailed target values and benchmarks to guide plans have yet been developed.18 In Japan, prefectural cancer mortality rates are well known.17 19 For example, cancer sites with apparent regional mortality differences include stomach, liver, lung and breast.17 19 However, besides these cross-sectional geographic analyses, no study has focused on individual sociodemographic patterns and inequalities in site-specific cancer mortality in the Japanese population. This is due to the lack of a national-level linkage framework between health and population statistics, limiting investigations of health-related inequalities in Japan. Moreover, this situation impedes the realisation of cancer prevention, treatment and mortality inequalities, which should be addressed to reduce health-related inequalities.

To address these limitations, a Japanese census-linked mortality dataset,20 developed by using our unique linkage method,21 22 provides a better tool for understanding health-related inequalities and an opportunity for individual-level data linkage across the entire Japanese population. This census-linked study represents an advanced example of a census-based (or registry-based) surveillance in East Asia, providing deep insights into socioeconomic differences in site-specific cancer mortality. We aimed to investigate educational inequalities in cancer mortality in Japan, focusing on site-specific cancers.

Methods

Data sources

We obtained census microdata in 2020 from the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications (MIC) to prepare the population data.23 The original dataset included data on 117 827 253 non-institutionalised Japanese individuals (57 475 369 men and 60 351 884 women) with complete birth year and month information. We also obtained all 2 949 946 death records between October 2020 and September 2022 (1 510 091 men and 1 439 855 women) from the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) following the required procedures.17 Foreign nationals residing in Japan were excluded due to concerns regarding the accuracy of data linkage.

Ethical approval was not required in this study, as it used secondary census and vital statistics data managed by the MIC and MHLW. Microdata from the census and vital statistics were obtained from the MIC and MHLW, respectively, in accordance with Article 33 of the Statistics Act of Japan. The MIC and MHLW provided the data after considering data management and protection compliance, and the data were used in compliance with their instructions.

Data linkage

Using these two datasets and a deterministic linkage method,21 22 we developed a Japanese census-linked mortality data, as previously described.20 Based on the census information in October 2020 as the baseline,23 death records (vital statistics)17 were linked individually through September 2022 (during a consecutive 24-month period); individuals without successful linkage were assumed to be alive. The five variables (‘linkage keys’) comprised sex, birth year/month, residential address (prefecture, municipality, census block (cho-aza) and basic unit block (kihontan-iku)), marital status and spouse’s age. To enhance the linkage success rate, data linkage was performed in stages using (1) Basic unit blocks, (2) Census blocks and (3) Municipalities as hierarchical linkage keys.20 Overall, the final analysed data included 479 545 cancer-related deaths (300 166 men and 179 379 women) linked to census records of 82 155 554 non-institutionalised Japanese individuals (aged 25–84 years: 39 841 742 men and 42 313 812 women). The developed mortality data covered 86.9% and 84.9% of all cancer-related deaths in men and women aged 25–84 years, respectively (online supplemental table 1).

This methodological framework—linking census data (or registry data) with death records to construct population-wide mortality datasets—has been extensively implemented, especially across European countries, and underpins a wide range of registry-based studies for identifying mortality.7 9–13

Assessment of socioeconomic inequalities

In a preliminary assessment, we explored whether the current register or survey data in Japan enable the mortality analysis by socioeconomic status using three key indicators: income, educational level and occupation. We ultimately used educational level, derived from the census information and classified according to the International Standard Classification of Education24—‘low’ (elementary school/junior high school graduates), ‘middle’ (high school graduates) and ‘high’ (technical professional school/2-year college/university/graduate school graduates)21—as the main indicator to assess socioeconomic inequalities since it is more robust across countries and widely used to compare socioeconomic inequalities in health. In Japan, educational level has been used to investigate socioeconomic inequalities in mortality in the early 2010s,21 22 coronavirus disease 2019 mortality,20 self-rated health,25 cancer screening participation26 and smoking.27 Occupational class was included as a sociodemographic characteristic, not a main socioeconomic indicator, because it was restricted to the working-age population, given that the census collects information on both occupation and employment status at the time of the census.20 22 We considered it infeasible to assess income-based mortality inequalities because relevant data are unavailable from the census.

Classifications of site-specific cancer

Site-specific cancer classifications were defined according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, as follows: all cancers (C00–C96); oral cavity and pharynx (C00–C14), oesophagus (C15), stomach (C16), colorectal (C18–C20), colon (C18), rectum (C19–C20), liver (C22), gallbladder and bile ducts (C23–C24), pancreas (C25), larynx (C32), lung (C33–C34), skin (C43–C44), breast (C50), uterus (C53–C55), cervix uteri (C53), corpus uteri (C54), ovary (C56), prostate (C61), bladder (C67), kidney and other urinary organs (C64–C66 and C68), brain, nervous system (C70–C72) and thyroid (C73) cancers; malignant lymphoma (C81–C85 and C96); multiple myeloma (C88 and C90) and leukaemia (C91–C95).

Analyses

Calculation of cancer mortality (pre-analysis)

Person-years were calculated as the aggregate time contributed by individuals at risk of all-cause death. During the first year (October 2020 to September 2021), all non-institutionalised Japanese individuals included in the mortality data contributed person-years; in the subsequent year, only those who survived the initial year (ie, had not died from any cause) continued to contribute person-years. No adjustments were made for censoring (eg, institutionalisation in geriatric health services facilities) when calculating person-years due to a lack of data. Accumulated person-years and deaths were finally classified by 5-year age intervals. Mortality rates (among non-institutionalised individuals aged 25–84 years) were calculated based on sociodemographic characteristics. These sociodemographic characteristics included marital status (single, married, widowed and divorced), household size (‘living alone’ to ‘five or more’), educational level, Area Deprivation Index (ADI) (at municipality level: quintiles ranging from 1 (least deprived) to 5 (most deprived))28 and occupational class (for those aged 30–69 years: five categories defined by the Erikson–Goldthorpe–Portocarero scheme and ‘non-employee/unknown’).20 22 Age-standardised mortality rates (ASMRs) for all cancers were calculated by marital status, household size, educational level, ADI (five categories) and occupational class. Standardisation was performed using the direct method with the 2015 Japan Standard Population.29 ASMRs were also compared using both the census-linked mortality data and the complete registry without linkage to validate cancer mortality estimations using census-linked mortality data (online supplemental table 1).

Before conducting the main analysis for educational inequalities in cancer mortality, sociodemographic patterns in age-adjusted cancer mortality were assessed using Poisson regression with robust standard error estimation to account for overdispersion. A multivariable Poisson regression analysis was also performed with individual age (5-year age intervals), marital status, household size, educational level, ADI and prefecture (n=47) as covariates.20 Person-years at risk were included as offsets, with death counts serving as outcomes. Occupational class was excluded from the covariates because it was restricted to the working-age population.20 The above analyses were conducted to confirm whether statistical adjustment for sociodemographic characteristics affected the evaluation of educational inequalities in mortality.

Assessment of mortality inequalities (main analysis)

ASMRs for all cancers and each site-specific cancer were evaluated by educational level (high, middle and low) to assess socioeconomic inequalities. Individuals with unknown educational levels were excluded from inequality analyses. We calculated the Relative Index of Inequality (RII) and Slope Index of Inequality (SII) for education.30 These measures consider all educational level groups and the proportional distribution of each group within the total population. In this analysis, the RII (modified version by Mackenbach and Kunst) represents the ratio of the estimated mortality rate between individuals at ranks 0 (the highest educational level) and 1 (the lowest educational level).30 The SII (ie, absolute inequality indices) quantifies the absolute difference in ASMRs between the highest and lowest educational levels.30 Both indicators were calculated using Poisson regression models. Discussion

The origin of diversity in site-specific cancer mortality inequalities ranges from exposure to cancer risk factors (such as individual and population behaviours and environmental factors) and interactions between biological and social factors to access to early diagnosis and screening programmes, as well as effective treatment. Site-specific cancer ASMR differences across educational levels were evident, as indicated by the RII and SII values. The strength of this study is the detailed analysis of site-specific cancer mortality, including minor cancers with low mortality. Estimating the contribution of each site-specific cancer to cancer mortality inequality is a rigorous approach that can support systematic assessment of inequalities and inform priority setting.

The most important finding in this study was that lung, stomach, colorectal, liver, gallbladder and bile duct and cervix uteri cancers accounted for most of the observed cancer mortality inequalities in Japan. Lung cancer had the largest contribution in both sexes (34.6% in men and 23.0% in women), which aligns with previous findings in Western countries.7 31 32 This is consistent with the observed socioeconomic inequalities in smoking-related risk factors.27 In Japan, smoking prevalence among men aged 25–64 years with low, middle and high education levels in 2010 was 60.0%, 49.2% and 32.9%, respectively.27 These patterns are clearly associated with large contributions to smoking-related cancers, and socioeconomic inequalities in cancer are likely to persist in the near future unless further equity-focused tobacco control measures are implemented.

Educational inequalities in absolute terms (figure 1) were also largely driven by stomach and liver cancers in Japan, which differed from those in Western countries, where these cancers did not make large contributions.7 31 These trends may form a unique pattern in East Asia. Although high absolute mortality rates for these cancers in Japan15 16 suggest that they would influence inequalities, our study clarified their specific contributions with large relative inequalities.

The Japanese government recommends a population-based screening programme (mainly provided in communities and workplaces) for five major types of cancer (lung, colorectal, stomach, female breast and cervical cancers) to enable early detection and reduce mortality.33 However, cancer screening participation was unequal by socioeconomic status in Japan, as individuals with lower socioeconomic status underuse healthcare opportunities. In 2016, colorectal cancer screening rates in individuals with low-, middle- and high-education levels (40-69 years) were 28.9%, 44.1% and 56.4% for men, respectively, and 25.9%, 38.9% and 46.4% for women, respectively.26 These patterns were similar across all five major types of cancer screening and were suggested to be associated with educational inequalities in lung, colorectal and stomach cancer mortality in both sexes. The current findings also suggested that educational inequalities in colorectal cancer mortality widened between the early 2010s and 2020s in Japan.21 Therefore, the nationwide initiative for reducing these inequalities is warranted in addition to improving the national average value of screening.

Our findings also underscore the significant contributions of infection-related cancers, such as stomach, liver and cervical cancers.34 Liver cancer mortality is particularly high in Western Japan,19 reflecting the region’s higher prevalence of hepatitis C.35 In addition to regional inequalities, reducing educational inequalities in liver cancer is also an important public health goal. Cervical cancer ranked sixth in its contribution to absolute inequalities among Japanese women, with a particularly high RII (=2.45). In Europe, cervical cancer also exhibits substantial educational inequalities in mortality (rate ratio between women with low and high education levels: 3.0 (95% CI 2.5 to 3.6)).7 Human papillomavirus vaccine promotion was suspended between 2013 and 2021 in Japan due to allegedly adverse events, which caused a drastic drop in vaccination rates from 70% to nearly zero.36 Regarding screening, socioeconomic inequalities were also apparent: cervical cancer screening rates were 29.6%, 41.7% and 48.6% for women with low, middle and high education levels in 2016, respectively.26 An equity-oriented approach to monitoring and implementing vaccination and screening programmes is needed to reduce cervical cancer mortality and its inequalities.37

Pancreatic, skin, brain and nervous system cancers, as well as leukaemia, had relatively small educational inequalities in mortality among the Japanese population, which was an additional finding of this study. We considered that these cancers show small educational inequalities because lifestyle-related risk factors contribute relatively little to their development. Therefore, efforts are needed to reduce average mortality rates for these cancers without widening inequalities.

Particular caution is required when interpreting the results for breast cancer because they differ from previous reports in Japan. In the 2010s, breast cancer mortality was higher among individuals with higher educational levels than among those with lower levels,21 a pattern consistent with findings in European countries including the UK, Spain, France and Norway.7 38 However, our current results showed lower breast cancer mortality among women with higher educational levels than among those with lower educational levels in Japan. This remarkable transition between the early 2010s and 2020s can be considered an epidemiological transition of inequalities due to changes in important risk factors for breast cancer (eg, declining birthrate) at the population level. In addition, the transition may be because of improved access to screening,26 followed by early effective treatment among women with higher educational levels.

Educational inequalities in Japan appear to be not larger than those observed in Western countries such as European countries (low- vs high-education: more than 60% higher in all Baltic/Central/Eastern European countries in men, and at least 40% higher in Northern European countries),7 the US (low- vs high education: 151% higher for men and 92% higher for women)8 and Australia (RII: 150% higher for men and 73% higher for women).9 The smaller educational inequalities in cancer mortality observed in the present study may be attributable, at least partly, to Japan’s affordable universal healthcare and widespread hygienic practices.

Income is strongly associated with educational level and considered a more direct indicator of an individual’s living conditions and the resources available to maintain health and access medical care (eg, time availability for cancer screening or copayment for expensive cancer treatment) when examining socioeconomic status. Particularly among the older individuals, such material resources may be more associated with cancer mortality.39 Nevertheless, incorporating income data into mortality analyses has been achieved in only a few countries, such as Italy and Norway,39 40 and remains highly challenging in many countries. Therefore, establishing a comprehensive system for monitoring health inequalities—including income—while ensuring robust data protection and privacy safeguards is critical. Such future efforts will enable a more precise understanding of socioeconomic inequalities in mortality and can substantially strengthen the evidence base for designing effective and equitable health policies.

This study has several limitations. First, the census-linked cancer mortality rates for the non-institutionalised individuals were approximately 5% lower than those from the complete mortality registry without linkage for all-cancer ASMR (online supplemental table 1).20 For example, when an “address change” or “marital status change” occurred after the 2020 census, subsequent linkage would no longer be possible and should be treated as censoring. However, this study did not account for such censoring, which may have led to an underestimation of mortality rates. Second, our results were restricted to non-institutionalised Japanese individuals aged 25–84 years recorded in the 2020 census; therefore, our findings should be cautiously interpreted.20 Because non-institutionalised individuals are assumed to have a lower mortality risk compared with the complete mortality registry, which includes institutionalised individuals, this may also contribute to an underestimation of mortality rates. Third, we discussed educational inequalities in mortality, but inequalities in incidence and survival rates were not considered in this study. Fourth, the proportion of individuals with an ‘unknown’ educational level was 11.2% among men and 10.9% among women. The ASMRs of the ‘unknown’ educational level were calculated as an independent category from educational level; however, the results were nearly identical to those of the low-education group. Therefore, we may slightly overestimate mortality inequalities but consider that excluding this category did not affect our RII and SII results, as well as the previous study.21 Fifth, individuals with a higher educational level were more likely to change their residential address after the census.23 This frequent mobility may have led to the underestimation of mortality rates in this group, thereby overestimating mortality inequalities.22 The magnitude of this bias could be quantified if linkage-rate estimators stratified by educational level were available; however, this is infeasible because death records lack information on education. Because changes in residential address are generally less frequent among the older individuals (high mortality generations),22 23 the bias was unlikely to be severe. Finally, false data linkage (ie, overestimation of mortality) may have occurred due to accidental matches of the linkage key, arising from misreported census information—such as incorrect reporting of birth year or month—or changes in residential address or marital status after the census. However, such false data linkages are expected to occur randomly and not be associated with educational level; therefore, the magnitude of bias in the estimate of educational inequalities is likely to be limited.

Conclusions

In Japan, individuals with low education levels experienced higher cancer mortality across many cancer sites than those with high education levels. Lung, stomach, colorectal, liver, gallbladder and bile duct and cervix uteri cancers were the major contributors, contributing 70%–80% of educational inequalities in cancer mortality for the Japanese populations. Equity-focused strategies to enhance primary and secondary prevention could help reduce inequalities.
