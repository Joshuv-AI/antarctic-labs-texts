RECQL1 as a potential therapeutic target for PARP inhibitor-resistant ovarian cancer
Authors: Hiroyuki Yoshida, Jiarui Li, Hiroaki Inui, Kyuji Rokugawa, Keiichi Fujiwara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOvercoming resistance to poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors is an urgent challenge for ovarian cancer treatment. Here, we investigated RECQL1, a RecQ helicase involved in homologous recombination repair, as a potential target for overcoming PARP inhibitor resistance in this disease. Patients with platinum-sensitive recurrent ovarian cancer treated with the PARP inhibitor olaparib between March 2018 and December 2021 were included. Immunohistochemistry assays were conducted to assess RECQL1 protein expression patterns in surgically resected ovarian cancer tissues. The effect of RECQL1 knockdown on olaparib resistance was evaluated in vitro using ovarian cancer cells transfected with an anti-RECQL1 siRNA. Among 44 patients, those with no response to olaparib had significantly higher RECQL1 expression levels compared with responders (P = 0.020). Kaplan–Meier curves showed significantly shorter overall survival in the high RECQL1 expression group than in the low expression group (median, 26.0 months vs. not reached; P = 0.027). Multivariate analysis revealed high RECQL1 expression to be a significant prognostic factor for shorter overall survival (P = 0.036). RECQL1 knockdown significantly enhanced the sensitivity to olaparib in two ovarian cancer cell lines. Overall, RECQL1 plays a critical role in PARP inhibitor resistance and is a promising therapeutic target to improve ovarian cancer patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21844-3.Keywords: PARP inhibitor, Ovarian cancer, Homologous recombination repair, RECQL1Subject terms: Ovarian cancer, Chemotherapy

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is the most prognostically unfavorable disease among gynecological cancers1. Because of the lack of an established effective screening method, many ovarian cancer patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage2. The general treatment method for advanced ovarian cancer involves surgery followed by platinum-based combination chemotherapy3,4. Although approximately 70% of ovarian cancer cases respond to initial treatment, many of these patients relapse and develop resistance to platinum agents5–9.

The treatment landscape for advanced ovarian cancer has significantly changed with the advent of poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitor therapy. PARP inhibitors have been shown to be an effective maintenance therapy in both primary and recurrent treatment settings for advanced ovarian cancer and have become widely used in daily clinical practice10–13. However, while PARP inhibitors are highly effective against ovarian cancer with homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), their efficacy is limited in ovarian cancer cases with homologous recombination proficiency (HRP)14. Furthermore, many ovarian cancer cases that are initially sensitive to PARP inhibitors often develop resistance over time15,16. Overcoming PARP inhibitor resistance is strongly desired in clinical practice to further improve ovarian cancer patient prognosis.

Multiple mechanisms contribute to the acquisition of PARP inhibitor resistance in ovarian cancer, with one of the major ones being the recovery of homologous recombination repair (HRR) capacity in cancer cells. This occurs when reversion mutations restore the functions of HRR-associated genes that had previously been rendered dysfunctional from genetic mutations17. Additionally, the loss of methylation in the promoter regions of homologous recombination-related genes can also lead to a restored HRR capability18,19. Other reported mechanisms for PARP inhibitor resistance development include the stabilization of replication forks observed at sites of DNA replication20, increased PARP enzymatic activity from PARP phosphorylation21, and enhanced drug efflux15,22,23. Despite these advances in understanding the mechanisms of PARP inhibitor resistance, a definitive strategy to overcome this resistance has not yet been established, posing a significant clinical challenge.

DNA helicases are enzymes that unwind double-stranded DNA into single strands, playing crucial roles in DNA replication, transcription, and repair, thus contributing to the maintenance of genomic stability24. Among the DNA helicases, the RecQ helicase family has five homologs in humans, each known to have various functions related to genome stability25. Genetic deficiencies in the functions of BLM, WRN, and RECQL4, members of the RecQ helicase family, result in hereditary disorders characterized by a high cancer incidence26. Another member of the RecQ helicase family, RECQL1, is reportedly involved in both tumor development and chemotherapy resistance in various cancer types27. For example, RECQL1 expression has been linked to increased cell proliferation and poor patient prognosis in ovarian cancer28,29. Studies have also suggested that RECQL1 contributes to cell proliferation and chemotherapy resistance in breast cancer and glioblastoma30,31.

RECQL1 plays a crucial role in HRR by acting as a helicase that unwinds Holliday junctions, which are intermediates of the HRR process32. Additionally, RECQL1 is an essential factor for the protection of replication forks33, which is necessary to prevent replication fork instability that can induce double-strand DNA breaks. Furthermore, RECQL1 interacts with PARP through its C-terminal domain and has been reported to be a critical factor in PARP enzymatic activity34.

RECQL1 is involved in three different factors related to PARP inhibitor resistance: HRR, replication fork stabilization, and enhanced PARP enzymatic activity. Thus, RECQL1 is expected to play a significant role in PARP inhibitor resistance. In fact, previous work has suggested that inhibiting RECQL1 could increase the sensitivity of multiple myeloma cells to PARP inhibitors35. However, few reports have explored the relationship between PARP inhibitor resistance and RECQL1 in other cancer types. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to elucidate the role of RECQL1 in the acquisition of PARP inhibitor resistance in ovarian cancer and to investigate if RECQL1 is a potential therapeutic target for overcoming such resistance in this disease.

Results

Patient inclusion and characteristics

From March 2018 to December 2021, olaparib was administered to 46 epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) patients diagnosed with platinum-sensitive recurrence. In this study, one patient did not meet the eligibility criteria and another patient was excluded because of insufficient clinical data. As a result, 44 patients were deemed eligible and included in the study (Fig. 1). The median follow-up period was 23.5 months (interquartile range = 19.0–34.5 months). The detailed characteristics of the patients are shown in Table 1. The mean age of the patients was 62.2 ± 11.1 years, with a mean body weight of 54.8 ± 10.9 kg. Among the patients, 84.1% had stage III or IV disease and 72.7% had high-grade serous carcinoma of the ovary. Additionally, 11.4% of the patients had BRCA mutations, while the remaining patients either had no mutations or their mutation status was unknown.

Patient characteristics and response to olaparib

The 44 patients were classified into responders and non-responders to olaparib as described in Materials and Methods. As a result, 16 patients were categorized as responders and 13 as non-responders. The remaining 15 patients were not classified into either group. The clinical characteristics of these patients are detailed in Table 2. No statistically significant differences were found between the responders and non-responders regarding age, body weight, stage, histological type, or response to most recent platinum-based therapy. A large proportion of the patients had an unknown BRCA status, which limited evaluation. However, there was a tendency for responders to exhibit BRCA mutations.

Among the responders, 50% exhibited high RECQL1 expression levels, whereas 92.3% of the non-responders demonstrated high RECQL1 expression patterns. Survival and prognostic analyses

Next, we examined the association between RECQL1 expression and patient survival using the log-rank test. For progression-free survival (PFS), Kaplan–Meier curves indicated a trend towards shorter survival in the high RECQL1 expression group compared with that in the low expression group, although the difference was not statistically significant (median, 9.0 months vs. 19.0 months; P = 0.074; Fig. 3a). However, overall survival (OS) was significantly shorter in the high RECQL1 expression group than in the low expression group (median, 26.0 months vs. not reached; P = 0.027; Fig. 3b). Additionally, we conducted a multivariate analysis of survival using a Cox proportional hazards model adjusted for potential confounders. As shown in Table 4, high RECQL1 expression was a significant prognostic factor for shorter OS (hazard ratio [HR]: 3.61; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.08–16.7; P = 0.036). Although high RECQL1 expression also tended to be associated with worse PFS, this trend did not reach statistical significance (HR: 1.93; 95% CI: 0.91–4.47; P = 0.09).

Inhibition of RECQL1 enhances the olaparib sensitivity of ovarian cancer cells

We investigated the RECQL1 protein expression patterns in two ovarian cancer cell lines, ES-2 and SKOV-3, using western blot analysis. RECQL1 protein was expressed in both cell lines (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. S1). We then transfected a small interfering RNA targeting RECQL1 mRNA (RECQL1-siRNA) into both ovarian cancer cell lines and assessed the suppression level 24 h post-transfection by western blot analysis. Compared with those in the mock-transfected and negative control-siRNA-transfected cells, RECQL1 protein expression levels were reduced in the RECQL1-siRNA transfected cells (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. S1). Thus, the RECQL1-siRNA used in this study could effectively knock down RECQL1 expression in ES-2 and SKOV-3 cells. Next, we evaluated if RECQL1 suppression could alter the sensitivity of ovarian cancer cells to olaparib. We found that siRNA-mediated knockdown of RECQL1 expression in ES-2 and SKOV-3 cells markedly increased their sensitivity to olaparib, demonstrating a significant enhancement compared with mock-transfected and negative control-siRNA-transfected cells (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this study, we found that ovarian cancer patients with high RECQL1 expression patterns displayed reduced olaparib effectiveness and a poorer prognosis. Furthermore, knocking down RECQL1 expression in ovarian cancer cell lines increased their sensitivity to olaparib. These results suggested that RECQL1 expression plays a critical role in olaparib resistance in ovarian cancer, implicating RECQL1 as a potential therapeutic target for overcoming such drug resistance in this disease.

The introduction of PARP inhibitors has significantly improved ovarian cancer patient prognosis. However, PARP inhibitors are less effective against ovarian cancers that do not exhibit HRR deficiencies. Additionally, even when initial responses to PARP inhibitor treatment are observed, many tumors eventually acquire resistance, rendering the treatment ineffective. Overcoming ovarian cancer resistance to PARP inhibitors remains an urgent challenge.

One of the primary mechanisms by which ovarian cancer cells can acquire PARP inhibitor resistance is through the restoration of HRR function in cells that were previously deficient in this process17–19. In addition, other mechanisms of acquired PARP inhibitor resistance have been reported, such as replication fork stabilization at DNA replication sites20, increased PARP enzymatic activity through phosphorylation21, and enhanced efflux of PARP inhibitors from the cells15,22,23. These findings suggest that multiple mechanisms contribute to the acquisition of PARP inhibitor resistance.

Many approaches have been explored to overcome resistance to PARP inhibitors, with the main strategy focusing on inducing HRD in ovarian cancer tumors with HRP. For example, angiogenesis inhibitors are known to induce hypoxic conditions in cancer cells, which in turn suppress the expression of HRR-related factors, leading to HRD induction36. From this, it was anticipated that the combination of angiogenesis inhibitors and PARP inhibitors could also be effective in HRP ovarian cancer cases. Clinical trials were conducted to evaluate the efficacy of a combined therapy with the angiogenesis inhibitor bevacizumab and the PARP inhibitor olaparib in ovarian cancer. While the combination therapy showed significant efficacy in HRD ovarian cancer patients, it did not demonstrate the anticipated effectiveness in HRP patients37. Similarly, therapeutic approaches combining PARP inhibitors with histone deacetylase inhibitors or ataxia telangiectasia and Rad3-related protein inhibitors have been investigated to induce HRD in ovarian cancer cases with HRP, demonstrating certain efficacy in preclinical studies38,39.

Another approach that aimed to overcome PARP inhibitor resistance was including Wee1 inhibitors. Wee1 plays crucial roles in cell cycle regulation and DNA replication, causing its inhibition to trigger DNA damage during the S-phase40. One report indicated that the combination of Wee1 inhibitors with olaparib demonstrated efficacy in ovarian cancer patients with PARP inhibitor resistance, regardless of their BRCA gene mutation status41. Furthermore, studies targeting checkpoint kinase 1 (CHK1) have also been conducted, with reports showing that a combination of CHK1 inhibitor prexasertib and olaparib resulted in partial responses in four of 18 ovarian cancer patients with PARP inhibitor resistance42.

An alternative strategy for counteracting this drug resistance involves preventing the efflux of PARP inhibitors from cells. Overexpression of the drug efflux protein p-glycoprotein has been identified as a cause of PARP inhibitor resistance. Efforts to overcome this resistance have focused on using PARP inhibitors that are not substrates of p-glycoprotein. For instance, pamiparib is not a p-glycoprotein substrate and thus does not result in a reduced intracellular drug concentration, potentially avoiding drug efflux-related resistance43.

Despite these various efforts to overcome PARP inhibitor resistance, a definitive solution has yet to be found. One of the main challenges associated with overcoming PARP inhibitor resistance in ovarian cancer is the high degree of intratumoral heterogeneity, where multiple resistance mechanisms may operate within a single tumor. Therefore, addressing PARP inhibitor resistance may require simultaneously targeting multiple resistance pathways.

RECQL1, a member of the DNA helicase family, plays a critical role in maintaining genomic stability and has also been implicated in chemotherapy resistance in various cancers27. For instance, RECQL1 has been reported to contribute to platinum resistance in ovarian cancer29. In addition, it also plays a significant role in doxorubicin resistance in breast cancer30. Since RECQL1 is involved in mechanisms related to PARP inhibitor resistance—such as HRR, replication fork protection, and enhanced PARP enzymatic activity—it is likely that RECQL1 contributes to the acquisition of PARP inhibitor resistance in cancer cells. This makes RECQL1 a particularly promising target in efforts to overcome PARP inhibitor resistance. Indeed, Viziteu et al.35 reported that multiple myeloma cells displayed increased sensitivity to PARP inhibitors after inhibiting RECQL1. They also showed an association between high RECQL1 expression levels and poor multiple myeloma patient prognosis. Specifically, multiple myeloma cells expressing RECQL1 developed resistance to chemotherapeutic agents like melphalan, whereas suppression of RECQL1 expression led to increased DNA double-strand breaks and enhanced sensitivity to PARP inhibitors. However, no reports have examined the association between PARP inhibitor sensitivity and RECQL1 expression patterns in other cancer types, such as ovarian cancer. In the current study, we demonstrated that using a specific siRNA to knock down RECQL1 expression in ovarian cancer cells could enhance their sensitivity to PARP inhibitors, which was consistent with the findings reported by Viziteu et al. However, unlike the previous study, we showed that high RECQL1 expression was associated with reduced therapeutic efficacy in patients who had received PARP inhibitor therapy. This allowed us to demonstrate the relationship between RECQL1 expression patterns and PARP inhibitor sensitivity at the patient level.

This study has several limitations. First, the examined ovarian cancer patient sample size was relatively small, which may have reduced the statistical power of the findings. Second, the analysis of ovarian cancer patient data was conducted retrospectively. However, our in vitro findings in ovarian cancer cell lines corroborated the results obtained from the patient samples, supporting the association between RECQL1 expression and olaparib resistance. Third, many of the included ovarian cancer patients had an unknown BRCA status. The lack of this information limited our ability to identify novel biomarkers. Although a large portion of the patients in this study had not undergone BRCA testing, most patients now routinely receive this test. Further investigations of this are planned.

In conclusion, this study suggests that RECQL1 may play a role in PARP inhibitor resistance in ovarian cancer. Resistance to PARP inhibitors poses a significant challenge in treating ovarian cancer, with a current lack of effective strategies to overcome such resistance. The DNA helicase RECQL1 is involved in multiple PARP inhibitor resistance mechanisms beyond HRR pathways, suggesting that RECQL1 could serve as a promising target for overcoming PARP inhibitor resistance in ovarian cancer. Future prospective studies are warranted.

Materials and methods

Patients

This single-institution, retrospective cohort study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Saitama Medical University International Medical Center (Approval Number: 2022-112). Because of the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee in accordance with the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects issued by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients with histologically confirmed EOC, fallopian tube cancer, or primary peritoneal cancer who were treated with olaparib at our institution between March 2018 and December 2021 were included in the study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients aged 18 years or older at the time of diagnosis and (2) patients with platinum-sensitive recurrent EOC, fallopian tube cancer, or primary peritoneal cancer. Platinum-sensitive recurrence was defined as ≥ 6 months progression-free interval from completion of prior platinum-based therapy. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with malignancies other than EOC, (2) patients who received olaparib in combination with other anticancer agents, and (3) patients with insufficient clinical data for analysis.

Olaparib was administered orally at a dose of 600 mg per day. Treatment was continued until disease progression or until it became difficult to continue treatment because of toxicity. To evaluate the treatment response to olaparib, patients who continued treatment for at least one year, including any periods of interruption, were defined as responders. In contrast, patients whose treatment was discontinued because of disease progression within six months were defined as non-responders44,45. PFS was defined as the time from the initiation of olaparib treatment to the first documented progression or death from any cause. For patients without documented progression, survivors were censored at the time of the last follow-up, while those who died without progression were censored at the date of death. OS was defined as the time from the initiation of olaparib treatment to death from any cause. Survivors were censored at the time of the last follow-up. The follow-up period ended on December 31, 2022.

Immunohistochemistry

Ovarian cancer tissues were fixed in 10% buffered formalin and embedded in paraffin. Five-micrometer sections were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated in a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed using Target Retrieval Solution (K8004; DAKO, Kyoto, Japan) at 95 °C for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating the sections with 0.3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min at room temperature. Subsequently, IHC staining was performed with a primary anti-RECQL1 antibody (Bethyl Laboratories, Montgomery, TX, USA, catalog no. IHC-00200) diluted 1:400 according to the DAKO Envision protocol (DAKO). The slides were visualized using 3,3'-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride and hematoxylin. Positive and negative controls were used in all experiments. The positive control was a liver tissue with known RECQL1 expression, while the negative control was an ovarian cancer tissue without application of the primary antibody. The quantitative analysis of RECQL1 expression was performed using the following method. The percentage of tumor cells with positive RECQL1 IHC staining was measured in three different fields at 100 × magnification and given one of the following scores: 0, no immunostaining; 1, 1%–25% positive cells; 2, 26%–50% positive cells; 3, 51%–75% positive cells; 4, 76%–100% positive cells. The intensity score of RECQL1 IHC staining was evaluated as follows: 0, no staining; 1, weak; 2, moderate; 3, strong. The expression score for each specimen (score of 0–12) was calculated by multiplying the score for the percentage of positively stained tumor cells by the staining intensity score. Two independent researchers, blinded to patient clinical data, performed the scoring. Using the RECQL1 expression score, patients were divided into two groups: low expression (an expression score ≤ 8) and high expression (an expression score > 8). The cutoff value of 8 was determined based on the median expression score in the cohort.

Cell culture

Two human ovarian cancer cell lines, ES-2 and SKOV-3, were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). The cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5A modified medium (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, CELLect, MP Biomedicals, Solon, OH, USA, catalogue no. 2917354H). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Before being used in experiments, the cells were passaged at least once after thawing from frozen stocks to ensure consistent growth conditions and viability.

Transfection of chemically synthesized siRNA

An anti-RECQL1 siRNA, named QL201-16, was used to knock down RECQL1 expression (chemically synthesized by Biopharma, Ajinomoto, Tokyo, Japan). The guide strand sequence of RECQL1-siRNA was 5’-UGAAGAAUCAUAUUCAUUGCU-3' and the passenger strand sequence was 5’-CAAUGAAUAUGAUUCUUCACC-3'. We used Firefly Luciferase siRNA named GL3 as negative control-siRNA (chemically synthesized by Biopharma, Ajinomoto). The guide strand sequence of GL3 siRNA was 5’-CUUACGCUGAGUACUUCGA-3’ and the passenger strand sequence was 5’-UCGAAGUACUCAGCGUAAG-3’. Cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well in a 6-well culture plate (TrueLine, Tokyo, Japan) with 500 µL of McCoy’s 5A modified medium containing 10% FBS, then incubated for 24 h until they reached approximately 20% confluence. The siRNA transfections were then performed. A mixture of 50 µL of Opti-MEM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 1.5 µL of Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen), and siRNA were incubated at room temperature for 5 min, then added to the appropriate wells. Twenty-four hours after transfection, the cells were collected and cell lysates were prepared for western blot analysis to examine RECQL1 protein expression levels, as described below. As controls, ovarian cancer cells were transfected with negative control-siRNA or treated with transfection reagent without siRNA (mock-transfected).

Western blot analysis

Equal amounts of protein from the cell lysates were separated using SDS-PAGE. The separated proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane (ATTO, Tokyo, Japan). The membrane was then probed with a monoclonal anti-RECQL1 antibody (1 mg/mL; Q1N3, Kanagawa, Japan; diluted 1:10,000) and a polyclonal anti-β-actin antibody (533 μg/mL; Proteintech, Tokyo, Japan; diluted 1:5,000) as the loading control. For the anti-RECQL1 antibody, an ECL anti-mouse IgG horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-linked secondary antibody (GE Healthcare, Tokyo, Japan) was used at a 1:10,000 dilution. For the anti-β-actin antibody, an HRP-conjugated Affinipure goat anti-rabbit IgG (Proteintech) was used at a 1:10,000 dilution. To confirm the results, the membrane was stained using EzWestBlue W (ATTO).

Chemosensitivity assay

The olaparib (AZD2281, Selleckchem, Kanagawa, Japan) stock solution was prepared by dissolving it in DMSO at a concentration of 10 nmol/L and stored at -30°C. The cell sensitivity to olaparib was assessed using the WST-8 assay (Nacalai, Osaka, Japan). Initially, the cells were treated with 5 nM of RECQL1-siRNA for 24 h. Mock-transfected and negative control-siRNA-transfected cells were used as controls. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to a series of olaparib concentrations (5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 μM) for 120 h. Following the exposure, the medium was replaced with 20 μL of WST-8 reagent, then the cells were incubated for an additional 1 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Wallac 1420 ARVOsx microplate reader (PerkinElmer, Kanagawa, Japan). Dose–response curves were plotted as the percentage of viable cells relative to untreated control cells. The experiments were performed in triplicate for each cell line.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation or as the median (interquartile range [IQR]). The clinical background of the patients and RECQL1 expression patterns were compared between the responder group and non-responder group. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. PFS and OS curves were constructed using the Kaplan–Meier method. Comparisons of PFS and OS between the groups were analyzed using the log-rank test. Multivariate analysis of PFS and OS was conducted using a Cox proportional hazards model, with the results presented as HR with 95% CI. The model was planned to be adjusted for age (< 65 or ≥ 65 years), stage (I/II or III/IV), histological type (serous or others), previous chemotherapy regimens (≤ 2 or ≥ 3), and response to the most recent platinum-based chemotherapy (complete response [CR] or partial response [PR]). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. As this was a retrospective study, no a priori sample size or power calculation was performed. The study included all eligible patients who met the inclusion criteria and were treated at our institution during the study period. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro 13 software (JMP Pro 13, SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).
