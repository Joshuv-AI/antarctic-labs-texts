Simultaneous Dehalogenation and Hydrogenation in Sonogashira Coupling
Authors: Huafeng Chen, Lei Zhai, Yulan Zuo, Xi Qin, Jinling Zhang, Kui Tian, Peng Xu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
We herein report a simultaneous dehalogenation and hydrogenation (DHH) reaction in Sonogashira coupling involving hexahalogenobenzene C6X6 (X = I, Br), in which a halogen on the aryl polyhalide substrate was substituted by a hydrogen atom. First, a steric bulky terminal alkyne 5 was designed and synthesized to study the influence of the reaction conditions (e.g., catalyst, solvent, temperature) and the halogens on the substrates C6X6 on the DHH reaction. Moreover, based on the optimized conditions, a terminal alkyne 6 with less steric hindrance was further synthesized to investigate the influence of alkyne on the DHH reaction. As a result, two aromatic polyynes 7 and 8 were obtained in the DHH process, and their photophysical properties were further studied and compared. The influence produced by the alkynes and aryl polyhalides substrate provide insights into Sonogashira coupling involving terminal alkyne with huge steric hindrance and polyhalogenated aromatic hydrocarbons.

Introduction

In the past few decades, a large number of transition-metal-catalyzed carbon–carbon cross-coupling reactions have been developed (e.g., Suzuki–Miyaura coupling,1 Kumada coupling,2 Negishi coupling,3 and Stille coupling4), which have been widely used in the synthesis of drugs, natural products, and functional materials.5,6 Since its discovery in 1975, Sonogashira cross-coupling is widely known due to the mild reaction conditions, excellent functional group tolerance, and ease of large-scale synthesis, which has exhibited remarkable advantages and huge potential in the construction of sp–sp2 carbon–carbon bonds7−9 and sp–sp3 carbon–carbon bonds.10−12

Based on Sonogashira coupling, numerous interesting alkynyl functional molecules have been synthesized, such as graphyne,13,14 dehydrophenylannulene,15−19 etc. Benefiting from Sonogashira coupling, we recently synthesized several curved π-conjugated helical polyynes and reported their intriguing photophysical properties.20 At present, there are various types of Sonogashira coupling that have been developed, such as asymmetric Sonogashira coupling,10−12 photocatalytic Sonogashira coupling,21,22 aqueous Sonogashira coupling,23,24 and Cu-free Sonogashira coupling.25,26

Although Sonogashira coupling involving small steric hindrance substrates often proceeds smoothly, it is difficult when steric hindrance from aryl/alkenyl halides and/or terminal alkyne is bulky.27,28 Accordingly, a common solution is to increase the reaction temperature.29 However, higher reaction temperatures may result more side reactions, such as the self-coupling of terminal alkyne substrates (i.e., Glaser coupling).30 Besides, when polyhalogenated aromatic hydrocarbons (PHAHs) serve as substrates, the simultaneous dehalogenation and hydrogenation (DHH) process cannot be ignored, in which one or more halogens on PHAHs are substituted by hydrogens. Neenan et al. reported the Sonogashira coupling of hexaiodobenzene 1a with trimethylsilyacetylene (TMSA) that afforded DHH product 2 (Scheme 1).31 Subsequently, when hexabromobenzene 1b with lower reactivity than 1a was used, the DHH reaction still existed and gave pentayne 4.32

Noticeably, several DHH products have been reported in the Sonogashira reaction, however, the effects of catalysts, solvents, temperature, and steric hindrance of alkynyl groups on the DHH reaction are still unclear. On the basis of the above analysis, two terminal alkynes 5 and 6 were designed and synthesized to study the DHH reaction in Sonogashira coupling when hexaiodobenzene 1a or hexabromobenzene 1b was used as the polyhalide substrate (Scheme 2). After the structures of the synthetic intermediates and resulting products were characterized, the photophysical properties of the two congested aromatic polyynes 7 and 8 obtained in the DHH reaction were further investigated.

Results and Discussion

To achieve the above purpose, we attempted to synthesize terminal alkyne 5 (Scheme 3). Initially, in the transformation of triazene 9 to diyne 10 utilizing Sonogashira coupling, a significant solvent effect was observed. Dialkynylation of triazene 9 was first conducted in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF). However, even under rigorously deoxygenated conditions and various temperatures, ranging from room temperature to 80 °C, no diyne 10 was detected after substrate 9 was completely consumed, which yielded an inseparable mixture. While the solvent DMF was replaced by tetrahydrofuran (THF), the reaction proceeded smoothly at room temperature and afforded diyne 10 in an excellent yield. After that, diyne 10 was converted into aryl iodide 11 by treating with iodomethane at 120 °C, which was further transformed into the trialkyne 12 through Sonogashira coupling. The terminal alkyne 5 was then successfully obtained in 95% isolated yield by treatment of potassium carbonate in CH3OH/THF under mild condition.

With the terminal alkyne 5 in hand, three frequently used catalysts (Pd(PPh3)4, Pd2(dba)3, and Pd(PPh3)2Cl2) in Sonogashira coupling were screened to investigate the influence of PdLn catalyst on the DHH reaction. As can be seen from Table 1, in comparison to Pd2(dba)3 and Pd(PPh3)2Cl2, the DHH reaction catalyzed by Pd(PPh3)4 afforded a relatively high yield of polyyne 7 in any of the three solvents N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP), THF, and acetonitrile (ACN). In addition, among the three solvents, the highest yield of 13% was obtained in ACN (entry 7, Table 1). The above observation indicated that Pd(PPh3)4 in ACN could lead to more reductive dehalogenation product 7 in Sonogashira coupling.

The chemical structure of polyyne 7 was characterized by standard spectroscopic techniques. As shown in Figures 1a and S1 (Supporting Information), the isotopic mass peak pattern of quasi-molecular ions [M + H]+ and [M + NH4]+ of polyyne 7 obtained by high-resolution atmospheric pressure chemical ionization Orbitrap mass spectrometry (HR-APCI-Orbitrap-MS) were in good agreement with the simulated values. In the 1H NMR spectrum, a characteristic 1H resonance at 7.83 ppm (i.e., signal a, Figure 1b) with a corresponding integral value is consistent with the proton on the central benzene ring. Moreover, other three aryl 1H resonances was observed from 7.31 to 7.35 ppm (i.e., signals b, c, and d), which could be assigned to the ten protons on the peripheral five benzene rings. After confirming the structure of reductive dehalogenation product 7, the influence of reaction temperature was further investigated. As can be seen from Table 2, when Sonogashira coupling of hexaiodobenzene 1a with an excess of terminal alkyne 5 was conducted at relatively low reaction temperature, the DHH reaction was not obvious (e.g., entries 1 and 2). However, after increasing the reaction temperature to 100 °C (see entry 3 in Table 2), more polyyne 7 was obtained, which indicated a significant DHH process occurred. However, with a further increase of the reaction temperature from 110 to 120 °C, the isolated yields of polyyne 7 showed a decreasing trend, which could be ascribed to the low stability of terminal alkyne 5 and/or related reaction intermediates at high temperature (entries 4 and 5).

Then, we further examined the steric hindrance from substrate terminal alkyne on the DHH reaction (Scheme 4). Initially, through Sonogashira coupling of compound 13 with 2-methylbut-3-yn-2-ol and the subsequent desilylation reaction of TMS of compound 14, terminal alkyne 6 was successfully synthesized. Although, in the subsequent Sonogashira coupling of terminal alkyne 6 with hexabromobenzene 1b under the optimized conditions, the reductive dehalogenation product 15 was not directly observed but yielded an inseparable mixture. However, after being treated with NaH, the above mixture afforded polyyne 8, which indirectly proved that the DHH reaction occurred in the reaction of terminal alkyne 6 with hexabromobenzene 1b.

Finally, referring to the relevant reports,33 a possible catalytic cycle in the DHH process was proposed (Scheme 5). Initially, Sonogashira coupling of 1a/1b with terminal alkyne 5 or 6 afforded halogenated aryl polyynes 16 or 17. Subsequently, oxidative addition of Pd(PPh3)4 to halides 16 or 17 in situ produced the ionic species 18. Obviously, the steric congestion around the halogen on 16 or 17 is beneficial for the formation of 18, rather than the formation of a neutral intermediate. Then, coordination of the positive ion of 18 with TEA, deprotonation of 19, and β-hydrogen elimination of 20 successively, afforded 22. Finally, the DHH products 7 or 15 were obtained by reductive elimination of 22.

Photophysical Properties

We further performed and compared the ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) electronic absorption and fluorescence emission spectra of polyynes 7 and 8 in ACN at 25 °C. As can be seen from Figure 2a and Table 4, the UV–vis absorption spectra of 7 with a concentration of 1 × 10–5 M exhibited an intense absorption band in the range of ∼305–425 nm maximized at 359 nm (ε = 79400 dm3 mol–1 cm–1) with a shoulder maximum at ∼403 nm (ε = 28600 dm3 mol–1 cm–1). Similar characteristic absorption were found in the UV–vis absorption spectra of polyyne 8, in which the maximal absorption were located at 340 nm (ε = 42000 dm3 mol–1 cm–1). Compared with polyyne 7, however, blue shift ∼20 nm and relatively weakened absorption were observed in the UV–vis absorption spectra of polyyne 8, which could be ascribed to the less alkynyl groups conjugated with the five peripheral benzene ring moieties.

Excitation of polyyne 7 at 360 nm in ACN with a concentration of 1 × 10–6 M leads to an emission spectrum in the range of ∼395–550 nm maximized at 431 nm with a shoulder maximized at 451 nm (Figure 2a and Table 4). Similarly, emission maximized at 428 nm was observed in the fluorescence spectra of polyyne 8 (Figure 2b and Table 4). Intriguingly, compared to polyyne 7, polyyne 8 exhibited obvious emission enhancement. The observed emission enhancement of polyyne 8 could be attributed to the less alkynyl substituents and thus smaller steric hindrance in the structure, which reduced the structural deformation and configuration flips of the molecules, and thus inhibited the nonradiative transition of the excited state.34,35

Conclusions

In summary, we have disclosed a simultaneous dehalogenation and hydrogenation process in Sonogashira coupling involving hexaiodobenzene and hexabromobenzene. Furthermore, the influence of catalyst, solvent, temperature, and halogens on the hexahalogenobenzene substrates has been studied. The synthetic methodology was successfully applied to afford two polyynes 7 and 8 with huge steric hindrance, and the corresponding structures were confirmed by MS, 1H NMR, and 13C NMR, etc. After that, electronic absorption and luminescent emissions properties of polyynes 7 and 8 were further investigated and compared, in which blue shift, weakened absorption, and enhanced emission were observed in the UV–vis spectrum of less steric crowding polyyne 8 compared with polyyne 7. In brief, this study presented new insights into Sonogashira coupling involving steric bulky terminal alkyne and aryl polyhalides.
