Pioneering the future: nanotechnology’s role in angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma
Authors: Hafiz Mustafa Anjum, Jawad Ahmad, Muhammad Talha, Laiba Shamim, Sakan Binte Imran
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

Angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma (AITL) is an infrequent subtype of peripheral T-cell lymphoma, which in turn is a type of non-Hodgkin lymphoma. The AITL represents only 1–2% of all cases of non-Hodgkin lymphoma. AITL affects individuals of advanced age, with a mean age of 65 years, who are diagnosed with the ailment. The most common physical findings and complaints include B symptoms (fever, unintentional weight loss, and night sweats) and lymphadenopathy. Bone marrow involvement and hepatosplenomegaly are also seen at diagnosis. Skin manifestations observed range from urticarial lesions to nodular tumors. Extranodal involvement is rare. Historically acknowledged other names of AITL in literature include angioimmunoblastic lymphadenopathy with dysproteinemia, immunoblastic lymphadenopathy, and lymphogranulomatosis X[1]. AITL tumor cells have a follicular T-helper cell phenotype[2]. This article is in line with the TITAN Guidelines on the need for transparency in AI use in healthcare[3].

Recent advancements in nanotechnology have the potential for detecting circulating tumor cells, including residual lymphoma cells, in the management of AITL. Among the nanomaterials developed so far are gold, magnetic material, and silica. These nanomaterials can be operationalized with biomolecules such as antibodies, aptamers, or peptides and can be utilized for the isolation of residual circulating cells. This nanoparticle (NP) approach towards detecting neoplastic cells could improve the efficacy in hindering metastasis and relapse of the symptoms. The recurrence risks after isolating residual cells are reduced as compared to invasive methods[4].

Recent clinical studies have demonstrated the diagnostic potential of NPs in the field of oncology. In a randomized controlled trial involving patients with colorectal carcinoma, the mean number of lymph nodes detected per patient was significantly higher in the ACNs-stained (activated carbon NPs) group (26.8 ± 8.4) compared to the non-stained control group (12.2 ± 3.2). This difference was statistically significant, with a P-value of less than 0.001, indicating that ACN staining markedly enhances lymph node detection in colorectal cancer patients[5]. These findings are supported by a broader view highlighting the role of nanotechnology in lymphoma detection and its treatment[6].

Despite the growing potential of NPs in cancer diagnostics, their clinical application is still limited by several key challenges. These include unresolved issues of biocompatibility and long-term safety, as well as suboptimal pharmacokinetics and biodistribution. Achieving high specificity toward target cancer cells is complicated by tumor heterogeneity and off-target effects. Moreover, the effectiveness of NP-mediated delivery is hindered by slow cellular uptake, inconsistent intracellular distribution, and rapid clearance by immune cells[7]. Despite these challenges, NPs exhibit multifunctionality, fewer adverse reactions, and superior diagnostic or curative effects compared to free drug delivery.

In conclusion, the diagnostic potential of NPs offers a promising avenue for detecting minimal residual disease in various lymphomas, including AITL. To fully realize its potential, future clinical trials should explore the integration of nanotechnology as a diagnostic tool for MRD assessment in lymphoid malignancies. Moreover, policymakers and clinicians must work together to make these advanced diagnostic tools accessible, helping move toward more personalized and targeted cancer detection.
