Aesthetic Component of the Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need (IOTN): Can large language models match the performance of orthodontists?
Authors: Arda Arısan, Celal Genç, Gökhan Serhat Duran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe assessment of orthodontic treatment needs often involves subjective judgment, particularly when using esthetic indices such as the Aesthetic Component (AC) of the Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need (IOTN). This cross-sectional diagnostic agreement study evaluated whether large language models (LLMs) could provide consistent and reliable IOTN-AC scores comparable to those assigned by expert orthodontists.MethodsThree experienced orthodontists (with 8–15 years of clinical experience) independently scored 147 standardized frontal intraoral photographs using the IOTN-AC at two time points (Time 1 and Time 2). Five LLMs (GPT-4.0, GPT-o3, Claude, Manus, and Grok) were used to evaluate the dataset. Agreement and reliability were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), Pearson and Spearman correlation values, mean absolute error (MAE), and match analyses (exact, near, and group matches).ResultsThe orthodontists showed moderate inter-rater reliability (ICC = 0.649; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.563–0.742). Among the LLMs, GPT-4.0 showed the highest agreement with expert scores (ICC = 0.771; 95% CI, 0.697–0.855), followed by GPT-o3 (ICC = 0.663). GPT-4.0 also yielded the strongest correlation (r = 0.773; P < 0.001) and the lowest MAE (1.09). In the match analyses, GPT-4.0 achieved the highest exact match (28.6%), near-match (47.6%), and group match (66.0%) rates. The remaining models showed lower performance across all metrics.ConclusionsMultimodal LLMs, particularly GPT-4.0, demonstrated substantial agreement with expert orthodontists in IOTN-AC scoring. These findings suggest that LLMs may serve as adjunct tools in assessments for orthodontic treatment needs. However, clinical decision-making should continue to rely on expert judgment.

INTRODUCTION

The Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need (IOTN) is widely used in clinical and public health settings to provide an objective basis for determining orthodontic treatment needs. While the Dental Health Component of the IOTN captures occlusal anomalies, the Aesthetic Component (AC) assesses the patient’s frontal dental appearance and psychosocial impact.1 Baram et al.2 demonstrated that the IOTN-AC was the only index that significantly correlated with indicators of psychosocial distress, including embarrassment and social withdrawal.

However, the AC is inherently subjective, and studies have documented substantial variability in AC assessments within and across professional groups.3,4 Scoring is further influenced by cultural and generational factors,5-8 and residual unconscious biases related to age, sex, or ethnicity may persist even after calibration.9,10 Orthodontists have been shown to assign higher AC scores than general dentists or laypeople when evaluating identical cases.11 Low et al.12 noted that although orthodontists performed better than non-experts in photograph-based assessments, the agreement still varied in relation to the specific dental features evaluated.

Artificial intelligence (AI) models, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have been applied to intraoral photographs to improve diagnostic precision and workflow efficiency. These systems have demonstrated high accuracy in detecting orthodontically relevant features, such as spacing, crowding, and crossbites,13-15 and multiview image integration has yielded further improvements.16-18 AI-based methods have also been applied to other dental imaging tasks, including caries detection, periodontal assessment, and restorative analysis.13,19 Despite these promising results, the internal processes by which CNNs generate predictions are not directly observable to users,20,21 which may limit their clinical acceptance in aesthetic assessment contexts where transparent justification is preferred.22

Large language models (LLMs) represent a newer class of AI systems capable of analyzing visual information alongside natural language. Although initially developed for language-based tasks, multimodal LLMs can interpret images and generate predictions, which are accompanied by brief descriptive outputs.23 In medical imaging, LLMs have shown promise in radiology, pathology, and dermatology.24-27 However, their application in orthodontic aesthetic assessment remains at an early and largely unexplored stage.

This study aimed to evaluate the ability of LLMs to generate consistent and interpretable IOTN-AC scores from standardized intraoral photographs and determine how closely their outputs aligned with expert orthodontist assessments.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical approval and informed consent

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Çanakkale Onsekiz Mart University (approval number: 2025-219). Written informed consent for the use of clinical photographs was obtained from all patients at the time of image acquisition.

Image dataset and standardization

A total of 452 standardized frontal intraoral photographs of patients aged 10–25 years were screened from routine orthodontic records collected between June 2024 and June 2025. All images were captured using a Canon EOS 5D Mark IV DSLR camera with a 100-mm macro lens (Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan) under uniform, shadow-free illumination provided by a dual softbox lighting setup (Elinchrom S.A., Renens, Switzerland). Standardized camera settings and a consistent color balance were applied to ensure comparable image quality across all photographs.

The inclusion criteria were complete dentition, proper retraction, adequate image quality, and adherence to the American Board of Orthodontics standards for frontal intraoral imaging. Images with orthodontic appliances, poor visibility, or exposure/angulation errors were excluded. After this screening process, 147 anonymized photographs were retained for final analysis.

The sample size was determined using the precision required for intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) estimation as the primary endpoint. In accordance with Bonett’s method for calculating sample sizes to achieve confidence intervals (CIs) of the desired width,28 and in line with established recommendations for reliability study design,29 samples on the order of 50 participants or more with multiple raters provide adequate precision for distinguishing between ICC reliability categories.30 Accordingly, the final dataset of 147 cases, evaluated by three orthodontists across two sessions and five LLM systems, yielded sufficiently narrow 95% CIs to support a meaningful interpretation (ICC = 0.771; 95% CI, 0.697–0.855 for GPT-4.0, CI width = 0.158).

Expert orthodontist scoring procedure

Three orthodontists with 8–15 years of clinical experience each independently evaluated the 147 photographs. Representative standardized frontal intraoral photographs illustrating the aesthetic range of the cases included in the study are shown in Figure 1. All raters used the official IOTN-AC reference guide to assign scores ranging from 1 to 10. Before scoring, a calibration session using 15 training images (excluded from the dataset) ensured consistent interpretation of the AC scale. Scoring was completed digitally using a shared Google Sheets interface containing only case identifiers. All the orthodontists independently rated the images and were blinded to each other’s assessments and clinical information.

To preserve inter-individual variation in aesthetic judgment, expert scores were stored separately rather than averaged, allowing the model performance to be evaluated against aggregated expert scores and across the full range of inter-examiner variability.

Model selection and evaluation framework

Selected large language models

Five multimodal LLMs capable of processing visual data were selected for evaluation: GPT-4.0 and GPT-o3 (OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA); Claude 3.7 Sonnet (Anthropic, San Francisco, CA, USA); Grok (xAI, San Francisco, CA, USA); and Manus (Monica AI, Singapore). All models were tested in their publicly available versions using web-based interfaces between April and May 2025. To ensure reproducibility, the technical details, including the model versions and parameter settings, are summarized in Table 1. All evaluations were conducted using standard commercial subscriptions without research-grade access.

Prompting protocol and output structure

Each LLM was instructed to assign an IOTN-AC score to standardized frontal intraoral photographs by comparing them with ten official IOTN-AC reference images (scores 1 to 10). These reference images were hosted with direct-access Imgur links (Imgur Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA; last accessed May 15, 2025) to ensure consistent availability across the models. The original IOTN-AC reference photographs were publicly available in the IOTN manual and associated literature. A standardized JSON-based instruction prompt was used. The models were required to rely exclusively on visual information, without clinical metadata or textual descriptions, and to return three outputs for each case: a numerical score (1–10), a brief justification, and a model-reported confidence value between 0 and 1. These justifications and model-reported confidence outputs were collected only for descriptive purposes and were not interpreted as explanations for the internal reasoning or calibrated uncertainty. An example of the prompt format is shown in Figure 2.

Data collection and execution

Each patient’s image was evaluated in a separate session, with single-image uploads used to avoid contextual bias or carry-over effects. The browser sessions were reset between cases to prevent context persistence. Model outputs were collected in a uniform structure and exported for statistical analysis. No fine-tuning, external retraining, or internal access was applied. All predictions were generated between April and May 2025 using the publicly available vision-capable versions of each model through their official interfaces.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi 2.6 (The Jamovi Project, Sydney, Australia). Inter-rater reliability among experts was assessed using ICCs calculated with a two-way random-effects model (absolute agreement, single measures), selected to account for both systematic and random variability and to support generalizability beyond the evaluated raters. ICCs were computed separately for each time point (Time 1 and Time 2) and for the averaged expert scores. Although the evaluated LLMs represented a fixed set of systems, a two-way random-effects framework was intentionally retained to yield conservative estimates and maintain comparability with prior reliability studies using similar reliability designs.

The expert mean score for each case was obtained by averaging each orthodontist’s ratings across both time points, and then across the three orthodontists to yield a single reference score per case (n = 147). The agreement between each LLM’s predictions and the expert rating was evaluated using ICCs, Pearson correlation coefficients, and mean absolute error (MAE). Categorical agreement was assessed at three levels: exact match (identity with any of the six expert ratings per case), near match (within ±1), and group match based on IOTN-AC treatment-need bands (Little Need: 1–4; Borderline Need: 5–7; Definite Need: 8–10). To contextualize the model performance relative to expert variability, the same metrics were additionally calculated between the best-performing LLM and each orthodontist individually.

Categorical reliability was examined using Cohen’s κ, weighted κ, and Krippendorff’s α (ordinal weighting across the three treatment-need categories). Given the ordinal nature of IOTN-AC scores, weighted κ and Krippendorff’s α were included to complement parametric agreement measures. Descriptive statistics (mean ± standard deviation) were calculated for model-reported confidence scores (range, 0–1). All tests were two-tailed with a significance threshold of P < 0.05. Clinically relevant misclassification patterns were explored using confusion matrices, boundary analysis at the AC 7–8 threshold, and classification error review.

The primary performance endpoint was the agreement between the predictions by each LLM and the expert mean score, which was assessed using the ICC. Secondary metrics included MAE, Pearson correlation coefficients, exact and near match rates, and categorical agreement indices (Cohen’s κ, weighted κ, and Krippendorff’s α).

RESULTS

Inter-rater reliability of expert orthodontists

The inter-rater reliability among the three orthodontists was moderate, with ICCs of 0.624 at Time 1 and 0.593 at Time 2. The overall ICC for the averaged ratings was 0.649 (95% CI, 0.563–0.742), which was consistent with the findings of previous reports on IOTN-AC reliability and aligned with the interpretive thresholds reported by Koo and Li.30 The ICC values for each rater are summarized in Table 2 and Supplementary Table 1 and shown in Figure 3.

Model-to-expert agreement

Among the LLMs, GPT-4.0 showed the highest agreement with the mean expert ratings, with an ICC of 0.771 (95% CI, 0.697–0.855) and a Pearson correlation coefficient (r) of 0.773 (P < 0.001). GPT-o3 had an ICC of 0.663 and r = 0.688. Claude, Manus, and Grok demonstrated moderate but statistically significant agreements, with ICCs of 0.555, 0.518, and 0.500, respectively. The model-wise ICCs, correlations, MAE values, and model-reported confidence scores are presented in Table 2, and the Bland–Altman plots visualizing model–expert agreement are shown in Figure 4.

To contextualize the performance relative to expert variability, the agreement metrics between GPT-4.0 and each orthodontist were also calculated (Supplementary Table 2). Substantial agreement was observed with orthodontists 1 (ICC = 0.757; r = 0.762; MAE, 1.16) and 2 (ICC = 0.720; r = 0.730; MAE, 1.28), whereas agreement with orthodontist 3 was lower (ICC = 0.535; r = 0.562; MAE, 1.61).

Threshold-focused error analysis

Threshold-focused analysis examined the model behavior near clinically relevant treatment-need boundaries. GPT-4.0 correctly identified all cases with expert mean scores ≥ 8 (8/8; sensitivity, 100%; 95% CI, 63.1–100%), with no false negatives at this threshold (Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Figure 1). Among the 28 expert-classified Borderline Need cases, three (10.7%) were upgraded to Definite Need, indicating a tendency to direct uncertainty toward higher-need categories. Although this tendency can reduce the risk of missed diagnoses, it can also result in over-referral.

At the critical AC 7–8 boundary, GPT-4.0 maintained a clinically favorable pattern by correctly classifying all Definite Need cases and upgrading only five of the 139 cases with expert scores ≤ 7. In contrast, GPT-o3, Manus, and Grok downgraded five, five, and seven of the eight Definite Need cases, respectively, whereas Claude showed the opposite tendency by upgrading 16 cases across the threshold. These findings highlight substantial inter-model differences at the decisive treatment boundary and suggest that GPT-4.0 minimizes the risk of missed definitive treatment in comparison with the other evaluated systems.

Distribution and correlation analysis

The distribution of the IOTN-AC scores assigned by the orthodontists showed comparable medians and interquartile ranges across both evaluation sessions (Table 3). Among the LLMs, GPT-4.0 and GPT-o3 produced score distributions that most closely aligned with those of the orthodontists, whereas Claude, Manus, and Grok exhibited broader variability.

The pairwise correlations among all the raters are shown in Figure 5. Orthodontists demonstrated strong intra-rater consistency (r > 0.85). Among the LLMs, GPT-4.0 showed the highest correlation with orthodontist scores (r ≈ 0.77), followed by GPT-o3 (r ≈ 0.69), while Claude and Grok showed more moderate associations (r ≈ 0.57–0.63). Manus showed a notably higher correlation (r = 0.752) despite its lower ICC (0.518), indicating a systematic deviation from the expert scores.

Model-reported confidence and bias evaluation

GPT-4.0 showed the lowest prediction error (MAE = 1.09), followed by GPT-o3 (1.16). Claude, Manus, and Grok demonstrated higher errors (MAE = 1.76, 1.82, and 1.69, respectively), indicating greater deviation from orthodontist assessments. Model-reported confidence scores were the highest for GPT-4.0 (0.915 ± 0.035), followed by Grok (0.895 ± 0.058), Manus (0.875 ± 0.056), Claude (0.864 ± 0.049), and GPT-o3 (0.603 ± 0.149). Despite GPT-o3’s strong match rates, its broader model-reported confidence distribution indicated greater internal variability. As shown in Figure 6, GPT-4.0 had the highest model-reported confidence and ICC values, whereas the other models exhibited higher model-reported confidence and weaker empirical agreement. The correlation between the model-reported confidence and actual reliability was negligible (R² = 0.038).

Bias analysis (Table 4) revealed minimal systematic errors across all models. GPT-4.0 showed a slight over-scoring tendency (mean difference = 0.08); GPT-o3 was nearly unbiased (−0.01); Claude and Grok showed slight overestimation; while Manus was essentially unbiased (mean difference = −0.02).

Overall performance and match analysis

GPT-4.0 and GPT-o3 produced numerical predictions closest to the expert scores. GPT-4.0 achieved the highest total match rate (76.2%), with 28.6% exact and 47.6% near matches, followed closely by GPT-o3 (74.2%, with 32.0% exact and 42.2% near matches). The remaining models showed substantially lower alignment (51.0–53.8%). The detailed match results are presented in Table 5 and Figure 7A.

When ratings were grouped into IOTN-AC treatment-need categories, notable differences emerged between the experts and LLMs. As shown in Table 6 and Figure 7B, experts distributed cases relatively evenly between Little Need and Borderline Need (≈39–41%) and classified ≈20% of the cases as Definite Need. In contrast, the LLMs showed a clear shift toward the mid-range, with GPT-4.0 and GPT-o3 assigning a higher proportion of cases to the Borderline Need category (≈58%) and fewer to the Definite Need group (≈8%). This pattern reflects a conservative scoring tendency centered around mid-range IOTN-AC values.

In addition, categorical agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s κ, weighted κ, and Krippendorff’s α. The α coefficient among the orthodontists was 0.195 (inter-LLM α = 0.244). This value was lower than the ICC (0.649) because α was computed on the three-category classification (Little/Borderline/Definite Need) using ordinal weighting, whereas ICC was calculated on the original 1–10 scores. The lower α reflects the substantial disagreement that arises when continuous scores are collapsed into categorical treatment-need groups. As shown in Table 7, GPT-4.0 achieved the highest categorical agreement with the orthodontists (κ = 0.468; weighted κ = 0.542), followed by GPT-o3 (κ = 0.379; weighted κ = 0.470). Although GPT-4.0 performed best among the evaluated models, the κ values remained modest in absolute magnitude, indicating limited categorical consistency and notable variability across LLMs.

DISCUSSION

Principal findings

This study applied a multi-level agreement framework to the evaluation of IOTN-AC scoring, capturing the non-linear and subjective characteristics of the AC, which are known to vary among clinicians and evaluators.2,11

A key finding was that the total match rates were consistently higher than group-level agreement across all models. For example, GPT-4.0 achieved a total match rate of 76.2%, but the group-level agreement was only 66.0%, indicating that small numerical differences near treatment thresholds, such as scores of 7 versus 8, may lead to different treatment-need classifications. Since IOTN-AC categories can influence access to care, group-level consistency is clinically more informative than pointwise agreement alone. Accordingly, the group-based evaluation framework used in this study provides a clinically aligned alternative to binary approaches such as AC ≤ 5 versus > 5.31 Despite the encouraging overall performance, categorical agreement remained modest, and inter-LLM reliability was low, indicating that these systems should currently be considered supportive rather than standalone decision-making tools.

The dataset of 147 standardized frontal intraoral photographs enabled a more detailed evaluation of clinically relevant treatment thresholds and exceeded the size of many prior IOTN-AC studies.14,16 The score distributions revealed a consistent mid-range bias across LLMs, with a greater proportion of cases classified as Borderline Need and fewer as Definite Need, in comparison with the assessments by expert orthodontists. Most disagreements occurred within this subjective mid-range, whereas GPT-4.0 correctly identified all cases classified as Definite Need by experts. Clinically, this conservative tendency supports the cautious handling of severe cases. However, borderline predictions require careful interpretation because small score shifts can influence triage decisions and referral pathways.

Comparison with previous artificial intelligence models

In comparison with previous CNN-based models, the LLMs in this study demonstrated competitive performance and, in some cases, superior performance on a more complex 10-class ordinal prediction task. Talaat et al.16 reported ICCs approaching 0.90 for detecting binary occlusal traits, while Noeldeke et al.17 achieved up to 98.6% accuracy for identifying crossbites. However, these models were trained for binary or fixed-label detection and not for the nuanced scoring of aesthetic severity. Stetzel et al.31 applied deep learning to binary IOTN-AC classification and achieved 82% accuracy (κ = 0.84), whereas our models operated across the full AC spectrum without custom training and still achieved high fidelity to expert assessments, most notably GPT-4.0 (ICC = 0.771; r = 0.773; and MAE = 1.09).

Advantages and interpretability of large language models

One methodological contribution of this study is the use of multimodal LLMs that produce structured outputs, including a numerical IOTN-AC score, a natural language explanation, and a model-reported confidence value. Although these explanations did not reveal the true internal mechanisms and should be regarded as post hoc rationalizations rather than validated reasoning, they may still support the clinical understanding of subjective aesthetic evaluation. GPT-4.0 showed the most stable output consistency, with consistently high model-reported confidence scores (mean, 0.915 ± 0.035). In contrast, GPT-o3, despite a similar match rate of 74.2%, exhibited markedly greater variability in model-reported confidence, indicating a less coherent evaluation process. Thus, in orthodontic assessments, advanced LLMs may function as informative and communicative assistants rather than purely opaque classifiers.

Clinical reliability and expert benchmark

The inter-rater reliability among the three orthodontists was moderate (ICC = 0.624 at Time 1 and 0.593 at Time 2; mean ICC = 0.649), which is consistent with previously reported IOTN-AC agreement levels.32 GPT-4.0 exceeded this benchmark with an ICC of 0.771 relative to the mean expert score, despite the absence of orthodontic-specific fine-tuning. Agreement analyses with individual orthodontists showed closer alignment between the two raters and lower agreement with the third, mirroring the variability observed among the experts. Overall, GPT-4.0 operated within—and in some comparisons exceeded—the natural range of clinician agreement, indicating that model performance should be interpreted in the context of inherent expert disagreement.

Threshold analysis further demonstrated that GPT-4.0 correctly identified all Definite Need cases (AC ≥ 8) and conservatively upgraded some borderline cases. Although this behavior minimizes false negatives, it may increase unnecessary referrals, highlighting a trade-off relevant to clinical triage. Consequently, borderline cases (AC 6–7) may still warrant expert reviews. The variability across models also indicates that system choice can influence clinical outcomes and supports cautious clinical use.

Limitations and future perspectives

All images were acquired under standardized conditions using a high-quality DSLR camera with controlled lighting and professional retraction. Consequently, the model performance may not be generalizable to routine clinical photographs acquired under variable conditions. Although the dataset was adequately powered, the malocclusion types were not stratified by skeletal Class I, II, or III relationships. Larger, more balanced samples would allow for clearer comparisons across the occlusal and aesthetic severity categories.

The analysis also did not identify the specific clinical or aesthetic features that posed the greatest challenge for the models. Subgroup analyses and structured error-pattern evaluations may clarify where model reasoning diverges from expert judgment. In addition, all reference ratings were provided by orthodontic specialists, and comparisons with general practitioners, dental students, and residents may further contextualize the model outputs across different levels of clinical experience. Moreover, the operational definition of an exact match, which was defined as identity with any of the six expert ratings per case, may have overestimated agreement relative to consensus-based references, such as the rounded expert mean.

Operational and regulatory considerations warrant further investigation. The clinical deployment of LLM-based systems requires robust privacy, security, and governance safeguards because external image transfer may pose confidentiality and compliance risks.

Further investigations may expand this evaluation paradigm by incorporating multi-angle intraoral imaging, consensus-based scoring frameworks, and patient-reported outcome measures.18 Fine-tuning LLMs on orthodontics-specific datasets may improve performance at clinically relevant decision thresholds. Widely applicable standardized image-driven AI platforms can support orthodontic triage and treatment prioritization by reducing subjective variability. Data governance and privacy requirements must be addressed using local or privacy-preserving deployment strategies.

CONCLUSIONS

GPT-4.0 consistently showed the highest agreement and reliability, indicating that in comparison with the other tested systems, this model can approach the consistency of trained clinicians in aesthetic judgments without task-specific training. These findings highlight the potential of LLMs as transparent and scalable tools for facilitating orthodontic diagnosis, education, and triage. With additional validation, these models may become useful decision-support tools that complement expert judgment.
