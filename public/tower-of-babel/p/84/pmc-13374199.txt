Electronic health records in patient care: strengths, facilitators, challenges, and future directions—a scoping review
Authors: Roohollah Askari, Mohammad Kazem Rahimi, Edris Heidari, Sajjad Bahariniya, Mohammad Ranjbar, Tahereh Shafaghat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElectronic health records are crucial medical informatics elements increasingly utilized in industrialized nations to enhance health care services. Given the significance of electronic health records in improving monitoring and evaluation, along with the necessity to deliver quality, cost-effective, and patient-centered services, this study aims to examine the strengths, limitations, challenges, facilitators, and prerequisites for the implementation and utilization of electronic health records.MethodsThis study was a systematic scoping review conducted in 2023. The protocol of the Joanna Briggs Institute (2015) was employed to design and execute this scoping review, while the model by Levak et al. (2010) served as a guide for its execution. Articles were searched across six major databases, including IS Web of Sciences, PubMed, Scopus, EMBASE, Cochrane, and ProQuest, using a predefined search strategy. Google Scholar was also utilized for supplementary searches. The search period spanned from 2002 to 2022.ResultsA total of 37,694 studies were identified across six primary databases. Following evaluation in three stages—title, abstract, and full-text—71 articles were deemed eligible for inclusion in this scoping review. Qualitative analysis and data classification of the selected studies identified four main themes and 31 sub-themes related to the strengths of setting up and utilizing electronic health records. Challenges associated with the launch and implementation of electronic health records were categorized into five main themes and 47 sub-themes, while facilitators and prerequisites for launching and utilizing EHRs were classified into five main themes and 30 sub-themes. It should be noted that some sub-themes were reported in only a single study; therefore, their weight should be interpreted with caution. Finally, a five-stage process was recommended to guide the successful launch and implementation of EHRs.ConclusionsHealthcare managers and policymakers should comprehensively consider the strengths, limitations, challenges, facilitators, and prerequisites for launching and using electronic health records. Given the relatively limited effectiveness of government incentive policies and financial support, there is a need to assess and revise these policies to enhance the implementation of EHR. Background

One of the most important goals of health systems is to maintain and improve population health [1]. Achieving this goal requires complete and accurate data collection, storage, and retrieval, which can generate large volumes of information. The effective use of this data through structured health information systems can significantly enhance healthcare delivery, decision-making, and policy planning [2]. Electronic health records (EHRs) are recognized as a valuable tool for improving health monitoring and evaluation, as well as informing clinical decisions [3]. EHRs allow secure sharing of patient care information among multiple authorized users and facilitate timely access to medical history, laboratory results and prescriptions [4].

Despite their potential, EHRs vary widely in terms of context, functionality, and implementation approach. While some systems are designed primarily for administrative purposes, others aim to integrate clinical and patient-centered functionalities [5, 6]. EHRs are increasingly being adopted in industrialized countries as a key component of medical informatics to improve healthcare quality, efficiency, and patient safety [7]. However, there is currently no universally accepted model for EHR development, and challenges such as standardization, interoperability, and alignment with local regulations persist [8, 9]. For countries yet to fully implement EHRs, understanding international standards, adapting them to local contexts, and learning from successful case studies are essential to guide effective adoption [10, 11].

Previous studies have investigated specific aspects of EHR implementation and use. For instance, Chao et al. (2013) explored the benefits and challenges of EHR adoption and highlighted that system inefficiencies, long processing times, and difficulty accessing laboratory reports reduced physicians’ willingness to use the system [12]. Keshta et al. (2021) focused on security and privacy concerns, reporting that IT security incidents in healthcare settings may undermine trust and adoption of EHRs [13]. Dornan et al. (2019) examined EHR implementation in Asia, emphasizing the importance of infrastructure, technical support, and organizational culture, which were identified as major barriers to successful adoption [14].

While these studies provide valuable insights, they are often limited to specific contexts, populations, or aspects of EHR implementation. Existing reviews, such as Kruse et al. (2016) and Fennelly et al. (2020), have synthesized evidence on certain challenges, benefits, or facilitators, but they do not provide a comprehensive mapping of all dimensions, including strengths, organizational and human factors, facilitators, and preconditions required for successful implementation. Moreover, many reviews focus predominantly on technical or clinical outcomes, leaving gaps in understanding the broader systemic and contextual factors that influence EHR adoption across diverse healthcare settings.

Given the critical role of EHRs in improving monitoring, evaluation, quality, cost-effectiveness, and patient-centered care, there is a need for a systematic scoping review that addresses these gaps. Therefore, the present study was conducted as a systematic scoping review with the aim of identifying and synthesizing the strengths, challenges, facilitators, and preconditions for implementing and utilizing EHRs. By providing a comprehensive overview, this study aims to inform future research, guide policymakers, and support healthcare organizations in effective planning and adoption of electronic health records.

Methods

This study employed a systematic scoping review methodology, conducted in 2023. The review was designed and executed in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) protocol (2015) [15] and guided by the framework proposed by Levac et al. (2010) [16]. The scoping review process, as outlined by Levac et al. and the JBI protocol, comprised six distinct stages, detailed below:

Defining the research question and aligning it with the study objectives

In alignment with established guidelines, the primary research question was formulated as follows: “What are the strengths, obstacles, challenges, facilitators, and prerequisites of Electronic Health Records (EHRs) based on the experiences of various countries worldwide?” A well-defined research question in a scoping review serves as a critical guide for establishing study inclusion criteria, designing the study protocol, facilitating evidence retrieval, and structuring the review’s reporting. The research question incorporated the Population, Concept, and Context (PCC) framework [17]. In this study, the population (P) encompassed all studies examining the strengths, obstacles, challenges, facilitators, and prerequisites of EHRs. The concept (C) focused on electronic health records, while the context included the experiences of different countries globally.

Literature search

A comprehensive search was conducted across six databases: ISI Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus, EMBASE, Cochrane, and ProQuest. Google Scholar was also utilized for supplementary searches. The search spanned publications from 2002 to 2022. The search strategy was developed using a combination of keywords and controlled vocabulary terms (Medical Subject Headings [MeSH] in PubMed where applicable). A comprehensive search was conducted across six databases; however, for clarity and readability, only the PubMed search strategy is presented in the main text (Table 1 of the appendices), while the full search strategies for all databases are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Search strategy in PubMed: (((“Electronic Health Record*”[Title] OR “Electronic medical Record*”[Title] OR “Computerized Medical Record*”[Title] OR “Automated Health Record*”[Title] OR “Computer-based Patient Record*”[Title] OR “Electronic patient Record*”[Title] OR “personal health record*”[Title] OR “Electronic clinical Record*”[Title] OR “Computerized patient Record*”[Title] OR “Computer-based medical Record*”[Title] OR “Computerized health Record*”[Title]) AND (English[Language])) AND ((“2002”[Date - Publication]: “2022”[Date - Publication]))) AND (journal article[Publication Type] OR review[Publication Type]) Filters applied: Full text.

The primary search for this scoping review was completed up to December 2022. Due to resource and time constraints, a full updated search prior to submission was not feasible, and this is acknowledged as a limitation.

Study screening and selection

Inclusion criteria comprised original research articles, review articles, and dissertations published in English with full-text availability, focusing on the strengths, obstacles, challenges, facilitators, and prerequisites of EHRs. Exclusion criteria included studies without full-text availability, non-English publications, and non-research materials such as book reviews, opinion pieces, commentaries, letters to the editor, and conference proceedings lacking a structured framework for evaluation.

To ensure comprehensiveness, gray literature and studies not identified through database searches were sought via Google Scholar and by reviewing reference lists of retrieved studies. Following that, two independent reviewers screened studies in three phases: title, abstract, and full-text review. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted when consensus could not be reached. Although quality assessment is not mandatory in scoping reviews [17], this study evaluated the methodological rigor of included studies based on their design. EndNote version 8 was used to manage the search and screening process, and the PRISMA1 protocol [18] guided study selection and reporting.

Data extraction and analysis

Data pertaining to the strengths, obstacles, challenges, facilitators, and prerequisites of EHRs were extracted from the selected studies and compiled into a structured table. This table included details such as the first author’s name, publication year, study title, location, study design, participants, and key findings.

Qualitative analysis was conducted to ensure comprehensive coverage of complex themes and concepts relevant to the review. This iterative process facilitated the identification and synthesis of key aspects related to EHR implementation, enabling a nuanced understanding of the data.

Synthesis and reporting

Two reviewers (Reviewer XX and Reviewer YY) independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (Reviewer ZZ). Prior to screening, all reviewers were trained on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data extraction was also conducted independently by the same two reviewers, following a standardized extraction form. Disagreements were resolved by consensus with the third reviewer. Although formal inter-rater agreement (e.g., Cohen’s kappa) was not calculated, the reviewers achieved high consistency during pilot screening and throughout the process.

Expert consultation

While optional in some scoping review frameworks, expert consultation was incorporated into this study as recommended by Levac et al. (2010). Input was sought from experts in electronic health records and members of the research team to validate and refine the findings.

Quality appraisal of included studies

To assess the methodological quality of the included studies, validated critical appraisal tools were selected based on the design of each study. Qualitative studies were evaluated using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist, which assesses methodological rigor, credibility, and relevance of qualitative research. Cross-sectional studies were appraised using the Appraisal Tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS). Systematic reviews and research syntheses were assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Systematic Reviews and Research Syntheses. Narrative review articles were evaluated using the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA), and mixed-methods studies were assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) version 2018.

For CASP, AXIS, JBI, and MMAT tools, studies were evaluated using response categories such as “Yes,” “No,” “Can’t tell,” “Unclear,” or “Do not know,” according to the guidance of each instrument. For SANRA, each item was scored from 0 to 2, with higher scores indicating better methodological quality.

The quality appraisal process was conducted independently by two reviewers to enhance reliability and reduce potential bias. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus, and when necessary, a third reviewer was consulted. The detailed results of the quality assessment are presented in the supplementary materials.

Overall, most studies demonstrated moderate to high methodological quality across the appraisal domains. Cross-sectional studies assessed with the AXIS tool generally achieved moderate-to-high scores, with several studies meeting most quality criteria. Qualitative and mixed-methods studies also showed acceptable methodological rigor, although some studies lacked sufficient reporting regarding reflexivity, sampling strategies, or integration of mixed-method findings. Systematic and narrative reviews were generally of moderate quality; however, a few studies showed limitations related to publication bias assessment, methodological transparency, or literature search description. Detailed quality appraisal results and scoring for all included studies are provided in the supplementary materials.

Results

Based on the systematic search, 37,694 studies were found from six databases, and after removing duplicates, 21,648 studies entered in the title review stage. After removing the studies with unrelated titles, the abstracts of the remaining 3865 studies were reviewed, of which 564 studies were suitable for review based on the full text of the article and 19 Records suggested by experts or by checking the references lists. At the end and after reviewing the full text of the remaining studies, 71 articles were eligible to enter the scoping review and extract the desired data from them (Fig. 1).

Most studies have been conducted in 2011 (n = 9 (13%)), 2020 (n = 9 (13%)) and 2021 (n = 9 (13%)) and then in 2012 (n = 8 (11%)) and 2016 (n = 8 (11%)). Most of the final included studies in the scoping review were related to the countries of USA (n = 39 (55%)) and Iran (n = 16 (22.5%)). Also, most of the selected studies in the scoping review in terms of the method were qualitative studies (n = 15 (21%)), cross-sectional or quantitative surveys (n = 15 (20%)), narrative reviews (n = 11(15%)) and systematic reviews (n = 10 (14%)). The results of qualitative analysis and classification of the data obtained from the text of the selected studies result in the classification of extracted items based on three main categories of 1. strengths, 2. challenges, 3. prerequisites and facilitators and 4. implementation steps.

The strengths of setting up and utilizing EHR systems include four main themes (patient care outcomes, organizational costs and resources, efficiency and process improvement, and organizational effectiveness and results) and 31 sub-themes. Across the 71 reviewed studies, the most frequently reported benefits related to the improvement of patient care quality and safety, with patient safety and error reduction reported in 12 studies, and overall quality of care improvement in 11 studies. EHRs were also associated with enhancements in preventive and public health services (4 studies), patient experience and quality of life (3 studies), and increased patient engagement in self-care and empowerment (3 studies). Some sub-themes, such as improving services in local health departments or enhancing responsiveness, were reported in only one study each, highlighting their rarity. Regarding organizational costs and resources, EHR implementation contributed to reductions in overall healthcare expenditures (7 studies) and specific treatment-related costs (1 study), while supporting more efficient use of workforce and physical infrastructure (2 studies). In the domain of efficiency and process improvement, EHRs improved workflow efficiency (6 studies), reduced duplication of tests and treatments (1 study), enabled faster and continuous access to patient information (6 studies), enhanced accuracy of documentation, coding, and billing (3 studies), improved medication management (1 study), and facilitated more effective outpatient referral systems (1 study). They also supported adherence to evidence-based guidelines (4 studies), strengthened monitoring and preventive care (1 study), and allowed controlled access to patient information (2 studies). Finally, EHRs contributed to organizational and clinical effectiveness, including disease monitoring and outbreak detection (2 studies), enhanced administrative oversight (2 studies), improved research quality through better data (4 studies), increased staff satisfaction (1 study), higher healthcare professional productivity (2 studies), and improved clinical management (2 studies). Reporting the frequency of studies for each sub-theme clarifies which benefits are widely supported and which are rare, preventing misinterpretation of the evidence as fully convergent (Table 1).

The strengths of setting up and using electronic health records

Challenges of launching and implementing EHR systems were categorized into five main themes—organizational, individual and human, financial, moral/legal, and technical—and 47 sub-themes. Among organizational challenges, the most frequently reported issues included the need for change and its inappropriate management (6 studies), resistance to organizational culture change (6 studies), and weak inter-organizational coordination (6 studies), while other issues such as insufficient management support, lack of communication, and lack of proven benefits of EHR use were reported in only one study each, indicating their rarity. Many healthcare settings lacked strategic planning for EHR integration with existing infrastructure, limited incentives for adoption, and inadequate attention to required resources, which contributed to fragmented implementation. Individual and human barriers were also prominent, with resistance to change among healthcare professionals reported in 10 studies, attitudinal, behavioral, and psychological issues in 9 studies, and insufficient training in 8 studies. Other challenges, such as reluctance to learn new skills or limited use of standard terms, appeared in only one study each. Financial constraints were reported in multiple studies, including high initial investment costs (6 studies), maintenance and support costs (3 studies), and lack of capital and equipment resources (3 studies), whereas concerns about return on investment and insufficient governmental funding were rare. Legal and ethical concerns mainly related to data security and privacy (5 studies), with issues like unauthorized access to patient information or absence of robust legal frameworks reported in only one study each. Technical challenges included weak and unreliable infrastructures (4 studies), insufficient technical support (3 studies), and extended time required to design, develop, and launch the system (3 studies). Other issues, such as limited computer access, dependence on non-governmental software, and implementation problems in long-term care centers, were each reported in only one study. Collectively, these findings highlight both widely experienced and rare barriers to EHR implementation, providing a clearer and more accurate representation of the evidence without implying convergence where it does not exist (Table 2).

Challenges in setting up and implementing electronic health records

Facilitators and prerequisites for launching and using electronic health records were also classified in the form of five main themes (Financial, Managerial, Technological, Organizational, and Human resource) and 30 sub-themes. The included studies identified several key facilitators for the successful implementation of electronic health records (EHRs), which were categorized into financial, managerial, technological, organizational, and human resource domains. Financial support was consistently highlighted as a fundamental prerequisite, particularly for system development, implementation, and long-term maintenance. In addition to adequate funding, efficient financial management and minimizing resource wastage were considered important for ensuring sustainability. Managerial factors, including effective project management, clear strategic planning, strong leadership, and government support, were also frequently emphasized as critical enablers of successful implementation. Technological readiness and organizational preparedness were other major facilitators. Studies stressed the importance of secure, user-friendly, and interoperable systems, supported by appropriate infrastructure and technical assistance. Organizational facilitators included a supportive culture, clear policies, inter-organizational coordination, and stakeholder engagement, which together enhance system adoption. Finally, human resource factors such as training, improved IT skills, perceived usefulness of the system, and user motivation were consistently identified as essential for encouraging acceptance EHRs (Table 3).

Discussion

This study highlights the transformative role of electronic health records (EHRs) in improving patient care. The findings demonstrate both the advantages and the challenges of EHR implementation. In the following sections, these results are discussed according to the main themes identified in the study—Strengths, Challenges, Facilitators, and Future Directions—providing an interpretation of the implications for clinical practice and healthcare management.

The strength of setting up and using EHR system

One of the main strengths of implementing electronic health records (EHRs) is their potential to improve patient safety. This is supported by previous studies, such as Tubaishat et al. (2019) [108] and Neves et al. (2020) [109], which highlight that EHR systems contribute to minimizing medication errors, improving data documentation, and enhancing patient care quality. These findings demonstrate that while EHRs offer significant benefits, the realization of these advantages depends on effective system design and proper usage. Poorly designed or improperly used systems can compromise data integrity and reduce patient safety. Overall, these comparisons underscore the critical importance of robust and well-implemented EHR systems in supporting safe and high-quality patient care [110].

Another key strength of EHR systems is their potential to reduce medical and medication errors, which are critical to patient safety. This finding aligns with previous studies, including Campanella et al. (2016), which demonstrated that properly implemented EHRs can enhance healthcare quality, improve adherence to clinical instructions, and increase efficiency [19]. These observations suggest that careful design and strategic implementation of EHRs are essential to maximize their benefits and minimize risks, reinforcing their role as a vital tool in improving patient safety and care quality.

Another strength of EHR systems is their potential to reduce healthcare costs. Implementing EHRs can lead to improved efficiency in care delivery, a decrease in preventable errors, and shorter patient stays, all of which contribute to cost savings over time. Evidence from previous studies, such as Highfill (2019) [111] and Pulleyblank et al. (2020) [112]., supports the idea that while immediate reductions in total healthcare costs may not always be observed, strategic use of EHRs—particularly those integrated with disease management systems—can generate long-term financial benefits for healthcare institutions. These findings highlight the importance of considering both short-term and long-term impacts when evaluating the economic advantages of EHR implementation.

Continuous and quick access to health information is another key strength of EHR systems, facilitating effective patient information management. By enabling healthcare providers and administrators to access accurate and comprehensive patient data, EHRs support not only individual patient care but also broader applications, such as healthcare planning and policy development [113]. Previous studies, including Häyrinen et al. (2008), have highlighted that the use of EHRs leads to more complete and timely documentation, improving overall efficiency and decision-making in healthcare settings [114]. These observations emphasize the critical role of well-designed EHR systems in enhancing both clinical and administrative processes.

Another strength is saving time for patients and medical staff. In a study, Pinevich et al. (2021) investigated the interaction time with EHR system. This study aimed to evaluate the time that healthcare providers spend interacting with electronic health records. EHR interaction times vary depending on the role of physicians and clinical settings, computer systems, and user experience. The average time spent by physicians on the EHR was about one-third of a working day, which represents substantial time savings. These findings are a possible indicator that shows that EHR has opportunities for performance improvement and workflow optimization [54].

Challenges of setting up and implementing EHR system

One of the key organizational challenges in implementing EHR systems is the need to adapt to organizational culture. Successful adoption of EHR requires alignment of governance, leadership, workflow, and human and technological factors. Previous studies, such as Fennelly et al. (2020) [84] and Jianxun et al. (2021) [115], highlight that organizational pressures and culture significantly influence the attitudes and engagement of healthcare professionals, which in turn affect EHR adoption. These findings emphasize that addressing cultural and organizational barriers is critical for the effective and sustainable implementation of EHR systems. Therefore, it is very important to pay attention to the organizational culture to implement EHR system.

Resistance to change among healthcare professionals is a significant personal and human challenge in the implementation of EHR systems. User resistance can prevent organizations from realizing the full benefits of EHR, making it crucial to address these behaviors proactively [116]. Previous studies, including Cho et al. (2021), highlight that factors such as self-efficacy, perceived value, peer influence, and organizational support play a role in shaping users’ attitudes toward adopting HER [12]. These findings suggest that healthcare leaders should employ targeted strategies to reduce barriers and enhance facilitators of user adoption, ensuring successful and sustainable EHR implementation.

Financial challenges represent a major barrier to the successful implementation of EHR systems. High initial costs, ongoing maintenance expenses, and limited resources—particularly in smaller or rural hospitals—can hinder adoption and effective use. Previous studies, including Adler-Milstein et al. (2015) [103], Aliabadi et al. (2020) [117], and Kruse et al. (2016) [39], highlight that addressing financial constraints is crucial for ensuring equitable and sustainable EHR implementation. These findings suggest that policymakers and healthcare leaders should develop targeted strategies, such as incentives and support mechanisms, to overcome financial barriers and facilitate broader adoption of EHR systems.

Security and privacy concerns represent a significant challenge in the implementation of EHR systems. Both healthcare professionals and patients may perceive risks related to unauthorized access, data integrity, and ethical use of health information. Previous studies, including Bani Issa et al. (2020) [118] and Papoutsi et al. (2015) [119], highlight that these concerns stem from administrative shortcomings, insufficient staff training, inadequate auditing, and potential misuse of sensitive patient information. Addressing these challenges requires the establishment of robust security mechanisms, clear organizational policies, and ongoing education for healthcare personnel to ensure compliance with ethical and legal standards. Moreover, engaging patients and the public in discussions about data privacy and demonstrating the benefits of secure information sharing can enhance trust and acceptance. Ensuring confidentiality and protection of health data is therefore crucial not only for maintaining patient trust but also for promoting the successful adoption, sustainable use, and overall effectiveness of EHR systems in improving healthcare delivery.

Facilitators and prerequisites for setting up and using EHR system

One of the facilitators of using electronic health records is adequate financial support. In a study, Sittig and Singh (2011) examined legal, ethical, and financial issues in the adoption and use of electronic health records and recommended that key stakeholders begin discussing these issues in a national forum. These actions can help to identify and prioritize solutions for the key issues discussed so that widespread, safe, effective, and interactive EHRs can help change healthcare. In this regard, adequate financial support can be a key issue [120].

Another facilitator of using electronic health records is paying attention to security requirements and protecting data security. In a study, Bincoletto (2020) examined data protection concerns in the context of cross-border interoperability of electronic health record systems in the European Union. Due to the importance of data protection and privacy principles, EHR systems should be designed efficiently and ensure data protection laws before being launched and implemented [121].

Another facilitator for the use of electronic health records is the provision of government incentives and support. Deutsch et al. (2010) conducted a study focusing on critical areas of national electronic health record programs. Based on the findings, mastering strategic, organizational and human challenges is usually more difficult than technical aspects. In this regard, the support of the government, especially the financial support of the government for the launch of EHR system, is vital [10].

Another facilitator is favorable organizational culture. Ballaro and Washington (2016) in a study investigated the importance of the influence of organizational culture and perceived organizational support on the successful use of electronic health records. Based on the findings, in order to successfully implement electronic health records, both factors of organizational culture and organizational support are very important, and upstream managers should pay more attention to these matters [122].

Training related to electronic health records is one of the other facilitators and prerequisites for the launch and use of electronic health records. Despite the widespread use of EHR, the benefits of this program have clearly not been realized because of inadequate training of physicians. Has not been [62]. In this regard, Humphrey-Murto et al. (2023) investigated the training of physicians and assistants to use electronic health records in a case study. Based on the findings of this study, despite mandatory training, physicians described limited transferability of training to the workplace. Training physicians to effectively use EHRs requires a change in organizational culture, because EHRs affect all aspects of clinical workflow. Analytical thinking to consider workflow, continuing education after initiation, and recognizing the interdependence of multiple factors is critical to prepare physicians to provide effective clinical care and potentially reduce burnout [123]. In the studies of Nuamah et al. (2022) and Heponiemi et al. (2021) the importance of personnel training before setting up and implementing EHR system has been discussed [124, 125].

Implementation steps for setting up and using electronic health records

Successful implementation of EHR systems requires careful planning, preparation, design, deployment, and ongoing evaluation. Studies indicate that attention to organizational readiness, leadership support, and user engagement are critical for adoption and sustainability. Incorporating patient-centered design, reliable infrastructure, standardized content, and robust technical support further facilitate effective use. Previous research emphasizes that continuous monitoring, evaluation, and improvement—including interoperability, security, and efficiency considerations—are essential to maximize the benefits of EHR systems [83, 88, 91, 94–102, 104, 106]. Overall, these implementation strategies highlight that both technical and organizational facilitators must be addressed concurrently to ensure successful and sustainable adoption of EHR systems in healthcare settings.

To support effective EHR implementation, policymakers should provide financial incentives and resources, particularly for smaller healthcare institutions. Robust data security and privacy regulations are essential to build trust among providers and patients. Promoting a supportive organizational culture and engaging leadership can reduce resistance and enhance adoption. Ongoing training integrated with clinical workflows is critical to ensure competent use and optimize efficiency. Finally, investment in technical infrastructure and interoperability standards is necessary to ensure seamless, sustainable, and high-quality EHR use across healthcare settings.

While natural language processing (NLP) and AI-assisted analysis of EHR data are emerging areas with potential to improve clinical decision support and outcome prediction, these technologies are beyond the primary scope of the current review, which focuses on the implementation of EHR systems in patient care. Future reviews could specifically address NLP and AI applications to fully capture their impact on EHR data quality and clinical utility.

Conclusion

Based on the findings of this review, managers and health policymakers should consider the strengths, challenges, facilitators, and prerequisites when setting up and using EHR systems. Addressing organizational, individual, financial, ethical-legal, and technical barriers is essential for successful implementation. The study findings suggest that government incentive policies and financial support require careful evaluation and possible reform, rather than assuming their limited effectiveness without clear evidence. Additionally, enhancing a supportive organizational culture is crucial to reduce resistance to change among users. Finally, the five-stage implementation pathway presented in this study was developed inductively from the included studies and reflects a synthesis of common strategies reported across the literature, providing a practical guide for the successful launch and adoption of EHR systems.

Study limitations

Some relevant studies might have been missed due to the restriction of searches to specific databases (e.g., English-language databases only). Also, the diversity in study designs, methodologies, and measurement criteria of the included studies may affect the synthesis and make direct comparisons challenging. Furthermore, in some specific topics, there may be a lack of sufficient data or limited evidence, which could weaken the conclusions of the review. Ultimately, scoping reviews are primarily designed to map existing evidence rather than provide definitive conclusions or specific recommendations for clinical practice. On the other hand, Due to resource and time constraints, a full updated systematic search beyond 2022 was not feasible prior to submission, which we acknowledge as a limitation. However, to mitigate this, we conducted a targeted review of key studies published from January 2023 to the present. These recent studies generally corroborate our findings on the strengths, challenges, facilitators, and preconditions for EHR implementation. Importantly, while the literature since 2022 includes advances such as post-pandemic telehealth integration and AI-assisted documentation, these developments do not substantially alter the overall mapping of evidence or the clinical applicability of the five-stage implementation pathway. Nevertheless, we recognize that ongoing EHR research may refine specific implementation strategies, and future updates of this review should integrate emerging evidence to ensure continued relevance.
