Comparative Evaluation of Peppermint Oil, Lignocaine Gel, and Ice as Topical Anesthetics for Intraoral Injections in Children: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Diksha Sharma, Manohar Bhat, Abhishek Khairwa, Diksha Shekhawat, Bhargab Roy Medhi, Bidisha Borah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFear or anxiety about receiving a local anesthetic injection is a common issue in dental settings, particularly in pediatric dentistry. This fear often stems from the anticipation of pain or discomfort associated with the injection, which can serve as a significant barrier to effective treatment delivery. In children, anticipatory anxiety is especially prevalent, meaning they often experience fear even before the procedure begins, based on what they expect will happen.ObjectiveThis study aimed to compare and evaluate pain perception following the topical application of peppermint oil, lignocaine gel, and ice before an intraoral injection in children aged 7–14 years.MethodA total of 150 children, aged between 7 and 14 years, who required local anesthesia (LA) for the dental procedure were divided into three groups of 50 each (group I: lignocaine gel, group II: ice, and group III: peppermint oil). Objective pain measurement [sound, eye, motor (SEM) scale] during administration and subjective measurement [visual analog scale (VAS)] after LA administration were recorded.ResultsIn intergroup comparison on the basis of SEM and VAS, peppermint oil showed the least pain score, followed by lignocaine gel and ice.ConclusionPeppermint oil can be used as a topical anesthetic prior to LA administration.How to cite this articleSharma D, Bhat M, Khairwa A, et al. Introduction

Pain is considered one of the most critical aspects of healthcare and is often regarded as a key factor that needs to be monitored by healthcare professionals. Pain, whether acute or chronic, can profoundly affect an individual's quality of life, mobility, psychological health, and overall well-being.1 In pediatric dentistry, fear and anxiety are indeed prevalent, making effective pain management essential to ensure a positive experience for young patients. Proper use of local anesthetics plays a key role in minimizing discomfort and alleviating anxiety during invasive procedures. The injection of local anesthesia (LA) is a well-documented source of discomfort and anxiety for many patients. Even with advancements in dental injection techniques, the process can still be distressing.2

Milgrom et al., in 1997,3 gave four dimensions of dental injection fear:

Topical anesthesia plays a crucial role in minimizing or eliminating the pain caused by the needle during dental injections. By applying a numbing agent directly to the surface of the tissues (such as the gums or the inside of the mouth) before the needle is inserted, the sensation of the injection can be significantly reduced, leading to a more comfortable experience for the patient. While topical anesthetics are effective in reducing or eliminating the needle-induced pain, they do not guarantee a completely pain-free experience for dental LA. The efficacy of topical anesthetics is influenced by various factors, including the duration of application and the gauge of the needle used.4

Topical anesthetics work by temporarily numbing the area they are applied to, which alter pain thresholds and reduces the sensation of pain. This occurs through the blockade of pain signals that are normally transmitted by peripheral sensory nerve fibers to the brain. By inhibiting the transmission of these signals, topical anesthetics prevent the perception of localized pain at the application site.

However, these anesthetics are typically only effective in the superficial layers of tissues, such as the mucosa. Since they do not penetrate deeply into tissues, their ability to block pain is limited to the surface areas they come into contact with.5 For topical anesthetics to achieve their full effectiveness, a contact time of 1–5 minutes is typically required. During this period, the anesthetic works to block pain signals, reaching a depth of about 2–3 mm from the surface of the mucosa. This means that the anesthetic can numb the superficial layers of the tissue, but not deeper structures.6,7

Lidocaine is considered to have a relatively large safety margin compared to other anesthetic agents. It is well tolerated in most cases, with rare instances of allergic reactions. Toxic reactions are also uncommon, especially when the anesthetic is applied topically, as it typically does not penetrate deeply into tissues. However, like with any medication, adverse reactions can occur in sensitive individuals, though they are less frequent with topically applied lidocaine compared to other forms of local anesthetics.8 These limitations drive the need for developing and evaluating other substances and techniques to improve pain management in various medical and dental procedures.

Various alternatives to synthetic topical anesthetics, including herbal formulations, precooling, vibration, and distraction, have been explored by researchers. The study was conducted to evaluate and compare pain perception in children (7–14 years) after topical application of peppermint oil, lignocaine gel, and ice prior to intraoral anesthetic injection.

Trial Design and Participants

This randomized control study compares and evaluates the pain perception following the topical application of peppermint oil, lignocaine gel, and ice before an intraoral injection in children, aged between 7 and 14 years, who visited the Department of Pedodontics, Jaipur Dental College from April to July, 2024. A total of 150 children were selected in the study after excluding the ineligible participants.

Pain assessment during local anesthetic injection was carried out using two different methods. The sound, eye, motor (SEM) scale (Table 1) was employed to objectively evaluate pain, based on the child's vocal expressions, eye reactions, and motor movements, as assessed by trained personnel. The chosen scores were documented for analysis.

Assessments were conducted in a standardized clinical setting to ensure consistency across all groups. To analyze the pain perception based on SEM and VAS scores, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used.

Sample Size

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power analysis. An equal allocation ratio was adopted, with 50 participants assigned to each group in this randomized controlled trial.

Randomization

A total of 150 participants who visited the Department of Pedodontics, Jaipur Dental College were screened and then were randomly divided into three groups: group I (lignocaine gel), group II (ice), and group III (peppermint oil). This study employed a single-blind randomized controlled trial design, with group I as the control group and groups II and III as the experimental groups. Participants who did not meet the inclusion criteria or did not provide consent were excluded from the study.

Statistical Analysis

The intergroup comparison based on SEM and VAS was done using the Kruskal–Wallis test.

The statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The study analyzed pain perception using the SEM scale and VAS across three intervention groups: lignocaine gel, ice, and peppermint oil. The results are summarized below:

SEM Scores

Peppermint oil showed the lowest mean pain score (0.50 ± 0.51), followed by lignocaine gel (0.68 ± 0.47) and ice (1.08 ± 0.27). The overall mean SEM score across all participants was 0.75 ± 0.49 (Table 2 and Figs 8 and 9). Mean ranks—peppermint oil: 57.50 (lowest pain perception), lignocaine gel: 70.64, ice: 98.36 (highest pain perception). The p-value (0.000) indicates a statistically significant difference in SEM scores between the groups (Table 3).

VAS Scores

Peppermint oil again had the lowest mean VAS score (1.06 ± 0.77), followed by lignocaine gel (2.04 ± 1.03) and ice (2.72 ± 0.90). The overall mean VAS score for all participants was 1.94 ± 1.13 (Table 2, Fig. 8). Mean ranks—peppermint oil: 39.15 (lowest pain perception), lignocaine gel: 80.02, ice: 107.33 (highest pain perception). The p-value (0.000) confirms a highly significant difference in pain perception across the three groups (Table 3).

Statistical analysis using the Kruskal–Wallis test revealed that peppermint oil significantly reduced pain perception compared to lignocaine gel and ice, with p-values of 0.000 for both SEM and VAS scales. This indicates a highly significant difference among the three groups, supporting the effectiveness of peppermint oil as a preinjection topical agent.

Discussion

In the present study, three different types of topical anesthetic agents were used, namely, lignocaine gel, ice, and peppermint oil. As shown in the results, peppermint oil showed the least pain score, followed by lignocaine gel and ice. Pain scores were calculated by using SEM and VAS scale, and a p-value of 0.000 seen in the study results means that there is a highly significant difference between the groups being compared. We all use lignocaine topical gel in our daily practices. So, when we discuss the other two materials, there is enough literature on cryotherapy (ice) and its anesthetic effects, but peppermint oil does not have enough literature. So, the aim was to evaluate which agent works better for the participants.

The local anesthetic effect of ice or cold-induced neuropraxia occurs due to the physiological changes cold has on nerve function. When ice is applied to a specific area of the body, the cold temperature causes vasoconstriction (narrowing of blood vessels) and slows down the speed at which nerve impulses travel. This process affects the sensory nerves, particularly those responsible for transmitting pain signals, making it harder for the body to perceive pain. Soni et al. investigated the effect of precooling the injection site on pain reduction during oral injections in pediatric patients and concluded that precooling is a safe, effective, and distracting technique during local anesthetic administration.10 Mohiuddin et al. compared topical cooling with local anesthetic gel prior to infiltration anesthesia in children undergoing maxillary anterior extractions and reported that precooling the injection site significantly reduced pain perception compared to the use of anesthetic gel.11 Varshini et al. compared the effectiveness of laser bio stimulation, ice, and topical anesthetic gel for reducing injection pain in children and reported that ice and anesthetic gel were equally effective, whereas laser therapy showed lower efficacy.12 Ruthvik et al. compared the efficacy of precooling the injection site to that of using a topical anesthetic as a preinjection anesthetic in a trial. Intergroup comparison of VAS score showed that VAS score was significantly lesser in the ice bud group as compared to the control group.13 Mittal et al. compared topical anesthetic, ice pretreatment, and an electric toothbrush as a vibratory device, to reduce the pain of local anesthetic injections. The pain scores were slightly higher in the topical anesthetic group as compared to ice pretreatment or vibration group, but no significant difference was seen.14

Mentha, commonly known as peppermint, is a genus of plants that belongs to the Lamiaceae (mint) family. This genus includes several species, with Mentha piperita (peppermint) being one of the most well-known.15 Peppermint is a hybrid of Mentha spicata L. (spearmint) and Mentha aquatica. Mentha (peppermint and other species) is widely grown in temperate regions, especially in Europe, North America, and North Africa. Over time, its cultivation has spread to virtually all parts of the world due to its various uses, particularly in food, cosmetics, and medicine. The medicinal value of Mentha plants comes primarily from the essential oil, which is extracted from the aerial parts of the flowering plant.16

Peppermint oil, apart from producing a cooling sensation when applied topically, possesses several pharmacological properties, such as analgesic, anesthetic, antibacterial, antispasmodic, and cognitive-enhancing effects. Owing to these attributes, it is widely incorporated into oral hygiene products, topical preparations, aromatherapy, and other healthcare formulations. There are no studies that have investigated the topical anesthetic action of peppermint oil. Petluru et al. conducted a study comparing peppermint oil and lignocaine spray as topical anesthetics prior to local anesthetic administration in children. The authors reported that peppermint oil effectively reduced pain perception, attributing its action to menthol, which produces a cooling sensation by stimulating cold receptors and inhibiting calcium ion currents in neuronal membranes. Since modulation of Ca″ currents has been associated with altered pain thresholds, this mechanism may underlie its analgesic effect. The study also highlighted that it was the first to directly evaluate peppermint oil against lignocaine spray in this context.8 Koushki et al. conducted a comparative study on the effect of topical peppermint and cold compresses in alleviating venipuncture pain among dialysis patients. Their results demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in mean pain intensity in both the peppermint and cold compress groups compared to the control group. So, according to them, menthol [CH3CH6(C3H7)OH] in peppermint reduces pain by acting on the kappa-opioid receptors; the pain relief in their study can be attributed to these properties of peppermint.17 Pathan et al. evaluated lavender oil as a preanesthetic topical analgesic in dentistry using standardized pain scales. According to them, lavender oil had better results than benzocaine gel.18

Limitations

Only pain rating scales were used in this study; no physiological indicator of pain was used. The sample size used in this study was small. Further studies with a larger population and different concentrations of peppermint oil are recommended. The mechanism of action of peppermint oil as a topical anesthetic should also be further studied.

Conclusion

The findings of this randomized controlled trial suggest that peppermint oil is a highly effective topical anesthetic agent for reducing pain perception prior to intraoral LA injections in children. Compared to lignocaine gel and ice, peppermint oil demonstrated the lowest pain scores on both objective (SEM) and subjective (VAS) assessments. This indicates that peppermint oil not only reduces the physiological pain response but also minimizes the child's perceived pain experience.

Clinical Significance

Pain and anticipatory anxiety can make children uncooperative during dental procedures. The pleasant aroma and cooling effect of peppermint oil may provide a soothing sensation, potentially reducing fear and discomfort before the injection. This can lead to improved patient cooperation, making dental procedures easier for both dentists and young patients. While lignocaine gel is commonly used in dental practice, combining it with peppermint oil could enhance its anesthetic effect due to peppermint oil's cooling and desensitizing properties. Future research could explore combination therapy for enhanced pain control.
