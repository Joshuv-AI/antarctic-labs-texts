Inhaled Levodopa for the Management of OFF Episodes in Parkinson’s Disease: A Cost-Effectiveness Analysis
Authors: Daniel Evans, Chetan Mistry, Harun Knight, Rushab Shah, Leandro Guerra-Primo, Hanne C Gadeberg, Stefano Perni, Wendy Clarke, Julie Brown, Georg-Alexander Pietsch, Ubong Silas, Camille Carroll
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionOFF episodes in Parkinson’s disease (PD), where symptoms worsen despite symptomatic treatment, are associated with significant burden on patients, carers and healthcare providers. There is an unmet need for an effective, on-demand treatment for OFF episodes that provides fast-acting symptom relief. This study estimated the cost-effectiveness of inhaled levodopa (LD) compared with relevant alternatives from a UK National Health Service and Personal Social Services perspective.MethodsA Markov model was developed comparing inhaled LD with subcutaneous (SC) apomorphine, sublingual (SL) apomorphine, dispersible LD and no on-demand treatment (no-ODT) in adults with advanced PD treated with LD and carbidopa and experiencing OFF episodes. A lifetime time horizon was adopted. Within-trial and beyond-trial models were applied for initial treatment and subsequent therapy, respectively. Model structure comprised 12 health states: ten ‘Off’ states, an ‘On’ state and death. Clinical trials and a network meta-analysis informed clinical inputs, with natural history progression applied from year 3. UK national databases and published literature informed costs (2025) and utility data. A 3.5% yearly discount rate is applied to costs and outcomes. Sensitivity analyses and three scenarios exploring alternative progression through health states, caregiver costs and disutilities, as well as subsequent therapy adverse events, were conducted.ResultsIn base-case analysis, inhaled LD dominated versus dispersible LD and no-ODT and was associated with lower cost and lower quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) versus SC and SL apomorphine. Nevertheless, inhaled LD resulted in net monetary benefit (NMB) of £14,226 and £25,939 compared with SC and SL apomorphine, respectively, at a £25,000 willingness-to-pay threshold. Key model drivers were subsequent therapy costs, discontinuation rates and health state utility values. The probabilistic sensitivity analysis highlighted uncertainty in the model; however, mean probabilistic sensitivity analysis results, along with two scenario analyses, remained consistent with the base case.ConclusionsInhaled LD may be an economically dominant treatment strategy when compared with dispersible LD and no-ODT. When compared with SC and SL apomorphine, cost-savings with inhaled LD may outweigh potentially fewer QALYs, resulting in an NMB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40273-026-01642-z.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the most prevalent neurodegenerative movement disorder, affecting between 108 and 257 per 100,000 individuals in Europe [1] and 240 per 100,000 in the UK [2]. PD is characterised by motor symptoms, including bradykinesia, tremors, rigidity and postural instability [1, 3]. As PD progresses, patients experience fluctuations in treatment response, alternating between periods of good symptom control, known as ON episodes, and periods where symptoms return, known as OFF episodes. OFF episodes are experienced by nearly all patients with PD and are associated with significant burden on the patient, impacting daily activities and decreasing quality of life (QoL), while also increasing caregiver and healthcare burden [3–7]. Research suggests that for every additional hour of daily OFF time, there is a decrease of 0.02 in the EuroQol 5-Dimension (EQ-5D) utility index and a decrease of 2.5 in the EQ-5D visual analogue scale (VAS), indicating deteriorating health status with increasing hours of daily OFF time [8]. Findings from a study conducted in the UK showed that there was substantial financial burden on those living with PD, with incurred costs of £5,851 and potential losses of income/benefits of £10,731 annually, meaning that a household in the UK in which a person has PD is £16,582 per year worse off (2016 cost year; prices inclusive of patient and caregiver costs) [9]. Further to this, patients with OFF episodes have significantly higher healthcare resource utilisation and healthcare costs than patients without OFF episodes [10, 11]. A US-based study found that the number of hospitalisations, intensive care admissions, emergency room visits, nights hospitalised, costs of consultations/hospitalisations and total direct costs were all higher for patients experiencing OFF episodes (p < 0.05) versus those without OFF episodes; however, it should be noted that patients with OFF episodes are likely to have more advanced disease, which may also contribute to increased admissions, visits and costs [12].

Over time, the effectiveness of long-term maintenance treatments, such as combination levodopa (LD)/carbidopa (CD), diminishes, resulting in the re-emergence of Parkinson’s symptoms and an increased number of OFF episodes. Current on-demand treatments for OFF episodes primarily include apomorphine, which is available as subcutaneous (SC) injection or sublingual (SL) film, and dispersible LD formulations. SC apomorphine has demonstrated improvements in Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) Part III scores that are typically seen within 10–15 min and last for 45–60 min [13, 14]. Apomorphine has additionally demonstrated a reduction in proportion of patients experiencing OFF episodes [14] and an improvement in non-motor Parkinson’s-associated symptoms [15]; however, treatment is associated with frequent adverse events (AEs) and subsequent discontinuation [14, 16–21]. Many patients prefer non-injectable options; however, apomorphine is ineffective if swallowed. Apomorphine sublingual film allows for systemic absorption via the oral mucosa, avoiding the first-pass metabolism in the gastrointestinal system; however, it is approximately seven times less bioavailable when compared with SC apomorphine at similar levels of exposure [13]. Dispersible LD, a fast-acting LD formulation, offers some benefit but is associated with erratic absorption and limitations around bioavailability [22, 23]. Studies have shown that gastroparesis, which is common in PD due to autonomic dysfunction, delays gastric emptying and disrupts LD absorption, while composition of meals, such as high levels of protein, can also reduce LD absorption [24–26].

Both SC and SL apomorphine require titration at treatment initiation to determine the most effective and tolerable dose. This process requires medical supervision in a clinic setting, resulting in substantial healthcare resource use and cost [27–31]. Coupled with high discontinuation rates, patient reluctance and patient preference, this can be a barrier to use of apomorphine in practice [14, 32–36].

Inhaled LD is an alternative treatment for OFF episodes, delivered via the pulmonary route using an inhaler provided with the product, allowing rapid absorption of LD into the systemic circulation [37]. In the phase 3 SPAN-PD trial, inhaled LD demonstrated statistically significant and clinically meaningful benefits versus placebo, including improved motor function measured by UPDRS Part III scores, and patient-reported improvement in Parkinson’s disease symptoms [38]. Improvements in UPDRS Part III scores were rapid and sustained, with greater reductions compared with placebo at 10 min, which reached statistical significance at 30 min and was maintained for at least 60 min [38]. Inhaled LD also has an acceptable safety and tolerability profile [38], using the same active ingredient as background LD/CD treatment.

To assess the economic value of inhaled LD, a de novo cost-effectiveness model (CEM) was developed from a UK National Health Service (NHS) and Personal Social Services (PSS) perspective to estimate the total costs and quality-adjusted life years (QALY) associated with inhaled LD compared with relevant treatment alternatives.

Methodology

Model Overview and Patient Population

A Markov model was developed in Microsoft Excel considering adult patients with advanced PD receiving LD/CD who were eligible for on-demand medication for the treatment of OFF episodes in line with the licensed indication for inhaled LD. Standard of care (SoC) was defined as SC apomorphine, SL apomorphine, dispersible LD, and no on-demand treatment (ODT) in line with availability of on-demand therapies in the UK.

All costs and quality-of-life (QoL) differences between inhaled LD and SoC were captured over a lifetime time horizon, calculated as 100 years minus the baseline age observed in the SPAN-PD trial (62.9 years) [38]. Cycle lengths for the first year were 4, 8, 12 and 16 weeks to align with the timepoints of the outcomes reported in the SPAN-PD trial. Following the first year, a 3-month cycle length was adopted. Cost and outcomes were discounted at 3.5% per annum, in line with the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) reference case [39]. Results are presented as net monetary benefit (NMB) using a threshold of £25,000, in line with the NICE manual [40].

Model Structure

The model consisted of 12 health states associated with 1-h increments of reduced motor function during a 24-h time period, replicating the structure used in the NICE assessment of foslevodopa–foscarbidopa (TA934) [41]. The ‘On’ state represented the patient experiencing adequate symptom control with no significant OFF episodes. ‘Off1’ represented patients experiencing up to 1 h of OFF time per day. This increased in 1-h increments to ‘Off10+’, which pooled patients experiencing more than 9 h of OFF time. An absorbing state for death was also included. Patients could transition between any health states while on treatment (Fig. 1). Following treatment discontinuation, patients moved to subsequent treatment and received a one-off improvement in OFF time based on the available subsequent therapies. Clinical efficacy was assessed by the reduction in OFF time for all interventions. As no head-to-head data were identified, a network meta-analysis (NMA) was used to estimate the relative reduction in OFF time [44]. For inhaled LD, change in OFF time was −0.633 h/day, and for SC apomorphine −2.448 h/day [44]. Owing to limited data availability, SL apomorphine was assumed to have same efficacy as SC apomorphine, given that they have the same mechanism of action.

Transition probabilities between health states for the first 52 weeks of the model for inhaled LD were informed by individual patient data sourced from the SPAN-PD clinical trial, mapping patients transitions through the health states [38]. Treatment effects for SC apomorphine and SL apomorphine were estimated using a relative change in OFF time compared with inhaled LD, derived from the NMA. Relative change in OFF time was calculated by dividing the difference in OFF time between inhaled LD and the comparator of interest by the change in OFF time for inhaled LD. The relative change and directionality between inhaled LD and the comparator of interest were applied to the transition probabilities for inhaled LD to estimate the transition probabilities for that comparator. For example, a 20% probability that patients transition from ‘Off4’ to ‘Off3’ over a given cycle will mean that patients receiving SC apomorphine will have a 17.4% probability of patients transitioning from ‘Off4’ to ‘Off1’ and 2.6% probability of transitioning from ‘Off4’ to ‘Off2’, given a relative change of −2.87 h.

The efficacy of no-ODT was assumed to be equal to the natural history (NH) of PD informed by constant transition probabilities derived from an exponential model fitted to OFF-time data from Palmer et al. 2002 (Supplementary Table 1) [45]. The efficacy for dispersible LD was estimated as 20% slower progression in OFF time compared with the NH of PD, based on dispersible LD showing a 11.72-min reduction in time from administration to ON state compared with immediate release LD/CD [46]. The change OFF time for dispersible LD could be estimated as −0.08 h/day on the basis of a 40% probability of patients in ‘Off1’ progressing to ‘Off2’ over a 3-month cycle (Supplementary Table 1).

As clinical trial data were only available for 52 weeks, long-term extrapolations of clinical efficacy were applied for inhaled LD, SC apomorphine and SL apomorphine. For years 1–3, last observation carried forward (LOCF) was used, meaning patients remained in their last observed health state until treatment discontinuation or death. Following year 3, patients were assumed to progress in line with the NH of PD, using the same approach used to estimate no-ODT efficacy. As dispersible LD and no-ODT efficacy was informed by the NH of PD, no long-term extrapolations were applied for these interventions, and the model instead followed the efficacy estimations described above for the duration of the modelled time horizon.

Treatment discontinuation rates were sourced from respective clinical trials and pooled where more than one trial was available (Table 2). Treatment discontinuation was based on withdrawal by subject, AE or lack of efficacy. Long- and short-term AE rates were sourced from respective clinical trials (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Upon treatment discontinuation, patients received subsequent therapy, including device-aided therapies (DATs), or no-DAT (only for patients not suitable for DATs). The distribution of subsequent therapy was informed by clinical opinion of a practicing PD expert, with 15% of patients receiving continuous SC apomorphine infusion, 5% receiving duodopa, 29% receiving produodopa, 17% receiving deep brain stimulation (DBS) and 34% of patients being ineligible for treatment with a DAT.

Patients receiving no-ODT could also progress onto subsequent therapy; the rate at which patients progress onto more advanced therapies was informed by the DISCOVER study, which assessed the safety and efficacy of duodopa [50]. It was estimated that all patients would be on advanced therapies following 18.7 years from PD diagnosis based on the mean and standard deviation time since PD diagnosis reported in the baseline characteristics. Efficacy of each subsequent therapy was modelled by a one-off improvement in OFF time based on the pivotal clinical trial for the appropriate intervention (Supplementary Table 4).

A PD-specific mortality rate ratio, sourced from a large UK cohort study, was applied to age- and sex-adjusted general population mortality from UK life tables (2022–2024) to capture excess mortality associated with PD [42, 51]. In the base case, 8 years since diagnosis was assumed to align with the SPAN-PD trial baseline demographics. Mortality estimates for year 10 were assumed to remain constant for the remainder of the modelled time horizon.

Cost and Resource Use

Treatment acquisition cost for inhaled LD was set as £413.95 per pack, containing 92 capsules, resulting in a cost of £4.50 per capsule, and treatment acquisition costs for SC apomorphine and dispersible LD were sourced from the British National Formulary (BNF) [52]. SL apomorphine was assumed to have the same treatment acquisition cost as SC apomorphine owing to limited data availability. Treatment administration frequency (Supplementary Table 5) for inhaled LD was informed by SPAN-PD [38]. For SC apomorphine and SL apomorphine, the average number of treatment administrations reported in respective clinical trials were distributed across health states in line with SPAN-PD [17, 53]. For dispersible LD, three administrations per day across all health states was used. No treatment administration costs were applied as all interventions can be self-administered. Sensitivity Analysis

One-way sensitivity analyses (OWSA) were performed to explore the uncertainty of individual parameters on results. The OWSA was conducted by allocating a ‘low’ value and a ‘high’ value to each parameter. In the absence of confidence interval data, the standard error was assumed to be 20% of the mean for all variables, as aligned with common practice.

Additionally probabilistic sensitivity analyses with 1000 iterations were performed to assess the combined uncertainty of all inputs on results. The beta distribution was used to vary parameters that needed to remain bounded between 0 and 1 (i.e. proportions). The gamma distribution was used to vary all other remaining parameters. Parameters and their respective distributions can be found in Supplementary File 2.

Scenario Analysis

Three scenarios were explored to assess the impact on results. The first assumed that, following year 3, all patients remained in their last observed health state until discontinuing treatment, rather than following the NH of PD. The second included caregiver disutilities and costs. Caregiver disutilities were sourced from Findley et al. [11]. Caregiver costs were estimated from the average hourly wage from the ONS average salary and the number of informal caregiving hours per week for each health state from Findley et al. [11, 63].

Model Validation

The model underwent thorough internal validation. The model was developed internally by two health economists and checked for accuracy by two other health economists who were not involved in its development. The technical quality control review followed an internal checklist, based on the principles of the TechVer checklist and the AdViSHE framework, ensuring a structured approach to assessing the model’s technical verification and conceptual validity. The internal validation included: ease-of-use checks to ensure the model was transparent and user-friendly, review of all calculations and macros to ensure accuracy, face validity checks that the model calculations and formulae were consistent and accurate, full input quality check ensuring all model inputs were accurately sourced and referenced, model results tests to check that varying model inputs had the anticipated effect on results and for internal consistency of model outputs and re-running sensitivity analysis and scenario analyses.

Results

Base-Case Results

Total costs, QALYs and incremental cost per QALY gained for inhaled LD versus each comparator are presented in Table 6. In the base-case analysis, inhaled LD was associated with a total cost of £136,129, the lowest total cost of all the comparators considered, and total QALY benefit of 6.979.

Inhaled LD was associated with fewer costs (−£18,305 and −£5,029) and greater QALY gains (0.082 and 0.097) compared with dispersible LD and no-ODT respectively, highlighting that inhaled LD dominated these interventions. When compared with SC apomorphine and SL apomorphine, inhaled LD was associated with lower costs of −£16,359 and −£27,410, respectively, and lower QALY gains of −0.085 and −0.059, respectively, and therefore was in the south-western quadrant of the incremental cost-effectiveness plane.

The model demonstrated no mortality benefit for any of the interventions assessed, with life years gained (LYG) equal across interventions (Table 6). Results of the probabilistic sensitivity analysis were consistent with the base case (Table 7; stepwise analysis Supplementary Table 14). The probability of cost-effectiveness of each intervention compared with inhaled LD is shown in Fig. 3 (stepwise analysis Supplementary Fig. 1). The shapes of the curves are relatively flat with no crossover between interventions, suggesting that the results are not very sensitive to different willingness-to-pay thresholds. This can be explained by relatively small differences in incremental QALYs gained between interventions. At a willingness-to-pay threshold of £25,000, the probability that inhaled LD was cost-effective compared with SC apomorphine, SL apomorphine, dispersible LD and no-ODT was 64%, 85%, 77% and 57%, respectively. The 1000 simulations for each intervention spanned all four quadrants of the incremental cost-effectiveness planes (Fig. 4), predominantly driven by small incremental differences in QALY gains between interventions, suggesting high levels of uncertainty. Discussion

Inhaled LD is associated with cost-savings over a lifetime horizon when compared with current SoC on-demand treatments for intermittent OFF episodes in patients with advanced PD. Inhaled LD was also associated with greater QALY gains when compared with no-ODT and dispersible LD, resulting in inhaled LD being a dominating treatment strategy for these patients; in other words, inhaled LD is a more efficient allocation of resources for the on-demand treatment of OFF episodes compared with dispersible LD and no-ODT. When compared with SC apomorphine and SL apomorphine, there are minimal QALY losses, resulting in an NMB to the healthcare system of £14,226 and £25,939 at a £25,000 willingness-to-pay threshold, respectively.

The cost-savings associated with inhaled LD were primarily driven by patients remaining on inhaled LD for longer than other interventions, delaying their transition to more costly subsequent therapies. This was highlighted in the one-way sensitivity analysis where cost of subsequent therapy and comparator discontinuation rates were identified as key model drivers. Costs of subsequent therapies were sourced from the BNF database and previous NICE submissions and therefore reflect current prices.

Discontinuation rates influence when patients move onto subsequent therapy within the model, with higher rates resulting in earlier transitions and increased costs. Discontinuation rates for all interventions were sourced from respective clinical trials, and it should be noted that rates for all the comparators were higher than for inhaled LD.

This is in line with published data for apomorphine and dispersible LD. For apomorphine, high discontinuation has been attributed to challenges around dose titration and high rates of AEs. Apomorphine requires careful dose titration to determine the most appropriate dose, balancing efficacy and tolerability. This needs close medical supervision and can be time-consuming. AEs associated with apomorphine include nausea, vomiting, orthostatic hypotension, somnolence and dyskinesia, all of which are frequently reported reasons for discontinuation of SC and SL apomorphine in clinical trials and real-world studies, in addition to injection-site reactions for SC apomorphine [14, 16–21]. For dispersible LD, values for treatment discontinuation are based on limited available data [46, 64]. Reasons for high treatment discontinuation are not clear, though one study reported that 66% of discontinuations were due to AEs, with nausea, the most frequent AE, leading to discontinuation [64]. Note that Chang et al. 2025 was used in the model, as this reported the most conservative treatment discontinuation with dispersible LD; however, this study was associated with limitations, as no reasons for discontinuation were given, and the study duration was only 4 weeks [46].

While not highlighted in the sensitivity analysis as a driver of the model, the costs of treatment initiation for inhaled LD were lower than for SC and SL apomorphine. This is primarily due to high resource costs associated with additional tests and the titration period required to determine the most effective and tolerable dose of apomorphine, as well as additional ECG and blood tests required if domperidone is used to manage nausea during apomorphine initiation [13, 27–31, 65]. In contrast, inhaled LD only requires a short training on using the inhalation device [13]. These costs do not have a substantial impact on the outcomes of the model; however, from a clinical and societal perspective, these may impact the choice of treatment in practice.

The probabilistic sensitivity analysis results were consistent with the base-case findings across all comparisons, demonstrating the robustness of the model. The analysis did, however, highlight substantial uncertainty within the results, with simulations spanning all four quadrants of the cost-effectiveness plane. For decision‑makers, this implies a finely balanced decision, where adoption may generate net health gains on average but carries a risk of opportunity cost if true effects are less favourable. Additionally, there is limited sensitivity of different willingness-to-pay thresholds, meaning that decision-makers should take into consideration other clinical considerations, such as safety profiles and treatment administration of the interventions.

Incremental QALYs were 0.085 and 0.059, for SC apomorphine and SL apomorphine versus inhaled LD respectively, highlighting that absolute QALY loss with inhaled LD was low, while cost-savings were £16,359 and £27,410 for inhaled LD versus SC apomorphine and SL apomorphine, respectively. In this situation, decision-makers must make a trade-off between costs saved and QALYs lost, which may be difficult to interpret. A net monetary benefit approach may be more appropriate to assess the cost-effectiveness of treatment. Inhaled LD resulted in net monetary benefit of £14,226 and £25,939 when compared with SC and SL apomorphine, respectively, at a willingness-to-pay threshold of £25,000. This suggests that at a £25,000 per QALY willingness-to-pay threshold, the cost-savings associated with inhaled LD outweigh the health benefits foregone relative to SC apomorphine and SL apomorphine. The low QALY loss for inhaled LD versus SC and SL apomorphine also highlights that efficacy and impact on quality of life are similar for these treatments, which aligns with the NMA conducted, showing that inhaled LD is non-inferior to SC and SL apomorphine [44]. Additionally at a £25,000 willingness-to-pay threshold, there is a modest 64% and 86% probability that inhaled LD is cost-effective compared with SC apomorphine and SL apomorphine, respectively.

Decision-makers should also consider the safety profiles and administration of the available treatments. As noted above, apomorphine is associated with a number of AEs, which can become severe and lead to treatment discontinuation [14, 16–21], while inhaled LD has a similar safety profile to the background LD/CD treatment that patients will already be receiving [66]. SC injections are usually less favourable than oral, sublingual or inhaled means of administration. This has been highlighted in a study looking at prescriber-reported patient preferences, where 93% of prescribers reported patient reluctance to use SC apomorphine, primarily owing to the need for self-injection [32].

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that inhaled LD may be considered as an on-demand treatment option for patients experiencing impactful OFF episodes. The small differences in QALYs relative to apomorphine, together with lower costs and a less complex mode of administration, indicate that inhaled LD could be appropriate for some patients such as those that have pre-existing apomorphine cautions and contraindications, including a history of cardiovascular disease, orthostatic hypotension or susceptibility to QT interval prolongation, who would not be considered suitable for apomorphine [27]. In addition, inhaled LD may be more suitable for those who have discontinued apomorphine owing to tolerability issues or those who have needle phobia or ECG concerns [32, 36, 65, 67]. However, apomorphine is likely to remain more suitable for those with more severe or refractory OFF episodes or those who may have difficulties using an inhalation device. Inhaled LD and apomorphine should be included in balanced discussion and shared decision-making between clinicians and patients. Overall, the availability of inhaled LD may broaden access to on-demand treatment while allowing treatment choice to be aligned with clinical need and efficient allocation of healthcare resource use.

Limitations of the model focus around the absence of data for some model inputs. Clinical efficacy for SC apomorphine efficacy was based on a clinical trial data for apomorphine infusion, while dispersible LD was assumed to have the same efficacy as inhaled LD, and SL apomorphine was assumed to have the same efficacy as SC apomorphine, owing to the lack of clinical trial data. When clinical trial data were available, most had a duration of only 1 year, and therefore extrapolation assumptions were applied.

In addition, no head-to-head trial data were available for the efficacy and safety of comparators relative to inhaled LD. Efficacy data from an NMA were used, potentially adding a source of uncertainty to the model; however, NMA is a robust methodology that is well-accepted by the community, including NICE.

With these limitations in mind, the CEM presents a reasonable estimate of the cost-effectiveness of inhaled LD for the on-demand treatment of OFF episodes in patients with advanced PD treated with LD/CD from a UK NHS and PSS perspective.

Conclusions

OFF episodes experienced by patients with advanced PD precipitate an increase in symptom burden, worsening QoL and reducing the ability to complete activities of daily living [5, 6]. Patients experiencing OFF episodes incur higher direct and indirect costs compared with those not experiencing them, along with an increased number of hospitalisations, intensive care admissions, emergency room visits and days spent hospitalised [8].

In the cost-effectiveness model base case, inhaled LD is the dominant treatment strategy when compared with dispersible LD and no-ODT, with lower associated costs and greater QALY gains over a lifetime horizon. Inhaled LD is associated with fewer costs and fewer QALY gains over a lifetime horizon when compared with SC apomorphine and SL apomorphine; however, the cost-savings of inhaled LD outweigh the fewer QALYs, resulting in an NMB of £14,226 and £25,939 at a £25,000 willingness-to-pay threshold, respectively. Sensitivity analyses demonstrated high levels of uncertainty around the results, particularly versus no-ODT, meaning the finding should be taken with a level of caution.
