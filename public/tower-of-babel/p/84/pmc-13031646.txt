Epidemiology of refractive errors and its associated factors among medical students
Authors: Alo Edin, Salah Mohammed, Ashenafi Abreha, Chalachew Muche, Elias Abera, Halima Ayalew Kebede, Asqual Gebreslassie Gebremariam, Angefa Ayele
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Refractive error is the leading cause of correctable visual impairment and preventable blindness worldwide. It is a growing public health concern, particularly among the younger population, including medical students, who represent a vital future workforce for developing countries like Ethiopia. Poor ocular health can negatively impact academic performance, productivity, and overall quality of life, further contributing to the economic burden. Despite the increasing prevalence of REs globally, there is a lack of evidence regarding their prevalence and associated factors among medical students in Ethiopia. Therefore, this study was aimed to assess the prevalence and factors associated with refractive error among medical students. An institutional based cross sectional study design using simple random sampling technique was employed to select 422 undergraduate medical students at Hawassa University College of Medicine and Health Sciences after proportional allocation done for five batches. The study was conducted from September 2023 to October 2023. The data were collected using semi –structured questionnaires and clinical examination check list adapted from previous studies. The collected data were entered by kobo tool and exported and analyzed with Statistical Packages for the Social Science version 26.Variables with p-value < 0.05 in multivariable logistic regression were considered as statistically significant. A total of 417 participants with a response rate of 98.8% were involved in this study. Prevalence of refractive error was 10.3% (95%CI: 7.2%-13.4%). In multivariable logistic regression analysis, lack of outdoor activity [AOR = 2.382(95% CI (1.084–5.234)], no Active rest [AOR = 2.442(95% CI 1.064–5.608)] previous eye operation [AOR = 4.382 (95% CI (1.781–10.784)] and eye trauma (AOR: 7.188; 95%CI: 2.178–23.724)] were significantly associated with refractive error. The study underscores a remarkable prevalence of refractive errors among medical students, consistent with findings from comparable settings and populations. The significant links identified between refractive errors and modifiable lifestyle factors such as reduced outdoor activity, lack of active breaks, prior ocular surgeries, and eye trauma emphasize the influence of demanding academic schedules and limited environmental exposure on visual health. These insights call for proactive screening and the implementation of targeted preventive measures to mitigate refractive error risks. Addressing these factors is critical for preserving the ocular well-being and academic efficacy of medical students.Keywords: Refractive, Prevalence, Error, Factors, Medical, StudentsSubject terms: Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Refractive errors (REs) are conditions in which the optics of the non-accommodating eye cannot focus parallel light rays on the retina. The three most common types of refractive errors (REs) are myopia (nearsightedness), hypermetropia (farsightedness), and astigmatism, which lead to blurred vision as the refractive power of the eye varies across different meridians, making it challenging for the ocular system to create a sharply focused image on the retina1. According to recent studies and World Health Organization (WHO) reports, REs are the leading cause of visual impairment and the second leading cause of visual loss worldwide, accounting for 43% of all visual impairments attributed to REs2. The rising prevalence rate is alarming, usually requiring preventive measures to deal with its progression and potential sight-threatening complications.

Refractive errors are the second most common cause of blindness and the most common cause of vision impairment worldwide3. It is estimated that more than 108 million people worldwide suffer from uncorrected refractive error. A global systematic review reported that in 2010, uncorrected refractive errors accounted for visual impairment in approximately 101.2 million individuals (20%) and caused blindness in approximately 6.8 million individuals (21%)4.

Refractive errors are a leading cause of vision impairment worldwide and a common reason for ophthalmological visits3, particularly affecting academically active populations, such as medical students4. These students are at a higher risk due to prolonged near work, extensive use of digital devices, limited physical activity, and academic stress, all of which may contribute to the development or worsening of refractive errors5,6. Poor vision among medical students can impair their academic performance, reduce productivity, and compromise future professional competence5,6. Given the increasing prevalence of refractive errors and their impact on quality of life and economic productivity, early detection through effective screening is critical. Although global initiatives such as WHO’s Vision 2020 emphasize refractive error prevention7,8, there are limited data on the prevalence and determinants of these errors among Ethiopian medical students. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and factors associated with refractive errors among medical students in the study area.

Methods and materials

Study settings

The study was carried out at Hawassa University College of Medicine and Health Sciences (HUCMHS) in Hawassa City, Sidama Regional State, Southern Ethiopia. The study involved undergraduate medical students and took place from September 2023 to October 2023. Hawassa, situated on the shores of Lake Hawassa in the Great Rift Valley, is located 273 km (170 miles) south of Addis Ababa. HUCMHS, one of the university’s five colleges, offers academic and community services through its specialized hospital, academic schools, and departments. The College encompasses 34 programs, including first degree programs, master’s degree programs, specialty programs, and a PhD program, all housed within the faculties of health sciences and medicine.

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted.

Population

All undergraduate medical students enrolled at Hawassa University College of Medicine and Health Sciences during the study period were included in this study. However, undergraduate medical students who were seriously ill during data collection or those for whom it was difficult to assess refractive error, such as students with ocular media opacity were excluded from the study.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample size was calculated by using Epi Info version 7.0 software considering the following assumptions: 5% level of significance, power of 80% and assuming the prevalence (p) of refractive errors as 50% for representation of the whole population.

Where: n= minimum sample size required for study, p= estimated prevalence from previous study, Z α/2 = critical value at 95% confidence level of certainty, d= margin of error 5%. By considering 10% non-response rate, the final sample size for this study was 422 undergraduate medical students of HUCMHS.

All undergraduate medical students were considered eligible for the study, except those excluded due to severe illness or ocular opacity. From this pool, 422 participants were identified as the required sample size. The 422 participants were then distributed proportionally across academic years (e.g., Year I–V) based on each year’s enrollment size. Subsequently, participants from each year were randomly selected using simple random sampling. Data procedure and ophthalmic assessment

The data collection process employed multiple tools and methods to ensure thorough and accurate information gathering. Drawing from prior research conducted internationally, the data collection instruments were refined and adapted accordingly. Data sources included a pre-tested structured checklist, a semi-structured self-administered questionnaire, and a clinical checklist encompassing eye examinations conducted at an ophthalmology clinic.

After completing the interview, each participant underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination and refraction assessment. Optometrists initiated the ophthalmic evaluation with a visual acuity (VA) test. Both monocular and binocular unaided VA, as well as VA following refractive correction, were measured using reduced Snellen acuity charts at a distance of 3 m under standard lighting conditions. If participants were unable to identify letters at 3 m, the testing distance was progressively shortened. When participants could not perceive letters at 1 m, VA was assessed through alternative methods including counting fingers, hand motion detection, light perception, and no light perception.

Subsequent to recording VA, a torch examination was performed to detect any corneal opacity, cataracts, or presence of pseudophakia/aphakia.

For objective refraction, a semi-dark room was arranged within the participant’s home to facilitate static retinoscopy. Streak retinoscopy was conducted for each participant, and the results were refined using monocular subjective refraction. The subjective refraction findings were documented for each eye, and the spherical equivalent was calculated based on these measurements. Participants with a spherical equivalent greater than + 0.50 diopters or less than − 0.50 diopters in either eye were classified as having refractive error (RE).

Data quality assurance

Prior to commencing data collection, the data collectors took a comprehensive one-day training session on the data collection tools. The principal investigator and supervisor closely monitored the data collection process on a daily basis to ensure data completeness and accuracy, offering regular feedback to the data collectors. To maintain data integrity, a pre-test was conducted on a small subset (5%) of the sample size at Negelle Arsi General Hospital and Medical College before the actual data collection period. This preemptive measure aimed to enhance the overall quality of the collected data.

Data analysis and processing

The collected data underwent rigorous checks for consistency and completeness before being entered into the Kobo Toolbox. Subsequently, the data was downloaded from the Kobo Toolbox user server, exported to Excel, and then imported into SPSS Version 26 for further analysis.

Categorical variables were analyzed by computing frequencies and percentages, while a chi-squared test was conducted. In the analysis, bi-variable logistic regression was initially employed to select potential variables for multiple logistic regression. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, with a p-value of 0.166.

To assess multicollinearity, the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and tolerance tests were conducted to examine correlations between independent variables and no problem of multicollinearity was detected. Variables with a bi-variable logistic regression p-value of less than 0.25 were considered for inclusion in the multi-variable logistic regression analysis. This analysis aimed to identify independent predictors of Refractive error, with statistical significance set at a p-value of less than 0.05.

Discussion

This study found a refractive errors prevalence of 10.3% (95% CI: 7.2–13.4) among medical students, aligning closely with reports from the University of Gondar (12.3%)13, and South Ethiopia (12.3%)14. These similarities likely stem from shared demographic profiles, environmental exposures, and intense academic demands typical of Ethiopian medical students. Comparable study methodologies and diagnostic criteria further support the consistency of these prevalence estimates, indicating a similar burden across these populations.

It is higher than the national prevalence of 7.36% reported for Ethiopian school children15,16and in Southern Nigeria (4.4%)17. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in study populations, as medical students are older and may have greater near-vision demands, leading to higher detection rates. Additionally, variations in sample size, diagnostic methods, and access to eye care services could also contribute to these differences. Environmental and genetic factors specific to the study groups might further explain the higher prevalence observed.

However, it is lower than the studies in Jordan (75%)18, New Delhi (62.9%)19, West Bengal (57.8%)20, Iraq (27.6%)21, Saudi Arabia22, Calabar Medical School23, Akhtar Saeed Medical and Dental College24, Nishtar Medical University Pakistan6and in Nepal25. These discrepancies may arise from differences in sample characteristics such as age distribution, study design, diagnostic criteria, and environmental or genetic factors influencing refractive error prevalence. Variability in the accessibility and utilization of eye care services, as well as differing academic and visual demands among medical student populations, might also contribute to the lower prevalence observed in our study compared to these other reports.

The present study highlights a significant association between the lack of active rest and an increased likelihood of refractive error among medical students, with those not engaging in active rest exhibiting twice the odds of refractive error compared to their peers who take regular breaks. This finding is consistent with previous studies from North India and Pakistan26,27, reinforcing the growing body of evidence that behavioral and lifestyle factors play a crucial role in the ocular health of medical students.

This study identified a significant association between lack of outdoor activity and the prevalence of refractive error among medical students. Odds of refractive error was 2 times higher among medical students who lack outdoor activity compared to their counterparts. This finding aligns with previous research conducted in Gonder town28, southern Ethiopia29, Saudi Arabia30, and Pakistan6, all of which reported similar associations between reduced outdoor exposure and increased odds of refractive error among students. The consistency of these results across diverse settings suggests that environmental and lifestyle factors particularly those related to academic demands and technological advancement play a critical role in the development of refractive errors in this population.

This study found that medical students with a history of previous eye operations were four times more likely to have refractive errors compared to those without such a history. This result is consistent with findings from European multicenter studies31 and study at the University of Colorado32, which also reported a significant association between prior ocular surgeries and subsequent refractive errors. Possible Explanations is could be that, the increased prevalence of refractive error among students with a history of eye operations may be attributed to the complexity and potential side effects of ocular procedures. Eye surgeries, even when successful, can alter the refractive status of the eye either temporarily or permanently, depending on the type and extent of the intervention. For example, surgical procedures for cataract, retinal disorders, or corrective surgeries for refractive error itself can induce changes in the corneal curvature, lens position, or axial length, all of which directly influence refractive outcomes.

This study found that medical students with a history of eye trauma were seven times odds of refractive error compared to those without such a history. This is consistent with the findings from studies in Turkey33and Florida34, as well as a review highlighting ocular trauma as a significant cause of preventable vision loss. Possible Explanations could be, eye trauma can lead to both immediate and delayed changes in vision. Mechanistically, trauma may cause: Alterations in corneal curvature, leading to astigmatism or irregular refractive surfaces, dislocation or subluxation of the lens, which changes the eye’s focusing power and changes in the axial length of the eye, potentially resulting in myopia or hyperopia. These structural changes can induce various types of refractive errors. In some cases, trauma-related refractive errors may be temporary, resolving as the eye heals. However, more severe injuries can result in permanent refractive changes if the anatomical disruption is significant or healing is incomplete.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Use of standardized clinical examination tools (Snellen chart, retinoscope, trial frame) by trained optometry professional’s ensured accurate diagnosis of refractive errors. Adaptation and pre-testing of the data collection tools, along with continuous supervision during data collection, improved data quality and consistency. However, cross-sectional design limits causal inference; associations cannot confirm temporality or directionality between risk factors and refractive errors. The study was conducted at a single institution, which may limit generalizability of results to medical students elsewhere or other university populations in Ethiopia. Reliance on self-reported data for behavioral factors (outdoor activity, active rest, visual device usage) may introduce recall or social desirability bias.

Conclusion

We found refractive errors in one in ten medical students in the study area, highlighting a notable burden of uncorrected vision issues among this group. The study also identified several factors that were significantly associated with refractive error. Specifically, we found that lack of active rest, less engagement in outdoor activities, previous eye operations, and history of eye trauma were all independently associated with a higher odds of refractive error.

These results underscore the importance of promoting healthy eye habits and regular eye examinations among medical students, a medical student that is at increased odds due to the intensive nature of their studies and long hours spent on near-work activities. Interventions targeting modifiable risk factors, such as encouraging regular breaks, outdoor activities, and prompt treatment of eye conditions, may help to mitigate the burden of refractive error in this population.

Future research should explore the longitudinal trends in refractive error development among medical students and investigate the potential impact of curriculum modifications or eye health promotion programs on the refractive errors.

Strengths and limitations

Combination of self-administered questionnaires and clinical ophthalmic examinations, including objective and subjective refraction, provided comprehensive and reliable data on refractive errors. Use of standardized and pre-tested data collection tools adapted from previous studies ensured consistency and comparability.

Cycloplegic refraction was not performed, which may have led to underestimation of hyperopia and potential misclassification of refractive status. Reliance on self-reported data for lifestyle factors such as outdoor activity and active rest introduces potential recall and social desirability biases. The study was conducted at a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other medical student populations or regions.
