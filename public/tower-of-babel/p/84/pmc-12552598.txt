Uncovering functional INDELs responsible for splice sites in sheep with different immune profiles naturally exposed to gastrointestinal nematode infection
Authors: Samla M F Cunha, Flavio S Schenkel, Niel A Karrow, Bonnie Mallard, Ángela Cánovas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastrointestinal nematodes (GINs) are responsible for high morbidity and mortality rates in the sheep industry. This study aimed to identify functional INDELs responsible for splice sites using RNA-Sequencing from the liver tissue of sheep with different innate immune responses after being naturally exposed to GIN infection. Two GIN-naïve groups of lambs with divergent cortisol levels (high stress responders - HSR and medium stress responders - MSR) were naturally exposed to GIN during grazing. The animals were euthanized, and six HSR and six MSR, in addition to four immunological control animals (GIN-unexposed), were selected for RNA sequencing from liver tissue. Variant INDELs were identified, and variant consequences analysis was performed to identify INDELs responsible for splice site (SS). A total of 34,168 and 39,380 INDELs were identified for the HSR and MSR, respectively. From those, 1,614 and 2,980 INDELs were responsible for possible SS. The location of the INDELs yielded a list of 514 and 855 genes for HSR and MSR, respectively. Among these genes, the STAT6 and CFI genes were identified. The CFI gene was previously identified as differentially expressed in resistant lines of sheep exposed to GIN, and the STAT6 gene appears to play an important role in the immune response to helminths. The Terminal pathway of complement was enriched (FDR > 0.05) and could be playing a role in building an innate immune response to GIN infection. This study identified novel INDELS responsible for possible SS in genes that could be modulating the immune response to GIN in sheep. Introduction

Gastrointestinal nematodes (GINs) are parasites that infect the gastrointestinal tract of humans and animals worldwide1. Sheep can become infected after grazing on contaminated pasture, and the clinical and economic importance of helminths will vary due to their level of pathogenicity and geographic location2. Infected animals can develop symptoms of parasitism, such as weight loss, diarrhea, hypoproteinemia, and, in extreme cases, death3. Economic losses can be expected, in a greater proportion, from a decrease in production and due to costs with treatments4,5. The over-reliance on available anthelmintics to treat infected animals has led to GIN resistance and increased concerns about residue in the food and environment5. The use of combined practices, such as genetics, to diminish the impact of parasitism in sheep production systems is fundamental6–8.

The genetic basis of the immune response to GIN infection is complex and likely polygenic8–10. Studies using different types of data and methodologies have been used to unravel the underlying genetic mechanisms responsible for the inflammatory response to GIN infection in sheep; however, little to no consensus has been reached so far11–15. One approach that can be used is exposing animals with different responses to acute inflammatory stress to GIN infection16. Animals with different acute inflammatory stress responses can be identified by the cortisol levels after a stimulus16. This happens because the immune system and the central nervous are interconnected through two major pathways, including the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis17,18. When the animal is stimulated by bacterial components such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), cytokines released in response to this stimulus will activate the HPA axis to secrete neuropeptides from the hypothalamus, which subsequently initiate the release of adrenocorticotropic hormone that will signal the release of adrenal cortisol16,17. In addition to helping identify different acute inflammatory stress responders, cortisol plays a crucial role in regulating inflammatory responses to ensure they are effective, but not excessive16–18. Using animals with different responses to acute inflammatory stress can facilitate the identification of differently expressed genes and metabolic pathways associated with the inflammatory response to GIN, allowing for in-depth study through the analysis of transcriptomic data at a high-throughput level. One method to analyze transcriptomic data is by identifying variants that might lead to functional consequences19. The identification of variants can help map causal mutations responsible for diseases and productivity traits and can also be used as molecular markers in genomic evaluations and genome-wide association studies to improve the accuracy of results20,21.

Splice sites (SSs) are regions responsible for signalling the specific place for cleavage of introns and are located at the boundary of exons and introns, also known as 5’ and 3’ splice sites22. Splicing mutations can lead to several impacts, like the creation and deactivation of SSs or the activation of cryptic SSs, which can disrupt the splicing process by skipping exons and including introns23,24. Splicing mutations can also impact splicing enhancers and silencers, preventing the binding of spliceosome elements25. Additionally, it can change the expression ratio between various messenger RNA (mRNA) isoforms and promote the production of different mRNA patterns26. In humans, studies have identified mutations in the SS region of genes that lead to different immune modulations. Bernard et al.27 described different examples of mutations located in SS region leading to alternative splicing, affecting immunotherapy response in cancer and components of the immune system, such as B cells and transcription factors. Lu et al.28 described various examples of mutations in immune-related genes, including a mutation in the 5’ splice site of exon 2 of the HMSD gene, which creates a new minor histocompatibility antigen affecting immune response. A similar mutation on exon 10 of the ERAP2 gene could also impact major histocompatibility antigen presentation28. While mutations responsible for splice site effect (SSE) are well-characterized in humans, in animals, they are not commonly characterized. This presents a novel approach in animal research that could enhance the understanding of the biology underlying health traits, as splicing events are especially important in complex systems, such as the immune system, where signalling and regulation are managed differently at various times, and a more refined system is needed29. Thus, this study aimed to (i) identify short insertions and deletions (INDELs) in coding regions potentially responsible for SSE in Rideau x Dorset crossbred sheep with different innate immune responses naturally exposed to GIN using transcriptomic data from liver tissue; (ii) predict the consequences of these functional INDELs on the transcripts; and (iii) describe possible genes and metabolic pathways that could be playing roles in the inflammatory response to GIN infection in sheep.

Results and discussion

This study aimed to identify INDELs responsible for SSE and predict their consequence in the protein sequence using RNA-Sequencing from Rideau x Dorset crossbred sheep with different immune profiles naturally exposed to GIN. Additionally, this study aimed to describe possible genes and metabolic pathways that could play a role in the inflammatory response to GIN infection.

Variant detection for high stress responder, medium stress responder, and GIN-unexposed groups

A total of 146,403 INDELs were identified. The number of INDELs unique for each group or shared among groups is described in Table 1. The total number of INDELs with functional consequences leading to amino acid change (AAC) and SSE were 8,248 and 9,420, respectively (Table 1). Changes in the sequence that do not change the amino acid are called silent mutations24. These mutations can interfere with the success of the splicing process, affecting the ability of exonic splicing enhancers to promote a correct splice process or direct the process to the appropriate position24. Unsuccessful splicing events can have an impact on the translational control, RNA splicing, processing rates, and protein production regulation. Silent mutations have been documented as the cause of human diseases24. Thus, INDELs responsible for SSE, regardless of whether they also led to amino acid change, were used for further analyses.

Most of the identified variants (99.9%) were classified as novel variants. This value is higher than the results found in the literature21,30. This could be explained by the type of variant detected, i.e. INDELs rather than single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), particularly those located in splice sites, the type of tissue used, which may alter the gene expression profile, and the version of the reference genome. The percentage of variants within each consequence class is described in Fig. 1. The top classes were splice region variant (26%), frameshift variant (24%), downstream gene variant (10%), splice donor variant (10%), upstream gene variant (9%), and intron variant (9%). Downstream and upstream gene variants are mutations happening after the 3’ and before the 5’ end of the gene, respectively. These consequences have a “modifier” impact since they are non-coding variants or happen in non-coding genes, making it hard to predict their impact, or evidence of their effect is not described. Similarly, intron variants are changes happening within an intron and have a modifier impact. Splice region variants are variants happening in the splice site region within 1 to 3 bases of the exon or 3 to 8 bases of the intron. This consequence is classified as “low” impact, meaning that it seems to be harmless or improbable to affect the final protein. The splice donor variant changes the two-base region at the 5’ end of an intron. Frameshift variant consequence will have the insertion or deletion of a number of variants that is not a multiple of three, leading to disruption in the translational reading frame. Splice donor and frameshift variants are classified as “high” impact, which indicates the variant is likely to disrupt the protein, leading to protein truncation, loss of function, or triggering nonsense-mediated decay. The definition of the consequences and their impact was based on the software description31.

Variant detection, gene annotation, and enriched metabolic pathways in the exposed versus GIN-unexposed groups

To better understand the impact of INDELs in each group, this study focused on the differences between the Exposed group (high stress responders - HSR and medium stress responders – MSR unique INDELs combined) versus the immunological control animals (GIN-Unexposed) to understand the specific genes and metabolic pathways related to the immune system between exposed and unexposed animals. After that, the results within the exposed group, HSR versus MSR, were compared to better understand the genes and metabolic pathways acting in animals with different immune profiles.

A total of 73,548 INDELs were identified for the Exposed group, from which 4,594 led to SSE. Except for two variants responsible for possible SSE, all other variants were confirmed as novel variants. The top consequence classes (Fig. 2) were splice region variant (28%), frameshift variant (25%), downstream gene variant (10%), splice donor variant (9%), upstream gene variant (8%), and intron variant (8%) and were described above. In the GIN-Unexposed group, 19,610 INDELs were identified, and 1,396 were responsible for SSE. Only one INDEL responsible for possible SSE in the GIN-Unexposed group was classified as an existing variant. The major consequence classes for the GIN-Unexposed INDELs (Fig. 2) were splice region variant (22%), frameshift variant (20%), splice donor variant (13%), intron variant (11%), and downstream gene variant (10%).The INDELs responsible for SSE identified in the Exposed and GIN-Unexposed groups yield 1,162 and 431 genes (Fig. 3), respectively. Functional analyses were performed for these lists of genes separately, and several enriched metabolic pathways (FDR < 0.05) (Supplementary Table S1) were identified. Metabolic pathways related to the immune system are summarized in Table 2. No enriched metabolic pathways associated with the immune system were identified for the GIN-Unexposed group, which could be expected since this group was not exposed to GIN infection, and the animals’ immune systems were not stimulated. However, the Exposed group had seven enriched metabolic pathways related to the immune system. Most of the metabolic pathways seem to be involved in Nuclear Factor Kappa B (NF-κB) activation and antigen cross-presentation. Furthermore, the “Neutrophil degranulation” metabolic pathway was identified.

Neutrophils are among the first cells to be recruited in the innate immune response. They may cooperate with other cells, such as mast cells and macrophages, within the affected tissue to enhance the initial inflammatory response32. During the degranulation process, neutrophils release granule-derived mediators that are stored in intracellular granules33 that can destroy microbes, damage tissues, and interfere with the neutrophils’ response to the environment. The function of neutrophils in nematode infection in sheep is not well established. According to Ajendra34, studies have shown that neutrophils play roles in killing nematodes and reducing the worm burden. Despite the “Neutrophil degranulation” pathway being enriched in the Exposed group, when this group was separated into HSR and MSR, this pathway was not enriched in the functional analyses (as shown next). The pathways related to NF-κB activation and antigen cross-presentation will be discussed later when HSR and MSR groups are compared.

Variant detection, gene annotation, and enriched metabolic pathways in the high stress responder versus medium stress responder groups

A total of 73,548 INDELs were identified for the HSR and MSR groups. Of those, 4,594 were responsible for SSE, of which 1,614 and 2,980 INDELs were identified for the HSR and MSR groups, respectively. In the HSR group, 99.9% of the variants were novel variants. The main consequences predicted for the HSR group were splice region variant (27%), frameshift variant (24%), downstream gene variant (11%), upstream gene variant (9%), splice donor variant (8%), and intron variant (8%). Only one INDEL identified in the MSR group was an existing variant. The principal consequence categories predicted for the MSR group were splice region variant (28%), frameshift variant (25%), downstream gene variant (10%), splice donor variant (10%), intron variant (8%), and upstream gene variant (8%). The list of INDELs yielded 514 and 855 associated genes, respectively (Fig. 5). Several significant metabolic pathways (FDR < 0.05) were identified as enriched for the HSR and MSR groups (Supplementary Table S2). However, significant metabolic pathways related to the immune system were only identified for the MSR group (Table 3). Considering that MSR animals would present a normal response in the face of stressors when compared to HSR animals, it is possible that more genes and metabolic pathways had to be activated to fight the natural infection promoted in our study, which led to the identification of more significant metabolic pathways for the medium group and more unique genes related to the immune system being also found in this group. The significant metabolic pathways enriched for the MSR group gene list were similar to pathways identified in the Exposed group. These pathways can also be categorized into two major groups: cross-antigen presentation and NF-κB related process.

The “Cross-presentation of soluble exogenous antigens (endosomes)”, “ER-Phagosome pathway”, and “Antigen processing-Cross presentation” metabolic pathways are related to the cross-presentation of antigens through different mechanisms: cross-presentation by dendritic cells (DC) using endosomes, by endoplasmic reticulum phagosome model, and by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) presenting exogenous antigens on Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) class 1 molecule to CD8 + T lymphocytes. Cross-presentation is a mechanism used by APCs when they encounter extracellular antigens and present them to MHC class I molecules35. This process will activate naïve CD8 + T cells into becoming cytotoxic Tc cells35. Among the APCs, DCs are the common cells playing this role in vivo35. According to Amigorena and Savina36, a lot of aspects of cross-presentation remain unclear, including the role of cross-presentation in the initiation of immune response and the molecular compartments involved in cross-presentation in DCs. During helminth infection in sheep, nematode antigens are presented by APCs to naïve T cells, leading to their differentiation into T helper (Th) cells or regulatory Treg (T) cells37,38. Since these pathways were significant in the MSR group, cross-presentation could be an alternative route for antigen presentation during nematode infection in sheep, increasing the effectiveness of the immune response.

The metabolic pathways related to NF-κB identified in this study are “TNFR2 non-canonical NF-kB pathway”, “Dectin-1 mediated noncanonical NF-kB signalling”, “NIK–> noncanonical NF-kB signalling”, “CLEC7A (Dectin-1) signalling”, “Downstream signalling events of B Cell Receptor (BCR)”, and “Activation of NF-kappaB in B cells”. The NF-κB plays several roles in the innate and adaptive immune response to pathogens and other physiological functions39. The importance of NF-κB in the immune response to GIN infection in sheep is not well described. However, in a Trichuris muris nematode infection study, knockout of different members of the NF-κB family resulted in different abilities to expel the infection or led to the development of chronic infection40. Mice deficient in c-Rel were capable of developing a Th-2 response capable of expelling the infection, and NF-κB1 and NF-κB2 knockout mice developed chronic infections with high concentrations of Interferon γ (IFN-γ)40. According to Bąska and Norbury39, other studies have shown the correlation between nematode infections and NF-κB functions, such as the regulation of macrophage-killing capacities. However, some parasites seem to be able to regulate NF-κB pathways, which implicates the modulation of the host immune response39.

The NF-κB can be activated through two main pathways: the canonical and non-canonical. The canonical pathway can be activated by several stimuli (e.g. pathogens and damage-associated molecular patterns – PAMPs and DAMPs, and receptors), and the non-canonical pathway can be activated by a specific set of receptors (e.g. Tumor Necrosis Factor Receptor 2 - TNFR2)39.

Some of the significant metabolic pathways identified in this study seem to be involved in activating the noncanonical NF-κB pathway. One of these pathways uses the TNFR2 to activate the non-canonical NF-κB signalling pathway. This receptor is expressed in immune and endothelial cells and seems to be responsible for activating the non-canonical NF-kB in T-cells and could be playing the same role in macrophages and DCs as well41. All three cells play important roles in the immune response during nematode infection38. After being triggered, the non-canonical NF-κB pathway starts. The NF-κB-inducing kinase (NIK), together with IKKα, activates the p100 kinase phosphorylation and processing into the p52 subunit that will bind to RelB to form a heterodimer and initiate gene transcription in the nucleus42,43. The non-canonical NF-kB pathway can also be activated by recognizing PAMPs by dendritic-cell-associated C-type lectin receptors (Dectin-1), which will trigger the receptor activation. Then, Dectin-1 will recruit and activate the spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK) that will activate NIK to initiate the non-canonical NF-κB pathway, as described earlier44,45. Dectin-1 seems to be an important receptor in the immune response against mucosal infections and initiating Th1 and Th17 responses against fungus45. It appears to play a crucial role in recognizing chitin, a PAMP that can be found in the eggshells of nematodes46. The recognition of this PAMP by Dectin-1 may lead to the initiation of inflammatory responses46. Ingham et al.47 compared the gene expression of susceptible and resistant sheep lines, identifying elevated expression of NF-κβ signalling pathway genes (IKBKB and NFKBIA), which indicated persistence in the inflammatory response in the susceptible line, in addition to a delayed response of regulatory markers47. The authors hypothesized that the susceptible line exhibited a delayed response due to reduced sensitivity, which may be attributed to a smaller number of Toll-like receptors (TLRs) or the ability of nematodes to manipulate the activation of TLRs. This study identified the IKBKB gene in the MSR group with a unique one-base insertion. This insertion can impact three of the four transcripts of this gene with a modifier impact on affected transcripts. The consequence was predicted as an Intron variant for one transcript and a 3’ untranslated region (UTR) variant for the remaining transcripts. The 3’ UTR refers to the non-coding region important for regulating mRNA48. A mutation in this region can affect mRNA function and be potentially deleterious48.

The NF-κB pathways can also be activated in B-cells, playing important roles in the survival, proliferation, and function of these cells49, being important in their late maturation50, development, and longevity of mature B-cells51. The triggered B-cell antigen receptor (BCR) will activate the protein kinase C (PKC) β that activates IKK. The IKK complex is composed of three kinases: IKK-α, IKK-ß and a regulatory subunit, IKK-γ. Through the canonical NF-κB pathway, the BCR will activate IKK-ß to phosphorylation and degradation of the IκB. The IκB, which is an inhibitor, will release NF-κB to the nucleus, where it controls gene expression49,52.

The Interleukin (IL) 1 and the Complement system are important players in the innate immune response. The IL-1 is responsible for inducing and mediating fever, B and T-cell proliferation, activating neutrophilia and acute phase proteins, and controlling the expression and production of chemokines and inflammatory cytokines, among other functions53. Besides its importance in the immune system, its role in the immune response against GIN infection in sheep is not described. The complement system is important in the identification and destruction of invading parasites. However, parasites have been able to evade the complement system through diverse mechanisms, which could be the reason why this pathway was identified in the MSR54. Zhang et al.55 identified the Complement cascade pathway enriched between resistant and susceptible lines in the Haemonchus selection flock and the Trichostrongylus selection flock using RNA-Sequencing (RNA-Seq) data from the abomasum of infected lambs.

In a more detailed analysis of the gene list within the MSR group associated with the immune system, several complement cascade genes were identified (CFD, C5, C6, C8A, and C9). Genes related to the complement system regulatory mechanisms were also identified (CFI, C4BPA, and C4BPB). Nematode infections in mice have been proven to activate the complement system to combat the infection56,57. According to Alba-Hurtado and Muñoz-Guzmán58, the complement pathway is one of the first responses to be activated in a Haemonchus contortus infection, which will also attract eosinophils to the area of infection, reinforcing the inflammatory response. Besides being important in the innate immune response, the complement system must be carefully controlled to avoid superactivation of its pathways59. Several genes playing roles in the activation and regulation of the complement system were identified in transcriptome and genome association studies55,60–65.

The CFI gene is responsible for encoding a proteinase responsible for the cleavage of C3b and C4b, avoiding the formation of C3 and C5 convertase enzymes66. The CFI gene was identified as upper-expressed in resistant lambs of Haemonchus and Trichostrongylus selection flocks after a primary infection with H. contortus55. High levels of this gene were also identified in the abomasum mucosa tissue of resistant lambs63. Additionally, the CFI gene was identified as upregulated in resistant animals’ abomasum tissue compared to a control group after infection62. Genome-wide association studies identified the CFI gene in regions associated with fecal egg count after 28 days of infection and traits measuring resistance to H. contortus10,13. Two novel insertions of one base were identified within the CFI gene on OAR 6, positions 15,986,060 and 16,007,746. Both insertions affect all seven transcripts of this gene. One of the insertions, for one transcript, had a modifier impact with an intron variant consequence. However, both insertions for all other transcripts had a high impact, with consequences of frameshift variant and splice region variant. This indicates that these insertions likely occurred within 1–3 bases of the exon and led to disruption of the translational reading frame.

The IRF6 gene, mainly expressed in epithelial cells, seems to be important in mucosal immunity; however, its specific role is not well understood. This gene appears to play a role in epithelial development and regulation of the innate immune response in knockout mice, reducing body growth and expression of genes related to epithelial differentiation and immunity pathways67. The novel insertion identified within this gene had a consequence of frameshift variant and splice region variant with a high impact on the unique transcript described for this gene. The LBP gene was another gene identified in the MSR group with INDELs leading to SSE. This gene is responsible for coding the Lipopolysaccharide Binding Protein that plays an important role in the acute-phase immune response to gram-negative bacteria68. The LBP protein is an acute phase protein that can have its concentration in the blood varying when the animal is facing stress or infection69. Zhong et al.69 identified a significant difference between LBP protein blood concentration in H. contortus uninfected and infected animals, where the authors concluded that this protein can be used as a biomarker in H. contortus infection in lambs. Two novel insertions were identified within this gene on OAR 13. The one-base insertion affected both transcripts of this gene, leading to a frameshift variant and splice region variant consequence with a high impact. The six-base insertion affected both transcripts of this gene with a protein altering variant and a splice region variant consequence with moderate impact. The protein altering variant consequence is an alteration that can lead to a change in the protein sequence. In this situation, the mutation likely happened within 1–3 bases of the exon and led to a change in the protein sequence.

Two members (STAT2 and STAT6) of the STAT family were identified in the list of genes for the MSR group. This family plays an important role in carrying information from the cell surface receptors to the nucleus to activate gene expression70,71. The STAT2 gene encodes a co-factor involved in Type I interferon signalling71. The INF-γ seems to be produced in the Th1-mediated response by T and natural killer cells and is normally associated with the immune response to intracellular bacteria, protozoa and viruses38. Although this gene seems not to be involved in the immune response of sheep to GIN infection, it was identified under selection in naturally resistant sheep challenged with an oral dose of H. contortus12 and differently expressed in abomasal lymph node tissue between Suffolk and Texel lambs72. A novel insertion within the STAT2 gene was identified, affecting all five transcripts of this gene. The insertion had a modifier impact in all transcripts with an intron variant consequence. The STAT6 gene seems to play a more important role in sheep’s immune response to GIN infection. IL-4 and 13 are responsible for activating the STAT6 gene that is responsible for several T-cell responses, the development of Th2 response, and the release of IL-470. IL-4 is a major cytokine for the activation and expansion of the Th2 response73. This, in turn, is the primary response in sheep’s helminth infection immune response, as shown by several authors10,38,74–76. All three transcripts of the STAT6 gene were affected by the novel insertion identified in this study. This insertion had a splice acceptor variant consequence with a high impact. Splice acceptor variants are mutations happening in the two-base region at the 3’ end of an intron.

Study limitations and impact

Two limitations of this study need to be acknowledged. Firstly, the animals were euthanized at the beginning of November, which was already the end of the Fall season in Canada. At this point, the weather was cooler and less humid, the worm burden on pasture was lower, and the parasites were likely becoming hypobiotic14. The peak of mean fecal egg counts was at 117 days (mid-August) of grazing (between 600 and 800 eggs per gram on average in the HSR and MSR groups)77. The RNA-Seq data used in this study better reflects the resolution phase of infection instead of the peak phase of the infection. A second limitation of this study is the use of liver tissue in opposition to the abomasum, lymph nodes, or small intestine tissue for RNA-Seq. Although tissue samples from these organs were also collected for the study, they could not be utilized for RNA-Seq and subsequent analysis due to the low quality of the extracted RNA. In the context of an infection, the liver participates in the acute-phase response and can alter the protein expression profile, increasing the production of the acute-phase proteins and reducing other protein expression (e.g. albumin)78. Additionally, the liver receives around 80% of the blood from the intestines through the portal vein. This blood contains several antigens that could be inoffensive or harmful79. Considering its role in the acute-phase immune response, it makes the liver a strong target for understanding the underlying genetic background of the immune response in sheep naturally exposed to GIN infection with different innate immune responses.

These results may indicate that INDELs responsible for SSE in genes playing roles in pathways related to cross-presentation and NF-κB related pathways may be involved in the immune response to GIN infection in sheep. Specifically, the sheep immune system may be using cross-presentation as an alternative route for antigen presentation, and NF-κB may be playing a major role in the activation and regulation of the immune system during GIN infection. However, it is hard to specify how both pathways may act in the infection since the immune response to GIN is a complex trait that can vary due to age, phase of infection, and animal genetic background. Further investigation needs to be performed to better understand the role of the NF-κB and cross-presentation in sheep immune response to GIN. Another important pathway identified was the “Terminal pathway of complement” indicating that this pathway could be helping the GIN infection resolution. Additionally, important genes (e.g. STAT6 and genes related to the complement cascade) were identified in the MSR group, which could indicate a major importance of these genes in building the inflammatory response to helminthic infection in sheep. The STAT6 gene may be an important candidate to be studied in detail in further studies to better understand its role in the immune response to nematode infection in sheep.

Conclusion

This study identified 4,594 INDELs responsible for splice site effect in sheep with different innate immune profiles naturally exposed to GIN infection (medium stress responder and high stress responder groups) and 1,396 INDELs in the GIN unexposed group. In addition, 1,162 and 431 genes were identified as associated with these INDELs for the GIN exposed and GIN unexposed groups, respectively. Several pathways were enriched using the list of genes that were identified, including pathways related to the immune system that could be playing roles in the inflammatory response to GIN infection, such as the “Terminal pathway of complement”. Additionally, identified genes such as STAT6 and CFI might have major roles in the immune response to GIN infection and should be considered for further studies.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

The procedures described here were approved by the University of Guelph Animal Care Committee (Animal Use Protocol #3380). The methods involving animals described in this study were performed in accordance with institutional, national, and international ethical standards, guidelines, and regulations following the Canadian Council on Animal Care (https://ccac.ca/Documents/Standards/Guidelines/Farm_Animals.pdf).

This study is part of a larger Rideau x Dorset sheep experiment that was described in detail by Borkowski77. Briefly, the University of Guelph Ponsonby Sheep Research Station (Ponsonby, Ontario, Canada) has maintained a GIN-free flock since 1988 with negative fecal egg counts and sporadic counts of non-pathogenic Nematodirus spp. From this population, 180 animals were challenged with Escherichia coli 0111:B4 LPS (Sigma Chemical Co., St. Louis, MO, USA) at 70 days of age to stimulate an acute-phase response and induce cortisol release by the HPA axis as proposed by You et al.16. Cortisol levels were measured 4 h after the challenge (population mean 117.0 nmol/L). Fifteen high stress responders (HSR) lambs with peak cortisol concentrations greater than one standard deviation above the population mean (ranging from 242 to 494 nmol/L), and fifteen medium stress responders (MSR) with the smallest absolute difference between the peak cortisol concentration and the population mean (ranging from 72 to 152 nmol/L) were selected. Both groups (ages ranging from 160 to 380 days) were transported from the University of Guelph Ponsonby Sheep Research Station to a commercial farm with a known parasitism history where the animals grazed with a GIN-contaminated flock. The animals grazed in contaminated pastures for 189 days (from April 28th to November 3rd, 2016). One animal from the MSR group was removed from the experiment due to an illness not related to parasitemia. A third group (n = 4) was also selected among the medium group and kept grazing in the facility in the absence of GIN, where they were not exposed to GIN at any time during the experiment (GIN-Unexposed). All selected males were castrated two months before grazing to avoid interference of testosterone on parasitism. During the grazing season, fecal egg count confirmed the presence of eggs in the exposed groups and the absence of eggs in the GIN-Unexposed group.

At the end of the grazing period, all animals (n = 37) were transported to a holding facility located less than 1 km from the University of Guelph abattoir. The animals were housed indoors in separate experimental groups for 72 h, with ad libitum access to hay and water to reduce transport-associated stress. Animal euthanasia was conducted using electrical stunning to induce unconsciousness and insensibility to pain, followed by exsanguination14,15. After slaughter, the abomasum, small and large intestines of each animal were sectioned separately, and their ends were tied off. Additionally, a sample (~ 5 g) of the center of the right lobe of the liver was removed from each animal, washed with phosphate buffer solution, and frozen in liquid nitrogen for transportation14. At the laboratory, the liver samples were stored at −80 °C for subsequent RNA extraction. The content of each gastrointestinal section was analyzed, and the number and species of gastrointestinal parasites were determined. The animals exposed to GIN that presented the highest and lowest GIN counts within each group were selected for liver RNA extraction. An average burden of 628.75 (± 1243.21) and 1887.50 (± 5074.76) parasites was identified in the abomasum and small intestines combined for the HSR and MSR groups, respectively.

RNA extraction

Six HSR, six MSR, and four GIN-Unexposed liver samples were further processed. A representative ~ 30 mg of liver tissue sample was processed using the Qiagen RNeasy Mini-Prep Kit (Qiagen, California, USA) for RNA extraction according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and quantity of the extracted RNA were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies Incorporation, California, USA), respectively. All RNA samples present good quality (RNA integrity value ≥ 8.0)14,15.

RNA-sequencing library preparation and sequencing

Library preparation was performed with Poly-A selection using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Massachusetts, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Library quality and concentration were assessed using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). Sequencing yielded 150 bp (bp) paired-end reads, and it was performed using the Illumina HiSeq 2000 analyzer14,15.

RNA-sequencing analysis

RNA-Sequencing (RNA-Seq) analysis was performed using the QIAGEN CLC Genomics Workbench 24.0 (QIAGEN, Aarhus, Denmark)80. Firstly, the sequences were trimmed. After that, quality control was performed as described by Cánovas et al.81. The quality control parameters required that all samples had the same length (150 bp), 100% coverage in all bases with a 25% contribution of each nucleotide (A, T, C, and G), 50% GC on base content, and less than 0.1% over-represented sequences. Most samples achieved the quality control assessment, except for one sample in the HSR group. The sequences were aligned to the annotated ovine reference genome Oar_rambouillet_v2.0 (released 110). Counts per gene were transformed using Log10 and normalized using reads per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPMK)82. After alignment, all sequences within the same group were merged into one unique sample, as proposed by Lam et al.19, to improve the accuracy and power of variant detection and reduce false positive results.

Variant detection and filtering

Variant detection and filtering process was performed as proposed by Lam et al.19 and Cánovas et al.83 using the QIAGEN CLC Genomics Workbench 24.0 (QIAGEN, Aarhus, Denmark). Variants were detected individually for each sequence using the “Fixed Ploidy Variant Detection” option in the software. After that, the “Identify shared variants” option was used to identify variants unique to each group (HSR, MSR, GIN-Unexposed) and shared among groups. Filtering steps were performed to maintain only INDEL-type variants.

Functional consequences analysis and filtering

The file with INDEL variants was used to perform functional consequence analysis. Firstly, the option “Amino Acid Changes” was used to identify INDELs that were responsible for amino acid changes (AAC) in the sequences. Secondly, the “Predict Splice Site Effect” function was used to identify INDELs responsible for the SSE. After the functional consequence analysis, filtering steps were performed and the number of INDELs, INDELs responsible for AAC and SSE unique and shared among groups was annotated.

Functional annotation analysis

The lists of INDELs unique for each group and shared among groups were used to retrieve the genes associated with the variants using the Biomart Ensembl84. Genes were retrieved using the annotated ovine reference genome Oar_rambouillet_v2.0 (released 110). After that, the lists of genes were used to identify enriched metabolic pathways (FDR < 0.05) using the Reactome Pathway Knowledgebase 202485. Analyses were performed for the list of genes unique and shared among groups.

Variant effect prediction

To predict the coding consequence of each INDEL on the protein sequence, the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) software was used31. The INDELs identified as responsible for SSE unique and shared among groups were used for effector prediction.
