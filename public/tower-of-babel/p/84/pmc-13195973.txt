Exploring Chinese older adults’ acceptance of digital mental health interventions: a qualitative study
Authors: Xin Mei, Jiali Liu, Yufei Qiu, MengJie Tong, Juan Gu, YaKe Yue, Fen Yang, Ling Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to explore the acceptance of digital mental health interventions (DMHIs) among Chinese older adults, and to provide a reference basis for the subsequent implementation of digital mental health intervention tools.MethodsThis study was a qualitative study using an empirical phenomenological approach. We conducted in-depth interviews with 20 elderly patients in the Chinese region and analyzed the content of the interviews qualitatively. The interview transcripts were converted to textual content by NVivo 15.0 software and analyzed using the Colaizzi phenomenological method.ResultsFive major themes were identified in this study: (1) Health Needs for Mind-Body Synergy; (2) Practicality and Perceived Effectiveness of Intervention Measures; (3) Concerns About Data Privacy Leaks (4) Usability of the User Interface and Operations; (5) Experience-driven dissemination and recommendation. in addition, nine sub-themes were extracted as more nuanced influences on the acceptance of DMHIs by older adults.ConclusionsThis study outlines the acceptability of DMHIs for older adults in China. Future research should older adults DMHIs should involve other stakeholders, such as healthcare professionals, to provide content that is relevant to the needs of older adults and make it more accessible to them.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27309-0.

Introduction

The global aging trend is intensifying. According to the latest data from the World Health Organization, the proportion of the global elderly population is expected to experience a significant increase between 2015 and 2050, with the elderly population share projected to rise from 12.0% to 22.0%, representing a staggering 83.3% increase [1]. Elderly individuals are more susceptible to psychological issues such as loneliness, anxiety, and depression due to factors including changes in social roles, declining physical functions, and reduced ability to cope with stress [2, 3]. According to a WHO report, approximately 14% of the global elderly population suffers from mental health issues, with depression and anxiety being the most common [4]. In severe cases, these conditions can lead to suicidal behavior and increased mortality risk [3]. However, access to mental health services remains severely inadequate for elderly individuals with mental health issues, particularly in developing countries [5]. Research indicates that in middle- and low-income countries, approximately 80%-90% of individuals with mental health issues remain undiagnosed or untreated, while in high-income countries, this proportion is around 50% [6]. As one of the countries with the most severe aging populations globally, mental health issues among the elderly in China are particularly prominent. According to a World Health Organization report, China has over 54 million people suffering from depression [7], accounting for approximately 17% of the global burden of mental illness [8], with middle-aged and elderly individuals being the high-risk group. Existing research indicates that the prevalence of depression among middle-aged and older adults aged 45 and above in China is significantly elevated [9], with the overall prevalence rate among those aged 60 and above ranging from 11% to 57% [10]. Projections suggest that the economic burden and stigma associated with depression will continue to rise in China [11]. Therefore, addressing these challenges in China holds the potential to significantly improve global mental health.

Digital mental health interventions (DMHIs) have emerged as an essential component of global mental health treatment systems, leveraging internet platforms, mobile applications, and virtual reality technology to address mental health need [12–14]. DMHIs have been widely adopted among younger populations and demonstrated effectiveness in improving multiple mental health outcomes [15]. Growing evidence indicates that digital mental health interventions can also effectively enhance mental health and overall well-being among older adults [16]. A systematic review (involving 15 randomized controlled trials with a total of 3,100 participants) indicates that DMHIs can alleviate depressive symptoms in older adults [17]. Another systematic review encompassing 144 interventions indicated that DMHL interventions appear to be more effective among older adults than adolescents [18].

Although older adults are often perceived as technologically disadvantaged, recent years have seen a significant increase in mobile device ownership and regular internet usage among this demographic [19]. Moreover, older adults demonstrate interest in digital technologies [19] and, in some cases, may even prefer digital health tools over traditional in-person healthcare services [20]. This growing interest, along with the increasing advantages of DMHIs in optimizing resources and promoting equitable service delivery, underscores the necessity of their development. In low- and middle-income countries, this model has effectively addressed the challenge of uneven distribution of traditional mental health services by providing remote mental health intervention tools that overcome time and space constraints, significantly reducing patients’ time costs and family care burdens [21].

However, older adults remain the group most significantly affected by the digital divide. In the actual use of DMHIs, older adults often face multiple barriers, including age-related declines in physical and cognitive function [22, 23], insufficient digital skills, and concerns about privacy, security, and intervention effectiveness [24–26]. These factors limit older adults’ ability to access and sustain use of DMHIs, thereby undermining their potential intervention effects [27]. While the user experience of DMHIs among younger populations has been extensively explored [28, 29], the unique needs and preferences of older adults remain underrepresented in existing digital intervention designs [22]. A Canadian study found that digital intervention tools with simple interfaces and intuitive operations are more likely to be favored by the elderly; conversely, complex content design and ambiguous instructional guidance not only distract users but also significantly reduce their willingness to use such tools [30]. Therefore, understanding older adults’ perceptions of the acceptability of DMHIs and the design of such tools, and providing this feedback to researchers to enable targeted improvements to digital mental health intervention tools, is an important way to enhance older adults’ acceptance of DMHIs.

To more clearly define the direction of this study and address the research gap regarding the digital mental health intervention needs of older adults, the interview guide was developed based on a comprehensive theoretical framework. We integrated theories such as the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [31], the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) [32], and system design elements to explore older adults’ acceptance of DMHIs and its influencing factors. The interview outline will focus on perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, perceived enjoyment, intention to use, and how system design impacts older adults’ acceptance of these tools, thereby aiding in the improvement of age-friendly intervention tool design.

Given the above context, it is necessary to systematically explore older adults’ acceptance of DMHIs and related influencing factors from their perspective. This study employs qualitative research methods, focusing on China’s elderly population, to address the following research questions: (1) How do older adults in China perceive the acceptability of digital mental health interventions? (2) What factors influence their willingness to use DMHIs? As a developing nation, China has experienced rapid digital technology advancement, yet its elderly population has had relatively limited exposure to DMHIs [33]. Consequently, the findings of this study will not only contribute to optimizing the design of digital mental health interventions tailored for Chinese seniors but also provide valuable insights for other developing countries with similar contexts.

Method

Study design

This study adopts an empirical phenomenological approach to investigate older adults’ acceptance of DMHIs. Empirical, experience-based data were collected from community-dwelling older adults through semi-structured, in-depth, one-on-one interviews and were analyzed according to phenomenological principles (see Supplementary File 1 for the interview guide). In this context, empirical phenomenology denotes the application of phenomenological inquiry to empirically derived interview data rather than to philosophical reflection alone. Through a systematic, inductive analytic process, this approach aims to identify the essential meanings underlying participants’ lived experiences while consciously bracketing the researchers’ preconceptions to preserve the authenticity of participants’ perspectives.

Sampling

Participants were recruited using purposive sampling, with attention to variation in characteristics relevant to the study objectives, including age, gender, living arrangements, and marital status. The sampling approach sought to capture a range of sociodemographic backgrounds and life circumstances among older adults, in line with the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, in order to reflect diverse life experiences. The study enrolled older adults from three communities in Hubei and Hunan provinces (Hongshan District, Wuchang District, and Yueyanglou District) who were interviewed between October and December 2024. During the recruitment process, efforts were made to include both men and women across different age groups within the target range, as well as participants with differing marital statuses and living arrangements. When initial recruitment yielded participants with similar characteristics, additional participants from different backgrounds were actively sought whenever feasible. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged between 60 and 80 years; (2) possessing basic proficiency in using smartphones and/or computers, and owning at least one of these devices; (3) providing informed consent and voluntarily participating in the study. Exclusion criteria included: (1) individuals with schizophrenia, organic mental disorders, or severe hearing or visual impairments that hindered the completion of the survey (such as deafness or cataracts); (2) participants with other serious physical illnesses that prevented them from cooperating with the survey. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Nursing, Hubei University of Chinese Medicine (approval number: 2025020).

Research team

The research team consists of 1 professor, 3 associate professors, and 2 graduate students, all of whom are female. Prior to conducting the interviews, team members received specialized training in qualitative research methods, and the corresponding author has over ten years of experience in the field of geriatric psychological nursing education. This combination of expertise ensured the rigorous implementation of the study and the accurate interpretation of findings.

Data collection

The interview guide was developed based on a comprehensive literature review and input from research team members. Interviews were conducted in private settings, either at community offices or participants’ homes, prioritizing comfort and privacy. Prior to each interview, the interviewers introduced the study objectives and obtained verbal informed consent from participants. Demographic questions were asked, and the concept of DMHIs was explained to the older adults. All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Each interview lasted between 30 and 45 min, and participants were invited to assist in organizing and confirming the interview content after the session. According to grounded theory, theoretical saturation is considered to be reached when new data no longer provide new contributions or challenges to the established theoretical framework [34]. Theoretical data saturation was reached after interviewing the 16th participant, as iterative analysis revealed no new emerging themes thereafter.

Data analysis

Within 24 hours of each interview, the recordings were transcribed, organized, and repeatedly reviewed using NVivo 15.0 software. Subsequently, Colaizzi’ s seven-step analytical method was applied to the textual data [35]. First, two researchers carefully read the interview transcripts, extracted significant statements, and marked recurring meaningful ideas. These marked ideas were then organized to create a detailed and comprehensive description, with similar viewpoints identified and distilled into thematic concepts. The preliminary analysis results were returned to the participants for verification. Finally, all team members engaged in discussions and revisions to ensure the accuracy and validity of the findings.

Quality assurance

To ensure the reliability and validity of the study, several quality control measures were implemented: (1) The interviewers, who were postgraduate nursing students, received systematic training in qualitative research, covering principles, skills, and procedures. They also conducted a literature review to familiarize themselves with relevant research before the study commenced; (2) Interviews were conducted in quiet, undisturbed environments; (3) Interview data were transcribed into text within 24–72 h and verified with participants to ensure accuracy; (4) Two researchers independently analyzed the data to minimize subjective bias, and the final themes were determined through group discussions. These measures collectively enhanced the rigor and credibility of the study results.

Theme 1: health needs for mind-body synergy

Older adults indicated that they place greater emphasis on physical health issues than on improving mental health issues. Older adults often suffer from various illnesses, and most mental health issues stem from physical illnesses. Most respondents stated that they would be more inclined to use DMHIs if they could improve their physical health.

“What I care about most is something that is more targeted toward our physical health.” (N1)

“What I care about most is something that is more targeted toward our physical health.” (N1)

“Nowadays, people are not only concerned about physical health but also beginning to prioritize mental health. For example, if physical pain decreases, mental well-being also improves; if one is constantly tormented by pain and looks gloomy, their overall state of being will inevitably deteriorate.” (N8)

“Nowadays, people are not only concerned about physical health but also beginning to prioritize mental health. For example, if physical pain decreases, mental well-being also improves; if one is constantly tormented by pain and looks gloomy, their overall state of being will inevitably deteriorate.” (N8)

“I hope it can provide functions that are beneficial to my physical health, and that mental health can be integrated with my physical health. ”(N9)

“I hope it can provide functions that are beneficial to my physical health, and that mental health can be integrated with my physical health. ”(N9)

Theme 2: practicality and perceived effectiveness of intervention measures

Gradual progression of intervention content

Older adults’ acceptance of intervention measures is closely related to their perception of practicality. Due to individual differences in cultural and educational backgrounds and digital literacy among the elderly population, coupled with cognitive decline associated with aging, most older adults generally prefer to acquire knowledge in a step-by-step manner and are more receptive to intervention content that progresses from basic to advanced concepts in a gradual and incremental manner.

“I think it should start from the basics and gradually progress to more advanced topics. You can’t dive into something too complex right away, as some people might not be able to handle it. After mastering the basics, you can gradually move forward step by step. You can’t overwhelm them with too much information at once, as they might find it too difficult to learn, especially since everyone has different levels of education and cultural backgrounds.” (N17)

“I think it should start from the basics and gradually progress to more advanced topics. You can’t dive into something too complex right away, as some people might not be able to handle it. After mastering the basics, you can gradually move forward step by step. You can’t overwhelm them with too much information at once, as they might find it too difficult to learn, especially since everyone has different levels of education and cultural backgrounds.” (N17)

Limitations of short-term interventions

The duration of interventions also affects older adults’ perception of effectiveness. Some older adults reported that while short-term, fragmented interventions are convenient to implement, they lack systematicity and continuity, making it difficult to achieve significant results.

“I think this is helpful, but the ones I’ve tried before were only for a few days, and the results were average.” (N4)

“I think this is helpful, but the ones I’ve tried before were only for a few days, and the results were average.” (N4)

“It just passed by because the time was too short. I went through it quickly, so it might not be effective.” (N9)

“It just passed by because the time was too short. I went through it quickly, so it might not be effective.” (N9)

Theme three: concerns about data privacy leaks

Although some elderly interviewees initially stated during the interview that they had little privacy due to their age, this may have been a protective response during the conversation.

“We are old; we have no privacy left.”(N5)

“We are old; we have no privacy left.”(N5)

However, in-depth interviews revealed that feelings of insecurity and distrust regarding privacy in the digital environment are widespread among this group.

Specific concerns about information leaks

Older adults expressed concrete concerns regarding the risks of personal information and financial security leakage when using DMHIs. These concerns focused on the potential negative consequences of data disclosure in both daily life and financial domains. Specifically, participants reported worries that personal information entered during DMHIs use—such as phone numbers or account details—could be leaked, resulting in frequent advertising messages or harassing calls. In addition, some participants expressed apprehension about online payment functions embedded in DMHIs, fearing possible financial loss or fraud associated with digital transactions.

“There are a lot of ads on my phone now, which is quite troublesome. Sometimes ads are pushed frequently, and there are also many scam calls. I worry that the program might leak my information, and then insurance companies will call me every day to harass me.” (N3)

“There are a lot of ads on my phone now, which is quite troublesome. Sometimes ads are pushed frequently, and there are also many scam calls. I worry that the program might leak my information, and then insurance companies will call me every day to harass me.” (N3)

“I won’t top up my account, and even if I do, I won’t top up much, because I’m afraid others might scam my money.” (N5)

“I won’t top up my account, and even if I do, I won’t top up much, because I’m afraid others might scam my money.” (N5)

“As you get older, you’re actually quite easy to deceive. Sometimes something pops up accidentally, and if you click on it, the money in your bank card is gone.” (N12)

“As you get older, you’re actually quite easy to deceive. Sometimes something pops up accidentally, and if you click on it, the money in your bank card is gone.” (N12)

Vigilance toward intervention sources

Older adults’ selective vigilance toward the access channels and official qualification of DMHI tools to avoid information leakage risks, with a clear preference for DMHI tools with formal professional certification and rejection of unqualified non-official ones.To avoid the aforementioned information and financial leakage risks, some elderly people showed obvious skepticism toward DMHI tools from unqualified non-official channels and they would only choose to use DMHI tools from officially certified professional channels.

“I only search within professional official accounts because they have professional certifications. If a stranger suddenly asks to add me on WeChat (Similar to the Chinese version of Instagram) or follow this or that, I won’t follow them. This could lead to my information being leaked. ” (N4)

“I only search within professional official accounts because they have professional certifications. If a stranger suddenly asks to add me on WeChat (Similar to the Chinese version of Instagram) or follow this or that, I won’t follow them. This could lead to my information being leaked. ” (N4)

Privacy anxiety caused by operational complexity

Some elderly individuals believe that complex operational steps, multiple identity verifications, or the need to download additional components increase the risk of privacy leaks.

“The simpler and more transparent the operation, the better. Don’t make downloading or logging in too complicated; too many verifications make my personal information particularly vulnerable to privacy leaks.”(N6)

“The simpler and more transparent the operation, the better. Don’t make downloading or logging in too complicated; too many verifications make my personal information particularly vulnerable to privacy leaks.”(N6)

Theme 4: usability of the user interface and operations

Age-friendly design of user pages

Due to age-related physiological characteristics, older adults have specific needs regarding the usability of the user interface and operations of digital mental health intervention tools. In terms of interface design, most older adults prefer a simple and intuitive visual presentation. They generally hope to increase the font size or customize it according to their preferences to accommodate individual differences in vision.

“Make the text larger, keep the content concise and clear, and avoid overly colorful designs.” (N8)

“Make the text larger, keep the content concise and clear, and avoid overly colorful designs.” (N8)

“I want the search bar to be placed in the most prominent position so I can find what I need with a single click.” (N9)

“I want the search bar to be placed in the most prominent position so I can find what I need with a single click.” (N9)

“The font size should consider our age group—larger but not too large to avoid looking unappealing. The interface should be simple; overly complex designs are confusing.” (N13)

“The font size should consider our age group—larger but not too large to avoid looking unappealing. The interface should be simple; overly complex designs are confusing.” (N13)

“The page layout or design is quite complex. Since it’s meant to relax the mind, it should be visually appealing and soothing at first glance. I’m also concerned about potential losses if I accidentally click the wrong option.” (N18)

“The page layout or design is quite complex. Since it’s meant to relax the mind, it should be visually appealing and soothing at first glance. I’m also concerned about potential losses if I accidentally click the wrong option.” (N18)

Usability of the operation process

Difficulty in operation is one of the main reasons for abandoning use, especially when the process is complex and requires multiple verifications, leading to frustration and concerns about risks.

“If the operation is too difficult, I won’t use it. If it’s too troublesome with many verifications, that’s why I won’t use it. The simpler the operation, the better. Don’t make it so complicated; the more complicated it is, the less operable it becomes for everyone.” (N6)

“If the operation is too difficult, I won’t use it. If it’s too troublesome with many verifications, that’s why I won’t use it. The simpler the operation, the better. Don’t make it so complicated; the more complicated it is, the less operable it becomes for everyone.” (N6)

“First, compared to younger people, we’re not very clear about online operations. Second, we can’t operate overly complex content. We’re older, and we can’t learn overly complex things.”(N15)

“First, compared to younger people, we’re not very clear about online operations. Second, we can’t operate overly complex content. We’re older, and we can’t learn overly complex things.”(N15)

From the perspective of interaction preferences, due to declining physical functions and lower digital literacy, handwriting input and voice input have become the mainstream information input methods for the elderly. Compared to traditional keyboard typing, they prefer to use natural language interaction to quickly access the desired intervention content. For relatively complex functional modules, corresponding step-by-step text guides or video tutorials should be provided to reduce cognitive barriers and operational thresholds, ensuring a smooth operational experience.

“For example, if I want to look up some information, there’s a voice assistant that can immediately provide what I need just by speaking, which saves me the trouble of typing.”(N10)

“For example, if I want to look up some information, there’s a voice assistant that can immediately provide what I need just by speaking, which saves me the trouble of typing.”(N10)

“There are so many issues encountered during use. For instance, some characters are unfamiliar, making reading difficult. Additionally, there are instances where you understand something but cannot type it out, so I hope that simply speaking it will generate the desired output.” (N14)

“There are so many issues encountered during use. For instance, some characters are unfamiliar, making reading difficult. Additionally, there are instances where you understand something but cannot type it out, so I hope that simply speaking it will generate the desired output.” (N14)

“If the program is complex, having a guide alongside would be more convenient. The guide would guide you through each step.” (N19)

Theme 5: experience-driven dissemination and recommendation

Initial experience determines continued use and willingness to recommend

Older adults’ continued use of and recommendation of interventions are closely related to their initial experience with them. For interventions that provide a good experience in the early stages of use, older adults not only form stable usage habits but are also more willing to recommend them to others. This positive experience fosters a sense of identification, prompting them to actively share it with friends or family, thereby expanding the application’s reach.

“I first tried some of their free trial classes. After using them for a while, I found them quite good. After experiencing them, I felt they were pretty good, so I continued using them. These trial classes helped relax both my mind and body.” (N1)

“I first tried some of their free trial classes. After using them for a while, I found them quite good. After experiencing them, I felt they were pretty good, so I continued using them. These trial classes helped relax both my mind and body.” (N1)

“Guidance on my condition and mental health. If I hadn’t been treated well, I wouldn’t recommend it to my family.” (N6)

“Guidance on my condition and mental health. If I hadn’t been treated well, I wouldn’t recommend it to my family.” (N6)

“If it’s good, of course I’d recommend it. Like the medicine I take regularly. If I think it’s good, I’ll recommend it too.” (N20)

“If it’s good, of course I’d recommend it. Like the medicine I take regularly. If I think it’s good, I’ll recommend it too.” (N20)

The influence of recommendations from trusted circles

Recommendations from trusted individuals within social networks significantly influence older adults’ willingness to use the app. Especially professional recommendations from healthcare providers can reduce older adults’ concerns about the app’s effectiveness and safety; while word-of-mouth recommendations from friends or family based on their own positive experiences encourage them to adopt a more positive attitude toward using the app.

“If a good friend recommends it to me, I’ll use it if they say it’s good.” (N3)

“If a good friend recommends it to me, I’ll use it if they say it’s good.” (N3)

“I’ll recommend it if it’s useful. If I feel it helps alleviate my psychological issues, I’ll recommend it. Plus, I like watching it with others. We’re all of a certain age now, so watching together is best.” (N9)

Discussion

Over the past decade, China has experienced rapid digital technology advancement, driving significant growth in the digital mental health market [36, 37]. Previous studies have examined acceptance or attitudes toward DMHIs in specific Chinese populations, including adolescents [38], people with mental disorders [39]or mental health professionals [40]. There is still a lack of research on the elderly, who face increasingly severe mental health pressures. Although perspectives and experiences regarding young people’s use of DMHIs have been extensively studied [41], these investigations often overlook the unique preferences and needs of older adults when utilizing such interventions. Qualitative research has demonstrated that populations in developing countries hold positive attitudes toward digital mental health interventions [42], a finding consistent with this study. Furthermore, Peng examined barriers and facilitators to DMHIs adoption among older adults [43], though their study did not delve into older adults’ acceptance of these interventions’ user interfaces and operational aspects. Given cultural differences across countries and regions, health-related needs and behavioral patterns regarding technology adoption among older adults may vary significantly. Within the Chinese context, traditional perceptions of mental health, social stigma, and acceptance of new technologies may influence older adults’ willingness to use DMHIs [44]. Therefore, to our knowledge, this study represents the first qualitative research focusing on Chinese older adults’ acceptance of DMHIs and exploring their perspectives on the page design of these intervention tools.

The results of this study indicate that older adults are more inclined to choose mind-body interventions, which essentially reflects their strong desire to feel the effects of the intervention. Given that older adults are generally unfamiliar with DMHIs, whether or not they can achieve their expected results is a key motivating factor for their continued use [45]. This study further found that some older adults indicated that intervention durations that are too short may result in intervention effects failing to meet expectations. However, a contradiction arises from previous extensive digital mental health intervention practices, which have shown that intervention durations that are too long may lead to a gradual decline in participation [46]. Therefore, designing effective interventions that sustain older adults’ willingness to use them and enhance their engagement is a critical component of improving intervention effectiveness. Only when older adults maintain sufficient engagement can they fully experience the effectiveness of the intervention, thereby motivating them to continue using it and forming a positive feedback loop. To improve engagement, it is recommended that future studies first conduct pilot trials and incorporate objective measures of engagement into the research survey. This can provide direct feedback to researchers and may necessitate further modifications to the intervention. Additionally, guided interventions typically have higher engagement rates than unguided interventions. A previous RCT (n = 433) also indicated that therapist-guided or self-managed iCBT may significantly reduce anxiety and depression symptoms in older adults [47].Some older adults in this qualitative study also noted that they preferred to receive operational instructions during the intervention to help them complete it.

Operational ease of use is another key factor influencing the adoption of DMHIs by older adults. Compared to younger age groups, older adults are generally less digitally literate, and complex operational interfaces tend to trigger their technology anxiety, which in turn may lead to resistance to DMHIs [48]. The present study also found that older adults tend to use simple operations and interfaces. This finding is consistent with the results of a meta-analysis that investigated factors in user engagement with digital mental health, which showed that bright graphics, large colorful icons, and easy-to-understand content promote acceptance [49]. Another literature review that analyzed public user reviews of mental health apps to gain insight into users’ perceptions also indicated that ease of use was a feature that users of mental health apps liked and recommended [50]. The design of DMHIs should prioritize ease of operation and simplicity of the interface for the physiological and cognitive characteristics of older adults. Optimizing the user interface and user experience, and lowering the technological threshold will directly increase the willingness of older adults to accept and effectively promote their adoption and continued use of digital mental health intervention tools.

Interpersonal relationships are also a key factor influencing older adults’ acceptance of DMHIs. In this study, we found that DMHIs with good experience were often recommended by older adults to their social circles; accordingly, older adults who received the recommendations were more likely to adopt the recommendations from their friends or doctors. This suggests that DMHIs recommended by social networks or trusted healthcare professionals are effective in increasing acceptance among older adults. This finding is consistent with previous research that encouragement from family members, peers, and healthcare providers is an important driver of DMHIs use among older adults [51, 52]. Based on this, efforts should be made to integrate the influence of social networks into DMHIs intervention strategies in clinical practice and community outreach. In the future, researchers can add social interaction modules when designing DMHIs to enable older adults to share their experiences and insights within the modules. At the same time, they can be encouraged to participate in online social forums and interact with their peers. These incorporation of social elements can help to reduce the psychological barriers of older users to the application of new technologies, thus enhancing their acceptance of DMHIs [53].

This study found that older adults are more inclined to use DMHIs if they can improve physical health. Previous research has also shown that older adults not only focus on the mental health benefits of interventions but place greater emphasis on their positive effects on physical health [48, 54]. Given that older adults commonly face multiple health challenges, particularly chronic conditions associated with aging, these issues not only impact their daily quality of life but also profoundly affect mental health [55]. Therefore, DMHIs that simultaneously enhance both physical and mental health are more likely to increase older adults’ acceptance of this intervention tool. A growing body of research indicates that physical and mental health are closely intertwined, with mental health recognized as a key factor influencing changes in physical health [56]. In this study, older adults’ willingness to accept DMHIs was closely linked to their perception of dual benefits for both physical and mental health. Existing studies also suggest that some older adults view DMHIs as offering dual benefits for both mental and physical health [57, 58]. Therefore, future DMHI designs should consider combining physical and mental health components to meet the multifaceted health needs of older adults, thereby enhancing their acceptance and willingness to use these services.

It is important to note that the majority of participants in this study had a relatively high educational level and mainly resided in large cities. Consequently, the research findings may primarily reflect the attitudes of educated older adults toward DMHIs. Higher educational attainment is typically associated with stronger digital literacy [59], which predisposes this group to be more inclined to engage with technology and adopt DMHIs approaches. Consistent with this, previous studies have shown that older adults with higher educational levels are more likely to access and adopt digital technologies, and hold more positive attitudes toward DMHIs [60]. Furthermore, higher education is likely to promote engagement with DMHIs by enhancing access to psychosocial resources and opportunities for personal growth [61]. This sample characteristic also explains why the older adults in this study generally held a positive attitude toward DMHIs. In contrast, older adults with lower educational levels may face greater challenges in using and accepting digital health tools. Therefore, future research should consider including more older adults with lower educational backgrounds to ensure that the findings have broader applicability and can reflect the true acceptance of DMHIs among older adults with diverse educational backgrounds.

Limitation

There are several limitations to this study. Firstly, the study population in this study was larger in the proportion of people living in urban areas, and there was no detailed survey of older adults in rural areas of China, so the results of the survey may have been targeted at a more digitally literate population. In addition, our study has only understood the acceptance of DMHIs among older adults, and future studies may consider examining the perspectives of other stakeholders, such as interviews with healthcare professionals, to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the factors associated with acceptance that affect older adults. Thirdly, the purposive sampling method used in this study has certain limitations. Specifically, it does not adequately account for key factors that could influence older adults’ acceptance of DMHIs, such as prior experience with digital health interventions and educational level. As purposive sampling is not designed to ensure a balanced representation of these factors, it may introduce bias. Future studies could consider using quota sampling, which allows for the setting of specific quotas based on these key factors. This approach would help achieve a more balanced representation, improve the sample’s overall representativeness, and reduce potential sampling bias.

Conclusion

This study outlines the acceptability and perceptions of DMHIs among older adults. Further to this this study analyzes and outlines the factors that influence older adults’ acceptance of digital mental health intervention tools. This study emphasizes that older adults’ acceptance of DMHIs is the result of a combination of effect expectancy, operational experience and social trust. Future DMHIs for older adults should take these factors into full consideration to promote the grounded application of DMHIs in the older population.
