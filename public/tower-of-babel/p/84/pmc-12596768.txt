Holmes tremor following midbrain hemorrhage – An illustrative case
Authors: Sohaib Ali, Alessandro Melatini, Cosimo Damiano Gianfreda, Domenico Cassitto, Alessandra Giaquinta, Francesco Mastromatteo, Manfredo Esposito, Marilena Rolli, Dario Palescandolo, Antonio Fasano, Angela Lupo, Marta Lorenzo, Giovanni Cirillo, Haleema Sadia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Holmes tremor (HT) is a rare, low-frequency tremor that presents at rest, during posture maintenance, and with intentional movement – associated with structural damage to the midbrain, thalamus, or cerebellothalamic pathways. Although vascular causes are well established, HT resulting from mesencephalic hemorrhage complicated by obstructive hydrocephalus has not been previously reported in the literature.Case Description: A 71-year-old male developed acute left hemiparesis and dysarthria following traumatic brain injury. Neuroimaging revealed right ponto-mesencephalic hemorrhage extending into the superior cerebellar peduncle, with infarction near the right tentorial edge and compression of the fourth ventricle, leading to supratentorial hydrocephalus. One-week post-trauma, the patient exhibited a right-sided tremor with resting, postural, and kinetic components, consistent with HT. Pharmacologic treatments, including antiepileptics, failed to produce clinical improvement. Serial imaging ruled out vascular malformations. Given patient’s progressive neurological recovery, a non-surgical, conservative management strategy was adopted.Conclusion: This case highlights a rare manifestation of HT secondary to mesencephalic hemorrhage complicated by obstructive hydrocephalus. The delayed onset and resistance to pharmacologic therapy emphasize the diagnostic and therapeutic complexity of HT. Clinicians should be vigilant in recognizing this condition in similar clinical scenarios and consider a tailored strategy, including delayed consideration of functional neurosurgery once hydrocephalus has stabilized.

INTRODUCTION

Holmes tremor (HT), initially described by Benedikt in 1889 and subsequently delineated by Holmes in 1904, is a low-frequency and large-amplitude tremor (<4.5 Hz), involving both proximal and distal musculature.[1,4] It manifests variably at rest, during voluntary movement, and is often sustained during maintenance of posture.[7]

Despite over a century of clinical recognition, the precise pathophysiological mechanisms underpinning HT remain incompletely elucidated. Current evidence supports the hypothesis that damage to the nigrostriatal dopaminergic pathway, the cerebellothalamic circuit, and the dentaterubro-olivary circuit may underlie the onset of HT. Current evidence supports involvement of the dopaminergic nigrostriatal pathway, the cerebellothalamic circuit, and the dentato-rubro-olivary circuit.[8-12]

The condition is most frequently associated with structural lesions involving the thalamus and midbrain; however, lesions affecting the cerebellum, pons, medulla oblongata, and even cortical regions have also been implicated.[3,8]

Vascular insults, particularly those secondary to traumatic brain injury, predominate.

Two principal mechanistic hypotheses have emerged.[5]

Nigrostriatal denervation: functional imaging studies have demonstrated reduced 18F-dopa uptake in the caudate and putamen nuclei in patients with HT secondary to midbrain lesions, suggestive of presynaptic dopaminergic dysfunction as observed in Parkinson’s disease.Disruption of cerebellar efferent pathways: Lesion of the dentato-olivary and dentato-rubro-olivary tracts is believed to underlie the tremor’s postural and intention-related components.

The delayed onset of HT, with symptoms emerging from several weeks to several years’ post-injury, indicates a time-dependent process which can be attributed to Wallerian degeneration of cerebellofugal fibers, failure of compensatory mechanisms, and changes of synaptic plasticity, causing aberrant oscillatory activity.[8,11]

Patients may present other neurological signs such as dystonia, choreoathetosis, ataxia, dysarthria, and hemiparesis.[6,7] Diagnostic evaluation includes neuroimaging – typically computed tomography (CT) and computed tomographic angiography (CTA) – to assess for structural and vascular pathology, often complemented by cerebral angiography when vascular anomalies are suspected.

Management of HT is largely symptomatic and remains suboptimal.[7,8,14] Pharmacological interventions, such as Levodopa, dopamine agonists, anticholinergics, GABAergics (gamma-aminobutyric acid), and propranolol, have yielded inconsistent therapeutic responses.[8] Antiepileptic agents such as topiramate, levetiracetam, and zonisamide have shown limited efficacy in select cases.[8,9] In refractory cases, neuromodulatory strategies such as deep brain stimulation (DBS) targeting the ventral intermediate nucleus of the thalamus, medial globus pallidus, or subthalamic nucleus have demonstrated variable benefit.[9,13] Thalamic ventralis intermedius nucleus (VIM) was the most common target for DBS, followed by globus pallidus internus (GPi), ventralis oralis anterior nucleus, and posterior subthalamic nucleus.[13] Single-electrode configuration with higher amplitude, frequency, and pulse width was necessary to produce therapeutic outcomes. Stimulation of VIM and GPi significantly improved overall and resting tremor.[13] In particular, GPi target demonstrated greater improvement in tremor, particularly in resting tremor, as compared to VIM.[13] However, the therapeutic outcome in patients with single-target and multiple-targets were not significant.[13] Other alternatives such as thalamotomy could be exploited as well, but remain inferior in efficacy. Nevertheless, standardized treatment protocols remain undefined.

CASE REPORT

A 71-year-old Asian male, with no known neurological or systemic conditions, presented with sudden-onset severe left hemiparesis and dysarthria. These symptoms emerged approximately 24 h after a motorcycle accident resulting in closed-head trauma. Cranial CTA revealed a right pontomesencephalic hemorrhage extending into the superior cerebellar peduncle with infarction of the right hemitentorial region and mild compression on the superior aspect of the fourth ventricle [Figure 1].

Premesencephalic hemorrhage (red circle) with mild compression on lateral wall of the IV ventricle (at hospital admission) in (a)axial and (b) sagittal projections.

The patient was admitted to our neurosurgical semi-intensive care unit to monitor his clinical status.

Three days later, the patient presented a clinical deterioration: The brain CT scan showed a hemorrhagic progression with increased mass effect and fourth ventricle narrowing caused by lateral wall compression [Figure 2]. These findings were concomitantly correlated with further worsening of the left hemiparesis.

Three day follow-up head computed tomography scan of the patient showing progressive extension of the hemorrhage (red circle) and further stenosis of the IV ventricle shown in(a) axial and (b) sagittal projections.

After 4 days, a repeat CT scan showed partial hematoma resolution and better ventricular patency [Figure 3]. Despite this, brain magnetic resonance imaging identified a new hemorrhage in the right ponto-mesencephalic area, with compression of the right anterolateral fourth ventricle wall and associated mild supratentorial hydrocephalus [Figure 4]. To elucidate the nature of the bleed, cerebral angiography was performed which excluded vascular malformations or aneurysms. However, the additional challenge posed by hydrocephalus was not addressed surgically because the patient was neurologically stable without any signs of increased intracranial pressure.

Follow up head computed tomography scan showing demonstrating progressive evolution of the hemorrhage and edema (red circle) with persistence of IV ventricle narrowing in (a)axial) and (b) sagittal projections.

(a) Magnetic resonance imaging scan T1 in sagittal projection depicting mesencephalic hemorrhage (red cirlce) highlighting significant narrowing of the IV ventricle. (b) Axial T2 showing hyperintense signal of the hemorrhage (red circle). (c) Axial fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) depicting progressive dilation of the lateral ventricles with transependymal edema. (d) Sagittal FLAIR illustrating the hyperintense signal of the hemorrhage (red circle).

Seven days following the trauma, the patient developed a right-sided intention tremor (~4 Hz) involving the head and the right arm with postural and resting components; the tremor improved by moving the neck to the contralateral side. Neurological examination also revealed neck dystonia. The electroencephalogram showed the presence of tremor artifacts in the posterior regions of the head with no epileptic discharges. Neurological assessment yielded a poor Fahn– Tolosa–Marin tremor score (19 points, indicating mild tremor). Therefore, a diagnosis of HT was made. Initial treatment with lacosamide (100 mg bid), followed by escalation to levetiracetam (500 mg bid), failed to produce improvement and was discontinued. Clonazepam (4 drops tid) was also introduced with only partial benefit.

Follow-up CT imaging demonstrated gradual resolution of the bleeding and concurrently also the supratentorial hydrocephalus [Figure 5]. However, given the patient’s pronounced neurological impairment due to brainstem injury and treatment-resistant tremor, he was transferred to a neurorehabilitation facility for targeted physiotherapy addressing persistent hemiparesis. At the time of discharge, the patient had partially recuperated left hemiparesis without any evolution of HT.

3 weeks follow up head CT scan (a) showing significant reduction of the perimesencephalic hemorrhage (red circle), (b) liberation of the IV ventricle, and progressive reduction and normalization of the lateral ventricles.

During the 1 month follow-up post-discharge, the patient showed remarkable improvement of the left hemiparesis and complete regression of the obstructive hydrocephalus, however, the HT persisted, and therefore, it was decided to refer the patient for functional neurosurgeon for evaluation and consideration for DBS.

DISCUSSION

This is a rare case of HT due of vascular origin which presented with supratentorial hydrocephalus which has not been documented in literature yet.

Observations

Only 155 cases of HT have been reported between 1904 and 2016, underscoring its extreme rarity.[2] The clinical phenotype of HT is typically linked to disruptions in the dopaminergic nigrostriatal pathway as well as in the cerebello-thalamo-cortical or dentato-rubro-olivary circuits; lesion sites are commonly located in the midbrain, cerebellum, or thalamus.[2,8]

The most frequent underlying etiologies include vascular insults, followed by hemorrhagic, traumatic, and demyelinating processes. Patients often present with a constellation of neurological signs such as hemiparesis, ataxia, hypoesthesia, and dystonia. Treatment remains challenging due to the heterogeneity of causative factors and the delayed onset of HT, which further contributes to functional impairment. Management options include pharmacological as well as surgical therapies. Lacosamide, levetiracetam with clonazepam were introduced in our patient with only partial clinical benefit. There are some studies reporting good clinical responses to DBS targeting the globus pallidus pars internus or thalamic nuclei.[13]

Our case is particularly notable due to the recurrence of brainstem hemorrhage leading to compression of the lateral wall of the fourth ventricle and subsequent complicated development of progressive supratentorial hydrocephalus. Cerebral angiography excluded underlying vascular malformations, which might have provided insight into etiology and informed future risk stratification. Furthermore, the obstructive nature of the hydrocephalus raised a clinical dilemma regarding conservative management versus surgical intervention.

CONCLUSION

Lessons

This case presents a complex clinical scenario involving concomitant HT with a transient complication of supratentorial hydrocephalus. A conservative “watch-and-wait” approach was adopted in this case, as the patient demonstrated gradual neurological improvement and responded positively to physiotherapy. Serial head CT imaging revealed regression of the hydrocephalus, and therefore, surgical intervention was not opted for. Despite negative cerebral angiography and no clear underlying etiology of the brain stem bleeding, it is imperative to monitor the patient clinically and by serial CT scans to evaluate for complications like obstructive hydrocephalus due to the intricate site of vascular insult. Surgical intervention for hydrocephalus was not opted for because the imaging showed resolution with improvement of the hydrocephalus without any worsening of the patient neurological status.

From a diagnostic point of view, it is important to consider some aspects to pin-point the pathological nature of HT in our case. For example, a DaTscan may be considered to confirm involvement of the dopaminergic system. However, due to limited resources, these options were not considered.

In conclusion, this is a case that illustrates a complex clinical scenario with peri-mesencephalic hemorrhage complicated by obstructive hydrocephalus and subsequent HT. Considering the vascular etiology and limited immediate interventional options, a conservative strategy was favored – yielding a favorable initial outcome and functional neurosurgical approach was considered following the 1-month follow-up after favorable clinical stability of the patient.
