A comprehensive dataset of agarwood tree (Aquilaria Malaccensis) leaf images for disease analysis in Brunei Darussalam
Authors: Wasswa Shafik, Rahayu Sukmaria Sukri, Chandratilak De Silva Liyanage, Nurul Hazlina Zaini, Soon Boon Yu, Mohammad Amiruddin Ruslan, Rosyzie Anna Awg Haji Mohd Apong, Ali Tufail
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe visual diagnosis based on foliar traits remains a cornerstone technique for the early identification of biotic stress, for instance, disease and pest infestations, in many economically valuable crops, including Aquilaria Malaccensis (agarwood). As a species of immense commercial and ecological significance, Aquilaria Malaccensis is particularly vulnerable to a range of pathogens and insect threats that can severely compromise resin production and tree viability. With the increasing integration of disruptive sustainable agricultural technologies, such as artificial intelligence (AI), especially in plant phenotyping and pathology, the development of robust and generalizable AI models hinges on the availability of large-scale and high-resolution image datasets. However, the current lack of such curated datasets for agarwood poses a substantial bottleneck to progress in automated identification systems. This deficiency limits the ability of scientists, technologists, and plant health experts to leverage machine learning and computer vision techniques for timely, accurate, and scalable solutions to different stresses in agarwood disease and pest management, including nematodes, viroids, viruses, pests, phytoplasmas, bacteria, fungi, and Protozoa. This paper presents a dataset of pests and diseases affecting agarwood trees, which impact farmers. It includes a total of 5472 leaf images classified into 14 categories. These categories consist of 8 types of agarwood diseases, 5 types of pests, and 1 category of healthy leaf images, encompassing both insect-damaged and healthy leaves. The images were captured using a PowerShot G7X Mark III camera. The images were captured from three different agarwood plantation sites of Batong, Benutan, and Bukit Silat in 2024, led by the Institute for Biodiversity and Environmental Research (IBER), Universiti Brunei Darussalam, by Botanical Research Centre (UBD BRC) scientists and biologists. This dataset is particularly valuable for training and validating deep learning (DL), computer vision, and machine learning algorithms aimed at identifying agarwood diseases and pests in agarwood leaves. Offering researchers and learners a robust data resource for analyzing and improving agarwood plant health through the development of advanced computational models. The designed models are vital and hold immense practical value for farmers, equipping them with the tools that timely detect and identify diseases in their agarwood trees, empowering them to make informed decisions and potentially intensify their profits.Keywords: Agarwood; Diseased leaves and pests; Computer vision, Image processing; Deep Learning (DL); Machine Learning; Aquilaria Malaccensis

Background

Aquilaria malaccensis (agarwood) is a high-value tropical tree prized for its aromatic resin, which is used extensively in traditional medicine, perfumery, and religious ceremonies. Nevertheless, the health and productivity of agarwood trees are habitually compromised by a wide array of biotic stressors, including bacteria, viruses, pests, and fungal pathogens. Early and accurate identification of these threats is vital to ensuring the sustainability of Aquilaria malaccensis plantations and the economic viability of growers, especially in regions such as Brunei Darussalam, where commercial cultivation is expanding. Brunei is striving to diversify its economy, and one of the initiatives is to focus on local crop cultivation. Agarwood trees comprise 21 species from the genus Aquilaria and the family Thymelaeaceae.1 They are particularly valuable commercially, and agarwood plantations in Brunei are increasing. Three species are known to occur in Brunei: Aquilaria beccariana Tiegh., Aquilaria microcarpa Baill. and Aquilaria Malaccensis Lam [1].

Aquilaria malaccensis is a medium-sized, evergreen tree characterized by a straight, cylindrical trunk, smooth greyish bark, and broad, lance-shaped leaves arranged alternately along its branches. Usually, this species grows in mixed dipterocarps and peat swamp forests at elevations ranging from 100 to 1000 m above sea level, while in Brunei, it is found in peat swamp, freshwater, and alluvial forests [2]. Throughout this range, it is believed that the tree will disperse naturally since this species is found in its natural habitat; however, young plants were occasionally recorded in abandoned agricultural lands in the Tutong district. It sustains environmental conservation in the dilapidated megadiverse ecosystem, particularly in Borneo, though stressed by several pests and diseases [3]. Farming of Agarwood trees in Brunei is now gaining special attention from the government authorities due to its known functionalities and biodiversity component. This unique tree thrives at temperatures ranging from 22 to 35 °C. Its natural growth is affected by the existence of earthquakes, volcanoes, fires, and other catastrophic events [4]. Deforestation for shifting agricultural cultivation and industrial plantations is a challenging factor. All these threats have led to an increase in gross non-performing assets because this tree is increasingly rare. Not only that, changes in the quality of the natural world, such as reduced water availability and quality, restricted light penetration, and physical damage to the canopy, are inadequate health factors [5]. Based on the nature of the tree, traditionally, the diseases and pest infestation identification in agarwood trees have relied on manual visual inspection by pathologists, botanists, or even farmers. While this method is cost-effective and largely intuitive, it comes with several limitations, including:

Several challenges beyond those identified above, AI-enabled identification systems, and computer vision offer a transformative solution. These systems can effectively process large volumes of leaf image data swiftly, detect subtle patterns that are imperceptible to the human eye, with real-time alerts for field interventions. Most importantly, such technologies require extensive, high-quality datasets for better training and validation, especially datasets that are representative of real-world variability in leaf orientation, lighting, and disease or pest manifestations. The successful diagnosis, treatment, and management of agarwood disease are vital to the parasitic fungal species that can lead to discoloration, decay, and, consequently, death of Agarwood trees. Heart rot is the decay of the wood that can rot the center of the trunk and roots of Agarwood trees, reducing their strength and structural rigidity, affecting the tree’s ability to stand and carry a live load. Meanwhile, shoot blight is a disease affecting the leaves and flowers, causing the spreading of black and brown spots, defoliation, and loss of fruits [6]. The destruction by any disease occurrence of the Agarwood tree can have an immediate and direct influence on the economic potential of the species. Understanding the disease diagnosis, timely analyses, and proper management can lead to a reduction in the potential occurrence of loss. It is important to study the existence of diseases in a tree species that can contribute to the related field; this is what farmers experience when venturing into Agarwood farming or setting up long-term plantations or forests in the country [7]. If a plantation contains diseased trees and they are not taken care of accordingly, the pests and diseases can spread and have socio-economic and ecological consequences.

The rate of infection from diseases, including trees, would be high; farmers would be hesitant to farm due to a lack of awareness of the disease and not having the proper tools and technical know-how to treat the affected areas. The community will be unaware of the disease when it starts until the area is severely affected, leading to neglect and loss for the farmers. Agarwood trees that are affected by diseases can destroy the quality of Agarwood, which can cause a huge economic loss to any farmer [8]. In addition, because farmers do not have the knowledge or education on disease analysis in their Agarwood plantations, the genetic diversity of Agarwood species must be compromised in line with the number of lost trees. Over the past several decades, forests and natural habitats around the world have decreased significantly due to agriculture, logging, and infrastructure development [9]. If the trees are infected with the disease, it can spread to other agarwood species and ecosystems if not controlled. As of now, there is no available dataset on agarwood that can be used to train AI models to identify these diseases and pests [[10], [11], [12]]. Based on the recent studies that were carried out on the dataset availability, the biggest percentage of the available datasets are curated in laboratory settings, and those that are in-field curated have controlled or removed backgrounds, making it hard for the models to perform in real-time deployment [13]. Unfortunately, there has been a notable absence of curated, labeled image datasets focused specifically on agarwood leaf health. This gap has hindered the development of AI-based tools tailored for agarwood disease and pest identification. The dataset presented in this paper addresses this critical bottleneck by offering a large collection of 5472 images in .jpg format, categorized into 14 well-defined classes, captured from three diverse agarwood plantation sites in Brunei. This data set, entailing both pests and diseases, can help foster several research efforts and can support models in learning from complex backgrounds with different pest and disease samples, improve disease surveillance, support precision interventions, and ultimately protect the health and yield of agarwood plantations.

Data Description

Extensive fieldwork and interdepartmental consultations were conducted in late 2023 to curate a distinct dataset of Aquilaria malaccensis leaf images. These commenced with signing collaboration agreements and attaining the consent of the private plantation owners and the Brunei Forest Department.2 Aquilaria malaccensis images were captured using two identical cameras, Canon PowerShot G7X Mark III models, each equipped with a 24–100 mm equivalent lens (f/1.8–2.8) and 4.2× optical zoom. 5472 images from various agarwood plantation sites, focusing on Aquilaria malaccensis, capturing healthy leaves, diseased leaves, and those affected by environmental stresses and pests, were captured between mid-October 2023 through May 2024. The images were captured under normal weather conditions, approximately between 9:00 AM and 04:00 PM, with temperatures ranging from 28 to 34 °C.

The dataset contains images that are organized as follows: 4401 images were collected on Aquilaria malaccensis diseases in 9 classes, including Downy mildew, Anthracnose, Black spots, Powdery mildew, Translucent lesions, Brown spots, Mosaic Viruses, Sooty mold, and one of which is healthy class, and 1071 images of Spider and Webs, Scale Insects, Mealybugs, Flea Beetles, and Brown Clumps in 5 pest classes. Each image was carefully captured to indicate specific regions as either healthy or diseased, given that each image includes a complex natural background with a diverse capturing resolution of 5472 pixels by 3658 pixels in JPG format an auto mode with an aspect ratio 3:2. Experimental Design, Materials, and Methods

The central objective in curating this dataset was to provide a realistic collection of agarwood leaf images to support and accelerate research in AI and image processing. The curation of this data comprised several critical steps.

Data collection and image acquisition

In the beginning, the School of Digital Science (SDS) initiated a data curation collaboration with the UBD Botanical Research Centre at the Institute for Biodiversity and Environmental Research (IBER) team to gain insights into Aquilaria malaccensis and its associated pests and diseases. This interdepartmental collaboration was essential since the SDS team's background in computer science required an in-depth expertise in disciplines such as botany, ecology, plant biology, and pathology, all of which IBER had. After a series of meetings and expert guidance, Aquilaria malaccensis was selected due to its economic significance. The research objectives were communicated and discussed with garden owners and representatives from the Brunei Forest Department; the IBER team was then granted permission to photograph Aquilaria malaccensis leaves in the Muara and Tutong districts of Brunei. Fig. 1 presents a group of the IBER curating team during image capture. To ensure wider coverage, images were snapped under various lighting conditions and different angles using a natural setup using a smart camera with a sensor of 20.1-megapixel 1.0-inch stacked CMOS sensor, DIGIC 8 image processor, and 24–100 mm (35 mm equivalent) f/1.8–2.8 lens. The images were then systematically and scientifically categorized into folders to rationalize the preprocessing phase of the dataset.

Image preprocessing techniques

Though the agarwood tree leaf images are predominantly clear and sharp, images that are not of high quality may affect the model performance in the identification of agarwood pests and diseases. The main objective of applying the pre-processing is to ensure the raw image has the highest quality before it gets analyzed. The resultant image of the pre-processing technique will be analyzed to detect the potential disease present within the given leaf data. Each captured image was manually reviewed, and images exhibiting poor texture quality or blurriness were discarded. The contrast enhancement brightens the bright and green regions, as well as eliminates the noise in the digital data set. In addition, image normalization was done to standardize the range of feature values in the given dataset to a single .jpg format. Some original images had varying resolutions and dimensions. This variation could lead to inconsistencies throughout model training, as AI models routinely expect fixed input dimensions. In the quest to solve this concern, all images were resized to a uniform resolution of 5472 × 3648 pixels using Keras’s ImageDataGenerator,3 an extensively applied tool for data augmentation and preprocessing. The entire process of data collection, image acquisition, and image pre-processing is demonstrated in Fig. 2.

Data collection process flowchart.

Even if most of the Aquilaria Malaccensis tree leaf images were captured in high resolution and appeared sharp, some images were found to be noisy, blurry, and would significantly affect model accuracy in identifying Aquilaria Malaccensis pests and diseases. Therefore, a rigorous preprocessing pipeline was implemented to ensure data quality and consistency, including image selection, formatting, and data splitting.

Under image selection, to ensure data quality, all captured images underwent manual inspection. Some images were excluded if they exhibited traits including blurring or motion artifacts, incomplete or occluded leaf structures, low contrast or overexposure, and poor texture quality or indistinct visual features. Thus, only images with complete, clear, and well-defined leaf traits were retained, guaranteeing that the final dataset supports robust and accurate model training. Under image formatting, the selected images were then standardized to ensure consistency in structure and format. This process involved unifying the image file format by converting all images to .jpg, as some figures were in different formats. Ensuring that all images are converted to the Red, Green, and Blue (RGB) color space and resizing all images to a uniform pixel size, meeting the input requirements of most DL models. Data review process

Each leaf image was independently examined and annotated for the disease type by at least two IBER team members. Annotators then reviewed others’ images to achieve consensus. All images labelled by one person only were excluded from the dataset. Additionally, a file was created to review the labeled images once they were checked into the tool. The results were scrutinized again to ensure their accuracy before being saved in the final format. At this final stage, the IBER team provided a detailed manual that provided a brief explanation about each pest or disease. A subject expert also reviewed and labeled the results. Some lesions were partially obscured or occurred alongside multiple stress symptoms, requiring careful judgement from the annotators. This process was time-consuming due to numerous images and complex backgrounds. To maintain data quality, the team took breaks to prevent fatigue. The experts had a keen eye for details, which was important for accurately labelling and identifying every lesion. The disease and pest type classifications in the dataset were conducted through expert visual inspection of the images by the IBER team based on visible symptoms such as the colour, shape, and pattern of lesions or pest damage, which included experienced biologists with specialized training in botany and horticulture on Brunei’s native flora, including Agarwood, and prior knowledge of common plant health problems. Their expertise enabled confident and reliable diagnosis of disease and pest types without formal scoring criteria. While laboratory tests and microscopic analyses were not conducted due to temporary inaccessibility of the Department of Agriculture and Agrifood’s Plant Pathology Lab, under renovation at the time, the team maintained close consultation with the department to ensure consistency with established diagnostic practices.

Limitations

While the availed dataset provides a valuable resource for the identification of diseases and pests affecting Aquilaria malaccensis in Brunei, two main limitations should be considered when applying models trained on this data to broader contexts.

Class imbalance

In spite of an endeavor to curate a diverse and representative dataset, some disease and pest categories are underrepresented compared to others, as detailed within Table 1 and more significant when it is split into training, validation, and testing in Table 2. This class imbalance poses a challenge for AI models, as it may cause biased learning toward majority classes and be less sensitive to minority or rare classes, poor identification of rare events and misleading performance metrics, slower and noisier convergence, and poor model calibration.

Geographic specificity

Variations in geographic location and environmental conditions influence plant growth and health, leading to differences in leaf texture and morphology. The dataset is geographically curated from three plantation sites within Brunei (namely, Batong, Benutan, and Bukit Silat). This implies that the pest and disease classes shown reflect the distinct agroecological conditions and biodiversity of Brunei. Environmental variables such as soil composition, light exposure, humidity, and pest ecology remarkably influence the manifestation of symptoms on Aquilaria malaccensis leaves. Therefore, models trained solely on this dataset may exhibit limited generalizability when applied to Aquilaria malaccensis plantations in other geographic locations with different environmental pressures or distinct species of pests and pathogens.

To address these complexities, to develop scalable, robust, and generalizable AI-based plant health monitoring systems for agarwood cultivation worldwide, researchers can mitigate class imbalance using data-level approaches such as under-sampling majority classes, data augmentation, or oversampling minority classes, algorithm-level techniques and evaluation-level strategies can be utilized in case the dataset is to be used in its current form. For geographic issues relating to data curation location, it restricts the immediate applicability of the model to global or regionally diverse settings unless the models trained on this dataset are fine-tuned with additional localized data. No sampling method was employed during data collection.
