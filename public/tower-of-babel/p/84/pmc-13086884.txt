Comparing artificial intelligence and healthcare professional performance in surgical and interventional video analysis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Amir Rafati Fard, Simon C. Williams, Kieran J. Smith, Jasneet K. Dhaliwal, Tomas Ferreira, Adrito Das, Joachim Starup-Hansen, John G. Hanrahan, Chan Hee Koh, Danyal Z. Khan, Danail Stoyanov, Hani J. Marcus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This systematic review and meta-analysis examines the design of studies comparing the performance of artificial intelligence (AI) with that of healthcare professionals in the analysis of videos from surgical and interventional procedures, and quantitatively evaluates the performance of AI, unassisted healthcare professionals, and AI-assisted healthcare professionals. From the 37,956 studies identified, 146 were included, with 76 providing sufficient information for inclusion in our exploratory meta-analysis. AI had significantly greater sensitivity and comparable specificity compared to unassisted healthcare professionals at their respective peak performance levels, with a relative risk of 1.12 (95% CI 1.07–1.19, p < 0.001) and 1.04 (95% CI 0.98–1.10, p = 0.224), respectively. AI-assisted healthcare professionals had significantly greater sensitivity and specificity compared to unassisted healthcare professionals across all levels of expertise, with a relative risk of 1.18 (95% CI 1.12–1.25, p < 0.001) and 1.05 (95% CI 1.02–1.08, p < 0.001), respectively. There was no significant difference in sensitivity and specificity of AI-assisted expert healthcare professionals versus AI, with a relative risk of 0.99 (95% CI 0.95–1.04, p = 0.787) and 1.03 (95% CI 0.97–1.08, p = 0.395), respectively. Whilst most studies to date have evaluated AI head-to-head against unassisted healthcare professionals, fewer studies examined AI as an assistive tool, despite the real-world integration of AI more likely to involve assistance than autonomy.

Introduction

Surgical and interventional procedures are an indispensable part of modern healthcare and represent the mainstay for the diagnosis and treatment of many diseases, with estimates of at least 310 million procedures performed worldwide annually1. In the digital era of healthcare, and with the emergence of fields such as surgical data science2, there is an ever-increasing availability of surgical and interventional videos, with artificial intelligence (AI) posited as a complementary adjunct to enhance the standard of care provided by healthcare professionals.

Proposed applications of AI in surgical and interventional video analysis are numerous and can be broadly categorized as spatial, such as the real-time identification of pathology or high-risk structures, and temporal, such as workflow analyses to automate procedural documentation, provide early warning prior to high-risk phases, and to predict the likelihood of adverse events3. Specific examples include using AI to identify polyps during colonoscopy, recognise normal anatomy to avoid during thyroid surgery, or detect which steps of the procedure are being performed during pituitary surgery. However, as highlighted by two previous landmark reviews comparing AI with healthcare professionals in medical imaging4,5, it is critical to distinguish between AI hype and theoretical applications generated from experimental studies and real-world applications evaluated by well-designed clinical studies. Furthermore, most studies to date evaluate the performance of AI alone against either no benchmark or solely against unassisted healthcare professionals. Whilst such evaluation likely reflects the current stage of development and clinical translation of AI, real-world integration of AI in healthcare is more likely to be in the form of AI-assistance, rather than autonomous functioning. As such, evaluation in a manner truly reflective of clinical practice remains crucial in assessing whether AI integration is of clinical benefit, and if so, for providing a framework for its safe and ethical clinical translation6.

As the evidence for the application of AI to the analysis of videos from surgical and interventional procedures grows, it is now timely to systematically evaluate this evidence. To this end, our objective was to critically appraise current methodological approaches and findings relating to AI integration in surgical and interventional video analysis, with particular emphasis on how AI performance is evaluated. Through meta-analysis, we compare the performance of AI, unassisted healthcare professionals, and AI-assisted healthcare professionals in the analysis of surgical and interventional videos.

Most studies spanned across multiple specialties, including: gastroenterology (103 studies), upper gastrointestinal surgery (57 studies), colorectal surgery (41 studies), ear, nose, and throat surgery (15 studies), pulmonology (six studies), neurosurgery (five studies), urology (five studies), hepato-pancreato-biliary surgery (four studies), obstetrics and gynaecology (four studies), anaesthetics (three studies), cardiothoracic surgery (three studies), ophthalmology (two studies), cardiology (one study), and interventional radiology (one study). One study assessed suturing skill without a specific specialty designation109. Procedures assessed varied widely, with oesophagogastroduodenoscopy (59 studies) and colonoscopy (34 studies) being the most frequent. Other procedures included laryngoscopy (nine studies), capsule endoscopy (eight studies), bronchoscopy (five studies), thyroid surgery (three studies), cataract surgery (two studies), cholangioscopy (two studies), colposcopy (two studies), endoscopic transsphenoidal surgery (two studies), microsurgical aneurysm clipping (two studies), nasal endoscopy (two studies), and robot-assisted radical prostatectomy (two studies). Fifteen other procedures were assessed in one study each.

Most (89/146) studies used solely retrospective cohorts, whilst 21 studies used only prospective cohorts, and 21 studies combined retrospective and prospective data. Fifteen studies did not adequately report on their cohorts12,23,26,35,59,60,72,82,91,104,113,118,135,139,147. A priori sample size calculations were performed in 36 studies. Data from open-access sources were used in 13 studies. Poor-quality videos and frames were reported to be excluded in 78 studies.

AI architectures

AI architectures most frequently incorporated convolutional neural networks (132 models), and less commonly vision transformers (11 models), support vector machines (three models), bag-of-words (two models), graph convolutional networks (two models), K-nearest neighbour (two models), random forests (two models), convolutional recurrent neural networks (one model), deep reinforcement learning (one model), graph attention networks (one model), K-means clustering (one model), and Siamese neural networks (one model). Four studies did not adequately report on AI model architecture7,95,107,150. Transfer learning was reported in 55 studies, with most (34/55) using the ImageNet dataset, whilst 12 studies did not specify the dataset used. Ground truth comparators used to assess accuracy of AI and healthcare professionals included: histopathology (87 studies), expert consensus (43 studies), single expert review (eight studies), clinical follow-up (six studies), urea breath test (three studies), and manual measurements (two studies). Three studies did not adequately report on their ground truth72,83,87. Whilst the same ground truth was used across all samples in most tasks, this was not the case for six studies46,52,88,96,101,119. To aid visual interpretability of AI output, 51 models provided bounding boxes, 35 models provided heatmaps, and 11 models provided segmentations.

Training and validation

Training and validation of AI models are summarised in Table 2. Most (125/146) studies trained their own AI model, whilst 21 studies assessed pre-trained models. Of the 125 studies that trained their own model, six did not report the size of their training dataset7,80,81,97,121,143, and 79 did not report on a tuning dataset. Internal validation was performed in 115 studies, where 10 studies did not report on how their training and validation datasets were split7,22,68,70,79,91,105,119,143,148. Of these 115 studies, 67 assessed video footage, 40 assessed video frames, and eight assessed a combination of both. External validation was performed in 39 studies, of which 25 assessed video footage, 11 assessed video frames, and three assessed both. Most (130/146) studies evaluated ‘AI vs unassisted healthcare professionals’, whilst 46 studies evaluated ‘AI-assisted healthcare professionals vs unassisted healthcare professionals’, and 29 studies evaluated ‘AI-assisted healthcare professionals vs AI’. The Idea, Development, Exploration, Assessment, and Long-term follow-up (IDEAL) stage of most (113/146) studies was IDEAL stage 0 (pre-clinical)6; 16 studies were stage 1 (first-in-human), 13 studies Discussion

The literature on the comparative performance of AI and healthcare professionals in surgical and interventional videos has been rapidly expanding, with over 90% (136/146) of included studies in this review published since 2020. This review aimed to take stock of the current literature to assess how AI performance is evaluated in the surgical and interventional specialties. Through meta-analysis, we quantitatively assessed the pooled performance of AI, unassisted healthcare professionals, and AI-assisted healthcare professionals across a range of tasks and specialties.

Five principal findings were established from our review. Firstly, findings from our review provide support for the potential clinical benefit of integrating AI into the analysis of videos from surgical and interventional procedures. In short, our meta-analysis showed that: AI demonstrated greater sensitivity and comparable specificity to unassisted healthcare professionals at their respective peak performance levels for both external and internal validation; AI-assisted healthcare professionals exhibited improved sensitivity and specificity compared to healthcare professionals without AI assistance; and AI-assisted expert healthcare professionals and AI alone performed at a similar level in terms of both sensitivity and specificity. Whilst the field remains nascent, these findings are encouraging and warrant further investigation of AI in the field of surgical and interventional video analysis.

Secondly, most studies to date evaluated the performance of AI alone against either no benchmark, as indicated by the significant number of studies excluded at study selection, or solely against unassisted healthcare professionals, aligning with the broader landscape of AI evaluation in other medical fields4,5. In contrast, far fewer studies evaluated AI as an assistive tool for healthcare professionals. This finding likely reflects the current early stage of development and validation of AI, with most studies included in our review being IDEAL stage 0 (pre-clinical). However, whilst evaluating AI diametrically against healthcare professionals has been valuable in demonstrating a proof-of-concept for its utility, it is not reflective of the real-world integration of AI. Instead, as a complex intervention, AI should be evaluated as close to its real-world application as possible, which is more likely to be in the form of AI-assistance, rather than autonomous functioning6,153,154.

Thirdly, as concluded in previous reviews4,5, most studies included in our review assessed AI performance in a setting and manner not reflective of clinical practice. Most studies assessed AI and healthcare professionals in isolation without additional clinical information (e.g. history or examination findings). Furthermore, over half of studies (78/146) removed poor-quality videos and frames from their datasets before analysis, despite the performance of contemporary AI architectures remaining significantly affected when encountering low-quality data, thereby inflating reported performance compared with real-world conditions155. Additionally, although all included studies focused on data from surgical and interventional videos, a significant number of studies assessed performance using solely video frames rather than actual video footage. Analysis of continuous video footage, which is the envisioned media format that will be presented to AI in most real-world surgical and interventional settings, typically requires more complex models capable of capturing temporal patterns and motion dynamics156. Of those that built AI models to analyse continuous video footage, fewer performed a real-time assessment of AI performance that matched real clinical environments, with most studies being retrospective and in silico. Ultimately, current evaluation skews the data in favour of AI by either replicating the conditions in which AI is trained or relying too heavily on conditions that are optimal, thereby not truly reflecting the real-world. This may also explain why so few AI models survive as they proceed down their evaluative pathway in real-world settings. Future studies should ensure that their design reflects the real-world clinical environment as close as possible from the earliest stages of evaluation, to prioritise AI models with the highest potential, whilst minimising the waste of valuable resources on models unlikely to succeed.

Fourthly, the generalisability of the included studies is limited by their design, as most studies in our review performed only internal validation. Nevertheless, our finding that there was no significant overestimation in the comparative performance of AI and healthcare professionals at internal and external validation is encouraging. It underscores the value of both validation types: internal validation as an initial indicator of a model’s potential, and external validation as a subsequent indicator of a model’s generalisability. Furthermore, most studies relied on high-quality video capturing equipment, which further restricts generalisability of their findings, particularly in the context of resource-limited healthcare systems. Moving forward, the development and sharing of publicly available datasets - including both algorithms and diverse, global training data - would be beneficial for improving model generalisability. Complementary strategies, such as multi-centre collaborations, federated learning across heterogenous sites, and synthetic data augmentation, will further enhance the robustness and generalisability of AI models.

Fifthly, we observed substantial heterogeneity in the transparency of reporting across studies, both in terms of AI development and evaluation, limiting the reproducibility of AI models and study results. Whilst only a small minority of studies (6/125) that developed their own model failed to report on their training datasets, the majority (79/125) inadequately reported their tuning datasets. The most rigorous studies provided clear flow diagrams outlining training, tuning, and validation data splits, along with their respective sources27. Furthermore, poor reporting at the evaluation stage hindered, or in some cases prevented, scrutiny of reported results. The development and widespread adoption of standardised reporting guidelines for AI algorithms analysing clinical video data, building on frameworks such as PROBAST + AI and TRIPOD + AI for prediction models157,158, would significantly improve transparency and reproducibility across the field.

AI integration into healthcare is unlikely to replace healthcare professionals, but rather complement their decision-making - this is particularly the case in surgical and interventional procedures, where healthcare professionals are essential for procedural execution, irrespective of any comparative difference in performance between healthcare professionals and AI in specific tasks159. We expect that as AI algorithms proceed along their evaluative pathway, as outlined by the IDEAL framework6, emphasis will shift from the proof-of-concept ‘AI versus healthcare professional’ comparisons towards an ‘AI-assisted healthcare professional’ framework. Extending this, we would suggest that all evaluations of AI, irrespective of stage of development or validation, should reflect how AI is likely to be used - as an assistive tool in surgical and interventional practice. Conceptually, adopting the ‘AI-assisted healthcare professional’ framework from the early stages of model development may yield dividends by providing a more real-world evaluation of its application, and, in turn, a more realistic roadmap for its integration. This will be important for addressing current challenges in clinical AI adoption and is supported by recent expert consensus160.

From the clinician's perspective, continuous feedback from healthcare professionals has been shown to improve model accuracy, generalisability, and clinician utility161,162, whilst their awareness of specific AI biases can be key to mitigating them163. Furthermore, whilst traditional performance measures may indicate that AI performs at the same level as (or even surpasses) that of healthcare professionals with or without AI assistance, it is important to acknowledge that AI is trained to purely optimise on a mathematical basis, whereas healthcare professionals prioritise for clinical outcomes. This distinction is particularly important in the high-stakes context of surgical and interventional decision-making, where it is often more desirable to avoid rare but critical events (e.g. a potential cancer diagnosis) rather than merely maximising overall performance164. From the patient perspective, patient attitudes towards clinical AI systems show a strong preference for maintaining healthcare professionals within the decision-making process, maximising the benefits from both healthcare professionals and AI165.

Importantly, although the ‘AI-assisted healthcare professional’ framework may be appealing in theory, its practical implementation presents distinct challenges. Clinicians must be able to recognise potential AI biases, a task complicated by the ‘black-box’ nature of many models, in which decision-making processes of AI are not readily interpretable166. This lack of transparency can lead to both over-reliance and under-reliance on AI outputs167. Optimising the human-AI interaction will require strategies that address both the technology itself and the human factors influencing its use. Explainable AI is a subfield that has been gaining traction in recent years as a potential solution by increasing human understanding of AI decision-making168. The formal integration of AI training into medical education and specialty training programmes will also be essential to ensure clinicians can use these tools to their full potential169. Crucially, evaluating AI from the outset in terms of its ability to assist healthcare professionals will allow early identification of issues related to human-AI interaction and trust, preventing the advancement of models that perform well in isolation but falter when integrated into clinical decision-making.

Moreover, the scientific community should critically appraise the metrics that define AI success in order to better match real-world applications. This includes moving beyond simplistic measures, such as true and false positives or negatives, towards composite measures that reflect clinical priorities by weighting errors by their potential clinical consequences170. Furthermore, whilst perhaps initially surprising, our finding that the current performance of AI-assisted healthcare professionals is comparable to AI alone aligns with a recent meta-analysis of human-AI collaboration171. The likely explanation for this is that AI models are already performing close to the upper limit of current performance metrics, rendering further gains in performance by AI-assisted healthcare professionals challenging to both assess and achieve. Non-linear evaluation scales have been proposed as one potential solution for assessing incremental improvements and comparing AI, unassisted healthcare professionals, and AI-assisted healthcare professionals in the future172.

Whilst there have been several published reviews evaluating AI for decision support using text and imaging, mainly within the diagnostic specialties, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis (to our knowledge) to focus on videos across all surgical and interventional procedures - that is, not just for diagnostic purposes, but also for treatment. By incorporating a wide inclusion criterion of studies from any surgical or interventional procedure, we provide the most comprehensive summary of the literature to date. Given the early stages of the field at present, this decision was purposeful to ensure that our findings were generalizable across specialties and in order to guide the next phase of AI development and evaluation. Following our broad search with nearly 38,000 studies identified, we strictly selected only the highest quality of studies, resulting in less than 0.5% of initially identified studies included in our final synthesis. Rigorous meta-analytical techniques, including selection of the model with the least over- and under-fitting for each analysis, and sensitivity analyses that closely match the results from our primary analyses, provide further confidence in our findings.

Despite these strengths, our study has several limitations. Although the introduction of several quality assessment tools for AI in healthcare, including PROBAST + AI and TRIPOD + AI157,158, we have not conducted a systematic assessment of potential biases across included studies. This decision stems primarily from the inherently broad scope of our review, which encompasses a diverse range of tasks, including both diagnostic and workflow analyses that cannot be uniformly assessed using a single tool. Nevertheless, we observed substantial heterogeneity in the transparency of reporting across studies, both in terms of AI development and evaluation, which prevented the construction of contingency tables for several studies otherwise eligible for meta-analysis, potentially impacting our findings. Whilst we were accepting of a large degree of between-study heterogeneity due to the broad nature of our review, our review lacks a quantitative exploration of heterogeneity beyond the between-study variance and covariance values provided in Supplementary Tables 4–6. Furthermore, we acknowledge that our meta-analysis inherently favours studies, and hence AI applications, that report performance metrics that permit the construction of a 2 × 2 contingency table. Extending this, our strict inclusion criteria may introduce a selection bias towards AI applications in surgical and interventional procedures that can be quantitatively assessed. Consequently, we recognise that our review omits several important AI applications that either can only be or, at least thus far, only have been qualitatively assessed. Finally, whilst our review focused on objective performance metrics, we acknowledge that these measures alone are unlikely to fully capture the complexities of surgical and interventional tasks. Research beyond conventional metrics - particularly those assessing the subjective dynamics of clinician-AI interactions, such as an AI model’s ability to assist healthcare professionals - will be essential in determining how this technology can be safely and effectively incorporated into real-world practice173.

In conclusion, the literature provides mounting evidence supporting the integration of AI into surgical and interventional procedures. Findings from our exploratory meta-analysis indicate that whilst early studies suggest that AI may perform better than unassisted healthcare professionals, they are limited by evaluation methods that fail to truly reflect the complexities of real-world clinical environments. More compelling, however, is the potential for AI to enhance healthcare professional performance, highlighting the need for future evaluation to shift towards an ‘AI-assisted healthcare professional’ framework. Realising this shift will introduce its own challenges, both in terms of developing AI models that complement clinical expertise and establishing robust evaluation frameworks to assess their real-world impact.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Supplementary Table 7)174. The protocol was registered on PROSPERO (ID: CRD42024555207).

Search strategy and selection criteria

The search strategy was developed and iteratively refined, including review by a medical librarian at the University of Cambridge (IK). Searches were performed in Ovid Medline (Wolters Kluwer, Netherlands), Web of Science, Scopus, and IEEE Xplore from inception to 5th June 2025 (Supplementary Table 8). Search sensitivity was evaluated against 11 studies known to meet inclusion criteria: all papers were successfully captured.

Primary studies that developed or validated a clinical AI model for the analysis of surgical or interventional videos (intervention), and additionally quantitatively evaluated its performance (outcome) against healthcare professionals with or without AI assistance (comparator), as outlined in Table 3, were included. Surgical or interventional procedures were defined as ‘invasive procedures, where deliberate access to the body is gained via an incision, percutaneous puncture, or natural orifice, performed by a healthcare professional using instruments including, but not limited to, endoscopes, catheters, scalpels, scissors, devices, and tubes’175. Studies were excluded if the title and abstract indicated failure to meet all of the specified inclusion criteria or satisfied at least one of the exclusion criteria. Two reviewers (ARF and KJS) independently performed title and abstract screening with blinding using Rayyan (Rayyan Systems Inc., Cambridge, USA). A pilot screen of 100 publications was first conducted to ensure concordance between reviewers. Any disagreements following unblinding were resolved by discussion between the reviewers until mutual agreement was reached. Data extraction

Articles were retrieved for full text screening and data extraction using a predefined data extraction sheet. Extracted variables included: author, year of publication, study design, subspecialty (including target condition and procedure), data source and range, sample characteristics (including selection criteria, size, and age), AI model (including architecture type, backbone model, and the presence or absence of transfer learning), type of comparison (i.e. unassisted healthcare professionals, AI-assisted healthcare professionals, or both), ground truth comparison, dataset splits (i.e. training, tuning defined as hyperparameter optimization, and validation defined as the final evaluation of model performance), quantitative outcomes of performance, relevant statistical analyses, main conclusions, and limitations. Where possible, 2 × 2 contingency tables were constructed consisting of true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives. Full text screening and data extraction were assessed independently in duplicate (ARF and KJS/JKD/TF) with blinding. Any disagreements following unblinding were resolved by discussion between reviewers until mutual agreement was reached. Any outstanding questions were resolved by discussion with the senior reviewers (SCW and AD).

Data analysis and reporting

A meta-analysis was conducted using a bivariate binomial method on studies providing contingency tables for two or more comparators (direct within-study comparison)176. Contingency tables were first grouped based on comparators: (1) ‘AI vs unassisted healthcare professionals’, (2) ‘AI-assisted healthcare professionals vs unassisted healthcare professionals’, and (3) ‘AI-assisted healthcare professionals vs AI’. For our ‘AI vs unassisted healthcare professionals’ analysis, contingency tables were further stratified based on the type of validation (external or internal, where the latter included temporally split data).

For our ‘AI vs unassisted healthcare professionals’ analysis, sub-group analyses were performed, evaluating only the single highest accuracy contingency table for AI and unassisted healthcare professionals in each task, to compare their respective peak performance levels. For our ‘AI-assisted healthcare professionals vs unassisted healthcare professionals’ and ‘AI-assisted healthcare professionals vs AI’ analyses, sub-group analyses were performed to investigate differences in healthcare professional performance at the non-expert and expert levels, with experts broadly defined as the level of consultants or attending physicians.

In our primary analysis, contingency tables for each comparator were averaged to ensure a direct one-to-one comparison between comparators and to prevent undue emphasis on studies with more individuals within each comparator group. As a sensitivity analysis, we also assessed the unaveraged contingency tables for each analysis to assess whether there was a significant change in results when information regarding the number of each type of comparator was incorporated.

For each analysis, seven generalised linear mixed models were generated (Supplementary Table 9). Model A was the most simplistic and assumed a combined sensitivity and combined specificity for both comparators at the fixed effects and random effects level. Models B-D similarly incorporated a combined sensitivity and combined specificity at the random effects level, but assumed variable variance for sensitivity and/or specificity at the fixed effects level. Finally, models E-G incorporated separate random effects for the sensitivity and/or specificity of both comparators, whilst also assuming variable variance for sensitivity and/or specificity at the fixed effects level. The Akaike information criterion was used to select the final model by considering the trade-off between goodness of fit and simplicity of each model. As a secondary assessment of model fit, likelihood ratio tests were conducted comparing the final model with the two models that differed by a single step change, where the null hypothesis was rejected for p < 0.05. The logarithmic odds ratio from the final selected model was used to assess statistical differences in sensitivity and specificity between comparators, where the null hypothesis was rejected for p < 0.05. Relative differences in sensitivity and specificity between comparators in the final model were then calculated with 95% confidence intervals. Results of the bivariate binomial model were transformed to coefficients of the hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic model, and the curves were visualised176. Due to the broad nature of our review across multiple types of tasks, procedures, and specialties, we were accepting of a large degree of heterogeneity across studies.
