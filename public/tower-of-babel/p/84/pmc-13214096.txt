Lesion formation and impedance dependence of v-HPSD ablation (QDOT-MICRO™) versus conventional power-control (SmartTouch™ SF): a head-to-head comparison in an ex vivo porcine model
Authors: Jinhong Guo, Yingjie Wang, Meixue Jia, Shuai Chen, Bin Ning
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to compare the lesion characteristics of v-HPSD and conventional PC ablation modes and to quantitatively evaluate their dependence on impedance variation. MethodsThe study was performed using an ex vivo porcine heart model. Ablations in the v-HPSD group were carried out with a QDOT-MICRO™ catheter, whereas conventional power-control (PC) ablations employed a Thermocool-STSF® catheter, set at a fixed power of 40 W and terminated upon reaching an ablation index of 500. To study the effect of baseline impedance, it was dynamically adjusted to low, medium, and high preset levels by incorporating a sliding rheostat in series with the ablation circuit. ResultsAblation lesions in the PC group were characterized by significantly smaller superficial and maximum diameters but greater depth compared to the v-HPSD group (Superficial diameter: 5.19±0.71 mm vs. 5.83±0.73 mm, p<0.01; Maximum diameter: 7.04±0.95 mm vs. 7.79±0.96 mm, p<0.01; Depth: 4.67±0.22 mm vs. 3.50±0.40 mm, p<0.01). Although the mean lesion volume was larger in the PC group, this difference did not reach statistical significance (74.08±30.02 mm³ vs. 65.98±21.99 mm³, p=0.097).The ablation mode and impedance level demonstrated a significant interaction, which differentially affected the lesion parameters: highly significant for maximum depth and mean volume (P < 0.001), significant for superficial diameter (P < 0.05), and non-significant for maximum diameter (P = 0.587).ConclusionThe v-HPSD mode exhibits superior adaptability, achieving comparable overall lesion volume in high-impedance settings by generating wider and depth-stable lesions, which underscores its enhanced performance predictability.Keywords: Radiofrequency ablation, Power control(PC), Very high Power short duration(v-HPSD), Baseline impedance, Lesion size

Introduction

Radiofrequency ablation is one of the most established and widely adopted techniques for the treatment of atrial fibrillation (AF) [1]. Its principle involves delivering high-frequency alternating current through an electrode catheter tip and a dispersive electrode pad attached to the patient’s skin, forming a closed circuit. As the current passes through the myocardial tissue, the electrical resistance of the tissue generates Joule heat, causing a localized temperature increase that leads to irreversible necrosis of cells, thereby achieving the therapeutic goal of isolating abnormal electrical signals [2]. The electrode-tissue interface temperature exhibits a positive linear correlation with both the depth and width of the resulting ablation lesion. Furthermore, a positive correlation exists between the electrical current (I) and temperature, meaning that an increase in output current leads to intensified tissue heating and, consequently, the formation of a larger ablation lesion [3, 4]. Under power control mode, the circuit impedance influences the power-current relationship. Therefore, maintaining a fixed power output does not guarantee a stable current, as it is affected by the initial impedance and its subsequent changes during the procedure. This variability in current prevents consistent and predictable lesion formation [5]. In irrigated ablation catheters, the temperature sensor is exposed to the internal cooling fluid. This exposure, especially during RF energy delivery at high irrigation rates, artificially depresses the measured temperature value, thus compromising its accuracy in reflecting the true temperature at the tissue-electrode interface. The QDOT MICRO catheter represents a new generation of temperature-controlled, auto-feedback ablation catheters designed for very-high-power short-duration (v-HPSD) ablation. It typically utilizes a 90 W power setting over a 4-second duration. Compared to existing catheters incapable of adjusting radiofrequency output based on the tissue-electrode interface temperature, this technology holds particular potential value. The overall impedance during ablation comprises two components. The first component is the resistance generated by the tissue and blood surrounding the ablation catheter tip. This resistance is relatively stable and is primarily influenced by the contact area between the tip electrode and the myocardial tissue. The second component arises from the medium between the catheter tip and the dispersive patch, which is mainly affected by factors such as skin, air, and adipose tissue, and exhibits significant variation among different patients [6]. We observed significant inter-patient variability in overall impedance. We hypothesize that these individual differences in impedance characteristics may lead to inconsistent lesion formation, consequently affecting postoperative recurrence rates. This study aimed to directly compare the lesion characteristics and their impedance dependence between the v-HPSD and conventional PC ablation modes. In an ex vivo porcine heart model, we employed dedicated catheters (QDOT-MICRO™ and Thermocool Smart Touch™ SF) to perform the respective ablation strategies. By actively modulating impedance, we quantitatively assessed its impact on the resultant tissue lesions.

Methods

Animal models and ablation parameters

This study utilized ten ex vivo porcine hearts as the experimental model. Porcine hearts sourced from a commercial supplier. As shown in Fig. 1, the myocardial tissue of the left ventricle was immersed in a T-shaped water tank. The tank was filled with an adequate amount of 0.9% sodium chloride solution, maintained at a temperature of 37 ± 0.5 °C to simulate the physiological environment. For the v-HPSD group, ablations were performed using a QDOT-MICRO™ catheter with a power setting of 90 W for 4 seconds, a temperature limit of 60°C, and an irrigation rate of 8 mL/min. For the conventional power-control (PC) group, ablations were conducted using a Thermocool-STSF® catheter with a fixed output power of 40 W and an ablation index (AI) target of 500. In both groups, the real-time catheter-tissue contact force was monitored via the Carto3 three-dimensional electroanatomic mapping system. The operator maintained catheter-tissue contact force within the target range of 10-15g through manual fine-tuning based on real-time feedback. Additionally, the catheter was maintained perpendicular to the tissue surface during ablation to standardize these two critical variables. To investigate the impact of baseline impedance on ablation efficacy, a sliding rheostat was connected in series within the ablation circuit. By adjusting its resistance, the baseline impedance was dynamically modulated and categorized into three groups: low (100-135 Ω), medium (135-170 Ω), and high (170-205 Ω), thereby simulating ablation lesions under different impedance conditions.

Data recording and injury assessment

For each catheter type, the baseline impedance was documented for every RF ablation procedure. Following each procedure, the tissue and catheter tip were inspected for evidence of charring, thrombus, or steam pop-associated tissue rupture. Subsequently, the myocardial tissue was repositioned to maintain adequate inter-lesion spacing, with each segment of myocardium receiving 10 ablation treatments at different locations. Each ablation lesion was marked with a numbered pin immediately after completion. After completion of ablation, transfer to Triphenyltetrazolium Chloride (TTC) for reactive staining, and after completion of staining, all ablation foci were centrally transected in a direction perpendicular to the surface, and the maximum depth and diameter of each foci were measured using digital precision calipers, as shown in Fig. 2. The volume of the lesions was calculated by the oblate spheroid equation [(4πr²R)/3] (where r is the transverse radius and R is the axial radius in millimeters), a method consistent with a previous study [7].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Normality was assessed, and comparisons were conducted using an independent samples t-test or one-way ANOVA for parametric data, and the Kruskal-Wallis test for non-parametric data. To account for multiple lesions obtained from the same porcine heart, linear mixed-effects models were employed with ‘pig heart’ (heart_id) included as a random intercept. Prior to model construction, null models were fit to calculate intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) and quantify clustering strength for each parameter. For parameters with ICC > 0, linear mixed-effects models were constructed with impedance group (low, medium, high) and catheter mode (PC v v-HPSD) as fixed effects, including their interaction term. For parameters with significant interactions, simple effects analyses were conducted with Bonferroni correction.All statistical tests were two-tailed, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Unless otherwise specified (for simple effects analyses where Bonferroni correction was applied), p-values were unadjusted for multiplicity. The reported p-values for main effects and interactions represent nominal significance levels without correction for multiple comparisons across different outcome parameters, consistent with the exploratory nature of this ex vivo study. This approach is consistent with the exploratory nature of this ex vivo study.All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 30, and figures were created using GraphPad Prism 10.

Steam-pop formation

In this study sample, steam explosions were rare events (overall incidence 0.83%). The sole steam explosion occurred in the low-impedance group during PC ablation mode (1/20, 5.0%), while none were observed in all other groups (including all V-HPSD impedance groups and the medium- and high-impedance PC groups, totaling 100 ablation points) (0%). Due to the small number of events, intergroup comparisons did not reach statistical significance (Fisher’s exact probability test, p = 0.222).

Discussion

This study was designed to systematically evaluate the independent effects and interaction between catheter type and impedance on radiofrequency ablation lesion formation. Our principal findings are as follows:

Stable Divergence in Lesion Morphology: A stable morphological dichotomy was observed across all impedance levels: v-HPSD consistently produced significantly wider but shallower lesions, whereas conventional PC ablation generated significantly narrower but deeper lesions, as evidenced by measurements of superficial diameter and maximum depth (all comparisons, p < 0.01).

Stable Divergence in Lesion Morphology: A stable morphological dichotomy was observed across all impedance levels: v-HPSD consistently produced significantly wider but shallower lesions, whereas conventional PC ablation generated significantly narrower but deeper lesions, as evidenced by measurements of superficial diameter and maximum depth (all comparisons, p < 0.01).

Performance Crossover and Reversal: A fundamental reversal in the performance hierarchy was identified concerning mean lesion volume. At low impedance, the PC group yielded a significantly larger volume. However, this advantage diminished and became non-significant at medium and high impedance levels, where the two modalities achieved comparable total ablation volumes.

At low impedance, the PC group yielded a significantly larger volume. However, this advantage diminished and became non-significant at medium and high impedance levels, where the two modalities achieved comparable total ablation volumes.

Impact of catheter type on ablation lesions

Our comparison of the QDOT MICRO (v-HPSD) and ThermoCool SmartTouch SF (PC) catheters showed that v-HPSD ablation is characterized by the creation of wider and more superficial lesions. Notably, this distinct morphological profile did not translate into a significant difference in the total ablation volume, which was similar between groups. An animal study by TAKIGAWA [8]et al. similarly demonstrated that, compared to conventional radiofrequency ablation, v-HPSD ablation created larger focal lesions [superficial length: 7.7 (7.0-8.7) mm vs. 6.0 (4.9–6.8) mm; maximum diameter: 7.8 (7.1–8.7) mm vs. 6.6 (5.6–7.8) mm; depth: 2.7 (2.3–3.4) mm vs. 3.8 (3.0-4.4) mm; p < 0.05 for all dimensions], though the lesion volume showed no significant difference [86.8 (63.6-115.4) mm³ vs. 89.4 (55.7-131.7) mm³, p = 0.97]. This phenomenon arises from fundamental differences in energy delivery and tissue heating mechanisms. Power-controlled mode operates on a fixed power output, whereby energy is deposited via resistive heating directly within a very confined volume of tissue in contact with the catheter tip, generating extremely high temperatures. This heat then slowly propagates into deeper tissue layers through thermal conduction. Due to this initial concentration of energy, the thermal gradient favors propagation in the depth direction, thereby facilitating the formation of deeper lesions. The core of v-HPSD ablation is temperature control. When the catheter tip temperature reaches 60 °C, the system delivers microsecond-pulsed energy outputs to maintain the target temperature and prevent tissue overheating and carbonization. Due to the extremely short application time, heat lacks sufficient duration to diffuse deeply into the tissue via thermal conduction, resulting in shallower lesion depth. However, the high power combined with this unique temperature-control mode enables the thermal effect to distribute more rapidly and uniformly radially, thereby generating wider superficial lesions. By comparison, the ablation lesions produced by the two catheters in our experiment were noted to be smaller - a finding potentially attributable to the substantially wider overall impedance range in our study. The actual influence of different overall impedance conditions on catheter efficacy is differential, as detailed subsequently.

Impact of overall impedance on ablation lesions

Studies have shown significant variation in baseline impedance (range: 100–190 Ω) [6] among patients undergoing pulmonary vein isolation, particularly in elderly patients, those with dry skin, or individuals with high body mass index. Our study found that as the overall impedance increases, the superficial width, depth, maximum diameter, and volume of ablation lesions decrease accordingly. These findings are consistent with previous research [6, 7, 9, 10]. This occurs because impedance influences current, energy deposition, and heat conduction. In a fixed-power mode, where power P = I²*R, a higher initial impedance results in reduced current flow. This, in turn, diminishes the efficiency of resistive heating in the tissue, leading to insufficient energy deposition. Consequently, in a high-impedance environment, the initial energy deposition is reduced, making it difficult to create an effective lesion with sufficient depth and volume. In their study using isolated porcine hearts, Barkagan [6]et al. categorized impedance into three groups. Radiofrequency applications at the lowest baseline impedance range (lowest third, 100–130Ω) resulted in larger lesion dimensions (width, 8.3 ± 1.4 mm; depth, 3.6 ± 0.7 mm) compared with intermediate baseline impedance (131–180Ω, width, 5.9 ± 0.9 mm; depth, 2.6 ± 0.3 mm) and high baseline impedance (180–224Ω, width, 5.0 ± 0.7 mm; depth, 2.3 ± 0.4 mm, P < 0.0001 for all comparisons). By comparison, the ablation lesions generated in our study were relatively smaller. This discrepancy can likely be explained by the distinct parameters used in our experimental protocol. Moreover, our findings demonstrated catheter-specific variations in the effect of impedance, with detailed results presented in the following section.

Impact of impedance variation on the two ablation catheters

Pattern of dynamic impedance change

The first finding is that the dynamic change patterns of impedance of the two catheters during ablation are quite different. At the same initial impedance level, the impedance decline observed in the v-HPSD group was significantly greater than that in the traditional power control group (all P < 0.01). Especially, with the change of the preset initial impedance from low to high, the impedance decline of the traditional catheter group decreased sharply (from 21.95 Ω to 7.95 Ω), while the impedance decline of the v-HPSD group increased significantly (from 26.35 Ω to 36.80 Ω). This phenomenon can likely be attributed to fundamental differences in their energy delivery mechanisms. The temperature-controlled, very high-power strategy employed by the v-HPSD mode may induce more pronounced electrophysiological alterations and electrochemical effects in the tissue. This mechanism potentially enables an adaptive optimization of energy delivery in high-impedance environments, thereby ensuring effective tissue heating.

Response of ablation lesions to impedance variation

Our analysis of ablation lesions further revealed a significant interaction between impedance and catheter type. Lesion Depth: The lesion depth created by the conventional power-controlled (PC) catheter exhibited a marked stepwise decrease as impedance increased (Low:5.52 mm→ High: 3.84 mm, P < 0.001). In contrast, the lesion depth with the v-HPSD catheter, while also decreasing with higher impedance, demonstrated a more gradual decline. Critically, no statistical difference was observed between medium and high impedance groups (3.50 mm vs. 3.31 mm, P 0.067), indicating superior stability. Lesion Width: Both the surface and maximum diameters of lesions decreased with rising impedance in both catheter groups. However, the v-HPSD group generated significantly wider lesions across all impedance levels (Surface Diameter at High Impedance: 5.18 mm vs. 4.27 mm, P < 0.001). This suggests that the v-HPSD technology facilitates more effective lateral distribution of energy, thereby mitigating the limitations imposed by high impedance on lesion expansion.

Lesion volume response to impedance and the performance crossover

A critical performance crossover was identified in lesion volume, a primary determinant of overall ablation outcome. In low impedance environments, the damage volume generated by traditional power control catheters is significantly larger than that of v-HPSD catheters (109.02 mm³ vs. 87.91 mm³, P < 0.001). However, when the impedance increased to medium and high levels, there was no statistically significant difference in the damage volume between the two types of catheters (medium impedance: P = 0.178; High impedance: P = 0.157). This may be due to the traditional catheter obtaining a larger volume at low impedance through depth advantage, but its performance deteriorates sharply with increasing impedance; QDOT-MICROTM catheter compensates for the shortcomings in absolute depth through its excellent width advantage and stable depth maintenance capability, thereby achieving an overall damage level comparable to traditional techniques in medium to high impedance environments.

This study confirms that impedance changes are a key factor in modulating the morphology and volume of ablation lesions. The performance of traditional power control catheters exhibits comprehensive impedance dependence, and their ability to cause damage is significantly limited in high impedance environments. The v-HPSD catheter, with its unique energy delivery mode, can better adapt to impedance changes, especially in high impedance environments where it can generate wider and volume equivalent damage, demonstrating better performance stability and predictability.

Clinical implications

v-HPSD may serve as a highly effective alternative in regions of high impedance, such as scar or fibrotic tissue. Although it does not achieve the same penetration depth as PC, it can create a comparable overall lesion volume by producing a wider lesion. This characteristic may be more advantageous for ensuring the continuity and transmurality of linear ablation lines.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The ex vivo model cannot replicate the complex LA anatomy and its effects on catheter stability. Limited by the available equipment, only the overall impedance value could be obtained, which may have affected the accuracy of the ablation lesion size assessment to some extent. Furthermore, some measurement error in evaluating lesion dimensions is inevitable. To minimize this, two independent researchers performed repeated measurements at each ablation site. Although we used an alternating allocation method to ensure comparable freshness across specimens, some selection bias may remain. Second, while we employed mixed-effects models to account for clustering, the relatively small number of hearts (n = 10) may limit the precision of random effect estimates, particularly for parameters with weaker clustering, such as lesion volume. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate our findings. The emergence of pulsed field ablation (PFA), a paradigm-shifting technology, is transforming clinical practice. Consequently, this shift diminishes the immediate clinical relevance of our findings for institutions that have already adopted PFA as the routine first-line therapy. This study was conducted in an ex vivo model, lacking physiological blood flow cooling (heat sink effect) [11, 12]. Experiments have demonstrated that in the absence of active cooling regulation, the average cross-sectional area of ablation lesions in regions adjacent to major blood vessels can be reduced by more than 30%, with maximum temperatures 5–10 °C lower than in regions farther from blood vessels [13]. This limitation affects the interpretation of v-HPSD results because, in temperature mode, the system perceives slow heating due to heat sink effects and may automatically increase power to reach the target temperature more quickly [14].The temperature readings at the distal end of the catheter in vitro experiments cannot reflect the state cooled by blood flow in vivo. Furthermore, v-HPSD is designed to outpace the heat sink effect, causing injury before the blood cooling effect can fully take effect [15], so this may differ from clinical applications. The observed morphological differences in injury patterns between v-HPSD and PC modes in this study require further validation in in vivo models with intact blood flow to clarify how heat sink effects modulate the relative efficacy of these two energy delivery strategies.

A specific methodological consideration is our use of an external rheostat to modulate baseline circuit impedance. While this approach provides excellent control for isolating the effect of total impedance on ablation outcomes, it is an intentional simplification. The rheostat introduces a purely resistive, centralized load that does not replicate the complex, distributed nature of biological impedance in vivo, which arises from a combination of tissue properties, interstitial fluid, blood volume, and catheter-tissue interface conditions [16]. Consequently, our model is highly effective for demonstrating the principle of impedance-dependent lesion modulation but cannot precisely predict the quantitative behavior of a given catheter in all specific clinical scenarios where impedance is dynamically affected by factors such as perfusion and tissue dehydration. The conclusions regarding trends are robust within this controlled framework, but direct extrapolation of absolute impedance values to patient settings requires caution.

Conclusions

This study confirmed through an in vitro model that v-HPSD and traditional PC ablation modes produce morphologically distinct damage. The v-HPSD mode produces wide and shallow damage, and its surface diameter is less affected by impedance, especially with significant advantages under high and medium impedance. The traditional PC mode produces narrow and deep damage, but both its depth and width decay sharply with increasing impedance. This significant interaction suggests that the v-HPSD mode may provide more stable and predictable therapeutic effects than traditional modes when performing linear ablation in high impedance or scar matrix.
