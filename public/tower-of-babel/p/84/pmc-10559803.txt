Ecological footprint, natural resource rent, and industrial production in MENA region: Empirical evidence using the SDM model
Authors: Wided Ragmoun
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBased on a spatial approach, this study aims to test and appreciate the relationship between natural resource rents, industrial production, and ecological footprint (EFP) for 17 countries in the MENA region over the period 2000–2018. Findings demonstrate the existence of (i) statical significant direct effects between environmental degradation, the level of local development, the resource rent, and the rate of industrialization. (ii) a significant positive spatial autocorrelation in EFP levels with a clear trajectory dependence characteristic in their geographic distribution. (iii) a positive interdependence between economic development, the level of industrialization, and resource rent with neighboring countries; (iv) only renewable energy conception has a negative interdependence with neighboring countries.Based on our result, regional planning can be dressed to maintain environmental quality in the region by defining the adequate compensation process between countries in the region. Developing a bio-economy seems to be a collective-collaborative process to maintain economic growth and industrial production without destroying the environment.Keywords: Ecological footprint, Economic growth, Industrialization, Resource rent, SDM model, Spatial effect

Introduction

One of the critical wild world challenges is sustainable development and especially environmental quality. In this state, human activities are still considered the main threat to environmental preservation by causing ecological distortions and climate change which are irreversible ([ [1,2]]). Proportionally, this activity is still needed for economic development and growth ([3]). Consequently, maintaining a balance between economic interest and environmental preservation becomes a very important research topic.

Recently, environmental quality and its importance become limited to the definition of an adequate climate system ([4]) which is called ecological footprint (EFP). EFP as a multidimensional concept dealing with environmental degradation, was developed and adopted by many empirical studies ([5]), and due to its importance, many studies were conducted to define EF and its determinants in the MENA region ([ [[6], [7], [8]]]) and others regions.

This concept is usually used, in recent research, to appreciate the effect of human activity on environmental quality ([ [[9], [10], [11]]]), and other natural aspects such as forest products, carbon footprint, and infrastructure ([12]) caused by urbanization, economic growth, and foreign direct investment ([ [[13], [14], [15]]). In the same line of thought, other empirical studies associated the use of renewable energy and its effect on EF ([ [15,16]]). Industrialization was also defined as a major determinant of environmental deterioration with a direct impact ([ [17,18]]). However, we can easily conclude that the role of these two last variables (use of renewable energy and industrialization) continues to be ambiguous until they are treated separately ([19]) and the majority of results in this way seem to be unclear and contradictory. Another group of studies tried to test an associated effect of two or more variables simultaneously [ [14,[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]] that investigate the eventual effects of renewable energy and economic growth on the EFP. They found that economic growth increases the EFP but industrial activity while using renewable energy, decreases the EFP, and the simultaneous effect wasn't confirmed.

There is another group of researchers, like [25,26] who suppose that this effect differs between countries (upper and lower quantiles) [27]. contradicts this report and supports the same effect for all quantiles. Under this hypothesis, some researchers tried to associate one or more factors in the same model to verify if this effect would differ or not. Here [2], claims that urbanization and renewable energy use matter much more for environmental quality and reduce the EFP. They insist on the quality of the corresponding energy consumption to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) in both developed and developing countries. Trying to study this same combination [21] reveals that urbanization has a positive effect on environmental degradation for both Countries of the G7 and MENA region ([28]) contrary to renewable energy which does not affect environmental quality. To explain this [18], concludes that the corresponding effect on EFP is directly related to the decrease in biocapacity and the abundant use of renewable energy [29]. argue that the energy quality problem is the most critical aspect directly associated with environmental degradation which cannot be delimited despite the amount of eventual technological development. The same conclusion is available for African economies ([30]). For this variable, energy use, a general trend supports that its effect exists but differs between countries: it is more intense for countries with extractive industries ([30]), acceptable if the industry is able to improve productivity, and the flow of production factors ([18]).

[31] Explain that this effect varies with the nature of regulations in the country: strict or weak regulations. In this way, they argue that developing countries will have dirty industries with weak regulations, and developed countries, because of their very strict regulations, use available energy with caution.

As we can see, a deeper analysis of previous research reveals a great difference between the results. Each variable can have a different impact on environmental degradation if the context varies, regulations differ or the number of variables according to a simultaneous or independent approach. Some researchers suppose that this difference depends on the way to approach these variables or the nature of the econometric model used for each research and the variables. For us, we will suggest, as [32] that there is spatial interdependence between neighboring countries which explains the divergence of results according to the transboundary approach. Further, many empirical and theoretical researchers confirm this hypothesis [33]. reported that some pollution effects will spread across borders to affect environmental quality in neighboring countries and this effect was empirically tested in other studies ([ [[34], [35], [36]]]).

In this case, this research completes and enriches the very limited studies using a spatial approach to delimit and define different determinants of ecological footprint. By dressing an exhaustive list of the general factors causing ecological footprint in the same country and proportionally to neighboring countries, we can prevent their effects and define friendly ecological alternatives. We admit that the divergence between results requires more deeper and specific analysis to find ‘the best path’ for the ecological footprint. Using different variables in the same model according to a logical association through a different approach can be more reasonable and effective in decreasing EFP and stopping environmental degradation.

More precisely, our interest is oriented to EFP in the MENA region due to its considerable environmental issues. This region is characterized by abundant and diversified natural resources which make the EFP one of the most critical problem in protecting these resources and delimiting their use for future generation ([7]). Especially, if we remember that its ecological footprints (EFP) continue to increase because most of them are using these resources (natural and energy resources) to sustain economic growth ([ [37,38]]).

[39] Studying determinants of EFP in MENA region demonstrates that almost all countries in this region indicate a high impact of a carbon footprint on global warming in 2016. If this continues, the MENA countries will continue to suffer more and more from deforestation and water shortages ([38]).

At this level, our research has gained critical importance in stopping or reducing environmental degradation in this region. By identifying the main factors of EFP, we will able to prevent its increasing development while maintaining an equilibrium between environmental quality and economic growth.

To provide an exhaustive approach to decrease EFP in the MENA region, we choose to adopt other factors not yet tested, especially industrialization and the rental of natural resources, to identify a critical path and delimit the degradation of the environment in a global approach. In addition, we adopted an extended period to verify if this effect will differ during the time (long or short). Results will not, only, enrich previous studies for MENA region but also, refine the main axes for the definition of a successful regional vision for environmental quality without stopping or delimiting economic growth.

In summary, this research aims to provide a clear and comprehensive analysis of the effect of selected factors on environmental degradation based on a general and spatial scenario. This is said, a sample of 17 countries on the MENA region was selected for the period 2000–2018. The methodology used is the SDM model because it sustains a global approach, which means determining the effect of these variables at the same time at two different levels: the country and its neighbors while considering the heterogeneous effects on the dependent variable: the ecological footprint (EFP) used to appreciate environmental degradation.

Based on our results, Section 4 reports the empirical results. Then, Section 5 is used to discuss the results. Review of the literature

According to Ref. [40], ecological footprint (EFP) is a metric used to evaluate the level of environmental resources used to prevent and appreciate the sustainability of human activity as well as its quality in a particular country or region. Previous researches used the concept of EFP as a measure of an ecosystem's capacity or ability to regenerate resources to produce ([41]). Others consider that the use of EFP assigns, at the same time, the level of pollution generated by the consumption of existent natural resources and emissions related to the consumption of each energy substances from every industrial agglomeration ([13]). In general, to be sustainable, the footprint must be lower than the correspondent biocapacity ([21]). Based on this brief analysis, studying the determinants of ecological footprint to delimit its negative effect is very important for sustainable development: promoting economic growth while delimiting the uses of natural resources and maintaining industrialization and urbanization.

By analyzing the existent literature review, we tried to delimit all determinants of EFP to dress an exhaustive list and define the relative importance of each determinant. Then, we had to approach this list proportionally to the context of the MENA region based on a spatial approach. We can find quality of democracy ([ [48,69]]), bio-capacity in country ([70]), economic growth ([ [6,28,43]]), trade openness ([ [17,38,45]]), urbanization ([ [7,15]]), industrialization ([ [12,13]]) and energy use ([ [15,48,50]]). Each determinant was defined and empirically tested in many previous types of research in different ways. For us, we will detail the most relative results for each one to delimit our research objective and specify our variables.

Economic growth seems to be the most important determinant and the most cited determinant treated by the majority of researchers. There is a consensus-based on which a high level of economic growth is achieved by exploiting and using environmental resources in low- and middle-income countries ([ [6,28,42]]) and developed countries ([ [22,43]]). Based on this, we can admit that higher economic growth is directly related to the increase in the level of EFP because the development of economic activities generates greater environmental damage by using resources ([ [15,22,28,36]])

Then, trade openness (TO) is also widely cited as an influent factor in environmental degradation, but contrary to economic development, different directions of causality are mentioned between environmental quality and trade openness. Some researchers illustrate that TO reduces ecological consumption thanks to new technologies (38; 44; 17). Some others believe that TO generates many activities which increase environmental degradation ([ [8,44,45]])

Urbanization as another key factor of environmental degradation was admitted but the significance of the causal direction was not confirmed and is still unclear. For example [15], demonstrate that urbanization causes a harmful environmental impact, especially in term of water pollution and CO2 emission. While [7] suggested that urbanization stimulates an increasing demand for green technologies as an efficient tool to delimit natural resource uses.

In regard to financial development, different results have been found. For example, if the financial system is strong, it could delimit environmental degradation by stimulating and encouraging investments in green technologies for efficient and eco-production ([51]). Here, many studies suggest that such financial development will define higher living standards and luxury demands harming the ecological ecosystem ([ [28,52]])

Energy use and its effect on environmental degradation were also supported. In this state, the relative effect depends on the energy type used. It is admitted, in general, that, only the use of renewable energy can decrease environmental degradation and consequently improve environmental quality ([ [[46], [47], [48], [49]]]). Several empirical studies have paid special attention to the impact of the use of renewable energy on the environment ([ [15,50]]). Most of them support the idea that the consumption of renewable energy (REC) is beneficial to the environmental quality by protecting and delimiting nonrenewable energy. In the same line of idea, another large proportion of studies focuses on the relative importance of industrialization as well as its direct impacts on the environment ([17; 18). The corresponding role of these two last cited variables in the environment is still an opportune topic of debate. The majority of research, they are debated separately and focused on specific and limited regions despite the complementarity between them which can be more influential on environmental quality through a compensation process.

Industrialization transforms natural resources into tangible products to be commercialized and satisfying different needs ([12]) which makes it, one of the main determinants of environmental degradation [13]. support this conception and demonstrate that rapid industrial growth hurts different natural components (soil, air, and water). This activity stimulates CO2 emission and introduces changes in different natural features cited below ([12]) and its effect varies but still exists. This result is adopted by several studies that support a proportional increase between industrialization and the emission of harmful waste causing and fostering environmental degradation ([ [6,53,54]]).

Other studies support the existence of an inverted U-shaped interrelationship between environmental degradation and industrialization ([ [[55], [56], [57]]]) as well as between pollution and industrialization ([ [58,59]]). This effect can be greater when a reciprocity effect is confirmed by ([60]). However, despite this negative effect, we cannot stop the industrialization process and this effect was widely debated by Ref. [61] who suppose that this effect remains significant only in the short run. While [62] denied this causal connection. Recently [63], proposed a new concept compared to industrialization which is de-industrialization, and demonstrated that this reverse activity decreases the pollution level.

So, it is admitted that the consumption of natural resources has a great impact on economic growth and development ([ [16,64]]) but, at the same time, natural resource contributes to the satisfaction of a huge human need through the development of industrialization process which is the main contributor to a considerable increase in resource consumption, especially nonrenewable resources ([65]). How to find the adequate equilibrium between environmental quality, industrialization, and resource use regains importance because we can't stop these processes directly related to the economic growth process. A spatial approach seems to be more adequate to delimit energy use by defining a compensation process between countries in terms of natural resources and non-renewable energy. In general, natural resources include oil, gas, minerals, coal, and forest, and they are appreciated by the total natural resource rent percentage of GDP.

A deeper analysis of existent literature confirms that economic growth and industrialization stimulate demand and use of national resource consumption and extraction causing environmental unsustainability ([66]). Excessive natural resource rent is directly associated, for several empirical types of research, with the deterioration of environmental quality ([21]).

Proportionally to the concept of EFP, this corresponding effect, compared to environmental quality, is still unclear and insufficient due to the existence of contradictory results and additional investigations seem to be necessary to promote a sustainable environment [67]. confirm that natural resources rent increase considerably the ecological footprint, but some studies support the opposite ([68]). It is more opportune to focus on the effect of industrialization and resource use on EFP instead of environmental quality for which the majority of results converge. In fact, EFP and environmental quality can be related to the same field of environmental research but are empirically different.

To reinforce our methodological approach, we tried to collect studies that used a spatial econometric approach to test the relationship between independent variables (factors and determinants of EFP) and environmental degradation as a dependent variable [71]. adopted a spatial econometric model to appreciate the interrelationship between growth, biocapacity, and ecological footprint [70]. tested the spatial interrelationship between ecological footprint and its determinant factors in China during the period 2004–2012 [36]. adopted a spatial Durbin model (SDM) to appreciate the determinants of EF for 158 countries between 2007 and 2016. Results support spatial effects for trade openness, GDP, and biocapacity as the main factors stimulating EF [39]. adopted the same spatial approach to study the effect of urbanization, trade openness, energy use, GDP, financial development, and quality of democracy in the MENA region between 2000 and 2006. Energy consumption seems to be the most determinant factor.

To summarize, a lack of empirical studies in the MENA region exists and we can't find research that integrates resource use, industrialization, and EFP. As discussed below, a spatial approach will be adopted to explain the opportunity of the interdependence between our main variables according to our reflexive approach detailed in this part.

Our main hypothesis supposes that there is a spatial interdependence between MENA regions for decreasing EFP. All five main factors are selected: industrialization, natural resource rent, urbanization, economic growth, and renewable energy use (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Table 4 shows all results for different spatial econometrics models: SDM, SAR, and SEM. The LM error value allows us to reject the null hypothesis that the SDM model could be simplified to an SEM model (LM error = 13.67, p-value = 0.03). Similarly, the LR-lag statistics show that we can reject the null hypothesis that the SDM model could be simplified to a SAR model (LM-lag = 16.91, p-value = 0.01). The SDM model has the highest value in both R2 and log-likelihood. The preference for the SDM model over the SAR and SEM model is confirmed.

The results show that the effect of different variables on the EFP remains statistically significant. For the GDP variable, the estimated coefficient is 0.45 which implies that an increase of 1% in GDP (of country (i)) leads to an increase of 0.45% in the EFP (of the country (j)) which converges with the previous findings ([ [15,21,28]]). It is obvious that economic activities decrease the quality of the environment in the country or its neighbor. The spatially lagged growth factor (W × lnGDP) impacts the EF. This signifies that an increase in growth level increases EF in both the country and its neighbor. This result diverges from previous results ([ [27,39,74]]) and confirms that a combined approach matters. There is a good combination to maximize environmental quality and decrease EF.

Industrialization ‘IND' and the urbanization rate show a positive and statistically significant effect on EFP. This result converges with [21]. For the NRR variable, a negative and statistically significant effect on environmental degradation was detected. It means that environmental degradation will decline with increasing natural resource rents.

The variable relating to the consumption of renewable energy shows a negative and statistically significant effect. This result suggests that the use of renewable energy helps reduce environmental degradation ([ [75,76]]) in the country. However, the spatially lagged (W*Ln_REC) are significant and negative, which implies that the increase in consumption of renewable energy in a neighboring country reduces the ecological footprint in the host country.

With reference to the externality effect, the results show positive effects for GDP, IND, and URB. This means that an increase in GDP, urbanization rate, and industrialization in the country (i), leads to environmental degradation in neighboring countries (country (j))

Contrary to the REC variable, the externality remains negative: an increase in renewable energy consumption in one country (i) leads to an improvement in the quality of the environment in the neighboring country.

Conclusions

This research investigated the spatial impact of economic growth, industrialization, natural resource rent, and urbanization on the EF for 17 countries in the MENA region from 2000 to 2018. The SDM model was considered the most suitable alternative analysis to appreciate the impact of these independent variables on one country on EF as dependent on the neighbor country. Global Moran's Index was added to confirm the spatial interdependence.

Results reveal a differentiated effect between variables as well as a direct and indirect effect for each factor. As a local determinant for EFP, a negative direct effect was obtained for both renewable energy consumption and total resource rent and a positive direct effect for urbanization, growth, and industrialization. Only a negative indirect effect was detected for the renewable energy conception, which confirms the opportunity of this variable to decrease the EFP. The SDM model that has a negative impact is still available for the spatial approach. For resources rent the effect seems to be positive. Industrialization, which is our second main variable maintains a positive direct/indirect effect and keeps this same effect for the spatial model.

We can conclude that the main variable to manage for the decrease of EFP is renewable energy for all countries, resource rent is the variable to consider at a spatial level. It can be compensated between countries depending on the degree of industrialization.

This research provides a unique contribution to previous literature dealing with ecological footprint and environmental degradation in the MENA region. It integrates two major variables that can be representative of the economic activity in the region: industrialization and natural resource rent based on a spatial econometric approach. It can help to understand the eventual interdependence between countries and prevent environmental degradation in the region. Most existing research neglects the potential effects of these variables, which may affect indirectly the intensity of economic growth to the detriment of environmental quality or the inverse. Added to this, by widening the study period and sample, this research enriches and completes the previous conception of the path of environmental quality in MENA countries to provide an improved version, which can be considered as a general critical path for the correspondent region. In fact, we selected 17 MENA countries among 22 countries over 18 years, while previous research concentrated on a limited number of countries.

Additionally, the study includes a mix of direct and indirect effects on one country and on its neighbor in the research model. This can be useful to determine a hierarchical order on the term of importance between different variables. This association may help policymaker to understand and define new dimensions of sustainability on term of ecological footprint decreasing. Our conception adopts a reflexive approach to maintain equilibrium between industrialization, natural resources, and ecological footprint. Third, the study uses a spatial approach. This econometric technique offers many advantages and is appropriate for reliable estimates in international reflection.

We must remember that this research was designed to explore, appreciate, and quantify the influence of different factors that impact environmental quality in the MENA region using a spatial approach. Further research, can integrate dynamic spatial panel data models or other econometrics methods to appreciate the stability of this effect or the existence of other determinant variables. Also, it seems to be more opportune to integrate all MENA countries with an expanded years number. Finally, we suppose that examining the impact of other determinant variables on environmental quality is required to enlarge our conception and define adequate recommendations to stop or reduce environmental degradation.
