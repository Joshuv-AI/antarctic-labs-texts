Novel evaluation of bone reaction/growth around full hydroxyapatite-coated stem using digital tomosynthesis imaging: confirmation of very early bone growth around the stem
Authors: Hideki Fujii, Tetsuo Hayama, Ayano Amagami, Keigo Yonemoto, Yohei Matsushita, Mitsuru Saito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFull hydroxyapatite (HA) coated implants are placed into the bone marrow cavity by compressing cancellous bone. The Full HA coated compaction stem, embedded in compressed cancellous bone, is known to form an osteogenic bridge with the endosteum. The aim of this study was to trace and characterize the changes occurring at the interface between compressed cancellous bone and the full HA coated compaction stem during the early postoperative period.MethodsThere were 37 of 48 hips that underwent total hip arthroplasty (THA) with full HA coated compaction stem (POLARSTEM™, Smith and Nephew, Baar, Switzerland) since January 2020. 37 hips were observed for bone reaction around the implant using tomosynthesis images and simple radiographs at 1 week, 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year and 2 years follow up. Digital tomosynthesis (DTS) with reduced metal artifact (T-smart®, SHIMADZU, Kyoto, Japan) was used for this study. Spot welds in cancellous bone were observed on DTS images and analyzed using image processing software (Image J) for temporal comparison.ResultsAt 6 months post-operatively, radiographs revealed multiple spot welds (MSWs) surrounding full HA coated compaction stem in 27.8% of patients. DTS images detected MSWs in 36.4% of cases at 6 weeks and in 100.0% at 3 months postoperatively. All patients had an excellent clinical progress and did not have thigh pain.DiscussionTomosynthesis images demonstrated MSWs suggestive of bone-bridge formation between the full HA coated compaction stem and the bone starting in the early postoperative period (6 weeks). These findings support the good clinical outcomes reported, including low implant loosening, minimal subsidence, reduced stress shielding, and low incidence of thigh pain.ConclusionThe osteointegration process was observed around the full HA coated compaction stem at 6 weeks postoperatively, suggesting that early fixation between the implant and the compressed cancellous bone had been achieved.

Introduction

The full hydroxyapatite (HA) coated compaction stem is reported to have good long-term clinical results [1–3]. This stem is placed by compressing cancellous bone, preserving the bone marrow endosteum responsible for intramembranous ossification in the process of fracture healing. This may be one of the speculated reasons why bone reaction is minimal, thigh pain is rare, and good long-term results have been reported [4]. It is also though that intimate contact between the HA layer and compressed cancellous bone promotes rapid bone–implant integration. However, to achieve stable stem fixation without subsidence or collapse, the optimal degree of cancellous bone compression required for stable fixation remains unclear. Additionally, the timing and progression of cancellous bone remodeling around the stem in the early postoperative period are not well characterized. To address these gaps, we conducted a retrospective study using digital tomosynthesis (DTS) to evaluate early biological fixation. DTS has proven effective in detecting radiolucent lines (RLLs) and diagnosing stem loosening [5], and its diagnostic capability has improved through advances in metal-artifact reduction [6–8]. In patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty (THA) with the POLARSTEM™ full HA–coated compaction stem (Smith & Nephew, Baar, Switzerland), we frequently observed radiographic changes over a 2-year follow-up, despite the absence of significant clinical symptoms. Spot welds are radiographic markers of osseointegration, seen as discrete endosteal bone bridges forming between the implant’s porous-coated surface and the host endosteal (inner bone) bone. Specifically, all cases exhibited spot welds extending from proximal to distal along the stem, which we defined as multiple spot welds (MSWs) (Fig. 1).We hypothesized that MSWs would be detected earlier via DTS than by plain radiographs. Methods

Forty-eight hips underwent THA using full HA coated compaction stem POLARSTEM™ (Smith&Nephew, Baar, Switzerland) beginning in January, 2020. 11 hips had no postoperative follow-up due to the spread of coronavirus infection. 37 hips were included for the study. The 37 hips included 8 bilateral cases for a total of 29 patients. Evaluation focused on the spot welds formed between full hydroxyapatite (HA) coated hip implants and the endosteal surface. Bone reaction around the implant was observed using DTS images and X-ray photographs at 1 week, 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year and 2 years postoperatively. DTS (T-smart®, SHIMADZU, Kyoto, Japan) with reduced metal artifacts was used to clarify the photograph reaction between the compressed cancellous bone and the implants in the early postoperative period. We analyzed MSWs in the cancellous bone as observed on DTS images by measuring grayscale values using ImageJ(Image Processing and Analysis in Java) [9] and compared the changes over time. To assess grayscale intensity within specific anatomical or histological structures, the 'Rectangle Tool' in ImageJ was used to define regions of interest (ROIs) over the target areas. The 'Plot Profile' function (Analyze—> Plot Profile) was then applied to extract pixel intensity values across each ROI, generating a one-dimensional intensity profile. To facilitate pixel value smoothing, the parameter H was set to 9 pixels, and measurements were taken distal to the stem. Grayscale values from 16-bit images (ranging from 0 to 65,536) were exported and subjected to statistical analysis, including calculation of the mean and standard deviation. These metrics were used to quantify the perceptual clarity of the target structure within the image. All measurements were performed under standardized imaging conditions to ensure comparability across samples. This method enables objective evaluation of image quality and supports the optimization of imaging protocols to enhance diagnostic accuracy.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the hospital (Approval number: 29–043(8659)), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to enrollment. All eligible patients were informed of their right to opt-out from the study at any time. Retrospective review of medical records as well as radiographs were taken by investigators at our institution. A total of 37 hips from patients who underwent THA using the POLARSTEM™ between January 2020 and March 2021 at our hospital were eligible for screening.

Surgery and postoperative management

The POLARSTEM™ was implanted by the same hip surgical team which was led by the author (HF) in all consecutive cases. Cases with extremely narrow medullary canal at the subtrochanteric area or cases with extreme anteversion of the femoral neck (more than 40 degrees) were contraindicated. All patients had osteoarthritis: 2 were male and 27 were female, with a mean age 66.7 years and a mean BMI of 23.2 (Table 1). According to the Dorr classification, the femurs were classified as Type A (champagne flute shape) in 6 hips, B (funnel shape) in 31 hips. The Canal Flare Index (CFI) is defined as the ratio of the diameter of the femoral canal at the isthmus in the anteroposterior view to the diameter of the medullary canal 2 cm above the lesser trochanter. There was no significant difference between the size during the preoperative planning and the actual implant size. The approach was Antero-Lateral Supine (ALS) in 37 hips (100%). The R3 acetabular cup system and OXINIUM™ head (Smith&Nephew, Memphis, USA) was used in 37 hips (100%). Radiographic assessment

Radiographs were taken in accordance with well-established protocol as Engh suggested [10] and same radiology team was responsible throughout the patient follow-up. Image assessment was conducted in accordance with the image assessment protocol which had been developed before the study started. The protocol was defined that MSWs were diagnosed as osteogenesis of cancellous bone bridging the stem and the cortical bone. Cortical hypertrophy (CH) was diagnosed when > 2 mm radiographic appearance was observed extra-cortically. Atrophy (AT) was qualitatively diagnosed when cortical absorption induced by stress shielding was observed and RLLs was also qualitatively diagnosed when RLLs was observed between the inner ridge of cortex and inserted stem implant. Additionally, all radiological investigators evaluated the test radiographs to ensure consistency in assessment. For 37 cases, we confirmed that inter-observer reliability, expressed as interclass correlation coefficient (ICC), was 0.933. Radiological assessment attempted twice; initial assessment was conducted by multiple assessors other than senior orthopedic surgeon (HF), and second assessment was done solely by the senior orthopedic surgeon (HF); agreement between first and second assessment was confirmed. In cases of disagreement during image assessment, discussion were held to finalize the assessment results. Radiological evaluation were recorded by Gruen zones 1 through 7 [11].

The investigators evaluated postoperative anteroposterior radiographs and DTS to confirm whether the patient showed a) CH, b) AT, c) MSWs or d) RLLs or not. Image assessments at 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year were conducted to confirm changes over time. CH was diagnosed when > 2 mm radiographic appearance was observed and both AT and RLLs were qualitatively assessed for changes. MSWs visibility was defined as invisible: 0, visible: 1, and well visible: 2. Visibility was defined as the total number of points at the time of observation divided by the number of observations (Fig. 1). No images were available for 4 cases at 6 weeks, 6 cases at 3 months, and 1 case at 6 months because patients refrained from coming to the hospital to avoid coronavirus infection. The appearance ratio of MSWs in each radiological assessment was calculated by dividing the number of hip joints exhibiting MSWs by the total number of available radiological images (Table 3).

Digital tomosynthesis (DTS)

The SONIALVISION G4® multipurpose radiography/fluoroscopy(R/F) system (SHIMADZU, Kyoto, Japan) was used. DTS is an imaging modality that can create multiple coronal tomographic images by using the Filtered Back Projection (FBP) method based on the projection data obtained using pulsed X-ray imaging from multiple directions. The cross-sections of interest region can be observed from multiple reconstructed tomographic images to avoid overlap in the depth direction.

Metal artifact reduction technology

The Side Station i3® system which is the workstation for tomosynthesis reconstruction combined with SONIALVISION G4® system, has metal artifact reduction process (T-smart®, Tomosynthesis Shimadzu Metal Artifact Reduction Technology). T-smart® uses a latest developed tomographic image reconstruction algorithm combining iterative approximation and a metal-isolating algorithm that further reduces the few metal artifacts that remain with tomosynthesis images. Clinical evaluation

The Harris Hip Score (HHS) was used as clinical assessment of the hip joint. Japanese Orthopaedic Association Hip Disease Evaluation Questionnaire (JHEQ) scores were obtained from all patients in accordance with standard practice [12]. JHEQ score is a Patient Reported Outcomes Measure (PROM), with a highest possible score of 84 and a lowest possible score of 0. The JHEQ score can be further assessed by individual domains: pain domain, movement domain, and mental domain are each assessed from 0 to 28 points.

Statistical analysis

Results were reported as number of observations with percentage for categorical data and comparison were conducted using chi-square test. Data values are expressed as means with range of data and comparison was done by t-test. All statistical analyses were performed with Microsoft Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). P values less than 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

DTS can detect spot welds earlier than X-ray, and even after 2 years, X-ray imaging identifies fewer MSWs compared to DTS, which visualizes all MSWs present. MSWs were observed in 19 cases (51.4%) on X-ray and 37 cases (100%) on DTS examination at 1 year. A comparison of cases with and without MSWs at 1 year showed no significant differences in demographic data or stem size (Table 1). Early postoperative subsidence was not confirmed. No patient complained of thigh pain postoperatively, the HHS score improved from 46.9 (SD 15.1) preoperatively to 93.6 (SD 11.5) at 1 year postoperatively and the JHEQ score improved from 22.4 (SD 11.4) preoperatively to 71.8 (SD 14.0) at 1 year postoperatively. On X-ray assessment, MSWs were observed in 27.0% of patients at 6 months postoperatively. MSWs were observed in 36.4% at 6 weeks postoperatively and 100% at 3 months by DTS (Table 3). No CH, RLLs or subsidence was observed in any of the patients from 6 weeks to 2 years postoperatively. MSWs were observed by X-ray and DTS images over time and the visibility of MSWs was recorded. The confirmed visibility of MSWs gradually increased from 6 months to 1 year with X-ray. The visibility of MSWs in DTS images increased from 6 weeks to 1 year and plateaued from 1 to 2 years. It was more clearly visualized compared to X-ray images (Fig. 1). Investigating the location and emergence of MSWs were by Gruen Zone it was observed from 6 months in Zones 2, 3, 5, and 6 on X-ray images and from 6 weeks on DTS images. From 3 months, MSWs was observed in more than 87% cases on DTS images (Fig. 2). DTS images of 74-year-old woman's right hip at 1 week, 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year analyzed by Image J showed cases of compressed cancellous bone with signal intensity falling once at 6 weeks postoperatively and then gradually increasing until 1 year (Fig. 3). Another 74-year-old woman's left hip, radiographic X-ray observation showed MSWs in the compressed cancellous bone around the implant from 3 months (Fig. 4). Discussion

HA is known to have an excellent biocompatibility and osteoconductivity [13, 14]. Osteointegration has also been reported to occur around HA coated stems embedded in a stable bone environment [15, 16]. Therefore, in the preliminary stage, it is crucial to compress the cancellous bone to create a stable cancellous bone sleeve, thereby achieving initial fixation of the stem. The contact between the full HA coated compaction stem and the cancellous bone bed promotes osteointegration and facilitates early acquisition of stable stem fixation. Other cement-less stems, such as tapered wedge or curved short stems, achieve fixation primarily through direct contact with the cortical bone. Many reports suggest that mismatches between the stem and medullary canal geometry can occur and may lead to unwanted bone reaction or stress shielding [17–19]. RLLs are radiographic signs of cortical hypertrophy or loosening due to localized stress concentration. Such findings are rare in full HA coated compaction stem, which is why these implants have been referred to as a “silent hip” [15, 16, 20].

DTS images in the present study revealed bone bridge formation between the full HA coated compaction stem and the cortical bone from 6 weeks postoperatively. The fact that MSWs were visible in the compressed cancellous bone as early as 6 weeks which is earlier than on radiographs, may help explain the excellent clinical outcomes reported for this implant. Observation of the interface between the stem surface and cancellous bone is limited when using radiographic images in a temporal series because X-rays project a three dimensional structure onto a flat surface. DTS proved useful for visualizing bone conduction around HA coated stems [21–24].

In Gruen zone 3/5, a new bone bridge formation was observed surrounding the stem and became more pronounced over time. A comparison of the MSWs over time, using the image analysis software (Image J) showed that their presence in the compressed cancellous bone decreased once at 6 weeks and then increased progressively until 1 year (Fig. 3). This suggests that the MSWs structure was gradually formed over the course of 1 year, reflecting the process of trabecular bone remodeling [25]. Standardization of both imaging conditions and image extraction procedures was essential to ensure consistency and reproducibility in the ImageJ-based analysis of DTS images. All DTS acquisitions were conducted in the radiology department using uniform imaging parameters. This approach enabled reliable grayscale quantification and facilitated the calculation of visibility scores across anatomically comparable regions. Comparisons over time were difficult in all cases due to variability in values originating from differences in measurement sites. However, using ImageJ, we quantified and observed in pixel values within the cancellous bone layer over time (Fig. 3). We anticipate that further study and ingenuity will be required when using this measurement in the future.

sIn Gruen zone 2/6 and 7, the medial and lateral interfaces between the proximal stem and cortical bone are in close proximity, complicating the observation of MSWs. However, careful examination of the images revealed the presence of a thin cancellous bone layer, suggesting that the similar changes occurred in the proximal portion of the stem as observed in the distal portion. It is considered that the stem design concept of proximal medial and lateral width enhances initial fixation more effectively than the earlier full HA coated compaction stem [26].

In Gruen zone 4, DTS images revealed the formation of a new bone bridge extending obliquely from the distal stem to the bone cortex, observed in all cases from approximately 3 months postoperatively. This finding does not represent pedestal formation which suggests stem loosening, but rather a new bone beam formed in response to stress transmitted to the stem tip, indicative of the osteoconductive properties of HA. Full HA coated compaction stems, often referred to as “silent stems”, typically exhibit minimal bone reaction such as RLLs or CH, in contrast to other cementless stems. DTS imaging revealed MSWs surrounding the stem within the trabecular bone layer as early as 6 weeks and continuing 3 months postoperatively. These bone reactions suggest that, provided the stem is stably implanted, the cancellous bone should be compressed enough to provide stem fixation. This likely reflects early postoperative remodeling driven by mechanical stress transmitted from the stem to cancellous bone bed due to loading and muscle pull transmitted by walking and standing up in the early postoperative period [27]. The presence of a cancellous bone bed may explain the low cortical bone response.

The cases analyzed in this study included a 2 years follow-up of 37 hips. While no problematic clinical outcomes or adverse bone reactions were observed, there were reported cases of fixation failure [28] and cases of loosening over time with full HA coated compaction stems [29–31]. There is a limitation in this study since absence of a control group and preventing demonstration of the comparative effectiveness of DTS. Continued follow-up, including long-term follow-up, is necessary to assess the clinical success of full HA coated compaction stem.

Conclusion

The osteointegration process was observed around the full HA coated compaction stem by 6 weeks postoperatively, suggesting that early fixation between the implant and compressed cancellous bone was achieved.
