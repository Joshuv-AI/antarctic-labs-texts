Asymptomatic pulmonary Mycobacterium heckeshornense infection detected by routine health screening
Authors: Miho Kanai, Yutaro Nakamura, Hisano Ohba, Kaoru Fujita, Eriko Iwaizumi, Tatsuru Eifuku, Yasuhiro Ito, Tomoko Oshima, Seiichi Miwa, Masahiro Shirai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mycobacterium heckeshornense is a rare nontuberculous mycobacterium that has been reported sporadically since its first description by Roth et al. in 2000. From the earliest reports, infections have been documented not only in immunocompromised patients or those with underlying lung disease, but also in immunocompetent individuals, with a wide range of clinical manifestations. However, owing to the limited number of cases and their heterogeneity, the full clinical spectrum of this organism has not been clearly defined. We report a case of asymptomatic pulmonary M. heckeshornense infection in a healthy 48-year-old man, incidentally detected by an abnormal chest radiograph during routine health screening. Chest computed tomography revealed a cavitary lesion in the left lower lobe and centrilobular nodules in the right middle lobe. Bronchoscopy demonstrated acid-fast bacilli, and M. heckeshornense was identified from sputum and bronchial lavage cultures using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry. Treatment with clarithromycin, rifampicin, and ethambutol resulted in marked radiographic improvement. By integrating this case with previously reported pulmonary infections in immunocompetent hosts, we demonstrate that M. heckeshornense infection encompasses a broad clinical spectrum ranging from highly destructive disease requiring surgical intervention to long-standing, subclinical infection. The present case represents the most indolent phenotype reported to date and underscores the importance of considering this rare pathogen even in asymptomatic, immunocompetent individuals.

Introduction

Pulmonary infection caused by Mycobacterium heckeshornense is a rare form of nontuberculous mycobacterial disease that has been reported intermittently since its initial description by Roth et al. in 2000 [1]. Despite more than two decades having passed since that first report, the total number of documented cases remains small.

The initial case reported by Roth et al. involved an immunocompetent host with severe cavitary pulmonary disease, and subsequent reports have continued to describe infections in patients without underlying lung disease [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], alongside cases occurring in immunocompromised hosts or those with structural lung abnormalities [8], [9]. These observations indicate that M. heckeshornense should not be regarded solely as an opportunistic pathogen, but rather as an organism capable of causing clinically relevant infection across a broad host spectrum.

Clinical presentations of pulmonary M. heckeshornense infection have varied widely, ranging from aggressive, destructive disease with cavitation and progression despite antimycobacterial therapy [8], [9] to more indolent or treatment-responsive courses [4], [5]. Extrapulmonary infections, including tenosynovitis, lymphadenitis [10], osteomyelitis [11], peritonitis [12], and disseminated disease [13], have also been reported, predominantly in immunocompromised individuals. However, because of the small number of cases and the heterogeneity of reported presentations, the natural history and determinants of disease severity remain poorly understood.

Accurate identification of M. heckeshornense remains challenging. The organism is phenotypically and phylogenetically similar to Mycobacterium xenopi and has frequently been misidentified by conventional methods such as DNA–DNA hybridization. Definitive diagnosis often requires culture-based identification with confirmation using advanced techniques, including matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry [14] or gene sequencing [12], [15]. In addition, no standardized therapeutic regimen has been established, and treatment strategies are typically extrapolated from those used for other nontuberculous mycobacterial infections.

Here, we report a case of asymptomatic pulmonary M. heckeshornense infection in an immunocompetent adult detected during routine health screening. By integrating this case with previously reported pulmonary infections in immunocompetent hosts over the past two decades, we aim to refine the clinical spectrum of this rare pathogen and to provide insight into its variable disease behavior.

Case report

A 48-year-old Japanese man with no significant medical history was referred to our hospital following an abnormal chest radiograph obtained during a routine health check-up. The image revealed a cavitary lesion in the left lower lobe and centrilobular nodules in the right middle lobe (Fig. 1a, b). The patient denied any symptoms, such as fever, cough, or sputum production. He was a former smoker (20 cigarettes/day between ages 15–29; Brinkman Index: 280), had no history of alcohol use, and reported no significant exposure to soil, pets, or untreated water sources. Nine years earlier, the patient was asymptomatic but was noted to have an abnormal shadow on a routine chest radiograph obtained during a health checkup. Chest CT performed at another institution revealed a pulmonary cyst with associated fluid accumulation (Fig. 2). The patient was empirically treated with a 1-week course of levofloxacin for a presumed respiratory infection. As the fluid component was judged to have improved following treatment, no further follow-up was pursued. The patient subsequently remained asymptomatic and did not seek additional medical care. He worked as an automobile mechanic until the age of 39, then shifted to office work, and currently works in auto sales.Fig. 2Figure of clinical course. Upon admission, the patient was afebrile (37.1°C), normotensive (128/96 mmHg), and hemodynamically stable. His oxygen saturation was 98% in room air. Physical examination was unremarkable, except for poor oral hygiene with multiple dental caries. Laboratory investigations revealed normal hematologic and biochemical parameters, with a mildly elevated eosinophil count (7.1%) and a C-reactive protein level of 0.14 mg/dL. β-D-glucan, Aspergillus antigens and antibodies, interferon-γ release assays for tuberculosis, anti-MAC antibody, tumor markers, and viral serologies (HBV, HCV, HIV) were all negative. Initial sputum smears were negative for acid-fast bacilli (AFB), and bronchoscopy was performed for further evaluation. Transbronchial biopsies were obtained from the left B6, B8, and B9 segments, brushings from B6 and B8, and bronchial lavage from B8. AFB staining was positive in both the brushing and lavage samples. However, PCR tests for Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex and Mycobacterium avium complex were negative. Following discharge, the patient’s prior sputum culture yielded growth of Mycobacterium heckeshornense after 62 days using a liquid culture medium. The organism was also isolated from bronchial lavage fluid, which became culture-positive after 49 days, and was identified by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry. Based on repeated culture positivity and species identification, a diagnosis of pulmonary non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection caused by M. heckeshornense was made.

Treatment with oral clarithromycin (800 mg/day), rifampicin (450 mg/day), and ethambutol (750 mg/day) was initiated in June of the same year. A follow-up chest CT performed in July of the following year demonstrated significant improvement in radiographic abnormalities (Fig. 2). The patient remains on treatment with a good clinical response and close follow-up.

Discussion

Since the initial report by Roth et al. in 2000 [1], pulmonary infection caused by Mycobacterium heckeshornense has been reported sporadically over more than two decades. Despite this extended time frame, the number of published cases remains limited, and the reported clinical presentations are remarkably diverse. These characteristics have contributed to ongoing uncertainty regarding the typical disease course, pathogenic potential, and optimal management of this organism.

Notably, immunocompetent hosts have been included among reported cases from the outset. Roth et al. described an immunocompetent young woman with severe cavitary pulmonary disease that was refractory to antimycobacterial therapy and ultimately required bilateral lobectomy [1], highlighting the organism’s strong tissue-destructive potential. Subsequent reports have reinforced this observation. Jauréguy et al. described a severe, recurrent, and chronic pulmonary infection in an immunocompetent elderly woman, in whom initial misidentification as Mycobacterium xenopi and dismissal as a contaminant led to delayed initiation of appropriate therapy and prolonged disease activity [3]. These early and intermediate reports demonstrate that M. heckeshornense can cause aggressive disease even in the absence of immunodeficiency.

Other cases in immunocompetent hosts have similarly shown progressive or refractory pulmonary disease. Kurosaki et al. reported a healthy woman whose disease progressed despite prolonged antimycobacterial chemotherapy and required surgical resection [6]. Van Hest et al. described symptomatic cavitary disease confirmed by molecular identification techniques [2]. Together, these cases indicate that immunocompetence does not preclude severe or destructive pulmonary infection caused by M. heckeshornense.

Conversely, less aggressive phenotypes have also been reported. Morimoto et al. described a middle-aged man whose pulmonary disease improved after short-term clarithromycin therapy following reclassification from M. xenopi to M. heckeshornense[5]. Hisamoto et al. similarly reported a case that responded favorably to antimycobacterial treatment despite initial diagnostic challenges [4]. These reports suggest that pulmonary M. heckeshornense infection does not uniformly follow a progressive or refractory course.

The present case further expands this clinical spectrum by demonstrating a prolonged, subclinical course in an immunocompetent host. Unlike previously reported cases, our patient was completely asymptomatic and was diagnosed incidentally during routine health screening. Retrospective imaging suggested that pulmonary abnormalities had likely been present for approximately nine years prior to diagnosis, representing the longest documented subclinical course among reported immunocompetent cases. This finding indicates that M. heckeshornense infection may persist in a low-activity state for extended periods before clinical recognition.

Interestingly, previously reported asymptomatic pulmonary cases of M. heckeshornense infection have all been described in Japanese patients. In Japan, preventive health screening is widely accepted, and routine medical checkups are commonly performed even in asymptomatic individuals. In addition, Japan has one of the highest numbers of CT scanners per capita worldwide [16], and chest CT is frequently performed during follow-up for other diseases. These factors may facilitate the early detection of radiographic abnormalities.

Indeed, previous studies have suggested that pulmonary MAC disease in Japan is often diagnosed at an earlier stage with relatively mild symptoms compared with cases reported in other countries [17]. Furthermore, the increasing availability of MALDI-TOF MS for species identification may allow more precise identification of NTM even before overt clinical symptoms develop. However, the number of reported M. heckeshornense cases remains limited, and further accumulation of cases will be necessary to clarify potential host or epidemiological factors.

From a therapeutic perspective, management strategies reported to date have varied widely. Several patients required surgical resection because of inadequate response to antimycobacterial therapy [1], [2], [6], supporting the concept that M. heckeshornense can exhibit strong pathogenicity and tissue-destructive behavior. In contrast, other cases, including the present one, responded favorably to macrolide-containing multidrug regimens without surgical intervention [4], [5]. Our case suggests that early recognition during a low-activity or subclinical phase may permit effective conservative management and avoidance of surgery.

Taken together, these findings indicate that pulmonary M. heckeshornense infection in immunocompetent individuals represents a heterogeneous disease entity, ranging from long-standing, subclinical infection to aggressive, destructive disease requiring surgical intervention. The determinants of this variability remain unclear but may involve host factors, local pulmonary conditions, bacterial virulence, and delays in accurate species identification. These findings suggest the importance of rapid and accurate identification of non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) isolated from pulmonary lesions, even in asymptomatic and immunocompetent patients. If the organism is identified as M. heckeshornense, the clinical course may be unpredictable, and careful follow-up and management may be warranted. Given its phenotypic similarity to M. xenopi and the potential consequences of misidentification, accurate species-level diagnosis using advanced microbiological techniques is essential. With increased awareness and improved diagnostic capabilities, additional indolent or subclinical cases may be recognized, further refining the clinical spectrum of this rare pathogen.

In conclusion, pulmonary M. heckeshornense infection has been reported sporadically since 2000 and has consistently included immunocompetent hosts with highly variable disease courses. The present case represents the most indolent phenotype reported to date and demonstrates that long-term subclinical infection is possible. Continued accumulation and careful characterization of cases are necessary to clarify the natural history, prognostic factors, and optimal management strategies for this uncommon but clinically significant organism.
