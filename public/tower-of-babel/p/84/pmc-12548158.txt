Secular trends in dietary patterns among Korean adults: using data from the 2007–2022 Korea National health and nutrition examination survey
Authors: Eunyoung Tak, Juhae Kim, Heejin Lee, Minji Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDietary patterns are an important research focus for evaluating associations with disease risk. However, studies investigating secular trends in dietary patterns among Korean adults remain limited. Therefore, we aimed to identify the major dietary patterns in the Korean population and to assess how these patterns have changed over the past 16 years.MethodsThis study included 33,408 men and 46,628 women aged 19 years or older who participated in the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) from 2007 to 2022. Dietary intake was assessed using a single 24-hour dietary recall. Dietary patterns were derived using cluster analysis based on the percentage contribution of 28 food groups to total energy intake. Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine secular trends in dietary patterns by sex and age group. Survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for cardiometabolic risk factors according to dietary patterns.ResultsThree dietary patterns were identified through cluster analysis: (1) Traditional, (2) Red Meat & Alcohol, and (3) Flour-based Foods & Sweets. The overall trend in dietary patterns among Korean adults has undergone a gradual transition from traditional to Westernized dietary patterns. Since 2013, the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern has become the most prevalent (p < .0001), and this pattern was more commonly observed aged 19–29 years and among women. After multivariate adjustment, men adhering to the Red Meat & Alcohol pattern had the highest odds of cardiometabolic risk factors, including hyperglycemia (OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.24–1.48), high blood pressure (1.33, 1.23–1.45), hypertriglyceridemia (1.16, 1.07–1.26), hypercholesterolemia (1.17, 1.07–1.27), abdominal obesity (1.18, 1.05–1.32), and overall obesity (1.28, 1.18–1.38). In women, adherence to the Flour-based Foods & Sweets Pattern was associated with higher odds of hypercholesterolemia (1.14, 1.08–1.20) and high LDL-cholesterol (1.08, 1.03–1.15).ConclusionsA transition from traditional to Westernized dietary patterns occurred among Korean adults from 2007 to 2022, which may have differential impacts on cardiometabolic health according to sex and age, underscoring the need for tailored nutritional interventions and sex-specific public health strategies.

Introduction

Dietary patterns have been extensively analyzed in nutritional epidemiological research [1, 2]. Dietary pattern captures the effects of overall dietary habits by accounting for the complex interactions among food and nutrients. It provides a more holistic understanding of diet-disease relationships and contributes to building the scientific evidence base for dietary guidelines development [3]. There are three approaches of dietary pattern analysis: the hypothesis-based approach, the exploratory analysis, and the hybrid approach which combines both approaches [2]. Among them, the exploratory approach which solely relies on dietary intake data, is considered to be particularly suitable for analyzing the cluster results that represent actual dietary patterns [4].

According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, one in five deaths is preventable through dietary improvement [5]. To explain the link between the rapid rise in non-communicable diseases over the past decade and their associations with changes in dietary trends, it is essential to focus on dietary patterns rather than on individual foods. Asian countries, including Korea, have experienced substantial changes in food consumption and dietary habits in recent decades [6, 7]. Thus, it is necessary to identify recent changes in dietary consumption trends to develop strategies that can improve dietary patterns and alleviate disease burden. Despite its importance, studies examining secular trends in actual dietary patterns among adults at the population level in Korea are insufficient.

Many studies have investigated dietary patterns and their association with disease risk factors, but they have primarily focused on food and nutrient intakes [8, 9], or standardized dietary quality scores such as Korean Healthy Eating Index (KHEI) [10, 11]. There have also been some studies on exploratory dietary pattern analysis, but they have typically used data from individual years, limiting the ability to capture long-term trends [12].

In this study, we used data from a large, nationally representative Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) from 2007 to 2022 to examine secular trends in dietary patterns among Korean adults. Dietary patterns were identified through exploratory, data-driven analysis to reflect actual eating behaviors. Specifically, we aimed to identify the main dietary patterns among Korean adults over the past 16 years, assess how these patterns have changed over time, examine differences in dietary patterns by age and sex, and investigate their association with cardiometabolic health outcomes.

Methods

Study participants

This study utilized data from KNHANES, a nationally representative cross-sectional survey of the non-institutionalized population residing in the Republic of Korea [13]. The survey is conducted by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) under the National Health Promotion Act, with informed consent obtained from all participants. KNHANES was implemented in 1998, 2001, and 2005, and has been conducted annually since 2007. In this study, data from 2007 to 2022 were used to examine continuous and long-term trends in annually shifting dietary patterns.

The initial sample included 82,516 adults aged 19 years or older who completed all three components of the survey: health interview/health behavior, health examination, and nutrition survey. Participants with implausible total daily energy intake (< 500 kcal or > 5,000 kcal; n = 1,366) were excluded. Additionally, pregnant or lactating individuals at the time of the survey were excluded (n = 1,114). This yielded a final analytic sample of 80,036 participants (men: n = 33,408; women: n = 46,628).

Dietary assessment

This study utilized data from both the dietary intake survey and the dietary behavior survey, which are part of the nutrition survey in KNHANES. The dietary intake survey, conducted using a 24-hour dietary recall, was administered by trained interviewers who collected detailed information on eating location, types of foods consumed, and portion sizes for all meals reported by participants within a single day [13].

From 2007 to 2021, dietary data were collected based on the day preceding the survey interview day, which was primarily conducted in participants’ residences [13]. Since 2022, the nutrition survey has been conducted in a mobile examination vehicle alongside health examinations and health interviews. To account for the fasting period required before blood sampling, the reference day for the 24-hour dietary recall was shifted from one day prior to two days prior to the survey. There was a minimal variation in nutrient and energy intake level before and after the shift in the nutrition survey day, with no discernible trend, as concluded by expert reviewers [13]. Based on this consensus, data from 2022 were also included in trend analyses, given the confirmed comparability with previous years.

Energy and nutrient intakes were calculated as total daily consumption from all foods and beverages based on 24-hour dietary recall. Nutrient intake was assessed across 14 types of nutrients, and the distribution of energy contribution from macronutrients was analyzed. Until 2015, Vitamin A intake was calculated using Retinol Equivalents (RE: retinol + 1/6 × β-carotene). However, with the revision of the 2015 Korean Dietary Reference Intakes (KDRI), the assessment unit for vitamin A was changed to Retinol Activity Equivalents (RAE: retinol + 1/12 × β-carotene). Accordingly, vitamin A intake from 2007 to 2015 was recalculated and reported based on the RAE standard.

Additionally, dietary behavior data included breakfast and dinner skipping rates and whether meals were consumed with family members. The frequency of eating out was categorized as less than once per week, 1–6 times per week, or at least once per day. Dietary supplement use was defined as follows: from 2007 to 2009, it included individuals who had previously consumed vitamins, minerals, or other functional health supplements. Since 2010, it has been defined as individuals who had taken dietary supplements for at least two consecutive weeks within the past year.

Analysis of dietary patterns

To analyze dietary patterns, food items were classified into 28 food groups based on similar characteristics using the KNHANES food codes. The classification was primarily informed by nutrient composition, while also incorporating aspects of culinary usage and processing level. Given the significant contribution of grains to total daily energy intake, a more detailed classification of staple food consumption was necessary. Accordingly, grains were subdivided into six categories: whole grains, refined white rice, refined grains, wheat flour and bread, processed white rice, and flour-based foods. Refined white rice referred to plain steamed rice, while processed white rice included mixed or prepared rice dishes such as porridge, gimbap, and fried rice, which differ in preparation methods and nutrient profiles. This distinction was intended to capture the diversity of rice consumption in the Korean diet. For vegetables, considering that salted vegetables, including kimchi, contribute more to sodium intake than fresh vegetables, they were classified into two groups: kimchi and salted vegetables, and non-salted vegetables. Mushrooms were incorporated into the non-salted vegetable group. To capture diversity in dietary intake, meat was categorized into three groups: red meat, poultry, and processed meat. Beverages were classified into fruit juices, tea and coffee, sugar-sweetened beverages, and alcoholic beverages, based on their potential to contribute empty calories. The final classification of food groups consisted of whole grains, refined white rice, refined grains, wheat flour and bread, processed white rice, flour-based foods, potatoes, sweets, legumes and tofu/soymilk, nuts, non-salted vegetables, kimchi and salted vegetables, seasonings, fruits, red meat, poultry, processed meat, eggs, fish and seafood, seaweed, milk and dairy products, plant oils, animal oils, fruit juices, tea and coffee, sugar-sweetened beverages, alcoholic beverages, and other foods. To assess the contribution of each food group to total energy intake, the percentage contribution of each of the 28 groups was calculated. Cluster analysis was then performed to identify dietary patterns, minimizing the influence of food items with large weight contributions and adjusting for differences in weight between solid and liquid foods. The optimal number of clusters was determined using Ward’s method, followed by K-means clustering to derive final dietary patterns while ensuring interpretability and an appropriate distribution of individuals across clusters. Each dietary pattern was assigned a descriptive name reflecting the dominant food groups within the cluster to enhance clarity in interpretation. To ensure comparability across survey years, the clustering was performed once on the pooled data from 2007 to 2022, and the resulting dietary patterns were applied consistently to each survey year to examine secular trends.

Demographic and lifestyle information

Data on age, residential area, household income, marital status and vigorous physical activity were collected through interviews conducted by trained surveyors. Smoking and alcohol consumption status were assessed using self-reported health questionnaires. Educational attainment was collected through face-to-face interviews until 2021; however, to expand remote survey methods, self-administered questionnaires were introduced in 2022 for participants under the age of 60.

Residential areas were classified as either city or rural. Educational attainment was categorized as middle school or below, high school, and college or above. Household income was analyzed based on the equivalized monthly household income, which was calculated by dividing monthly household income by the number of household members. Income levels were then classified into four quartiles: low, lower-middle, upper-middle, and high. Marital status was categorized as either married or unmarried. Smoking status was categorized as never smoker, former smoker, and current smoker. Monthly alcohol consumption was assessed for individuals aged 12 years or older, with participants classified as “Yes” if they had consumed alcohol at least once per month in the past and “No” otherwise. Vigorous physical activity was defined as engaging in at least 10 min of intense physical activity per week and categorized as “Yes” or “No”. Menopausal status was assessed for women. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using anthropometric measurements taken by trained surveyors and classified according to the World Health Organization (WHO) Asia-Pacific obesity criteria as follows: Underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), Normal (18.5–22.9 kg/m²), Overweight (23–24.9 kg/m²), and Obese (≥ 25 kg/m²) [14]. Due to the limited number of underweight participants, BMI was regrouped into three categories for analysis: Underweight/Normal, Overweight, and Obese.

Cardiometabolic risk factors

Blood pressure was measured three times after the participants had been seated and rested for at least five minutes, and the average of the second and third measurements was used for analysis. During the period from 2008 to 2010, discrepancies in arm height (heart level) during blood pressure measurement were identified. To address this issue, a correction factor was applied using the mean arm height recorded for men and women in 2011, and the corrected blood pressure data were used [13]. Blood samples were collected from the median cubital vein or cephalic vein after a minimum fasting period of eight hours [13].

Hyperglycemia was defined as a fasting plasma glucose level of ≥ 110 mg/dL according to the diagnostic criteria of the WHO [15], and high blood pressure was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg or a diastolic blood pressure ≥ 85 mmHg according to the 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines [16]. Four lipid parameters of dyslipidemia were classified according to the 2018 Korean Guidelines for the Management of Dyslipidemia: (1) total cholesterol ≥ 200 mg/dL, (2) LDL cholesterol ≥ 130 mg/dL, (3) triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL, and (4) HDL cholesterol < 40 mg/dL for men or < 50 mg/dL for women [17]. The TG/HDL cholesterol ratio, an additional indicator of dyslipidemia, was assessed based on a cutoff value proposed in a previous study [18]. Abdominal obesity was defined as a waist circumference of ≥ 90 cm for men and ≥ 80 cm for women, and overall obesity was defined as a BMI of ≥ 25 kg/m² according to the WHO Asia-Pacific criteria [14].

Statistical analyses

KNHANES employs a complex, stratified, and clustered probability sampling design [12]. To obtain unbiased estimates and achieve accurate variance estimation, all analyses in this study incorporated survey weights, stratification variables, and clustering.

Dietary patterns were identified using hierarchical clustering with Ward’s method and non-hierarchical clustering with the K-means method. Characteristics of participants were analyzed according to dietary patterns using weighted means for continuous variables and weighted frequency for categorical variables. Continuous variables were presented as weighted means with standard errors (SEs), while categorical variables were expressed as weighted proportions with SEs. A Rao-Scott chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables across dietary patterns, and a linear regression model was used to assess differences in continuous variables. To examine the association between dietary pattern and nutrient intake, we estimated the least-squares (LS) means and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using a weighted linear regression model. The association between dietary patterns and various health outcomes was examined using a survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression model, with results reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs. For each of the three dietary patterns, model 1 was adjusted for age (years, continuous) and energy intake (kcal/day, continuous). Model 2 was additionally adjusted for region of residence (city, rural), education level (middle school or below, high school, college or above), household income status (low, lower-middle, upper-middle, high), marital status (married, unmarried), smoking status (never, former, current), monthly alcohol use (no, yes), and vigorous physical activity (no, yes). In women, model 2 was further adjusted for menopause status (no, yes). Model 3 included additional adjustment for BMI category (underweight/normal, overweight, obesity). Covariates were chosen considering their potential to confound the associations between dietary patterns and outcomes of interest, including nutrient intake and cardiometabolic risk factors. To evaluate the secular trends in the distribution of dietary patterns by sex and age group, stratified analyses were conducted using the Rao–Scott chi-square test, incorporating weighted frequencies. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Significance was assessed using two-sided tests with a threshold of P <.05.

Results

Characteristics of dietary patterns

A cluster analysis was conducted based on the percentage contribution of 28 food groups to total energy intake, resulting in the identification of three distinct clusters (Table 2). The first cluster accounted for 48.6% of the total population (n = 38,889) and was characterized by relatively a higher intake of refined white rice, legumes & tofu, soymilk, kimchi & salted vegetables, and seaweed. This cluster was labeled as the ‘Traditional’ group. The second cluster comprised 12.9% of the population (n = 10,289) and exhibited a high consumption of red meat and alcoholic beverages and was therefore named the ‘Red Meat & Alcohol’ group. Sociodemographic characteristics by dietary pattern

Tables 3 and 4 present the sociodemographic distribution of dietary patterns among men and women, respectively. Among men, participants in the Traditional pattern tended to be older (50.1 years), whereas those in the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern were younger (42.3 years). The Traditional and Red Meat & Alcohol patterns were more prevalent in rural areas, whereas the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern was more common in urban settings (p <.0001). Participants in the Flour-based Foods & Sweets group tended to be more educated with 48.1% having a college degree or above. Regarding income, participants in the Traditional pattern had the highest proportion of low-income (19.3%), whereas the Red Meat & Alcohol (37.5%) and Flour-based Foods & Sweets (35.8%) patterns were more likely to have a higher income status. Regarding marital status, the proportion of married individuals was higher across all dietary patterns. Alcohol consumption was most frequent in the Red Meat & Alcohol group, where 92.5% reported drinking at least once per month in the past year. The distribution of vigorous physical activity among groups was statistically significant (p <.0001), although all proportions of engaging in vigorous physical activity remained within the 30% range. Notably, participants in the Traditional pattern tended not to engage in at least 10 min of vigorous activity in the past week (71.2%), the highest proportion among all patterns. Regarding BMI, the Traditional pattern had the highest proportion of individuals classified as underweight or normal weight (37.8%). In contrast, obesity was most prevalent in the Red Meat & Alcohol (43.3%) and Flour-based Foods & Sweets (41.8%) groups. The Traditional pattern had the highest proportion of individuals having breakfast (69.5%) and dinner (74.9%) with their families. Secular trends in dietary patterns by sex

Secular trends in the distribution of three dietary patterns—Traditional, Red Meat & Alcohol, and Flour-based Foods & Sweets—from 2007 to 2022, examined overall and stratified by sex, are presented in Fig. 1. Overall, the Traditional pattern accounted for the largest proportion until 2013. Both men and women showed a declining trend in adherence to the Traditional pattern, whereas the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern showed an increasing trend. However, the magnitude of these changes differed by sex over time (p <.0001). Among men, the proportion of the Red Meat & Alcohol pattern peaked at 24.1% in 2016, followed by a gradual decline to 19.9% in 2022. In contrast, among women, the Red Meat & Alcohol pattern consistently accounted for about half the proportion observed in men. Of note, the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern showed higher proportions throughout the study period compared to men.

Trends in dietary patterns by age group

Figures 2, 3 and 4 illustrate secular trends in the distribution of three dietary patterns across four age groups (19–29, 30–49, 50–64, and 65 years and older) over the past 16 years in the total population, men, and women, respectively. Based on stratified Rao–Scott chi-square tests incorporating weighted frequencies, the analysis demonstrated that year-to-year changes in the three dietary patterns within each age group were statistically significant across all three populations (p <.0001). The Traditional pattern generally declined over time, whereas the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern showed an overall increasing tendency, particularly among participants aged 19–29 years. When examining trends in dietary pattern consumption by age group in the total population, young adults aged 19–29 consistently had the highest proportion of the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern, except in 2008. Association between dietary patterns and cardiometabolic risk factors

Tables 7 and 8 present the associations between dietary patterns and cardiometabolic risk factors for men and women, respectively. In men, multivariable-adjusted analyses showed significant differences in the prevalence of cardiometabolic risk factors across the three dietary patterns (p <.05 for all). Among the three dietary patterns, the Red meat & Alcohol pattern demonstrated the highest prevalence of hyperglycemia, high blood pressure, hypertriglyceridemia, hypercholesterolemia, abdominal obesity, and overall obesity. The Red Meat & Alcohol pattern was associated with higher odds ratios (95% CIs) for several risk factors, including hyperglycemia 1.35 (1.24–1.48), high blood pressure 1.33 (1.23–1.45), hypertriglyceridemia 1.16 (1.07–1.26), hypercholesterolemia 1.17 (1.07–1.27), abdominal obesity 1.18 (1.05–1.32), and overall obesity 1.28 (1.18–1.38), when compared to the Traditional pattern. In women, multivariable-adjusted analyses showed statistically significant differences in the prevalence of most cardiometabolic risk factors across the three dietary patterns, indicating distinct associations by dietary pattern (p <.05 for most risk factors). No significant differences were observed for abdominal obesity (p =.2371). The Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern was associated with higher odds of hypercholesterolemia 1.14 (1.08–1.20) and high LDL cholesterol 1.08 (1.03–1.15). Discussion

This study identified three dietary patterns in a nationally representative sample of Korean adults: Traditional, Red Meat & Alcohol, and Flour-based Foods & Sweets. We found that the proportion of adults adhering to the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern increased steadily between 2007 and 2022. Across all study periods, the Red Meat & Alcohol pattern was consistently more prevalent among men than women, though it was not the predominant pattern among men. In contrast, the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern was more prevalent among women than men. Furthermore, compared with those adhering to the Traditional pattern, men adhering to the Red Meat & Alcohol pattern had higher prevalence of elevated fasting blood glucose, blood pressure, triglycerides, total cholesterol, waist circumference, and BMI, and lower prevalence of low HDL-cholesterol. Among women, those adhering to the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern had a lower prevalence of low HDL-cholesterol and higher prevalence of elevated fasting blood glucose, total cholesterol, LDL-cholesterol, and BMI than those adhering to the Traditional pattern. While these patterns were named according to their predominant food groups for interpretability, it should be noted that each cluster encompassed heterogeneous food components. For example, the “Traditional” pattern was rice-based but also included seaweed and legumes, while the “Flour-based Foods & Sweets” pattern comprised not only sweets and flour-based products but also dairy products and fruits. Thus, the cluster labels are intended to reflect the dominant characteristics rather than to capture all food groups within each pattern, and should not be interpreted as value judgments of the overall healthfulness of the patterns.

All three dietary patterns identified in this study had a high proportion of refined white rice, with the Traditional pattern having the highest proportion at 52.6%. Correspondingly, the Traditional pattern had the highest proportion of energy derived from carbohydrates among the three patterns, with 68.7% for men and 70.9% for women. Our findings indicate that the proportion of carbohydrate intake in the Traditional pattern is relatively high, exceeding the 55–65% range recommended by the KDRI [19]. The long-term secular trend of the Traditional pattern showed a steady decrease, consistent with the decreasing trend in carbohydrate intake reported in the 2020 KNHANES nutrient intake analysis [20]. These results suggest that the dietary patterns of Koreans are gradually changing, moving away from traditional diets and towards more diverse food groups. The declining trend in rice consumption, a key feature of the traditional pattern, has been consistently reported in previous studies. Two studies using data from the KNHANES, one based on 1998, 2001 and 2005 [6], and the other on 1998, 2007–2009 and 2013 [21], have reported a declining trend in rice consumption. Moreover, a cross-sectional study using 3-day dietary records over 13 years confirmed the persistence of Korean dietary pattern characterized by rice, vegetables, and kimchi; approximately 40% of the study population consistently adhered to this dietary pattern over time. This is lower than the average of 53% of men and women adhering to the Traditional pattern in this study in 2010, which may be due to differences in the foods that make up the pattern [22].

The Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern, the highest of the three patterns in 2022, has shown a steady increase in both men and women over the study period. Previous studies have identified several flour-based food patterns, but sweets have not been associated with them. The previous studies showed a higher increased risk with disease outcomes: ‘flour-based food and meat’ pattern and dyslipidemia [23], ‘flour-based food and meat’ pattern and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [24], and ‘fatty fish, meat, flour-based food’ pattern and glycemia [25]. The Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern was in accordance with the nutrient recommendation of KDRI, showing odds ratio less than 1 in TG/HDL in both sexes. Moreover, the inverse associations with high blood pressure and triglycerides were not statistically significant in men. The discrepancy—namely, the weak associations with cardiometabolic disease indices in this study—possibly explained by the food composition of the derived dietary patterns. Specifically, the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern was not characterized by a high proportion of meat, but higher proportion of non-salted vegetables, eggs, milk and dairy products and vegetable oil. These food compositions may have contributed to the inverse association for cardiometabolic risk factors. Nevertheless, due to the cross-sectional design of KNHANES, temporal causality cannot be drawn from identified dietary patterns and cardiometabolic outcomes [26]. Therefore, these findings may require further validation through future long-term follow-up studies or intervention studies.

Of note, sugar-sweetened beverages accounted for 3.7% of the total energy intake in the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern. Sugar-sweetened beverages are a major source of added sugars in the diet, which have been reported to be associated with weight gain, increased risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease and a high risk of some cancers [27]. Despite global concerns over the chronic disease of sugar-sweetened beverages, intake levels in Asia remain among the lowest, less than 50 g/d. These relatively low levels of sugar-sweetened beverage intake may explain the less unfavorable cardiometabolic profiles observed in the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern. Similarly, a “fried snacks, sweets, and high-fat dairy products” pattern among South Asians in the United States, was inversely associated with metabolic risk factors [28]. The increase in Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern may be related to the Westernization of Korean dietary patterns. Sweets, which accounted for 3.2% of total energy intake in the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern, a higher proportion than in the other two patterns, are generally reported to be high in energy and added sugars and low in essential nutrients and fiber. Although certain sugary foods, such as dark chocolate, have been reported to have potential health benefits, such as reducing blood pressure and LDL cholesterol levels, the evidence still suggests that sugar consumption should be limited [29]. The increase in this pattern coincides with an increase in the consumption of ultra-processed foods, including ready-to-eat products, sweet snacks and sweets, and mass-produced bakery products. In fact, a recent study using the KNHANES reported long-term trends and patterns of ultra-processed foods consumption from 1998 to 2022, showing a consistent increase in energy intake from ultra-processed foods including sweets, with the exception of the COVID-19 pandemic in Korea [30].

The Red Meat & Alcohol pattern has also been identified in previous studies using factor analysis [31, 32]. Our findings indicate that adherence to the Red Meat & Alcohol pattern among men was significantly associated with several cardiovascular risk factors, including elevated fasting blood glucose, blood pressure, triglycerides, total cholesterol, waist circumference, BMI, and lower HDL-cholesterol levels. A recent ecological study found that red meat and alcohol consumption may be associated with shorter life expectancy [33], which is in line with our findings.

A posteriori exploratory approach offers flexibility to derive new patterns. In our study, we analyzed 16 years of data among adults aged ≥ 19 years and identified three major clusters. A study focusing on older adults (≥ 50 years) over a 3-year period identified four clusters: “condiment, vegetables, and meat,” “wheat flour, bread, fruits, milk, and dairy products,” “white rice, fish, and seaweeds,” and “whole grain, bean products, and kimchi” [34]. Another study of children and adolescents aged 6–17 years identified “fast food and soda,” “white rice and kimchi,” and “oil and seasoned vegetable” patterns [35]. The discovery of such novel and atypical dietary patterns highlights the flexibility of the posteriori approach, enabling a more realistic reflection of the unique dietary habits and food group characteristics of specific populations and cultural contexts [2]. In particular, our study revealed a persistent 16-year increasing trend in the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern, which can be interpreted as reflecting the growing public health concern regarding ultra-processed foods. Thus, our findings extend beyond exploratory or index-based approaches, offering new evidence through the consistent application of a cluster methodology to long-term secular trend analysis. The observed increase in flour-based foods and sweets among urban residents and young adults underscores the policy relevance of developing targeted dietary guidelines for these vulnerable groups.

Among the urban population, adherence to the traditional dietary pattern was relatively low as shown in Tables 2 and 3. In contrast, the adherence to the flour-based dietary pattern remained consistently high among urban women. A similar trend was observed among young adults in their 20 s, with an increasing proportion of individuals adhering to a diet of flour-based foods and sweets, as illustrated in Fig. 2. This trend may be partly explained by recent cultural phenomena in Korea, such as the popularity of ‘bakery tours’, which have contributed to increased consumption of sweetened breads and confectionery. Supporting this observation, a recent study reported that the proportion of energy intake from ultra-processed foods including breads and sweet bakery products significantly increased in 2020–2022 compared to 1998–2005 [30]. From a public health perspective, these findings highlight the need of dietary guidelines that address the excessive consumption of flour-based foods and sweets. This is particularly important given that the flour-based dietary pattern was significantly associated with higher odds of hypercholesterolemia in women in the present study. Therefore, targeted dietary recommendations may be necessary for vulnerable groups, particularly young adults and urban populations, to mitigate the potential health risks associated with this emerging dietary pattern.

Beyond cultural influences, structural factors such as urbanization, economic development, and globalization of the food industry may have contributed to the dietary transitions observed in this study. Over the past decades, many countries worldwide have undergone a profound “nutrition transition,” shifting from diets rich in fiber and whole grains to those high in refined sugars, fats, and sodium [36–38]. Such transitions often occur alongside broader demographic, socioeconomic, and environmental changes, including rapid urbanization, industrial development, and climate change [39–41]. In Korea, urbanization and increased workforce participation have brought about marked changes in dietary behaviors. Recent evidence indicates that younger single-person households, in particular, exhibit higher frequencies of convenience food purchases and lower reliance on traditional home-cooked meals [42]. Moreover, convenience stores and online delivery services have expanded to the extent that they now rival or even replace the role of traditional supermarkets, substantially enhancing access to ready-to-eat products and franchise-based food outlets [43]. In addition, economic growth and rising household incomes may have increased the affordability of processed and imported foods, thereby accelerating the shift away from traditional rice-based diets. Consistent with this, higher consumption of ultra-processed foods has been observed among higher-income groups in Korea [44]. Furthermore, the globalization of the food industry, particularly the market expansion of transnational food corporations, has increased the availability and marketing of ultra-processed foods, including sweet confectioneries and packaged snacks, thereby promoting the spread of Westernized dietary patterns [45]. These macro-level drivers may provide an important context for explaining the decline of the Traditional pattern and the concurrent rise of the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern.

In addition to these structural factors, the observed dietary transition has important implications for both diet quality and the sustainability of the food system. From a nutritional perspective, the expansion of the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern may further exacerbate the already insufficient intake of calcium and vitamin C among Koreans, raising concerns about potential long-term risks to skeletal health and the maintenance of physical function [9, 46, 47]. Moreover, high reliance on refined grains and ultra-processed foods increases energy use, greenhouse gas emissions, food waste, and packaging waste throughout production and distribution, thereby threatening the environmental sustainability of the food system in the long term [48, 49]. These findings therefore highlight the need for public health nutrition strategies that address not only individual-level dietary quality but also the structural determinants of the food environment and sustainability.

Young adults in the present study showed the steepest increase in adherence to Westernized dietary patterns, typified by higher intakes of flour-based foods and sweets. Because dietary habits established in early adulthood often track into later life, sustained adherence to this pattern may bring forward the onset of metabolic syndrome and, amplify cardiometabolic burden in midlife. This interpretation is consistent with longitudinal evidence. In a recent Australian longitudinal cohort that followed participants from ages 21 to 30, adherence to an unhealthy Western dietary pattern predicted a higher risk of metabolic syndrome and increased insulin resistance [50], implying the importance of optimizing diets in early adulthood to improve later cardiometabolic health. With acknowledging our cross-sectional design precluding causal inference, our age-stratified findings suggest that young adults can be a target for chronic disease prevention. Tailored counseling for this group may help mitigate future population-level cardiometabolic burden.

Sex-specific studies dealing with dietary intakes remain limited. A cross-sectional study on sex differences of health behaviors and their associations with metabolic syndrome among middle-aged Korean adults found that alcohol consumption in men and an unhealthy diet in women were associated with higher prevalence of metabolic syndrome [51]. Similarly, a cross-sectional study conducted in China identified sex-specific dietary patterns and their associations with metabolic syndrome among adults aged 20 to 65: an ‘animal and fried food’ pattern in men and a ‘high-salt and energy’ pattern in women, both of which were associated with higher prevalence of metabolic syndrome [52]. Another Korean study using Korea Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES) found that favorable associations of hansik intake with body composition, dyslipidemia, and blood pressure differed by sex among adults aged over 40 [53]. Most prior Asian studies to date, which examined sex-specific differences, have focused on a limited age range. Our study addresses this knowledge gap by including a broader age range, capturing dietary trends across different life stages by including adults aged 19 years or older based on exploratory method. These findings contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of sex-specific dietary patterns and their potential long-term health implications. The primary finding regarding sex differences in this study is that following the Red Meat & Alcohol pattern was associated with higher prevalence of cardiometabolic disease factors, including high fasting blood glucose, high blood pressure, in men, whereas this trend was less pronounced in women. Similarly, adherence to the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern was also associated with higher prevalence of cardiometabolic related health outcomes, particularly in terms of waist circumference and BMI levels in men, whereas this trend is less pronounced in women. A recently published paper analyzed dietary intake trends using the KHEI for adults of both sexes aged 19 years and over from the same national data as our study and analyzed that, based on the KHEI, younger ages show lower diet quality and men consume more sugar-sweetened beverages and less fruit. This is interpreted as a similar finding to our study of more Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern at younger ages in both sexes [11].

In addition to these sex- and age-specific findings, another important consideration in interpreting our results is the role of BMI. As shown in Tables 7 and 8, we presented results both with and without BMI adjustment. BMI may act as both a confounder and a potential mediator in the association between dietary patterns and cardiometabolic outcomes. Therefore, Model 2 reflects the overall association, whereas Model 3 assesses associations independent of body weight status. Presenting both models provide a form of sensitivity analysis, allowing readers to evaluate the robustness of our findings under different assumptions.

Strengths and limitations

There are several limitations to this study. First, the single-day 24-hour dietary recall used to assess dietary intake is self-reported and therefore inherently susceptible to recall bias and measurement error. Previous studies have conducted methodological comparisons of approaches in dietary pattern estimation. For example, it has been reported that the single 24-hour recall is limited in characterizing dietary patterns, while the 48-hour recall has demonstrated superior performance compared with both single recalls and food diaries in a large British cohort of 2,266 individuals [54]. Energy underreporting is a well-documented concern in such assessments, particularly among overweight and obese individuals. Validation studies in Korean adults have demonstrated that total energy intake estimated by 24-hour recalls is underestimated by approximately 12% when compared with the doubly labeled water method [55]. Moreover, analyses of KNHANES data have shown that underreporting is more prevalent among women, individuals with obesity, and those with lower education levels [56]. Such misreporting could have influenced cluster formation and potentially attenuated the observed associations, especially for dietary patterns characterized by less healthy food choices. Although no additional sensitivity analyses or validation procedures were conducted to directly address this issue, we explicitly acknowledge it as an important limitation that should be considered in the interpretation of our findings. Second, although we adjusted for the potential confounding factors, the unknown, unmeasured, or residual confounding factors cannot be ruled out. Third, dietary patterns were derived from a single clustering solution applied to pooled data from 2007 to 2022, which assumes stability of the underlying cluster structure across 16 years. This assumption may not fully capture gradual shifts in food consumption behaviors over time. Fourth, the definition of dietary supplement use differed across survey years—defined as “ever use” from 2007 to 2009 and as “use for at least two consecutive weeks in the past year” since 2010. This inconsistency may have affected the interpretation of supplement use prevalence, but we included it as a descriptive characteristic (Tables 3 and 4) because supplement use is considered an important indicator of health-related behaviors. Importantly, our main analyses were based on nutrient intakes from foods and beverages, and supplement use was neither incorporated into the derivation of dietary patterns nor included as a covariate in regression models. Therefore, it is unlikely that this definitional change influenced the primary findings. Fifth, the dietary patterns identified by cluster analysis do not have an objective criterion for public recommendation. Lastly, as these dietary patterns are culture- and population-specific, they may not be generalizable to other populations.

Despite these limitations, the present study has several strengths. First, we utilized data from the KNHANES, which is a national representative survey with a large sample size in Korea. This enables us to generalize our findings to the Korean adult population. Second, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to analyze exploratory dietary patterns over an extended period, including the recent year 2022, among adults aged 19 years and older. Therefore, this research can provide valuable insights into dietary patterns and composition in the Korean adult population. Additionally, subgroup analyses by age and sex provided a more nuanced understanding of how these demographic factors are associated with secular trends in dietary patterns and the prevalence of cardiometabolic health outcomes.

Conclusions

In this nationally representative sample of Korean adults, dietary patterns have gradually shifted from traditional to more Westernized forms over the period from 2007 to 2022, characterized by the Red Meat & Alcohol pattern and the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern. These changes differed by sex and age, with the Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern being particularly prevalent among women and younger adults, indicating a generational and sex-based shift in dietary behaviors. The Red Meat & Alcohol pattern was associated with multiple cardiometabolic risk factors, and the associations were more pronounced in men. The Flour-based Foods & Sweets pattern was related to adverse body composition and lipid profiles in both sexes. These findings suggest that the health impact of dietary transitions may differ by sex and age, underscoring the importance of targeted nutrition interventions and sex-specific public health strategies. For instance, workplace-centered strategies for men that offer nutrition education including the promotion of moderate drinking and initiatives to address after-work drinking culture and excessive meat consumption, could mitigate risks related to the “Red Meat & Alcohol” pattern. For women, campaigns encouraging reduced consumption of refined flour and sweet by addressing portion size reduction and mandatory disclosure of minimum sweets content in beverages and desserts may be relevant. Implementing such gender-tailored nutrition education could enhance the translation of our findings into effective public health practice. Further longitudinal and intervention studies are warranted to elucidate the causal relationships between dietary patterns and cardiometabolic outcomes.
