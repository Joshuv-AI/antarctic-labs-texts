The effect of therapeutic play-based intervention programme applied to earthquake victim preschool children on psychosocial well-being: a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Sümeyra Topal, Sinem Yalnızoğlu Çaka, Gülzade Uysal, Erhan Alabay, Yasin Demir, Semiha Cömertoğlu Arslan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTherapeutic play intervention is an effective strategy that can promote cognitive development, reduce anxiety and stress, and increase confidence, enabling children to explore their problems, find solutions, and regulate their emotions. The current study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of the therapeutic play-based intervention program (TOTEM), which was developed to reduce the adverse psychosocial effects of earthquakes in preschool children.MethodsThis study was conducted as a randomised, single-blinded, two-arm trial with a pre-test and post-test control. The study was conducted between May and September 2024 with 53 earthquake survivor children aged 4–6 years (Intervention Group: 26, Control Group: 27) in a container area in Kahramanmaraş. They were randomised to either a control group receiving usual care or an intervention group receiving a therapeutic play-based intervention program. The Descriptive Information Form, Preschool Anxiety Scale (PAS), Psychosocial Status Assessment Scale for Children Aged 3–6 Years – Parent-Form (PSAS 3–6), and Sleep Disturbance Scale for Children (SDSC) were used to collect the data.ResultsWhen the differences between the mean scores of the PAS, PSAS 3–6 and SDSC were examined, it was found that there was no significant difference between the groups before the intervention (p > 0.05). However, a statistically significant difference was observed in the post-test of the first month after the intervention (p < 0.05). Accordingly, it was determined that the scores of the experimental group were lower than those of the control group.ConclusionsThis study concluded that TOTEM can be an effective intervention that can support the psychosocial well-being of preschool children with disaster experience. This study may provide insight into the necessity of including therapeutic play in intervention programs aiming to contribute to the psychosocial well-being of all professional groups working with children.Trial registrationThis study was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov on 24/02//2025; registration number: NCT06845111.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03317-4.Keywords: Preschool child, Anxiety, Psychosocial factors, Sleep-wake disorders, Nursing care

Introduction

Earthquakes are unpredictable natural disasters with devastating effects on people and buildings [1]. On February 6, 2023, two major earthquakes with magnitudes of 7.7 and 7.6, centered in Kahramanmaraş, occurred, followed by more than 1,000 aftershocks [2]. Earthquakes may lead to profound psychological trauma that affects the cognitive, emotional, and physical development of children who are especially vulnerable [3–5]. Post-traumatic stress symptoms, anxiety, sleep, and attention problems are observed in 50% of children after an earthquake [6–8]. Furthermore, security anxiety and depression are also among the frequent impacts of earthquakes [6, 7, 9]. Children’s post-traumatic reactions vary according to their developmental periods, environmental factors, and coping strategies. Especially preschool children cannot understand cause-and-effect relationships like adults since they are in the preoperational stage of cognitive development and, therefore, experience negative emotions more intensely. Additionally, the fact that their language skills are still at the developmental stage makes it challenging for them to express these emotions effectively [5, 10].

Emotions are a decisive factor directing individuals’ behaviors. Negative emotions, such as fear, anger, sadness, and guilt, experienced during disasters, are basic reactions. Children’s emotional dysregulation after a disaster after a disaster can lead to long-term psychological problems. Hence, strategies for facilitating healthy expression of emotions through socially acceptable behavior in the aftermath of a catastrophe are essential. Art, play, and behavioral techniques are effective methods for reducing traumatic symptoms [8, 11, 12]. Therapeutic play enables young children to express their emotions and thoughts, supporting emotional regulation [13, 14]. Moreover, it reduces anxiety and stress, promotes cognitive development, and fosters a sense of trust [5, 11, 12]. Play therapy applied after the Soma mine disaster effectively reduced psychological problems in children [8, 15]. Therapeutic play represents an important psychosocial intervention tool to support children’s well-being after a disaster [11, 16]. Therapeutic play-based approaches after an earthquake are accepted as an effective method to help children cope with traumatic stress. The approaches above allow children to express their emotions through play and receive psychological support. However, limited information is available on therapeutic play-based approaches after an earthquake [11, 16, 17]. Providing appropriate support to children after a traumatic event, such as an earthquake, is critical to preventing future mental health problems and psychopathologies. In this regard, providing supportive approaches, such as play therapy, to children and having these methods carried out by expert teams can contribute to children’s overall well-being. Based on this need, the purpose of the current research was to evaluate the effectiveness of the Therapeutic Play-Based Intervention Program (TOTEM), developed to reduce the adverse psychosocial effects that an earthquake may cause in preschool children, and its impact on children’s psychosocial well-being.

Primary research question

What is the effect of a play-based intervention programme on anxiety, psychosocial status, and sleep quality in preschool children who experienced a traumatic disaster, such as an earthquake, compared to usual care?

Secondary research questions

Is the play-based intervention programme, including family involvement, affected by children’s sociodemographic and earthquake experience-related characteristics?

Methods

Study design

The research was conducted as a single-blinded, two-arm randomized controlled trial with pre-test and post-test control groups in a container area (emergency shelter) in Kahramanmaraş by creating intervention and control groups with preschool children affected by the earthquake. The study aimed to evaluate differences in the scale means between the intervention and control groups. Participants were randomised 1:1 to intervention and control groups. Additionally, a clinical trial number was received from ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06845111). The statistician was the only one blinded, as it proved challenging to blind the participants and the intervenor.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Health Sciences University Hamidiye Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Date: 11 August 2023, Decision No: 2023/15). The informed consent form was read to all participants before the study commenced, and their verbal and written consent was obtained. The study ensured the protection of individual rights by conforming to the principles outlined in the ‘Declaration of Helsinki’. Informed written consent to participate in the study was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of children.

Sample size calculation

The analysis conducted using the program G*Power (v3.1.9.2) to determine the sample size calculated the minimum sample size to be 58 children with a first type error (alpha) of 0.05, a second type error (beta) of 0.20, a test power of 0.80, and an effect size of 0.75 considering the parameters in the reference study [18]. When the sample size reached 53 participants (intervention group: 26, control group: 27), the study power was recalculated. Based on the difference between the intervention and control groups for the second measurement time in the scales, the power was calculated as 0.82 for sleep, 0.87 for psychosocial status, and 0.98 for anxiety, respectively, at a high level. According to this power level, the sample size was found to be quite adequate, and the research was completed with a total of 53 individuals (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of research

Participants and setting

The research was conducted between May and September 2024 in a container area with preschool-age children who were victims of the earthquake. Children aged 4–6 years who were not diagnosed with any psychiatric and/or chronic disease, whose parents were alive, who had adequate receptive-expressive language skills, who had not previously attended an education program on psychosocial well-being, who did not participate in any preschool education institution during the study, and whose parents were literate were included in the study through simple random sampling (n = 110). Children and parents were excluded from the study if they wished to leave, did not attend one or more intervention sessions, had been diagnosed with any psychiatric or physical disease, declined to participate in the tests, or were unable to comply with the study requirements. The sample to be selected from the population was determined using the lottery method, which is a type of simple random sampling.

Randomisation and allocation

In this randomized controlled trial conducted to examine the TOTEM program’s effectiveness, after the children selected for the sample group were grouped according to their age groups (5 and 6 years old), an independent person randomized them to the intervention (n = 26) or control (n = 27) group using a randomization program (GraphPad Software QuicksCalcs, GraphPad Software Inc., USA) with the URL address https://www.graphpad.com/quickcalcs/randomize2/ before the intervention. Figure 1 shows the CONSORT flow chart (Fig. 1). Third-party blinding (by a statistician) was used in the study.

Instrument

Parents who agreed to take part in the research were informed about the study and signed the Informed Consent Form. Afterward, the parent or guardian completed the following scales: the “Sociodemographic Data Form” prepared by the researchers to investigate children’s and parents’ sociodemographic characteristics and the status of being affected by the earthquake, the “Preschool Anxiety Scale” to determine children’s anxiety levels, the “Psychosocial Status Assessment Scale” to evaluate children’s psychosocial well-being, and the “Sleep Disturbance Scale for Children” to assess children’s sleep.

Sociodemographic data form

This form, prepared by the researchers, includes the sociodemographic characteristics of children and their families, as well as earthquake-related data, and consists of 15 questions [3, 5, 9, 11, 13]. The form contains questions about the child’s age, sex, chronic disease status, parents’ age, education, and family type. In addition to sociodemographic data, information was obtained about the family’s status of being buried in the wreckage, being injured, receiving treatment, losing a neighbor/friend, and experiencing a traumatic event during the earthquake.

Preschool anxiety scale (PAS) (Parent form)

Şahin (2020) adapted the Preschool Anxiety Scale (PAS) (developed by Spence, Rapee, McDonald, and Ingram (2001) to Turkish [19, 20]. Whereas the scale’s original version has 28 items that measure social anxiety, separation anxiety, physical injury fears, generalized anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive disorder, five items regarding post-traumatic stress disorder were added when adapting the scale to Turkish, and validity and reliability studies were conducted with these items. The scale is a 5-point rating scale, and each item is scored between 0 and 4. A high score on the scale indicates high anxiety in preschool children, while a low score refers to low anxiety in preschool children. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated to be 0.93, which falls within the acceptable range for the scale’s reliability [19]. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was computed to be 0.91 for this study.

Psychosocial status assessment scale for children aged 3–6 years (Parent form) (PSAS)

It is an assessment instrument consisting of 31 items developed by Güneş-Şan and Altay (2021) and measuring psychosocial status based on parental reports in children aged 3–6 years [21]. For each item on the PSAS, a minimum of “0” and a maximum of “124” points can be obtained from the 5-point Likert scale. A low total scale score indicates that the child is not at risk for psychosocial problems, whereas a high score demonstrates a risk for psychosocial issues. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was computed to be 0.83, which falls within the acceptable range for the scale’s reliability [21]. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated to be 0.88 for the present study.

Sleep disturbance scale for children (SDSC)

The scale developed by Bruni et al. (1996) was adapted to Turkish, and Ağça-Bilmenoğlu (2019) performed its validity and reliability study [22, 23]. The scale developed to investigate sleep disturbances in children comprises 26 items, and each question is scored between 1 and 5. The assessment instrument includes questions about disorders of initiating and maintaining sleep, sleep breathing disorders, disorders of arousal, sleep-wake transition disorders, disorders of excessive somnolence, and sleep hyperhidrosis. The scores that can be received on the scale range from 26 to 130, and high scores are interpreted as indicating high sleep disturbances. Within the scope of the scale’s reliability, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated to be 0.84, and test-retest reliability was estimated to be 0.79 [23]. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was computed to be 0.85 for the current study.

Therapeutic play-based intervention (TOTEM) program developed for earthquake victim children

The games developed for the program’s content are based on creative play, dramatic play, and games that require energy. The program lasts five weeks and consists of a total of 10 one-hour sessions, held twice a week. The prepared program was developed based on the psychosocial development domain of ​​5-6-year-old children living in the largest container area in Kahramanmaraş (Fig. 2).

Formation stages of the TOTEM programme

Theoretical framework of the TOTEM program

The TOTEM program was prepared using D. Ausubel’s meaningful learning theory, Jean Piaget’s cognitive development theory, A. Bandura’s social learning theory, and L. S. Vygotsky’s social-cultural development theory. Meaningful learning theory suggests starting from what children know and progressing to what they do not know and using concrete materials [24]. The said approach was implemented using concrete materials and familiar concepts in games. Cognitive development theory emphasizes that developmental periods should be taken into account in children’s learning processes [25]. In this respect, the program included symbolic games and games that supported imagination, considering the characteristics of the pre-operational period within the sample age group. Social learning theory suggests that the environment has a significant impact on learning [26]; therefore, games that require group and cooperative play were also included. Socio-cultural development theory emphasizes the skills that children can acquire with an adult’s support and the importance of teamwork [27]. Based on this theory, learning with assistance and group games was included in the program, and at the end of the games, it was ensured that children expressed their feelings and thoughts together as a group. Trained mental health professionals (SCA and YD) led children in expressing their feelings and thoughts together as a group at the end of the sessions by sitting in a circle with the children and asking them concrete questions, such as “Who felt sad or scared when they drew their picture?” Trained mental health professionals ensured that each child was emotionally regulated before leaving the session.

Validity analyses of the TOTEM program

Content validity

The Content Validity Index (CVI) was calculated by averaging the Content Validity Ratios (CVRs) to assess the necessity of each item in the program. The Lawshe method (1 = suitable, 2 = needs improvement, 3 = not ideal for each item) was employed to collect the opinions of 12 experts in the relevant field [28]. According to the opinions obtained, the CVI score for the overall scale was calculated to be 0.94, and the CVI scores for all components ranged from 0.89 to 1.00. The critical value for the CVI is accepted as 0.66 for 12 experts [29]. Since the CVI average in each of the eight components of the TOTEM program content was 0.56 and above, content validity was ensured.

Pilot study

Before data collection, a preliminary study was conducted with 5 participants who had the same characteristics as the sample group in their kindergartens and with the participation of their families, considering the children’s ages (3 children aged 5 and 2 children aged 6) and sex (3 boys and two girls) in terms of the comprehensibility of the program draft’s sessions. Furthermore, feedback was received from the parents of these five children on the suitability of the forms. In line with the opinions received from researchers, teachers, and participating parents, there were no incomprehensible statements or games in the program.

Data collection

The sessions were conducted in the TOTEM group, 2 days a week (Wednesday and Thursday) for 5 weeks (10 sessions in total), in groups of 12 children in kindergartens located in the container city. The sessions were conducted by the TOTEM program team in groups of 12 children at kindergartens located in the container city. Each session lasted 45 min, with at least one book read and at least three different games played. During the sessions, psychiatrist SCA, one of our researchers, observed the play groups to assess the children’s psychological state. Five-minute breaks were taken between each game. The control group was not invited to the play area in the container during this process, and no initiatives related to the TOTEM program were implemented. A total of three evaluations were performed simultaneously for the TOTEM and control groups, including a pre-test one week before the program began, a post-test immediately after the program ended, and retention test measurements at the end of the first month. In line with ethical principles, the TOTEM program was applied to the children in the control group after the study was completed, and the educational materials used in the TOTEM group were made available after the research was completed. Supplementary Table 1 lists the titles, content, sample plays, and order of application of the TOTEM program.

All sessions were conducted by the same researchers (EA, ST, SCA, YD, and fellow HE) who developed the TOTEM program to ensure consistency and prevent potential problems arising from differences in the role of the trainer. Prior to the implementation of the TOTEM program, researchers EA, ST, and the scholarship recipient were responsible for the primary-level sessions, and all sessions were completed by the same researchers. Researcher EA is a faculty member with an associate professorship in child development and holds certifications in play therapy. Researcher ST holds an associate professorship in pediatric nursing and has conducted numerous studies related to different research designs. Researcher SCA (associate professor) is a child and adolescent psychiatrist, and YD is a faculty member with an associate professorship in psychology. Researchers SCA and YD were responsible for the psychological assessment of the children and monitoring their compliance with the sessions. Fidelity with the TOTEM protocol was ensured by these two researchers reviewing the protocol checklists during and after each session.

Statistical analysis

The data were evaluated using the SPSS 23.0 package program. Frequency distribution was presented for categorical variables, whereas mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values ​​were given for numerical variables. The data’s normal distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test, as well as the skewness and kurtosis values. The independent samples t-test was conducted for normally distributed data, and the Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data. The dependent samples t-test was used for time difference analysis, and Pearson’s correlation coefficient was applied to examine the correlation between the variables. A repeated measures test was performed for the intra-group comparisons of normally distributed data, and the Friedman test was used for non-normally distributed data. The scale’s reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. The results were considered significant at a 95% confidence interval and p < 0.05 level.

In the control group, a statistically significant difference was observed between the measurement times in terms of the PAS scale total scores (p = 0.026) and the separation anxiety subscale scores (p = 0.017), with the first measurement being higher than the third measurement. However, no statistically significant difference was found between the measurement times in terms of the PAS scale generalized anxiety, social anxiety, physical injury fears, and obsessive-compulsive disorder subscale scores (p > 0.05) (Figure 3).

Changes in anxiety scores according to measurement times

No significant difference was observed between the TOTEM and control groups in terms of total PAS scale scores and subscale scores at the first measurement (p > 0.05). According to the second measurement results, only the PAS scale physical injury fears subscale scores were lower in the intervention group than those in the control group (p < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was revealed between the two groups in terms of the PAS scale total scores and other subscales (p > 0.05). As seen from the third measurement results, the PAS scale total scores and all subscale scores were significantly lower in the TOTEM group than in the control group (all p < 0.05).

Sleep disturbance scale for children (SDSC) scores of the sample

Table 3 presents the SDSC scores for the sample and the corresponding eta values. In the TOTEM group, there was a statistically significant difference between the measurement times in the evaluations of the SDSC total scores (p < 0.001), disorders of initiating and maintaining sleep subscale score (p < 0.001), sleep-wake transition disorders subscale score (p = 0.007), and disorders of excessive somnolence subscale score (p = 0.008), and the third measurement scores were significantly lower than the other two measurements. However, the SDSC sleep breathing disorders, disorders of arousal subscale scores, and sleep hyperhidrosis subscale scores did not display a statistically significant difference between the measurement times in the TOTEM group (p > 0.05). In the control group, there was a statistically significant difference between the measurement times in terms of the SDSC total scores (p = 0.045), sleep breathing disorders subscale (p = 0.044) score, and disorders of excessive somnolence subscale score (p = 0.010), and the first measurement was significantly higher than the third measurement.

Discussion

This study found that the TOTEM program, a play-based intervention developed to support the psychosocial recovery of preschool earthquake victim children, positively affected children’s anxiety, psychosocial problems, and sleep problems. Play therapy is accepted as an effective tool for ensuring children’s emotional and psychological recovery after trauma. This approach enables children to express themselves and work through their problems through play, which is their natural language [13, 30]. Our study results demonstrated that a play-based group intervention is effective in the young age group after a major disaster, such as an earthquake. While the study found no significant difference between the two groups in the initial measurements regarding anxiety levels, it determined that the final measurements of the TOTEM group were lower than those of the control group in terms of total anxiety levels and all subscales. A decrease was observed in the TOTEM group, especially from the first to the second and third measurements. Studies have reported that post-traumatic play-based therapies reduce the adverse psychosocial effects of trauma in children, help them feel safe and express their emotions, improve their social and emotional skills, and effectively reduce their social anxiety and generalized anxiety levels [31–36]. Another study demonstrated that play-based interventions for trauma also effectively reduced physical injury fears [37]. Our findings contribute to the existing literature, which supports the notion that play-based approaches are an essential tool in psychosocial interventions, particularly during the preschool period, and have a positive impact on children’s psychological and psychosocial aspects, effectively reducing anxiety levels [31, 32, 38]. Additionally, our findings showed that the systematic and process-oriented structure of the TOTEM program had a positive effect on anxiety over time, which agrees with meta-analysis studies suggesting that therapeutic interventions can have lasting effects over time [39].

Moreover, although a significant decrease was identified in the total anxiety and separation anxiety scores in the control group, these differences were less evident in comparison with those in the TOTEM group (Table 2). The result above showed that anxiety levels could decrease over time despite no intervention being applied in the control group, and agrees with findings in the literature revealing that anxiety levels in children after a disaster can naturally change over time [11, 17, 40]. In addition to publications reporting that anxiety levels decrease over time in post-earthquake follow-up studies, it is also known that they may persist or worsen in some children. No apparent reason could be found to explain this variability. Nevertheless, it has been asserted that environmental supportive factors, such as family support and the school environment, as well as children’s hereditary characteristics, including age, sex, temperament, and coping mechanisms, may be effective [6, 40–42]. Therefore, it is thought that structured interventions, such as TOTEM, may be a program that can provide a more effective and systematic solution to reducing anxiety levels in addition to natural changes.

Our study found that the PSAS scores were significantly lower in the TOTEM group at the third measurement compared to the control group. While there was no significant difference between the three measurements in the control group, a substantial difference in the third measurement compared to the previous measurements in the TOTEM group demonstrates the positive impacts of the TOTEM program on psychosocial problems (Table 3). This finding agrees with the literature supporting the effectiveness of play-based interventions in reducing children’s psychosocial problems due to trauma [43]. The finding above is not surprising considering the effects of play-based therapies on strengthening children’s coping mechanisms, improving emotional regulation skills, and reducing stress and anxiety levels [13, 43, 44]. The positive effect of the TOTEM program on psychosocial problems as a trauma-sensitive play-based intervention after natural disasters may also be an intervention model that positively contributes to preventing issues that may occur in the long term.

Another important finding of the study is that both the total sleep disturbances scale score and the disorders of initiating and maintaining sleep subscale scores were significantly lower in comparison with the control group at the third measurement (Table 3). Studies have stated that sleep problems adversely impact post-traumatic recovery processes, and post-traumatic sleep regulation interventions can support recovery processes by effectively reducing emotional and behavioral issues and lowering anxiety and fear levels [6, 8, 36, 45]. Furthermore, the effective role of play-based interventions in reducing stress-related sleep problems in children is frequently highlighted [6, 8, 37]. Our study findings indicated that TOTEM also positively affected sleep disturbances, especially difficulties initiating and maintaining sleep in children after the earthquake.

Study strengths and limitations

The current research investigated the effects of the TOTEM program, a trauma-sensitive play-based intervention after natural disasters, on the anxiety levels, psychosocial problems, and sleep disturbances of 4-6-year-old children who were affected by a major disaster, the Kahramanmaraş earthquake. Considering the challenges of working with a small age group in container cities during the study and the difficulties of implementing and following up an 8-session program, the present study is considered valuable.

Some limitations should be considered when evaluating the study results. The results cannot be generalized to the entire population due to the study’s cross-sectional nature. Compliance with the TOTEM protocol was ensured by these two researchers reviewing the protocol checklists during and after each session. Although fidelity with the TOTEM protocol was ensured by the project’s own researchers, the fact that it was not carried out by an external observer (mental health professional peer) /supervisor may have led to bias. Additionally, mothers’ reports served as the basis for evaluating self-report questionnaires.

Conclusion and recommendations

Our study showed that the TOTEM program positively affected anxiety, psychosocial problems, and sleep problems in preschool children, revealing that TOTEM can be an effective intervention model in post-traumatic recovery. Implementing programs such as TOTEM enriches trauma-focused care in pediatric nursing and provides a holistic approach, presenting physical and psychosocial support for children’s recovery. Pediatric nurses can encourage participation in the program by guiding families and increasing family support during the recovery process. Collaboration of multidisciplinary teams is recommended to improve the program’s impact. Furthermore, there is a need for longitudinal studies to examine the long-term effects of the TOTEM program.
