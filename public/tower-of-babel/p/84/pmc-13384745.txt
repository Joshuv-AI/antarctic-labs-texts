A Rare Case of Scrofuloderma Presenting as Recurrent Tuberculosis in a Young Patient
Authors: Yoshihiro Okada, Asuka Okada, Shin Miyagawa, Ami Arimoto, Taisei Furumuro, Masanori Izuka, Yuma Watabe, Izumi Sato, Yusuke Inui, Masahide Ueda, Takahiro Ibaraki, Hideaki Takenaka, Sumito Choh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 23‐year‐old Indonesian man was referred to our institution due to a skin ulcer with white exudate on the right chest wall. He had undergone treatment for pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) in Indonesia 3 years prior to the consultation. Chest ultrasonography and computed tomography revealed multiple intercostal abscesses and a thickened right pleura with calcification. Histological findings from a skin biopsy of the ulcer and a positive polymerase chain reaction for Mycobacterium tuberculosis in the exudate indicated the involvement of TB. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with scrofuloderma and multiple intercostal abscesses. Recurrent TB may result from either the regrowth of residual strains or reinfection with a different strain. This report highlights that recurrent TB can manifest as scrofuloderma through contiguous spread from underlying intercostal abscesses. In cases of skin ulcers with an unclear aetiology, thorough histopathological and bacteriological assessments facilitate a prompt and accurate diagnosis.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is an infectious illness caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb). Cutaneous TB is an uncommon form of TB, and scrofuloderma is a clinical manifestation of cutaneous TB characterized by direct infectious spread from subcutaneous lesions [1]. Recurrent TB results from endogenous reactivation of the residual strain or exogenous reinfection with a distinct strain and is linked to diverse social and clinical factors [2]. Here, we present a rare instance of scrofuloderma that manifested as recurrent TB in a young patient.

Case Report

A 23‐year‐old Indonesian man was referred to our institution from a local hospital with a persistent skin ulcer on the right chest wall that had not improved over 2 months. Part of the ulcer was surgically removed at a local hospital to eliminate necrotic tissue. The patient had been diagnosed with pulmonary TB and had undergone anti‐TB treatment in Indonesia 3 years prior to his first consultation. The patient was a non‐smoker with no underlying medical conditions or prescribed medications. No symptoms suggestive of TB were reported. The skin ulcer was surrounded by redness and covered with white exudate; however, it was not accompanied by pain or warmth (Figure 1A). Blood tests showed no elevation of inflammatory markers, and tests for anti‐human immunodeficiency virus antibodies were negative. Chest ultrasonography revealed multiple intercostal abscesses just beneath the skin ulcer. Some abscesses were interconnected via a fistula; however, the connection between the abscesses and the pleural cavity was uncertain (Figure 1B). Chest radiography displayed no abnormal lung shadows suggestive of a recurrence of pulmonary TB (Figure 1C). Chest computed tomography (CT) exhibited a tissue opacity in the intercostal region and thickened right pleura with calcification (Figure 1D,E). The presence of rib lesions and pleural effusion was inconclusive. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing for Mtb in the exudate was positive. Acid‐fast bacilli smear was negative, and no bacteria were identified in the culture tests. A skin biopsy of the ulcer was conducted for further evaluation, and revealing granulomatous inflammation with necrosis (Figure 1F). Both bacteriological and histological findings indicated Mtb infection, suggesting that intercostal abscesses might have directly contributed to the development of skin ulcers. The patient was diagnosed with scrofuloderma and TB recurrence. Treatment with four anti‐TB agents (isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol and pyrazinamide) was initiated 8 days after referral. The size of the lesion decreased, and the ulcer surface began to crust over the commencement of anti‐TB treatment (Figure 2A–C). Surgical resection is considered if skin lesions and intercostal abscesses persist after the completion of anti‐TB treatment.

Discussion

Scrofuloderma is a rare clinical presentation of TB, characterized by direct infectious extension from subcutaneous lesions. The underlying subcutaneous lesions often involve the lymph nodes, bones or joints [1]. The patient was diagnosed with scrofuloderma based on a history of TB, PCR positivity for Mtb, pathological findings of cutaneous lesions, and the presence of intercostal abscesses contiguous with the skin ulcers. The negative culture results may have been attributed to specimen quality and partial excision of the ulcers prior to referral.

Patterson et al. reported that Mtb infection may induce the formation of new lymphatic vessels around the lesion [3]. In the present case, a thickened and calcified right pleura, indicative of previous tuberculous pleuritis, was observed adjacent to the intercostal abscesses. We hypothesize that newly formed lymphatic vessels and pleural adhesions at the site of old tuberculous pleural lesions may have facilitated lymphatic dissemination of Mtb to the intercostal region, thereby contributing to the formation of intercostal abscesses. Therefore, bacteriological evaluation of abscess fluid and pathological evaluation of intercostal lesions could enhance diagnostic accuracy. However, draining small intercostal abscesses was challenging, and complete surgical resection was invasive. Consequently, we initiated treatment with anti‐TB drugs alone and planned surgical resection based on the treatment response.

Case reports of scrofuloderma presenting as recurrent TB are lacking. Recurrence of TB is a significant global concern, with multidrug‐resistant (MDR) Mtb infection being a key risk factor, alongside poor treatment adherence and reduced host immunity [2, 4]. In the present case, the patient initially acquired Mtb infection in Indonesia and experienced a recurrence of TB following treatment in Japan. The patient was unlikely to have been immunocompromised; however, Indonesia has a high prevalence of MDR TB, and details regarding the infecting strain and previous treatment regimen were unavailable. Therefore, the possibility of recurrent TB needed to be considered. Similarly, Skiijevic et al. reported two cases of scrofuloderma in immunocompetent Serbian adults without a history of TB and emphasized the difficulty of making an early and accurate diagnosis [5]. A strength of the present case is that recurrent TB was considered in the initial phase, enabling timely diagnosis and treatment. Consequently, the assessment of drug sensitivity was hindered by negative culture results, posing challenges in determining the most appropriate treatment regimen. If skin and intercostal tuberculous lesions persist despite retreatment, surgical resection and bacteriological reassessment of the intercostal abscesses should be considered.

In the present case, the concern of recurring TB in a chest skin ulcer during the initial stage prompted timely diagnosis and treatment. Therefore, when encountering unexplained persistent skin ulcers in individuals with a history of TB, it is essential to consider the potential involvement of TB and to conduct a multifaceted diagnostic approach. Furthermore, detailed evaluation of underlying extracutaneous lesions could enhance diagnostic precision of cutaneous TB and aid in selecting a suitable treatment strategy.
