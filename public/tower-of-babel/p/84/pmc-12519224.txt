Proof-of-principle demonstration of epithermal neutron resonance spectroscopy utilizing a compact laser–driven electron accelerator
Authors: Jie Feng, Jie Ren, Hao Xu, Mingyang Zhu, Bingzhan Shi, Guoqiang Zhang, Jie Bao, Wenchao Yan, Yifei Li, Jinguang Wang, Xin Lu, Liming Chen, Jie Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceEpithermal neutron resonance spectroscopy has been limited by bulky spallation and accelerator-based sources, hindering on-site nuclear material analysis. This study employs a compact laser–driven electron accelerator to generate photonuclear neutrons, combined with neutron moderation and time-of-flight spectroscopy. It overcomes traditional limitations by achieving 0.8 μs time-resolution at 5 eV and better than 3% energy-resolution, enabling high-fidelity resonance detection in 115In and 109Ag. This breakthrough paves the way for tabletop, high-precision neutron spectroscopy in nuclear security and fundamental nuclear data acquisition.

Neutron resonance spectroscopy analysis (NRSA) (1), a highly advanced and specific nondestructive assay technique, offers a distinct means of interacting with atomic nuclei within materials, permitting the derivation of essential information, including elemental composition, isotopic ratios (2). The NRSA typically demands a short pulsed epithermal neutron source and is capable of attaining high-resolution spectroscopy (3), especially for energy range from 1 to 100 eV, which have many individual resonance characteristic peaks for different nuclides. Generally, the spallation neutron sources and electron accelerators based photonuclear neutron sources can meet the requirement of the NRSA (4–6), and their epithermal neutron durations are typically on the order of several microsecond (μs). But these facilities are usually large in size and cannot be flexibly moved, making it difficult to apply to some specialized scenarios, such as performance evaluation of stockpiled weapon (7), nondestructive testing of high-level nuclear waste (8, 9), etc.

In recent years, laser plasma accelerators (LPAs) have become increasingly mature (10–14), with ultrahigh acceleration gradients for electrons (100s GeV/m) and ions (10s TV/m) (15, 16). In addition, the LPAs, including the laser system and the charged particle acceleration system, can achieve a tabletop and compact design, which has the great potential to drive flexible neutron sources. The LPAs based neutron sources can be driven by photonuclear reaction via electrons (17–20), and nuclear fusion via proton or deuterium ions (21–23). These neutron sources usually have short pulse duration less than nanosecond (ns) and energy range between 100s keV to 10s MeV. Of course, these fast neutron sources can also be slowed down by a moderator to epithermal or even to cold neutron energy range (24, 25).

The LAPs based epithermal neutron sources can provide the pulse duration less than μs (26). They have also been used to realize NRSA and successfully applied to nuclide identification (24, 27), resonance imaging (24, 26), temperature measurement (28). However, all of these applications are based on LPAs ion sources, which are driven by the high power high energy laser facilities but with low repetition rate. What’s worse, these huge laser facilities would generate ultraintense γ-ray burst, which results in the saturation of the neutron Time-of-Flight (nToF) detector. Even with a high-voltage gating system, the decay tail of the γ-ray burst remains extremely strong, enough to drown out the faster neutron signals. Thus, with present laser-based epithermal neutron sources, it is difficult to achieve high signal-to-noise-ratio (SNR) measurements and flexible applications that feature low construction costs.

In this letter, to address the deficiencies of the present laser based epithermal neutron source, a compact terawatt (TW) femtosecond (fs) laser system has been employed to drive plasma wakefield electron accelerator. A repetition rate intense electron source with peak current of kiloampere, energy of ∼45 MeV has been realized and utilized to drive photonuclear reaction in a lead converter. The generated neutrons have been further moderated to acquire the short duration epithermal neutrons. Moreover, the influence of electron energy on neutron spectra is also analyzed in detail. Subsequently, the repetition rate epithermal neutron source has been utilized for the NRSA as a proof-of-principle application with high SNR and high energy-resolution.

Experimental Setup

The experiment was conducted using a table-top Ti: sapphire laser system at the SECUF within the Institute of Physics, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The experimental setup is depicted in Fig. 1. A linearly polarized laser pulse with a repetition rate of 2 Hz (maximum 100 Hz), a center wavelength of ∼800 nm, a duration of 20 fs, and an energy of 50 mJ was focused by an f-number 5.5 off-axis parabolic mirror onto a supersonic gas jet with a radius of 150 μm and a near Gaussian density distribution. By regulating the gas back pressure, the outflow nitrogen gas density ranged from 1×1017 to 5×1019cm−3. The laser pulse was focused to a main spot with radius w0≈6.5 μm, containing approximately 71% of the laser power, i.e., ≈2 TW, resulting in an intensity of ≈4.1 ×1018 Wcm−2 and the normalized vector potential a0≈1.38. The plasma channel was monitored by a top-view system. After the laser interaction with pure nitrogen gas (purity 99.999%), the electron beam from the laser plasma wakefield acceleration bombarded the DRZ fluorescent screen emitting fluorescence, which was detected by an EM-CCD to obtain an electron beam spot image. The electron beam was deflected by the magnet and bombarded the other DRZ screen to display its energy distribution. When moving in the lead (Pb) converter, the accelerated electron beam bombarded a 10 cm-thick lead converter located 1 m away from the gas nozzle to generate bremsstrahlung radiation. Then, the collimated γ-rays mainly induced the photonuclear reaction (γ, n) in the converter. Four BD-PND bubble detectors were used to measure the angular distribution of neutrons and the source intensity (18). Subsequently, the photonuclear neutrons emitted from the side of the converter were moderated by a polyethylene (PE) moderator to increase the yield of epithermal neutrons. The moderator has a thickness of 5 cm and near the converter. The generated epithermal neutrons passed through two boron-containing (20%) polyethylene collimators with a hole diameter of 5 cm and were detected by a plastic scintillator (EJ-420) (29) detector 1.72 m away from the moderator. A sample composed with a 1 mm thick indium (In) sheet and a 0.5 mm thick silver (Ag) sheet was placed in the front of the second collimator.

Laser-Driven Electron Accelerator.

The concept of laser wakefield acceleration (LWFA) was first proposed by Tajima and Dawson (30). The most efficient regime is bubble (31) or blow out (32), which requires intense laser pulse with a0>1 and short pulse duration τ≤2πc/ωp, where ωp=4πnee2/me is the plasma frequency. When the laser pulse ionizes the gas jet and propagates in the underdense plasma, its ponderomotive force would push the background electrons away from the high-intensity region and drive a relativistic plasma wave, as shown in the inset of Fig. 1. The wave consists of a string of ion cavities, and the trapped electrons inside can be accelerated by the longitudinal electrostatic fields over E=cmeωp/e which is produced by the separation of electrons and ions. Due to the effect of laser self-focusing, the laser transversal size would be matched in higher-density plasma, i.e., plasma bubble radius R=2ca0/ωp≈w0, which results in that the laser peak intensity exceeds the ionization threshold of nitrogen atom inner shell electron (552 eV for N+6), i.e., a0≈1.7. Thus, these electrons would be ionized close to the peak of the laser intensity envelope, then they appear rest and slip backward relative to the plasma bubble, eventually captured and accelerated (33). In addition, as the laser plasma wave enters the lower-density region, the laser defocusing and its intensity decreases, and the ionization injection process stops, so that a quasi-mono-energetic electron beam can be obtained (34).

In the experiment, the stable, collimated and energetic electron beam is achieved by adjusting the position of laser focus in the gas target and optimizing the gas jet density. The electron energy distribution of 1,000 consecutive shots is shown in Fig. 2A, where the peak energy is approximately 45 MeV, and the full width at half maximum (FWHM) is approximately 30 MeV. The beam average charge is ∼10 pico-Coulomb. The beam divergence (FWHM) is 17 mrad in horizontal and 12 mrad in vertical, as shown in Fig. 2B. Moreover, the beam pointing stability is 0.5mrad and 0.7 mrad (SD) in horizontal and vertical respectively, as shown by black circles in Fig. 2C.

Photonuclear Neutrons Generation and Moderation.

In order to demonstrate the influence of electron beam energy on the energy spectra of generated and moderated neutrons, Monte Carlo simulations were carried out via the Geant4 code (35). The electron beams with different single energies bombard the lead converter, whose parameters are the same as the experimental situation. It can be seen that for different electron energies, the neutron energy spectra have roughly the same shapes below 1 MeV. Additionally, these neutrons are moderated by the moderator. After that, the number of neutrons with energies below 10 keV increases significantly, and the shapes of the energy spectra are even more similar below 1 MeV, as shown in Fig. 3B. For the epithermal neutron energy range of 1 to 100eV which is of more concern in applications, the shape of their spectra is almost unaffected by the electron energy. However, the higher the energy of the driving electrons, the smoother the neutron spectrum in this energy range. Since the number of electrons used in the simulation was only 107, when this number increased significantly, the fluctuations would also be smoothed out accordingly.

To simulate the neutron energy spectrum generated in this experiment more realistically, the average of the electron energy spectra from 1,000 shots obtained in the experiment was input into the Monte Carlo simulation. The spatial distributions of neutrons emitted from the side of the lead and modulator are shown respectively in Fig. 3C. With respect to the direction of the EJ-420 detector, the lateral source size of the moderated neutrons is approximately 3 cm×5 cm in FWHM. The corresponding neutron energy spectrum is shown by the red solid line in Fig. 3D, and the number of neutrons in the energy range of 1 to 100 eV has increased nearly fourfold compared to that of the unmoderated neutrons (blue solid line). The energy spectra of neutrons emitted from different regions of the side of the moderator are also highly consistent, as shown by the yellow solid line. In order to further demonstrate the temporal characteristics of the moderated neutron source, the time distribution of epithermal neutrons with different energies was statistically analyzed in the Monte Carlo simulation, and the results are presented in Fig. 3E. The lower the neutron energy, the lower the time-resolution becomes. Even so, considering this experimental configuration, when the energy is 1 eV, the corresponding time-resolution is less than 2 μs. When the energy is 5 eV, the corresponding time-resolution can be as low as 800 ns. This is of utmost importance for the realization of high-resolution resonance spectroscopy. Moreover, the time-resolution and energy satisfy the power exponential relationship very well as shown in Fig. 3F, and the energy-resolution is flat (<3%), which is very beneficial for data analysis.

Epithermal Neutron Resonance Spectroscopy.

In order to achieve high-resolution epithermal neutron resonance spectroscopy, the technique of single-neutron counting ToF is adopted here, which avoids the reduction of measurement accuracy caused by the stacking of neutron signal pulses. The typical ToF spectrum is shown in Fig. 4A, where the starting point of the two pulses is the moment when the γ-ray/neutron reaches the detector respectively. The neutron kinetic energy En can be calculated based on the time difference between the two arrival moments Δt and the detection distance L, as En=12·mn·(LΔt+L/c)2, where mn is neutron mass, c is light speed. For the measurement of the neutron resonance spectrum, nearly 100,000 laser shots are accumulated at a repetition rate of 2 Hz in the experiment, and the total neutron source intensity is approximately 107 n/s in 4π sr. We conduct a statistical analysis of the arrival times of the collected neutrons. Owing to the relatively low count rate in the epithermal neutron energy range, a time bin of 3 μs is employed. The sample utilized in this experiment contains 115In and 109Ag nuclides, which exhibit prominent resonance peaks at 1.46 eV and 5.19 eV (36), respectively, as depicted in Fig. 4B. The resulting statistical ToF spectrum clearly demonstrates significant dips at the two corresponding positions, as illustrated by the blue solid line in Fig. 4C.

To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the epithermal neutron resonance spectroscopy and verify the experimental results, the Geant4 code is further employed to simulate it. The simulated spectrum, depicted by the red solid line in Fig. 4C, exhibits a high degree of agreement with the experimental measurement. By subtracting the simulated background from the measured spectrum, a clean neutron resonance spectrum can be obtained. Subsequently, the transmittance curve, shown as the blue solid line in Fig. 4D, can be derived. The transmittance curve can also be theoretically calculated using the formula T=e−Nσl, where N denotes the number density of target nuclei, σ represents the total nuclear reaction differential cross-section, and l is the thickness of the sample. When the experimental results are compared with the calculated transmittance, the positions of the two prominent resonance dips can be distinctly identified. Moreover, the shapes of these dips closely correspond to the theoretical calculations. However, there is a minor limitation. The dips at 3.82 eV and 5.19 eV cannot be clearly resolved, mainly because of the low count of measured neutrons.

Discussion

The terawatt femtosecond laser–driven electron accelerator has the characteristics of being table-top-sized and generating femtosecond pulsed electron beams (37–40), and these characteristics endow the epithermal neutron resonance spectroscopy facility with the advantages of compactness (∼10 m2), low cost (∼two million dollars), and high time-resolution. Certainly, a differential cavity can be used to improve the beam repetition rate to 100 Hz, which would increase the neutron source intensity to 109 n/s (41). Moreover, the laser-to-electron conversion efficiency of the LWFA process here is only approximately 0.8%, which results in a laser-to-neutron conversion efficiency of only ∼1 ×10−5-lower than the laser-to-neutron conversion efficiency of the best reported approach (2×10−4) (19). Further optimization is necessary to increase the laser-to-electron (and thus laser-to-neutron) conversion efficiency (42, 43). This facility has advantages in terms of convenience and great application value for the nondestructive testing of nuclear materials in special environments. For example, 1) performance evaluation of key core components of nuclear weapons, 2) high-precision measurement of nuclide abundances for calculating the burnup of nuclear reactors, and monitoring the activity of special radionuclides (e.g., α or β decay) of nuclear waste. What’s more, the neutron source is generated by the femtosecond electron beam irradiating a single converter and moderator. It has shorter pulse duration and an excellent power exponential relationship between time-resolution and energy. It overcomes the deficiencies of the spallation neutron source and plays an important role in promoting the acquisition of high-precision nuclear data.

Conclusion

We have successfully employed a compact TW-class femtosecond laser to drive the LWFA, and demonstrated an advancement in the application of LWFA-based neutrons. A 2-Hz 45 MeV quasi-mono-energetic electron beam was generated and subsequently utilized to produce photonuclear neutrons with a source intensity of ∼107 n/s in 4π. Then, these neutrons were moderated to increase the yield ∼4 times in the epithermal neutron energy range and applied to NRSA. This neutron source features a short pulse duration, with a time-resolution less than 0.8 μs at 5 eV. We have demonstrated at the proof-of-principle stage that the LWFA-based neutron source can be used to realize high-resolution resonance spectroscopy and distinctly identify the shape of resonance dips of 115In at 1.46 eV and 109Ag at 5.19eV. This compact facility holds great promise for on-site nuclear material analysis and high-precision nuclear data acquisition, promoting the development of neutron resonance spectroscopy in related fields.
