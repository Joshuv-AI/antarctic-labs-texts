The comprehensive oral microbiome landscape unveils its interplay with poor oral health in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma risk
Authors: Peipei Gao, Huangbo Yuan, Zhendong Mei, Xiaolin Yin, Yixin Zeng, Zhenqiu Liu, Xiaorong Yang, Jiangli Xue, Zidong Liu, Yanfeng Jiang, Weimin Ye, Ming Lu, Chen Suo, Xingdong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryGrowing evidence links poor oral health to an increased esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) risk, with the oral microbiome recognized as a key contributor. However, human-based evidence remains limited. Here, we analyze salivary shotgun metagenomic data from 390 ESCC case-control pairs and 16S rRNA sequencing data from 206 incident esophageal cancer (EC) case-control pairs. We identify 50 bacterial species altered in ESCC (e.g., enriched Porphyromonas catoniae and depleted Campylobacter rectus) and disruptions in 54 biochemical pathways (e.g., inosine 5′-phosphate degradation). These features potentially mediate the association between poor oral health and ESCC. Notably, this association is stronger among individuals with lower Streptococcus mitis levels, implicating pathways related to thiamine salvage and energy metabolism. Consistent findings in the validation dataset further support the interplay between the oral microbiome and oral health in EC risk. Our results highlight the promise of precision-targeted microbial interventions to improve oral health for ESCC prevention and management.Keywords: oral microbiome, oral health, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, mediation, classification

Introduction

Oral health problems affect nearly 3.5 billion people globally and remain a substantial public health challenge, especially in underdeveloped regions.1 Increasing evidence links poor oral health to a higher risk of esophageal cancer (EC),2,3,4 which comprises two major pathological subtypes—esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC)—together responsible for approximately 445,000 deaths annually.5 The oral microbiome has emerged as a key contributor to EC pathogenesis6,7,8; however, the precise mechanisms remain to be fully understood. East Asia, particularly China, bears the highest ESCC burden.5,7 In this high-risk region, investigating the interplay among oral health, the oral microbiome, and ESCC holds significant public health importance.

The oral cavity harbors the second-largest microbial community in the human body,8 encompassing bacteria, archaea, fungi, viruses, and phages that collectively help maintain oral homeostasis.8,9,10 Disruptions in this microbial ecosystem can lead to oral health diseases and may contribute to carcinogenesis by altering metabolic, inflammatory, and immune functions.11,12 An inflammation-associated microbial cluster known as the “red complex,” including Porphyromonas gingivalis, Tannerella forsythia, and Treponema denticola, has been related to an elevated EC risk.13,14 However, most existing EC-related oral microbiome studies relied on 16S rRNA gene sequencing with inferred functions.15,16 Emerging approaches, such as shotgun metagenomic sequencing, offer a more in-depth and precise view of the microbiome.8,10 As precision medicine advances, elucidating whether and how oral microbiome variations affect the relationship between oral health and EC becomes increasingly critical. This underscores the need for comprehensive human studies that integrate detailed microbial profiling with epidemiological data—an area still largely underexplored.

In this study, we analyzed integrated epidemiologic and oral microbiome data from a population-based case-control study in Taixing, China, a region with high EC risk and prevalent poor oral hygiene.7,17 By profiling a broad spectrum of oral microbial taxa (bacteria, archaea, viruses, and fungi) and their metabolic function signatures, we characterized the oral microbiome landscape and its interplay with oral health in relation to ESCC risk. We further assessed the potential mediating role of the oral microbiome in the relation between poor oral health and ESCC and validated key findings in the Taizhou Longitudinal Study (TZL).18,19

Results

Overview of oral health and the oral microbiome assayed in the study

In the Taixing case-control study, we included 390 pathologically confirmed ESCC cases and 390 controls with an average age of 66.4 years (standard deviation [SD] = 8.0, range from 40 to 85 years; 68.7% males) in this analysis (Figure 1A; Table S1). We found a positive relation between poor oral health and an elevated ESCC risk, consistent with our phase 1 study.7 Specifically, poor oral health indicators, including teeth lost after age 20, a high number of missing and filled teeth, teeth lost without denture use, infrequent tooth brushing, and a high oral health risk score (Figure S1), were significantly associated with a higher risk of ESCC (Table S2).

To investigate the role of the oral microbiome in the relationship between oral health and ESCC, we performed shotgun metagenomic sequencing on saliva samples from all 780 participants (STAR Methods, Figures 1A and S2). Consistent with previous studies using 16S rRNA sequencing,20,21 the oral bacterial variation is primarily driven by a trade-off between Bacteroidetes (e.g., Prevotella intermedia) and Firmicutes (e.g., Rothia mucilaginosa and Streptococcus mitis; Figures S3 and 1B). The leading phyla in archaea, viruses, and fungi were Euryarchaeota, Uroviricota, and Ascomycota, respectively (Figure S4A), which generally aligned with those observed in the gut microbiome.22 Among the 69 bacterial genera included, Neisseria23,24,25 and Prevotella were the most abundant (Figure S4B). Predominant genera in archaea, viruses, and fungi included Methanobrevibacter, Valbvirus, and Fusarium, respectively, with archaea exhibiting greater diversity. We observed that the most abundant bacterial species, such as N. flavescens, R. mucilaginosa, and S. mitis, were not only prevalent in other Asian countries (Japan26) but also in the US Human Microbiome Project27 and European countries (Ireland28), indicating that certain salivary microbes may be shared across diverse populations.26

A total of 231 bacterial, 381 archaeal, 316 viral, and 83 fungal species, as well as 203 biochemical pathways and 823 enzymes, were retained after quality filtering (STAR Methods). The overall demographics of the oral microbiome revealed a clear separation between kingdoms (Figure 1C), demonstrating their pronounced territory specificity. Distinct ecological characteristics across the four kingdoms were observed (Figures 1D and S5A): archaea had the highest richness and evenness, while fungi showed a skewed distribution with high evenness but the lowest richness, and viruses exhibited the lowest evenness, indicating their essential complexity. Employing permutational multivariable analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) to determine the percentage of variance in the oral microbiome explained by oral health indicators and covariates, we found that oral health was a major contributor to the variation of the overall oral microbial community structure (Figures 1E and S5B; Table S3), especially the number of missing and filled teeth and the composite oral health risk score (PERMANOVA-p < 0.05).

Oral microbial features differentially abundant in ESCC

The alpha diversity of the Chao1 index was significantly reduced in ESCC cases compared to controls across bacteria, archaea, and viruses, showing lower species richness in these communities (Figures 2A and S5A, p < 0.05). This aligns with our previous study using 16S rRNA sequencing data.29 In contrast, the Pielou and Simpson indices were elevated in ESCC for bacteria, suggesting its increased species evenness. The overall community structure of bacteria showed a significant association with ESCC based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity (PERMANOVA-p = 0.003, Figures 2B and S5B) and on weighted and unweighted UniFrac dissimilarity metrics and the Barnes-Hut t-SNE method (Figure S5C, all p values < 0.05). Per-feature analysis identified 50 bacterial, 7 archaeal, 3 viral, and 2 fungal species alterations in ESCC cases (false discovery rate-adjusted p value [q] < 0.05, Figures 2C, 2D, and S6; Tables S4, S5, S6, and S7). Among these, 19 bacterial species were more abundant in ESCC. Although no significant changes were observed in the red complex pathogen P. gingivalis, a borderline significant enrichment in T. denticola (q = 0.052) and two other periodontal pathogens (q < 0.05), P. catoniae and T. sp oral taxon HOT 286,30 was identified in ESCC cases. We previously reported elevated Prevotella and decreased Neisseria, Actinomyces, Campylobacter, and Lautropia in ESCC.29 The current analysis corroborated these findings and further revealed significant increases in specific Prevotella species (P. oulorum, P. dentalis, P. oris, and P. sp oral taxon 473)—members of the orange pathogen complex associated with periodontal disease, exhibiting lower pathogenicity than the red complex,30,31 and known to be enriched in both adult32 and pediatric dental caries33—alongside reduced abundances of Neisseria (e.g., N. flavescens, N. elongata, and N. subflava), Actinomyces (e.g., A. johnsonii and A. naeslundii), Campylobacter (C. rectus and C. gracilis), and Lautropia (L. mirabilis). When controls were further matched based on oral health status, these alterations largely remained consistent, with the results highly correlated with the original main analysis (rho = 0.965, Figure S7). In the remaining kingdoms, most differential features were reduced in ESCC (q < 0.05, Figure S8), including archaea (Thermoplasma acidophilum), viruses (Rimavirus drgrey), and fungi (Aspergillus flavus). All these associations remain highly concordant after further adjustment for several other potential confounding factors including (1) fruit intake, pickled vegetable consumption, and Helicobacter pylori infection, as well as (2) medication use and (3) comorbidities (Figure S9).

In line with the hypothesis of metabolic reprogramming and inflammation driven by microbial dysbiosis in cancer, we observed significant alterations in 54 MetaCyc pathways in ESCC (q < 0.05, Figures 2E and S10; Table S8). These included widespread alterations in metabolism and amino acid biosynthesis pathways, along with specific increased levels in pathways related to carbohydrate biosynthesis, cofactor biosynthesis, and genetic information processing in the case group. Methionine metabolism is known to modulate the tumor microenvironment by regulating antioxidant defenses, formate overflow, and methylation reactions34,35; uric acid, acting as a systemic antioxidant but with pro-inflammatory properties, has been implicated in cancer pathogenesis.36 Correspondingly, several pathways involved in L-methionine biosynthesis IV (PWY-7977) and uric acid synthesis (PWY-5695: inosine 5′-phosphate degradation and PWY-6353: purine nucleotide degradation II) were elevated in ESCC (q < 0.05). Moreover, previous studies have documented metabolic reprogramming of urea metabolism in tumors—suggesting that reduced urea levels may influence cancer risk37,38,39—while in the oral cavity, the hydrolysis of urea and arginine metabolism are primary routes for alkali generation in dental plaque.40 In our analysis, pathways involved in urea metabolism, such as L-arginine biosynthesis (ARGSYN-PWY and PWY-5154) and L-arginine degradation XIII (PWY-8187), were reduced in ESCC compared to controls. We also observed that altered expression of glycolysis and fermentation processes occurred in ESCC cases, which reinforces the dominant state of reprogrammed energy metabolism in cancer.41 Furthermore, several known cancer-related pathways are uniquely associated with ESCC (Figure S10B), such as the glyoxylate cycle (GLYOXYLATE-BYPASS)42 and isoprene biosynthesis (PWY-6270).43,44

Co-abundance correlations among ESCC-related oral microbial profiles

The ESCC-depleted feature network, encompassing 99 species and 2,419 significant correlations (Figure 3A; Table S9), is more complex than the ESCC-enriched network, which includes 40 species and 506 correlations. Both intra- and inter-kingdom associations are predominantly positive, reflecting a cooperative dynamic within these communities. Inter-kingdom interactions primarily occur between bacteria and archaea, while the strongest associations remain within the bacterial community. For instance, within the ESCC-depleted taxa, species such as N. flavescens, C. rectus, and Eubacterium spp. exhibit robust connections, and in the ESCC-enriched taxa, species like Prevotella spp., Capnocytophaga spp., P. somerae, and Fusobacterium periodonticum dominate the network’s centrality. We noticed that P. somerae and T. denticola are positively correlated with several other potential periodontopathogenic species, including F. periodonticum, P. intermedia, P. marshii, and P. shahii. Additionally, certain features, such as the archaeon Methanosarcina sp. WH1, defy these ecological boundaries by maintaining significant relations with bacteria like E. brachy in the ESCC-depleted network. Although infrequent, cross-kingdom interactions may collectively contribute to ESCC risk, indicating that taxonomic boundaries do not preclude ecologically relevant interplay.10

Oral microbial features improve the classification of ESCC

To explore whether the oral microbiome offers additional value in identifying ESCC, we constructed XGBoost classification models incorporating oral microbial features and traditional risk factors (Figure 3B). While tobacco use, alcohol consumption, and poor oral hygiene are well-established ESCC risk factors,45 models incorporating oral microbial features outperformed that based solely on traditional factors. Specifically, the model including oral bacterial taxa alone (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUROC]: 0.740, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.706–0.774), the combined model of traditional factors with oral bacteria (AUROC: 0.741, 95% CI: 0.707–0.776), as well as the combined model of traditional factors with features from multi-kingdoms (AUROC: 0.744, 95% CI: 0.710–0.778), significantly exceeded the performance of the traditional factor model (AUROC: 0.639, 95% CI: 0.600–0.678). Notably, A. sp oral taxon 414 demonstrated strong performance in both the XGBoost model and multivariable regression model (Figure 3B). We also developed classification models incorporating distinct numbers of bacterial features (STAR Methods), including those incorporating 90 bacterial features potentially related to ESCC (q < 0.15) and all available bacteria. All these models revealed that combining bacterial features markedly enhances the ability to classify ESCC (Figure S11).

Oral microbiome covariates with oral health status

We also identified a series of oral microbial features associated with oral health measures, including 79 bacterial, 3 archaeal, 13 viral, and 4 fungal species (q < 0.05, Figures 2C, 2D, and S6A). For example, the abundance of several potential pathogen complexes, e.g., A. segnis, P. sp oral taxon 278, and P. intermedia, was positively correlated with lower tooth brushing frequency, while Campylobacter spp. and Eubacterium spp. showed negative relations with higher oral health risk score. Among the individual components of oral health risk score, missing and filled teeth showed the strongest association with the oral microbiome. Tooth lost indicators (missing and filled teeth) showed consistent direction for alterations in the oral microbiota, such as the reduced anaerobic species A. tannerae. Tooth loss and hygiene practice (tooth brushing) appeared to show distinct associations with the oral microbiome, especially in bacteria and viruses (Tables S4, S5, and S6; Figures S6A and S8), suggesting intricate communications among the oral microbiome and oral health. Even after accounting for other potential confounders, including fruit intake, pickled vegetable consumption, H. pylori infection, medication use, and comorbidities, all of the associations remained highly consistent (Figure S9B). When the analysis was limited to healthy controls (Figure S6B), the association between poor oral health and the oral microbiome remained largely in line with the findings from the primary analysis. Notably, several microbial features were related to both poor oral health and ESCC (Figure 2D). For instance, C. rectus abundance was lower in ESCC cases and decreased with poor oral health (q < 0.01), while A. tannerae was positively associated with ESCC but correlated with better oral health (q < 0.05). Nevertheless, for most microbial taxa potentially related to both ESCC and poor oral health, the association direction was consistent.

In pathways related to poor oral health (Figure S10), elevated L-arginine synthesis (PWY-5154) and reduced L-arginine degradation (PWY-8187) align with tumor cells being auxotrophic for arginine and reliant on external supply.46,47 Certain oxidative stress functions,48,49 such as tetrapyrrole biosynthesis (PWY-5188) and fatty acid oxidation (PWY-5136), are specifically linked to poor oral health. A few metabolic pathways—such as PWY-8187 (L-arginine degradation XIII) and PWY-7118 (chitin deacetylation)—exhibited consistent directions of association with both poor oral health and ESCC. In contrast, several other pathways (e.g., DAPLYSINESYN-PWY: L-lysine biosynthesis I and TRNA-CHARGING-PWY: tRNA charging) showed opposite association directions with poor oral health and ESCC. We noticed that these pathways were predominantly driven by N. flavescens and P. melaninogenica, both of which also exhibited inverse association patterns with ESCC and poor oral health. This suggests that microbial functions are not intrinsically fixed but could be dynamically shaped by the host microenvironment.50,51,52 The context-dependent behavior of the oral microbiome implies its diverse contributions to carcinogenesis.

Oral microbes potentially mediate the association between oral health and ESCC

To further understand the complex interplay between oral microbiome, oral health, and ESCC, we performed mediation analyses to assess the potential mediating role of the oral microbiome (STAR Methods). We observed 66 significant mediation effects for bacterial features (average causal mediation effect [ACME]-p < 0.05, Figure 4A; Table S10). Among them, C. rectus (mediation proportion: 45.0%) and E. brachy (mediation proportion: 41.8%) exhibited the strongest mediation effects in the association between the oral health risk score and ESCC (Figure 4B, p < 0.001). A. cardiffensis and A. johnsonii significantly mediated the association of missing and filled teeth, and oral health risk score, with ESCC (mediation proportions: 18.1%–25.4%). The differential species of T. sp oral taxon HOT 286 mediated 18.4% of the relation between denture wearing and ESCC. At the functional level, metabolic pathways such as peptidoglycan biosynthesis (PWY-6470), an inflammation- and immune evasion-related pathway,53 also played a significant mediating role, accounting for 10.9% of the oral health risk score-ESCC association (p = 0.024). Collectively, these findings suggest the potential role of the oral microbiome in linking poor oral health and ESCC risk, though causality cannot be inferred from this observational study.

Oral microbiome interacts with oral health in relation to ESCC

We then explored whether and how the oral microbiome modulates the association between oral health and ESCC, focusing on whether this association varies by oral microbiome profiles. We initially carried out hypothesis generation by summarizing the overall oral bacterial structure using principal coordinates (PCo) loading scores and testing their potential interactions with oral health risk score in a multivariable-adjusted logistic regression model. The positive association between oral health risk score and ESCC was significantly stratified by PCo2, both when modeled as a continuous (p interaction = 0.009, Figure 5A) and a binary term (p interaction = 0.004, Figure 5B). Similarly, the association of missing and filled teeth (p interaction = 0.003) and teeth lost (p interaction = 0.056) with ESCC varied by PCo2 (Figure S12A; Table S11).

We further examined potential interaction effects in the top nine most abundant bacterial species, which together account for 43.3% of the overall community. Among these, only S. mitis consistently showed significant interactions with poor oral health (Table S12). This common oral commensal bacterium, comprising 4.2% of the community and present in 99.6% of study participants, is also highly prevalent worldwide.26,54,55 It showed a negative correlation with PCo2 (Spearman correlation = −0.44, Figure S13A). Previous studies have linked this bacterium with the risk of oral squamous cell carcinoma56,57 and EC.58 In our study, S. mitis showed a trend toward positive association with both poor oral health and ESCC (Figures S6A, S13B, and S13C). Moreover, the associations between ESCC and oral health indicators, including oral health risk score (p interaction = 0.008), missing and filled teeth (p interaction = 0.009), and tooth brushing frequency (borderline significance, p interaction = 0.180), were more pronounced in participants with low S. mitis levels (Figures 5C and S12B).

To explore functions underlying the interaction between S. mitis and oral health, we focused on 25 biochemical pathways predominantly contributed by S. mitis after quality control filtering (Figure 5D and STAR Methods, among the top three contributors). Of these, 11 pathways showed significant interactions with oral health risk score (q interaction < 0.05, Figure 5E). These included 4 involved in energy or molecular metabolism, 2 in genetic information processing, and 5 in metabolite biosynthesis. For example, the association of oral health risk score with ESCC was strengthened in participants with lower levels of D-galactose degradation I pathway (PWY-6317, q interaction = 0.027, Table S13) and guanosine nucleotide de novo biosynthesis II (PWY-6125, q interaction = 0.013). A similar pattern was observed for thiamine salvage IV (PWY-7356, q interaction = 0.013), which recycles thiamine (vitamin B1) and has been implicated in cancer development.59 Notably, 10 of the 11 pathways contained at least one enzyme where S. mitis was a major contributor (among top three contributors), and these enzymes also showed significant interactions with oral health risk score (p interaction < 0.05, Figure 5F; Table S14). Examples include the UDP-glucose-hexose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase (Enzyme Commission nomenclature number, EC2.7.7.12) in D-galactose degradation I, nucleoside diphosphate kinase (EC2.7.4.6) in guanosine nucleotide de novo biosynthesis II, and both aminopyrimidine aminohydrolase (EC3.5.99.2) and hydroxyethylthiazole kinase (EC2.7.1.50) in thiamine diphosphate salvage IV. These findings suggest a potentially protective role of S. mitis in modulating biochemical pathways that could mitigate the risk of ESCC in the context of poor oral health, which highlights the potential of microbial contributions as targets for therapeutic intervention.

Validation of oral microbiome’s potential mediation role in a prospective cohort

The TZL is a large prospective cohort study. We analyzed the oral microbiome of 206 newly diagnosed EC cases (median follow-up, 3.9 years) and 206 matched healthy controls (median follow-up, 7.9 years) within this cohort (Figure 6A). At the time of saliva sample collection, participants had a mean age of 59.1 years (SD = 6.5; Table S1). Poor oral health was significantly associated with an increased risk of incident EC (Table S2). We identified several microbial features associated with EC—overlaid onto the same species or genera that were significantly related to ESCC in the main analysis of the Taixing study. Specifically, EC cases showed enrichment of species from Prevotella and Oribacterium and depletion of species from Neisseria, Aggregatibacter, Granulicatella, and Haemophilus (Figure 6B, p < 0.05) at baseline. Consistent with the Taixing case-control findings, we observed significant mediation effects involving the oral microbiome (Figure 6C). For example, S. salivarius significantly mediated the association between the oral health risk score and EC (mediation proportion: 5.9%, p = 0.026). Similarly, P. oulorum mediated the association of missing and filled teeth with EC (mediation proportion: 13.5%, p = 0.016). Furthermore, S. mitis was among the top three most abundant species and showed positive association with poor oral health (Figure 6D). The association between poor oral health and EC tended to be stratified by S. mitis levels (Figure 6D, p interaction = 0.065), with a stronger association observed among individuals with lower S. mitis abundance. These findings in this prospective data support a potential mediating and modulating effect of the oral microbiome in the association between poor oral health and EC.

Discussion

The relation between poor oral health and EC risk is well established, yet the mechanisms and individual variability remain unclear. In the large-scale population study of its kind, we systematically investigated the interplay among oral health, oral microbiome, and ESCC. By profiling multi-kingdom microbial features, we identified a range of microorganisms associated with ESCC, including implicated periodontal pathogens P. catoniae, T. sp oral taxon HOT 286, and Prevotella spp. Our results corroborated the oral microbiome’s potential mediating role in the association and extended this by showing how the microbiome stratified this association—specifically, the enhanced association in a certain subgroup could be attributed to lower levels of S. mitis, which carries biochemical pathways involved in molecular and energy metabolism. The consistent findings in the prospective TZL cohort further highlight the oral microbiome as an important factor involved in the relation between poor oral health and EC.

Over the past decades, periodontal pathogens have been implicated in carcinogenesis.60,61 Petrick et al. found little prospective association between red complex pathogens and pancreatic cancer risk in African Americans.61 In contrast, Peters et al. observed elevated T. forsythia levels in EAC and a marginal association between P. gingivalis and ESCC.13 Similarly, Kawasaki et al. found higher T. forsythia abundance in the dental plaque of individuals with EC.62 In our study, over 82% of participants carried red complex pathogens, and more than half had four or more missing and filled teeth. T. denticola showed a borderline positive association with ESCC, and we also identified elevated levels of emerging pathogens, such as P. catoniae and T. sp oral taxon HOT 286, along with several orange complex species. The high burden of dental caries and periodontal pathogens in this population may obscure distinctions in caries-related profiles between cancer cases and controls. On the other hand, population heterogeneity, tumor histology, and sample type variability may lead to inconsistent findings. Nonetheless, these results support the contribution of disrupted oral microbial homeostasis in ESCC risk.13,25

Manghi et al. have proposed oral microbial load as a surrogate for oral health.63 Among the oral health indicators of our analysis, missing and filled teeth exhibited the strongest association with the oral microbiome. Tooth loss and hygiene practices tended to show different relations with periodontal pathogens, implying distinct biological mechanisms. Dental fillings may alter tooth surface morphology to improve cleanliness and reduce pathogen adhesion, whereas infrequent tooth brushing promotes plaque and biofilm accumulation, favoring pathogen growth.64,65 While the oral cavity’s complex microbial ecosystem complicates causal inference,66 our mediation analysis offers insight by identifying specific microbes that link tooth loss and hygiene behaviors to EC risk. Different microorganisms appear to mediate certain associations, reinforcing the hypothesis that microbial signatures contribute to the biological pathways connecting oral health and EC.

Our findings revealed significant interactions between oral health and the oral microbiome, with poor oral health-ESCC association varying by oral microbial profiles, particularly S. mitis levels. As a commensal and opportunistic pathogen, S. mitis produces short-chain fatty acids and hydrogen peroxide that inhibit pathogens, yet its pro-inflammatory activity has been linked to carcinogenesis.6,67,68 Its acid resistance enables it to survive esophageal transit and reach the stomach via the enteral route,69,70 and it can also disseminate hematogenously.70 Prior studies have identified oral microbes within gastrointestinal tumor tissues, providing evidence for their involvement in these cancers.71,72 This direct contact supports the potential role of oral microbes in modulating the carcinogenic effects of poor oral health. The metabolic activities of S. mitis and its synthesized metabolites may contribute to its potential modulation effect. It can also enhance the acid tolerance of certain microorganisms by increasing their cytoplasmic pH, aiding their survival in acidic environments.40 Additionally, microbial communication, such as signaling transduction and niche competition,8,10 may reduce harmful bacteria in the presence of abundant S. mitis, potentially mitigating cancer risk. Further research is needed to clarify the mechanisms of the interplays and assess how modulating microbial profiles can improve oral health and lower EC risk.

In summary, this study provides a systematic multi-kingdom characterization of the oral microbiome in a high-risk population. The results highlight the potential mediating role of the oral microbiome in the association between poor oral health and EC and suggest that personalized modulation of the oral microbiome may offer a strategy to reduce cancer risk. Future large-scale longitudinal and interventional studies are warranted to evaluate beneficial microbial interventions, along with detailed mechanistic investigations—including microbiome co-culture and profiling of microbial metabolites within tumor tissue—to advance the understanding of potentially carcinogenic mechanisms involving the oral microbiome.

Limitations of the study

Certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, the observational case-control design limits causal inference, and reverse causation cannot be fully ruled out. It is also possible that the oral microbiome may act as a common cause of both poor oral health and EC, rather than a mediator. Nonetheless, consistent prospective association and significant mediation effects observed in the prospective validation dataset help mitigate this concern. Second, despite efforts to minimize bias, such as standardized saliva collection and adjustment for key confounders, residual confounding (e.g., nutrition intake) cannot be excluded. Lastly, the microbiome classifier needs external validation, and the generalizability of findings to other populations remains uncertain.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study design and participants

We conducted a population-based case-control study in Taixing, a high-risk region of esophageal cancer (EC), China, which has been detailed previously.80,81 In brief, we attempted to enroll all newly diagnosed EC cases between October 2010 and September 2013 from four major local hospitals, covering nearly 90% of clinical diagnoses of EC cases. Suspected EC cases during the endoscopic examination were asked to complete a questionnaire administered by trained interviewers. The histopathologically confirmed EC cases were included in the study; additional cases missed by endoscopy units were supplemented annually through the local Cancer Registry. Overall, we identified 1401 suspected cases from hospitals and added 280 cases from the Cancer Registry. After review of the pathological sections by the study pathologist and reassessment of the surgical pathological reports for cases without pathological sections, 1,499 EC cases (1,418 cases of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [ESCC] and 81 cases of non-ESCC) were confirmed (78.3% based on the estimated number from the local Cancer Registry). The controls were randomly selected from the same study region using frequency-matching by sex and 5-year age groups, resulting in 2,011 eligible controls (70.4% participation rate).80 After excluding those outside the age range (40–85 years), 1,992 controls were finally included. After excluding those who have incomplete information on the main exposure of oral health variables, evaluations of the association between oral health and the risk of ESCC included 1372 ESCC cases and 1972 controls. The metagenome analysis in this study investigated the interplay among oral health, oral microbiota, and ESCC risk. Sample size calculations were performed using MedCalc (version 22), with an alpha level of 0.05, a beta level of 0.1 (90% power), a null hypothesis value of 0.5, and an effect size range from 2.0 to 5.147,25, which yields a required sample size of 27–145 participants for each group. We included 780 participants who provided oral saliva samples—390 with ESCC and 390 matched controls (by age and sex), meeting the sample size to achieve the required statistical power.

The validation cohort was derived from a sub-cohort nested in the Taizhou Longitudinal Study (TZL), a large-scale prospective cohort study initiated in Taizhou, Jiangsu Province, China.18,19 The study population included residents aged 30 to 80 years. Eligible individuals were invited to participate through formal invitation letters, supported by government-led publicity and health promotion campaigns. A Regional Coordinating Center, along with field survey teams with trained staff, was established to conduct the baseline survey. Participants have been continuously followed for cancer incidence via systematic linkages to local cancer registries and health insurance databases. Baseline exposure information was obtained through structured questionnaires and standardized physical examinations. Biological specimens, including saliva and other samples, were collected. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment in the TZL. For the current study, we included 206 EC cases (>90% are ESCC cases), diagnosed more than six months after baseline, and 206 matched (age and sex) healthy controls to serve as the validation cohort.

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Fudan University (number: FE20020). All participants provided written informed consent before the interview and sample collection.

Method details

Oral health and covariable measurements

All subjects underwent face-to-face interviews by trained interviewers using a standardized questionnaire, which collected basic information on age, sex, family history of esophageal cancer, body mass index (BMI), family wealth, smoking, alcohol consumption, and tea drinking. BMI was calculated as each participant’s weight in kilograms divided by their height in meters squared, and was categorized based on Chinese standards for BMI (<18.5 kg/m2, 18.5–24.0 kg/m2, ≥24.0 kg/m2). In Taixing case-control study, smoking status was classified into three categories: never smoked, ≤30 pack-years, and >30 pack-years, determined by each participant’s lifetime cumulative tobacco use. Alcohol consumption was categorized as never, ≤82.5 g/day, and >82.5 g/day, based on the average daily ethanol intake. Tea drinking was divided into never and ever (defined as having consumed tea for at least six months and at least once daily). The family wealth score was derived from the ownership of valuable household items via multiple correspondence analysis.82 In the TZL, family wealth information (monthly income, CNY) was grouped into four categories: 0–10,000; 10,000–20,000; 20,000–30,000; 30,000+. Smoking, alcohol consumption, and tea drinking status were categorized as never and ever.

The trained interviewers conducted oral cavity examinations and collected information on oral health including teeth lost, the age of first teeth lost in adulthood, the number of missing and filled teeth, denture wearing and the frequency of tooth brushing. We built an oral health risk score for each participant based on four oral health factors including tooth loss indicators: teeth lost (no, yes) and the number of missing and filled teeth (continuous), and oral hygiene practices: denture wearing (No teeth loss, teeth loss and denture wearing, teeth loss and no denture wearing), and frequency of tooth brushing (≥2, ≤1 time/day), using multiple factor analysis (MFA)83 with the “FactoMineR” and “factoextra” packages. The first three dimensions, explaining 93.69% of the variance, were used to construct the score with a weighted approach. This composite score effectively captures the information of its individual components (Figure S1). Subjects were categorized into three groups based on the tertiles of the risk score: low, medium, and high, with a primary focus on comparing the high versus low score groups. The oral health risk in the TZL was computed using the same method as the above.

Saliva sample collection

About 2–3 mL of saliva was collected from each participant following overnight fasting, as described previously.29 Saliva collection took place prior to the antitumor treatment of cases, and for both cases and controls, no prescription of antibiotics was reported within one month prior to the interview. The saliva sample was mixed with 2 mL lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 50 mM EDTA, 50 mM sucrose, 100mM NaCl, and 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate); the mixture was transported to the laboratory the same day of collection and stored in the −80°C freezers.

Metagenomic sequencing and profiling in the Taixing study

In the Taixing case-control study, shotgun metagenomic sequencing was conducted. Salivary DNA was extracted using standard protocols and quantified using the Qubit DNA Assay Kit in Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA). Sequencing libraries (paired-end 150bp, insert size: 350 bp) were generated using the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Sequencing reads underwent quality control using the KneadData 0.7.10 pipeline (http://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/kneaddata) with the human reference genome (hg37decv0.1) to remove low-quality read bases and reads of human origin. After quality control, the average read depth was 13.0 million (SD: 10.1 million) per sample. Taxonomic profiling was conducted with MetaPhlAn76 (v3.1.0) for bacteria based on approximately 1.1 million clade-specific marker genes derived from ∼90,000 bacterial genomes, and Kraken284,85 (v2.1.2) for archaea, viruses and fungi, based on a standard Kraken2 database from 517 archaeal, 12,141 viral, and 97 fungal reference genomes obtained from the NCBI RefSeq (by August 2023). Kraken2 classified reads by mapping k-mers to the lowest common ancestor of matching reference genomes, and Bracken (v2.8.0) was applied to quantify taxon abundance from Kraken2 profiles. Functional profiling was performed by using HUMAnN 3.1.1.76 Reads are recruited to sample-specific pangenomes using Bowtie2 (version 2.2.3),74 including all gene families in the identified microbes. Unmapped reads are aligned against UniRef9086 by applying DIAMOND translated search.87 Gene family hits are counted and normalized for length and alignment quality. The resulting UniRef90 abundances, from both the nucleotide and protein levels, were then mapped to level 4 Enzyme Commission nomenclature88 and combined into structured pathways from MetaCyc.89 We applied the MMUPHin77 approach to correct for batch effects. The permutational multivariable analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) showed that the variance explained by the batches was only 0.09% (Figure S2, p = 0.999) after correction. Taxonomic profiling generated 508 bacterial, 396 archaeal, 1,730 viral, and 83 fungal species, while functional profiling identified 500 MetaCyc pathways and 2,569 enzymes.

16S rRNA gene sequencing and profiling in the TZL cohort

In the TZL study, 16S rRNA gene sequencing targeting the V3–V4 region was performed using primers 341F (5′-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′) and 805R (5′-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′) under standard PCR conditions. Amplicons were purified with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter Genomics, Danvers, MA, USA), quantified using the Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA), and assessed with the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, USA) and KAPA LTP library preparation kit (Kapa Biosystems, Woburn, MA, USA). Libraries that passed quality control were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq PE250 platform. Raw sequencing reads were first demultiplexed based on unique barcode sequences to separate data by sample. Barcode and primer sequences were then trimmed from the reads. Paired-end reads were subsequently merged using FLASH (v1.2.8) based on their overlapping regions. Quality filtering was conducted using fqtrim (v0.94), and chimeric sequences were identified and removed with Vsearch (v2.3.4), resulting in a set of high-quality, non-chimeric reads. Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) were inferred using DADA2.78 Sequencing reads were first quality-filtered using the “filterAndTrim” function. Error rates were then estimated from a subset of the filtered reads, with error models learned separately for each sequencing run to account for potential run-specific variability. Following error learning, reads were dereplicated, and amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated. Taxonomic classification was performed using the SILVA90 (release 138) and NCBI nucleotide-16S91 reference databases for microbial taxa annotation.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

Unconditional logistic regression models were used to derive odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of each oral health indicator for the risk of ESCC, after adjusting for age (continuous), sex, family history of EC, family wealth score, BMI 10 years ago, smoking, alcohol drinking, and tea drinking (Table S1).

All microbiome data were scaled to the relative abundance. We applied total sum scaling combined with the arcsine square root transformation to the relative abundance of microbial features in the per-feature association and mediation analyses. The distribution of the oral microbial community was firstly visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP).79 Alpha diversity metrics at the species level, including Chao1 index, Pielou index, Shannon index, and Simpson index, were calculated and were compared across outcome groups using a logistic regression model with the same covariate adjustments as mentioned above. We computed the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity metric for each sample and conducted principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) to assess variations in species composition. To determine the percentage of variance in each microbial kingdom explained by oral health indicators, we applied PERMANOVA with the “adonis2” function from the R package “vegan” (version 2.6.4) in the control group with the same covariate adjustments as mentioned above. We then conducted the PERMANOVA analysis in all samples (cases and controls) to confirm the overall configuration of oral microbiome associated with oral health and ESCC status. All PERMANOVA tests were two-sided (p < 0.05, indicated by asterisks). We also evaluated the association between the oral bacterial community and ESCC, after multivariable adjustment, using unweighted and weighted UniFrac dissimilarity metrics (with the PERMANOVA test) and the Barnes-Hut implementation of t-SNE for dimensionality reduction (with the multivariable analysis of variance [MANOVA] test).

Association analysis

For per-feature tests, the taxonomic features were required to have a minimum relative abundance of 0.01% in at least 10% of the samples, and the pathways and enzymes were required to have a minimum relative abundance of 0.001% in at least 10% of the samples. In addition to the filters of minimum abundance and prevalence, functional features were filtered using a greedy correlation-based method (Spearman correlation <0.9), retaining the most abundant feature from each correlated group as the representative.92,93 This filtering yielded 231 bacterial, 381 archaeal, 318 viral, and 83 fungal species, 203 pathways, and 823 enzymes. We conducted per-feature analyses using the linear regression models in the MaAsLin 2,75 simultaneously adjusting for age, sex, family history of EC, family wealth score, BMI of 10 years ago, smoking, alcohol drinking, and tea drinking. In the per-feature tests examining the association between the oral microbiome and oral health indicators, models were further adjusted for the ESCC group; when examining the association between microbiome and ESCC, models were further adjusted for oral health risk score. As the sensitivity analysis, regression models were further adjusted for: (1) fruit intake, pickled vegetable consumption, and Helicobacter pylori infection; (2) past antibiotic, antihypertensive, and antidiabetic medication use; (3) comorbidities, including coronary heart disease and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, to exclude their potential impact. In another sensitivity analysis, we evaluated the association between oral health in controls and compared the results with the main analysis that in all participants. In the validation data, we focused on the taxa that were significantly associated with ESCC in the main analysis of the Taixing case-control study, including those overlaid into the same genera (n = 39) or species (n = 98).

Correlation network analysis

To explore potential correlation patterns among ESCC-related microbial features, we selected species with a q-value <0.25 from the association results (96 bacterial, 46 archaeal, 6 viral, and 2 fungal species) from MaAsLin 2 output and performed co-abundance correlation analysis using the SparCC method. Microbial relative abundance data were scaled by 1,000,000 and rounded to resemble count data. A permutation test was used to assess the statistical significance of the correlations. For each pair, the SparCC correlation coefficients were randomly permuted and recalculated 1,000 times to establish a null distribution. Significance was determined by comparing the observed coefficients to this distribution, with coefficients deemed nonsignificant if they appeared more extreme in any permutation. The final report included results with q-values below 0.05 and coefficients at least 0.25. The inter-kingdom microbiome correlation network was visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.10).

Mediation analysis

A mediation analysis was conducted to investigate the potential influence of poor oral health on ESCC risk through oral microbiome. The associations between oral health and ESCC (Direct effect), as well as the oral microbiome, were initially evaluated in the “Association analysis” section. Only significant associations in the per-feature tests were considered for subsequent mediation analysis. We focused on oral bacterial taxa and pathway profiles in the mediation analysis, as they accounted for the majority of the results of interest. The linear regression and logistic regression model from the R package “mediation” was employed for the mediation analysis. Ultimately, pairs with significant Average Causal Mediation Effects (ACME, p-value <0.05) were reported, representing the oral health indicators’ association with ESCC risk potentially mediated by the oral microbiome. The circle plot was generated using the R package “circlize” (v.0.4.15).

Interaction analysis

To evaluate the potential modification effects of oral microbes in the association of poor oral health and ESCC risk, we first conducted hypothesis generation to examine interactions between poor oral health and overall oral microbial community structure (defined as the first two principal coordinates, PCo1 and PCo2) with ESCC risk. Our models included the main effects of oral health indicators and PCo1 or PCo2, along with their interaction terms and covariates the same as association analysis. Out of the 231 microbial species meeting quality control standards, the nine most abundant species represented 43.3% of the total community abundance. In addition to identifying significant interactions between oral health and PCo1 or PCo2, we extended our analysis to examine the interactions between oral health and the median relative abundance of the top nine individual microbial species. Using the median abundance level of each species as a cutoff, we dichotomized the samples and assessed their interactions with oral health indicators on ESCC risk. After examining the interaction effect of S. mitis with oral health, we further investigated the biochemical pathways and enzymes mainly contributed by S. mitis (among the top three contributors) to elucidate the mechanisms through which it modulates the association between poor oral health and ESCC risk. For all interaction analysis, unless otherwise specified, we used median values for dichotomous grouping.

Classification model

To assess whether incorporating oral microbial features improves ESCC classification, we constructed classification models based on the eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) method,94 which is under the gradient boosting framework, using 5-fold cross-validation algorithm. We compared the classification performance of four models: (1) a basic model including traditional risk factors, namely age, sex, family history of EC, BMI of 10 years ago, family wealth score, smoking, alcohol drinking, tea drinking, and oral health risk score; (2) a model including the significant bacterial taxa only; (3) a model that incorporated traditional risk factors with bacterial taxa; (4) a model incorporating traditional risk factors with all four kingdom profiles, including bacteria, archaea, viruses, and fungi. In addition, other models that incorporated different numbers of bacterial profiles, including all available bacteria, and 90 statistically significant bacterial profiles associated with ESCC (q < 0.15), were also developed. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Differences between model performance were assessed using the Delong test, implemented via the “roc.test” function in the R package “pROC” (v.1.18.0).

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (v.4.2.3) and Python (v.3.10). All the statistical tests were two-sided. In the high-dimensional microbiome analysis of the Taixing case-control study, a q-value <0.05 (indicated by asterisks) was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified. For the validation dataset, where a subset of taxa was analyzed, significance was defined as a p-value <0.05. Boxplots, violin and scatterplots were plotted using the R packages “ggpubr” (v.0.6.0) and “ggplot2” (v.3.5.1). The circular phylogenetic tree was produced by “GraPhlAn” (v.1.1.3).
