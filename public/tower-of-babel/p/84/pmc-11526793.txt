Toward a Circular Lithium Economy with Electrodialysis: Upcycling Spent Battery Leachates with Selective and Bipolar Ion-Exchange Membranes
Authors: Zi Hao Foo, Trent R Lee, Jakob M Wegmueller, Samuel M Heath, John H Lienhard
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecycling spent lithium-ion batteries offers a sustainable solution to reduce ecological degradation from mining and mitigate raw material shortages and price volatility. This study investigates using electrodialysis with selective and bipolar ion-exchange membranes to establish a circular economy for lithium-ion batteries. An experimental data set of over 1700 ion concentration measurements across five current densities, two solution compositions, and three pH levels supports the techno-economic analysis. Selective electrodialysis (SED) isolates lithium ions from battery leachates, yielding a 99% Li-pure retentate with 68.8% lithium retention, achieving relative ionic fluxes up to 2.41 for Li+ over transition metal cations and a selectivity of 5.64 over monovalent cations. Bipolar membrane electrodialysis (BMED) converts LiCl into high-purity LiOH and HCl, essential for battery remanufacturing and reducing acid consumption via acid recycling. High current densities reduce ion leakage, achieving lithium leakage as low as 0.03%, though hydronium and hydroxide leakage in BMED remains high at 11–20%. Our analysis projects LiOH production costs between USD 1.1 and 3.6 per kilogram, significantly lower than current prices. Optimal SED and BMED conditions are identified, emphasizing the need to control proton transport in BMED and improve cobalt–lithium separation in SED to enhance cost efficiency.Keywords: battery leachate, circular economy, electrodialysis, ion-exchange membranes, lithium hydroxide

Introduction

Lithium is a crucial element in the electrodes and electrolytes of modern lithium-ion batteries (LiBs), which are indispensable for contemporary portable electronics, electric vehicles, and renewable energy storage systems.1,2 The high energy density, lightweight design, and extended cycle life make ternary LiBs a preferred choice for energy storage. LiBs, consequently, account for 77.9% of total installed battery capacity today.3−5 Traditionally, lithium is extracted from primary sources such as spodumene ores in Australia or continental salt lakes in South America and Asia.6,7 Conventional extraction techniques, especially the evaporation of salt-lake brines or mining leachates, are highly water-intensive, consuming about 800 m3 of freshwater per metric ton of lithium carbonate.1,8,9 The over-reliance on evaporation ponds exacerbates water scarcity in arid regions and poses significant environmental risks, including aquifer contamination and wetland degradation.1,10 Further, the protracted production cycles associated with evaporation ponds create substantial bottlenecks, culminating in an inelastic supply chain that is slow to respond to market demands.1,8

Recycling modern LiBs, which comprise intricate assemblies of electrodes, casings, and electrolytes, is technologically challenging but offers a sustainable solution to bridge demand gaps and foster a circular battery economy. Environmentally, battery recycling reduces raw material demand, which in turn minimizes adverse ecological degradation from mining and end-of-life landfill disposal.5,11−13 Economically, a circular economy enhances the stability of the supply chain by mitigating the risks associated with raw material shortages and price volatility.14,15 This is particularly important for critical materials like lithium, cobalt, nickel, and manganese, which are essential for battery production but are subject to geopolitical and supply chain constraints.16,17

The capacity of LiBs degrades with repeated charge/discharge cycles, typically falling below 60% after 500 to 6000 cycles, with an average lifespan of 5 to 10 years for most applications.4,18 Once expended, batteries are commonly recycled in sequential processes: pyrolysis and physical and magnetic separation, followed by hydrometallurgical extraction through acid leaching, yielding effluents rich in valuable metals such as lithium, cobalt, manganese, and nickel.18−21 Although sequential ion exchange columns are generally used to selectively capture these metals,22 the process is energy-intensive and produces substantial acid waste, making large-scale deployment both economically and environmentally challenging.5,23,24 Consequently, less than 6% of spent LiBs are recycled globally, with the majority ending up in landfills, posing severe environmental concerns due to the potential release of toxic gases and heavy metal contamination of groundwater.5,18,25

Advancing selective technologies for critical metals recovery can alleviate longstanding environmental challenges associated with spent batteries by fostering a circular battery economy.5,11,26 Emerging technologies, such as solvent-driven techniques using ionic liquids,27 deep eutectic solvents,28 fractional crystallization,13,29 and organic chelants,26,30 along with electrochemical methods like capacitive deionization31 and electrochemically switched ion exchange,32 and variants of conventional membrane processes, including nanofiltration33−36 and electrodialysis (ED),37−39 are paving the way toward more efficient and environmentally benign metal recovery processes. ED, in particular, maintains significant academic and commercial interest as a key unit operation due to its inherent acid stability and relative ease of scalability.40−42 As illustrated in Figure 1, ED offers a potential pathway to establish a circular lithium economy while minimizing chemical waste generation by recycling the recovered acid for upstream hydrometallurgical extraction. Here, ED with selective membranes is employed to isolate lithium ions from multi-ionic battery leachates;43,44 the extracted Li-rich streams are further processed using bipolar ED membranes to produce high-purity hydrochloric acid and lithium hydroxide,45−47 the latter being a preferred feedstock for manufacturing ternary battery cathodes such as nickel–manganese–cobalt (NMC) types.4,5,20

A circular manufacturing economy for LiBs can be facilitated by SED and BMED. First, the spent LiB is processed into a black mass and subsequently leached to produce a highly acidic inorganic mixture. The battery leachate comprises lithium, cobalt, nickel, and manganese and has been dissolved in 1 M or higher concentration of a strong acid (e.g., HCl or H2SO4). Then, the battery leachate is treated with SED, which leverages ion mobility differences to produce a high-purity Li-rich retentate stream. Next, the Li-rich stream is further processed by BMED, generating concentrated (1 M) LiOH and HCl products. Here, two circular economies are fostered by ED. First, the HCl is recycled for battery leaching, minimizing acid consumption in hydrometallurgy extraction and lowering post-treatment cost for chemical wastes. Second, the LiOH is sufficiently pure for use as feedstock for battery remanufacturing, thereby establishing a circular life cycle for the LiB economy.

In this work, we conduct experimental and techno-economic analyses to ascertain the viability of ED in facilitating a circular lithium economy for batteries. First, we investigate selective electrodialysis (SED) employing membranes that appear to leverage ion mobility differences to isolate Li+ from transition metal cations in leachates (e.g., Co2+, Mn2+, and Ni2+) and other potential contaminants introduced during aqueous processing (e.g., Na+, K+, Mg2+, and Ca2+). Next, we assess bipolar membrane electrodialysis (BMED) to synthesize high-purity LiOH and HCl, with HCl recycled to minimize acid consumption in upstream hydrometallurgy leaching and LiOH upcycled as feedstock for battery remanufacturing. For both SED and BMED processes, we conduct parametric analyses, varying current density from 2.5 to 100.0 mA cm–2 and pH from 0 to 4, to facilitate mechanistic investigations and quantify the impact on selectivity, energy efficiency, ion leakage, and product yield. We compile an original data set that comprises over 1700 ion concentration measurements. Finally, we perform a techno-economic analysis (TEA) to evaluate the economic competitiveness of the proposed technology, understand key contributors to process cost, and determine necessary improvements to enhance economic viability.

Materials and Methods

Bench-Scale Experimental Characterization

Standard-grade (CMB and AHA) and selective-grade (CXP-S and AXP-D) cation and anion ion exchange membranes (IEMs), and bipolar IEMs (BP-1EX-SB) are procured from Astom Corporation (Tokyo, Japan). The anion exchange membranes (AEMs) contain positively charged quaternary ammonium groups for anion conduction, while cation exchange membranes (CEMs) generally feature negatively charged perfluorosulfonic acid moieties that facilitate cation transport.40 The bipolar IEM incorporates a water dissociation catalyst between the cation- and anion-exchange layers, designed to operate in reverse bias for acid and base production.48,49

In this study, two bench-scale ED setups are designed to evaluate the efficacy of SED and BMED for battery leachate upcycling. The SED setup consists of 10 repeating cell pairs of selective CEMS and AEMs, as illustrated in Figures 2A and S3A in the Supporting Information. These membranes are housed within a standard two-flow chamber ED cell (PCCell ED 200) with a total membrane area of 0.43 m2. The SED membranes (CXP-S and AXP-D) are chosen for their high chemical resistance and stability, particularly in highly acidic environments, and are rated for continuous operation across a pH range of 0 to 10. This ensures their durability under the experimental conditions used in this study. Conversely, the BMED setup utilizes a stack of 10 repeating units of the standard CEM, BPM, and AEM (each repeating membrane unit is illustrated in Figures 2B and S3B in the Supporting Information), arranged in a three-flow chamber ED cell (PCCell ED 64004), with a total membrane area of 0.20 m2. The BMED membranes (CMB, AHA, and BP-1EX-SB) are designed for operation in pH 0 to 14 solutions. Polypropylene spacers, each 0.45 mm thick and oriented at a 45° angle, are utilized to separate the diluate, concentrate, acid, base, salt, and rinse streams in the respective setups. Titanium with mixed metal oxide coating electrodes is employed as the cathode and anode. To suppress the evolution of chlorine gas, a 0.2 M Na2SO4 solution is employed as the rinse electrolyte.50

Bench-scale experiments are conducted with acid leachates as characterized from prior hydrometallurgical processing of spent batteries.2,25,43 The nominal composition of the battery leachates from upstream electrochemical assisted acid leaching is delineated in Table 1.25 ReagentPlus-grade LiCl, MnCl2, CoCl2, NiCl2, HCl (37%), and NaOH (anhydrous, >98%) from MilliporeSigma and Type 1 ultrapure Water (18.2 MΩ cm) are used to prepare large volumes of stock leachate solutions necessary for IEM characterization throughout. Initially, the selective IEMs undergo surface activation by being immersed in 1 M HCl for 4 h, followed by storage in a buffered 0.5 M NaCl solution. Subsequently, to ensure membrane stability, the IEMs are equilibrated with the electrolyte streams in the SED and BMED cells for 4 h before applying any potential difference.

For SED, we evaluate the ion selectivity and electrical impedance of each repeating IEM unit using NMC battery leachates with experiments conducted at current densities between 2.5 and 30.0 mA cm–2 and pH levels from 0 to 4. As illustrated in Figure S3A in the Supporting Information, SED produces a lithium-rich retentate and a concentrate stream, the latter being enriched with transition metal cations like Mn2+, Co2+, and Ni2+. We conducted the SED experiments separately from the BMED experiments to maintain a consistent feed solution across all tests. This approach ensures that we can systematically analyze the effects of varying pH, current density, and other process parameters on the selectivity and energetic performance.

For BMED, we assess the counterion selectivity and electrical impedance of each repeating unit at current densities ranging from 10.0 to 100.0 mA cm–2. BMED generates HCl, LiOH, and desalinated product streams from LiCl feed solutions with initial concentrations of 0.3 to 1.5 M, as depicted in the Supporting Information (Figure S3B). Similar to SED, we conduct the BMED experiments independently to enable a focused investigation of process parameters under controlled conditions. We choose the concentrations of LiCl to reflect the expected upper and lower limits of salt concentrations found in industrial NMC leachates, allowing for a comprehensive understanding of performance under different scenarios.

Aqueous samples from the diluate and concentrate streams of SED and from the acid, base, and salt streams of BMED are periodically sampled and stored in chilled centrifuge tubes. The elemental composition of each sample is analyzed using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES, Agilent ICP-OES 5100). A five-point calibration curve is prepared with Trace-Cert standards from MilliporeSigma, with the maximum uncertainty in concentration measurement being approximately 5%. The concentrations of hydronium and hydroxide ions in each sample are diluted and measured (Hach PHC 301 and 80501). Cumulatively, approximately 1700 ion concentration measurements, corresponding to 270 unique aqueous samples, have been collected. The experimental data set corresponds to 21 unique experimental conditions, varying in current density, solution pH, and ion concentrations. This original data set supports the evaluation of the techno-economic viability of promoting a circular lithium economy through ED. The experimental data are organized in Tables S3–S38 in the Supporting Information as a basis for future comparison.

Multi-Ionic Transport in Ion-Exchange Membranes

Multi-ionic transport models are developed to support process modeling for SED and BMED. The process models are calibrated against the ion concentration measurements from the bench-scale SED and BMED experiments, enabling projections of anticipated energy costs, membrane area requirements, and other capital and operational expenditures for commercial ED.51,52 The derivation of the transport model is detailed in the Supporting Information. The Nernst–Planck equation incorporates ion and water transport by electromigrative and diffusive mechanisms and has been widely leveraged to model species transport across IEMs.53−56 Here, the electromigrative flux is described by the species transport numbers, which quantifies the fraction of the total applied current conducted by each ion, characterizing the combined selectivity effects from ion partitioning and mobility across the IEM.54,57 On the other hand, diffusive transport is characterized by the ion and water permeability coefficients that are regressed from multi-ionic diffusion experiments.50,58 Additionally, concentration polarization effects in the electrolyte streams are accounted for utilizing mass transfer correlations for the spacers employed in our experimental setups.59 The limiting current density of each electrolyte stream is estimated to ensure that the experiments are conducted within the Ohmic regime.59

Process Modeling and Techno-Economic Analysis

Here, we utilize our experiments with NMC battery leachates along with our calibrated SED and BMED ion transport models to project the expected energy, capital, and other operating costs involved in leachate upcycling to realize a circular battery economy. The lithium recovery capacity is dynamically determined based on optimized operational conditions identified in our experiments—namely, 22.5 mA cm–2 for SED and 33.3 mA cm–2 for BMED, with respective considerations for pH and ion selectivity. These conditions are incorporated into the TEA, reflecting realistic operational scenarios. We stress that these techno-economic projections represent the ideal cost of producing LiOH from battery leachates and do not account for profit margins, consulting fees, legal expenses, permitting, and other latent business costs that would factor into the actual market price.50,65

To ascertain the commercial viability of SED, we model an industrial-scale ED module typically employed for water reclamation in desalination and agricultural applications.50,52 The SED module includes 1000 cell pairs of selective CEMs and AEMs and has a total membrane area of 2.94 × 104 m2.52,59 As illustrated in Figure 2C, we apply the ion transport numbers derived from SED experiments (as determined in eq 3) and compute the transient ion concentrations in the diluate and concentrate streams by integrating and solving the species conservation equations with the modeled ion fluxes. The initial compositions of the diluate and concentrate streams are based on the NMC battery leachate and pure water, respectively.

For BMED, we model an industrial-scale ED module used for the production of acids and bases from hypersaline brines, comprising 160 repeating units of CEM–BPM–AEM, with a total membrane area of 4.94 × 104 m2.46,51,66 Similar to the approach with SED, the ion transport numbers, limiting current density, and membrane charge density derived from relevant BMED experiments are employed in the process modeling. As depicted in Figure 2D, the transient changes in ion concentrations in the acid, base, and salt streams are integrated and resolved based on species conservation and the modeled ion fluxes. The initial composition of the salt stream is set at either 0.3 or 1.5 M LiCl, while the acid and base streams start as 1.0 mM HCl and LiOH, respectively.

Following module-scale modeling, we adapt a techno-economic model originally developed to assess high-salinity desalination with ED to estimate the specific cost of producing LiOH from battery leachates.50,52 The TEA incorporates variable lithium recovery rates based on the optimized conditions for SED and BMED, ensuring that the analysis reflects the dynamic nature of lithium recovery throughout the process. Labor costs are calculated based on data from the Economic Research Institute, using “Chemical Engineer” as the job title and factoring in salaries from the most populous cities. Electricity costs are sourced from public utility department records, ensuring that the model reflects real-world operational expenses. In this analysis, the capital equipment costs for pumps, heat exchangers, throttle valves, and pipes are modeled to scale with the membrane area.50,52 We adopted commercial prices for the membranes and did not assume any discounts for bulk purchases. Results and Discussion

Exploiting Ion Mobility Differences for Lithium Recovery with SED

NMC battery leachates are first treated with SED to isolate Li+ ions from a mixture of multivalent transition metal cations, including Co2+, Mn2+, and Ni2+. Constant current experiments are performed, and the transient ion concentrations in the diluate and concentrate streams are monitored as functions of solution pH and applied current density (1368 ion concentration measurements as compiled in Tables S3–S32 in the Supporting Information). These experimental measurements are contrasted with predictions from calibrated SED process models, as presented in Figures S5–S9, covering a pH range from 0 to 4 and current densities from 2.5 to 30.0 mA cm–2. The experimental transient ion concentrations align with the model predictions, registering root-mean-square deviations of less than 5.0% across all tested conditions. To achieve this accuracy, transport numbers are initially treated as constants but are renormalized as respective species are depleted during the process, which is reflected in the kinks observed in the transient ion concentration profiles in Figures S5–S9. The maximum error with each ICP measurement is also around 5%, indicating good agreement between the experimental and modeled results. Additionally, across the current densities of 2.5–30.0 mA cm–2, the transient ion concentrations exhibit a strong linear correlation with process duration (minimum ri2 > 0.985), indicating that electromigrative contributions dominate the net ion flux in the SED process.58

Further, we emphasize that the selective CEMs utilized in this study are distinct from the conventional monovalent selective membranes commonly employed in salt production.40,50,58,63 Conventional monovalent selective CEMs are asymmetric composites and acquire cation selectivity through surface coatings endowed with a high density of positively charged moieties, such as quaternary ammonium groups, which enhance Donnan exclusion of multivalent cations.40,59 These monovalent selective CEMs, consequently, exhibit a negatively charged Stern layer in the electric double layer and possess a positive zeta potential.44,59 In contrast, the selective CEMs used in this study exhibit negative zeta potentials, and our subsequent measurements suggest that they amplify ion mobility differences to impart ion-selective properties.40 This design facilitates the selective retention of lithium in the diluate stream during SED operation as the mobility of Li+ ions in solution is typically the lowest compared to other cations present in the battery leachate.40,67 These other cations include transition metal cations such as Co2+, Mn2+, Ni2+, and Fe2+, as well as other ubiquitous cations that may be introduced during battery recycling processes, such as Na+, K+, Mg2+, Ca2+, and Al3+.18,19,30

Figure 3A presents the transient ion concentrations in the diluate stream from a batch SED experiment designed to assess the relative migration rates of Li+ compared to those of other cations. The experiment begins with an initial concentration of 0.1 M for each cation—Al3+, Ca2+, Co2+, Fe2+, K+, Li+, Mn2+, Mg2+, Na+, and Ni2+—with Cl– as the counteranion, under a constant current density of 30.0 mA cm–2. Average ion fluxes are calculated and normalized relative to Li+ and compared with conductivity measurements reflecting relative ion mobility in the bulk solution (Figure S4A).67 We emphasize that bulk solution ionic conductivity is used instead of membrane conductivity to decouple the effects of the partitioning and mobility. The former reflects the mobility of ions under an externally applied electric field. A comparison of the relative ion flux with the relative hydration Gibbs free energy is shown in Figure S4B. The ion flux ratio correlates strongly with bulk solution ionic conductivity (R2 = 0.96) but poorly with hydration Gibbs free energy (R2 = 0.008), suggesting that ion mobility, rather than partitioning, is the dominant mechanism driving Li+ separation.40,54 The ion flux ratios for the other nine cations relative to Li+ exceed unity, confirming that Li+ is selectively retained in the diluate stream. For example, the SED membranes achieve separation factors of 1.99 and 2.41 for Li+ over multivalent transition metal cations like Mn2+ and Ni2+ and selectivities of 5.64 and 2.01 for Li+ over monovalent cations like K+ and Na+. The observed ion permeation rates—K+ > Al3+ > Ca2+ > Ni2+ ≃ Co2+ ≃ Fe2+ ≃ Mg2+ ≃ Mn2+ > Na+ > Li+—are consistent with previous studies of CEMs exhibiting mobility selectivity.40,67 Notably, the separation of Li+ from other monovalent cations remains unattainable with conventional monovalent selective membranes, which rely solely on Donnan exclusion mechanisms.40,58,59,68

Within the Ohmic regime, counterion selectivity in CEMs is governed by a combination of partition and mobility selectivity.40,55,64,68,69 Partition selectivity arises from the differential sorption of cations into the negatively charged polymer matrix;40,64 in this process, multivalent cations like Mn2+ and Co2+ are preferentially absorbed over anions and monovalent cations such as K+ and Li+. Conversely, mobility selectivity is influenced by the favorability of electrostatic interactions between the negatively charged moieties and the cations, which is controlled by the functional group density within the membrane.40,68−70 This density is modulated by the solution pH and the degree of polymer hydration.70,71 The findings from the relative ion migration rate experiment presented in Figure 3A indicate that K+ permeates faster than the multivalent alkaline earth and transition metal cations, which would not occur if partition selectivity is the dominant mechanism.40,64 In essence, the multi-ionic experiments reveal that mobility selectivity likely plays a more significant role in our CEMs, corroborating the near linear correlation between ionic flux and bulk ion mobility recorded in Figure S4.

Subsequently, batch SED experiments using NMC battery leachates are conducted under 15 distinct process conditions, encompassing all possible combinations of 5 current densities (2.5, 7.5, 15.0, 22.5, and 30.0 mA cm–2) and 3 pH levels (0, 2, and 4). These conditions are chosen to reflect a realistic range of operating current densities50,69 and to assess the influence of acid leaching concentration on the efficiency of SED in lithium retention.5,19,25,43 The SED experiments aim to elucidate the effects of process conditions on lithium retention rates in the diluate stream and to quantify the power consumption per repeating membrane unit. As depicted in Figure 3B, lithium retention (RLi) is defined as the proportion of lithium remaining in the diluate stream after the transition metal cations have been depleted, achieving 99% Li purity in the recovered solution. At the experimental end point (tend), the separation factor (αLi) between Li+ and transition metals (e.g., Ni2+, Co2+, and Mn2+) is calculated based on the total ionic fluxes and plotted in Figure 3C. The experiments reveal that the lithium separation factor exceeds unity under all tested conditions, with higher lithium selectivity generally observed in more acidic solutions, underscoring that SED effectively and selectively retains lithium in the diluate stream from NMC battery leachates. Moreover, lithium selectivity exhibits an upward trend, approaching an asymptotic value with increasing current density at pH 0, while remaining relatively invariant across varying current densities at pH 4. For instance, αLi/Co increases from 1.63 to 3.88 as the current density increases from 2.5 to 30.0 mA cm–2 at a solution pH of 0. Unlike conventional monovalent selective CEMs, the CEMs maintain strong mobility selectivity even at lower pH levels, thereby avoiding the typical attenuation caused by weakened Donnan exclusion mechanisms with acidified solutions, as detailed in our prior publication.59 This resilience in selectivity, along with the acid stability of the IEM polymers, makes SED particularly effective for treating highly acidic battery leachates.25,43

Figure 3D illustrates the impact of enhanced selectivity at higher current densities and lower solution pH on lithium retention (beige) and power consumption (blue) in the SED membrane stack. The improved lithium selectivity, resulting from lower pH levels and increased current densities, significantly enhances lithium retention in the diluate stream. Across all tested pH levels, lithium retention increases with higher current density and approaches an asymptotic value. For example, at pH 0, lithium retention increases from 29.1% to 68.8% as the current density increases from 2.5 to 30.0 mA cm–2. Additionally, more acidic battery leachates lead to greater lithium retention, with the retention rates improving from 56.1% at pH 4 to 68.8% at pH 0, under a constant current density of 30.0 mA cm–2.

Conversely, in theory, energy dissipation from Joule heating in a constant impedance Ohmic conductor follows a quadratic relationship with current density, as depicted by the dashed line in Figure 3D.7 Our power consumption measurements reveal a power law exponent ranging from 1.41 to 1.68, indicating that the effective impedance of the ED stack decreases with increasing current densities. The most significant reduction in stack impedance is observed with NMC battery leachates at pH 0, suggesting that protonation of the functional groups enhances ionic conductivity, aligning with prior electrochemical impedance spectroscopy characterizations of similar polymers.40,72,73

In essence, our SED experiments demonstrate that NMC battery leachates can be efficiently processed using SED, producing a diluate stream that is 99% Li+-rich and effectively isolating it from a mixture of multivalent transition metal cations. The findings from the relative ion migration rate experiments suggest that ion mobility differences are likely the primary mechanism behind this selectivity. Constant current experiments show that lithium retention in the diluate stream significantly improves with higher current densities and lower pH levels, achieving up to 68.8% retention at pH 0 and 30.0 mA cm–2. Concurrently, power consumption increases less than proportionally with the current density due to enhanced membrane conductivity at higher current densities. The LiCl-rich diluate solution recovered from SED was utilized as feedstock for subsequent BMED processes.

Ameliorating Lithium Leakage in BMED with Higher Current Density

Here, we deploy BMED to generate LiOH and HCl product streams from the LiCl recovered from the upstream SED processes. Constant-current BMED experiments are conducted, monitoring transient ion concentrations in the acid, base, and salt streams as a function of the applied current density. In total, 378 ion concentration measurements with BMED are compiled in Tables S33–S38 in the Supporting Information, spanning current densities from 10.0 to 100.0 mA cm–2. In all experiments, the initial concentrations are 10 mM HCl in the acid stream and 10 mM LiOH in the base stream, while the salt stream contains either 0.3 or 1.5 M LiCl. The BMED experiments continue until 1.0 M HCl and 1.0 M LiOH are recovered from the LiCl feed solution.

Figure 4A illustrates the ion transport across the BPM and the CEM and AEM during BMED operation. In an ideal BMED process, Cl– and Li+ ions migrate through the AEM and CEM, respectively, while the BPM facilitates water dissociation into H3O+ and OH–, producing LiOH and HCl streams.39,46,63,74 In practical BMED operations, however, undesirable co-ion leakage occurs,49,51 as represented by the dashed arrows in Figure 4A. This leakage amounts to reduced current efficiency and lower yields of LiOH and HCl, thereby increasing the specific energy consumption.51,54 Here, we employ the same Nernst–Planck model used in our preceding SED section to calibrate ion transport across the CEM and AEM. Further, we adapt the validated BPM model developed by Strathmann et al.61 and Culcasi et al.51,62 to calculate ion transport across the CEL and AEL within the BPM. The concentration profiles of Li+, Cl–, H3O+, and OH– for a BPM operating in reverse bias are depicted in Figure S1 in the Supporting Information.48,53 The predicted BPM limiting currents from the model are juxtaposed with the reference values from Strathmann et al.,61 registering root-mean-square errors below 1%. The transport models for the BPM, CEM, and AEM are combined and calibrated using the experimental transient ion concentration measurements. A forward Euler scheme is applied to simulate concentration changes based on species conservation principles. As illustrated in Figures S10–S15, the calibrated transport model aligns closely with empirical data, achieving root-mean-square errors of under 3.5% across all tested conditions.

The dashed curves indicate undesirable ion leakage across the AEM, CEM, and BPM. Figure 4B depicts the impact of current density and LiCl feed concentration on Li+ leakage across the BPM, as well as the leakage of H3O+ and OH– across the AEM and CEM, respectively. In general, the amount of co-ion leakage across the CEM, AEM, and BPM decreases with higher current density.69,75 For instance, undesirable net Li+ leakage across the BPM decreases with increasing current density, falling from 0.042% to 0.017% with a 1.5 M LiCl feed solution and from 0.008% to <0.001% with a 0.3 M LiCl feed solution. The relatively low Li+ leakage observed across the BPM is attributed to the applied current density being approximately 2 orders of magnitude higher than the BPM’s limiting current density, effectively minimizing co-ion crossover.61,76

Similarly, H3O+ leakage across the AEM decreases from 28.2% to 20.0% with 1.5 M LiCl and from 24.8% to 17.8% with 0.3 M LiCl as current density increases from 10.0 to 100.0 mA cm–2. Over the same current density interval, OH– leakage across the CEM decreases from 25.1 to 20.0% with 1.5 M LiCl and from 24.9 to 10.8% with 0.3 M LiCl. Despite improvements in counterion selectivity with increasing current density, H3O+ and OH– co-ion leakage remains significantly high, reducing net current efficiency and LiOH and HCl yield from BMED by as much as 11–20%, which agrees with prior ED reports.40 This persistent high leakage has been rationalized by the Grotthuss mechanism in prior publications,40 which greatly enhances proton transport within the confined interstitial phase in hydrated IEMs, resulting in H3O+ and OH– mobilities being approximately 6 to 10 times greater than those of ions like Li+ and Cl–. In other words, our experiments indicate that controlling H3O+ and OH– crossover across the AEM and CEM is the most critical and sensitive variable that remains to be optimized for effective BMED operation in battery recycling.77

Figure 4C illustrates the apparent BMED counterion selectivity between OH– and Cl– in the base stream (αOH/Cl), as well as between H3O+ and Li+ in the acid stream (), as functions of the applied current density and LiCl concentration. The reduced co-ion leakage enhances apparent counterion selectivity in BMED at higher current densities. For example, increases from 0.97 to 0.99 with 0.3 M LiCl and from 0.94 to 0.99 with 1.5 M LiCl as current densities increase from 10.0 to 100.0 mA cm–2. Conversely, αOH/Cl remains consistently near unity as Cl– remains undetected by ICP-OES in the base stream. The BMED experiments suggest that co-ion leakage in CEMs is less pronounced than that in AEMs, aligning with recent mechanistic reports.40,69 However, higher LiCl feed concentrations consistently result in lower apparent counterion selectivity due to increased residual leakage of Li+, driven by the larger concentration gradient across the AEM with more concentrated feeds.53

Figure 4D depicts the net Li+ leakage and power consumption of the BMED system as functions of the current density and LiCl feed concentration. Throughout the experiment, enhanced counterion selectivity across the BPM, CEM, and AEM results in reduced Li+ leakage, decreasing from 1.11% to 0.03% with 0.3 M LiCl and from 1.82% to 0.28% with 1.5 M LiCl. Conversely, while higher current densities yield increased Li+, they also lead to a disproportionate rise in power consumption. The dashed line in Figure 4D represents the expected quadratic relationship between the power and current density in an Ohmic conductor. However, the actual power law relationship observed in the BMED experiments is less pronounced, with power law exponents of 1.72 for 0.3 M and 1.48 for 1.5 M LiCl. Similar to the SED analysis, this lower than expected dependence is attributed to the higher conductivity across the BMED stack, likely due to improved ionic conductivity in the IEMs.72,73 The reduced power consumption with more concentrated LiCl feed solutions results from the higher solution conductivity, which minimizes the potential drop across the bulk solution and concentration boundary layers.67

In essence, our BMED experiments demonstrate that LiCl recovered from upstream SED processes can be effectively valorized into concentrated LiOH (1 M) and HCl (1 M) product streams. The LiOH is sufficiently concentrated for use as feedstock in battery manufacturing,5,18 while the HCl can be recycled to minimize acid consumption in upstream hydrometallurgy battery leaching.2,19 Our experiments indicate that undesirable co-ion leakage across the BPM, CEM, and AEM reduces with current density; however, enhanced counterion selectivity is accompanied by a greater than proportional increase in power consumption, representing a trade-off in BMED operations. Importantly, despite improvements in counterion selectivity, significant H3O+ and OH– leakage across the AEM and CEM, respectively, remains close to 20%, which is the most critical parameter to optimize for improving LiOH and HCl yield and enhancing current efficiency.54

Projecting Cost of Battery Leachate Upcycling with SED and BMED

In this section, we perform a preliminary TEA to estimate the ideal production costs of LiOH recovery from spent battery leachates using SED and BMED. The energy cost, membrane area, and capital equipment required for both processes are evaluated using process models that are calibrated against the NMC battery leachate and LiCl experiments, as detailed in the preceding sections. The projected capital and operational costs for commercial-scale ED are assessed with validated techno-economic frameworks previously applied to analyze high-salinity desalination and greenhouse effluent treatment52,58 and normalized per mole of Li. The TEA aims to evaluate the economic feasibility of the proposed membrane technologies in achieving high-purity LiOH production. Our analysis excludes potential economic benefits from the valorization of Li-depleted byproducts from SED, such as concentrate streams rich in transition metal cations such as Mn2+, Co2+, and Ni2+. Additionally, it does not account for anticipated cost reductions from decreased acid consumption due to HCl recycling from BMED, nor does it include potential savings in electricity costs from nearby photovoltaic plants.50 The reported LiOH production costs are consequently anticipated to be conservative estimates.

Figure 4A,B illustrates the projected annual cost of operating an SED and BMED plant for pH 0 NMC battery leachate treatment as a function of the applied current density. Here, the annual cost is composed of capital expenditures (CapEx) and operational expenditures (OpEx), as delineated in Section 2.3. For both SED and BMED, CapEx decreases disproportionately with higher current densities due to the reduced membrane area required to achieve the same recovery ratio.54 This smaller membrane area also lowers the costs of auxiliary mechanical equipment and piping, further reducing overall CapEx.50 Conversely, in general, OpEx for both SED and BMED increases sharply with the current density because the power consumption of the ED modules follows a power law relationship with the current density, which significantly elevates energy costs. Notably, for SED, OpEx decreases between the current density range of 2.5–22.5 mA cm–2 due to a significant increase in Li+ retention, while costs for labor, maintenance, chemicals, and membrane replacement remain fixed.50,52 When the opposing trends in CapEx and OpEx are superimposed for SED and BMED, a minimum annual cost is observed, as indicated by the star markers in Figure 5A and B, respectively. This minimum point typically represents the optimal operating condition chosen for commercial ED operations.54

Projected annualized cost of LiOH production for (A) SED and (B) BMED. The capital cost decreases with current density due to the reduced membrane requirements, while the operational cost increases with current density by virtue of the power law relationship between energy consumption and current. The SED and BMED annual costs exhibit a local minimum at the current densities of 21.0 and 35.9 mA cm–2. (C) Plot of the specific cost of Li extraction for SED and BMED (left vertical axis) and LiOH production (right vertical axis) from battery leachates for six major economies with pre-existing lithium battery recycling programs. The techno-economic assessment is conducted based on publicly available information as of October 2023. We stress that the specific costs represent the ideal production cost and do not include latent business expenses such as those from consulting, permitting and siting, legal, and trucking. The projected cost of LiOH production via ED is approximately an order of magnitude lower than prevailing commodity prices, providing a significant margin for additional business expenses.

Based on the identified optimal points, the following experimental conditions are selected for their proximity to ideal operation: SED is operated at 22.5 mA cm–2 and BMED is operated at 33.3 mA cm–2 to determine the net specific cost of LiOH production. The specific costs per kmol of Li extracted in SED (unhatched bars) and BMED (hatched bars) are presented in Figure 5C for six countries with established battery recycling programs. These countries are chosen to evaluate the impact of interest rates, electricity, and labor costs on LiOH production. Generally, BMED incurs higher energy costs per unit mole of Li extracted than SED due to the higher power density required for water dissociation in the BPM.46,51,78 The effect of local interest rate variation on the amortized capital cost of ED appears to have a minor impact on the total production costs. Nonetheless, electricity costs remain the dominant contributor to the overall production cost for both SED and BMED, attributed to the high feed concentration in SED59 and the substantial water dissociation potential in BMED.48

Finally, the specific costs of Li extraction via SED and BMED are combined to project the overall cost of LiOH production from NMC battery leachates, as depicted by the diamond markers in Figure 5C. The estimated LiOH production costs range between USD 1.1 and 3.6 per kilogram, which is significantly lower, by a factor of 8 to 10, than the prevailing LiOH commodity prices as of October 2023. However, in practical ED operations, we emphasize that actual production costs of LiOH may increase substantially due to factors such as material leakage, pipe corrosion, membrane fouling, and other indirect business expenses including transportation costs and profit margins.65 Conversely, production costs could decrease with the availability of low-cost photovoltaic energy and cost savings from acid recycling.2,50 Overall, the economic analysis suggests that ED holds the potential for economically viable LiOH valorization from NMC battery leachates, warranting further industrial consideration.

Implications for Circular Lithium Economy

In this study, we investigate the efficacy of SED in isolating lithium ions (Li+) from multivalent transition metal cations present in NMC battery leachates, employing membranes that appear to magnify differences in ion mobility. In total, over 1700 ion concentration measurements are recorded. Our SED experiments across varying current densities (2.5 to 30.0 mA cm–2) and pH levels (0 to 4) reveal that higher current densities and lower pH levels significantly enhance lithium retention. With a multi-ionic solution comprising 0.1 M of 10 distinct cations, lithium selectivity increases with increasing current density, achieving separation factors up to 2.41 for Li+ over multivalent cations like Ni2+ and Mn2+ and a selectivity of 5.64 over monovalent cations such as K+ and Na+. With NMC battery leachates at pH 0, lithium retention from SED improves from 29.1 to 68.8% as current density increases to 30.0 mA cm–2, while producing a diluate stream with a Li+ purity of 99%.

BMED experiments further convert LiCl recovered from SED to LiOH and HCl. Our BMED experiments are conducted across current densities from 10.0 to 100.0 mA cm–2 and LiCl concentrations of 0.3 and 1.5 M. These experiments demonstrate that higher current densities reduce undesirable ion leakage and enhance counterion selectivity, significantly decreasing lithium leakage across the BPM to 0.03%. Nonetheless, significant current efficiency losses of up to 20% from hydronium and hydroxide leakage across the AEM and CEM persist, indicating a need to control proton transport in BMED.

Our preliminary TEA identifies optimal operating current density near 22.5 mA cm–2 for SED and 33.3 mA cm–2 for BMED. The projected costs of LiOH production range from USD 1.1 to 3.6 per kilogram, which is approximately 1 order of magnitude lower than prevailing market prices, suggesting the economic viability of these processes.

Before concluding, we project the effects of enhanced selectivity in SED and BMED on lithium retention, current efficiency, and specific energy consumption. This analysis elucidates the necessary selectivity levels required to achieve specific improvements in product yield and energy reduction. First, our SED analysis reveals that optimizing Co/Li, Mn/Li, and Ni/Li separation factors is crucial for improving the techno-economic feasibility of battery recycling. Figure 6A demonstrates the impact of an enhanced Co/Li separation factor on lithium retention and specific energy consumption during SED. Assuming identical separation factors for Co/Li, Mn/Li, and Ni/Li to simplify the analysis, the current SED membrane performance is denoted by the star marker. Here, improved Co/Li selectivity can be facilitated by process condition optimization or through enhanced mobility differences brought about by different polymer chemistry and modification of the membrane charge density.40 As αCo/Li increases, lithium retention nears 100%. Our findings suggest that to attain 90% and 99% Li+ recovery from NMC battery leachates, αCo/Li must be at least 7.38 and 85.7, respectively. Enhanced electrostatic friction within the IEM, driven by a higher charge density of negatively charged moieties, can likely improve the mobility selectivity between Li+ and transition metal cations.38,40 However, ED membranes inherently face a trade-off between selectivity and conductivity; membranes designed for higher selectivity often exhibit lower conductivity, which raises the energy cost of separation.55,56 In Figure 6A, we employ a conductivity–selectivity trade-off correlation based on recent studies55,56 to evaluate the potential impact on the specific energy consumption. The blue bands indicate potential variations in this relationship, highlighting that while improved membrane selectivity reduces specific energy by increasing Li+ recovery, it may also lead to higher energy costs if membrane conductivity becomes excessively low.

(A) Plot of the lithium retention rate and specific energy ratio of SED as a function of the Co/Li separation factor. Conversely, our BMED analysis indicates that minimizing hydronium and hydroxide leakage is essential for enhancing the current efficiency and reducing specific energy consumption during leachate upcycling. Figure 6B illustrates the impact of hydronium and hydroxide transport numbers in a BMED stack on the current efficiency and specific energy. For simplification, the transport numbers of hydronium and hydroxide across the AEM and CEM are assumed to be identical, with the star marker representing current BMED membrane performance. We speculate that CEMs and AEMs incorporating proton blockers could reduce proton leakage, thus decreasing and τOH.79 As and τOH approach zero, the current efficiencies of the CEM and AEM approach 100%. Specifically, achieving 90% and 95% current efficiency requires reducing and τOH to 9.85 × 10–2 and 4.77 × 10–2, respectively. Furthermore, a similar conductivity–selectivity analysis for BMED shows that while improved membrane selectivity can lower specific energy consumption by increasing Li+ recovery, excessively low membrane conductivity could result in higher energy costs.55,56

Our experimental and techno-economic analyses of SED and BMED indicate significant potential for upcycling battery leachates, enabling efficient LiOH production and HCl recycling. Further reductions in energy consumption depend on enhancing Co/Li, Mn/Li, and Ni/Li selectivities in the SED and minimizing hydronium and hydroxide leakage in BMED membranes. To advance a circular lithium economy using ED, several key areas require additional investigation. The impact of anionic composition in the battery leachate on cation separation efficiency, potentially affected by cation–anion transport coupling due to electroneutrality constraints, remains unexplored.80−82 While our experiments confirm membrane performance under varying pH conditions, including pH 0, long-term durability is crucial. The membranes are known for their stability in highly acidic environments, as supported by the existing literature, but further long-term testing is necessary to ensure consistent performance over extended use. Additionally, the potential for inorganic and organic fouling in battery leachates, particularly in BMED, and the related pretreatment costs, must be evaluated for economic viability.54,83,84 Further studies with local battery leachates are essential as the presence of ions with a mobility lower than that of Li+ could complicate the ED process.

In our SED experiments, the influence of pH on ion transport in IEMs was found to significantly impact selectivity, with notable changes observed at pH 0 due to likely alterations in the membrane charge density. However, the observed stability at pH 4 suggests that the relationship between pH, charge density, and selectivity is complex and not fully understood,55 indicating that further research is needed to fully elucidate these mechanisms and optimize membrane performance across different pH conditions.

In our SED experiments, no scaling was observed, primarily due to the high solubility of Mn, Ni, and Co chlorides, which kept their saturation indices below the solubility limits and prevented precipitation. However, sulfate-rich leachates could potentially cause scaling on membrane surfaces along the concentrate stream if saturation indices are exceeded, underscoring the importance of evaluating solution compositions when leachate chemistry changes.83 Similarly, in our BMED experiments, no scaling on the membrane was detected as the solubility limits of LiOH were not exceeded, even with concentration polarization. However, hydroxides of Mn2+, Ni2+, and Co2+ could induce scaling before 1 M LiOH and HCl concentrations are produced if their solubility limits are approached, necessitating careful monitoring to prevent scaling and ensure efficient operation. Finally, assessing potential cost savings from a reduced ion exchange treatment for residual multivalent cations is crucial for understanding the full economic benefits of SED and BMED.
