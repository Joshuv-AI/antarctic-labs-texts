The cardiovascular health diet index and incidence of metabolic syndrome: a prospective cohort study of Iranian adults
Authors: Hamid Ahmadirad, Hossein Farhadnejad, Mitra Kazemi Jahromi, Reza Sadeghi, Farshad Teymoori, Parvin Mirmiran, Fereidoun Azizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmerging evidence suggests the that Cardiovascular Health Diet Index(CHDI) may serve as a predictor of cardiovascular diseases risk; Therefore, the current study aimed to investigate the association between CHDI score and the risk of metabolic syndrome(MetS).MethodsThe present prospective cohort study included 4,628 adults (aged ≥ 18 years) with complete nutritional data from the 3rd and 4th phases of the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS), with follow up through 7th phase. Dietary intakes were collected using a validated 168-item food frequency questionnaire to compute the CHDI score. To estimate the risk of MetS across quartiles of CHDI scores, multivariable-adjusted Cox regression model were employed to report the hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals(95%CIs).ResultsThe mean±standard deviation(SD) for age and body mass index of the study population(40.4% men) were 36.2 ± 12.8 years and 25.5 ± 4.28 kg/m2, respectively. Median (25th to 75th interquartile range) of CHDI score in the overall population was 71.0 (63.3–78.8). The incidence of MetS was 35.7% during 9.33 years of follow-up. In the multivariable model, after adjusting for age, sex, smoking, physical activity, energy intake, education status, marital status, occupation status, baseline values of fasting plasma glucose, waist circumference, blood pressure, and triglycerides/HDL-C the risk of MetS was reduced by 20% across the quartiles of CHDI score (HR = 0.80; 95%CI:0.70–0.93, P-trend = 0.003). Also, each one SD increment in CHDI score was inversely associated with the risk of MetS(HR = 0.92, 95%CI: 0.88–0.97, P-value = 0.003).ConclusionsOur results demonstrate that a higher adherence to a diet with greater CHDI score may be associated with reduced risk of MetS among Iranian adults.

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) comprises a group of cardiometabolic traits, including central obesity, hypertriglyceridemia, lower high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), elevated blood pressure (BP), and hyperglycemia. The presence of three or more of these risk factors is identified as MetS [1, 2]. These cardiometabolic factors are linked to an increased risk of developing chronic diseases such as cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), type 2 diabetes (T2D), cancer, and mortality [3–5]. A meta-analysis of global data estimated the global prevalence of MetS was 25.4% based on the National Cholesterol Education Panel-Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP-ATPIII) [6]. Also, the prevalence of MetS is estimated at 28.0% in 2025 among the Iranian population according to the NCEP-ATPIII [7]. Although the pathogenesis of MetS is not distinctly understood, both genetic predisposition and lifestyle factors contribute to MetS incident and its development [8]. Among modifiable risk factors, dietary habits have been identified as important modifiable determinants in either preventing or promoting MetS [9].

Recent publications explored the relationship of specific dietary components, nutrients, or food groups with risk of MetS [10, 11]; however, diet quality score or indices may provide more comprehensive assessments or more accurate predictions of chronic disease risk compared to evaluating individual foods or nutrients in isolation [12, 13], in this regards, various dietary indices, such as the dietary approach to stop hypertension (DASH) diet [14], the Mediterranean diet score (MDS) [15], macronutrients quality indices [16], or the Healthy Eating Index (HEI) [17] have been studied for their association with MetS risk. These indices assess specific aspects of diet-related health effects, and significant evidence suggests they are linked to a reduced risk of MetS.

Recently, a new dietary index with health-promoting potential called the Cardiovascular Health Diet Index (CHDI) has been validated and developed among the Brazilian population according to the American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines for a healthy diet to improve cardiovascular health and prevent CVDs events. AHA recommends a higher intake of fruits, vegetables, fish and seafood, dairy products, whole-grain cereals, nuts, and legumes; however, it recommends a reduced intake of red meat, processed meats, sugar-sweetened beverages, and ultra-processed foods [18]. Limited data are available on the association between the CHDI score and health outcomes [19]. Cacau et al. found that there is no association between CHDI score and incidence of coronary artery calcification after a 4-year follow-up period; however, there is a protective association between CHDI score and subclinical atherosclerosis after an 8-year follow-up period [19].

Given the high prevalence of MetS in Iranian populations and the potential role of the CHDI score in predicting chronic disease risk, such as MetS, as well as the lack of studies that assessed the relationship between the CHDI score and the risk of MetS in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, such as Iran, the present research is the first study aimed at assessing the possible relationship between the CHDI score and MetS risk among Iranian adults.

Materials and methods

Study population

In the third survey of the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS) (2006–08), of 12,523 participants, 3568 were randomly selected for dietary assessment, and in the fourth survey (2009–2011), 7930 randomly selected subjects agreed to complete dietary assessment. For the current study, participants with complete dietary data on the third examination of TLGS and the new entry participants in the fourth examination were included. If a participant in the third survey had under report or over report of energy intake (lower than 800Kcal/d and higher than 4200 Kcal/d, respectively) (n = 225), was excluded; but if their energy intake was in the normal range in the fourth survey (n = 98), their dietary intake in the fourth survey was included as their baseline dietary data. Finally, after excluding those with over-under report of energy intake among those added from the fourth survey (n = 261), 8845 participants with complete dietary data were included. The response rate of participants providing valid dietary data after excluding improper completion and extreme energy reporters in the third and fourth surveys were 93.7% and 92.3% respectively.

For the present study, of 7357 individuals, aged ≥ 18 years, participants with the myocardial infarction (MI) and cardiovascular accident (n = 70), cancer history (n = 16), pregnant and lactating women (n = 185), and participants with missing MetS data (n = 75) or individuals with MetS in the baseline (n = 2246) were excluded. Some of them may fall into more than one category. Dietary intake assessment

Dietary intake information was recorded at the study’s baseline using a reliable and validated semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) consisting of 168 items, which has been developed for the Iranian population [20]. Trained dietitians conducted face-to-face interviews to evaluate participants’ usual dietary intake. During these interviews, individuals were asked to specify how often they consumed each food item over the previous year, responding on a daily, weekly, or monthly basis. To determine the energy and macro- and micronutrient composition of the foods, the US Department of Agriculture (USDA) food composition tables (FCT) was used. Traditional food items and food items that did not find in USDA FCT, were analyzed using the Iranian FCT [21].

CHDI score calculation

The CHDI score was computed for all study population using the guidelines recommended by AHA, which advised a regular intake of fruits, vegetables, nuts, whole grain cereals, beans, dairy products, and fish-seafood, and a lower intake of red meat, processed meat, sugar-sweetened beverages, and ultra-processed foods [18]. The CHDI score is based on 11 components, and the score for each component ranges from 0 to 10. For fruits, vegetables, nuts, whole grain cereals, beans, dairy products, and fish-seafood, a maximum score (a value of 10) is assigned to participants who have intake above the 340 g/d, 180 g/d, 12.9 g/d, 90 g/d, 80 g/d, 250 g/d, and 28.6 g/d, respectively. However, participants received a minimum score of 0 points if there was no consumption (0 g/day). Participants received a minimum score (0 points) if they had higher intake than 28.6 g/d of red meat, 12.9 g/d of processed meat, 142.9 ml/d of sugar-sweetened beverages, and 23 points of ultra-processed foods. However, a maximum score (a value of 10) is assigned to participants who have no consumption (0 g/d). Thus, this score ranges from 0 (minimal score of CHDI) to 110 (maximal score of CHDI).

Physical activity assessment

The Modifiable Activity Questionnaire (MAQ) was utilized to assess each participant’s physical activity levels at the study baseline. It has previously been validated to assess the physical activity of the Iranian population. A trained interviewer gathered information from participants regarding the frequency and duration of their activities over the previous year. Physical activity levels were calculated by multiplying the number of hours spent on a specific activity per week by its corresponding metabolic equivalent (MET) and expressed in MET-hours per week (MET-h/week) [22].

Demographic, anthropometric, and clinical assessment

Data on socio-demographic, anthropometric, and clinical measurements were determined at the baseline of the study. Information regarding participants’ age, sex, smoking status, academic status, marital status, and employment was obtained through a standardized personal questionnaire. Participants’ weights were measured using a digital scale accurate to 100 g, with all individuals wearing light clothing and no shoes during the assessment. The height of each participant was measured in a standing position, without shoes, using a stadiometer precise to 0.5 centimeters. Body mass index (BMI) is determined by taking a person’s weight in kilograms and dividing it by their height in meters squared (kg/m²). Waist circumference (WC) was assessed using a flexible, non-elastic tape measure and was accurate to 0.1 centimeters. Measurements were taken at the umbilical level, over light clothing, ensuring that no pressure was applied to the skin.

The systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were assessed by trained TLGS personnel following established protocols, utilizing the Korotkoff sound technique and a standard mercury sphygmomanometer with a precision of 2 mmHg. Measurements were taken with participants seated in a chair after resting for 15 min. A trained physician performed two BP readings, spaced at least 30 s apart, and the average of these two readings was recorded as the individual’s BP.

Biochemical measurements

Blood samples were obtained from participants following a 12–14 h overnight fast at the baseline of study. Samples were collected based on standard protocol while the individuals were seated, then centrifuged within 30 to 45 min after collection. All analyses were performed at the TLGS research laboratory on the same day as sample collection. The samples were processed using a Selectra 2 auto-analyzer (Vital Scientific, Spankeren, Netherlands). Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels were determined with an enzymatic colorimetric method utilizing the glucose oxidase technique. Triglyceride (TG) levels were determined through an enzymatic colorimetric assay utilizing glycerol phosphate oxidase. Total cholesterol (TC) was measured via an enzymatic colorimetric technique using cholesterol esterase and cholesterol oxidase. High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) was assessed following the precipitation of apolipoprotein B-containing lipoproteins with phosphotungstic acid. All measurements were conducted with commercial kits supplied by Pars Azmoon Inc. (Tehran, Iran). The inter- and intra-assay coefficients of variation were 2.2 and 2.2 for FPG, 1.6% and 0.6% for TGs, 2% and 0.5% for TC, and 2% and 0.5% for HDL-C, respectively.

Definitions

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) was diagnosed based on the guidelines provided in the joint interim statement, which required the presence of at least three out of five specific criteria: [1] abdominal obesity, indicated by a WC of 95 cm or more for men and women, based on updated WC standards for Iranian adults [23]; [2] FPG levels equal to or greater than 100 mg/dL, or anti-diabetic medication use; [3] fasting TGs reaching 150 mg/dL or higher, or specific treatment for lowering lipid levels; [4] reduced HDL-C levels, defined as below 50 mg/dL for women and under 40 mg/dL for men, or medication prescribed to manage HDL-C levels; and [5] increased BP, described as SBP of at least 130 mmHg or DBP of at least 85 mmHg or the use of antihypertensive medications [2].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed via the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software (version 15.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Histogram charts and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test were employed to assess whether the distributions of variables followed a normal pattern. Details regarding the baseline characteristics of participants based on quartiles of CHDI score were presented as mean±standard deviation (SD) or median (25th to 75th interquartile range) for continuous data and as percentages for categorical data. To test the statistical significance of categorical and continuous variables across quartiles of the CHDI score, chi-square and linear regression analyses were conducted, respectively.

Follow-up duration was calculated as person-years from the baseline dietary assessment date until the first occurrence of MetS (defined as above), censoring date, or administrative end of follow-up, whichever came first. MetS event date was defined as the midpoint between the examination date when MetS was first detected and the previous examination date. For participants without MetS or lost to follow-up after ≥ 1 follow-up visit, follow-up time was computed from study entry to the last recorded follow-up examination.

We utilized the Cox proportional hazards regression analysis to calculate the hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the MetS risk across quartiles of CHDI score. The proportional hazards assumption was checked using the graph of the log(− log(survival)) versus the log of survival time, and the assumption was satisfied (lines in the plots were parallel). The first quartile of CHDI scores served as the reference group and HR with 95% CIs were reported for model 1 (adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, physical activity, and energy intake), and model 2 (adjusted for model 1 and education status, marital status, occupation status, baseline values of FPG, WC, BP, and TGs/HDL-C). Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). All VIF values were below 2.0, confirming no multicollinearity concerns in the Cox regression models. Complete-case analysis was performed due to minimal missing data (< 4% for all covariates), with no imputation required.

Statistical significance for all tests was established with p-values < 0.05.

Results

The mean ± SD for age and BMI of participants (40.4% men) were 36.2 ± 12.8 years and 25.5 ± 4.28 kg/m2, respectively. Median (25th to 75th interquartile range) of CHDI score in the overall population was 71.0 (63.3–78.8). The incidence of MetS was 35.7% (n = 1650) after a mean of 9.33 years of follow-up.

The baseline general characteristics, dietary intakes, anthropometric, and clinical data of the study population according to quartiles of CHDI score are shown in Table 1. Participants in the highest quartiles of CHDI score were more likely to be older, female, married, have higher FPG and TC level, have higher SBP, academic education and have higher intake of carbohydrate, protein and dietary fiber, however, they have lower smokers, have lower physical activity and dietary intake of fat and sodium compared to individuals in the lowest quartiles of CHDI score (P-trend < 0.05).

Table 3 reports the HR (95% CI) of MetS across quartiles and per 1 SD of CHDI score. After further adjustment for smoking, physical activity, and energy intake in Model 1, the risk of MetS decreased across quartiles of CHDI score (HR = 0.75; 95%CI: 0.64–0.86, P-trend < 0.001). Also, after further adjustment for education status, marital status, occupation status, baseline values of FPG, WC, BP, and TGs/HDL-C ratio in model 2, the relationship between CHDI score and MetS risk remained significant. We found that the risk of MetS reduced by 20% across the quartile of CHDI score (HR = 0.80; 95%CI: 0.70–0.93, P-trend = 0.003). Also, these findings shows that there is protective relationship between CHDI score and risk of MetS per 1 SD increased in the CHDI score in Model 1 (HR = 0.89; 95%CI: 0.85–0.93, P-trend < 0.001) and Model 2 (HR = 0.92; 95%CI: 0.88–0.97, P-trend = 0.003).

Discussion

The current prospective cohort study with a mean follow-up of 9.33 years revealed that the risk of MetS decreased by 20% across the quartile of CHDI score, after adjusting for potential confounding factors, including age, sex, smoking, physical activity, energy intake, education status, marital status, occupation status, baseline values of FPG, WC, BP, and TGs/HDL-C ratio.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the association between the CHDI and MetS risk, demonstrating that a higher CHDI score is significantly associated with a reduced risk of MetS. Our findings contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting the role of diet in metabolic health and suggest that the CHDI as a dietary index specifically designed to assess cardiovascular health may also serve as a useful tool for MetS prevention. The CHDI score is a novel diet quality index, and just one study has examined the association of this index with health outcomes [19]; Cacau et al. study revealed that no relationship between CHDI score and incidence of coronary artery calcification was observed after a 4-year follow-up period, however, they showed a protective relationship between CHDI score and subclinical atherosclerosis after an 8-year follow-up period [19]. Although no prior studies have directly assessed the relationship between CHDI and MetS risk, our results align with research on other dietary indices, such as DASH diet [14], MDS [15], macronutrients quality indices [16], all of which have been inversely associated with MetS risk. Also, some studies investigated the relationship between a similar index to CHDI, the HEI that were developed based on the recommendations of the Dietary Guidelines for Americans and the risk of MetS. In line with the present study, Saraf-Bank et al. revealed that greater HEI score was inversely associated with MetS odds among Iranian women [24]. Also, in another cross-sectional study conducted among the Iranian population found that greater HEI score were associated with reduced odds of MetS [17]. However, in contrast with our results, a cross-sectional study conducted on 2326 participants reported that there is no significant association between HEI scores and odds of MetS [25].

As previously mentioned, our findings indicated that a greater CHDI score had a protective effect on MetS risk. This protective role may be explained through some biological mechanisms. This index recommends a higher intake of fruits, vegetables, nuts, whole grain cereals, beans, dairy products, and fish-seafood; however, it recommends a reduced intake of red meat, processed meats, sugar-sweetened beverages, and ultra-processed foods. Most components of CHDI including fruits, vegetables, nuts, whole grain cereals and beans are rich in antioxidant nutrients such as carotenoids, vitamin C, selenium, manganese, and flavonoids [26] which may justified the protective effects of the CHDI, as they are believed to have potential effect in preventing the MetS [27], through neutralized mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, cell apoptosis, protein and DNA damage induced by reactive oxygen species (ROS) [28]. Another possible mechanism of the protective effect of CHDI on MetS is the high fiber intake. Previous studies revealed that high fiber consumption through improves gut microbial composition, increases levels of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), increases insulin sensitivity, reduces the appetite and energy intake contributes to improving the metabolic profile, such as WC [29], TG [30], BP [31], and hyperglycemia [32]. It is reported that high dairy product intake might have beneficial effects on MetS and its components [33, 34]. It seems calcium in dairy products, through inhibition of fat absorption by forming insoluble soaps (combines with bile acids), reducing fatty acid synthase, and inducing lipolytic activity, contributes to MetS risk reduction [34]. Furthermore, a systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that higher intake of fish via the effect of omega-3 on inhibiting TGs synthesis, improving insulin sensitivity, and reducing the level of angiotensin-converting enzyme in the aorta, as well as the effect of fish protein on increasing HDL-c level, may reduce the MetS risk and its components [35]. Gou et al. indicated that higher intake of red meat and processed meat is associated with MetS risk. Saturated fatty acids and Iron are major compounds in red meat, and their excess intakes increase weight gain and induce oxidative stress may contribute to the etiology of MetS, respectively [36]. Higher intake of ultra-processed foods, as one of the CHDI prohibited components, leads to excessive energy intake and high intake of sodium, fructose, saturated fatty acids, trans fatty acids, and added sugar that, via inducing oxidative stress, inflammation, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia, contribute to increased risk of MetS and its components [37]. Also, sugar-sweetened beverages, through inducing weight gain, hyperglycemia, and dyslipidemia, might be associated with the risk of MetS and its components [38]. An overview of the literature shows that higher intakes of fruits, vegetables, nuts, whole grain cereals, beans, dairy products, and fish-seafood, as well as lower intakes of red meat, processed meats, sugar-sweetened beverages, and ultra-processed foods, individually can reduce the risk of MetS. It seems that their cumulative and synergistic effects in the form of CHDI could also predict the MetS risk reduction.

Our population-based prospective cohort study has several notable strengths. First, to the best of our knowledge, this study provides the first evidence examining the association between the CHDI score and the risk of MetS. Second, the prospective nature of the present study could reduce selection bias and recall bias, which are commonly found in other observational designs. Third, the confounding factors were controlled as far as possible. Furthermore several methodological strengths enhance study reliability including long follow-up minimizes reverse causation, large sample enhances power, validated FFQ/MAQ reduce exposure misclassification, high retention limits selection bias, and repeated MetS assessments decrease outcome misclassification beyond typical dietary studies. There are some limitations related to this study. First, the use of questionnaires for retrospective assessment of dietary intakes and physical activity may introduce measurement errors and recall bias, however, we mitigated these limitations by employing validated instruments specifically adapted for the Iranian population. Second, Nutrient and energy intake were assessed using the USDA FCT as the Iranian FCT lacks complete nutritional data. Also, alcohol consumption may play a role as a confounding variable in the analysis related to the CHDI-MetS relationship, however, considering the prohibition issue on the intake of alcoholic beverages in Iran because of religious-cultural beliefs and legal restrictions, it is not possible to record individuals’ data for alcohol intake. Although we made every effort to adjust for all potential confounding variables, residual confounding may persist due to currently unidentified variables in this emerging research area, and practical limitations in measuring certain factors.

Conclusions

This prospective cohort study utilizing TLGS data revealed that a greater adherence to a diet with a higher CHDI score may be related to a lower risk of MetS. Further observational studies, especially prospective studies, with larger sample sizes among different populations, are recommended to elucidate the possible protective role of the CHDI score in predicting the risk of MetS.
