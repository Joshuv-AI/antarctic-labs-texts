An early protective effect of IL-9 in murine systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Maya S Krishnan, Baohua Zhou, Dongming Yang, Joao I Goncalves, Kaitlyn G Jackson, Gloria R Xue, Matthew J Turner, Mark H Kaplan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a progressive antibody-mediated autoimmune disease characterized by systemic immune complex deposition. A subset of SLE patients has elevated CD4+IL-9+ T cells as well as increased levels of secreted interleukin (IL)-9 and IL9 messenger RNA compared with healthy control subjects. However, because IL-9 can have both pro- and anti-inflammatory effects in autoimmune disease, its function in SLE is unclear. We use the MRL/lpr murine model of SLE to demonstrate that IL-9 exhibits protective activity in the early stages of disease. Treatment of these mice with an IL-9 neutralizing antibody from 6 to 12 wk of age results in an expansion of immune cells, leading to exacerbation of disease. In contrast, treatment with anti-IL-9 from 6 to 18 wk of age does not significantly alter disease course compared with isotype control. Anti-IL-9 antibody treatment of these mice results in reduced systemic IL-2 levels, IL-9+ type 2 innate lymphoid cells, and regulatory T cells in the kidney, suggesting an IL-9–dependent suppressive cellular circuit similar to that observed in rheumatoid arthritis. Importantly, supplementation of IL-2 during IL-9 blockade recovers regulatory T cell numbers and limits disease. Together, these data demonstrate an IL-9–dependent suppressive circuit that is evident early in the development of SLE which may be amenable to manipulation to achieve a therapeutic benefit.Keywords: IL-9, ILC2s, SLE, Treg cells

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an antibody (Ab)-mediated autoimmune disease characterized by systemic immune complex deposition with widespread effects on the skin, joints, kidneys, and brain.1 In patients, SLE diagnosis involves clinical symptoms and quantitative factors including antinuclear antibodies, most reliably anti-double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) Ab.1–3 The incidence rate is 5 times higher in African Americans compared with Caucasians and 9 times higher in women compared with men.4–6 While the diagnostic tools and therapeutic options for patients have improved with the advent of biologic immunotherapies, a detailed understanding of cytokine and cellular circuits during disease progression is still lacking.7–10

IL-9 has complex effects on inflammatory disease. In many disease models, IL-9 promotes recruitment of immune cells by inducing chemokine production and modifying cellular function. In models of asthma, IL-9 production from T cells is proinflammatory and promotes the migration, activation, and expansion of mast cells, eosinophils, monocytes, and interstitial macrophages in the lung.11,12 In models of autoimmunity such as experimental autoimmune encephalitis (EAE), a model of multiple sclerosis, IL-9 was implicated in regulatory T (Treg) cell function, regulation of mast cell recruitment, and T helper 17 (Th17) differentiation.13 Similarly, in models of colitis, IL-9 from T cells is proinflammatory and contributes to disease pathogenesis.14

In some models, however, IL-9 can be anti-inflammatory. In a murine model of rheumatoid arthritis (RA), IL-9–secreting type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) were able to reduce joint swelling and induce Treg cell–mediated immune suppression to foster disease resolution.15 Treg cells are critical mediators of immune tolerance and there are a multitude of diseases that ensue upon Treg cell dysfunction.16,17 The specific contributions of IL-9 signaling in controlling Tregs or ILCs in models of SLE have not been established.

There is some evidence in a cohort of SLE patients of increased serum IL-9 and IL9 messenger RNA levels as well as an increase in CD4+ IL-9+ cells that associate with increased disease activity.18,19 In cutaneous lupus erythematosus patients, IL-9 levels were higher in the skin compared with blood and higher in the cutaneous lupus erythematosus patients who progressed to SLE.20 IL-9 is highly pleiotropic and exhibits pro- and anti-inflammatory functions in the context of autoimmunity.21 In the lupus prone mouse model, MRL/lpr,22 Th9 expansion was observed along with increased germinal center (GC) formation upon infiltration of IL-9+ lymphocytes in the spleen. Further, a neutralizing Ab to IL-9 used in 3-mo-old mice for an additional 3 mo was shown to marginally reduce anti-dsDNA Abs.23 Yet, a more detailed functional analysis of IL-9 in the early stages of SLE-like disease has not been performed.

We investigated the role of IL-9 using the MRL/lpr model at early stages, which rapidly manifests several aspects of human SLE disease. We treated 6-wk-old mice to assess the effect of IL-9 neutralization. We show that acute treatment with anti-IL-9 Ab exacerbates SLE disease during early time points, promotes the expansion of proinflammatory cells, and depletes IL-9+ILC2s and Treg cells. Furthermore, we show that these effects can be reversed by administration of IL-2, which suggests a nexus between IL-9 signaling and IL-2-mediated control of Tregs in the context of SLE. Collectively, these data provide novel insight to the role of IL-9 during the initiation phase of SLE, information that may be leveraged for future therapies.

Methods

Murine models

MRL/MpJ-Faslpr/J (MRL/lpr; Strain #: 000485) mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. All mice were maintained in specific pathogen free animal facilities (ambient room temperature 70–72 °F, humidity 50%, 12 h light/dark cycle). The University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approved all experiments.

In vivo anti-IL-9 neutralizing Ab treatment

Mice were injected subcutaneously with anti-IL-9 (100 μg/mouse; Bio X Cell; Catalog #9C1) or IgG2a isotype control (100 μg/mouse; Bio X Cell; Catalog #BP0088) once per week for 6 or 12 wk of treatment.

Tissue processing

Mice were intraperitoneally injected with ketamine followed by cervical dislocation. Blood serum and urine were collected from each mouse. Each mouse was perfused with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Both kidneys were removed and decapsulated. Half of a kidney and rostrum was fixed in 10% formalin, and the other 1.5 kidneys were further processed as tissue lysates. In brief, kidneys were cut into small pieces and incubated in digestion buffer (collagenase A and DNase I) at 37 °C for 45 min. RBC lysis (ACK Lysis Buffer; Catalog #118-156-721) was performed for 5 to 10 min followed by 40%/80% Percoll density centrifugation. The spleen and lymph node were pushed through 70 µm cell strainer and red blood cells in the spleen samples were lysed with RBC lysis buffer. Cells were counted, plated in a round-bottom 96-well plate, and stimulated with PMA and ionomycin for 3 h, and monensin was added for another 3 h before staining for flow cytometry.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays

Anti-dsDNA Ab enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

A 96-well plate with high-affinity binding was coated with 10 μg/mL of Herring sperm dsDNA antigen in carbonate/bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.6) and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The plate was washed before blocking buffer was added (1% bovine serum albumin in 1× PBS) for 90 min at 37 °C. The plate was washed 1× with wash buffer (0.05% Tween in 1× PBS) before the serum samples were added for 90 min at 37 °C. The plate was washed before incubation for 90 min 37 °C in detection Ab. The plate was washed and incubated in streptavidin HRP (Invitrogen; Catalog #N100) for 90 min at 37 °C. The plate was washed with 1× PBS before TMB solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Catalog #34028) was added for 5 to 15 min before 2 N H2SO4 acid stop solution was applied and the absorbance was read on a plate reader at 450 nm.

Creatinine ELISA

Urine was collected and creatinine levels were assessed using the Ethos Biosciences Creatinine kit (Catalog #NC9129340) per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Albumin ELISA

Urine was collected and albumin levels were assessed using the Ethos Biosciences Albuwell kit (Catalog #NC9182134) per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Rostrum skin was either flash frozen with liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C or cut into smaller pieces and kept in RNA later solution (Invitrogen; Catalog #AM7020). The tissue was homogenized in TRIzol and RNA was extracted with the Qiagen RNeasy Isolation kit (Catalog #74104). The concentration of RNA was assessed for each sample using the Nanodrop. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was made using qScript synthesis kit with reverse transcriptase and qScript Mix. At least 2,000 ng of RNA was used for each sample, nuclease-free water, reverse transcriptase, and qScript mix and followed a standard thermal cycle for optimal cDNA synthesis (22 °C for 5 min, 42 °C for 30 min, 85 °C for 5 min, 4 °C indefinitely). The QuantBio 3 cycle system was used for quantitative polymerase chain reaction in a 96-well plate, with cDNA, TaqMan Fast Master Mix (Catalog #4444557), nuclease-free water, and Thermo Fisher Scientific TaqMan primers.

Flow cytometry

Following tissue processing and stimulation with PMA, ionomycin, and monensin, single-cell suspensions were stained with fixable viability dye and surface marker. After surface staining, cells were fixed in 1% paraformaldehyde for 10 min in the dark at room temperature or the Fix/Perm Buffer (eBioscience) for 1 to 15 h at 4 °C. After fixation, cells were permeabilized (eBioscience) for 30 min to 1 h before intracellular cytokine or transcription factor staining for 1 h at 4 °C. After intracellular staining, cells were washed with FACS buffer and analyzed with Attune NxT flow cytometer or the Cytek Aurora provided by the flow core at the Indiana University School of Medicine. The data was analyzed with FlowJo 10.8.1 (TreeStar). The flow antibodies used are displayed in Table S1.

Histology

Kidney and rostral tissues were fixed with 10% formalin for 24 h at room temperature before they were moved to a 70% ethanol solution at room temperature. The Indiana University Histology Core then embedded these tissues in paraffin and sectioned and stained them with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Rostrum sections were also stained with acidified toluidine blue. Images were scored in a blinded, randomized way to collect semi-quantitative scores. For kidney sections, a score of 0 denotes no inflammatory infiltrate and no irregular shaping of the glomerulus; a score of 1 denotes mild (1%–20%) infiltrate or hypercellularity and slightly irregular glomerular borders, 2 denotes moderate (20%–50%) infiltrate, decreased Bowman’s capsule space, and mesangial expansion; and a score of 3 indicates severe (>50%) infiltrate, mesangial expansion, presence of the crescent, sloughing of the basement membrane, and complete loss of structural integrity. For skin sections, a score of 0 denotes no inflammatory infiltrate in the dermal or epithelial layer and no thickening of the epithelium; a score of 1 denotes mild (1%–10%) inflammatory infiltrate and mild thickening of the dermal or epithelial layer; a score of 2 denotes moderate (10%–30%) infiltrate and increased thickening of the dermal/epithelial layer; and a score of 3 denotes severe (>30%) infiltrate with significant thickening of the dermal and epithelial layer.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism V_9.4.1 (GraphPad Software). Data were presented as mean ± SEM. Unpaired or paired Student t tests, 1-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post hoc corrections, or Mantel-Cox log rank test were used for data analysis. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

SLE-like symptom exacerbation in response to acute anti-IL-9 Ab treatment

In both human SLE and murine models of SLE, serum IL-9 concentrations and CD4+IL-9+ cell percentages increase in the spleen with progressive SLE development.19 To evaluate the effect of systemic IL-9 depletion during disease initiation in murine SLE, we treated 6-wk-old female MRL/lpr mice with 100 µg of anti-IL-9 Ab or IgG2a isotype subcutaneously for 6 wk (Fig. 1A). The brachial lymph node, axillary lymph node, and inguinal lymph node as well as the spleen were visually enlarged in the anti-IL-9 group compared with the isotype-treated mice (Fig. 1B) and remained quantitatively larger when corrected for body weight (Fig. 1D, E). Anti-dsDNA Ab levels were significantly higher in the anti-IL-9 group compared with the isotype group (Fig. 1C). This suggests that there was not only an increase in inflammation in the anti-IL-9–treated mice, but also lupus-like disease exacerbation.

Anti-IL-9–treated SLE mice also developed a cutaneous phenotype. We noticed an increase in hair loss, erythema, and whisker loss on the rostrum of mice from the anti-IL-9 Ab–treated group compared with the isotype control group (Fig. 1F–H). The representative H&E images of the rostrum show a thickened epidermal layer and increased dermal infiltrate in the anti-IL-9–treated mice (Fig. 1H). To further evaluate the effect of anti-IL-9 treatment on lupus-like disease, we performed H&E staining of kidney sections to assess incidence of lupus nephritis. Representative images are shown along with blinded, semi-quantitative scoring (Fig. 1I). The H&E stains revealed an increase in mesangial cellularity, cellular crescent formation, increased tubulointerstitial cellularity, decreased Bowman’s capsule space, and loss of structural integrity in the glomerulus. We also observed thickened glomerular walls, immune deposits in the extraglomerular space, and increased sclerosis in the glomerulus via periodic acid–Schiff staining (Fig. S1A). These qualitative and semi-quantitative findings were confirmed by an increase in the albumin-creatinine ratio and concentration of albumin in urine or proteinuria in the anti-IL-9 Ab–treated mice (Fig. 1J). Together, these data suggest that anti-IL-9 treatment exacerbated hallmarks of SLE-like disease and that IL-9 is protective early in disease.

IL-9 controls systemic immune cell populations and cytokine concentrations

Given the exacerbation of splenomegaly and lymphadenopathy in the anti-IL-9 treatment group, we determined changes in GC B cell and follicular helper T (Tfh) cell populations in the secondary lymphoid tissues using flow cytometry. Gating on live singlets, we identified Tfh cells as CD4+Foxp3–PD-1+CXCR5+ and follicular regulatory T (Tfr) cells as CD4+Foxp3+PD-1+CXCR5+ (Fig. 2A) and GC B cells as B220+GL7+CD38– cells (Fig. 2B). Tfh, Tfr, and GC B cell populations were significantly increased in spleen and lymph nodes following anti-IL-9 treatment (Fig. 2C, D; Fig. S1B). To further investigate the potential proinflammatory mechanisms in the anti-IL-9 treatment group, we profiled Th subsets in the spleen. Interestingly, there was an expansion in CD4+IFNγ+, CD4+IL-17+, and CD4+IL-9+ cells in response to IL-9 neutralization (Fig. 2E).

The protective effect of IL-9 is restricted to early phase disease

To build on these data and assess longer-term effects of IL-9 depletion, we extended the treatment for an additional 6 wk for a 12-wk period (Fig. S2A). When we extended the duration of treatment from 6 wk to 12 wk, we no longer observed disease exacerbation in the anti-IL-9 treatment group compared with the isotype control (Fig. S2B). The splenomegaly and lymphadenopathy were heterogeneous among biological replicates, and overall, there were no significant differences between groups in cell number (not shown) or weight of spleen or lymph node (Fig. S2C). We profiled the cellular compartments of the spleen and found no differences in the frequency of Tfh or GC B cells of the spleen (Fig. S2D, E). We found no difference in CD4+IL-9+ T cells in the spleen or lymph node, and we found a trending decrease in IL-9 levels in serum of anti-IL-9–treated mice (Fig. S2F, G) after 12 wk of treatment. Despite no significant differences in CD4+IL-13+ T cells, we show here a significant decrease in the concentration of IL-13 after 12 wk of anti-IL-9 treatment (Fig. S2H, I). No significant differences were found in serum levels of tumor necrosis factor α, interferon γ (IFN-γ), IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-22, IL-5, or IL-4 (Fig. S2J–L).

Anti-IL-9 treatment increases cutaneous mast cells and decreases Treg signature in rostrum skin

To further investigate the cutaneous lupus-like phenotype, we stained tissue sections with acidified toluidine blue for mast cells. In the representative images, arrowheads point to the mast cells, which were quantified across 6 different regions of interest and presented as an average mast cell number per region of interest (Fig. 3A). We also assessed the expression of genes associated with mast cell function including mast cell protease 5 (Mcpt5), a chymase that is specific to connective tissue mast cells,24 Fcer1a (encoding the high-affinity IgE receptor),25 and the mast cell growth factor receptor Kit,26,27 all of which were increased in the anti-IL-9 treatment group compared with the isotype control (Fig. 3B). In addition to mast cell expansion, we observed a decrease in gene signatures associated with Treg cells in the rostrum, Foxp3 and Cd4 (Fig. 3C).

Similar to the observation in the rostral skin, we found a significant decrease in the frequency of Treg cells in the spleen, lymph node, and kidney after 6 wk of anti-IL-9 Ab treatment (Fig. 4A, B). We found a significant and dramatic decrease in IL-2 concentrations in the serum of anti-IL-9–treated mice at both 6 and 12 wk (Fig. 4C) even though Treg cells were not significantly different after 12 wk (data not shown). After 12 wk of anti-IL-9 Ab treatment, the concentration of IL-2 is depleted by almost 50% as well, which suggests that IL-2 levels decline as disease progresses in SLE. Interestingly, this decline does not impact disease progression after 12 wk of IL-9 depletion as it did earlier in disease progression (Fig. S2). As Treg cells require exogenous IL-228 and IL-2 control of Tregs impacts disease in lupus models,29,30 these data suggest that the loss of an IL-2/Treg cell pathway may contribute to the proinflammatory phenotype following early anti-IL-9 treatment.

Anti-IL-9 limits ILC2 expansion and alters the pattern of cytokine production

Previous reports with an arthritis model suggested that loss of Tregs were linked to IL-9 production by ILCs.15 To identify ILC2s in the kidney, we eliminated doublets, gated on Live CD45+, Lin− cells, and analyzed KLRG1+, Sca-1+, CD127+ and ST2+ cells (Fig. 5A). After 6 wk or 12 wk of treatment with anti-IL-9 Ab, we observed a significant decrease in the ILC2s (Fig. 5B). At 6 wk, there was a significant decrease in the percentages of IL-5+ and IL-13+ ILC2s in the kidney. By 12 wk of treatment, IL-5+ ILC2s were still depleted in the anti-IL-9 group; however, the proportion of IL-13+ILC2s was robustly (∼50% versus 20%) increased compared with age-matched isotype control–treated mice. IL-13 is associated with increased fibrosis, which may point to exacerbated fibrosis associated with progressive glomerulonephritis in chronic SLE-like disease.31 Most interestingly, we observed a significant decrease in the IL-9–secreting ILC2s at both 6 and 12 wk (Fig. 5C).

IL-2 partially rescues the protective effect lost by IL-9 depletion in murine SLE

The persistent and significant depletion in serum IL-2 could explain the sustained depletion of ILC2s after 6 and 12 wk of treatment, and diminished Treg cells at the early time point. To investigate the role of IL-2 in the changes observed following IL-9 blockade, we modified our original experimental design by treating with an IL-2/anti-IL-2 complex (IL-2C) twice per week that extends the half-life of IL-2 delivery in vivo32 after 3 wk of IL-9 depletion (Fig. 6A). The significant lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly in the anti-IL-9 treatment group was mitigated by the addition of IL-2C (Fig. 6B). This qualitative evidence was further validated by a significant decrease in total cell numbers in the lymph node and spleen, as well as a decreased lymph node- and spleen-to-body weight ratio in mice receiving combined anti‑IL‑9 and IL‑2C treatment compared with those receiving anti‑IL‑9 alone. (Fig. 6C). Autoantibody levels were also decreased in the group receiving anti-IL-9 and IL-2C which suggests attenuation of SLE-like disease (Fig. 6D). Reduced autoantibody titers corresponded to the ability of IL-2 to inhibit Tfh cell differentiation, and there was a reduced frequency of Tfh cells in spleens of isotype and anti-IL-9–treated groups in which IL-2C was co-administered, compared with groups treated with isotype or anti-IL-9 Ab alone (Fig. 6E). GC B cells were likewise significantly decreased in the IL-2 supplemented anti-IL-9 group (Fig. 6E). Interestingly, Treg cell percentages in IL-9–depleted mice were rescued with the addition of IL-2C, but in the isotype treated mice, the addition of IL-2 did not further expand Treg cells (Fig. 6F). In contrast to Tregs, addition of IL-2C did not change the ILC2 population in isotype-treated mice, and although anti-IL-9 treatment significantly reduced ILC2 population, IL-2C failed to rescue IL-9 neutralization induced reduction of ILC2s in the kidney. The classic ILC2 cytokines including IL-5 and IL-13 were increased in the anti-IL-9 plus IL-2C group, which suggests that overall the ILC2s were more activated (Fig. S3A). Most interestingly, the population of IL-9–secreting ILC2s, which were decreased in the anti-IL-9–treated group, was restored in the anti-IL-9 plus IL-2C group to levels comparable to isotype controls, suggesting that IL-2C can rescue this protective population of IL-9+ ILC2s in the absence of IL-9 (Fig. 6F; Fig. S3A). Together, these data support a model in which IL-9 production early in the development of SLE-like disease increases IL-9–secreting ILC2s and Treg cells at least partially by promoting IL-2 production in vivo.

Discussion

Our data show a systemic, proinflammatory effect of anti-IL-9 Ab treatment in 6-wk-old MRL/lpr mice treated for 6 wk, although the effect IL-9 blockade is lost over time, as disease in the isotype-treated MRL/lpr control mice becomes more severe with time. At earlier time points, there is an expansion of protective ILC2s and a population of IL-9+ ILC2s. Our data are consistent with previous reports of IL-9–secreting ILC2s that foster Treg cell expansion and mitigate Ab-mediated autoimmunity.15 We further observe a dramatic decrease of serum IL-2 concentrations in anti-IL-9–treated mice. Importantly, supplementation of IL-2 to mice treated with anti-IL-9 results in a reduction of disease parameters to control levels. These results suggest that IL-9 signaling is required to maintain IL-2–dependent regulatory circuits in autoimmunity.

IL-2 has pleiotropic effects in immune regulation. It can directly impair Tfh differentiation, reducing GC formation GC B cell numbers, and Ab production.33 It also decreases Th17 differentiation (at least in mice)33 but promotes Th9 differentiation and stimulates IL-9 production from ILCs.34 IL-2 also maintains Treg cells in vivo that could be responsible for several of the secondary effects including cytokine-producing Th cells and mast cell expansion in the skin that is potentially linked to hair and whisker loss.35,36 Mast cells have been implicated in patients with acute telogen effluvium, a nonscarring alopecia that occurs after a stressful event, and alopecia areata, an autoimmune disease characterized by hair loss.37,38

Interestingly, and despite the depletion of IL-2, there is a significant increase in proinflammatory Th subsets such as CD4+ T cells that secrete IL-9, IL-17, and IFN-γ. In both a lung infection and lupus nephritis model, IFN-γ acted as a negative regulator of ILC2s.39,40 In ex vivo cultures of IL-33 activated ILC2s isolated from MRL/lpr kidney, IFN-γ neutralized any activation, expansion, and cytokine production induced by IL-33. It has also been shown that SLE patients have increased levels of IL-17 and expanded CD4+IL-17+ T cells compared with healthy control subjects.41 Last, the expansion in IL-9–secreting CD4+ T cells supports previous data in models of murine and active human SLE disease.19,23 These factors ultimately synergize to favor a proinflammatory phenotype with more Th9 cell expansion over ILC2 activation and expansion.

The source of IL-2 in vivo that is impacted by IL-9 blockade is still unknown. IL-9 can act on T cells, and it is possible IL-9 regulates the proportions of IL-2–secreting T cells. It is also possible IL-9 acts on other cells to promote IL-2 production. Based on murine models of lung inflammation, IL-9 can induce IL-2 secretion from mast cells, which can activate both Th9 cells and ILC2s.42,43 Finally, it is possible that IL-9 acts indirectly to control systemic IL-2 concentrations. Future studies that track the IL-2–producing cells during disease development will be required to distinguish these possibilities.

ILCs are stimulated by many cytokines including IL-2 and IL-33. Damaged epithelial cells secrete IL-33 and in the MRL/lpr model, IL-33 can activate resting ILC2s in the kidney to resolve inflammation.39 However, it is not known if IL-33 promotes an IL-9+ ILC2 population in lupus nephritis. The role of IL-33 in the context of SLE may also be context dependent. In one study, IL-33 treatment of MRL/lpr mice reduced glomerular immune infiltrates and ameliorated lupus nephritis.39 In another study, 6-wk neutralization of IL-33 in MRL/lpr mice resulted in decreased proteinuria, decreased serum anti-dsDNA Abs, and decreased mortality.44 Further studies may better define if IL-33 impacts suppressive function of ILCs in this or other models and its influence on IL-9+ ILC2’s.

Control of the ILC population is likely in situ. Parabiosis experiments have demonstrated that ILC2s are predominantly tissue-resident cells with self-renewal capacity.45–47 These findings were maintained in a Foxp3DTR parabiont wherein human diphtheria toxin receptor engagement in Treg cells induced systemic autoimmunity. Parabiotic mice had no hematogenous contributions to ILCs in Treg-depleted lymphoid and nonlymphoid organs.45 Based on these data, it is likely that ILC2s in the MRL/lpr model are acutely triggered by the inflammatory milieu of the nephritic kidney tissue in the isotype-treated mice; loss of IL-9 is associated with a loss of the ILC2 population. We show here that the addition of IL-2 in the context of anti-IL-9 rescues Treg cells, re-expands IL-9–secreting renal ILC2s, and attenuates SLE-like disease severity. While it is unclear if an IL-9/ILC2/Treg circuit is active in lupus, Treg cells influence disease activity in SLE models.48–50 Moreover, exacerbation of lupus nephritis is associated with decreased ILC2s.39

It may also be possible that Treg cells display positive feedback upon ILC2 engagement and can induce the ILC2 skewing to promote IL-9 secretion and further augment the suppressive capacity of Treg cells.

IL-9 has complex effects in autoimmunity. Previous work suggested that IL-9 blockade in later stages of an SLE-like disease model resulted in modest decreases in anti-dsDNA targeted antibodies.23 In contrast, our study of early IL-9 blockade resulted in disease exacerbation and an increase in these antibodies. We found evidence of altered populations of cells that are involved in an IL-9–dependent suppressive circuit identified in rheumatoid arthritis models and patients.15 Our work suggests that an ILC2/IL-9/Treg pathway limits SLE-like symptoms early during disease progression. The focused time window for these effects might be due to IL-9–dependent Treg activity being overwhelmed as inflammation worsens over time. Importantly, these observations provide a basis for studies in patients where this pathway might have therapeutic potential.
