Association between low-dose trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole blood concentration and clinical outcomes in non-HIV patients with Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ziwei Deng, Wei Fu, Feng Chen, Yuanxiang Jin, Qingxia Shu, Xiang Zhao, Jinque Luo, Yueping Jiang, Chengfeng Qiu, Liming Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTrimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (SMX-TMP) represents the first-line treatment for non-human immunodeficiency virus (non-HIV) patients with Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP). However, the relationship between SMX peak serum concentrations (Cmax) and clinical outcomes of PJP is not clear yet. This study aim to investigate the impact of initial Cmax of SMX on clinical outcomes non-HIV patients with PJP who were predominantly treated with low-dose SMX-TMP, and to evaluate related adverse drug reactions (ADRs), providing evidence for optimal clinical medication strategies.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 66 non-HIV patients with PJP who received SMX-TMP treatment and had SMX Cmax monitoring results at Hunan University of Medicine General Hospital from January 2022 to December 2024. Patients were divided into high-concentration (SMX Cmax >100 µg/mL) and low-concentration (SMX Cmax ≤100 µg/mL) groups. Statistical analyses were performed to compare clinical improvement rates, 28-day mortality rates, and ADR incidence between the two groups. Factors influencing SMX Cmax, including daily dose and glomerular filtration rate (GFR), were also analyzed.ResultsThe median SMX Cmax was 108 (78.6–151.0) µg/mL among the 66 patients, with 37 patients (56.66%) achieving concentrations above 100 µg/mL. The overall patients received SMX median daily dose was 49.2 (40.0, 63.2) mg/kg, in the group of patients with high concentration, the median daily dose was 56.1 (43.6, 66.7) mg/kg. All these values are lower than the daily dose of 75–100 mg/kg recommended by the guidelines. Only 7 patients (10.61%) meeting guideline recommendations dosage. Compared with the low-concentration group, the high-concentration group showed significantly higher improvement rates (83.8% vs. 55.2%, P = 0.011) and significantly lower 28-day mortality rates (2.7% vs. 20.7%, P = 0.038). Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified SMX Cmax as an independent predictor of patient improvement (OR = 4.2, 95% CI: 1.34–13.13). Subgroup analyses revealed no significant interactions with grouping factor and SMX concentrations. Correlation analysis demonstrated a moderate positive correlation between SMX daily dose and Cmax (r = 0.458, P < 0.01), while GFR showed no significant correlation with blood concentrations. When stratified by SMX Cmax >150 µg/mL, no significant differences were observed in ADRs including rash, gastrointestinal discomfort, hepatic/renal dysfunction, thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, or hyperkalemia between concentration groups, absolute ADRs incidence rates in the high-concentration group were generally higher than in the low-concentration group.ConclusionIn non-HIV PJP patients predominantly receiving low-dose SMX-TMP therapy, SMX Cmax >100 µg/mL was associated with improved clinical outcomes. This study observed that most patients did not achieve guideline-recommended dosing, yet many still attained therapeutic SMX concentrations, suggesting that weight-based standard dosing alone may be insufficient for Chinese populations. Routine therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) of SMX should be implemented in clinical practice to achieve individualized precision dosing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-12952-0.

Introduction

Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) is an acute [1, 2], life-threatening opportunistic infection that primarily affects immunocompromised individuals, such as patients with malignancies, organ transplant recipients, or those receiving immunosuppressive therapy. Although historically associated with human immunodeficiency virus/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (HIV/AIDS) epidemics, non-HIV immunosuppressed patients experience more severe PJP with more rapid onset. The mortality rates for non-HIV PJP and HIV-PJP are 30–60% and 10–20% [3, 4], respectively. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (SMX-TMP) is a fixed-dose combination (5:1 ratio) of sulfamethoxazole (SMX) and trimethoprim (TMP), and represents the first-line treatment for mild, moderate, and severe PJP [5]. Regarding the therapeutic concentration of SMX-TMP, existing studies and guidelines show some controversy. The European Conference on Infections in Leukemia (ECIL) guidelines mention that when treating PJP infection, optimal efficacy can be achieved when SMX concentrations reach 100–150 µg/mL and TMP concentrations reach 3–5 µg/mL [6]. A detailed compilation of therapeutic and toxic plasma concentrations for over 1100 drugs published in Critical Care in 2020 recommends an SMX Cmax of 100–200 µg/mL for PJP treatment [7] (see Additional File 1 of [7]). Additionally, two retrospective cohort studies [8, 9] of critically ill PJP patients (including 60 and 109 patients, respectively) both confirmed that SMX-TMP Cmax is directly associated with treatment outcomes and ADRs-failure to achieve target SMX Cmax during treatment significantly increases treatment failure rates and 28-day all-cause mortality risk (P < 0.05).

However, in clinical practice, especially among Chinese patients, SMX-TMP daily doses are often lower than international guideline recommendations(SMX<75-100 mg/kg) [6]. Under this low-dose treatment regimen, whether therapeutic blood concentrations can be achieved and whether achievement of target concentrations remains clearly associated with clinical outcomes lacks sufficient evidence. Therefore, this study aims to clarify the correlation between therapeutic blood concentration achievement and clinical outcomes in low-dose SMX-TMP treatment through retrospective cohort analysis.

Through retrospective analysis of clinical data from 66 non-HIV PJP patients receiving SMX-TMP treatment, this study aims to explore the relationship between initial SMX Cmax and patient clinical outcomes and ADRs, analyze factors influencing blood concentrations, and provide scientific evidence for rational medication use and therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) practice in these patients.

Methods

Study participants

This retrospective cohort study included non-HIV PJP patients hospitalized at Hunan University of Medicine General Hospital from January 2022 to December 2024. All patients received SMX-TMP treatment and completed initial SMX Cmax monitoring, with 66 patients ultimately included.

Inclusion criteria: ① Pulmonary CT [10, 11]: Clinical physician assessment of pulmonary CT suggesting PJP; ② Detection of Pneumocystis jirovecii by next-generation sequencing of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid; ③ Presence of PJP-related clinical symptoms including fever (temperature > 38.0 °C), cough (dry cough or with small amounts of white sputum), and dyspnea (resting or exertional shortness of breath) [12]; ④ SMX-TMP tablet treatment duration ≥ 48 h (without interruption due to ADRs or efficacy issues) [6].

Exclusion criteria: ① Age < 18 years; ② Co-infection with HIV; ③ Prophylactic use SMX-TMP (see Fig. 1).

This study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Hunan University of Medicine General Hospital (Ethics approval number: No. KY- 2026011204). Given that this study utilizes only retrospective, fully anonymized medical data and poses no risk to the participants, the requirement for obtaining individual written informed consent was waived. All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

SMX peak concentration monitoring method

Venous blood was collected after patients received SMX-TMP for ≥ 2 days following diagnosis. Collection was controlled at 2 h after single-dose administration to ensure SMX concentration peak. Serum SMX concentrations were determined using high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry [13, 14]. Chromatography was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC I-Class system, consisting of a ACQUITY UPLC I-Class BSM (Quaternary pump), ACQUITY UPLC I-Class SM-FTN (Autosampler) and ACQUITY UPLC I-Class CH-A(Column oven) (Waters, Zellik, Belgium). Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) using a Xevo TQD (Waters, Zellik, Belgium). Based on initial SMX Cmax results, patients were divided into high-concentration (SMX Cmax >100 µg/mL) and low-concentration (SMX Cmax ≤100 µg/mL) groups using 100 µg/mL as the cutoff. For subsequent ADR analysis, a cutoff of 150 µg/mL was used to further divide patients into high-concentration (SMX Cmax ≥150 µg/mL) and low-concentration (SMX Cmax <150 µg/mL) groups.

Data collection

Data extraction was performed by trained clinical pharmacists and physicians with authorized access to the hospital’s electronic medical record (EMR) system. All extracted data were independently carried out by two researchers. Patient information was collected according to a case information collection form designed for the study protocol, including: (1) General clinical data such as age and sex, and information on underlying diseases; (2) Underlying diseases and immune status: presence of malignancies, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, hypertension, rheumatoid arthritis, and other underlying conditions; any immunosuppressive therapy for more than 4 weeks, including corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide, or biologic agents [15],(3) Laboratory results: Clinical data and laboratory test results within 24 h, including white blood cell count (WBC), platelet count (PLT), C-reactive protein (CRP), (1,3)-β-D-glucan (BDG) test, absolute lymphocyte count, NK cell count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), procalcitonin (PCT), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), initial oxygen saturation (PaO₂), and glomerular filtration rate (GFR); (4) Medication information: SMX-TMP dosing regimen (SMX daily dose calculated in mg/kg body weight), concomitant use of antifungal drugs (such as caspofungin), dose adjustments due to subtherapeutic SMX blood concentrations or ADRs; (5) Concurrent infections (fungal, bacterial, viral) were defined as those microbiologically confirmed (by culture, antigen testing, or PCR) or clinically diagnosed during the course of SMX-TMP treatment. (6) Clinical outcomes and ADRs: Patient clinical outcomes at discharge (improved/not improved, determined according to preset criteria), 28-day all-cause mortality; all suspected ADRs duFring treatment. Patients with missing SMX Cmax data or incomplete outcome information were excluded from the analysis.

Outcome measures and ADR criteria

The primary outcome measure was improvement rate. It was defined as meeting at least two of the following criteria within 14 days of treatment initiation: (1) resolution or improvement of respiratory symptoms (dyspnea, cough); (2) improvement in oxygen saturation (PaO₂ increase ≥ 10 mmHg or SpO₂ increase ≥ 5%); (3) reduction in inflammatory markers (CRP decrease ≥ 50% from baseline); (4) radiological improvement on chest imaging. Treatment failure was defined as death, need for mechanical ventilation escalation, or lack of clinical improvement after 21 days of therapy [6]. The secondary outcome measure was 28-day mortality rate. For safety, suspected ADR events with causal relationships to SMX-TMP tablets judged as “possible”, “probable” or “certain” according to China’s Administrative Measures for Adverse Drug Reaction Reporting and Monitoring were included in ADR statistics, using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed using Free Statistics v2.3 and GraphPad Prism v9.0. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Continuous variables between groups were analyzed using t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical data are presented as frequencies and proportions, with between-group categorical variables analyzed using chi-square (χ²) tests. Adjustments were made for potential confounders (age, sex, GFR, PCT, initial PaO₂, and daily medication dose age). Results were expressed as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value < 0.05.

Patients were grouped into high-concentration (SMX Cmax >100 µg/mL) and low-concentration (SMX Cmax ≤100 µg/mL) groups. Using improvement rate as the dependent variable, logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the relationship between the 2 groups with PJP improvement rates. Potential confounding variables were adjusted including age, sex, initial SMX daily dose, PCT, PaO₂, and GFR, with results presented as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Subgroup analyses were further performed to examine whether interactions existed between SMX initial Cmax and improvement rates in different subgroups. Pearson correlation analysis was used to analyze correlations between SMX daily dose, GFR, and Cmax, with correlation coefficient r indicating correlation strength (r absolute value 0.1–0.3 = weak correlation, 0.3–0.5 = moderate correlation, > 0.5 = strong correlation). Based on whether initial SMX Cmax >150 µmol/L, Fisher’s exact test was used to compare ADR occurrence between groups.

The significance level was set at α = 0.05, with two-sided P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

This study ultimately included 66 PJP patients with a median age of 68 years (59.25, 79.25), including 51 males (77.27%). The median initial SMX Cmax was 108 µmol/L (78.6, 151.0). Based on a cutoff of 100 µg/mL, patients were divided into two groups: high-concentration group (> 100 µg/mL) with 38 patients (57.58%) and low-concentration group (≤ 100 µg/mL) with 28 patients (42.42%). However, the overall patients received SMX median daily dose was 49.2 (40.0, 63.2) mg/kg, in the group of patients with high concentration, the median daily dose was 56.1 (43.6, 66.7) mg/kg. All these values were lower than the daily dose of 75–100 mg/kg recommended by the guidelines. Only 7 patients (10.61%) received SMX daily doses meeting international guideline recommendations, indicating that this study cohort generally received low-dose SMX-TMP treatment (Table 1).

Clinical outcomes

The overall improvement rate was 71.21% (47/66), with a 28-day mortality rate of 10.61% (7/66). Between-group comparisons showed that compared with the low-concentration group, the high-concentration group had significantly higher improvement rates (83.8% vs. 55.2%, P = 0.011) and significantly lower 28-day mortality rates (2.7% vs. 20.7%, P = 0.038) (Fig. 2).

Baseline data comparison revealed no significant differences between groups in most demographic characteristics, comorbidities, and laboratory indicators. However, the high-concentration group had higher baseline procalcitonin (PCT) levels [0.3 (0.1–1.4) vs. 0.1 (0.1–0.3), P = 0.004], higher initial oxygenation index (PaO₂) [103.1 (81.6-136.7) vs. 68.0 (57.5-107.9), P = 0.042], and lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (GFR) [64.2 (37.3-102.3) vs. 99.7 (77.8-127.4), P = 0.002] (Table 1).

Relationship between initial blood concentration and PJP outcomes

The relationship between SMX blood concentration and clinical improvement rate, 28-day mortality were analyzed through univariate and multivariate logistic regression (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 1). As shown in Table 1, GFR, PCT, initial PaO₂, and daily medication dose may be potential confounding factors affecting outcomes (P < 0.05 between groups). In the unadjusted model (Model 1), for every 1 µg/mL increase in SMX blood concentration, the odds of improvement increased by 1% (OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00-1.03, P = 0.024); when grouped by > 100 µg/mL, the high-concentration group had 4.2 times the odds of improvement compared to the low-concentration group (OR = 4.2, 95% CI: 1.34–13.13, P = 0.014). After sequentially adjusting for age and sex (Model 2), and for age, sex, GFR, PCT, initial PaO₂, and daily medication dose (Model 3), SMX blood concentration (whether as a continuous variable or grouped variable) remained an independent positive predictor of patient clinical improvement (all P < 0.05).

Similarly, for mortality (Supplementary Table 1), higher SMX blood concentrations were significantly associated with lower 28-day mortality. After adjusting for age and sex (Model 2), for every 1 µg/mL increase in blood concentration, mortality risk decreased by 2% (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.96-1.00, P = 0.042); the high-concentration group had only 9% the mortality risk of the low-concentration group (OR = 0.09, 95% CI: 0.01–0.88, P = 0.039).

Subgroup analysis results (Fig. 3) showed that when divided into subgroups by sex, age ≥ 60 years, normal G-test, normal absolute lymphocyte count, combination with caspofungin, and dose adjustment, the trend of better clinical outcomes with higher SMX blood concentrations (> 100 µg/mL) remained consistent. No interactions were found between any subgroups and SMX blood concentration (P > 0.05), indicating that the positive effect of SMX blood concentration on improvement rate was relatively robust across subgroups with different clinical characteristics.

Factors affecting initial SMX Cmax

Linear regression analysis explored the effects of daily medication dose and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) on SMX Cmax (Table 3). Without adjusting for covariates (Model I), higher daily doses were significantly associated with higher blood concentrations (β = 1.21, 95% CI: 0.56–1.86, P = 0.001). After stepwise adjustment for sex, age, PCT, and initial PaO₂ (Model III), this positive correlation persisted, although the effect size was somewhat attenuated (β = 1.04, 95% CI: 0.23–1.85, P = 0.019). A scatter plot of daily dose versus Cmax further confirmed a moderate positive correlation between the two (r = 0.458, P < 0.0001, Fig. 4). In contrast, the relationship between GFR and SMX blood concentration showed no statistical difference after multivariate adjustment (Model III) (β = 0.036, 95% CI: -0.22-0.93, P = 0.236).

Adverse drug reactions

A total of 37 patients experienced ADRs possibly related to SMX, accounting for 56.06%. Discussion

PJP is a serious opportunistic infection with particularly high mortality and complex clinical management challenges in non-HIV immunosuppressed patients [12]. As a first-line treatment agent, there are few research on Therapeutic Drug Monitoring (TDM) for SMX-TMP in the treatment of Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP). European working group adopts a neutral stance on the TDM of TMP-SMX. They mentioned there were insufficient clinical data to support altering co-trimoxazole dosing for maximal outcomes in critically ill patients. Weight-based dosing depending on the pathogen was appropriate [16]. In recent years, the role of SMX-TMP drug monitoring (TDM) in individualized medication has been attracting increasing attention [8, 9]. Through retrospective data analysis, this study confirmed a significant correlation between achievement of initial SMX Cmax targets and patient clinical outcomes in a cohort predominantly receiving low-dose SMX-TMP treatment.

Study results showed that patients with SMX Cmax >100 µmol/L had significantly higher clinical improvement rates than the low-concentration group (83.8% vs. 55.2%, P = 0.011), with significantly lower 28-day mortality rates (2.7% vs. 20.7%, P = 0.038). Research by Yang Shu et al. [9]showed that SMX-TMP blood concentrations were positively correlated with clinical efficacy, especially in non-HIV patients, where insufficient blood concentrations were an important risk factor for treatment failure. Pan Huanyan et al..’s [8] study of severe PJP patients also showed significantly improved clinical remission rates in the SMX concentration > 150 mg/L group, further supporting the conclusions of this study. Guidelines from the European Conference on Infections in Leukemia (ECIL) recommend an SMX target blood concentration range of 100–200 µg/mL [6]; this study’s results validated the effectiveness of the lower limit of this range (100 µg/mL) in Chinese non-HIV PJP populations, further highlighting the significance of achieving this minimum target concentration for improving outcomes.

A phenomenon worthy of in-depth discussion is that under low-dose treatment conditions (in this study, 89.4% of patients’ SMX daily doses did not reach the guideline-recommended standard of 75–100 mg/kg/day). The overall patients received SMX median daily dose was 49.2 (40.0, 63.2) mg/kg, in the group of patients with high concentration, the median daily dose was 56.1 (43.6, 66.7) mg/kg. More than half (57.58%) of patients achieved SMX Cmax above 100 µg/mL, which was significantly associated with clinical outcomes. This suggests that in Chinese non-HIV PJP populations, weight-based standardized dosing regimens may result in significant pharmacokinetic variability, with some patients potentially achieving effective blood concentrations at lower doses, while others may still experience insufficient exposure even with recommended doses. This finding is inconsistent with the statement in the research [16]. In this study, among 7 patients who used recommended doses, 1 had blood concentrations below 100 µg/mL. Dimeas et al. [17]. believe that low-dose regimens (SMX < 75 mg/kg) may have similar survival outcomes to conventional doses (SMX 75–100 mg/kg) for non-HIV PJP, with fewer ADRs. Tatsuya Nagai [18] observed in a multicenter retrospective cohort study that SMX doses below 62.5 mg/kg had outcomes comparable to conventional doses. These views are similar to this study, where low-dose SMX-TMP enabled most patients to achieve target SMX Cmax, and target Cmax was positively correlated with outcomes.

In multivariate logistic regression models, even after adjusting for age, sex, lymphocyte count, GFR, and dose adjustment, SMX blood concentration remained independently associated with improvement rates. This suggests that initial SMX Cmax may be a biomarker for predicting outcomes. Additionally, subgroup analyses revealed no significant interactions, demonstrating the robustness and generalizability of these findings across patients with different sex, age, immune status, and treatment strategies.

In safety assessment, this study showed an overall ADR incidence of 56.06%, similar to ADR incidence rates reported in the literature [19, 20]. No significant differences were observed between groups in rash, gastrointestinal discomfort, hepatic/renal dysfunction, thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, or hyperkalemia. However absolute incidence rates in the high blood concentration group (> 150 µg/mL) were generally higher than in the low blood concentration group, suggesting that excessively high blood concentrations may increase related ADR occurrence. Among deceased patients, 85.71% (6/7) had SMX Cmax <100 µg/mL, and those with abnormal G-tests received concomitant caspofungin. For severe PJP infection, evidence-based medical evidence supports initial use of SMX-TMP combined with caspofungin. Li H et al. [21] found in a retrospective cohort study that severe PJP patients using SMX-TMP combined with caspofungin and glucocorticoids had significantly higher treatment success rates than the SMX-TMP monotherapy group. Hui Qi et al. [22] similarly found that initial combination therapy with caspofungin and TMP/SMZ was superior to monotherapy for severe PJP patients.

Limitations

Firstly, small sample size (n = 66), single-center design with potential selection bias; although blood concentration monitoring was standardized at 2 h post-dose, patient medication compliance and sampling time errors may still affect concentration measurement accuracy. Our study did not systematically collect data on several factors that may influence SMX pharmacokinetics, including hemodynamic status, disease severity scores (e.g., APACHE II, SOFA), and renal replacement therapy. These variables could potentially affect initial Cmax and warrant investigation in future prospective studies. Secondly, the vast majority of patients had SMX blood concentrations monitored only once, with 15 patients having 2 or more monitoring records, of which only 3 had doses adjusted and re-monitored due to initial subtherapeutic levels, failing to reflect the relationship between full-course concentration dynamics and efficacy, while also indicating that clinical awareness of the importance of SMX blood concentration monitoring remains lacking. Thirdly, this study did not monitor TMP concentrations, and whether TMP and SMX concentration changes are consistent remains to be explored. Last, Although the odds ratio used in this study may overestimate the real risk correlation to some extent, the observed significant trend still supports the correlation between low blood drug concentration and adverse clinical outcome, which suggests that it is necessary to monitor blood drug concentration in clinic and carry out prospective research for further verification.

Conclusion

In summary, this study confirmed in a non-HIV PJP patient cohort predominantly receiving low-dose SMX-TMP treatment that achievement of SMX blood concentration targets (> 100 µg/mL) was significantly associated with clinical outcomes. This study observed that most patients used low-dose SMX-TMP, meaning daily doses did not reach guideline-recommended standards (SMX 75–100 mg/kg), yet their blood concentrations could still achieve targets, potentially indicating that weight-based standard dosing alone may be insufficient for Chinese populations. Routine SMX therapeutic drug monitoring should be implemented in clinical practice to achieve individualized precision dosing.
