Efficacy of Calcineurin Inhibition in Children With Steroid-Resistant Nephrotic Syndrome

Steroid resistance in childhood nephrotic syndrome (NS) bodes a poor prognosis.1 Algorithmic treatment options for steroid-resistant NS (SRNS) remain unclear because of limited natural history descriptions and even more scant randomized clinical trial data. Several agents, either alone or in combination, have been used in SRNS, including but not limited to mycophenolate mofetil, calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs), cyclophosphamide, or B-cell inhibitors. To date, there is no clear evidence supporting an optimal standardized sequential approach. A few randomized clinical trials have indicated efficacy of CNIs over other treatments in SRNS.2,3 As of now, the International Pediatric Nephrology Association clinical practice guidelines advocate for the use of CNIs as the first-line therapy for SRNS.

In the original publication “Efficacy of Calcineurin Inhibition in Children with Steroid Resistant Nephrotic Syndrome,” Trautman et al.4 share contemporary real-world data outcomes in 278 children with SRNS, both genetic and nongenetic, that received CNIs. They report relapses and kidney survival along with predictors of CNI responsiveness and evaluate the impact of CNI dosage versus trough levels on proteinuria. Oral and i.v. solumedrol was used as an adjunct therapy in 97.3% and 17.3%, respectively, and 22% of children were cotreated with mycophenolate mofetil.

Applying the International Pediatric Nephrology Association guidelines for complete, partial, and nonremission, Trautman et al.4 reported that complete remission at 12 months was noted in 41.6% of nongenetic SRNS, and the percentage increased to a promising 54.8% when remission was extended to anytime during follow-up. Similarly, partial responsiveness at 12 months was noted in 20.5% and stayed in a similar range of 19.5% when applied to anytime during follow-up. Nonresponsiveness at 12 months was relatively high at 37.9% in nongenetic SRNS-group but decreased to 25.6% anytime during follow-up. However, relapses were frequently noted both during and after cessation of CNI treatment in those with complete and partial remission. Reassuringly, majority of the children experiencing breakthrough relapses on therapy regained complete remission while continuing CNI therapy. The promising finding of CNI responsiveness leading to improved long-term kidney survival was noted in this manuscript.

It is notable that younger age was associated with higher likelihood of achieving remission compared to the adolescent age group. In addition, no associations between treatment responsiveness and disease severity, estimated glomerular filtration rate, histopathological features, or CNI type, dose, and serum level were found in this cohort. CNI dosing inversely correlated with degree of proteinuria without impacting remission status; interestingly, CNI serum levels did not correlate with degree of proteinuria as one would typically expect. The achievement of complete remission with CNIs was not related to dosing or trough levels in this cohort.

This manuscript advances knowledge about managing SRNS in the clinical setting from the standpoint of expected response to treatment as well as quality-of-life–related aspects. For treating physicians, patients, and caregivers, it is important to understand the expected response from CNI therapy for which this manuscript provides a reference. CNI therapy reduces proteinuria and improves kidney outcome; however, it may take 24 months or longer to achieve the protein-lowering effects. Thus, it should be a strong consideration to keep CNIs as part of a treatment regimen for that duration before assigning lack or responsiveness. Importantly, because CNI-induced remission rates did not statistically vary between the administered dose and blood level, this manuscript’s findings raise the possibility of treating SRNS with the lowest possible CNI dose based on weight; a finding that strongly warrants future investigation. Use of a lower CNI dose would limit toxicities, which are often dose-dependent.5 Achieving serum level–based dosing is challenging because of the level of variability in diet, concomitant medications, and intermittent illnesses, among other factors. From the patient’s perspective, limiting the amount of blood draws to monitor CNI levels offers an improved quality of life; physically getting to a clinical laboratory at a frequency ranging from weekly to every 3 months, for a timed laboratory draw that captures trough levels is challenging. For children missing school and for caregivers missing work to ensure timely laboratory draws is disruptive to work and family routines. In addition, levels are run by varying methodologies giving different values on the same sample often with a 30% to 40% variability.

This manuscript clarifies the role of CNI in genetic NS. In confirmed genetic cases, there was a short-term reduction in proteinuria after CNI initiation, likely due to nonimmunosuppressive effects of CNIs on podocyte cytoskeleton and podocyte viability. However, because in most cases, proteinuria worsened over time to initial levels or worse by 6 to 9 months, it will be important for future studies to determine if CNIs improve kidney outcomes in genetic NS.

The authors acknowledge that there are limitations of the study, notably voluntary participation, methodological variation in assessment of proteinuria, and a rapidly evolving field of what is considered genetic NS. In addition, the cohort is predominantly European, and cyclosporine was the CNI used in > 90% of the studied population. Therefore, it remains unclear to what extent these observations will apply to non-European populations or those treated with tacrolimus.

Looking to the future, 2 important elements warrant discussion as follows: (i) the discovery of antinephrin antibodies in NS, and (ii) the emerging role of B-cell inhibition for treatment of NS. Ongoing scientific advances may better define the clinical and therapeutic implications of antinephrin antibody positivity resulting in potential reclassification of NS. At present, the presence of antinephrin antibodies has been reported primarily in steroid-responsive and steroid-dependent NS during relapses and correlates with higher risk and shorter duration between relapses. It is possible that immunosuppressive treatment of SRNS suppresses antibody production, preventing the detection of such antibodies.6,7 In addition, patient fluidity in NS classification can occur, transitioning from steroid-responsive at presentation to an eventual steroid-dependent or steroid-resistant state. Reclassification based on antinephrin positivity at a treatment-naïve time at presentation may inform therapeutic approaches such as B-cell inhibition, potentially allowing movement away from reliance on CNI as the mainstay of SRNS treatment.8 The study by Trautmann et al.,4 provides important contemporary real-world evidence to build on as the field advances rapidly in this domain.
