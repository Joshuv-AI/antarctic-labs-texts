Complications during interventional rigid bronchoscopy with general anaesthesia and high-frequency jet ventilation: a 5-year retrospective pilot study
Authors: Cécile Maeyens, Dimitri Leduc, Jean François Fils, Turgay Tuna, Denis Schmartz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDHigh-frequency jet ventilation (HFJV) is the standard technique for rigid bronchoscopy. Few studies have comprehensively examined the complications associated with this technique. Previous evidence suggests that an ASA 4 score and basal SpO2 less than 95% may increase the likelihood of periprocedural complications.OBJECTIVE(S)This retrospective study aimed to quantify and characterise the frequency and types of complications linked to HFJV, and to identify independent factors associated with their occurrence.DESIGN A retrospective pilot cohort study.SETTINGThis monocentric pilot study was conducted in a tertiary university hospital in Brussels, Belgium, between 1 January 2019, and 31 December 2023.PATIENTSA total of 1385 patients who underwent bronchoscopy under general anaesthesia were screened, of whom 833 (444 men and 389 women) met inclusion criteria and were retained for final analysis. Inclusion criteria were age at least 18 years, rigid bronchoscopy and general anaesthesia using HFJV. Exclusion criteria included endotracheal intubation, incomplete medical records or explicit refusal to allow the use of their medical records for research.MAIN OUTCOME MEASURESComplications were defined as hypoxaemia (oxygen saturation ≤ 90% for at least 1 min), hypotension (SBP < 90 mmHg), cardiac arrhythmia, laryngospasm or bronchospasm, pneumothorax or requirement for ICU admission. Univariate analyses were performed to compare groups of patients with and without complications, and variables with P less than 0.05 were entered into a backward logistic regression to identify independent predictors of complications.RESULTSHypoxaemia and hypotension were the most frequent periprocedural events occurring in 11 and 28% of cases, respectively. Shorter stature, stent implantation and basal oxygen saturation below 96% emerged as independent predictors of incidents during HFJV for rigid bronchoscopy (logistic regression model, area under the curve 0.62 (95% CI 0.58 to 0.67).CONCLUSIONSWe observed a relatively high number of minor complications associated with general anaesthesia for interventional bronchoscopy using HFJV. Shorter stature, lower basal SpO2 and stent procedures were independent predictors of incidents; however, the model demonstrated poor predictive value and limited clinical applicability.TRIAL REGISTRATIONNCT06285994.

Introduction

High-frequency jet ventilation (HFJV) involves intermittent manual or automatic injection of high-velocity gas into the trachea via an injector. The gas is delivered at high frequency, typically between 120 and 600 insufflations per minute, producing tiny tidal volumes followed by passive exhalation.

Although HFJV is frequently used during rigid bronchoscopy, few studies have investigated complications associated with this type of ventilation during this procedure. In 2006, Fernandez-Bustamante et al.1 showed that an American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status (ASA) IV score and a baseline oxygen saturation of 95% or less were two independent predictors of complications during rigid bronchoscopy under HFJV.

Major complications reported include barotrauma, such as pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, subcutaneous emphysema and inadequate gas exchange or circulatory collapse. Restrictive pulmonary pathology and airway narrowing have also been identified as risk factors.2

In 2018, Altun et al.3 studied the risk factors associated with HFJV during laryngeal surgery.

In this study, we aim to analyse the frequency and type of complications that occur during rigid bronchoscopy under general anaesthesia with HFJV. Using a multivariate analysis, we sought to identify the factors independently associated with the occurrence of complications.

All patients who underwent bronchoscopy under general anaesthesia between 1 January 2019 and 31 December 2023 were screened for inclusion. Patients were extracted from the operating room database using the keywords ‘bronchoscopy’ and ‘general anaesthesia’. Data were extracted from the hospital's electronic patient records.

Procedure

Patients were equipped with a peripheral intravenous line and standard monitoring: ECG, pulse oximetry and noninvasive blood pressure monitoring. Anaesthesia was induced by target-controlled infusion of propofol and remifentanil.8,9 Muscle relaxation was obtained using bolus rocuronium injections and neuromuscular transmission was monitored using a TOF-Watch monitor (Organon, Swords, Ireland).2,10 After induction of anaesthesia, a rigid bronchoscope (Universal Bronchoscope, size 8.5, Karl Storz, Tuttlingen, Germany) was inserted by the pneumologist. HFJV was then applied through the lateral port of the bronchoscope using a Monsoon III jet ventilator (Acutronic Medical Systems AG, Hirzel, Switzerland). Initial settings of HFJV included a driving pressure of 1 to 3 bar (100 to 300 kPa), an inspired oxygen fraction of 80 to 100%, an injection frequency of 150 to 200 breaths per minute and an inspiratory time to expiratory time (I: E) ratio of 30 to 35%. Before using HFJV, pause pressure and high peak inspiratory pressure alarms were set to a value between 10 and 15 mbar (1.0 to 1.5 kPa) and were continuously monitored throughout the procedure. The gas humidification system of the jet ventilator was used routinely. Settings were adapted to achieve oxygen saturation above 90%. If necessary, a norepinephrine infusion was administered to maintain mean arterial pressure above 65 mmHg. At the end of the procedure and once the train-of-four ratio more than 0.9, the bronchoscope was removed and anaesthesia discontinued. Patients were ventilated by mask until spontaneous ventilation was adequate and recovery complete before transfer to the postanaesthesia care unit.

When either hypothesis was not met, a nonparametric Wilcoxon-signed-rank test was applied. Categorical variables were analysed using the χ2 test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median [IQR], as appropriate, and categorical variables as counts and percentages.

Then, variables with a P value less than 0.05 were entered into a multivariate regression model, and variables with a P value more than 0.05 were removed sequentially (backward selection procedure). As this is an exploratory pilot study, we report exploratory P values.

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.0; R Core Team, 2021, and results are reported in accordance with the TRIPOD statement (Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis).11,12

Results

From 1 January 2019 to 31 December 2023, 1385 patients scheduled for bronchoscopy under general anaesthesia were screened. Of these, 552 were excluded: six were minors (<18 years), 432 underwent flexible bronchoscopy through an endotracheal tube, three procedures were cancelled, 17 had a flexible bronchoscopy under local anaesthesia or sedation with spontaneous ventilation, 11 had a flexible bronchoscopy through a tracheotomy and 83 had incomplete medical records. In these latter cases, the anaesthetic record was missing, preventing analysis of arterial blood pressure and SpO2. All remaining patients had complete datasets. The only data not available for all patients were pulmonary function tests, which were only performed when judged clinically useful by the pulmonologist. For pulmonary function tests, only available data were analysed. Finally, 833 patients were included for analysis.

Multivariate logistic regression model

Next, we entered the variables with P values less than 0.05 (height, baseline SpO2, tobacco use, pre-operative oxygen, administration, ASA score, stent procedure and hospital stay) into a multivariate regression using a backward selection procedure, progressively removing terms with a P value more than 0.05 one by one.

In the final model, height, preprocedure SpO2 and stent procedures remained relevant predictors of complications during HFJV. Using these variables, we constructed a model to predict the likelihood of complications. Discussion

The aim of this retrospective, pilot study was to describe the complications associated with rigid bronchoscopy performed with HFJV, for either diagnostic (EBUS, biopsies) or interventional procedures (dilatation, stenting, laser or argon procedures). All procedures in our cohort of 833 patients were performed by three pneumologists with extensive training. Few major complications were observed, with most events being minor. Shorter stature, lower basal SpO2 and stent procedures appeared to be associated with complications.

A total of 296 patients (36%) experienced at least one complication, a rate similar to the 38% reported by Fernandez-Bustamante et al.1 These rates are higher than those reported by Fortin et al.4 (6.7%) and Ernst et al.6 (19.5%) and Ost et al.7 (3.9%); however, there were substantial inter-centre variations ranging from 0.9 to 11.7%. Several factors may explain these discrepancies. The cited studies aimed to examine complications of both flexible and rigid bronchoscopy rather than only rigid bronchoscopy performed under general anaesthesia with HFJV. In addition, all three were multicentric studies: Fortin et al.4 included data from eight centres, Ernst et al.6 from four and Ost et al.7 from 26. All three studies were conducted by pulmonologists. The differences observed may be explained by:(1)different patient groups. Ost et al.7 report a lower rate of complications, possibly due to their inclusion of only malignant obstruction, and differences in event documentation.(2)procedures that were not solely rigid bronchoscopies(3)variation of the complications reported in the different studies. For example, Fortin et al.4 have fewer complications than Ernst et al.,6 but only because minor bleeding is not included in their reporting.(4)the prospective or retrospective nature of data collection. Ernst et al.6 and Ost et al.7 performed prospective studies, whereas Fortin et al.4 carried out a retrospective analysis.

Anaesthesia and ventilation management also differed between studies. Ernst et al.6 used general anaesthesia in 91% of cases, of which 65% were rigid bronchoscopies. Fortin et al.4 used HFVJ in 73.5% of patients and Ost et al.7 used either HFJV or volume-controlled ventilation for rigid bronchoscopy.

In our cohort, morbidity showed a clear correlation with disease severity; 71% of ASA 4 patients and 57% of GOLD IV COPD patients experienced at least one complication. Higher ASA scores have been associated with increased complication rates.7

In this study, 196 patients had COPD with a reported GOLD status. Many referred patients had been previously assessed in internal medicine or pulmonology in the previous month. During the pre-anaesthesia visit, the GOLD grade was noted but not specifically re-evaluated. Interventional pulmonology is a part of endoscopic surgery and is considered low risk.13 Furthermore, patients with newly diagnosed COPD most often have low GOLD severity grades and are not at an increased risk of postoperative pulmonary complications. A specific assessment of COPD and pre-operative pulmonary function tests does not provide any additional benefits compared to standard clinical assessment in patients with or suspected of having COPD.14

One patient with pulmonary fibrosis developed a pneumothorax immediately after a cryobiopsy. However, this was unlikely to be related to HFJV, as cryobiopsy itself carries a pneumothorax risk of approximately 9% (ranging from 1 to 30% across studies), compared with 0.5% for HFJV.4,15–20

No peri-procedural deaths occurred in our cohort. Low mortality rates have been reported consistently: Fortin et al.4 report a peri-procedural mortality rate of 1.2%, Ost et al.7 0.5% and Ernst et al.6 0.18%.

Hypotension defined as a SBP less than 90 mmHg was the most frequent incident occurring in 27% of cases. This rate is higher than those reported by Hautmann et al.21 (3.7%) and Bustamante et al.1 (3.6% phase I 20% phase II). In the study by Hautmann et al.,21 flexible fiberscopes were used with nasotracheal catheters for HFJV. Hypotension was probably not specifically related to jet ventilation, but rather to the deeper general anaesthesia required for rigid bronchoscopy.2,19,21 General anaesthesia remains the gold standard for rigid bronchoscopy, as it offers superior procedural conditions, oxygen delivery and patient comfort.22,23 It is also associated with a lower complication rate. The study by Ost et al.7 combines data from fifteen centres. They reported higher complication rates (12.2%) in the four centres using moderate sedation, compared with (3%) in the 11 centres using general anaesthesia, underlining the importance of access to high-quality general anaesthesia for therapeutic bronchoscopy.

Hypoxaemia was the second most common event affecting 11% of patients, with 4% experiencing severe hypoxaemia (SpO2 < 85%). These rates are lower than those reported by Bustamante et al.1 (18.5% phase I, 32.5% phase II).

Our cohort included both asymptomatic patients referred for rigid diagnostic bronchoscopy and symptomatic patients referred for rigid therapeutic bronchoscopy, which may explain the lower incidence of hypoxaemia compared with Fernandez-Bustamante et al.1 who included only symptomatic patients with tracheobronchial stenosis. Jet ventilation was administered through the lateral port of the rigid bronchoscope causing dilution of the injected gas by entrained ambient air (Venturi effect).19,24 Thus, even when 100% oxygen is administered, the inspired oxygen fraction present in the trachea is lower.2 Laser was used in about half of patients in the study by Fernandez-Bustamante,1 where the inspired oxygen fraction is usually reduced to minimise fire hazard, increasing the likelihood of hypoxaemia.25,26 In our study, laser therapy was used in only 9% of cases, allowing higher inspired oxygen fractions, possibly explaining the lower incidence of hypoxaemia.

In our study, 0.5% of patients developed atrial fibrillation, a rate consistent with published data.1,4

The 1.2% rate of bronchospasm in our study is comparable to the 1.7% overall intra-operative rate reported in the literature.27 HFJV and rigid bronchoscopy therefore do not appear to increase bronchial reactivity. Our rate was lower than that of Hautmann et al.21 (3.1%), who performed flexible bronchoscopies in patients with tracheobronchial stenosis using nasotracheal jet ventilation without general anaesthesia, where the rate of bronchospasm reached 6%.

In our study, pre-operative SpO2 of 96% or less patient height and stent procedures were independent predictive factors for complications. Fernandez-Bustamante et al.1 found baseline saturation of 95% or less and ASA IV status to be independent predictors of complications. A saturation value ≥ 95% is considered normal.28–30 Pulse oximeters show over 90% sensitivity and specificity for detecting hypoxaemia and are typically accurate, within ±4 to 5% (95% confidence interval limits).30,31 The threshold of 96% lies within the confidence interval.28 ASA IV status was not a relevant predictor of complications in our cohort, possibly due to the low number of ASA IV patients (1.7%), compared with 14.5% in the study by Fernandez-Bustamante et al..1

The second independent risk factor for complications identified in our study was shorter stature. Height influences pulmonary function and is included as an explanatory variable in reference equations predicting normal spirometry values for lung function.32,33 A person's height is directly related to the size of their rib cage and lungs. Taller individuals, therefore, have greater lung volumes and higher forced vital capacity and forced expiratory volume values, while shorter individuals have proportionally lower values.34,35 However, pulmonary function tests did not appear to be independent predictors of complications in our study. In fact, pulmonary function results are standardised, and measured values of forced vital capacity and forced expiratory volume are compared with predicted values based on height, sex and age, to enable a standardised analysis of lung function and determine whether it is normal, obstructive or restrictive.34,35 However, the link between height and the risk of complications is surprising. In conventional ventilation, tidal volume is adjusted to 6 to 8 ml kg−1 of ideal body weight, whereas in jet ventilation, settings are adjusted using the driving pressure and frequency to obtain adequate oxygenation and carbon dioxide elimination. Because tidal volume variations are small, and the physiology of gas exchange in HFJV is complex, poorly documented and involves several mechanisms, these may contribute to explaining our findings.36,37

Stenting was also found to be an independent risk factor for complications. This therapeutic procedure, used to restore tracheobronchial airway diameter, is performed only in symptomatic patients.38 In our study, stenoses were either benign (post intubation or post tracheotomy tracheal stenoses or anastomotic stenoses of the stem bronchi in lung transplant patients) or tumour-related (due to endobronchial extension or extrinsic compression). In our cohort, patients undergoing a stent procedure accounted for 20% of those with complications, with hypotension being the main complication observed (31% of stenting procedures). Fernandez-Bustamante et al.1 showed in their univariate analysis that stenting procedures were associated with higher rates of haemodynamic instability; however, in their multivariate analysis, stenting was not an independent predictor of complications.

Stenting is a therapeutic procedure with an intrinsic risk of complications in patients with impaired respiratory function. It is therefore unsurprising that this procedure should constitute a risk factor regardless of the ventilation technique used. The predictive model yielded an area under the curve of 0.63, indicating a poor predictive value. The high intercept suggests that a substantial portion of baseline prediction is unrelated to the included variables. As this is a retrospective pilot study, data quality and possible reporting bias may have contributed to the limited predictive value. In the clinical setting, such a low area under the curve limits its usefulness for pre-interventional risk assessment or stratification for individual patients. At best, it may alert the physician's attention to a possible risk in these patients.

Our study has several limitations. The area under the curve for the prediction value is low, limiting its usefulness in clinical settings. As a retrospective pilot study, its statistical power is lower than that of a prospective randomised design. Missing data due to incomplete patients’ charts are an inherent limitation of retrospective studies. As this was not a prospective study, patient evaluations were not standardised, which may have resulted in the omission of certain pre-operative investigations in healthier patients. In this study, there is a lack of empirical thresholds for continuous variables. Overall, rigid bronchoscopy performed with HFJV was associated with a complication rate slightly higher than that observed in general surgical patients (overall incidence is 16 to 25%).39,40 However, rigid bronchoscopy using HFJV requires trained anaesthesiologists and pneumologists able to understand and quickly manage any complications. It would be interesting to do prospective studies to optimise the management of patients scheduled for these procedures. Prospective studies would be valuable to optimise peri-operative management in patients scheduled for these types of procedures.
