AMPK/ULK1 enhances mitophagy and steroidogenesis in duck Granulosa cells under heat exposure
Authors: Xiaozhi Zhan, Pei Luo, Youtian Yang, Zhichao Deng, Cheng Zhang, Chen Yang, Yunmao Huang, Yunbo Tian, Wenjun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mitochondria play crucial roles in energy metabolism and steroidogenesis. Heat stress suppresses steroidogenesis in ovarian granulosa cells (GCs) and impairs poultry reproduction. Our previous study revealed that mitophagy protects duck GCs from acute heat exposure. Notably, numerous studies have demonstrated that the AMP-activated protein kinase/Unc-51-like kinase 1 (AMPK/ULK1) pathway is a key regulatory pathway for mitophagy. However, how AMPK coordinates mitophagy and steroidogenesis under heat stress remains unclear. This study aimed to elucidate the role of the AMPK/ULK1 axis in heat-induced mitophagy and steroidogenesis. Using a duck heat-treatment model and in vitro GC culture models, we combined Western blotting, targeted metabolomics, siRNA-mediated gene knockdown, and mitochondrial function assays to assess the impacts of the AMPK/ULK1 axis on mitophagy and steroidogenesis. We showed that heat treatment induced mitochondrial dysfunction, activated the AMPK/ULK1 pathway, triggered mitophagy, and suppressed steroidogenesis in duck ovarian tissue. In vitro, GCs exhibited similar mitochondrial impairment and AMPK/ULK1-dependent mitophagic activation under heat treatment. The LC-MS/MS analysis also confirmed elevated intracellular AMP levels in heat treatment GCs. Furthermore, activation of AMPK by 5′-aminoimidazole-4´-carboxamide ribonucleotide (AICAR) triggered mitophagy and enhanced steroidogenesis in GCs under heat treatment by upregulating the expression of steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (StAR), cytochrome P450 11A1 (CYP11A1), and cytochrome P450 19A1 (CYP19A1), whereas inhibition of AMPK by compound C exerted the opposite effect. Additionally, genetic knockdown of AMPK or ULK1 via siRNA reduced mitophagy and steroidogenesis under heat treatment. Taken together, the AMPK/ULK1 axis mediates heat-induced mitophagy and enhances GC steroidogenesis, positioning AMPK as a therapeutic target to improve heat-stressed poultry reproduction.

Introduction

Exposure of poultry to high ambient temperatures triggers physiological, behavioral, and immunological responses that adversely affect their reproductive performance directly or indirectly(Kumar et al., 2021). Granulosa cells (GCs), the largest cell population and primary source of steroid hormones in the ovary, are the important somatic ovarian components (Berkel, 2024). In avian species, progesterone (P4) synthesized by GCs layers is transported to theca interna where it is converted to testosterone (T), which is subsequently aromatized to estradiol (E2) in theca externa (Hu et al., 2023). Notably, GC layers of hierarchical follicles can also synthesize and secrete E2 directly to regulate oocyte maturation and follicle development (Hu and Zadworny, 2017). GCs dysfunction leads to disruption of ovulation and ovarian dysfunction (Cao et al., 2025). Understanding the molecular mechanisms by which GCs respond to heat exposure is therefore critical for developing strategies to mitigate heat-induced reproductive losses.

Mitochondria play crucial roles in energy metabolism and steroid hormone biosynthesis (Miller, 2013). Mitophagy, a form of selective autophagy targeting mitochondria, enables the recycling of mitochondrial components and maintains homeostasis (Baechler et al., 2019). During stress conditions such as ischaemia and hypoxia, cells enhance mitochondrial quality through the activation of mitophagy, leading to the reduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and prevention of excessive opening of mitochondrial membrane permeability transition pores. This ensures that the basic energy needs of the cell under stress conditions are met, thereby promoting cell survival (Kubli and Gustafsson, 2012). Our previous study showed that heat treatment impairs mitochondrial function and induces oxidative injury in duck GCs. The dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1)-regulated, PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1)-dependent mitophagy protects follicular GCs from acute heat treatment-induced injury (Yang et al., 2024). However, the upstream signaling pathways that activate mitophagy under heat exposure remain understood.

AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is an energy sensor that perceives cellular energy status through the competitive binding of AMP, ADP and ATP to three sites in its γ subunit (Kjøbsted et al., 2018). Notably, AMPK directly phosphorylates Unc-51-like kinase 1 (ULK1) at multiple residues (including Ser317, Ser467, Ser555, and Ser637), thereby initiating autophagosome formation and mitophagy (Egan et al., 2011). AMPK can also induce the fragmentation of damaged mitochondria within the network and promote the translocation of the autophagy machinery to damaged mitochondria, thereby facilitating the autophagic degradation of mitochondria (mitophagy) (Li and Chen, 2019). Evidence indicates that pharmacological activation of the AMPK/ULK pathway promotes mitophagy, enhances thermogenic capacity, and improves lamb survival rates (Li et al., 2025). While mitochondria are indisputably the central organelles for steroidogenesis, the specific impact of AMPK-regulated mitophagy in this critical cellular function remains to be elucidated.

In this study, using a duck heat-treatment model and in vitro GC culture models, we assessed heat treatment effects on mitochondrial function, the AMPK/ULK1 pathway, mitophagy, and steroidogenesis. Furthermore, through 5′-aminoimidazole-4´-carboxamide ribonucleotide (AICAR, HY-13417, MedChemExpress, USA) and compound C (HY-13418, MedChemExpress, USA) treatments combined with targeted metabolomics, we defined AMPK's regulatory role in mitophagy and steroidogenesis. Additionally, siRNA knockdown confirmed the critical function of AMPK/ULK1 in heat treatment-induced mitophagy and steroidogenesis. Notably, these findings provide a mechanistic basis for targeting AMPK/ULK to improve heat exposure poultry reproduction via dietary or pharmacological activation.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval

The animal protocol was established in accordance with the guidelines from the Ministry of Agriculture and Food Requirements for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purposes. Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Ethics Committee at the Zhongkai University of Agriculture and Engineering, China (No. 2025031011).

Animals and heat treatment experimental design

Sixty female Shan Ma ducks, aged 60 days, were bred at the Education Scientific Research Base of Zhongkai University. Ducks were fed a standard diet and provided with water ad libitum. Animals were maintained at 24°C ± 2°C in an environment-controlled room. After 15 days, the animals were placed in an artificial climate room at 39°C (without food or water) for 0, 30, and 60 min. Twelve ducks from each group were sacrificed using CO2. After the ducks were sacrificed, the left ovary was carefully dissected and cleared of adherent fat and connective tissue without damaging the ovarian parenchyma. The whole ovarian tissue (including the ovarian cortex and medulla, containing follicles at various developmental stages) was collected and stored at −80°C for use in subsequent experiments.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Ovarian tissues were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4°C for 1 week. The tissues were then stained at room temperature for 2 h in 1% osmium tetroxide, rinsed three times in 0.1 M phosphoric acid buffer, and dehydrated during ethanol grading. The tissues were then permeated and embedded during acetone grading overnight at 37°C. After polymerising for 48 h at 60°C, 80-nm sections were cut using a Leica UC7 ultramicrotome (Leica) and picked up on copper grids. The grids were post-stained with 2% uranyl acetate and 2.6% lead citrate. The sections were visualised using a Hitachi HT7800 microscope (Hitachi).

Isolation of the follicular GCs and culture

Female Shan Ma ducks aged 150 days were bred at the Education Scientific Research Base of Zhongkai University. The F1–F3 ovarian follicles of egg-laying ducks were collected, immediately placed in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Gibco, USA) with 2% penicillin-streptomycin (15140122, Gibco, USA), and washed three times with PBS. Although F1 and F3 follicles exhibit differential steroidogenic activities, we pooled GCs from F1 to F3 follicles to obtain sufficient cell numbers for the subsequent multi-omics analyses (Western blotting, LC-MS/MS, etc.). This pooling strategy prioritizes the investigation of general heat exposure responses across preovulatory follicles rather than follicle stage-specific differences. After half of the egg yolk was released, the membrane layer removed, and the granule layer gently separated, collected, and rinsed well. Type II collagenase (0.1%; 17101-015, Biosharp, China) was added, and the mixture was digested at 37°C for 15 min. Digestion was stopped using BASIC Medium 199 (M199; C11150500BT, Gibco, USA) containing 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS; 10270106, Gibco, USA). The cell suspension was filtered through a 70-μm cell sieve; the filtrate was collected for centrifugation and then resuspended in M199 with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The density of GCs was adjusted to 5 × 105 cells per mL, seeded into a 6-well culture plate, and incubated at 39°C in a 5% CO2 cell culture chamber (Thermo Scientific, USA).

Western blot analysis

After the treatment, proteins from GCs were extracted using RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China) containing 1 mM PMSF (ST506, Beyotime, China), and the protein concentration was quantified using the BCA assay kit. Samples containing equal amounts of protein were loaded onto 8–10% polyacrylamide gels and separated using sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, then transferred to a 0.45 μm polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (IPVH00010, Millipore, USA). After blocking in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (9005-64-5, Solarbio, China) with 5% bovine serum albumin for 1 h at 37°C, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with specific primary antibodies (Supplementary Table S1). The membranes were washed and incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated AffiniPure IgG for 1 h at 37°C. The blots were visualised by incubating with an enhanced chemiluminescence Western blotting detection reagent (GK10008, Beyotime, China) and a Tanon 5200Multi (Tanon, China) imager. Densitometry analysis was done using the ImageJ software V1.8.0 (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Estradiol (E-OSEL-Ch0001, Elabscience, China) ELISA kits were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Serum samples were prepared by centrifuging whole blood at 1,000 × g for 20 minutes after standing at room temperature for 2 hours. For ELISA using a 96-well plate, 50 μL of standard, blank diluent, or sample were added to designated wells, followed by 50 μL of HRP-conjugate working solution. After incubation at 37°C for 60 minutes, the plate was washed 5 times with 350 μL of wash buffer (each wash: shake for 5 seconds, aspirate for 0.5 seconds). Then, 90 μL of TMB substrate solution was added and incubated at 37°C for 15 minutes in the dark. The reaction was terminated by adding 50 μL of stop solution, and the OD was measured at 450 nm.

Mitochondrial ROS detection

MitoSOX Red staining to detect mtROS was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (RM02822, ABclonal, China), and the fluorescent intensity was measured by flow cytometry. A 0.5 μM MitoSOX Red working solution was prepared by diluting the 5 mM stock solution (RM02822, ABclonal, China) 10,000‑fold in an appropriate buffer (e.g., HBSS containing Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺), taking into account potential changes in mitochondrial morphology and the probe’s selectivity for nucleic acids versus the cytoplasm. An appropriate volume of the working solution was added to fully cover the cells grown on coverslips and incubated at 37°C for 10 minutes in the dark. The cells were then washed three times with pre‑warmed appropriate buffer. Finally, a suitable counterstain was applied if desired, and the coverslips were mounted for microscopic observation. The data were analysed using FlowJo v10.6 software (BD Life Sciences, USA).

Detection of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex activity

The activities of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes I–V in GCs were detected using assay kits (BC0510, BC3235, BC3240, BC0945, BC1445, Solarbio, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions for GCss. Cells (5 × 10⁶) were collected and homogenized in 1.0 mL of extraction buffer on ice. The homogenate was centrifuged at 600 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was further centrifuged at 11,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant (cytosolic fraction) was used to assess complex leakage from mitochondria. The pellet was resuspended in extraction buffer and subjected to ultrasonic disruption (200 W, 5 s on/10 s off, 15 cycles) for complex enzyme activity and protein assays. The microplate reader was preheated for >30 min, and the spectrophotometer was zeroed with distilled water. Samples were measured in micro-glass cuvettes or 96-well plates after adding the specified reagents.

Measurement of ATP levels and ATPase activity

ATP levels were measured using an ATP assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (S0026, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Cells were lysed with 200 μL of lysis buffer per well of a 6-well plate. After lysis, the lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected. The ATP standard was diluted with ATP assay lysis buffer to generate a standard curve. ATP assay working solution was prepared by diluting the ATP assay reagent 10-fold with the provided diluent. Then, 100 μL of working solution was added to each well and incubated for 3–5 min at room temperature to deplete background ATP. Subsequently, 20 μL of sample or standard was added, mixed rapidly, and after at least 2 seconds, the RLU or CPM was measured using a luminometer. ATP concentrations were calculated from the standard curve. The activities of Na+K+-ATPase, Mg2+Ca2+-ATPase, and T-ATPase were detected using an ATP enzyme test kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (A070-6-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). Cells were homogenized in normal saline, and the supernatant was collected after centrifugation. According to the kit instructions, 100 μL of sample and mixed reagents were added and incubated at 37°C for 10 minutes. Reagent R7 was then added, followed by centrifugation at 3,500 rpm for 10 minutes. The supernatant was collected, and phosphorus determination reagent was added. After standing at room temperature for 2 minutes, the stop solution was added. The absorbance was measured at 636 nm with a 1 cm light path.

Establishment of a heat treatment model in cultured GC

The study indicated that maintaining ducks in an environmental temperature at 37±1°C for 3 h markedly increased body temperature. The rectal temperature can reach 43.3°C (Zhu et al., 2014). Moreover, exposure to 43°C has been shown to significantly inhibit cell proliferation and increase the proportion of cells in the G1 phase in duck GCs (Yang et al., 2021). Based on these, the control group was cultured at 39°C (normal temperature for poultry cell culture), whereas the heat treatment group was cultured at 43°C. GCs were isolated and cultured as previously described. After 24 h of incubation, the cell density increased to 80%, and the cells were used as follows: control group (39°C without any heat treatment), 1-h heat treatment group (43°C for 1 h), 2-h heat treatment group (43°C for 2 h), and 4-h heat treatment group (43°C for 4 h). Since the normal avian body temperature ranges from 40.5°C to 41.5°C and GCs are routinely cultured at 39°C, exposure to 43°C establishes a thermal gradient of approximately +4°C, effectively inducing cellular heat stress responses. After the treatment, proteins were extracted from the cells to detect changes in the levels of mtROS, adenosine triphosphates (ATP), ATPase activity, mitochondrial respiratory chain complex activity, and the expression levels of proteins related to glucose metabolism and AMPK/ULK1-associated mitophagy.

Targeted metabolomics analysis of energy metabolism

The GCs in the control and heat treatment 4 h groups were used to quantify 80 biomarkers related to energy metabolism using ultra-performance liquid chromatography–high-performance mass spectrometry (UPLC–HRMS). Metabolites were extracted in ice-cold methanol, and endogenous metabolite profiles were obtained using LC-MS/MS as detailed below. The data were acquired using a SCIEX 6500+ QTRAP mass spectrometer (Applied Biosystems, USA). Metabolite levels were normalized to the total protein content, determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (P0012, Beyotime, China), on a duplicate set of cells treated identically to the experimental cells. MultiQuant software 3.0.3 (Applied Biosystems, USA) was used for the LC-MS/MS performed at Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Wuhan, China. The screening of differentially produced metabolites is considered significant if the variable importance of projection is greater than 1, the fold change ≥2 or ≤0.5, and the false discovery rate is less than 0.05.

Biochemical intervention

GCs were grown to 80% confluence and subjected to different treatments. The cells were treated with 1 mM AICAR, an AMPK activator, with or without 10 µM Compound C, an AMPK inhibitor for 12 h, followed by a 4 h heat treatment. The concentrations of AICAR (1 mM) and Compound C (10 μM) were selected based on previously published studies, which have demonstrated their efficacy and specificity in activating or inhibiting AMPK in avian cell models (Tosca et al., 2008; Yao et al., 2023).

Knockdowns with siRNAs

GCs were transfected using a riboFECT CP transfection kit (C10511-05, RiboBio, China) along with 50 nM AMPK or ULK1 siRNA specific for each gene. GCs were seeded in 6-well plates 24 h prior to transfection to reach 70-90% confluence at the time of transfection. siRNA (50 nM final concentration) was diluted in riboFECT CP Buffer, mixed with riboFECT CP Reagent, and incubated at room temperature for 15 min to form transfection complexes. The complexes were then added to the cells and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO₂ for 24-72 h before further analysis. siRNA plasmids targeting duck AMPK (si-AMPK), ULK1 (si-ULK1), and a negative control siRNA (si-NC) were synthesised by Sangon Biotech. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by Western blotting.

Statistical analysis

All treatments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates, and each biological replicate had sufficient technical replicates to ensure accuracy. Statistical analyses were done using GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., USA), and the values were presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Multiple comparisons were performed using a two-way analysis of variance. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Heat treatment induces mitochondrial dysfunction, activates AMPK/ULK1 pathway, induces mitophagy, and inhibits steroidogenesis in duck ovarian tissue

The ultrastructure of duck GCs within whole ovarian tissue sections was observed by TEM. The mitochondria in the ovarian GCs of the control group had complete and clear structures, but those in the GCs of the heat treatment group appeared swollen, and their cristae were decreased in number or disappeared (Fig. 1A). Concurrently, we found that the phosphorylation of AMPK at Thr183 and ULK1 at Ser556 was increased in the duck ovarian tissue from the 30 min group compared to those from the control group (P < 0.05, Fig. 1B-C). It was also found that after heat treatment, the microtubule-associated protein 1A/1B-light chain 3 ratio (LC3-II/LC3-I) and expression of PINK1 and Parkin were increased (P < 0.05), while the expression of translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 20 (Tom20) was decreased in the 60 min group (P < 0.05, Fig. 1B-C). Furthermore, protein expression of steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (StAR), 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (3β-HSD), cytochrome P450 11A1 (CYP11A1), and cytochrome P450 19A1 (CYP19A1) (Fig. 1D-E), and the E2 content (Fig. 1F) in the ovaries of the heat treatment group were reduced compared to that in the control group (P < 0.05). Concurrently, heat treatment activated the AMPK/ULK1 pathway, indicated by significantly increased phosphorylation of both AMPK and ULK1 after 2 and 4 h of treatment (Fig. 2H-I). Heat treatment induces glycolytic upregulation and inhibits energy metabolism in in vitro GCs

Western blotting showed that 4 h of heat treatment upregulated the protein expression levels of IDH1, CS, PFKFB1, PKM2, GAPDH, and LDHA (P < 0.05, Fig. 3A-D). Combined with the results from Fig. 2 showing that AMPK/ULK1 pathway activation and mitophagic flux reached a stable level at 4 hours after heat treatment, we used this same time point to further evaluate how heat treatment affects steroidogenesis. We further examined the influence of heat treatment on specific metabolites using LC-MS/MS. The metabolic profile indicated a clear shift in the heat-treated GCs when compared to the control GCs (Fig. 3E-H). All metabolomic data are provided in Supplementary File S1. The Principal Component Analysis results show the sample distribution under two main components (PC1 and PC2). PC1 explained 74% of the variance, while PC2 explained 8.1% of the variance. As shown in Fig. 3E, there were significant differences in the distribution of PC1 and PC2 between the heat treatment group and the control group, indicating that heat treatment has a significant impact on the overall characteristics of the sample. The heatmap shows significant differences in the expression patterns of energy metabolites between the two groups of samples, with most energy metabolites being significantly expressed lower in the heat treatment group than in the control group (Fig. 3F). The volcano map shows that a total of 44 metabolites related to energy metabolism were monitored, among which 11 were significantly down regulated in the heat treatment group and 33 showed no significant changes (Fig. 3G). There were 11 significantly downregulated metabolites (P < 0.05): gluconate, pyruvic acid, d-glucose-1-phosphate, d-fructose-6-phosphate, d-mannose-6-phosphate, d-glucose-6-phosphate, glycerol-3-phosphate, d-(+)-glucose, sedoheptulose-7-phosphate, phosphoenolpyruvic acid, and uracil. Although the increase in AMP levels was not significant, the results indicated an upward trend in heat-treated GCs when compared to control GCs (Fig. 3H). AMPK regulation of mitophagy and steroidogenesis in heat treatment in vitro GCs

Treatment of GCs with the AMPK activator AICAR (1 mM) significantly enhanced mitophagy, evidenced by increased phosphorylation of AMPK and ULK1 (P < 0.05), elevated LC3-II/LC3-I ratio, and upregulated PINK1/Parkin expression, while reducing Tom20 and p62 levels (Fig. 4A-B). Compared with the control group, heat treatment for 4 h significantly downregulated the expression of StAR, 3β-HSD, CYP11A1, and CYP19A1 (P < 0.05, Fig. 4C-D). In contrast, the addition of AICAR with or without heat treatment significantly increased the expression of these proteins (P < 0.05, Fig. 4C-D). Conversely, AMPK inhibition with compound C (10 μM) exacerbated mitophagy defects under heat treatment, marked by decreased AMPK/ULK1 phosphorylation, reduced LC3-II/LC3-I ratio, and elevated Tom20 levels (P < 0.05, Fig. 4E-F). This indicated that AMPK inhibition effectively suppressed mitophagy in GCs during heat treatment. Furthermore, the use of Compound C alone markedly diminished the expression of 3β-HSD, CYP11A1, CYP19A1, and StAR in comparison to no-treatment group (P < 0.05), while Compound C along with heat treatment further hindered the expression of 3β-HSD, CYP11A1, and CYP19A1 (P < 0.05, Fig. 4G-H). These findings demonstrate that AMPK activation ameliorates heat-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and steroidogenic deficits in GCs, whereas its inhibition exacerbates these impairments. Knockdown AMPK or ULK1 inhibits mitophagy and steroidogenesis in heat treatment in vitro GCs

To further demonstrate the effect of AMPK on the regulation of mitophagy in heat treatment GCs, endogenous AMPK was knocked down using siRNA. This intervention significantly reduced AMPK protein expression (P < 0.05, Fig. 5A-B), accompanied by decreased LC3-II/LC3-I ratios and suppressed expression of PINK1 and Parkin (P < 0.05), alongside elevated p62 and Tom20 levels (P < 0.05, Fig. 5A-B), collectively indicating that AMPK is critical for heat-induced mitophagy. Concurrently, the expression of 3β-HSD, CYP11A1, and CYP19A1 in heat-treated GCs was further inhibited by AMPK knockdown (P < 0.05, Fig. 5C-D). To verify whether AMPK regulated mitophagy and steroid hormone synthesis via ULK1, ULK1 was knocked down in GCs with siRNA. The protein expression of ULK1 was reduced after the knockdown (P < 0.05, Fig. 5E-F). The LC3-II/LC3-I ratio and expression of PINK1 and Parkin were reduced (P < 0.05), accompanied by increased expression of p62 and Tom20 (P < 0.05, Fig. 5E-F). The expression of StAR, 3β-HSD, and CYP19A1 under heat treatment was further inhibited by si-ULK1 (P < 0.05, Fig. 5G-H). ELISA results showed that heat treatment significantly reduced E2 levels in the supernatants of GCs (P < 0.05), with AMPK or ULK1 knockdown further reducing E2 levels (P < 0.05, Fig. 5I). Discussion

High temperatures are a major factor affecting animal production. When intrinsic thermoregulatory mechanisms become inadequate to manage increased environmental temperatures, animals develop a heat stress response (Guthrie and Garrett, 2000). Heat stress affects follicular and early embryonic development in animal models (Ealy et al., 1993; Ozawa et al., 2005). It also impacts fertility through various mechanisms, including altered ovarian functions (Zhu et al., 2024). For example, heat stress reduces the reproductive performance of cows by affecting the metabolism of ovarian follicles (Alves et al., 2014). The mechanism of steroid biosynthesis involves StAR transporting cholesterol from outside to inside the mitochondrial membrane, and CYP11A1 catalysing the conversion of cholesterol to pregnenolone (Manna et al., 2016). In the follicle, CYP19A1 initiates and maintains E2 synthesis to regulate sex differentiation (Mendelson et al., 2005). It has been reported that heat stress inhibits the expression of genes involved in steroidogenesis and the synthesis of E2 in bovine GCs (Yang e t al., 2021). One study found that heat stress impairs GC function in mice by reducing steroidogenesis and inducing apoptosis (Luo et al., 2016).Our previous study showed that the number of duck GCs arrested in the G1 phase increased upon exposure to heat treatment. Concurrently, heat stress inhibited E2 synthesis in GCs by decreasing the expression of CYP11A1 and CYP19A1 genes (Yang et al., 2021).

In this study, we found that raising the ambient temperature to 39°C promoted mitophagy and activated the AMPK/ULK pathway in the duck ovary, and mitophagy levels were further enhanced as heat exposure time increased from 30 to 60 minutes. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that Drp1 regulates the PINK1-dependent mitophagy pathway to protect follicular GCs from acute heat treatment-induced injury (Yang et al., 2024). Moreover, increasing the ambient temperature to 39°C inhibited ovarian steroidogenesis in ducks, with steroidogenesis levels further declining from 30 to 60 minutes of heat exposure. Interestingly, among the steroidogenic enzymes examined in this study, 3β-HSD exhibited the most rapid response to heat treatment, with protein expression levels decreasing significantly within 30 minutes and remaining comparable at 60 minutes. This rapid decline can be explained by post-translational mechanisms. Heat treatment rapidly activates cellular stress responses, including the ubiquitin-proteasome degradation pathway. Within minutes of heat exposure, misfolded or damaged proteins are targeted for degradation. Since 3β-HSD is a membrane-associated protein whose stability is highly dependent on proper interaction with the lipid bilayer, it may be particularly sensitive to thermal destabilization, leading to its rapid turnover (Payne and Hales, 2004). Meanwhile, we detected a decrease in E2 levels in heat treatment duck ovarian tissue. While theca cells are widely recognized as the primary source of E2 in avian species, it is noteworthy that GCs of hierarchical follicles have also been reported to synthesize and secrete low levels of E2, which may participate in the regulation of oocyte maturation and follicle development (Hu and Zadworny, 2017). These findings extend previous knowledge and offer new insights into the mechanisms by which AMPK/ULK1 regulates mitophagy and steroidogenesis under heat stress.

Mitochondria are central to energy metabolism and signal transduction. Harmful stimuli disrupt mitochondrial function and the energy metabolic balance, leading to disruptions in overall energy metabolism (Wang et al., 2026). Studies have shown that heat stress reduces the ATP content in Sertoli cells and increases glucose uptake and the expression of glycolysis-related enzymes, enhancing glycolytic activity to compensate for energy deficits (Deng et al., 2022). In our study, results revealed that glycolysis in GCs was enhanced after heat treatment in vitro. The mitochondrial respiratory chain, a major source of biological ROS, is highly sensitive to temperature, and under heat treatment, ROS oxidize complexes I, II, and IV, thereby inactivating the respiratory chain, reducing electron flow, and leading to decreased ATP synthesis (Aryal et al., 2025). For instance, heat stress decreased ATPase and mitochondrial respiratory chain complex activity in chicken spleens (Xu et al., 2017). In addition, heat stress reduced mitochondrial activity in bovine oocytes, which affects cell function (Abdelnour et al., 2020). In our study, in vitro heat treatment of duck granulosa cells increased mtROS, suppressed mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes I, II, IV, and V activities, reduced ATP levels, and concurrently enhanced AMPK and ULK1 phosphorylation and mitophagy-related protein expression. These results revealed that heat treatment damages the mitochondrial respiratory chain in GCs, causing mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to a decrease in ATP levels and ATPase activity, ultimately reducing energy release. These data indicate that heat-induced mitochondrial dysfunction in duck granulosa cells is due to the impairment of the mitochondrial respiratory chain and energy metabolism and provide a hypothesis that activation of the AMPK/ULK1-mediated mitophagy pathway may be a potential strategy for protecting against heat stress-induced reproductive decline in poultry.

AMPK, a systemic energy receptor, meets the energy demands of cells by integrating different signalling pathways to inhibit energy-depletion processes and activate energy-production processes (Malik and Shaw, 2025). Studies have shown that heat stress leads to a decrease in ATP content and an increase in AMPKα phosphorylation levels in rat skeletal muscle cells (Goto et al., 2015). The AMPK/ULK1 pathway serves as a classical link between energy metabolism and autophagy (Mandic et al., 2025), and plays a critical role in promoting mitophagy (Liang et al., 2015). ULK1- or AMPK-deficient cell lines exhibit increased accumulation of morphologically altered mitochondria, suggesting that AMPK-dependent ULK1-phosphorylation is essential for selective mitochondrial clearance (Egan et al., 2011). Heat stress activates ATP–AMPK–mTOR axis, inducing protective autophagy in hepatocellular carcinoma cells, both in vitro and in the duck heat-treatment model (Jiang et al., 2019). AICAR, a well-known AMPK activator, enters the cell via the adenosine transporter, is phosphorylated by adenosine kinase on the cell membrane, and is metabolised to form AICAR monophosphate, which mimics AMP and activates AMPK (Russell and Hardie, 2020). Compound C is a potent and reversible AMPK inhibitor that selectively competes with ATP and has no significant inhibitory effects on other kinases (Zhou et al., 2001). Our results show that AICAR activates mitophagy in duck GCs. In contrast, the addition of compound C significantly inhibited mitophagy in duck GCs subjected to heat treatment. Our results revealed that the AMPK/ULK1 pathway plays a key role in the regulation of heat-activated mitophagy. Our results from knocking down AMPK and ULK1 revealed that this interference significantly decreased the expression levels of LC3-II/LC3-I, PINK1, and Parkin and inhibited mitophagy in heat-treated GCs. Combined with the results of the drug assays, these findings confirmed the hypothesis that AMPK is involved in inducing mitophagy during heat treatment.

Previous studies have shown that AICAR dose-dependently increased the phosphorylation level of AMPK in chicken GCs, increased the protein expression levels of StAR and CYP11A1, and promoted P4 secretion (Tosca et al., 2006). In this study, we found that the heat treatment combined with AICAR significantly upregulated the protein expression of steroid synthases CYP11A1, CYP19A1, 3β-HSD, and StAR, indicating that activation of the AMPK pathway can alleviate the inhibitory effect of heat treatment on steroidogenesis. AICAR alone significantly increased the levels of estrone, 17α-estradiol, and 17β-estradiol. However, inhibition of AMPK by compound C or siRNA-mediated knockdown resulted in a significant decrease in protein expression of CYP11A1, CYP19A1, 3β-HSD, and StAR. In addition, under heat treatment, knockdown of AMPK or ULK1 led to a further decrease in E2 levels in GC supernatants. Consistent with our findings on the protective role of AMPK/ULK1-mediated mitophagy in heat-treatment GCs, previous studies have demonstrated that FSH exerts protective effects in d-gal-induced prematurely aged GCs of laying hens. Specifically, FSH has been shown to restore cell damage, mitochondrial injury, and energy metabolism disorders by regulating mitophagy to maintain mitochondrial homeostasis and by modulating glycogenophagy and glycolysis to increase glycogen utilization (Dong et al., 2022). These findings, together with the present results, suggest that regulating mitophagy and cellular metabolism represents a common strategy for protecting GC function under various stress conditions, including heat exposure and aging. Therefore, AMPK serves as a promising target for nutritional or pharmacological intervention; dietary supplementation with AMPK activators (such as metformin or certain plant extracts) or developing AMPK-targeted drugs may offer a practical and effective strategy to enhance steroidogenesis and protect ovarian function in heat exposure.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the AMPK/ULK1 pathway mediates heat-induced mitophagy and steroidogenesis in duck GCs. AMPK activation enhances mitophagy and restores steroidogenesis, while its inhibition exerts opposite effects. These findings identify AMPK as a potential target for improving reproductive performance in heat exposure poultry, an effect that can be achieved through dietary or pharmacological activation.
