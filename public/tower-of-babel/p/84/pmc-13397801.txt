Salivary cortisol as a neuroendocrine stress biomarker for xerostomia in multiple sclerosis patients: a comparative cross-sectional study
Authors: Tanaz Khabazian, Aynaz Khabazian, Muhammad H A Saleh, Fatemeh Samavatijame, Saba Mohammadi, Maryam Koopaie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundXerostomia is a common and clinically relevant complaint in patients with multiple sclerosis (MS), adversely affecting oral health, nutrition, and the quality of life. Neuroendocrine and autonomic dysregulation associated with MS may contribute to salivary gland dysfunction. It was hypothesized that MS patients with xerostomia would indicate higher salivary cortisol levels compared to individuals without xerostomia, the independent of self-reported anxiety.MethodsIn this comparative cross-sectional study, 39 MS patients were enrolled, including 23 with xerostomia and 16 without. Demographic characteristics, medication use, salivary cortisol concentrations, xerostomia severity, and anxiety levels were assessed. Unstimulated whole saliva was collected using the spitting method, and cortisol levels were quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Furthermore, xerostomia was evaluated clinically using the Abslang test and subjectively using the Short Xerostomia Inventory (SXI). Anxiety was also assessed using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI).ResultsNo significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of age, gender, medication use, or other assessed confounding factors. The patients with xerostomia demonstrated significantly higher salivary cortisol levels than controls (8.5 ± 1.8 vs. 4.7 ± 2.2 ng/mL, p = 0.045) and greater xerostomia severity scores (7.10 ± 2.20 vs. 2.80 ± 1.50, p = 0.001). In contrast, the anxiety scores were significantly lower in the xerostomia group compared with the controls (11.77 ± 8.30 vs. 17.23 ± 10.21, p = 0.03).ConclusionsSalivary cortisol levels were associated with the presence and severity of xerostomia in MS patients, despite lower self-reported anxiety levels. This lack of association demonstrates that salivary cortisol may reflect physiological stress or neuroendocrine/autonomic dysregulation rather than subjective anxiety alone. While causality cannot be inferred due to the cross-sectional design, these findings support the potential role of salivary cortisol as a biomarker for xerostomia in MS and provide the importance of integrating biological stress markers into oral health assessment in this population.Keywords: Multiple sclerosis, Xerostomia, Salivary cortisol, Physiological stress, Anxiety, Biomarker, Oral health

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic immune-mediated disorder of the central nervous system characterized by inflammation, demyelination, and progressive neurodegeneration, leading to diverse neurological and systemic manifestations [1, 2]. Globally, more than 3 million individuals are affected by MS, with disease onset most commonly occurring between 20 and 40 years of age and a marked predominance in females [3, 4]. Beyond its neurological burden, MS is associated with a wide range of secondary complications that significantly impair quality of life and daily functioning [5].

Oral health disturbances, particularly xerostomia or the sensation of dry mouth, are increasingly recognized as clinically relevant yet underappreciated complications of MS [6]. Xerostomia has been reported in up to 60% of individuals with MS and may arise from multiple interacting factors, including autonomic nervous system dysfunction, altered salivary gland innervation, immune-mediated mechanisms, and medication-related effects [7]. Clinically, xerostomia is associated with the increased risk of dental caries, periodontal disease, oral infections, dysphagia, and impaired speech, thereby exacerbating morbidity and reducing overall well-being in this population [8].

Despite its high prevalence and clinical consequences, the pathophysiology of xerostomia in MS remains incompletely understood. Current diagnostic approaches rely largely on subjective symptom reporting and clinical examination, which may not fully capture underlying biological mechanisms [9]. The identification of objective, non-invasive biomarkers could therefore improve the assessment of xerostomia, facilitate early recognition, and provide insight into disease-related physiological alterations affecting salivary function.

Cortisol, a glucocorticoid hormone regulated by the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, plays a central role in physiological stress regulation and neuroendocrine homeostasis. The measurement of salivary cortisol provides a reliable and non-invasive estimate of biologically active free cortisol and is widely used to assess HPA axis activity [10].

The dysregulation of cortisol secretion has been reported in several chronic inflammatory and neurological disorders, including MS, where altered baseline cortisol levels and abnormal cortisol awakening responses have been observed [11]. These alterations are thought to reflect physiological stress, neuroendocrine imbalance, and possible autonomic dysfunction rather than subjective psychological distress alone [12].

Evidence has revealed that cortisol dysregulation may also influence oral health [13]. Psychological and physiological stress can modulate salivary secretion through central and peripheral neural pathways involving the hypothalamus, limbic system, and autonomic nervous system [14].

Previous studies have reported associations between stress, salivary cortisol, and xerostomia have produced mixed results across different populations. Some investigations report significant associations between perceived stress, elevated salivary cortisol levels, reduced salivary flow, and increased xerostomia severity, indicating a role for stress-related neuroendocrine mechanisms in dry mouth symptoms [15, 16]. Other studies, however, found no direct correlations, providing the complexity of cortisol as a physiological stress biomarker and the influence of methodological and contextual factors, including saliva sampling challenges in xerostomia [17–19].

Studies related to oral diseases, such as burning mouth syndrome, further support the link between cortisol dysregulation and dry mouth, while recent research has reported dissociation between biological stress markers and subjective psychological measures in populations with chronic diseases. In patients with multiple sclerosis, xerostomia is common but remains understudied from a biological perspective. Although MS is associated with autonomic and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis dysfunction, the relationship between salivary cortisol and xerostomia in this population has not been systematically investigated, revealing the need for focused investigation [16, 20–22].

In the context of MS, limited research has specifically evaluated the relationship between salivary cortisol and xerostomia. Although xerostomia has been documented as a common complaint among MS patients, the contribution of physiological stress and HPA axis activity to its development and severity is still not fully understood. Addressing this gap is clinically relevant, as it may enhance the understanding of MS-related salivary dysfunction beyond medication effects or psychological symptoms alone.

Therefore, the present study was designed to evaluate the association between salivary cortisol levels and xerostomia in patients with MS. By examining both biological and psychological parameters, this study aimed to clarify the potential role of physiological stress and neuroendocrine dysregulation in MS-related xerostomia and to explore the utility of salivary cortisol as an objective biomarker in oral health assessment for this population.

Materials and methods

Study design

This comparative cross-sectional study was conducted in 2022 at the Neuroscience Research Center of Imam Khomeini Hospital, Tehran, Iran. This study was designed to test the hypothesis that patients with MS and xerostomia would exhibit higher salivary cortisol levels than MS patients without xerostomia, the independent of self-reported anxiety, thereby exploring salivary cortisol as a potential biomarker of physiological stress and neuroendocrine/autonomic dysregulation rather than subjective anxiety alone. A total of 39 patients with a confirmed diagnosis of MS were enrolled and divided into two groups based on the presence or absence of xerostomia.

Participants

The participants were recruited consecutively from the neurology clinics. All patients had a confirmed diagnosis of MS based on neurological evaluation and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings. The final sample included 26 women and 13 men. 23 patients reporting xerostomia constituted the case group, while 16 patients without xerostomia served as controls. The two groups were comparable in terms of age, sex distribution, and medication use [23].

Diagnostic criteria for multiple sclerosis

All participants were required to have a confirmed diagnosis of multiple sclerosis established by a neurologist according to the 2017 revisions of the McDonald criteria, ensuring a rigorously defined and clinically homogeneous study population. These criteria permit diagnosis based on the evidence of dissemination of central nervous system lesions in space and time, supported by MRI findings and, when indicated, cerebrospinal fluid–specific oligoclonal bands. Dissemination in space was defined by the presence of characteristic lesions in at least two typical regions (periventricular, cortical/juxtacortical, infratentorial, or spinal cord), while dissemination in time was demonstrated by the simultaneous presence of enhancing and non-enhancing lesions on a single MRI, new lesions on follow-up imaging, or CSF oligoclonal bands. Alternative neurological or systemic conditions that could mimic MS were carefully excluded to enhance diagnostic specificity. The use of standardized McDonald criteria strengthened the internal validity of this study [24].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria comprised a confirmed diagnosis of multiple sclerosis established by a neurologist according to standard clinical and magnetic resonance imaging criteria, ensuring a well-defined MS population in whom xerostomia represents a clinically relevant complication affecting oral health, nutrition, and the quality of life. To minimize confounding factors that could independently influence salivary cortisol concentrations or salivary gland function, individuals with systemic autoimmune diseases known to cause xerostomia (e.g., Sjögren’s syndrome or rheumatoid arthritis), chronic inflammatory or dermatologic disorders, malignancies, or a history of cancer therapy (surgery, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy) were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria included pregnancy, diabetes mellitus, tobacco or alcohol use, oral mucosal lesions, and chronic periodontitis, as well as other systemic conditions or medications known to significantly alter HPA axis activity or salivary secretion. These criteria were applied to increase internal validity by isolating the association between salivary cortisol and xerostomia in the MS population; however, due to the cross-sectional design, residual confounding factors were not completely eliminated and thus causal inferences could not be made.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined a priori to test the explicit hypothesis that patients with MS presenting with xerostomia would demonstrate higher salivary cortisol levels than MS patients without xerostomia. Based on previously reported moderate effect sizes (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.5) for differences in salivary cortisol between comparable clinical groups, a two-sided independent samples t-test with an alpha level of 0.05 and 80% statistical power (β = 0.20) indicated that a minimum of 16 participants per group was required [25]. For enhancing robustness and account for potential variability or incomplete data, the xerostomia group was modestly oversampled, resulting in a total of 39 participants (23 with xerostomia and 16 without). Although the groups were unequal in size, the calculation assumed the homogeneity of variance between groups, and this assumption was verified during statistical analysis. The sample size estimation was performed using G*Power version 3.1.

Saliva collection and cortisol measurement

The unstimulated whole saliva was collected using the passive drool (spitting) method to avoid the external stimulation of salivary glands. In order to control for diurnal variation in cortisol secretion, all samples were collected at a standardized time (10:00 AM). The participants were instructed to refrain from eating, drinking (except water), brushing teeth, or performing oral hygiene for at least one hour prior to sampling. The saliva was collected over a five-minute period into sterile polypropylene tubes, immediately placed on ice, and transported to the laboratory. The samples were centrifuged at 2400 × g for 10 min to remove debris, and the supernatant was aliquoted and stored at − 70 °C until analysis [26]. Then, the salivary cortisol concentrations were measured in duplicate using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (ZellBio GmbH, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions [27]. The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were maintained below 10%. The cortisol concentrations were expressed in ng/mL and were not corrected for salivary flow rate, as salivary cortisol reflects free, biologically active hormone levels.

Assessment of xerostomia

Xerostomia was assessed using a combined subjective and objective approach. The clinical oral examination was performed to evaluate mucosal dryness, saliva pooling, and lip moisture. The Abslang test was used as a semi-objective indicator of salivary wetting on the tongue. The subjective severity and functional impact of xerostomia were also assessed using the validated Short Xerostomia Inventory (SXI), consisting of 11 items rated on a five-point Likert scale. Higher scores indicated the greater severity of xerostomia symptoms.

Clinical examination

A standardized clinical examination was conducted to objectively evaluate the signs of xerostomia. The oral mucosa was inspected for the signs of dryness or mucosal abnormalities, such as atrophy, pallor, or a glazed appearance. The moisture level of the lips was assessed, with particular attention given to cracking, peeling, or a dry texture. In addition, the presence and consistency of saliva were evaluated by observing spontaneous salivary flow and palpating the major salivary glands. These clinical parameters were used to corroborate self-reported symptoms and offered a direct, the observable measure of oral dryness [26].

The Abslang test

The Abslang test was administered as a semi-objective screening tool to further evaluate xerostomia. In this method, a standardized piece of absorbent paper or filter paper was placed on the dorsum of the tongue for a specific duration, typically ranging from 30 s to 2 min. The degree of wetting or moisture absorption by the paper was observed and recorded. The reduced or absent wetting of the paper indicated decreased salivary secretion, supporting the diagnosis of xerostomia. This test served as a quick, the non-invasive indicator of oral dryness that complemented both the clinical findings and subjective questionnaire data [26].

Xerostomia inventory (SXI)

The xerostomia severity was also evaluated using the validated SXI questionnaire, which was designed to quantify the subjective severity and functional impact of dry mouth symptoms in daily life. The SXI consisted of 11 items, each addressing specific symptoms or challenges associated with xerostomia, such as difficulty in speaking, swallowing, or experiencing oral discomfort. The participants rated each item on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). The responses were summed to generate a total score, which reflected both the intensity and the functional consequences of xerostomia. This score allowed for stratification and comparison among individuals and provided insight into the personal burden of xerostomia symptoms in the MS population [28].

Evaluation of anxiety

The evaluation of anxiety levels among the participants was conducted using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) questionnaire, developed by Beck in 1988 [29]. This self-report questionnaire consisted of 21 items, each rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (extremely). Each of the 21 items on the BAI corresponds to specific anxiety symptoms, such as nervousness, fear of losing control, and difficulty relaxing. The BAI encompassed cognitive, somatic, and affective components of anxiety, thereby providing a multidimensional profile of each participant’s psychological state. Total scores on the BAI ranged from 0 to 63, with higher scores indicating greater severity of anxiety. The high reliability and validity of the BAI established it as a suitable tool for capturing stress-related factors that could potentially confound cortisol levels. This assessment facilitated the examination of potential correlations between anxiety severity and salivary cortisol levels, as well as their combined influence on xerostomia symptoms [29].

Salivary cortisol measurement

The salivary cortisol levels were measured to evaluate HPA axis activity in both the MS patients and the control group. The participants were instructed to refrain from eating, drinking (except water), brushing their teeth, or using tobacco products for at least 30 min prior to saliva collection to avoid contamination and ensure accurate readings. The saliva samples were collected using the passive drool method, which involves allowing saliva to accumulate in the mouth and then drooling it into a sterile polypropylene collection tube. All samples were collected between 8:00 AM and 10:00 AM to control for diurnal variation in cortisol secretion. Following collection, the samples were immediately stored on ice and subsequently transported to the laboratory, where they were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to remove mucins and cellular debris. The supernatant was then aliquoted and stored at -20 °C until analysis. Afterwards, the cortisol concentrations in saliva were quantified using a commercially available ELISA kit specifically designed for salivary cortisol detection (ZellBio GmbH, Cat No. ZX-44107-96) [30]. Each sample was assayed in duplicate to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the results. The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were maintained below 10%, indicating a high level of precision in the measurement. Moreover, the quality control procedures were implemented throughout the process to ensure the integrity of the data. All assays were performed by trained personnel blinded to the case or control status of the samples.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data, with continuous variables presented as means and standard deviations, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test also was used to assess the normality of data distribution, which was found to be normal. The Chi-square test was then applied to compare categorical variables between groups, while the independent two-sample t-test was used to compare the means of continuous variables, such as salivary cortisol concentration. Finally, obtained data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0 and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Of these, 23 patients (59%) reported xerostomia and were assigned to the case group, while 16 patients (41%) without xerostomia constituted the control group. Discussion

This study evaluated the association between the salivary cortisol levels and xerostomia in patients with MS. According to the results, the significantly higher salivary cortisol levels were observed in the MS patients with xerostomia compared to those without xerostomia, supporting the proposed link between physiological stress and salivary gland dysfunction. Interestingly, despite the elevated cortisol, the self-reported anxiety scores were significantly lower in the xerostomia group, demonstrating a lack of association between the biological and subjective stress markers.

The associations observed in this study was independent of demographic characteristics such as age and gender, with no significant differences observed between the groups, minimizing potential confounding from these variables. Similarly, lifestyle factors including smoking, alcohol consumption, and diabetes were absent in all participants, further strengthening the validity of the association. In addition, the medication use, including antidepressants, corticosteroids, disease-modifying therapies, and symptom-control drugs, was not significantly different between the groups, indicating that pharmacological factors were unlikely to account for the observed cortisol differences or the presence of xerostomia. In contrast to the observations of Warth et al., it was reported that xerostomia could affect the reliability of cortisol sampling in palliative care patients. The potential role of illness severity and medication in confounding cortisol measurements was revealed in that study, whereas this effect was not observed in the present study [20].

Xerostomia is a common and clinically significant symptom in individuals with MS, with wide-ranging consequences for oral health, nutrition, communication, and the overall quality of life. Despite its prevalence, the biological mechanisms underlying xerostomia in MS remain poorly understood [9, 33]. In a systematic review, García-Rios et al. revealed that the most frequently reported oral manifestations in patients with MS were hyposalivation, gingival inflammation, increased DMFT (decayed, missing, and filled teeth) and plaque indices, dental sensitivity, and oral pain. Their results demonstrated statistically significant associations, including a correlation between xerostomia and MS phenotype (p < 0.05) and between periodontal health and the degree of disability (p < 0.05) [34]. Their findings were consistent with the present results that xerostomia is a common and clinically significant oral manifestation in MS patients.

Additionally, previous studies have also reported the association of cortisol with xerostomia in other populations. Donly (2018) demonstrated elevated salivary cortisol in postmenopausal women with xerostomia, while Singh et al. (2021) reported a positive correlation between cortisol levels and xerostomia severity [19]. Besides, Bulthuis et al. observed associations between perceived stress and decreased salivary flow rates, and Scribante et al. showed elevated cortisol in diabetic patients with xerostomia. Together, these studies reported a role for cortisol as a potential mediator of salivary gland dysfunction, indicating the relevance of neuroendocrine factors in xerostomia pathophysiology [14, 17].

The clinical relevance of xerostomia in MS is substantial. Xerostomia affects oral health, speech, swallowing, and the quality of life, yet its pathophysiology remains incompletely understood [35]. By demonstrating a significant association between the elevated salivary cortisol and xerostomia independent of common demographic and pharmacological confounders, the present study provides novel evidence supporting the involvement of neuroendocrine and autonomic mechanisms in this symptom [36]. The evaluation of these mechanisms is crucial for developing targeted interventions that may alleviate xerostomia by addressing stress-related neuroendocrine dysregulation [37].

The present study revealed novel evidence linking elevated salivary cortisol, a non-invasive biomarker of HPA axis activity, to xerostomia severity in individuals with MS, suggesting that neuroendocrine dysregulation may contribute to the pathophysiology of xerostomia in this population. In contrast, Kapel-Reguła et al. reported that MS patients experience more xerostomia than healthy controls, along with other oral issues such as swallowing difficulties, pronunciation problems, and taste disturbances. However, this study found no significant difference in salivary cortisol levels between MS patients and controls, and cortisol was only correlated with disability scores and not directly with xerostomia [21]. Their study and the results of this study emphasized that xerostomia is a common and clinically important problem in MS, that MS patients experience more oral health problems than healthy individuals, and that the difference lies in the proposed mechanism.

The multifactorial nature of xerostomia in MS is well-supported by previous studies, implicating autonomic dysfunction, neuroinflammatory processes, and altered salivary gland regulation [38]. The results of this study extend prior observations from non-MS populations, including postmenopausal women and individuals with other oral sensory disorders, where elevated cortisol has been associated with salivary gland dysfunction and xerostomia symptoms. By demonstrating a similar relationship in MS, this study revealed cortisol-mediated neuroendocrine mechanisms as a potential contributor to xerostomia in neurologically complex, chronically inflamed conditions [39].

MS is characterized by chronic inflammation and autonomic nervous system imbalance, both of which can disrupt HPA axis function. Altered HPA axis activity may lead to abnormal cortisol secretion patterns, which in turn can directly or indirectly impair salivary gland function [40, 41]. Cortisol is a key regulator of stress responses and autonomic nervous system balance. The chronic elevation of this hormone may suppress parasympathetic activity, the primary driver of salivation [42, 43]. Autonomic dysfunction combined with altered cortisol signaling may exacerbate salivary hypofunction, providing a plausible mechanistic link to xerostomia in MS [9, 37].

Beyond its effects on the autonomic nervous system, cortisol also modulates immune and inflammatory responses. Considering that neuroinflammation is a marker of MS, increased cortisol may affect the local inflammatory environment in the salivary glands and further impair gland function [44]. This demonstrates a possible biological link between systemic neuroendocrine changes and the development of xerostomia in MS patients [21]. The measurement of salivary cortisol provides a noninvasive method for assessing biologically active cortisol, making it a useful biomarker for studying HPA axis function in relation to xerostomia [45]. Previous studies in non-MS populations, such as postmenopausal women and patients with oral sensory disorders, have similarly shown an association between increased cortisol and xerostomia, indicating a conserved mechanism by which stress-related neuroendocrine dysregulation affects salivary gland function [19].

Furthermore, the inconsistent findings of the lower self-reported anxiety, despite the higher cortisol concentrations, were consistent with emerging evidence suggesting a lack of association between subjective stress and biological markers in chronic or medically burdened populations. For example, Špiljak et al. reported that salivary cortisol levels may not correspond directly to perceived stress among dental students, indicating the potential influence of neuroendocrine and autonomic dysregulation rather than psychological perception alone [22]. Similarly, Lee and Suk demonstrated elevated cortisol levels in patients with burning mouth syndrome and xerostomia, suggesting that stress-induced HPA axis activation may manifest as physiological changes independent of subjective anxiety [16]. These findings revealed that salivary cortisol may serve as an objective indicator of physiological stress and neuroendocrine or autonomic dysfunction in MS, rather than merely reflecting psychological anxiety [16, 22].

High cortisol may cause the dysregulation of the HPA axis, a key component of the physiological response to stress [40]. In MS, chronic disease-related stress, neuroinflammation, and autonomic nervous system alterations may contribute to persistent HPA axis activation, resulting in higher cortisol levels [46]. Sustained cortisol elevation can impair salivary gland function through multiple mechanisms, including the reduced parasympathetic stimulation of salivary secretion, modulation of local immune responses, glandular atrophy, and decreased glandular perfusion [47]. These mechanisms provide a plausible neuroendocrine pathway linking stress and xerostomia in patients with MS [39].

In summary, the present study provided a significant association between elevated salivary cortisol and both the presence and severity of xerostomia in MS patients, independent of demographic or pharmacological factors. The findings demonstrated the potential of salivary cortisol as a biomarker of physiological stress and neuroendocrine or autonomic dysregulation in MS-related xerostomia. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to elucidate causal mechanisms, explore the role of autonomic and inflammatory pathways, and assess interventions aimed at modulating HPA axis activity to mitigate xerostomia and improve the quality of life of MS patients.

Future perspectives

The observed association between salivary cortisol levels and xerostomia in the patients with MS revealed a potential involvement of physiological stress pathways in salivary gland dysfunction. In this study, higher cortisol levels may reflect neuroendocrine or autonomic dysregulation rather than subjective anxiety alone, indicating the lack of association between the biological and psychological stress markers that has been reported in chronic conditions. Although these findings provided preliminary evidence for a cortisol-related mechanism contributing to xerostomia, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference and potential confounding factors cannot be completely ruled out.

Future research should employ longitudinal designs with larger cohorts to clarify the temporal relationships among stress, cortisol secretion, and salivary dysfunction in MS. Investigating the molecular, immunological, and neuroendocrine mechanisms underlying the effect of cortisol on salivary glands could uncover novel therapeutic targets. In particular, the role of the HPA axis in MS-related xerostomia warrants further exploration using advanced neuroimaging, inflammatory biomarker profiling, and comprehensive hormonal assessments.

Considering the lack of significant differences in self-reported anxiety between the patients with and without xerostomia, future studies should also evaluate the non-psychological sources of physiological stress and their contribution to salivary gland alterations. Interventions aimed at modulating cortisol levels such as behavioral stress-management strategies, pharmacologic agents, or neuroendocrine therapies may be evaluated for their potential to alleviate xerostomia symptoms. Ultimately, a more precise understanding of the relationship between cortisol and xerostomia in MS patients could improve symptom management and increase broader knowledge about neuroimmune interactions and endocrine dysfunction in chronic neurological disorders.

Limitations

Several limitations were noted in this study that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The cross-sectional design was used, which prevented conclusions regarding causality or the temporal relationship between salivary cortisol levels and xerostomia in MS patients. It was suggested that longitudinal studies be conducted to determine whether physiological stress contributed to salivary gland dysfunction or if xerostomia itself influenced cortisol secretion.

Moreover, a relatively small sample size was included, and unequal group sizes were observed, with more patients presenting xerostomia than controls. Although this imbalance reflected the clinical distribution of xerostomia in MS and the larger case group provided adequate statistical power, the limited sample was considered to potentially restrict the generalizability of the results. Main demographic and medication variables were matched between groups, and the homogeneity of variance was confirmed by statistical analyses, supporting the reliability of group comparisons.

Furthermore, potential confounding factors such as disease duration, MS subtype, concurrent medications, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors were recognized as possible influences on both cortisol levels and salivary function. While efforts were made to control for these variables, residual confounding could not be fully excluded. Besides, the lack of an observed association between elevated cortisol and self-reported anxiety suggests that caution should be exercised when interpreting cortisol as the sole biomarker of psychological stress.

Conclusion

This study revealed a significant association between salivary cortisol levels and the presence of xerostomia in patients with MS. The patients experiencing xerostomia demonstrated higher salivary cortisol concentrations compared to those without xerostomia, indicating that physiological stress and potential neuroendocrine or autonomic dysregulation may contribute to salivary gland dysfunction in this population. Notably, despite elevated cortisol, the xerostomia group reported lower self-reported anxiety, demonstrating the lack of an association between biological and subjective stress markers and revealing the complexity of stress-related mechanisms in MS.

Moreover, the xerostomia severity was substantially greater in affected patients, confirming the clinical relevance of this symptom and the importance of considering endocrine factors in its management. Although no significant differences were observed between groups in demographic factors, medication use, or other potential confounders, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference. These findings provide preliminary evidence that salivary cortisol could serve as a biomarker for identifying MS patients at higher risk for xerostomia and provide the need for longitudinal and mechanistic studies to further elucidate the underlying pathways.
