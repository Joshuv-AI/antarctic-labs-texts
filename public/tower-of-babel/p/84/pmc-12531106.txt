Revolutionizing Early Detection: A Cross‐Sectional Comparative Study of Pink Luminous Screening Versus Breast Self‐Examination Among Nursing Students
Authors: Fatma M Ibrahim, Gharam Sami, Shabnam Bano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsBreast cancer is a leading cause of cancer‐related deaths among women worldwide. This study compares the effectiveness of the Pink Luminous Breast LED‐based Technology Device with traditional Breast Self‐Examination (BSE) among nursing students.MethodsA comparative cross‐sectional study was conducted with 191 nursing students aged 20 years and above. Data was collected using socio‐demographic characteristics, knowledge of breast screening, and feedback on screening methods. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0, employing Chi‐square tests and thematic analysis.Results63.4% (121/191) of participants preferred Pink Luminous over BSE. Significant associations were observed between preference for Pink Luminous and marital status (88.9% [16/18] vs. 60.7% [105/173], p < 0.001), contraceptive use (85.7% [12/14] vs. 100% [9/9], p < 0.001), and year of study (e.g., 89.3% [50/56] among fourth‐year students, p < 0.001). Participants with good breast cancer screening knowledge (132/191) had a 75% (99/132) preference for Pink Luminous versus 33.3% (20/60) among those with poor knowledge (p < 0.001).ConclusionThe Pink Luminous Device demonstrates promise as an effective tool for breast cancer screening among nursing students. Integrating advanced screening technologies into nursing education could enhance awareness and early detection of breast cancer.Keywords: breast cancer, breast self‐ examination, pink luminous, screening

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most prevalent cancers and the leading cause of cancer‐related deaths among women worldwide, with a steadily increasing global annual incidence accounting for approximately 2.3 million new cases in 2020 alone [1]. In the United Arab Emirates (UAE), breast cancer is the leading cause of cancer‐related morbidity among women, with a rising incidence in younger populations [2].

Detecting breast cancer at an early stage allows for more effective and less aggressive treatment options, potentially reducing the need for extensive surgeries or chemotherapy [3, 4]. Despite the availability of approved methods such as mammography, ultrasound, and breast self‐examination (BSE), challenges associated with access, awareness, and adherence remain [5, 6, 7]. Breast self‐examination is, however, utilized in the advocacy for breast health literacy and recognition of normal breast appearance and touch, most preferably in young women [8]. Development in technology has brought in new handheld devices that have been designed to facilitate early detection behaviors. This emphasis is consistent with prior commentary on screening trials and oncology perspectives [9, 10].

Background on the Pink Luminous Breast Device and Clinical Application

The pink luminous breast (PLB) device is a handheld light‐emitting diode (LED) transillumination tool that emits red and near‐infrared light within approximately 640 to 800 nm. This range is the “optical window,” where tissue is relatively transparent and hemoglobin absorbs light. In principle, where there is higher hemoglobin, associated with higher blood flow, such regions may look darker during transillumination [11, 12]. Transillumination has been investigated over several decades, as has optical breast imaging. The current diffuse optical methods have promise as adjuncts, not substitutes, to mammography in population‐wide screening [13].

Evidence related to PLB includes a study of 170 women in Puerto Rico, which reported a sensitivity of 89.6% and specificity of 96.4% for detecting abnormalities shown in mammography, using an imperfect gold standard [14]. Breast‐i/Breastlight, another alternative cheap transillumination device, demonstrated variable performances as adjuncts in resource‐poor settings. Their emphasis is generally based on acceptability and potential to promote clinical judgment rather than being independent diagnostic substitutes [15, 16]. Earlier generations of light scanning demonstrated limited sensitivity for small or non‑palpable cancers [17, 18, 19], underscoring the need for cautious interpretation and confirmatory imaging.

In terms of regulations and guidelines, in April 2024, the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommended biennial screening mammography for women aged 40 to 74 at average risk [20]. The American Cancer Society recommends annual mammography starting at age 45, with the option to begin at 40 [21]. These guidelines do not support the use of LED transillumination devices as a primary screening method. In the U.S., the FDA sent a warning letter to the PLB manufacturer in 2020 for marketing the device without required clearance. In 2024, the FDA also issued a Class II recall for distribution without premarket approval. The FDA classifies the device as a transilluminator and is not approved as a replacement for mammography [22, 23].

Considering these restrictions, PLB might serve as an additional awareness tool to promote breast self‐exams and encourage timely clinical attention to new findings. This would have several benefits in populations who delay or avoid mammography because of availability or pain, and where a nonionizing substitute is called for, such as in pregnancy [14]. Any doubtful outcome of the PLB should lead to a diagnostic work‐up along predetermined protocols. On the other hand, a negative PLB assessment should not postpone routine screening. This study examines the acceptability and perceived usefulness among nursing students, who will become future health educators.

Conventional screening tools, such as mammography, ultrasound, and breast self‐examination (BSE), are well‐established; yet barriers to access, awareness, and compliance persist [5]. Late diagnosis remains a significant issue in the Middle East, mainly due to insufficient awareness and limited access to screening programs [7]. However, there is scant published literature from this region on the reasons for the delayed presentation of female Breast Cancer [24]. There has also been little comparison between communities and countries about similarities and differences in reasons for such delays. More than 75% of breast cancer patients in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) seek medical advice after experiencing a sign or symptom of the disease, and many seek such advice late [6].

Breast self‐examination is used for both screening and promoting breast health to empower women by fostering familiarity with the normal appearance and texture of their breasts. This strategy boosts women's confidence in recognizing changes. Self‐examination helps young women who have not undergone formal screening establish lifelong breast health awareness [8]. While mammography and breast self‐examination (BSE) are widely used for screening, the development of new technologies offers potential for more efficient and user‐friendly options.

The Pink Luminous Breast LED‐based Technology Device is a prime example of such innovation, offering a promising screening option. LED technology makes this screening equipment safe and effective for high‐risk pregnant and breastfeeding women. By using a specific wavelength of light to penetrate breast tissue and detect abnormalities, this technology allows for at‐home self‐examination, reducing the need for frequent doctor visits [25]. Smith et al. [26] found that LED‐based breast imaging can be used as an accessible, safe alternative for early detection, especially among high‐risk groups such as pregnant and lactating women. Moreover, Ocasio‐Villa et al. [14] found that the Pink Luminous device has demonstrated sensitivity ranging from 80% to 90% and specificity above 95% in clinical settings [14].

Research indicates that early detection improves therapy and survival outcomes. With high sensitivity (89.6%) and specificity (96.4%), the Pink Luminous device shows promise for future breast cancer screening [27]. There is a lack of nursing student research comparing the Pink Luminous gadget to established breast screening methods. For many reasons, preservice nursing students are fascinating and crucial. As future healthcare professionals, students will educate and advocate for patients, including breast cancer awareness and early detection. By the time they become medical students or residents, their knowledge, attitudes, and practice habits about breast cancer screening should influence their own preventive health behaviors and those of the communities they serve.

Evidence‐based practice is also significant to the nursing educators. Although the new breast screening technologies such as the Pink Luminous Breast have devices that can be unfamiliar to the students, familiarity may make students feel safe and knowledgeable to propose or perform breast screening on their patients. The present paper addresses the gap in research on the effectiveness and utility of the devices among nursing students and provides instructional programs that could potentially drive the outcomes of breast cancer outcomes among the populations.

Therefore, it is about time to train nursing students to use and accept new screening examinations as it will transform them into better health literate, capable of becoming effective public health advocates and health educators, and force multipliers when they graduate.

Material and Methods

Study Design

A comparative cross‐sectional study using a mixed approach as recommended in Creswell & Plano Clark's guidelines for mixed methods research [28].

Study Setting & Sampling

The sample was recruited from the College of Nursing. The aim of having students representing all academic years and the most effective heterogeneity in the experiences and the levels of knowledge was the reason to choose purposive sampling. The participants with different backgrounds were sought to be included by providing different class candidates. Although this approach can be considered non‐generalizable, it has been considered to fit the exploratory type of research and a lack of feasibility in the setting conditions. Based on a priori power analysis, which is an accepted method in the rationalization of a study sample size (when using quantitative and mixed research methodologies), a sample of 191 participants was calculated. It was computed according to the capacity to detect a medium effect (typically defined as Cohen d = 0.5 or the similar to categorical data) with a statistical power of at least 80% (β = 0.20) and a significance level of α = 0.05 [29].

Inclusion Criteria

Female students aged 20 years and above agreed to participate in the study.

Exclusion Criteria

Female students with a previous medical history of breast cancer or who have undergone mastectomy were excluded.

Instrument

Data was collected using three structured tools:

Tool I: Demographic Characteristics, Including age at marriage, Number of children, Duration of breastfeeding to the last child, usage of contraceptive methods, personal history of breast lump, family history of breast cancer, and family history of mastectomy of breast cancer.

Tool II: Breast screening knowledge, covering the recommended start age of periodic breast examinations, history of breast abnormalities, Frequency of doing the breast self‐screening and screening method.

Tool III: Feedback questionnaire on the usability and perceived effectiveness of the Pink Luminous device and BSE

The tools used for data collection were rigorously checked for validity and reliability. Questionnaires construction was made based on a thorough literature search and was improved by feedback from a panel of five experts in the topics of oncology nursing and health education. There was a Content Validity Index (CVI) that examined the content validity with all items having a higher value than the recommended value of 0.80. Forward‐backward translation was applied to make the English version become Arabic and the difference was solved by reaching consensus among the bilingual experts. The pilot (9 students) was excluded, ensuring the accuracy of the results. Consequently, the reliability of Tool II and Tool III was α = 0.75 and 0. 89, indicating a high level of trustworthiness in the data collected.

Data Collection

Data were collected through self‐administered questionnaires and face‐to‐face interviews lasting 20–30 min. Interviews were conducted to gather qualitative feedback on participants' experiences with both screening methods. Interviews were carried out using a semi‐structured interview guide to be able to create consistency during the interviews. The interviewers were trained in the methods of qualitative interviewing, and all the interview sessions were audio‐taped and word to word transcriptions were made. All data collection occurred between February 2nd and April 4th, 2024. The interviews employed open‐ended questions to explore students' experiences and preferences on breast cancer screening methods. This method enhanced the data by capturing the subtleties of their impressions and yielded vital insights into how they contrasted the Pink Luminous Breast device with standard Breast Self‐Examination. Through direct interaction with the students, the interviews provided a more thorough understanding of their knowledge and preferences, improving the study's overall results.

Data Analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS version 28.0. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages along with actual numerators and denominators (n/N). Before conducting Chi‐square tests, data were examined to ensure that expected cell counts met statistical assumptions. Chi‐square tests (χ²) were employed to explore associations between categorical variables. A prior significance level was set at p < 0.05. p values are reported per SAMPL guidelines, with p < 0.001, values between 0.001 and 0.01 to the nearest thousandth, and ≥ 0.01 to the nearest hundredth [30]. All tests were two‐sided. Confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for proportions where applicable following recommendations from standard statistical reporting frameworks [30, 31]. Missing data were minimal (< 5%) and handled using listwise deletion.

Qualitative data were analyzed using inductive thematic analysis. Two researchers independently coded responses using NVivo software. At the very first step, open coding was utilized to construct initial codes in a methodical approach that aims to identify the pattern and themes in the qualitative data. The transcripts were coded separately by two different researchers, and the areas of inconsistencies were examined and consensus achieved through consensus meetings to increase dependability. Cohen kappa coefficient was used to determine inter‐rater reliability by examining a sample of transcripts and it was used to make sure that the transcripts got classified consistently. The codes were improved and aggregated into the overarching themes in an iterative approach and the themes sorted the range of experience and viewpoints on the adoption and the effectiveness of Pink Luminous Screening tool. Thematic consistency was also given by the regular team discussion and appraisal of the findings (triangulation of the quantitative data). Such a technique made it possible to evaluate the effect of the specific screening tool in a complex and sophisticated manner, taking into consideration both quantitative and qualitative views.

Ethical Consideration

This study complied with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was conducted after obtaining ethical approval from RAK Medical and Health Sciences University Research Ethics Committee, (RAKCON‐REC‐25‐2023/24‐F‐M). The researchers comprehensively explained the study's objectives and methodology during a personal interview. Participants were then requested to sign the informed consent form, indicating their voluntary decision to participate in the study. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any point without penalty.

Results

Demographic Characteristics of the Participants

The study sample included 191 nursing students. The majority were aged 20–29 years (189/191; 99.0%). Most participants were single (173/191; 90.6%), while 18/191 (9.4%) were married. Among married participants, 16/23 (69.6%) had fewer than two children, and 5/22 (68.2%) breastfed their last child for less than 24 months. Only 14/191 (7.3%) reported contraceptive use. Participants were mostly in their third (51/191; 26.7%) or fourth year (56/191; 29.3%) of study. A minority reported smoking (13/191; 6.8%), and 116/191 (60.7%) had health insurance coverage (Table 1).

Self‐Perceived Preference for Screening Method

Most participants preferred the Pink Luminous screening for regular breast examination, with (121/191; 63.4%) indicating their willingness to uptake it. In contrast, (70/191; 36.6%) of participants favored the breast self‐examination (BSE) method (Figure 2).

Significant associations were observed between preference for Pink Luminous and marital status (16/18; 88.9%), contraceptive use (12/14; 85.7%), and year of study (50/56; 89.3% among fourth‐year students) (all p < 0.001). Self‐Perceived Preference for Pink Luminous by Subgroups

Most participants preferred Pink Luminous over BSE (63.4%, 121/191; 95% CI: 56.1%–70.2%). This preference was found to be significantly associated with marital status: 88.9% of married participants (16/18; 95% CI: 65.3%–98.6%) favored Pink Luminous, compared to 60.7% (105/173; 95% CI: 53.1%–68.0%) of single participants (p < 0.001). Among contraceptive users, 85.7% (12/14; 95% CI: 57.2%–98.2%) preferred Pink Luminous, while all non‐users (100%, 9/9; 95% CI: 66.4%–100%) favored it (p < 0.001). The preference also varied significantly by year of study, with the highest rate observed among fourth‐year students (89.3%, 50/56; 95% CI: 77.8%–96.0%) (p < 0.001).

Participants with good breast cancer screening knowledge demonstrated a notably higher preference for Pink Luminous: (75.0%, 99/132; 95% CI: 66.6%–82.3%) compared to those with poor knowledge (33.3%, 20/60; 95% CI: 22.0%–46.3%) (p < 0.001) (Table 7).

Logistic Regression Analysis of Predictors of Preference for Pink Luminous Screening

A binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors of participants' preference for Pink Luminous breast screening over breast self‐examination (BSE). In the univariate analysis, students with good knowledge of breast cancer screening were significantly more likely to prefer Pink Luminous compared to those with poor knowledge (OR = 4.50, 95% CI: 2.19–9.25, p < 0.001). Similarly, year of study was a significant predictor, with fourth‐year students showing a strong preference for Pink Luminous compared to first‐year students (OR = 3.43, 95% CI: 1.37–8.59, p = 0.008). The use of contraceptive methods was also associated with a higher odds of preferring Pink Luminous (OR = 6.86, 95% CI: 1.42–33.1, p = 0.017). In the multivariate model, after adjusting for all variables, both the year of study and the knowledge level remained statistically significant. Fourth‐year students were nearly four times more likely to prefer Pink Luminous screening compared to first‐year students (adjusted OR = 3.78, 95% CI: 1.45–9.84, p = 0.007). Likewise, good knowledge of breast cancer screening remained a strong predictor (adjusted OR = 4.01, 95% CI: 1.88–8.56, p < 0.001). The association with contraceptive use showed a positive trend but did not reach statistical significance after adjustment (adjusted OR = 3.22, 95% CI: 0.94–11.04, p = 0.063) (Table 8).

Summary: Through triangulation and thematic analysis, the investigation of participants' comments indicates that the Pink Luminous Screening Tool is generally well‐received. It is praised for its effectiveness, ease of use, and accessibility. Nevertheless, the feedback also highlights areas that require enhancement, particularly regarding efficacy and security. Addressing these concerns is crucial to facilitating the tool's progress and increasing its adoption.

Discussion

Breast cancer remains a critical public health issue, and early detection through effective screening is essential for improving patient outcomes [32]. This study evaluated the effectiveness and adoption of the Pink Luminous Breast screening device compared to Breast Self‐Examination among nursing students, a population that plays a vital role in promoting breast health awareness and screening practices. Our findings provide insights into the factors influencing the perceived effectiveness and adoption of this innovative screening tool compared to the traditional screening method.

Among nursing students, PLB was preferred over BSE, with preference associated with marital status, contraceptive use, study year, and knowledge. While preference and perceived usability are important for acceptability, they are not substitutes for clinical effectiveness. The current evidence for LED transillumination, including PLB, remains preliminary and heterogeneous; transillumination in the early days was not sensitive enough as a tool for population screening [17, 18, 19], whereas current optical methods are being actively explored as adjuncts [11, 12, 13]. This promising data suggests that the Pink Luminous Breast device holds significant potential as an effective tool for early breast cancer detection among nursing students, instilling hope and optimism in the future of breast cancer detection.

Most people felt the simplicity of Pink Luminous device, but not many users would be able to evaluate the accuracy of the diagnostic procedure utilizing this device, compared to the gold‐standard mammography. This difference establishes a necessity of clinical validation studies suggesting that we cannot assume that usability leads to similar sensitivity or specificity.

The findings showed that more than 60% of participants reported that the Pink Luminous Breast device was more effective than the Breast Self‐Examination. However, comparing this with other studies, evaluating similar screening tools is essential. Our results are similar to Ocasio‐Villa et al. [14] where they also indicated the good perspective of the users regarding the Pink Luminous tool, as well as Sun et al., [33] whose data on the diagnosis using the new breast imaging technologies clearly demonstrates the value of the device in the emerging possibilities (sensitivity of 80%–90%). But, as opposed to Emami et al. [34], the perceived effectiveness was not found to be influenced by age, which may indicate the contextual variation between the student and the general populations. These emerging technologies, which are newer and more advanced, have shown sensitivity ranging from 80% to 90% in detecting breast cancer, compared to traditional mammography's sensitivity of around 70%–80%. This finding corroborates the study's assertion that the Pink Luminous Breast gadget has the potential to serve as a feasible substitute for early detection, especially among groups with restricted availability to conventional screening techniques.

Interestingly, marital status and contraceptive use were significantly associated with screening preferences. Married participants and those using contraceptives showed a stronger preference for Pink Luminous [34, 35]. Several studies have reported that married women are more likely to participate in breast cancer screening programs compared to unmarried women [35]. This could be attributed to the support and encouragement from spouses and the perceived need for early detection due to familial responsibilities.

The higher perceived effectiveness among fourth‐year nursing students compared to lower years is an exciting finding. As the students' progress through their nursing education, they gain more knowledge and exposure to breast health and screening practices. This may contribute to their positive perception of new screening tools like the Pink Luminous Breast device. This finding is supported by a study by Ranganath et al. [36], which found that nursing students in higher academic years had better knowledge and practices regarding breast self‐examination. The higher preference among fourth‐year students may reflect increased exposure to evidence‐based screening during clinical training. Similarly, married participants and those using contraceptives may perceive themselves as being at greater risk, influencing their openness to new technologies.

The study's findings reveal a significant association between personal and family history of breast cancer, as well as detecting abnormalities during screening, with the self‐reported effectiveness of the Pink Luminous Breast device aligns with [37, 38] reported that women with a personal or family history of breast cancer are often more vigilant about screening and may be more receptive to advanced screening tools. Similarly, individuals who detect abnormalities during screening may perceive the tool as more effective, as it has fulfilled its purpose of detecting potential issues.

There was a positive association between good knowledge of breast cancer screening and the perceived effectiveness of the Pink Luminous Breast device, which is consistent with previous studies. Increased knowledge and awareness about breast cancer screening have been linked to higher acceptance and adoption of screening practices [24]. This underscores the importance of individual responsibility in staying informed about breast cancer screening, as those with good knowledge may be better equipped to appreciate the potential benefits of innovative screening tools and be more willing to adopt them, empowering them to take charge of their health.

Participants with good knowledge of breast cancer screening were more likely to report the Pink Luminous Breast device as effective compared to those with poor knowledge. This finding is consistent with previous literature highlighting the importance of knowledge and awareness in promoting the adoption of breast cancer screening practices [39]. However, it is essential to note that a significant proportion of participants with good knowledge did not find the device practical, particularly in terms of its ease of use and accessibility. This suggests that while knowledge is a key factor in the perceived effectiveness of the device, practical considerations also play a significant role in its adoption. The strong preference among senior nursing students suggests integrating novel screening technologies into curricula may enhance acceptance and future advocacy.

This study relied on self‐reported preferences rather than objective behavioral outcomes. The purposive sampling method limits generalizability beyond nursing students. Additionally, no multivariate modeling was performed to adjust for potential confounders such as socioeconomic status or insurance coverage. Longitudinal follow‐up is required to assess real‐world performance of the Pink Luminous device.

Conclusion

The proves that the participants favored Pink Luminous tool rather than Breast Self‐Examination as a tool to conduct regular breast examination and this preference was associated with influxes such as marital status, the use of contraceptives, and the year of study. The people already having or having a family history of breast health issues or those educated about breast cancer screening were more likely to be attracted to the Pink Luminous way. The Pink Luminous gadget was more efficient and easier to use by nursing students, especially in their final years which justifies the need of incorporating modern screening technologies to the field of nursing education. This would enhance the future efficient practice of breast cancer screening in future care givers. Although Pink Luminous device received a positive response and seems to be effective, further clinical confirmation and long‐term research must be carried out to prove its accuracy in diagnosing and the long‐term value of its use in early detection.

Implications for the Profession and Patient Care

Nursing students, as prospective clinicians and educators, gain advantages from engagement with novel adjunct technologies, provided these are correctly contextualized as instruments that enhance awareness and facilitate prompt assessment, all while supporting compliance with evidence‐based screening recommendations [20, 21, 40]. With their influence, the healthcare system can improve early detection rates, leading to better patient outcomes and potentially reducing the overall burden of breast cancer.

Limitations

are purposive sampling, a reliance on self‑reported preference, and a lack of clinical outcomes. Future research must prospectively assess PLB against reference standards in follow‑up diagnosis, investigate learning curves, and determine if adjunct use influences presentation in a timely manner for diagnostic imaging.
