Are specific PTSD criteria associated with treatment outcome in traumatized veterans and police officers?
Authors: Ruud A Jongedijk, Jeroen W Knipscheer, Niels van der Aa, Annelies M de Haan, Rolf J Kleber, Paul A Boelen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: A significant proportion of patients with posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) do not respond sufficiently to trauma treatment. To date, it has not been clearly demonstrated which patients respond well and which do not.Objective: This study examined whether lifetime direct and interpersonal trauma exposure as well as the four distinct symptom clusters of PTSD were associated with treatment outcome.Method: Participants were 262 veterans and police officers with PTSD (measured by the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5, CAPS-5). Linear Mixed Model Analysis was performed to identify whether direct and interpersonal traumatization and the distinct symptom clusters of PTSD were associated with pre- to post-treatment changes in PTSD and general psychopathology according to the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) and Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI), respectively. Predictors were mean-centred to reduce multicollinearity in models with interaction terms.Results: 51% Of participants reported clinically significant improvement for PTSD symptoms and 45% for symptoms of general psychopathology. Trauma characteristics and the severity of four PTSD symptom clusters pre-treatment were not associated with changes in total PTSD scores. Time showed consistent significant PTSD improvement across models. Higher pre-treatment symptom severity of cluster D of PTSD was associated with greater improvement in general psychopathology from pre- to post-treatment. There were two non-significant trends: higher severity of cluster C symptoms before treatment tended to be associated with smaller improvement in cluster E, and higher severity on cluster D with greater improvement of the same cluster D.Conclusions: Direct and interpersonal trauma exposure as well as pretreatment PTSD symptom cluster scores were not robustly related to overall PTSD treatment outcome. However, across all models, a significant reduction in PTSD symptoms over time was observed. To improve treatment efficacy, future research is recommended to explore individual symptoms and psychosocial contexts to achieve more personalized treatment approaches.KEYWORDS: PTSD, trauma, symptoms, treatment outcome, veterans, police officers, linear mixed models, predictors

Introduction

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a common mental disorder that often occurs alongside other mental disorders and tends to have a chronic course (Brady et al., 2000; Spinhoven et al., 2014). Several psychotherapies have been proven to be effective in reducing PTSD symptoms with large effect sizes (Bisson & Olff, 2021). However, not all patients benefit equally: approximately one-third to one-half of patients still meet criteria for PTSD after treatment (Bradley et al., 2005; Keyan et al., 2024), and up to 75% of patients report residual symptoms after treatment (Bisson et al., 2013; Larsen et al., 2019).

To date, it is not yet fully understood which patients with PTSD will benefit from trauma-focused treatments and which may benefit less or not at all. Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses identified a wide variety of predictors of poor treatment outcome, including higher levels of PTSD symptom severity, comorbid depression, pain, sleep disturbances, anger, and alcohol abuse, low social support, poor quality of life, male gender, veteran, refugee, of non-Caucasian ethnicity, lower in education, or older in age (Barawi et al., 2020; Dewar et al., 2020; Keyan et al., 2024; Semmlinger et al., 2024). Particularly veterans with PTSD and combat experiences often experience poor treatment outcome (Jacoby et al., 2022; Keyan et al., 2024; Steenkamp et al., 2015). Another uniformed group with a high conditional risk of developing work-related PTSD are police officers (Maguen et al., 2009). Military and police service leads to different occupational environments and trauma exposures. Veterans, for example, have been sent abroad, have frequently found themselves in combat situations, and have often had difficulty adjusting upon returning from deployment (Romaniuk & Kidd, 2018); police officers often encounter traumatic events (such as riots, fatal accidents, suicides) in a civilian environment and for a much longer time during their career (Rentmeesters & Hermans, 2023). Despite these differences, they also have much in common. Evidence suggests that both groups encounter multiple serious, potentially traumatic events (PTEs) and report high levels of work-related stress (Blumberg & Papazoglou, 2019; Queirós et al., 2020). The current study aimed to determine whether specific pre-treatment components of PTSD are associated with treatment outcome in a sample including veterans and police officers.

Association of trauma types and treatment outcome

The DSM-5 definition of PTSD includes Criterion A, or the PTE (APA, 2013). Although most evidence suggests that the number of PTEs does not influence treatment outcome (Schneider et al., 2020), some studies have found a negative association (Keyan et al., 2024). However, several studies have shown that it is precisely the specific characteristics of PTEs that can influence treatment outcomes. For example, interpersonal PTEs, such as physical and sexual violence and combat trauma, have been found to be associated with poorer response to PTSD treatment compared to other types of PTEs (Fonzo et al., 2020; Jakob et al., 2017; Kessler et al., 2017). Interpersonal PTE types are thought to carry the highest risk for PTSD (Kessler et al., 2017). In particular, both sexual trauma and combat trauma were more highly associated with PTSD than other types of trauma in a veteran population (Jakob et al., 2017). In interpersonal trauma, the betrayal of trust can lead to complicating phenomena such as negative self-evaluation, powerlessness, as well as anger and thoughts of revenge. Furthermore, sexual trauma, especially within the military culture with masculine gender norms, increases shame and fear of exclusion, which makes recovery more difficult (Jakob et al., 2017; Litz et al., 2009).

Next to interpersonal PTEs, the proximity of experiencing a PTE may be an important determinant of treatment outcome (Jongedijk et al., 2022; Marx et al., 2024). In particular, it has been suggested that direct exposure to PTEs (i.e. directly experiencing a PTE yourself) will lead to a worse response to treatment than indirect exposure to PTEs, such as witnessing PTEs or learning that PTEs occurred in others. Direct trauma types typically lead to intense peritraumatic responses with higher levels of emotional and physical intensity compared to indirect trauma types, and therefore tend to result in a more profound disruption of psychophysiological reactivity and to the individual’s feelings of vulnerability, helplessness, sense of safety, and control. In this sense, it may overwhelm an individual's capacity to process the experience, especially when it involves a high degree of personal threat or physical injury (May & Wisco, 2016).

Association of pre-treatment PTSD severity and treatment outcome

Research on the relationship between pre-treatment PTSD severity and treatment outcomes in veterans has produced mixed results. Several studies reported that higher pre-treatment PTSD severity was associated with poorer treatment outcomes (Allan et al., 2017; Murphy & Smith, 2018; Phelps, Steel, et al., 2018), while other studies found no such association (Clapp et al., 2016) or even a positive association (Elliott et al., 2005; Forbes et al., 2003). Still other studies found that treatment was less effective for both low and high pre-treatment PTSD severity levels compared to moderate levels (Haagen et al., 2015; Walter et al., 2022). For police officers, one study reported that PTSD severity was unrelated to treatment outcome (Martinmäki et al., 2021). These varying findings may be due to methodological differences between studies, including differences in instruments used to assess outcomes, patient characteristics, research design, and treatment interventions (Elliott et al., 2005).

Association of the specific PTSD symptom clusters and treatment outcome

PTSD is defined very heterogeneously in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) (APA, 2013; Brady et al., 2000; Galatzer-Levy & Bryant, 2013); in addition to the definition of the traumatic stressor (Criterion A), many symptoms are grouped around four comprehensive symptom clusters (Criteria B-E), all associated with the traumatic event(s). Currently, treatments mostly focus on overall PTSD symptom change.

Studies assessing overall changes in PTSD symptoms without examining individual clusters or symptoms fail to identify which aspects of this heterogeneous disorder are more or less responsive to treatment or influential on recovery. For example, several trajectory studies among veterans found that the intrusion cluster showed the least improvement after treatment (Gross et al., 2024; Levi et al., 2022), while others found this to be the case in both the intrusion and avoidance clusters (Phelps, Steele, et al., 2018).

In the current study, we specifically examined which pre-treatment PTSD symptom clusters were associated with treatment outcome. To our knowledge, no study has previously examined this in veterans or police officers. Yet, several studies have shown that certain symptom profiles resembling PTSD symptom clusters are associated with poor response to trauma treatment, including depression and hyperarousal symptoms (Barawi et al., 2020; Dewar et al., 2020), both of which are associated with PTSDs symptom clusters D and E (Contractor, Caldas, et al., 2018). Depressive symptoms such as lowered mood and numbing can lead to reduced accessibility of emotions and cognitions that need to be elicited during exposure therapy (Angelakis & Nixon, 2015). The same is claimed for hyperarousal (Krupnik, 2021). Furthermore, depression may lead to avoidance strategies such as rumination and overgeneralization of traumatic memories, which may reduce the effectiveness of trauma therapy (Barawi et al., 2020; Rauch & Foa, 2006). Finally, neither depressive symptoms nor hyperarousal symptoms are specifically anxiety-related symptoms and may therefore respond less well to interventions focused on mitigating anxiety, such as exposure-focused treatments (Keyan et al., 2024; Semmlinger et al., 2024).

Several veteran studies found pre-treatment depressive symptoms or numbing symptoms (Murphy & Smith, 2018; Phelps, Steel, et al., 2018) and higher arousal, anger, and anxiety (Allan et al., 2017; Lloyd et al., 2014) to be associated with poor treatment response in veterans. Nevertheless, these findings are not unambiguous. For example, some studies found that depressive symptoms were not associated with poorer treatment outcomes (Walter et al., 2022), while others found that in particular, re-experiencing symptoms (in addition to depressive symptoms) were associated with poorer treatment outcomes (Murphy & Smith, 2018).

If PTSD clusters associated with poor treatment outcomes can be identified, this could lead to a more deliberate selection of existing treatments or the development of new interventions specifically targeting these clusters (Gross et al., 2024; Levi et al., 2022). Despite many similarities, the various evidence-based trauma-focused treatments all have their own focus, sometimes with more focus on fear provocation, and sometimes more on cognitive or contextual aspects of traumatization (Schnyder et al., 2015).

Aims of the study

Our first aim was to examine whether the lifetime number of PTE types related to interpersonal and direct trauma exposure, as well as pre-treatment severity scores of distinct PTSD symptom clusters, were associated with changes in PTSD severity and general psychopathology from pre- to post-treatment.

We measured ‘general psychopathology’ alongside PTSD to investigate whether this variable was specifically associated with PTSD or showed its own association. It is important to note that, with respect to trauma types, we assessed the total range of trauma types that the patient had experienced in their lifetime, rather than focusing on one specific index trauma. This approach was chosen to recognize the large number and variety of trauma types that our patients had experienced and because other significant PTEs, in addition to the index trauma, had also been part of the treatment.

Based on the limited literature as mentioned earlier, we expected that more direct trauma exposure, more interpersonal trauma exposure, and higher pre-treatment levels of the PTSD symptom cluster D and cluster E, in particular, would be associated with smaller reductions in symptom severity scores for PTSD and general psychopathology between pre- and post-treatment.

Our second aim was to gain insight into which PTSD symptom clusters would specifically show improvement between pre- and post-treatment. We therefore exploratively analysed the associations between distinct pre-treatment PTSD cluster severity scores and changes in these distinct PTSD cluster scores from pre-treatment to post-treatment.

Method

Participants and procedure

In this study secondary analyses of data from routine clinical assessments were performed. Participants were referred for outpatient treatment at ARQ Centrum’45, a Dutch national institute for diagnostics and treatment of long-lasting trauma-related disorders. The sample consisted of veterans and police officers who had been traumatized during their work. In general, all patients reported enduring PTSD according to the CAPS-5 and many comorbid symptoms according to the BSI, consistent with previous comparable study samples as described from our institute (Jongedijk et al., 2019; Martinmäki et al., 2021).

Based on impressions from clinical practice, approximately all veterans were no longer serving in the military and 80-90% of police officers were no longer serving in the police force. It was estimated that 15% of the study participants had a mixed background: they first served in the military and later became police officer. The majority of patients had been on sick leave for several years. Based on the relationship between the trauma and the current complaints, one of the two treatment programmes was chosen (see 2.2).

All veterans and police officers who enrolled the outpatient treatment programme between 2014 and 2022 were included in this study. Data were primarily collected for diagnostic and treatment purposes and secondarily used for research purposes. The Dutch versions of the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5 (CAPS-5) (Boeschoten et al., 2014a), Life Event Check List for DSM-5 (LEC-5) (Boeschoten et al., 2014b), PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) (Boeschoten et al., 2014c), and Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI) (De Beurs, 2011) were administered at the initial routine diagnostic assessment before the start of treatment. At the end of treatment, the PCL-5 and BSI were administered again. The CAPS-5 was administered by trained clinical staff members. All data were pseudonymized and stored in a research database.

Treatment programme

The treatment consisted of two specialized treatment programmes (one for veterans and one for police officers) for one day per week with an average duration of 19.37 months (SD = 12.01). Some patients received individual outpatient follow-up care for their residual complaints. The treatment programme always included at least one form of evidence-based trauma-focused treatment (TFT) and consisted of Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR), Prolonged Exposure (PE), Narrative Exposure Therapy (NET), or Brief Eclectic Psychotherapy for PTSD (BEPP). The focus of the trauma-focused therapies was not only on the index trauma, but on multiple, most disabling traumatic events in the patient's history. In the case of severe, persistent PTSD, individual trauma-focused therapies were usually supplemented by group treatments with e.g. art therapy, psychomotor therapy, and supportive counseling.

Measures

The Dutch version of the CAPS-5 was used to assess pre-treatment PTSD symptoms. The CAPS-5 is a 30-item structured clinical interview matching the DSM-5 classification for PTSD. Items with regard to symptom severity over the past month are rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 0 (absent) to 4 (extreme/incapacitating). Total severity scores were calculated by summing the 5 items of cluster B (re-experiencing), 2 items of cluster C (avoidance), 7 items of cluster D (negative alterations in cognition and mood), and 6 items of cluster E (hyper-arousal). Total scores range from 0-80, with higher scores indicating more severe PTSD symptoms. The Dutch CAPS-5 has shown good psychometric properties in a sample of patients with different trauma backgrounds (Boeschoten et al., 2018).

The Dutch version of the LEC-5 was used to assess exposure to PTEs. The LEC-5, a self-report questionnaire, comprises 17 PTE types for which participants need to indicate whether it: (a) happened to you personally, (b) you witnessed it happen to someone else, (c) you learned about it happening to a close family member or friend, (d) you were exposed to it as part of your job, (e) you’re not sure if it fits, and/or (f) it doesn’t apply to you. Exposure to a PTE was present if respondents responded with option a, b, c, or d. The total number of PTE types was computed by counting the number of PTE types to which a respondent was exposed to (response option ‘a’) and not the total amount of PTEs experienced. To distinguish between interpersonal and non-interpersonal PTEs, the LEC-5 items were independently reviewed and categorized into interpersonal and non-interpersonal PTE types by two of the authors (RJ and JK) with initial full agreement. According to this procedure, the following PTE types were identified as interpersonal PTEs: Physical assault (item 6), Assault with a weapon (7), Sexual assault (8), Other unwanted or uncomfortable sexual experience (9), Combat or exposure to a war-zone (10), and Captivity (11). The total number of interpersonal PTEs was computed by summing the overall number of interpersonal PTE types (i.e. option a, b, c, and d). Incidentally, directly experienced interpersonal PTEs count in both PTE categories, which means that there is an overlap in the numbers of the PTE categories ‘direct’ and ‘interpersonal’ PTE.

The Dutch version of the PCL-5 was used to measure pre- and post-treatment PTSD symptom severity. The PCL-5 is a self-report questionnaire, consisting of 20 items assessing how much a participant has been bothered by DSM-5 symptoms of PTSD over the past month. Items need to be rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely). PTSD symptom severity was calculated by summing the 5 items of cluster B, 2 items of cluster C, 7 items of cluster D, and 6 items of cluster E. For diagnosing PTSD, cut off scores are assumed to be between 31-33. Good psychometric properties have been reported for the Dutch version of the PCL-5 (Van der Meer et al., 2017; Van Praag et al., 2020).

The Dutch version of the BSI was used to measure general psychopathology. The BSI is a self-report questionnaire assessing how much a participant has been bothered by 53 symptoms of general psychopathology during the past week. The BSI covers nine symptom dimensions i.e. somatization, obsession-compulsion, interpersonal sensitivity, depression, anxiety, hostility, phobic anxiety, paranoid ideation, and psychoticism. Items need to be rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely). Severity of general psychopathology was computed by averaging all 53 item scores and has a clinical cutoff score of 1.2. The BSI is an international well validated instrument and good psychometric properties have been reported for the Dutch version (De Beurs, 2011).

The variable Time was coded as 0 = pre-treatment and 1 = post-treatment for interaction analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data processing and statistical analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics version 27. Linear Mixed Model analysis (LMM) was used because LMM can handle missing data flexibly by utilizing all available data instead of listwise deletion which is applied in other statistical techniques. This allows all available data to be included without excluding incomplete cases. The participants in the study sample (n = 262) had either a BSI or a PCL-5 pre- and post-measurement or both. Of these, 145 had completed the CAPS-5, 253 the PCL-5, 259 the BSI, and 145 the LEC-5 (see Table 1). Because LMM requires that data are missing at random, we conducted Little’s Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) test (Little, 1988) on our core numeric variables (pre–/post–PCL-5 and BSI totals, CAPS-5 clusters, trauma-load indices, demographic covariates, and treatment duration). To reduce the risk of overfitting the models (due to the moderate sample size in combination with many independent variables) the hypothesized associations were tested in separate models and all analyses were corrected for multiple testing by Bonferroni-correction. For aim 1, we applied a Bonferroni correction to the alpha threshold (.05 / 6 comparisons) and considered values of p < .0083 as statistically significant (ignoring the time-only models). For aim 2, we applied a Bonferroni correction to the alpha threshold (.05 / 4 comparisons) and considered values of p < .0125 as statistically significant (ignoring the time-only models).

First, in order to test whether severity of PTSD symptoms and general psychopathology decreased between pre- and post-treatment, two separate models including a fixed effect of time (pre- and post-treatment measurements) and a random intercept were used. A compound symmetry (CS) covariance structure was assumed for all models regarding change in outcome measures between pre- and post-treatment.

Next, to address the first aim, we tested whether the severity of each of the four PTSD symptom clusters (according to the CAPS-5), as well as the amount of interpersonal and direct PTEs (LEC-5) (all as independent variables), were associated with change in overall PTSD symptom severity (PCL-5) and general psychopathology (BSI) (as dependent variables) from pre- to post-treatment. To facilitate interpretation and reduce multicollinearity in models that included interaction terms, all continuous predictors were mean-centered prior to analysis. To accomplish this aim, next to the time-only model (Model 1), three separate models per outcome variable were used. Model 2 included two two-way interactions: fixed effects of time and total number of direct PTE types participants were exposed to and the total number of PTE types, as well as the interaction between time, total number of PTE types, and number of direct PTE types. Model 3 included the same variables, but regarding interpersonal PTE types. Because participants were exposed to many different PTE types, it is likely that they scored frequently on both categories of PTEs. In order to better highlight the relative share of the two PTE characteristics on the total of PTE types, the hypothesized effects of the amount of direct and interpersonal exposure to PTE types were included in the model as interactions with respect to the total number of PTE types. Model 4 included four two-way interactions: fixed effects of time and the severity of the four PTSD symptom clusters, as well as the interaction between time and these symptom clusters. The hypothesized effects of the amount of direct and interpersonal trauma exposure were included in the model as interactions because of the reason mentioned above.

Last, to address the second aim, we chose to repeat the analysis for exploratory purposes. We examined not only the influence of the severity scores of the four PTSD symptom clusters before treatment (CAPS-5) on the change in total PTSD severity scores from pre- to posttreatment (PCL-5) (aim two), but also on the changes in scores on the distinct PTSD symptoms clusters (PCL-5).

For the first aim, to examine changes in PTSD severity and in severity of general psychopathology, six LMMs were conducted, which included the predictors ‘interpersonal exposure to trauma’, ‘direct exposure to trauma’ and ‘baseline PTSD symptom cluster severity B-E’ (plus two time models). For the second aim, to examine changes in specific PTSD symptom clusters, four LMMs (one for each PTSD symptom cluster) were conducted for the predictor ‘baseline PTSD cluster severity’ (plus four time models).

Intra-individual changes between pre- and post-treatment in severity of PTSD symptoms and general psychopathology were examined using cut-off values for reliable change. A difference of 10 points between 2 measurements on the PCL-5 can be used as a minimum threshold for clinically meaningful change (Weathers et al., 2013). Likewise, a difference of 0.35 points between 2 measurements on the BSI can be used as a threshold for statistically reliable change (De Beurs, 2011).

Results

Descriptive and pre-treatment results

Between 2014 and 2022, 487 veterans and police officers were enrolled in an outpatient treatment programme at ARQ Centrum’45. Data from a subsample of 262 patients for whom pre- and/or post-treatment data on at least one of the two outcome measures were available were included in the statistical analyses. In Table 1 sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the study sample (N=262) are presented. The mean total number of PTE types (lifetime) per participant was 9.56, with the mean number of PTE types related to direct exposure being 4.91 and interpersonal exposure being 3.62, respectively. Symptom severity on the CAPS-5, PCL-5, and BSI was clearly within a clinical range.

The Little’s MCAR test on our core numeric variables (pre-/post-PCL-5 and BSI totals, CAPS-5 clusters, trauma-load indices, demographic covariates, and treatment duration) (χ²(539) = 578.65, p = .115) indicated that missingness in the tested data did not significantly deviate from MCAR.

Changes in PTSD and general psychopathology from pre- to post- treatment

The first objective of the study was to investigate whether changes in overall PTSD symptom severity and general psychopathology from pre-treatment to post-treatment were associated with direct exposure to trauma, interpersonal exposure to trauma, as well as pre-treatment symptom severity regarding the distinct PTSD symptom cluster severity (Criteria B-E). Results of the analyses are presented in Tables 2 and 3.

Results of the first model showed that there was a significant decrease in the severity of PTSD symptoms (Table 2) and general psychopathology (Table 3) from pre- to post-treatment. Across all models, Time emerged as a strong and consistent predictor of symptom reduction (p < .001), reflecting that symptoms decreased consistently over time. Analysis of intra-individual change indicated that 51% of patients reported clinically meaningful improvement with regard to PTSD symptom severity, 39% remained unchanged and 11% deteriorated. With regard to general psychopathology, 45% of the patients reported statistically reliable change, 39% remained unchanged, and 16% deteriorated.

Neither the interaction of the number of direct PTE types with the total number of PTE types nor the interaction of the number of interpersonal PTE types with the total number of PTE types was significantly associated with the difference in PTSD severity or general psychopathology from pre- to post-treatment. This indicated that the amount of exposure to direct trauma types and the amount of exposure to interpersonal trauma types are not significantly related to change in the severity of PTSD symptoms and general psychopathology during trauma treatment (Tables 2 and 3).

Results of the fourth model (Table 2) showed that none of the pre-treatment PTSD symptom clusters were significantly associated with change in overall PTSD symptom severity. However, the results of the fourth model (Table 3) showed that more severe pre-treatment Criterion D symptoms were significantly associated with a larger decrease in severity of general psychopathology (p < .005). The other PTSD symptom clusters were not significantly associated with changes in general psychopathology.

Changes in PTSD symptom clusters during treatment

With regard to the second aim of the study, we explored the associations of pre-treatment scores on the four PTSD symptom clusters according to the CAPS-5 with changes in the same symptom clusters from pre-treatment to post-treatment, as assessed with the PCL-5. Four separate LMMs were conducted, each with one symptom cluster (B, C, D, and E) as the outcome. Each model included a fixed effect of Time and a two-way interaction between Time and the corresponding pre-treatment symptom cluster score (Tables 4–7).

Across all models, Time was a significant predictor of symptom reduction (p < .001), confirming that patients showed improvement across PTSD symptom clusters from pre- to post-treatment. The estimates were precise, with relatively small standard errors and narrow confidence intervals. While most pre-treatment symptom cluster scores were not significantly associated with changes in their respective outcomes, higher pre-treatment Criterion D (Negative alterations in cognitions and mood) scores tended to be associated with greater reductions in that same cluster over time (PCL-5, p = .012) (Table 6). Additionally, higher pre-treatment Criterion C (Avoidance) scores were associated with less improvement in Criterion E (Arousal) symptoms (PCL-5, p = .030) (Table 7). These findings suggest potential symptom-specific patterns in treatment responsiveness, although most interactions did not reach statistical significance after correction for multiple testing.

Discussion

Most treatment outcome studies take PTSD as a full diagnosis. Because PTSD, especially in the DSM-5, has a great diversity of symptoms, it is very relevant to focus on the distinct components of PTSD. To investigate which components of PTSD are specifically associated with treatment outcome, the current study examined whether two aspects of Criterion A (i.e. direct trauma exposure and interpersonal trauma exposure; assessed by a standardized self-report questionnaire) and the pre-treatment severity of distinct PTSD symptom clusters (Criteria B to E, assessed by a clinical interview) were associated with (a) changes in the total severity of PTSD and general psychopathology from pre-treatment to post-treatment and (b) changes in the distinct PTSD cluster scores from pre-treatment to post-treatment (as assessed by standardized self-report questionnaires).

In this study, the total number of PTEs was not summed, but the total number of reported PTE types, as categorized in the LEC-5. Not only the trauma burden, but also the nature of different PTEs types appears to influence symptom presentations and possibly also treatment outcome (Contractor, Caldas, et al., 2018). In this regard, the index trauma was not categorized according to the CAPS-5. Compared to patients with fewer/single PTEs, in patients with multiple PTEs, a narrow focus on the index trauma may be insufficient to reduce the more severe and broader posttraumatic symptomatology in treatment (see e.g. Contractor, Greene, et al., 2018). In clinical practice, these patients will not only be treated for the index trauma, but usually also for other PTEs. By working on multiple adverse traumas in treatment, different symptom patterns associated with the different traumas are addressed. This can significantly prolong the trauma treatment. Nevertheless, this approach is in line with clinical practice.

Changes in PTSD and general psychopathology

Our first goal focused on changes during treatment. About half of the participants reported improvement in total PTSD symptoms (51%) and general psychopathology (45%) after treatment. Alongside improvement on total symptom severity, the distinct PTSD symptom clusters also improved during treatment. However, a significant proportion of participants reported unchanged or worsened symptoms, highlighting the importance of identifying which PTE types and PTSD symptom clusters influence treatment outcome.

Contrary to our hypothesis, PTE types defined as ‘direct exposure’ and with an ‘interpersonal nature’ were not associated with less reduction of PTSD and general psychopathology symptoms over the course of treatment. Several factors may explain this. Participants reported a wide range of PTE types (mean 9.58), making it difficult to distinguish between types of traumas and likely blurring the boundaries between ‘direct versus indirect’ and ‘interpersonal versus non-interpersonal’ trauma and therefore their distinct influence. Additionally, as several studies suggest, the impact of PTEs in patients with long-standing psychological complaints may be overshadowed by long-term post-trauma stressors such as persistent psychological problems, relationship problems, work and adjustment problems, and other health problems (e.g. Armour et al., 2015; Jongedijk et al., 2019). Furthermore, early childhood trauma, an important form of interpersonal trauma, was not explicitly measured in the current study, although it has a significant impact on mental health symptomatology and treatment (Contractor, Caldas, et al., 2018).

We hypothesized that the severity of pre-treatment symptom clusters D and E would be associated with less changes on both the PCL-5 and BSI between pre- and post-treatment. However, none of the distinct pre-treatment PTSD symptom clusters were associated with changes between pre- and post-treatment total PTSD scores. Time was a consistent and significant predictor across models, with relatively narrow confidence intervals. Only (higher) severity of PTSD symptom cluster D was associated with better improvement of symptoms associated with overall general psychopathology (BSI).

Based on these findings, it can be argued that the symptom clusters of PTSD are not distinctive and predictive of changes in overall PTSD symptom severity during treatment. This would mean that the heterogeneity of the current PTSD concept according to DSM-5 does not have significance for predicting treatment outcomes. It seems that this heterogeneity is mainly intended to encompass the multitude of psychological complaints that patients exhibit (Jongedijk et al., 2023) and that there are likely other predictors than the PTSD symptom clusters that are associated with treatment outcome.

First, it might be insufficient to look at the symptom clusters as a whole, as they might still be too heterogeneous. Specific symptoms within the PTSD clusters might be a better predictor of treatment result. However, the findings about this in the literature are not always clear (Dewar et al., 2020) and it may be necessary to look at symptoms more specifically: A recent review showed that, for example, within the sleep disorder symptoms, specifically ‘sleep disordered breathing’ influenced the outcome of PTSD treatment (Bottari et al., 2023). In addition, there are several other factors besides symptoms that can influence treatment results and which were not investigated in the current study, such as persistent psychosocial stress, maladaptive coping and attachment styles, lack of social support, poor quality of life (Dewar et al., 2020; Keyan et al., 2024), financial compensation procedures (Martinmäki et al., 2021), or consistent childhood emotional neglect (Teicher et al., 2022).

Changes of distinct PTSD symptom clusters

One of our findings was that higher severity scores on Criterion D of PTSD (negative alterations in cognition and mood) were associated with better treatment outcome for general psychopathology. Moreover, in our explorative analyses we found that higher severity scores of symptom cluster D (CAPS-5) tended to be associated with a greater reduction in specifically symptom cluster D and not in the other symptom clusters (PCL-5). Our specialist treatment programme, which included both evidence-based trauma-oriented therapy and additional treatment modules, provided additional attention to general psychopathology, possibly particularly in patients with high cluster D scores. These patients likely received targeted interventions for depression-related symptoms, leading to better outcomes for this specific aspect of psychopathology. Furthermore, trauma-focused treatment itself is effective for general and depression-related psychopathology in addition to PTSD symptomatology (Dominguez et al., 2021).

Another finding was a trend showing that the more severe the pre-treatment avoidance symptoms were (symptom cluster C on the CAPS-5), the less change in arousal symptoms (changes in symptom cluster E on PCL-5 from pre- to post-treatment) was observed. This finding may be interesting, because Smid et al. (2018) found that symptoms of cluster E, in particular concentration problems, persisted in a significant proportion of police officers after trauma treatment. Perhaps in patients with high scores on the PTSD avoidance cluster, extra attention should be paid to these symptoms in treatment in order to achieve a greater reduction in the arousal related Cluster E symptoms.

These findings align with research on processes maintaining PTSD symptoms. The cognitive model of PTSD (Ehlers & Clark, 2000) explains how persistent psychopathology is often associated with maladaptive strategies such as negative appraisal and avoidance, which lead to persistent feelings of threat. From this perspective, cognitive therapy for PTSD (CT) has been developed (Ehlers et al., 2005). CT focuses primarily on changing these maladaptive strategies. This approach differs slightly from exposure-based trauma therapies that participants in our study underwent and which are largely based on the emotional processing theory (Rauch & Foa, 2006). Arguably, CT can be particularly useful for patients with persistent complaints. For example, the results of the current study suggest that more severe avoidance may be associated with less change in hyperarousal symptoms. By having patients experiment with breaking through avoidance using CT, hypervigilance and other PTSD symptoms may be reduced. As mentioned before, trajectory studies have shown that such processes often precede apparent symptom reduction (Phelps, Steele, et al., 2018).

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study examining the association between the different PTSD components according to DSM-5 in conjunction with each other and treatment outcome in such a specific patient sample. The sample was quite large in size and was a naturalistic, clinical sample of multiple traumatized patients with severe and long-lasting psychotrauma complaints. Moreover, pre-treatment PTSD diagnosis was carried out with the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5 (CAPS-5), which is seen as the golden standard in diagnosing PTSD.

The study had also limitations. First, there was a fairly large amount of missing data, particularly in the post-treatment assessments. For example, not all patients completed both pre- and post-treatment measures of the CAPS-5, PCL-5, or BSI. To address this, we used Linear Mixed Models, which allow inclusion of participants with partial data. Although missingness was plausibly MCAR, the reduced sample size may have limited statistical power, especially for detecting smaller or interaction effects. Second, as the study was a naturalistic study based on archival data, there was therefore no standardized treatment procedure and no comparison group and consequently no causal conclusions about the intervention can be drawn. Third, data on change between pre- and post-treatment came from self-report questionnaires, which may introduce response bias compared to clinician-based clinical interviews. Fourth, we did not measure the overall total amount of PTEs but specifically the number of different PTE types, which means that nothing can be said about the influence of the total number of PTEs. Fifth, we had only global information about the content of treatments, but no precise information about, for example, the total number of sessions, the duration, complementary treatments such as supportive therapies and pharmacotherapy, or the length and the nature and precise focus of trauma treatment. Sixth, it should be noted that we considered the influence of the cumulative, total reported PTE types in the participants’ history on psychopathology and not just the influence of the index trauma according to the CAPS-5. This means, among other things, that it is not clear whether the same index trauma was used in the pre-treatment assessment and in the post-treatment measurements, which could explain non-significant associations. And finally, the nature of the sample (uniformed patients who had previously had one or more trauma-oriented treatments and showed long-term psychotrauma complaints) implies that extrapolation of the current findings to other traumatized patient groups should be done cautiously.

While this study revealed significant time effects across all models, some trauma-related interaction terms remained non-significant. This may reflect either genuinely small effects or residual limitations in statistical power for detecting higher-order interactions. Therefore, we recommend interpreting those exploratory findings with caution.

Implications

Although we found a significant reduction in PTSD severity and general psychopathology symptoms in a sample of severely traumatized patients, half did not benefit from treatment, prompting further investigation into the associations between the different components of PTSD and outcome of the treatment.

No association was found between the amount of direct and interpersonal traumatization and treatment outcome. We also found no influence of the distinct pre-treatment PTSD symptom clusters on treatment outcome. However, it turned out that a higher severity of cognition and mood related symptoms in cluster D (according to the CAPS) was associated with a stronger improvement in general psychopathology (according to the BSI) over time.

As we were unable to identify PTSD symptom clusters associated with PTSD treatment outcome, it could be argued that these clusters are too heterogeneous. After all, they contain from two to even seven symptom items. It is possible that the distinct symptoms within the clusters have more influence on treatment outcome than the overall symptom clusters. This is consistent with the literature that advocates a more symptom-oriented diagnostic approach (Fried, 2022; Schmidt, 2015). It is recommended that future studies look more into the role of specific symptoms and their association with response to PTSD treatment outcome. This could yield patient profiles that provide insight into which patient will respond better or less well to treatment. From here, patients who do not respond well to treatment could be offered a more specific existing or a renewed or adapted treatment approach, which could be targeted more specifically at specific symptom profiles (Bryant, 2025). Furthermore, the findings of specific symptom profiles may have conceptual consequences: they can increase knowledge that can lead to insights into the fundamental processes underlying treatment and can facilitate modifications or the development of new approaches that improve treatment outcomes (Barawi et al., 2020). In addition, they can identify PTSD symptoms that are more relevant than others, potentially reducing the heterogeneity of the current PTSD construct (Jongedijk et al., 2023).

Next to these reflections, we argue for a broader view than just psychopathology. Both for diagnostics, treatment and for future research, it is recommended to pay attention to more context-related factors that could be associated with treatment outcome, such as comorbid mental and physical problems, psychosocial dysfunctions, dysfunctional personality variables, and also pre-trauma variables such as a history of emotional neglect in childhood.
