Infective Endocarditis Complicated by a Mitral–Aortic Junction Abscess
Authors: Yasmin Zouggari, Iman Chouchane, Paolo Scarfo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPerivalvular abscess is a life‐threatening complication of infective endocarditis. Early recognition using transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), including advanced three‐dimensional (3D) imaging, is crucial for diagnosis, surgical planning, and timely intervention. This case highlights the value of 3D TEE in defining peri‐annular extension and guiding complex procedures, such as the Commando surgery.

Clinical Image

Perivalvular abscess is a severe complication of infective endocarditis, occurring in 30%–40% of cases, and more frequently involving the aortic valve [1]. It is associated with increased morbidity and mortality [1].

A 65‐year‐old man presented to the emergency department with progressive dyspnea and chest pain on exertion. Symptoms had worsened over the previous 48 h with significant functional limitation. On examination, the patient was afebrile (36.5°C), tachycardic (110 bpm), with signs of pulmonary congestion.

The electrocardiogram showed no signs of ischemia. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed a mass in the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) associated with moderate aortic regurgitation.

TEE revealed widespread infective endocarditis of the mitral and aortic valves. In the two‐chamber view at 70°, a large abscess was identified at the mitral–aortic junction, measuring 48 × 31 mm (Figure 1).

Transesophageal echocardiography Mid‐esophageal two‐chamber view (70°) showing a large peri‐annular abscess (48 × 31 mm) at the mitral‐aortic junction.

Further evaluation of the aortic valve in the short‐axis view at 45° (Figure 2) and with X‐plane imaging at 135° (Figure 3) showed destruction of the left coronary cusp and the non‐coronary cusp, resulting in severe aortic regurgitation due to leaflet perforation and loss of coaptation [2].

Transesophageal echocardiography Aortic valve short‐axis view (45°) showing destruction of the left coronary and non‐coronary cusps.

Transesophageal echocardiography X‐plane (135°) showing leaflet perforation and loss of coaptation.

A 3D TEE acquisition (Figure 4) provided precise anatomical delineation of the abscess cavity and its extension.

Three‐dimensional (3D) transesophageal echocardiography view of the aortic valve demonstrating the presence of an abscess at the mitral‐aortic junction (indicated by arrow).

Color Doppler imaging demonstrated systolic flow entering the abscess cavity during ventricular systole, suggesting fistulization into the intra‐aortic space (Figure 5).

Color Doppler transesophageal echocardiography demonstrating systolic flow filling the abscess cavity, indicating fistulization into the intra‐aortic space.

The Heart Team recommended urgent surgery. The patient underwent combined aortic and mitral valve replacement with reconstruction of the intervalvular fibrous body (also known as the Commando procedure). Unfortunately, he died postoperatively from cardiogenic shock.

This case underscores the significance of multimodality imaging and illustrates how 3D transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) can improve lesion characterization and guide complex surgical decisions in patients with high‐risk infective endocarditis.

Echocardiography remains an essential diagnostic tool, particularly in native valve endocarditis. TEE offers superior diagnostic sensitivity and specificity for detecting vegetations, abscesses, pseudoaneurysms, intracardiac fistulas, valvular perforations, and aneurysms [1].

However, conventional 2D TEE has its limitations when it comes to visualizing volumetric structures.

In this case, 3D transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) addressed previous limitations by offering unlimited planar reconstructions (Multiplanar Reconstruction, MPR) and realistic volumetric views (Volume Rendering, VR). This provided detailed structural information essential for planning a complex Commando surgery.

The MPR tool allowed for precise evaluation of the morphology of the vegetation, accurate measurement of lesion dimensions, and enhanced detection of paravalvular complications. Meanwhile, the VR imaging produced realistic, three‐dimensional images that illustrated the spatial relationships of the affected structures.

For this patient, 3D TEE effectively defined the borders of the abscess, demonstrated its connection with the left ventricular outflow tract, and clearly depicted its spatial relationship with the mitral‐aortic junction.
