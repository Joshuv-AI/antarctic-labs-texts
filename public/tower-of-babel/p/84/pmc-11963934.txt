Electrophysiological Mechanism of Catestatin Antiarrhythmia: Enhancement of I to, I K, and I K1 and Inhibition of I Ca ‐L in Rat Ventricular Myocytes
Authors: Ying Zhang, Hua Chen, Qingmin Ma, Hui Jia, Hongyu Ma, Zishuo Du, Yan Liu, Xiangjian Zhang, Yi Zhang, Yue Guan, Huijie Ma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular disease remains one of the leading causes of death globally. Myocardial ischemia and infarction, in particular, frequently cause disturbances in cardiac electrical activity that can trigger ventricular arrhythmias. We aimed to investigate whether catestatin, an endogenous catecholamine‐inhibiting peptide, ameliorates myocardial ischemia‐induced ventricular arrhythmias in rats and the underlying ionic mechanisms.Methods and ResultsAdult male Sprague‐Dawley rats were randomly divided into control and catestatin groups. Ventricular arrhythmias were induced by ligation of the left anterior descending coronary artery and electrical stimulation. Action potential, transient outward potassium current, delayed rectifier potassium current, inward rectifying potassium current, and L‐type calcium current (I Ca‐L) of rat ventricular myocytes were recorded using a patch‐clamp technique. Catestatin notably reduced ventricular arrhythmia caused by myocardial ischemia/reperfusion and electrical stimulation of rats. In ventricular myocytes, catestatin markedly shortened the action potential duration of ventricular myocytes, which was counteracted by potassium channel antagonists TEACl and 4‐AP, and I Ca‐L current channel agonist Bay K8644. In addition, catestatin significantly increased transient outward potassium current, delayed rectifier potassium current, and inward rectifying potassium current density in a concentration‐dependent manner. Catestatin accelerated the activation and decelerated the inactivation of the transient outward potassium current channel. Furthermore, catestatin decreased I Ca‐L current density in a concentration‐dependent manner. Catestatin also accelerated the inactivation of the I Ca‐L channel and slowed down the recovery of I Ca‐L from inactivation.ConclusionsCatestatin enhances the activity of transient outward potassium current, delayed rectifier potassium current, and inward rectifying potassium current, while suppressing the I Ca‐L in ventricular myocytes, leading to shortened action potential duration and ultimately reducing the ventricular arrhythmia in rats.Keywords: action potential, arrhythmia, catestatin, L‐type calcium channel, potassium channelSubject Categories: Arrhythmias, Electrophysiology

Cardiovascular disease, principally ischemic heart disease and stroke, remains the leading cause of death worldwide, with an estimated 17.9 million people succumbing to it annually, making up 32% of the total global deaths worldwide. 1 , 2 Myocardial ischemia and infarction often result in disturbances in cardiac electrical activity, 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 which can trigger and maintain life‐threatening ventricular arrhythmias such as premature ventricular contractions, ventricular tachycardia, and ventricular fibrillation (VF). 8 These ventricular arrhythmias may lead to sudden cardiac death. 2

Catestatin, an endogenous 21‐amino acid peptide derived from proteolytic cleavage of chromogranin A, exhibits cardioprotective and anti‐inflammatory properties. 9 Clinical studies have confirmed that serum catestatin concentration markedly increases in atrial fibrillation, 10 and plasma catestatin level also rises in patients with myocardial ischemia, 11 , 12 suggesting a compensatory increase in heart disease. Patients with low circulating catestatin levels are more likely to develop arrhythmogenic ventricular attacks. 12 Therefore, catestatin can be used as a valuable biomarker for forecasting the onset of cardiovascular disease. 11 , 12 , 13 Moreover, catestatin has therapeutic potential for the treatment of ischemic heart disease. Chronic catestatin treatment reduces atrial fibrillation vulnerability in rats with myocardial infarction‐induced heart failure, 14 and decreases the cardiac sympathetic drive 15 and ventricular arrhythmias caused by myocardial infarction in rats. 16 This suggests that the increase in catestatin may be a compensatory response during myocardial infarction. However, the cellular and ionic mechanisms underlying the cardioprotective and antiarrhythmic effect of catestatin are still unclear.

K+ currents plays a crucial role in the repolarization of the cardiac action potential (AP), 17 and malfunction of ventricular K+ channels due to cardiac ischemia can often cause fatal arrhythmias. 18 Dysfunction of transient outward potassium current (I to) channels has been associated with various types of arrhythmias, including ventricular tachycardia and fibrillation. 19 Mutations in genes encoding I to channels (KV4.3) can lead to inherited arrhythmia syndromes such as Brugada syndrome and early repolarization syndrome. 20 Moreover, a decrease in delayed rectifier K+ current (I K) results in high risk for torsades de pointes‐type polymorphic ventricular tachycardia and VF. 21 In addition, activation of the L‐type calcium channel (I Ca‐L) provides the necessary calcium entry for excitation‐contraction coupling and contributes to the plateau phase of the action potential of cardiomyocytes. 22 Due to these crucial physiological roles, even minor disruptions in I Ca‐L function can result in fatal cardiac arrhythmias. 23 Therefore, we hypothesize that catestatin may reduce ventricular arrhythmias by modulating the function of K+ and Ca2+ channels.

In this study, we investigated the effect of catestatin on ischemia‐induced arrhythmias in an acute myocardial ischemia and reperfusion (I/R) rat model and electrical stimulation‐triggered arrhythmias in isolated rat heart. Then, the effects of catestatin on AP and current as well as changes in the potassium channels (eg, I to, I K, and I K1) and I Ca‐L were investigated by single‐cell patch clamp.

METHODS

Animals

Forty‐two adult male Sprague‐Dawley rats (250–280 g) were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China) and randomly divided into control and catestatin groups using a random‐number method. All rats were kept in an animal care facility at 22 °C with a 12‐hour/12‐hour light/dark cycle and provided with standard rodent laboratory food and water ad libitum. The experiments were performed following the National Research Council's Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments 2.0 guideline, and the animal experimental protocols used in this study were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Hebei Medical University (IACUC‐Hebmu‐2021007). All experiments were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health (publication number 85–23, revised 1996). We made efforts to minimize both the suffering and the number of animals used.

Acute Myocardial I/R Model of Rat In Vivo

Rats were anesthetized intraperitoneally with urethane (800 mg/kg) and ventilated with a rodent ventilator (RWD‐407; Shenzhen, China) at 60 to 70 breaths per minutes and a tidal volume of 15 mL/kg. An ECG of lead II was recorded using a PowerLab8 system with LabChart 8.0 (AD Instruments, Australia). After thoracotomy in the third and fourth intercostal space, the left anterior descending coronary artery was ligated with a 5‐0 silk suture between the pulmonary conus and the left atrial appendage to establish the myocardial I/R model. A successful I/R model was confirmed by observation of acute ST‐segment elevation and T‐wave changes on the body surface ECG and regional pallor on the cardiac surface. A catheter was inserted into the right jugular vein, and catestatin was administered in the catestatin group of rats, whereas the control group of rats were injected with the same dose of saline. Arrhythmias were identified according to Lambeth conventions, 24 and the severity of the arrhythmia was evaluated by using an arrhythmia score based on the Johnston criteria. 25

Optical Mapping Determination of Rat Isolated Heart In Vitro

Rats were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of urethane (800 mg/kg). Heparin (1000 U/kg) was given intraperitoneally as an anticoagulant 10 minutes before surgery. Rat heart was excised and retrogradely perfused via the aorta with Krebs‐Henseleit solution (95% O2/5% CO2) at 8 mL/min and 37 °C on a Langendorff apparatus. First, the heart was stabilized by perfusion with Krebs‐Henseleit solution for 20 minutes. Then, 300 μL of blebbistatin (1 mg/mL in DMSO, number APN22087‐1‐1; Abcam) was added to 100 mL of circulating Krebs‐Henseleit solution and injected through the delivery port. Blebbistatin gradually decreased cardiac motion artifacts and halted the heartbeat within approximately 1 to 2 minutes. F‐127 (20% w/v in DMSO, number 2291566; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was perfused for 10 minutes, followed by perfusion of a voltage sensitive dye RH 237 (1 mg/mL in DMSO, number SC‐499456; Santa Cruz) for another 10 minutes to record the AP. Fluorescence above 638 nm was filtered using a 700 nm long‐pass filter and captured by a complementary metal oxide semiconductor camera for recording voltage signals (OMS‐PCIE‐2002; MappingLab). The total mapping area of the heart is about 16 × 16 mm, with a spatial resolution of 128×128 pixels and a temporal resolution of 900 frames/s. The data were recorded using OMapRecord 4.0 software (MappingLab). For the analysis of optical mapping signals and generation of isochronal maps, the data were processed with OMapScope 5.0 software (MappingLab).

A bipolar pacing lead was positioned at the apex of the left ventricle, with an output set at twice the diastolic threshold current. To determine the diastolic pacing threshold, the stimulation current was gradually raised from 0 mA (over a 2‐milliseconds interval), and the ECG was simultaneously recorded. The diastolic pacing threshold current intensity was established when the ECG exhibited significant 1:1 pacing at the specified stimulation frequency. The pacing current intensity was adjusted to twice the diastolic threshold, and then the sinus AP signal was recorded.

The S1S1 pacing protocol was performed with a series of pulse trains at a pulse width of 2 milliseconds. Starting at 150 milliseconds, S1S1 interval was progressively shortened to 40 milliseconds by 10‐milliseconds decrements until the arrhythmia was elicited. Each S1S1 pacing train consisted of 30 pulses and was separated by a 2‐s pause. A sustained VF lasting >1 minute was considered as a successful arrhythmia induction.

Isolation of Rat Ventricular Myocytes

Rat ventricular myocytes were isolated by enzymatic digestion as described previously. 26 Briefly, rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of urethane (800 mg/kg). Heparin (1000 U/kg) was injected 10 minutes beforehand for anticoagulation. Rat hearts were excised, and Tyrode solution was retrogradely perfused via the aorta at a perfusion rate of 8 mL/min for 2 minutes on a Langendorff apparatus. The heart was then perfused with Ca2+‐free Tyrode solution supplemented with 2,3‐butanedione monoxime (15 mmol/L) and collagenase II (300 mg/L) at 37 °C for 5 minutes. Finally, the left ventricle was removed and cut into small pieces in Krebs solution. The isolated ventricular myocytes were collected after filtration through a nylon mesh (pore size 200 μm) and stored in Krebs solution at room temperature for at least 1 hour. The concentration of Ca2+ in the Ca2+‐free Tyrode solution was gradually restored to 1.8 mmol/L before the experiment.

Cell Culture

HEK293 cells stably transfected with α1C (CaV1.2)/α2δ/β2a subunits were cultured in DMEM (high glucose; Gibco, United States) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (ScienCell, United States) and 1% penicillin streptomycin mixture (Gibco) in 5% CO2 at 37 °C. When cells reached 70% to 80% confluence, they were treated with 0.25% trypsin‐EDTA (Gibco) and transferred into disposable petri dishes (diameter: 3 cm) in preparation for patch‐clamp electrophysiological experiments.

Electrophysiological Recording

Whole‐cell patch‐clamp recordings were performed on isolated ventricular myocytes at room temperature. Myocytes were placed in the recording chamber (1.0 mL) mounted on the stage of an inverted microscope (IX71; Olympus, Japan). After settling to the bottom of the chamber, the cells were perfused with extracellular solution for 5 minutes at a rate of 2 to 3 mL/min. Signals were processed using an Axon MultiClamp 200B amplifier (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA), filtered at 2 kHz, and digitized at 10 kHz with a Digidata 1440A (Molecular Devices). The electrode for recordings was pulled from borosilicate glass capillaries with a micropipette puller (P‐97; Sutter Instrument, Novato, CA). The impedance of the pipette was 2.5 to 5 MΩ after the pipette was filled with internal pipette solution. After the giga Ω seal was formed, the cell membrane was ruptured with a brief vacuum to obtain the whole‐cell configuration. Computer‐generated voltage pulses were programmed using pCLAMP 10.2 software (Axon Instruments). The AP was evoked by current injection of 600 to 1200 picoampere (pA) at a frequency of 1 Hz for a duration of 5 milliseconds in current‐clamp mode.

The potassium current was recorded in voltage clamp mode. To study I to and I k, serial test pulses were applied from a holding potential of −80 mV to +60 mV in 10‐mV increments and 500‐milliseconds duration. The steady‐state activation curves of I to were derived from the current‐voltage (I‐V) curve of I to. The voltage dependence of steady‐state inactivation was examined by applying prepulses from −120 mV to +30 mV in duration of 500 milliseconds before a test pulse of +60 mV (500 milliseconds). To determine the kinetics of recovery from the inactivated channel state, 2 identical 500‐milliseconds depolarizing pulses were applied, ranging from −80 mV to +60 mV, with pulse interval varying from 0 to 3000 milliseconds. Kinetic curves were constructed by normalizing I to after each prepulse (I/I max) and plotting these values against prepulse voltage. Boltzmann distributions were fitted to these normalized values: I/I max=1/(1+exp–[(V 1/2−V m)/k]).

I K1 was recorded by 500‐milliseconds test pulses ranging between −100 mV and +30 mV in 10‐mV increments, starting from a holding potential of −80 mV. A prepulse of −40 mV for 100 milliseconds was applied to block I to, and values obtained at the end of the 500‐milliseconds pulse were used for analysis.

To study I Ca‐L, serial test pulses were applied from a potential of −50 to +60 mV in 10‐mV increments that lasted 300 milliseconds, starting from a holding potential of −80 mV. A prepulse of −60 mV for 1000 milliseconds was applied to block the potassium channel.

For CaV1.2‐HEK293 cell line, serial test pulses were applied from a potential of −50 to +60 mV in 10 mV increments that lasted 100 milliseconds, starting from a holding potential of −80 mV. A prepulse of −60 mV for 1000 milliseconds was applied to block the potassium channel.

Kinetic measurements of I Ca‐L were then determined in additional experiments. The steady‐state activation curves of I Ca‐L were derived from the I‐V curve of I Ca‐L. The voltage‐dependent steady‐state inactivation I Ca‐L channel was examined by applying a series of prepulses (from −80 mV to +30 mV in 10‐mV increments for a duration of 1000 milliseconds) followed by test pulses (holding potential at −10 mV for 300 milliseconds) to the myocyte. To determine the kinetics of recovery from the inactivated channel state, we applied 2 identical 500 milliseconds depolarizing pulses from −80 mV to −10 mV, varying the interpulse interval from 0 to 3000 milliseconds. Kinetic curves were constructed by normalizing measured I Ca‐L after each prepulse (I/I max) and plotting these values against prepulse voltage. Boltzmann distributions were fitted to these normalized values: I/I max=I/I max=1/(1+exp–[(V 1/2−V m)/k]).

Statistical Analysis

Data are expressed as mean±SEM. Sample sizes for biochemical studies were not predetermined using statistical methods, but they were comparable to standard sizes in the field. The data were analyzed by staff who were blinded to group assignments using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). In electrophysiological recording experiments, we monitored cell capacitance, input resistance, series resistance, and baseline holding current. Cells were excluded from the study if the recordings showed signs of rundown. The differences between the 2 groups were compared with the 2‐tailed unpaired Student t test. The χ2 test was conducted to analyze the incidence of ventricular ectopy or reentry. Differences among groups were compared using 1‐way ANOVA followed by the Tukey post hoc test. Differences within the same group before and after drug application were analyzed by repeated measure of 1‐way or 2‐way ANOVA followed by the Tukey post hoc test. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Catestatin Reduces the Myocardial Arrhythmia Induced by Acute Myocardial I/R in Rats

After recording a stable baseline ECG for 30 minutes, the left anterior descending coronary artery of the heart was ligated, and ischemia was induced for 30 minutes, followed by 120 minutes of reperfusion. Catestatin or vehicle (saline) was administered intraperitoneally 20 minutes before left anterior descending coronary artery ligation (Figure 1A). Pretreatment with catestatin markedly reduced the incidence of premature ventricular contractions during both ischemia (P=0.0057, t (10)=3.51) and reperfusion (P=0.0087, t (10)=3.251, Figure 1B and 1C) periods compared with the control group. Meanwhile, pretreatment with catestatin significantly reduced the duration of VF (P=0.0075, t (10) =3.337, Figure 1B and 1D) and VT (P=0.0353, t (10)=2.433, Figure 1B and 1E) during ischemia. However, catestatin did not affect the duration of ventricular tachycardia (P=0.0568, t (10)=0.4121, Figure 1D) and VF (P=0.1797, t (10)=1.443, Figure 1E) during reperfusion. In addition, pretreatment with catestatin notably reduced the arrhythmia score during ischemia period compared with the control group (P=0.0035, t (10)=3.796, Figure 1F).

Catestatin Diminishes the Incidence of Arrhythmia Triggered by Electrical Stimulation in Isolated Rat Heart

We used optical mapping techniques to analyze the effects of catestatin on the isolated heart (Figure 2A and 2B). The catestatin hearts exhibited a greater resistance to arrhythmias compared with the control hearts when subjected to stimulation at 50‐milliseconds and 40‐milliseconds intervals (Figure 2C). Catestatin significantly decreased arrhythmia scores at 40 milliseconds for the S1S1 interval (P=0.0267, t (5)=3.11, Figure 2D).

Catestatin Shortens the Action Potential Duration in Ventricular Myocytes

Given that catestatin reduces the incidence of ventricular arrhythmias during myocardial ischemia, we next investigated whether catestatin could affect the AP of ventricular myocytes using the patch‐clamp technique. Rat ventricular myocytes were isolated by enzymatic digestion, as described previously (Figure 3A). AP of acute isolated ventricular myocytes from rats was recorded under current‐clamp mode (Figure 3B). Ventricular myocytes were recorded after incubating with catestatin for 5 minutes. We showed that administration of catestatin significantly shortened the action potential duration (APD) of ventricular myocytes evoked by current injection in a concentration‐dependent manner (Figure 3C and 3D). Specifically, the APD 30 (P<0.001, F (3,20)=48.13, Figure 3E), APD 50 (P<0.001, F (3,20)=47.19, Figure 3F) and APD 90 (P<0.001, F (3,20)=30.1, Figure 3G) of ventricular myocytes were shortened by catestatin in a concentration‐dependent manner.

Blocking Potassium Channels and Opening the I Ca −L Channel Reverses the Effect of Catestatin on the APD

To determine whether catestatin exerts cardioprotective effects through potassium channels, 4‐AP (2 mmol/L, a specific voltage‐gated potassium channel blocker) and TEACl (20 mmol/L, a nonspecific potassium channel blocker) were pretreated for 2 minutes to block potassium channels before catestatin administration. The results showed that potassium channel blockers significantly prolonged catestatin‐induced APD shortening in ventricular myocytes (Figure 4A). Specifically, the catestatin‐induced shortening of APD 30 (P<0.001, F (3,20)=45, Figure 4B), APD 50 (P<0.001, F (3,20)=51, Figure 4C), and APD 90 (P < 0.001, F (3,22)=82.41, Figure 4D) was significantly prolonged by pretreatment with 4‐AP and TEACl, suggesting that increased potassium channel activation and opening are involved in the catestatin‐induced repolarization process of AP. Administration of potassium channel blockers (TEA++4‐AP) alone also prolonged APD 30, APD 50, and APD 90 (Figure 4B through 4D).

Moreover, to determine whether catestatin exerts its cardioprotective effects through calcium channels, Bay K8644 (I Ca‐L channels agonist, 2 mmol/L) was pretreated before catestatin administration (Figure 4E). The results showed that the pretreatment of I Ca‐L channels significantly prolonged catestatin‐induced shortening of APD. Catestatin‐induced shortening of APD 30 (P<0.001, F (3,20)=104.9, Figure 4F), APD 50 (P<0.001, F (3,24)=116.6, Figure 4G), and APD 90 (P<0.001, F (3,20)=100.9, Figure 4H) were prolonged significantly by pretreatment with Bay K8644, suggesting that decreased I Ca‐L channel activation and opening are involved in the catestatin‐induced AP repolarization process. Pretreatment with Bay K8644 alone also prolonged APD 30, APD 50, and APD 90 (Figure 4F through 4H).

Catestatin Enhances I to Current in Rat‐Isolated Ventricular Myocytes

To further investigate which potassium channels are specifically affected by catestatin, we observed the influence of catestatin on the I to, I K, and I K1 currents of ventricular myocytes. We first examined whether catestatin affects the I to current in rat ventricular myocytes. I to current was recorded in serial test pulses from −80 to +60 mV in 10‐mV increments and 500‐milliseconds duration (Figure 5A). We showed that catestatin increased I to current density and shifted the I‐V curve upward in a concentration dependent manner (Figure 5B). In particular, 1‐nmol/L, 10‐nmol/L, and 100‐nmol/L catestatin increased peak current density of I to (at +60 mV) from +18.9±1.6 pA/picofarad(pF) to +29.2±2.4 pA/pF (P=0.0443, F (5,15)=3.34, Figure 5C), +34.6±2.3 pA/pF (P=0.0127, F (5,15)=3.34, Figure 5C), and +43.6±2.3 pA/pF (P=0.0035, F (5,15)=3.34, Figure 5C), respectively.

Moreover, we also observed the effect of catestatin (100 nmol/L) on the kinetics of I to, including its activation, inactivation, and recovery from inactivation. The activation curve was shifted to the left in the presence of catestatin (100 nmol/L) compared with the control group, indicating accelerated activation and faster opening of the I to channel (Figure 5E). Furthermore, the inactivation curve was shifted to the right in the presence of catestatin (100 nmol/L) compared with the control group, indicating slower inactivation and delayed closure of the I to channel (Figure 5D and 5E). Specifically, catestatin markedly changed the half‐maximal activation potential from +31.4 ± 3.8 mV to +24.2 ± 1.1 mV (P=0.01, t (10)=5.395, Figure 5E), and the half‐maximal inactivation potential from −10.4±0.6 mV to −5.6±1.6 mV (P=0.0261, t (10)=2.61, Figure 5E).

We also examined the effect of catestatin on the recovery from inactivation of I to. The recovery curves of I to were analyzed by applying 2 identical 500‐milliseconds depolarizing pulses (from −80 mV to +60 mV) with interpulse intervals ranging from 0 to 3000 milliseconds (Figure 5F). The results showed that catestatin (100 nmol/L) did not significantly influence the recovery curve of I to (P=0.9917, t (10)=0.011, Figure 5G).

The above results suggest that catestatin enhances the I to current by accelerating the activation and slowing down the inactivation of the I to channel, thereby accelerating the repolarization of the AP in ventricular myocytes.

Catestatin Increases I K and IK1 Currents in Rat Isolated Ventricular Myocytes

Next, we determined the effect of catestatin on the I K current of ventricular myocytes. Our results showed that catestatin increased the I K current of ventricular myocytes in a concentration‐dependent manner (Figure 6A through 6D). Catestatin at concentrations of 1 nmol/L, 10 nmol/L, and 100 nmol/L significantly increased the peak current density of I K (+ 60 mV) from 11.9±4.0 pA/pF to 17.8±1.7 pA/pF (P=0.01), 25.5±4.9 pA/pF (P = 0.0035), and 35.1 ± 7.1 pA/pF (P = 0.0025, F (5.15) = 5.857, Figure 6D), respectively.

Catestatin Reduces I Ca −L Current in Rat Isolated Ventricular Myocytes

Our aforementioned data suggest that the I Ca‐L channel agonist can reverse the shortening effect of catestatin on APD, which prompted us to further investigate whether catestatin can directly affect I Ca‐L. The effects of different concentrations of catestatin (1 nmol/L, 10 nmol/L, 100 nmol/L) on I Ca‐L were observed in voltage clamp mode (Figure 7A). The results showed that catestatin reduced I Ca‐L current density in a concentration dependent manner (Figure 7B). In particular, 1‐nmol/L, 10‐nmol/L, and 100‐nmol catestatin reduced peak current density of I Ca‐L (at −10 mV) from −9.7±0.8 pA/pF to −6.1±0.4 pA/pF (P=0.0356, F (5,15)=1.084, Figure 7C), −5.1±0.4 pA/pF (P=0.001, F (5,15)=1.084, Figure 7C), and −4.2±0.4 pA/pF (P=0.001, F (5,15)=1.1, Figure 7C), respectively.

Moreover, we also observed the effect of catestatin (100 nmol/L) on the kinetics of I Ca‐L, including its activation, inactivation, and recovery from inactivation. The inactivation curve was shifted to the left in the presence of catestatin (100 nmol/L) compared with the control group, indicating accelerated inactivation the I Ca‐L channel (Figure 7D and 7E). Particularly, the half‐maximal inactivation potential was changed from −29.2±0.7 mV to −32.4±0.6 mV (P=0.0036, t (10)=5.153, Figure 7E) by catestatin (100 nmol/L). However, catestatin did not affect the activation curve.

Finally, we examined the effect of catestatin on the recovery from inactivation of I Ca‐L. The recovery curves of I Ca‐L were analyzed by applying 2 identical 500‐milliseconds depolarizing pulses (from −80 mV to −10 mV) with interpulse intervals ranging from 0 to 3000 milliseconds (Figure 7F). The results showed that catestatin (100 nmol/L) slowed down the recovery of I Ca‐L from inactivation compared with the control group (P=0.0267, t (10)=2.595, Figure 7G).

To exclude the involvement of NO and calcium‐calmodulin (CaM)‐dependent protein kinase II in the effect of catestatin on I Ca‐L, we tested the effect of catestatin on I Ca‐L from CaV1.2‐HEK293 cells. 14 , 27 , 28 Catestatin (100 nmol/L) significantly inhibited the I Ca‐L current density in CaV1.2‐HEK293 cells, which could be abolished by nifedipine, an I Ca‐L channel blocker (Figure S1A). Specifically, catestatin decreased the peak current density of CaV1.2‐HEK293 (+10 mV) from 17.8±1.7 pA/pF to 11.9±4.0 pA/pF (P=0.004, t (10)=6.221, Figure S1B and S1C).

DISCUSSION

The most salient finding of our study is that catestatin enhances the I to, I K, and I K1 currents and inhibits the I Ca‐L current, thereby shortening the APD and decreasing the incidence of ventricular arrhythmia in rat ventricular myocytes during I/R. Catestatin is a multifunctional peptide involved in regulating the cardiovascular systems. Although it has been shown that catestatin reduces the incidence of atrial fibrillation and ventricular arrhythmias, the ionic mechanisms are still unknown. 27 For the first time, our study focused on the cardioprotective effect of catestatin on ion channels during APs of cardiomyocytes to clarify its antiarrhythmic mechanisms.

Studies have shown that ventricular attack occurs with low levels of circulating catestatin in myocardial ischemia. 29 The occurrence of ventricular arrhythmias is closely related to the AP and the state of ion channels of ventricular myocytes. 30 Myocardial infarction may prolong the duration of ventricular repolarization (eg, QT interval and APD 90), 16 which can easily lead to early afterdepolarizations and torsades de pointes arrhythmias. 31 Our study demonstrated that catestatin attenuated ischemia‐induced arrhythmias, such as premature ventricular contractions, ventricular tachycardia, and VF, and reduceed the ventricular arrhythmia score during ischemia and reperfusion. Optical mapping recordings of isolated rat hearts also demonstrated that catestatin significantly decreased the occurrence of cardiac ectopy and reentry arrhythmia induced by electrical stimulation. In addition, using a single‐cell current‐clamp technique, we confirmed that catestatin concentration dependently shortened the APD of isolated ventricular myocytes, which is consistent with previous studies reporting that chronic administration of catestatin can shorten APD of the rat heart in vivo. 16 It has been demonstrated that catestatin can produce cardioprotective effects by reducing catecholamine release. 32 In our study, we hypothesize that catestatin's effect on APD is through direct action on the ion channels of the cardiomyocytes, because the influence of neural and humoral regulation has been eliminated in acutely isolated ventricular myocytes.

Enhanced opening of potassium channels during AP repolarization usually results in a shortening of the AP time course. 33 To verify whether the shortening effect of catestatin on APD is related to the activation of potassium channels, we applied potassium channel blockers 4‐AP and TEACl. We found that not only the shortening effect of catestatin on APD in ventricular myocytes was completely suppressed, but the APD prolongation exceeded the baseline duration. This may be due to the potent combination of the potassium channels blockers are strong enough to fully inhibit the effect of catestatin. Because the effects of both catestatin and potassium blockers on APD in ventricular myocytes only last for several minutes, we speculate that the above effects of catestatin result from the potassium channel currents and kinetics changes during AP repolarization.

The transient outward potassium current (I to) is the major contributing current to phase 1 of repolarization of the cardiac AP, and therefore abnormalities in the I to channel have been implicated in the pathophysiology of various cardiac arrhythmias. 34 Both gain‐of‐function and loss‐of‐function mutations in genes encoding the components of I to have been associated with the development of arrhythmogenic phenotypes. Decreasing I to current density leads to prolongation of APD, which increases the incidence of arrhythmias such as ventricular tachycardia. 35 Our study demonstrated for the first time that catestatin concentration dependently enhanced the current density of I to and accelerated the activation and slowed down the inactivation of the I to channels in ventricular myocytes. Based on our in vivo data showing that catestatin reduces the incidence of ventricular arrhythmia in cardiomyocytes, we hypothesize that catestatin may function as an I to activator, providing a novel approach for the treatment of ventricular arrhythmias.

Delayed rectifier potassium current (I K) is mainly manifested in the middle and late stages of ventricular repolarization. 21 The I K current, by regulating the duration of the AP, influences the refractory period, which in turn governs the susceptibility to reentrant arrhythmias. Two prominent components of I K, I Kr (I K, rapid), and I Ks (I K, slow), have been identified in many mammalian species ventricular myocytes. However, various species show different expressions of I k. For instance, I kr, rather than I Ks, was predominantly identified in rat ventricles. 36 Clinically, mutations of the I Kr and I Ks currents can lead to prolongation of the APD and the emergence of potentially life‐threatening ventricular arrhythmias in long‐QT syndrome. 37 Our study in rats showed that catestatin concentration dependently increased the current density of I K, which means that catestatin accelerates repolarization and shortens APD in cardiomyocytes, thereby reducing ventricular arrhythmias.

I K1 plays a critical role in the maintenance of the late rapid repolarization process of ventricular repolarization. 38 The dysregulation of I K1 has been implicated in the genesis of various arrhythmias. Reduced I K1 density or altered kinetics can lead to delayed repolarization, prolongation of the APD, and ultimately the development of early afterdepolarizations. These early afterdepolarizations can trigger ventricular tachyarrhythmias, such as torsades de pointes, and potentially lead to sudden cardiac death. 37 Our study showed that catestatin increased I K1 current density in a concentration‐dependent manner. This confirms that catestatin can also shorten APD by enhancing the inward flow of potassium ions during the late rapid repolarization phase of ventricular myocardial cells. Thus, I K1 is another target for modulating cardiac repolarization and restoring the electrical stability of catestatin.

I Ca‐L plays a critical role in the generation and propagation of cardiac APs, and dysfunction of I Ca‐L has been implicated in the development of arrhythmias. 39 , 40 Dysregulation of I Ca‐L can lead to abnormalities in the repolarization of the cardiac myocytes, which can predispose the heart to the development of early afterdepolarizations and delayed afterdepolarizations. 22 These abnormal electrical events can trigger the onset of arrhythmias such as torsades de pointes, a specific form of ventricular tachycardia that can degenerate into VF and sudden cardiac death. 41 In our study, catestatin was found to inhibit I Ca‐L and shorten the APD, thereby restoring the normal electrical activity of the heart and preventing the onset of arrhythmias. However, the calcium channel agonist completely abolished the APD shortening caused by catestatin. Moreover, the APD was even more prolonged than baseline duration. This indicates that the action of the calcium channel agonist led to almost full activation of the calcium channel, which may have canceled out the inhibitory effect of catestatin on I Ca‐L.

The intricate orchestration of potassium and calcium currents on cardiomyocytes by catestatin is crucial for its cardioprotective role in reducing ventricular arrhythmias. Further research into the molecular and cellular basis of ionic channels by catestatin are expected to uncover the new mechanisms for its antiarrhythmic effects, ultimately paving the way for more effective treatment of ischemic arrhythmias.

A limitation of this study is that we did not measure circulating catecholamines during the induced myocardial infarction, which means we cannot exclude the possibility that the antiarrhythmic effect of catestatin is mediated by modulating the spillover of catecholamines (primarily norepinephrine) into the peripheral circulation. 15 However, the direct antiarrhythmic effect of catestatin could be partially explained by its impact on the APD of isolated cardiomyocytes and I Ca‐L current in the CaV1.2‐HEK293 cell line. Our findings provide new insight into the mechanism of the antiarrhythmic effect of catestatin and guide the development of new strategies for treating ventricular arrhythmia. Future study may focus on identifying the specific receptor or molecular signaling pathway underlying the action of catestatin on cardiomyocytes, which might be translated into clinical practice in the future.

CONCLUSIONS

Catestatin reduces acute myocardial I/R‐induced arrhythmias mainly by enhancing the activity of the I to, I K, and I K1 channels and inhibiting I Ca‐L in ventricular myocytes (Figure 8). This new information advances our understanding of the ionic mechanism of the antiarrhythmic effect of CST and provides a theoretical basis for the application of catestatin in the diagnosis and treatment of ischemia‐induced ventricular arrhythmias.
