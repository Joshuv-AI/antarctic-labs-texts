Place-based communication methods and prior knowledge increase public perceptions regarding West Nile virus risk and personal protective behaviors in Colorado
Authors: Shelby M Cagle, Cheyenne M Schad, Aidan K Wolfe, Madison Bristol, Christopher M Roundy, Nicole C Kelp
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncidences of emerging and re-emerging infectious diseases are increasing as disease vector populations and habitats expand due to climate change. Public health organizations must communicate effectively to guide public health behaviors to safeguard against vector-borne diseases. Specifically in Colorado, West Nile virus (WNV) burden is increasing.MethodsIn this study, we used the Health Belief Model (HBM) as a theoretical framework to examine public risk perceptions and behaviors related to WNV and changes in these constructs related to different forms of messaging. We utilized a survey experiment with n = 358 participants across Colorado, testing the impact of four communication interventions spanning social media infographics, narrative messaging, and place-based risk information, including trailhead infographics and risk heatmaps.ResultsWe found that any form of messaging about WNV statistically increased some HBM constructs regarding personal protective behaviors. However, the infographic displayed at a trailhead and the heatmap interventions led to statistically significant increases in more HBM constructs. Additionally, higher pre-existing knowledge about WNV increased the magnitude of change in perceived susceptiblity induced by the interventions.ConclusionThis study suggests that public health organizations could utilize place-based information regarding real-time, real-place risk to guide public health decision-making during emergence of vector-borne infectious diseases. Additionally, there appears to be a synergy of messaging with a beneficial effect of pre-existing knowledge, highlighting the need for diverse, longitudinal messaging strategies to reach people multiple times.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24831-5.Keywords: West Nile virus, Health Belief Model, Risk communication

Introduction

West Nile virus

West Nile virus (WNV) is the most common vector-borne disease (VBD) in the United States [1]. WNV transmits primarily through the bites of infected Culex spp. mosquitoes. Human disease is primarily characterized by flu-like symptoms, but can progress to neuroinvasive disease [2]. The incidences of VBD have increased in recent years and have expanded to previously unaffected geographic areas as climate change reshapes vector habitats [3]. In 2023, the state of Colorado recorded the most disease cases of WNV in the country with 584 cases—more than double the number of cases by the next highest state (California: 251 cases) [4].

WNV case reduction requires a coordinated public health effort that hinges on community members adhering to personal protective behaviors, such as mosquito bite reduction and reducing mosquito rearing habitats [5]. Some public health campaigns encourage people to “Fight the Bite”; such campaigns do increase VBD awareness and prevention practices, but there are regional and demographic differences in efficacy [6]. While public and private spraying for mosquitoes has become a common intervention for vector control, research shows a rise in insecticide resistance to these chemical control methods [7]. Therefore, personal protection remains a necessary control strategy. However, with public hesitancy regarding the safety and efficacy of DEET-containing repellants [8, 9], messages that effectively target public risk perceptions and protective behaviors are critical. The current study investigated various public messaging strategies regarding WNV and their effectiveness for increasing public risk perceptions and personal protective behaviors against mosquitoes. We utilized a cross-sectional survey and aimed to answer the following research question:

RQ: What public messaging strategies are most effective for increasing public risk perceptions and mosquito prevention behaviors regarding WNV?

Theoretical framework: Health Belief Model

Health communication strategies are most effective when grounded in established theory and evaluated empirically [10]. Thus, in this study we chose a theoretical model to ground our quantitative evaluation of message efficacy. According to the Health Belief Model (HBM), an individual’s perception of the risk of a disease outcome should correlate with their preventative behavior [11]. This perceived risk includes both perceived susceptibility to the disease and perceived severity of the disease [11]. Conversely, barriers may hinder implementation of healthier behaviors. Several studies have investigated how risk perception and barriers correlate with behaviors relevant to WNV in Colorado, New York, and other areas using HBM-based surveys, Knowledge Attitudes Practices (KAP) surveys, or other approaches [12–18]. However, many of these studies occurred during prior outbreaks and do not represent the post-pandemic public health sector, whereby arbovirus surveillance, diagnostics, education, and clinical treatment have been disrupted as efforts pivoted toward Covid-19 response [19]. Thus, we aimed to use the HBM to structure a survey in Colorado in 2023 to assess risk perception and behaviors in response to different communication strategies, within the contemporary societal landscape.

Public health messaging strategies

Many public health departments utilize infographics to relate complex information in visually appealing ways [20], and infographics have been shown to be effective in many contexts [21]. For example, infographics explaining the Four D’s of mosquito control encourage people to drain standing water to remove mosquito breeding habitats; dress in long sleeves and pants to prevent bites; defend oneself by spraying approved insect repellent, especially that containing DEET; and to refrain from being outdoors at the peak mosquito feeding times of dawn/dusk [22]. However, further research on the efficacy of infographics [21] and other messaging strategies for WNV is merited.

Furthermore, stories have been shown as efficacious messaging strategies by appealing to emotions, values, and shared goals [23]. When compared to didactic informational campaigns, narratives engaged readers through an elicited emotive response and yielded improved information comprehension and retention [24]. Narrative messages have been proposed as effective health messaging tactics during disease outbreaks [25], and these narrative messages can influence public response positively [26]. A 2015 northern Colorado study recommended implementation of narrative messaging as an effective tool to encourage personal adoption of protective health behaviors against WNV [16]. Thus, we made the following hypothesis:

H1: Communication interventions that convey narrative messaging will increase risk perception and intention to utilize protective behaviors more so than a standard infographic about mosquito protection.

Another potential public health messaging strategy incorporates place-based risk information. Making a risk tangible by showing its intensity or proximity on a map can help graphically communicate real-time and real-place risk. Maps have been utilized to convey natural disaster risk, such as flooding and hurricane pathways [27]. Heat maps have also been validated as a useful tool to communicate infectious disease prevalence and risk [28]. The formatting of disease maps may influence how people adjust their knowledge and risk perception in response to the maps, and further information beyond the map may be necessary [29]. With new or emerging threats, perceived proximity to the place of the threat can increase people’s perception of risk of the threat; conversely, with established issues, time without major consequences can diminish people’s perception of risk of the issue [30]. In addition to maps, infographics or other messaging about vectors at the location where vectors are likely to be encountered, such as trails, can influence public reaction to these messages [31, 32] and can potentially encourage people to take real-time action like wearing bug spray on the trail. Given that WNV is an increasing risk in Colorado, especially in rural, agricultural, and recreational areas, we made the following hypothesis:

H2: Communication interventions that convey proximity to risk will increase risk perception and intention to utilize protective behaviors more so than a standard infographic about mosquito protection.

Impact of pre-existing knowledge on health communication

A previous study utilized structural equation modeling to show that as individuals are seeking health information, greater prior health knowledge increased their perceived efficacy of health communication they received [33]. Having more knowledge about an infectious disease can improve coherent cognition about the disease and reduce anxiety [34]. Additionally, analysis of public health campaigns, such as the Real Cost campaign to help youth know the cost of tobacco smoking, showed that exposure to messaging and recall of messaging increased risk perception towards smoking [35]. We wanted to test how pre-existing knowledge about WNV impacted participants’ response to communication about WNV. Specifically, we were interested in how pre-existing knowledge of WNV impacted individuals’ risk perception in response to our messaging strategies. Previous studies of the HBM have shown that perceived susceptibility is a stronger predictor of preventative behaviors rather than treatment behaviors [36], and our study was focused on factors influencing participants’ protective preventative behaviors. We made the following hypothesis:

H3: Increased pre-existing knowledge will increase individuals’ risk perception towards WNV in response to messaging about WNV.

Study context

We performed this survey in winter 2023 in Colorado, after a severe WNV season in summer 2022.

Methods

Survey

An anonymous online survey was administered to Colorado residents in January 2023 to assess the impact of different health communication methods on WNV risk perceptions and behavior. The survey (Table 1; Fig. 1) was designed and distributed through Qualtrics. This digital management software was used for survey construction, website link and QR code generation to access the survey, anonymous response collection and storage, and exporting response data. Responses were analyzed using GraphPad Prism. We assessed individuals’ pre-existing knowledge of WNV transmission and protective behaviors. Then, we measured participants’ HBM constructs of perceived susceptibility, perceived protection from susceptibility, perceived severity, health behaviors, and barriers before and after viewing one of four randomized health communication interventions. These HBM scales were developed based on relevant literature that applied these HBM metrics for WNV perceptions [16]. All scales had acceptable inter-scale reliability determined by Cronbach’s alpha analysis (Table 1). At the end of the survey, respondents were asked a series of demographic questions to ascertain age, gender, race, level of educational attainment, household income, and housing type.

The health communication interventions in the survey were developed using a combination of existing literature regarding health communication about vector-borne diseases as previously described in the Introduction as well as results from focus group interviews conducted during Spring 2022 by the authors. Recruitment took place virtually by posting on local public health social media sites, including Larimer and Boulder County public health accounts. Three focus groups comprised of Colorado residents were conducted to inquire about the following question: “What communication strategies would be useful for your friends and families to understand WNV, the changing risks, and actions they can take to protect themselves and their communities?” Thematic analysis of focus group responses revealed themes related to desire for more specific communications about current risk and protection strategies. These themes guided our decision to utilize place-based information about WNV in our health communication interventions.

To assess the impact of various communication techniques on community members’ perceptions of WNV, four types of communication methods were used (Fig. 2). The Four D infographic on social media is currently utilized by public health departments; thereby, this intervention represents messaging that was active in the study region and that was developed by local health departments. We aimed to both assess the efficacy of this strategy as well as examine the relative efficacy of other strategies. Each of the interventions were preceded by a short prompt and randomly assigned in equal distributions to participants (Table 2).

First, we had a control communication intervention, which was an infographic that participants were told to envision that they saw on social media during peak mosquito season. This infographic, which displayed tactics to prevent mosquito bites, was acquired from the Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment (CDPHE) and had been implemented by them since 2019. The infographic on social media served as the control measure for this study because this messaging strategy had already been utilized by the local health departments.

To test H1 (impact of narrative), we employed a fictional narrative which survey participants were told to imagine that they read in a local news report (Fig. 2; Table 2). This passage was inspired by a real news report in The Coloradoan newspaper and modified for the study applications. By resembling a local paper, this intervention emulated a contemporary and relevant local communication method. A narrative intervention was utilized due to literature evidence supporting the use of emotion-evoking narratives as an effective tool to encourage desired health behavior change among at-risk individuals [16]. Responses to this narrative intervention were compared to responses following exposure to our control intervention.

To test H2 (impact of place-based messaging), we utilized two additional interventions. Some participants were shown the same infographic described above, but they were told to imagine they encountered the intervention at a trailhead (Fig. 2; Table 2). The trailhead represented a location where people could be near mosquitoes or enact behaviors such as applying bug spray. Secondly, we used a heatmap of the local community, with colored markers to indicate the prevalence of WNV-positive mosquito pools. The heatmap intervention included a prompt describing how to interpret the colored markers (Fig. 2; Table 2). WNV-positive pool data were acquired from a county-wide surveillance initiative with Colorado State University, CDPHE, and from Vector Disease Control International.

To test H3 (impact of pre-existing knowledge), we asked questions that rated participants’ knowledge of WNV transmission only before seeing the communication interventions (Fig. 1; Table 1).

Recruitment

Survey participants were recruited via purposive sampling using physical and digital media. Printed flyers were distributed throughout the northern region of Larimer County, in areas with anticipated high visitation from our target population: people who spend time outdoors for recreation or occupational purposes. Flyer distribution locations were opportunistic and included outdoor supply stores, veterinary clinics, local libraries, and the university campus. Locations were determined by the authors based on their knowledge about local and regional recreational and occupational attractions. Additionally, flyers were distributed digitally through social media posts on the CDPHE Instagram and Facebook and the Colorado School of Public Health at Colorado State University Student Council Instagram. The flyers contained a QR code for participants to scan with their mobile phones, taking them to the online survey. Surveys were completed from January to March 2023. This timeline was strategically chosen to predate the spring and summer months when mosquito populations are influx and WNV cases rise. These studies were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Colorado State University and informed consent was collected from all participants. Participants were not compensated for completing the survey.

Data analysis

The data collected from the survey responses were analyzed using various parametric measures. One-way ANOVA was used to establish that pre-intervention metrics were the same in all groups, showing that the average participants in each group were the same and thus the impact of interventions could be compared. A power analysis was performed (small effect size (f) = 0.20, α = 0.05, power (1-β) = 0.80) to determine the target sample size as 81 participants per intervention type. Paired t-tests were used to analyze the difference in HBM metrics (susceptibility, insusceptibility, severity, behaviors, and barriers) after viewing the randomly assigned intervention. To further explore the relationship between pre-existing knowledge and HBM metrics, simple linear regressions were performed.

Results

Responses received

By the end of the survey period, we received 410 responses. Incomplete surveys (those in which not all questions were answered by the respondent) were removed, leaving a total of 358 responses for analysis. The distribution of interventions across participants was proportional: 92 participants received the infographic on social media(control intervention), 88 received the story (narrative intervention), 87 received the infographic at the trailhead (place-based intervention), and 91 received the heatmap (place-based intervention).

Nearly 90% of participants were between the ages of 18–39 years old, with over half of the participants reporting an age between 35 and 39 years. The gender distribution was nearly equal, with women having a slightly higher proportion. Approximately 75% of participants were White, with Black (7.7%) and American Indian and Alaskan Natives (7.4%) making up the next largest racial demographics. Nearly 35% of participants reported being Hispanic or Latino. Most participants reported a degree in higher education, with less than 20% of participants reporting their highest level of education as high school or below. The income distribution was also relatively high, with the greatest proportion reporting an income between $50,000 - $74,999. Lastly, most participants reported living in a house or apartment-style housing, including dormitories (Supplemental Materials 1).

First, we verified that all four groups of randomly assigned participants were comparable. ANOVA tests revealed no change in any pre-intervention metrics between the groups, suggesting comparability of the groups.

The control intervention, infographic on social media, significantly increased participants’ perceived susceptibility to (p = 0.002) and their intended protective behaviors against WNV (p = 2.02 × 10−5) (see Table 3).

The narrative intervention significantly increased participants’ intended protective behaviors (p = 0.001) (see Table 3). There were no significant effects of this intervention on the other HBM metrics measured. Since there were fewer HBM constructs impacted than the control condition, this suggests that H1 (impact of narrative) is not supported by our data.

The infographic at a trailhead (H2) increased participants’ perceived susceptibility (p < 0.01) and behavioral intentions (p = 0.01), but this intervention also significantly increased the participants perceived protection from susceptibility for WNV (p = 0.02) (see Table 3). The heatmap increased participants’ perceived susceptibility (p = 0.01), perceived severity (p = 0.03), and intended personal protective behaviors (p = 0.004) regarding WNV (see Table 3). These results suggest that place-based risk information, especially via the heatmap, had a influence on a greater number of HBM metrics than the control condition, supporting H2 (impact of place-based messaging).

Impacts of preexisting knowledge on intervention efficacy

Linear regressions showed significant effect of perceived susceptibility on protective behaviors (linear regression; R2 = 0.207, p < 0.0001), supporting our use of perceived susceptibility as an important predictor of preventative behaviors for WNV and thus a relevant outcome variable for testing of H3 (impact of pre-existing knowledge). We ran a linear regression with pre-existing knowledge of WNV predicting change in perceived susceptibility in responses to messaging. Pre-existing knowledge of protective behaviors had a significant positive influence on the change in perceived susceptibility (linear regression; R2 = 0.029, p = 0.035). Thus, knowing more about WNV made participants have a greater response to communications about WNV.

Discussion

In this study, we showed that multiple forms of health communication interventions about WNV increased individual’s risk perception and behavior. However, our results indicated that some health communication interventions are more effective than others. While other communication literature has found evidence for narratives as efficacious communication strategies [23–26], our data showed that the story intervention elicited lower risk perception and less behavioral intentions to protect oneself against WNV among our participants. Thus, H1 (impact of narrative) was not supported in this study. The story intervention did significantly increase participants reported intended behaviors to protect themselves from WNV. This may be attributed to the high stakes conveyed by the narrative, whereby WNV was associated with fatal outcomes. The formulation of a message may dictate the efficacy of narrative interventions [37].

Heatmaps of local regions’ WNV-positive mosquito pools and the infographic placed at trailheads led to increases in a greater number of HBM constructs than did the control (infographic on social media). Between these two place-based risk communication interventions, the heatmap may be the most efficacious. The infographic at a trailhead increased participants’ perceived susceptibility as well as perceived protection from susceptibility. From this, it can be inferred that participants gained an awareness for WNV risk, but from the presented messages, they perceived a means by which they could reduce their susceptibility. Conversely, the heatmap increased the two main components of risk perception, perceived susceptibility and perceived severity, suggesting that this intervention may make individuals take WNV more seriously. These results support H2, our hypothesis that communication interventions which convey proximity to risk will increase risk perceptions and intention to utilize protective behaviors, especially when risk is communicated via real-time, real-place associations. This study capitalizes on the timeliness of WNV communication evaluations in light of increasing annual WNV case reporting [4], attributed in part to the shifting ecological landscape and increasing insecticide resistance of mosquito vectors [7]. Place-based approaches provide transparency about WNV data and risk, which increases audience agency in personal decision-making about protective behaviors, thereby increasing inclusivity in public health communication. Although our findings are specific for WNV communication in the United States, other studies have shown that approaches providing real-time, real-place data were also useful during evolving case numbers during the COVID-19 pandemic in Europe [38] and during a chikungunya outbreak in the Caribbean [39].

Additionally, increased pre-existing knowledge of protective behaviors increased the amount by which individuals’ perceived susceptibility increased after seeing the health communication interventions, suggesting that repeated exposure to information about WNV is useful in public health education and promotion. This is promising for public health communication about WNV – many strategies will have some effect, and there is a synergy in learning more about WNV.

Previous work by Trumbo et al. addressed a correlation between proximity, HBM metrics, self-protective behaviors, risk perceptions, and ecological characteristics regarding WNV (similar to our data); however, that study noted a need for pre-survey data, which our study includes [15, 16]. Moreover, the aforementioned work did not test variable message strategies; thus our work extrapolates on these findings by providing public health message evaluations. More work needs to be done to examine the audience demographic and community characteristics that drive the efficacy of vector-borne disease communications. Finally, there has been a decrease in public outreach and engagement about VBDs after COVID-19 [40], so our work provides timely information on potential ways to improve public health education about VBDs in today’s disease landscape.

Limitations

This study utilized a purposive sampling design, which sought recruitment from specific demographics: individuals who spend time outdoors and are thereby more likely to contact mosquitoes. Additionally, survey distribution was accompanied by the sharing of recruitment flyers on the Colorado School of Public Health and CDPHE social media pages, which may have biased our survey responses to individuals’ who are health conscious or seeking information regarding public health topics. Diverse social groups have different values that impact which social factors most influence their health behaviors towards vector-borne diseases [41], so further work to contextualize our results for other audiences and locations is merited.

Implications for public health departments

The results of our study are most directly pertinent to public health departments and other organizations that communicate WNV risk information, such as news outlets and vector control groups. While these groups often utilize maps for messaging human cases and positive mosquito tests [42], our results indicate that this should be done in a timely manner that focuses on local heatmaps rather than showing positive mosquito testing at the city or county level. This approach would highlight specifically which areas are susceptible as well as the severity of current WNV circulation. Additionally, our results indicate that health departments should work with parks departments to place messaging where individuals are at a high risk of WNV exposure, such as trailheads, lakes, and parks. These serve as a reminder of susceptibility and can be used to remind visitors to use protective measures such as mosquito repellant. Furthermore, while health departments and news organizations often use narratives to communicate possible severity of WNV infection, such as [43], our results indicate that such a strategy may be less effective than previously thought. Narrative messaging should be accompanied by real-time, real-place data when possible. The effectiveness of storytelling depends upon demographics of a target population, community-specific values, and the public health issue being communicated [44]. Lastly, our results regarding pre-existing knowledge suggest that multiple, longitudinal messaging strategies will synergize to impact individuals’ knowledge and risk perception and behaviors to prevent WNV. While these findings consider WNV perceptions, these data may be pertinent and applicable for other vector-borne disease public health communications.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that place-based communication that conveys individuals’ proximity to risk is an efficacious strategy when messaging to the public regarding emerging infectious diseases, like WNV. These findings hold significant implications for public health practitioners who invest resources in this type of communication to the public. The data acquired from the study can guide more efficient and evidence-based messaging practices, which will support the likelihood of community members’ adopting healthier behaviors.
