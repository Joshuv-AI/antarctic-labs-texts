Achieving cardiovascular health equity in community mental health: study protocol for a cluster-randomized hybrid Type 3 effectiveness-implementation trial
Authors: Christina T. Yuan, Nae-Yuh Wang, Tyler Fink, Daniel Almirall, Lawrence J. Appel, Courtney Cook, Lisa A. Cooper, Arlene T. Dalcin, Joseph Gennusa, Stacy Goldsholl, Erin Kirley, Celeste Liebrecht, Emma E. McGinty, Shawna N. Smith, Alison Terry, Amy M. Kilbourne, Gail L. Daumit

ABSTRACT
Background People with serious mental illness die 10–20 years earlier than the overall population, mainly from cardiovascular disease. Although effective interventions to manage cardiovascular disease risk in this population exist, they have not been widely implemented in community settings. IDEAL Goals is an empirically supported, cardiovascular risk reduction program tailored for people with serious mental illness (i.e., “clients”) and designed to be delivered by clinicians and staff in community mental health settings. In this trial, we use Replicating Effective Programs (REP) as the foundational implementation strategy to test the effects of two additional strategies, Coaching and Facilitation, on improving the number of IDEAL Goals sessions clients receive in community mental health organizations in Maryland and Michigan.Methods This cluster-randomized hybrid Type 3 effectiveness-implementation trial will use a non-restricted sequential, multiple-assignment randomized trial (SMART) design that randomizes organizations at two points, months 0 and 6, of the 18-month IDEAL Goals intervention. Organizations will receive one of four sequences of implementation strategies: (1) REP only; (2) REP + Coaching; (3) REP + Facilitation; or (4) REP + Coaching + Facilitation. The primary aim is to determine the effect of the most intensive sequence of strategies (REP + Coaching + Facilitation) versus REP only on the number of IDEAL Goals sessions clients receive over 18 months. The secondary aim is to determine the marginal effects of Coaching and Facilitation on the number of IDEAL Goals sessions clients receive over 18 months. Exploratory aims include: (1) assessing tailoring variables to inform a future adaptive implementation intervention to scale IDEAL Goals; (2) estimating the cost of delivering IDEAL Goals and implementation strategies; and (3) examining the relationship between different sequences of implementation strategies on: clients’ receipt of cardiovascular disease risk factor management processes and outcomes over 18 months; and clients’ receipt of IDEAL Goals over 30 months. Qualitative efforts will explore implementation strategy mechanisms, adaptations, and participants’ experience of delivering and receiving IDEAL Goals.DiscussionTo meaningfully reduce premature mortality for people with serious mental illness, it is imperative to test strategies that can facilitate optimal uptake and continued sustainability of cardiovascular risk reduction programs in community settings.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06674616, registered on November 1, 2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13012-025-01461-4.

Background

People living with serious mental illness (SMI) – including schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or major depression – are dying 10–20 years earlier than the overall population [1–3], driven in large part by cardiovascular disease (CVD) [4]. Underlying this grave disparity is the high prevalence of CVD risk factors (obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus) and risk behaviors (tobacco smoking, physical inactivity, unhealthy diet) [4–7], compounded by low rates of CVD risk factor control [8]. Challenges related to effects of chronic mental illness and antipsychotic medications [9, 10], socioeconomic [11], and health system factors [12, 13] all contribute to the substantial inequities in cardiovascular health faced by individuals with serious mental illness.

To effectively address the high burden of CVD as well as the multifactorial causes of CVD risk in persons with SMI, there is an urgent need to scale-up effective interventions that have been tailored to meet the specific needs of this population [14, 15]. Our team developed two such interventions derived from the Collaborative Chronic Care Model [16, 17]: 1.) IDEAL, which emphasized tailored health behavioral counseling and care management activities [18]; and 2.) Life Goals, which emphasized self-management and included care coordination [19]. Both interventions led to clinically significant cardiovascular risk reduction in persons with SMI in clinical trials [18, 20, 21]. However, the uptake of these and other physical health interventions for people with SMI into real-life clinical settings remains limited.

To expand the reach of these interventions into community-based settings, our study team embarked on a 3-year planning phase as part of the Disparities Elimination through Coordinated Interventions to Prevent and Control Heart and Lung Disease Risk (DECIPHeR) Alliance, funded by the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute. We leveraged a theory-informed, community-engaged process [22] to combine and adapt the two evidence-based interventions (IDEAL + Life Goals) to create IDEAL Goals, a multicomponent CVD risk reduction intervention for people with SMI engaged in community-based mental healthcare (hereafter referred to as clients). The IDEAL Goals program incorporates the core elements of both IDEAL and Life Goals (education and care management) and has been tailored to be delivered by clinicians and staff in community mental health settings.

In this study, clinicians and staff at participating community mental health organizations will be trained to deliver the IDEAL Goals program and will receive one or more implementation strategies, including 1.) Replicating Effective Programs (REP) [23], the foundational implementation strategy, which focuses on site engagement, training, dissemination of the intervention package, technical assistance, and collaborative implementation planning; 2.) Coaching [18, 24, 25], which targets provider-level barriers by enhancing staff knowledge, self-efficacy, and skills related to delivering the IDEAL Goals program; and 3.) Facilitation [26–31], which targets organizational-level barriers through a process of interactive problem-solving and support. We selected these strategies based on prior work highlighting the importance of highly-specified, theory-based implementation strategies that: 1.) address both provider and organizational barriers [32]; 2.) enable tailoring to meet local needs [23]; 3.) are delivered by implementation support professionals with training in both the intervention and organizational change [32–34]; and 4.) are strategically timed in a way to maximize provider learning without leading to overburden [26, 31].

We will test the effects of these implementation strategies on clients’ receipt of IDEAL Goals sessions using a cluster-randomized hybrid Type 3 effectiveness-implementation trial that uses a non-restricted sequential, multiple assignment randomized trial (SMART) design [35–37]. This design allows us to test which combination of implementation strategies are most effective in improving implementation and client outcomes [25, 30]. Moreover, while multi-level implementation strategies are desirable, our previous research found that the timing of introducing these strategies was crucial to avoid overwhelming providers and putting them at risk of burnout [30, 31]. Thus, we opted for a cluster-randomized, non-restricted SMART design so that we could determine the best sequence of implementation strategies for community mental health organizations to maximize the number of IDEAL Goals sessions received by clients. Since the design combines elements of cluster-randomized and implementation trials, we follow reporting guidelines in the CONSORT extension to cluster randomized trials [38] and the Standards for Reporting Implementation Studies (StaRI) [39]. Completed checklists for both standards are included as additional files.

Methods

Study aims

The primary aim is to determine the effect of the most intensive sequence of strategies (REP, Coaching, and Facilitation) compared to the least intensive (REP only) on the number of IDEAL Goals sessions clients receive over 18 months. The secondary aim is to determine the marginal effects of each of the two added implementation strategies (Coaching and Facilitation) on the number of IDEAL Goals sessions clients receive over 18 months.

The exploratory implementation aims are to: 1.) assess pre-specified tailoring variables for the implementation strategies based on theory and established evidence, including mechanisms (e.g., staff self-efficacy), moderators (e.g., infrastructure), and other relevant factors (e.g., client perspectives) to inform an adaptive implementation intervention tailored to community needs to further scale up the IDEAL Goals program; and 2.) estimate the cost of delivering the IDEAL Goals program and implementation strategies. In the sustainment phase, the exploratory aim is to examine the effect of the different sequences of implementation strategies on clients’ receipt of IDEAL Goals sessions over 30 months.

The exploratory health-related aims are to examine the effect of the most intensive sequence of strategies (REP, Coaching, and Facilitation) compared to the least intensive (REP only) during the 18-month IDEAL Goals intervention on: 1.) client receipt of CVD risk management processes (including the proportion of visits with recommended activities related to tobacco smoking, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and weight management); and 2.) CVD risk factor outcomes related to tobacco smoking, blood pressure, hemoglobin A1c level, lipid values, Body Mass Index (BMI), and estimated cardiovascular risk.

In addition, our qualitative data collection and analysis efforts aim to contribute to a richer, more nuanced understanding of implementation strategy mechanisms, adaptations to the intervention and implementation strategies, clinician and staff members’ experience of the implementation process, and clients’ perceptions of the IDEAL Goals program.

Implementation frameworks

Our study is guided by the Replicating Effective Programs (REP) implementation framework [40], the updated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [41], and Proctor et al.’s conceptual model [42] of implementation research. The REP implementation framework offers a systematic approach to translating evidence-based interventions into community-based settings that maximizes fidelity to the interventions’ core elements while engaging with end-users to tailor the intervention to fit local needs and preferences. During the pre-implementation phase of REP, corresponding to our completed 3-year planning phase, we engaged with community partners to adapt the intervention and implementation strategies [22], identify anticipated implementation barriers and facilitators to delivering the IDEAL Goals program, and articulate the hypothesized mechanisms by which implementation strategies will influence implementation outcomes. Setting

Eligible sites are community mental health organizations that have implemented a behavioral health home, which is a model of care that provides enhanced care support to people with both physical and mental illnesses. Multiple state Medicaid programs have implemented behavioral health homes as part of the Affordable Care Act Medicaid Health Home Waiver [44–47], which reimburses qualified mental health programs for physical health care coordination and management. Although some evidence suggests that behavioral health homes have improved primary care access and preventive screening for persons with SMI; they have not reduced CVD risk factors [44, 47], likely owing to limited use of evidence-based practices [48, 49]. Given the clear need to expand the reach of effective CVD risk reduction interventions into community-based settings, as well as the intervention’s alignment with established goals and workflows, behavioral health homes represent an optimal setting with which to implement the IDEAL Goals program. We plan to enroll at least 24 outpatient community mental health organizations across Maryland and Michigan.

Study participants

Clinicians and staff

Each community mental health organization will identify at least 3 implementers, including 2 direct care staff (e.g., counselors, care managers, psychiatric rehabilitation program staff) and 1 health home staff (e.g., RN) to implement IDEAL Goals. To participate, clinicians and staff must be age 18 years or older; English-speaking; able to complete applicable data collection; and agree to participate in project meetings and engage in study activities according to their role. For all staff who wish to be involved in this research study, we will obtain a waiver of documentation of consent. Site staff will be compensated for data collection, which will include surveys, interviews, and focus groups.

Clients

The study team will receive a limited dataset with client-level data from the organizational electronic health record for clients 18 years and older who have received IDEAL Goals or who are enrolled in the behavioral health home (or a similar integrated care program) with a qualifying SMI diagnosis. This data will be obtained with a waiver of informed consent for the clients and through a data use agreement with the organization. In addition, a select number of clients that are receiving/have received the IDEAL Goals program will be recruited and consented to participate in interviews. These clients must be age 18 years or older; English speaking; enrolled in the behavioral health home at a participating organization; be currently receiving or have received the IDEAL Goals program; and agree to complete the interview. These client participants will be compensated for completing interviews.

Prescribers

We plan to offer prescribers affiliated with the community mental health organizations or caring for behavioral health clients the opportunity to participate in a brief, asynchronous online tobacco smoking cessation pharmacotherapy training that describes medication options, including dosing, safety, and efficacy among people with SMI. They would complete brief data collection (e.g., demographics, knowledge) through a continuing education website.

Intervention

IDEAL Goals is a multi-component behavioral lifestyle and care management intervention that addresses the cardiovascular and mental health of people with SMI (Fig. 2). The program has been designed to be delivered by staff at community mental health organizations (e.g., counselors, care managers, psychiatric rehabilitation program staff) and supported by a nurse or another clinician. The program can be delivered either in person or remotely.Fig. 2Description of the IDEAL Goals intervention

Description of the IDEAL Goals intervention

The intervention’s two core components encompass: (1) education & self-management; and (2) care management. The education & self-management component consists of a core module (8 sessions) and custom modules that address CVD and mental health risk factors (1–6 session modules delivered based on a client’s unique risk factor profile). These sessions are designed to be delivered at the group level and alternatively can be offered at the individual level. The care management component is intended to address any uncontrolled risk factors and subsequently provide ongoing follow-up care for all risk factors according to current clinical guidelines. These activities, focused at the individual level, include both direct client engagement and coordination with healthcare providers, and are considered “sessions” in the study, as IDEAL Goals sessions are the primary study outcome. Of note, the care management component includes elements of care coordination (Fig. 2); we use the term care management in this study for simplicity.

A minimum of two IDEAL Goals sessions, including one from each type of core component, are recommended per month, although the frequency and dose will vary depending on the client’s number of CVD risk factors, whether those risk factors are controlled or uncontrolled, and the state’s behavioral health home requirements and reimbursement model. Upon completion of the IDEAL Goals program, clients should have received at least 8 core module educational sessions, the custom modules relevant to their risk factor profile, and 18–36 care management activities including ongoing support for behavior change.

Assurance of fidelity to IDEAL Goals intervention

Implementation support professionals who are experts in the concepts and content of the intervention will provide the IDEAL Goals training. We plan to offer training to any eligible staff to promote reach and ensure cross-training, as we anticipate staff turnover during the trial. We expect to hold multiple trainings across both Michigan and Maryland prior to the start of the 18-month intervention period. All trainings will follow similar formats and use the same materials to ensure consistency in training across sites. Live training is planned for approximately 16 h and will be delivered via videoconference. In response to community input, we also plan to offer asynchronous online training modules to implementers unable to attend live trainings and to new staff onboarded after the intervention period has begun. After completion of IDEAL Goals training, all staff will receive a certificate of completion; social workers and nurses who attend the full live training will also receive continuing education credits.

The Coaching implementation strategy [18, 24, 25] aims to address staff effectiveness in implementing a new intervention by providing clinical support and feedback [56, 57] to further knowledge, self-efficacy, and skills related to delivering the IDEAL Goals program [52, 58, 59]. Coaching’s core functions include reinforcing lessons learned during the IDEAL Goals program training, improving staff members’ confidence and skills in program delivery, and facilitating helpful conversations between staff around delivering IDEAL Goals.

The Facilitation strategy [26–31, 60], based on the PARIHS (Promoting Action on Research Implementation in Health Services) framework [53, 54], addresses potential organizational-level barriers to delivery through a process of interactive problem-solving and support. Facilitation’s core functions include building rapport and trust with staff and leadership, managing and tailoring team processes, fostering organizational change, and engaging in problem solving and strategic thinking to support the IDEAL Goals program. Unlike with Coaching, the barriers addressed by these actions are typically at the organizational level.

Assurance of fidelity to implementation strategies

The REP Specialists, Coaches, and Facilitators will all be trained prior to the start of the 18-month intervention period. To ensure there is no contamination across roles and arms of the study, we will have different implementation support professionals serving in the roles of REP specialist, Coach, and Facilitator. We will monitor fidelity to each of the three implementation strategies using electronic logs of implementation strategy sessions delivered and implementation activities completed during those sessions. For REP fidelity, all sites will receive the same IDEAL Goals package, which includes materials for intervention delivery, access to technical assistance, and training by the designated study trainer(s).

Study design

This is a cluster-randomized hybrid Type 3 effectiveness-implementation trial [61] that uses a non-restricted sequential, multiple-assignment randomized trial (SMART) design, implemented over 4 phases. Implementation trial design and times of outcome data collection

The first phase constitutes the 3-month “run-in” period, in which all sites will be trained and offered support for implementing IDEAL Goals via REP. The second phase begins at baseline of the 18-month IDEAL Goals program, at which point all sites will be randomized to either continue receiving REP only or augment to receive REP + Coaching during the 6-month “Phase 1 of Active Implementation.” At the beginning of the third phase, sites will be randomized a second time to either continue with their current support (REP or REP + Coaching) or augment current support with Facilitation for a further 12 months during “Phase 2 of Active Implementation.” Our primary outcomes will be measured at the end of the Active Implementation phase (i.e., 18 months since Phase 1 randomization). The fourth phase, a 12-month sustainment phase during which Coaching and Facilitation are no longer provided, will be used to evaluate sustainment from 18- to 30-months since Phase 1 randomization.

The two independent randomizations will result in sites receiving one of four sequences of implementation strategies: (1) REP only; (2) REP + Coaching; (3) REP + Facilitation; or (4) REP + Coaching + Facilitation. The non-restricted SMART would result in 4 treatment sequences, akin to a 2 × 2 factorial design, with sequential randomization, and allows evaluation of main effects and interaction of Coaching and Facilitation augmentations to REP in addition to the primary aim of contrasting the most intensive sequence of strategies (REP + Coaching + Facilitation) vs the least intensive (REP only). The design also allows for exploration of baseline moderators for both Coaching and Facilitation effects as well as time-varying Phase 1 moderators of Facilitation’s effectiveness. Moderation analyses can then inform characteristics of sites that benefit most from Coaching and/or Facilitation, and as appropriate, can be used to tailor provision of Coaching and/or Facilitation in future adaptive implementation efforts that can further scale up the IDEAL Goals program more efficiently and effectively by adapting implementation support to baseline and time varying site need.

To inform tailoring of our implementation strategies for future scale up of IDEAL Goals, we have pre-identified the candidate tailoring variables to guide our moderator analyses, including baseline moderators for Phase 1 Coaching effects and baseline and time-varying moderators during Phase 1 for Phase 2 Facilitation effects.

Randomization

Randomizations will occur at the site level and will be constrained within allocations with acceptable balance between arms among a pre-selected set of important covariates for both Phase 1 and Phase 2 randomization. Covariates for the constrained randomization will include state (Michigan or Maryland); composite measures of site-level baseline characteristics, including the ratio of clients to total staff trained in the IDEAL Goals program, client volume (number of clients in health home), and client complexity according to distributions of clients’ demographics, mental health conditions, and CVD risk factors and behaviors; as well as a composite measure of a site’s readiness to implement (factoring in sites baseline EHR capabilities and changes). The Phase 2 randomization will also be at the site level, stratified by the first stage assignments (i.e., Coaching vs No Coaching), and within stratum will be constrained through a similar approach as the first stage randomization using similar baseline variables. We will use the l1 balance metric with equal priority across the baseline covariates specified above. Site assignment will be randomly selected among all eligible site allocations with selection probabilities inversely proportional to the l1 metric imbalance. The lead statistician will develop the computer script for generating the eligible site allocation schemes and performing the Phase 1 randomized allocation selection at the end of the REP run-in, after completion of baseline data collection.

Masking

Study sites and their staff cannot be masked to implementation strategy assignment as Coaching and Facilitation implementation strategies require active staff participation. Research staff outside of the primary investigators and statisticians will not be masked to the sequence of assigned strategies. Site assignments to receive implementation strategies will only be revealed once that site reaches the period where that strategy is delivered. The honest broker who receives the electronic health record data from participating organizations and provides it to the research staff will be masked to implementation strategy assignment. The study team will be masked to primary outcomes by implementation strategy throughout the course of the intervention period. Site implementation staff may receive interim, but not primary or secondary data on sites assigned to their implementation strategies as appropriate for them to successfully deliver their implementation strategies. Collection of primary and secondary quantitative outcomes will occur through direct (staff) participant report into REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) [62, 63] hosted at Johns Hopkins University and the mental health organization’s EHR. The study team will follow automated and manualized procedures to follow-up with staff participants across all sites.

Data collection and measures

Data collection will occur at baseline (before Phase 1 randomization) as well as 6 months, 18 months, and 30 months since Phase 1 randomization (Fig. 3). Data will be collected from: 1.) the organization’s electronic health record (EHR) system; 2.) participating clinicians and staff from community mental health organizations; 3.) implementation support professionals (REP specialists, Coaches, Facilitators); and 4.) a subset of clients providing interviews.

Measures from EHR data

The primary outcome – the number of IDEAL Goals sessions clients receive – will be collected through the organization’s EHR system, which was adapted during the planning phase to include dedicated fields in the client encounter forms for implementers to document IDEAL Goals sessions (including client receipt of education & self-management sessions as well as completion of specific care management activities). Wherever possible, the documentation for IDEAL Goals was aligned with existing forms and workflows.

In addition to the primary outcome, EHR data will be used to calculate measures of reach and adoption [64]. EHR data will also capture other client measures, which will be used to evaluate client health outcomes, receipt of IDEAL Goals, and fidelity to IDEAL Goals care management components and some care management processes (Table 2). Important aspects of the implementation context and process will also be qualitatively assessed over time via one-hour interviews with two implementers, including one nurse and one direct care staff (at baseline, 6, and 18 months) and one administrator (at baseline only) at each of the participating organizations. We will also conduct focus groups with a purposive sample of implementers and administrators at 30 months to explore sustainment of the IDEAL Goals program as well as perceptions of the intervention and implementation process that could inform future efforts to disseminate and scale the IDEAL Goals program. Interviews and focus groups will be audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and managed with MAXQDA 24 [76].

Measures from implementation support professionals

Implementation support professionals who provide REP, Coaching, or Facilitation to sites will log activities they complete related to these strategies in dedicated forms in a REDCap database. These will be utilized to evaluate fidelity to the implementation strategies, support time estimation of the implementation strategies to facilitate cost estimation of the implementation strategies [77, 78], and for capturing potential adaptations to the intervention or implementation strategies. Identified adaptations to the intervention or implementation strategies will be tracked in detail based upon The Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications-Expanded (FRAME) [79] and Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications to Evidence-based Implementation Strategies (FRAME-IS) [80] in a separate REDCap database. Research staff will also be responsible for tracking completion of specific implementation activities using a study-specific Stages of Implementation Completion tool, which is an eight-stage tool of implementation process and milestones [81].

Client interviews

Qualitative data from the client interviews will address client-level perspectives related to their experience of participating in the IDEAL Goals program, specifically, as well as their efforts to improve their cardiovascular health, more generally. These data will be collected from a purposeful sample of up to 50 consented clients, at one timepoint for each client between month 3 and month 18. A brief survey capturing demographics and information related to mental health and cardiovascular risk factor history will be collected for all clients during the qualitative interview.

Statistical analysis plan and power

Analysis for the primary aim

Our primary intention-to-treat analyses will compare the number of IDEAL Goals sessions received by clients at sites assigned to the most intensive sequence of strategies (REP + Coaching + Facilitation) vs. clients at sites assigned to the least intensive sequence (REP only) over 18 months post-randomization. Mixed-effects models will be used to account for clustering of clients within sites. This model will include fixed effects for intercept and binary-coded (1/0) indicators for the Phase 1 (REP vs. REP + Coaching) and Phase 2 (Facilitation vs. no Facilitation) strategies and an interaction between Phase 1 and Phase 2 strategies. A site-level random effect will account for client clustering. Models will also include variables included in our constrained randomization as covariates.

Given the limited number of clusters, some relevant baseline characteristics (especially at client-level) may not be balanced. Sensitivity analyses will add relevant baseline characteristics not balanced by constrained clustered randomization as additional covariates in the model. We will further incorporate random effects for providers as an additional sensitivity analysis for the primary outcome, where weighted random effects for providers who work directly with the clients are included into the model to account for potential effects of individual providers on the client level outcomes.

Sample size and power for the primary aim

This study is powered for our primary analysis assuming 24 sites, equally allocated to the 4 intervention sequences, with at least 60 participating clients per site, which is conservative. Based on IDEAL Trial data, we will have 90% power to detect an effect size of 0.54 (10.8 sessions) in difference in delivery of IDEAL Goals comparing REP + Coaching + Facilitation (6 sites) vs. REP alone (6 sites) using a 2-sided test with α = 0.05 and assuming a conservative site level intracluster correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.05. The distribution of the number of sessions delivered over 18 months observed in the IDEAL Trial was symmetric, and the mean number of 40 (SD 19) sessions received over 18 months in IDEAL is averaged to 2.2 sessions per month per participant and was associated with a ≈10% relative reduction in Framingham Risk Score in IDEAL over the 18 months trial period. The minimal detectable difference of 10.8 sessions between the two implementation sequences translate to detecting, for example, 2.2 vs 1.6 sessions per month per participant, on average, for implementation sequence B comparing to sequence C with high statistical power. While this is a moderate effect size, it is in line with effect sizes from recent comparative effectiveness trials of implementation strategies (e.g., Smith et al. 2022 [82]). Additionally, given the cost of augmentations like Coaching and Facilitation over REP alone, a moderate effect size of the combined strategies vs. REP alone is likely necessary to justify additional expenditures for more intensive implementation strategies.

Analysis for the secondary aim

Secondary analyses will examine the main effects of the Coaching and Facilitation augmentations on the number of IDEAL Goals sessions received under the primary outcome model with bootstrapped standard errors for the different study phases.

Analysis for the exploratory implementation aims

Exploratory analyses will compare all four sequences of implementation support shown in Fig. 2 (e.g., REP, REP + Coaching, REP + Facilitation, REP + Coaching + Facilitation) on the number of IDEAL Goals sessions received over 18 months after Phase 1 randomization. Fitted regression models separating each phase of the study (see Fig. 3) will then be used to calculate estimates of average IDEAL Goals delivery for each phase. Modeling by phase will allow for more robust estimation of the effects of each implementation strategy (Coaching & Facilitation) by not allowing IDEAL Goals program delivery that happens during Phase 1 to be impacted by Phase 2 strategies which are offered after Phase 1. Sensitivity analyses will also incorporate phase-specific weighted random effects for providers who work directly with the clients to account for potential effects of individual providers on the client level outcomes. Analyses for secondary 30-month outcomes, also including IDEAL Goals sessions received during the 12-month sustainment phase (months 18–30), will follow a similar approach to the primary analysis model above, except the outcome will be at 30 months after Phase 1 randomization.

Analysis for exploratory cost aim

Economic outcomes will include cost of the implementation strategies and IDEAL Goals program delivery (site-level). Descriptive analyses will present likely ranges for costs. Analyses will also explore incremental costs per incremental improvement in relevant client outcomes (e.g., reduced emergency department, inpatient visits). We will be able to perform sensitivity analyses by varying assumptions for labor and other costs.

Exploratory moderator and mediation analyses

To inform an adaptive implementation strategy tailored to site needs to further scale up the IDEAL Goals program, and to better understand how implementation strategies are working to address identified barriers, we will conduct exploratory moderator and mediator analyses of both Coaching and Facilitation to assess the impact by prespecified, candidate tailoring variables for the intervention strategies. Moderator analyses will use Q-learning [83], a generalization of moderated regression analysis that can accommodate multiple phases of treatment, to evaluate whether effects of Coaching or Facilitation vary depending on baseline site-aggregated provider characteristics (see Table 3).

We will also test mechanisms through which the Coaching and Facilitation implementation strategies have been hypothesized to increase adoption of the IDEAL Goals program and improve downstream client clinical outcomes through mediation analyses (see Table 3). After the full treatment effect has been determined, we will use mediator-analysis methodologies to decompose the full treatment effects of both Coaching and Facilitation separately to explore whether our hypothesized mechanism(s) partially mediate the effect of these two implementation strategies.

Exploratory analysis: client health outcomes and care processes

Similar mixed effects regression modeling approach, as previously described for the primary analysis, will be used to estimate various intervention effect contrasts on systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, lipids, hemoglobin A1c, and BMI while accounting for outcome clustering and the previously prescribed (e.g., used for randomization) and additional covariates (e.g., with observed imbalance between implementation strategies/sequences), as appropriate. Skewed outcomes will be log-transformed as appropriate. Additionally, baseline value of the outcome will be adjusted in the respective models. Proportion of clinic visits over 18 months per client that meet guidelines of care will be modeled using mixed-effects regression analysis for continuous outcomes as previously described. Among the baseline smokers, generalized estimating equations approach will be used to compare smoking cessation outcome while accounting for clustering and appropriate covariate adjustment.

Qualitative analyses

An inductive approach will be used with the initial phase of qualitative data collection to develop a more theoretically driven deductive approach for the rest of the data collection-analysis cycle. Initial coding will be indexed to the interview guides, with subsequent rounds of coding driven by specific qualitative questions. Methods appropriate to analyzing multi-perspective longitudinal qualitative data will be employed [84–86]. Focus group data will be collected at the end of the sustainment phase and similarly analyzed.

Adaptations to IDEAL Goals and the implementation strategies will be coded using FRAME and FRAME-IS, respectively. We will then use content analysis to identify themes related to the adaptation of the intervention/implementation strategies and stakeholder perceptions of the community engagement process.

Methods to handle missing data

We will use the closest outcome data available within ± 3 months to a specified data analysis time point (i.e., 6-, 18-, and 30-months) as the outcome value for that time point. This data window will be applicable to both the implementation outcomes as well as the physical health outcomes. Clients in health homes have at least one billable visit per month so are expected to be seen monthly. Clients will contribute data (through EHRs) as long as they stay in the clinic. Client attrition due to leaving the clinic discontinues IDEAL Goals contacts and ceases contribution toward session delivery counts but does not induce missing data under the intention to treat principles. Interpretation of trial findings will be anchored on results from analyses conducted under the missing at random (MAR) assumption, carried out through multiple imputation using imputation models consistent with the model for the corresponding analysis. Robustness of the MAR based results will be evaluated through sensitivity analyses, including analysis conducted to include only clients who join the clinic before the intervention and stay through the 18 months intervention period, as well as analyses of imputed data based on sensible non-MAR mechanisms. This general approach will be used to address missing data in the primary, secondary, and quantitative exploratory outcomes.

Discussion

To “move the needle” in improving the cardiovascular health of people with serious mental illness, it is imperative to scale-up multi-component, evidence-based interventions that can address all CVD risks by leveraging implementation strategies that can overcome complex barriers at multiple levels. This is not an easy feat. The target population has complex medical, mental health, and social health needs and the implementation setting – behavioral health homes in community mental health organizations – serve in a critical but challenging role as a bridge between the traditionally “siloed” health care and mental health care delivery systems. In these complex systems, this trial will enable us to advance our understanding of which combination and sequence of implementation strategies are most effective in improving implementation and client outcomes, and eventually, to design adaptive implementation strategies that can best fit local needs.
