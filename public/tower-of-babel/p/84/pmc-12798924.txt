Manganism in an End-Stage Renal Disease Patient on Maintenance Hemodialysis
Authors: Aparna V Kumar, Seerapani Gopaluni, Pooja Kalantri, Siva P Chavva, Palepu B Gopal, Balasubrahmanyam Chandrabatla, Lakshmi S Puvvula
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Manganism in end-stage renal disease (ESRD) patients on maintenance hemodialysis (HD) is rare, with a few published case reports.[1234] We present a 57-year-old hypertensive and diabetic male with ESRD on HD, and coronary artery disease (CAD) post-percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty (PTCA), who developed neuropsychiatric symptoms, stiffness in limbs, and gait disturbance over several months, and gradual cognitive decline over years, as per attendants. He had received anti-Parkinsonian therapy elsewhere without benefit and was referred to us in view of a significant increase in Parkinsonian symptoms with altered sensorium.

On examination, he was drowsy and hallucinating, with neck stiffness but normal limb muscle tone and stable vital signs. Recent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) films of the brain were not immediately available for review; however, it was reported as uremic encephalopathy. Initial investigations revealed hemoglobin 10.3 g/dL, normal white blood cell (WBC) count, and serum creatinine 5.1 mg/dL. Serum ammonia, coagulation profile, liver function test (LFT), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis were normal.

He was empirically treated with pulse steroids and immunoglobulins for possible autoimmune encephalitis. A negative CSF-autoimmune-encephalitis-panel ruled out autoimmune encephalitis subsequently. Antinuclear antibody (ANA) profile and paraneoplastic panel were also negative. The repeat MRI showed T1 and Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR) hyperintensities in bilateral basal ganglia along with chronic lacunar infarcts. The possibilities considered were non-ketotic hyperglycemia, manganism, chronic hypoxic sequelae, chronic liver disease (CLD), and Wilson’s disease. Normal LFT and ultrasound abdomen ruled out CLD. There was no history of hyperglycemia or hypoxic injury in the past. Serum ceruloplasmin was normal with mild elevation of serum copper (nonsignificant). Serum manganese level was three times (49.06 ug/L) above the normal range (4–15 μg/L). There was no ingestion of oral supplements or herbal remedies containing manganese. Standard dialysate was used for hemodialysis without risk of manganese contamination. The patient worked in the iron and steel industry as a welder for 2 years and as a crane operator for nearly 10 years. Occupational exposure to manganese is reported to occur in workers involved in welding, mining, smelting, ferroalloy steel production, and dry-cell battery production,[5] suggesting manganism in our patient based on the clinical and radiological findings, along with a history of occupational exposure. Although seizures are unlikely to be a direct consequence of manganism, we believe the cause for seizures in our patient was multifactorial, potentially triggered by multiple antibiotics, dyselectrolytemia in the background of manganism.

The patient was treated with antiepileptics with other supportive medications. Levodopa was discontinued due to worsening of psychiatric symptoms and absence of improvement in motor symptoms. Regular dialysis was continued during his stay in the hospital. Treatment of manganism involves removal of the source of exposure. Chelation therapy for manganism involves the use of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and para-aminosalicylic acid (PAS). EDTA is highly water soluble, it does not cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and may not reverse the toxic effects of manganese in chronic exposure. PAS and its metabolites concentrate within the brain parenchyma and thus can be a potential alternative.[5] However, the usual route of elimination of the chelated compounds is through the kidneys, hence, unlikely to be effective in our patient with ESRD on hemodialysis, as the molecular size of the chelated compounds is much larger for efficient removal on dialysis. Also, in patients with advanced manganism, chelation therapy is not likely to reverse the clinical deterioration.[6] Still, EDTA was considered, but rendered impractical due to unstable hemodynamics and rapidly declining clinical condition post-status epilepticus. Renal transplantation is another option for such patients, with one reported case of successful management of manganism with renal transplantation.[4] However, our patient was not a suitable candidate in view of septic shock with unstable hemodynamics, a downhill course, and eventually succumbed to septic shock.

Manganese is an essential trace element that enters the human body via ingestion or inhalation. It binds to transferrin and the Divalent Metal Transporter 1 (DMT1) protein in blood. Inhaled manganese particles can reach the brain directly via the olfactory bulb, bypassing BBB. The liver controls manganese levels by excreting excess amounts through bile.[6] A small amount is also excreted in urine. In conditions of high manganese levels, manganese can cross the BBB and preferentially accumulate in the basal ganglia due to high expression of DMT1 and transferrin receptors. Neuronal loss in this area alters dopaminergic activity, leading to Parkinsonism.

Apart from occupational exposure, manganese levels can be high in patients with CLD, ESRD, those on total parenteral nutrition, substance abuse, and genetic mutations causing manganese dyshomeostasis.

Trace element distribution in ESRD patients is still not well understood. ESRD can have high or low manganese levels.[7] One reason for high manganese levels in ESRD patients could be iron deficiency anemia[8] as iron and manganese are bound to the same protein in the bloodstream. When the iron levels are low, there will be upregulation of DMT1 and transferrin, leading to greater uptake of manganese. In our patient, we do not suspect iron deficiency anemia to be the cause of manganism; however, it may be a contributing factor. Other reasons are dysfunction of manganese transport channels facilitated by dialysate therapy, an increase in protein-bound (undiffusible) manganese that is not removed by hemodialysis, disturbance of excretion through the biliary tract, excessive manganese intake that exceeds the excretory capacity, and the effect of uremia on manganese metabolism.[3] Another reason could be the presence of manganese in the dialysate and erythropoietin treatment given to ESRD patients.[9]

Uremic patients show T2 hyperintensities in the basal ganglia, while manganese, being a paramagnetic substance, shows T1 and FLAIR hyperintensity in the basal ganglia.[2] Radiological features seen on MRI in the form of symmetrical T1 hyperintensities in bilateral basal ganglia [Figure 1] further supported the diagnosis of manganism in our case.

Manganism patients, in comparison with typical Parkinson patients, present with earlier neuropsychiatric symptoms and late extrapyramidal features with poor response to antiparkinsonian medications like Levodopa. A study[10] indicates that the clinical progression in patients with manganism continues even 10 years after cessation of exposure.

This case calls for heightened clinical suspicion and thorough diagnostic evaluation in patients with neuropsychiatric disturbances with Parkinsonism and occupational exposure history, particularly when standard treatments fail to yield improvement.
