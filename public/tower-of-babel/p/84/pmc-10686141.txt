Combining multi‐omics analysis to identify host‐targeted targets for the control of Brucella infection
Authors: Jiuwang Yu, Hongwei Yuan, Jiarong Guo, Zhiheng Dong, Sha Li, Quan Fu, Bilige Aode, Sachula Baoyin, Lidao Bao, Lan Wu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman infections caused by Brucella (called brucellosis) are among the most common zoonoses worldwide with an estimated 500,000 cases each year. Since chronic Brucella infections are extremely difficult to treat, there is an urgent need for more effective therapeutics. As a facultative intracellular bacterium, Brucella is strictly parasitic in the host cell. Here, we performed proteomic and transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses on Brucella infected patients, mice and cells that provided an extensive “map” of physiological changes in brucellosis patients and characterized the metabolic pathways essential to the response to infection, as well as the associated cellular response and molecular mechanisms. This is the first report utilizing multi‐omics analysis to investigate the global response of proteins and metabolites associated with Brucella infection, and the data can provide a comprehensive insight to understand the mechanism of Brucella infection. We demonstrated that Brucella increased nucleotide synthesis in the host, consistent with increased biomass requirement. We also identified IMPDH2, a key regulatory complex that controls nucleotide synthesis during Brucella infection. Pharmacological targeting of IMPDH2, the rate‐limiting enzyme in guanine nucleotide biosynthesis, efficiently inhibits B. abortus growth both in vitro and in vivo. Through screening a library of natural products, we identified oxymatrine, an alkaloid obtained primarily from Sophora roots, is a novel and selective IMPDH2 inhibitor. In further in vitro bacterial inhibition assays, oxymatrine effectively inhibited the growth of B. abortus, which was impaired by exogenous supplementation of guanosine, a salvage pathway of purine nucleotides. This moderately potent, structurally novel compound may provide clues for further design and development of efficient IMPDH2 inhibitors and also demonstrates the potential of natural compounds from plants against Brucella.

INTRODUCTION

Brucella is an interesting prototype to study host functional rearrangements and disease pathogenesis because it has refined strategies for evading immune responses and regulating cellular metabolism, including signalling pathways, translation machinery, and RNA interference. Brucella abortus is a zoonotic bacterial pathogen that establishes long‐term infections in its host. In vivo, the most common hosts are macrophages (Xavier et al., 2013). Bacteria replicate together with endoplasmic reticulum‐associated compartments of macrophages and interact subsequently with a modified autophagy pathway to promote cell‐to‐cell spread (de Bolle et al., 2015). To colonize its ecological niche, Brucella resists host defence while obtaining nutrients from the host environment to produce energy and biomass for its survival and replication. Brucellosis, a zoonotic infection caused by Brucella, is an inflammatory disease in which inflammation is present in both the acute and chronic stages of the disease and in almost all affected organs (Guimarães et al., 2020). The disease is transmitted to humans by direct/indirect contact with infected animals or through the consumption of contaminated foods. In humans, the disease can present as an undulating fever with arthralgia, sometimes with chronic and severe complications such as orchitis, spondylitis and arthritis (Mehari et al., 2021; Skyberg et al., 2011).

In recent years, with the wide application of high‐throughput sequencing technology, and the exponential growth of omics data, researchers have conducted in‐depth research on brucellosis from different aspects of omics. The results suggest that Brucella–host interactions and microbial immune evasion involve the manipulation of both host gene transcription and RNA translation. However, a global and integrated view of the multiple biological layers is required to understand the host antiviral response and mechanisms leading to disease. Here, we have analysed the proteomic and metabonomic data of a patient with blood before infection, acute phase, chronic phase and after recovery to confirm our findings. This patient is a male, 32 years old, from Xilingol City, Inner Mongolia, China. Blood samples were collected from the patient before infection, at the acute stage of infection (the fifth day of symptoms), at the chronic stage (the 65th day of infection) and after recovery. At the same time, we use B. abortus as a model to evaluate the disturbance of infection on cell homeostasis. We combined the most advanced B. abortus infection model with cutting‐edge bioinformatics technologies including proteomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics and numerical modelling, as well as multi‐source cellular analysis from public databases, to gain insight into the mechanisms of Brucella–host interaction.

In this study, we found that inosine‐monophosphate dehydrogenase 2 (IMPDH2) showed robust rhythmic expression before infection, acute phase, chronic phase and after recovery. Consistent with this finding, we observed that the IMP level of this patient also showed rhythmic oscillation through metabonomics. Inosine 5′‐monophosphate dehydrogenase2 (IMPDH2) is a rate‐limiting enzyme that catalyses the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)‐dependent oxidation of inosine monophosphate (IMP) to xanthosine monophosphate (XMP), which is an essential step in de novo biosynthesis of guanine nucleotides (Duan et al., 2018). Our study showed that IMPDH2 plays an important role in the control of the growth and reproduction cycle of Brucella. Finally, we propose that de novo purine synthesis may be the “accelerator and brake valve” that mediates Brucella biological cycle. This hypothesis sheds new light on how bacteria evade innate immune responses, reprogram host cell metabolism and trigger chronic inflammatory and metabolic disease, as well as new directions for drug development against Brucella.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

Ethics statement

All procedures involving patients and animals were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Inner Mongolia Medical University, and all experimental protocols were performed in accordance with approved guidelines.

Information of patient No. 0

The patient, a 32‐year‐old male from Xilinguole, Inner Mongolia, was a herder with cattle and sheep at home. The diagnosis was confirmed by laboratory tests with Rose‐Bengal plate agglutination test (>++) and standard tube agglutination test (SAT) titre 1:100++. Before the infection, the patient came to our hospital for a physical examination, had blood drawn, and he had blood drawn during the acute phase of the infection (day 5 of the onset of symptoms), during the chronic phase (day 65 of the infection), and after he had recovered. The patient had symptoms of excessive sweating, fever, and malaise. The collected blood samples were cultured for bacteria and identified by biochemical and PCR as B. abortus.

Liquid chromatography mass spectrometry

The UHPLC‐QE‐MS system consisted of a hybrid quadrupole‐orbital trap mass spectrometer and a Vanquish UHPLC system (Q‐Exactive, Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA). For non‐targeted metabolite analysis, separations were performed on a Vanquish Accucore C18+ UHPLC column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.5‐μm) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. The A phase of liquid chromatography was aqueous phase containing 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 25 mmol/L ammonia, and the B phase was acetonitrile with a re‐equilibration time of 10 min. The sample temperature was set to 4°C, the column temperature was set to 40°C, and the injection volume was 2 μL. The gradient started at 10% B and then increased to 95% B within 20 min and remained for 10 min. The mass scan range was set to m/z 100–1500 under electrospray with positive ions. The MS source parameters were as follows: sheath gas, 30 Arb; auxiliary and purge gas, 5 Arb; spray voltage, 3.2 kv; capillary temperature, 320°C. The mass spectrometry data were noise reduced, peak extracted, peak identified, normalized using Mzmine software, and then imported into SimcaP 14.0 software for principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS‐DA). Differential metabolites were screened according to variable importance in projection (VIP) >1.0, fold change (FC) between groups < 0.67 or >1.50 and p < 0.05, and differential metabolites were identified according to HMDB and KEGG databases.

Transcriptomics

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol kit, contaminating DNA was removed with RNA‐free DNaseI, and RNA quality was assessed on the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and RNA integrity was detected by the RNA Nano6000 assay kit on the Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Using the NEB⁃Nect® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina®, sequencing libraries were constructed from samples with RNA Integrity Number (RIN) values >7, and these libraries were then sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000. An FPKM (fragment per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) value was calculated for each transcription region to quantify its expression abundance and variations using StringTie software. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened by the thresholds of log2 (FC) ≥ 1 and False Discovery Rate (FDR) ≤ 0.05, and transcriptional expression patterns were explored by cluster analysis.

Proteomics

Lysis solution (1% SDS, 8 mol‐L−1 urea, 1 g‐L−1 protease inhibitor mixture) was added to each sample separately, homogenized for 3 min using an ultrasonic cell disruptor, and the supernatant was collected after centrifugation. Proteins were quantified with a BCA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), after which 300 mug of total protein was mixed with sequencing‐grade trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) at an enzyme‐to‐protein ratio of 1:50 and incubated at 37°C for 16 h. Peptides obtained from the digestion were dried by vacuum centrifugation. The peptide mixture was desalted using an Ultimate 3000 system connected to an XBridge C18 reverse column. Lyophilized peptides were dissolved in 95/3/0.1 (% v/v/v) water/acetonitrile/formic acid and analysed by online C18 nano HPLC MS/MS with a system consisting of an Easy‐nLC 1200 gradient HPLC system (Thermo Scientific, Bremen, Germany), and an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). The peptide sample was loaded onto the analytical column (Acclaim PepMap C18, 75 mum × 25 cm) and separated with a 120‐min gradient, from 5% to 35% B (B: 0.1% formic acid in ACN) at a flow rate of 200 nL/min. The electrospray voltage of 2 kV was used. The raw data from the DIA was processed and analysed with Spectronaut X. The proteomics data were normalized, the data were log2 transformed and analysed at the peptide level and protein level, and the statistical significance of the observed ploidy was determined by a one‐sample t‐test. DEPs were selected with|log2FC| > 1.5, and the difference was statistically significant (p < 0.05).

Metabolomics

One hundred microliters of serum sample were mixed with 400 muL of solution (acetonitrile: methanol = 1:1) and vortexed (30 s), then sonicated (10 min, ice water bath), and subsequently incubated (1 h, −40°C) to precipitate proteins. Samples were centrifuged at 5000 g and 4°C for 10 min, and the supernatant was passed through a 0.22 mum PTFE syringe filter. The sample volumes were all 2 μL, and the supernatant was placed in the injection vial and subsequently analysed by liquid chromatography mass spectrometry.

Purification and culture of bacteria

B. abortus 104 M was purchased from China CDC, identified by biochemical method and PCR, and then lyophilized and stored at −20°C. The B. abortus strain was inoculated in line on TS agar medium and incubated for 2 d at 37°C in a warm oven. Colourless and transparent, finely rounded, raised in the middle, neatly edged, smooth and moist surface colonies were selected, and the colonies were picked under aseptic conditions and inoculated in TS liquid medium, incubated at 37°C with shaking for 2–3 days to observe the concentration of bacteria, and stored at 4°C for backup.

Cell culture and infection

RAW264.7 was cultured in Dulbecco's Basic Essential Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), without antibiotics, and incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 atmosphere. RAW264.7 cells (3 × 106 cells per well) were inoculated onto 6‐well tissue culture plates and inoculated in DMEM (5% FBS) with an MOI of 100:1. After centrifugation at 250 × g for 10 min, the plates were placed in 5% CO2 and maintained at 37°C for 3 hours. The cells were then washed 3 times with DMEM and incubated in DMEM (5% FBS) containing 5 μg/mL gentamicin at 37°C and 5% CO2 for the indicated times. Cells were collected and sent to Shanghai Baiqu Biotec Corporation for metabolomic analysis.

Animal experiments

6–8 weeks female C57BL/6J wild‐type mices were used in the present study. Mice in the infected group were inoculated intraperitoneally (ip) with 0.2 mL of phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) containing 5 × 105 CFU of B. abortus. Mice were euthanized by CO2 at 0, 3, 9, 15, 21, 30, and/or 60 days post‐infection, depending on the experiments performed, and their livers and spleens were collected aseptically at necropsy. Spleens were homogenized in 2 mL PBS and serial dilutions of the homogenate were spread over TSA and/or TSA + kanamycin to calculate CFU. Samples were sent to Shanghai Baiqu Biotec Corporation for proteomic and transcriptomic assays.

CRISPR/Cas9 knockout cell line generation

THP‐1 macrophages expressing Cas9 and eGFP (Shanghai Enzyme Link Biotechnology Co.) were resuspended at a concentration of 106 cells/mL in RPMI with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). An amount of 20 mM HEPES buffer solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and lentiviral vectors with guide RNAs (LentiArray CRISPR Guide RNA; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for IMPDH2 were added (MOI = 5). Tubes were centrifuged (800 rpm, 2 h, 37 °C) for spin infection. Transduced cells were seeded into 6‐well plates and incubated for 24 h. After 24 h, the medium was changed. Cells were expanded and 4 days after transduction puromycin was added. Efficacy of knockout was confirmed by Western Blot analysis.

GMP and AMP rescue experiments

RAW264.7 were infected with B. abortus at MOI 100. MMF (0.1 g/mL) was added four hours after infection in the presence or absence of 10 μM GMP and AMP (Sigma). Infectious progeny assays were performed after 48 hours to determine infectivity.

Western immunoblotting

Total protein was extracted from the cells using RIPA buffer and the protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit. Protein (1 mg) per lane was loaded onto a 10% gel and separated by SDS‐PAGE. The isolated proteins were then transferred to PVDF membranes and closed with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were incubated with anti‐IMPDH2 (1:1000 dilution) and anti‐GAPDH overnight at 4°C. After incubation with the primary antibody, the membrane is incubated with the secondary antibody (anti‐IgG; 1:20,000) for 1 h at room temperature. Total proteins were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (ECL), and blots were analysed using ImageJ version 1.48u software.

RNA extraction and quantitative RT‐PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using TRIzol lysate. Total RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using the SuperScript Reverse Transcriptase kit at 37°C for 15 min. This was followed by a two‐step qPCR using SYBR Green and the CFX96™ Real‐Time PCR Detection System. Thermal cycling conditions using the following format: 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 60°C for 30 s, extension at 95°C for 10 s and finally extension at 65–95°C for 5 s. The primer sequences used to amplify IMPDH2 were: 5′‐GTTTCTGCGGTATCCCAATC‐3′ (forward) and 5’‐CGAGCAAGTCCAGCCTAT‐3′ (reverse). GAPDH was used as a standardized internal control. Three replicate wells were set up for each sample. Relative gene expression was determined by relative quantitative calculation of ploidy changes (2−ΔΔCq method).

Determination of cytotoxicity

RAW264.7 cells in 96‐well tissue culture plates were incubated with different concentrations of MMF (0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0 and 2.0 μg/mL) for 48 h at 37°C and 5% CO2 atmosphere in a total volume of 100 μL per well. Cell viability was measured using the 3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) cytotoxicity assay. Briefly, at the end of the culture, the cells were incubated in RPMI 1640 containing MTT (0.5 mg/mL) for 3 h under standard culture conditions. The MTT solution was then removed, 150 μL of DMSO was added to each well and incubated for 15 min. The absorbance of each well was measured at 540 nm. Cell viability was calculated by dividing the average absorbance of the treated cells by the average absorbance of the control cells. The highest non‐cytotoxic concentration of MMF was used in the subsequent experiments.

Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay

Secretory levels of TNF‐α and IFN‐γ were determined by ELISA kits (Shanghai Enzyme Link Biotechnology Co.) according to the manufacturer's instructions. These experiments were performed in triplicate, with three biological replicates.

HE staining experiment

Liver and spleen tissues were first made into sections, placed in liquid and carried on slides, followed by drying at 38°C for 24 h. The dried sections were dewaxed and hydrated, followed by staining with haematoxylin for 5.5 min, rinsing in tap water for 10 s to return to blue, and finally staining in eosin for 8 s. The stained sections were dehydrated and transparent, and finally sealed with neutral resin and coverslips.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Liver and spleen tissues were first made and thin sections were placed in liquid, carried on slides, and subsequently dried at 38°C for 2 h. The dried tissue sections were dewaxed and hydrated, and antigen repair and sealing of the tissue sections was performed by direct boiling antigen repair method using citric acid tissue antigen repair solution. The PBS solution was removed, rabbit anti‐p65 primary antibody was added and incubated overnight at room temperature. Enzyme‐labelled sheep anti‐rabbit IgG polymer antibody was added and incubated for 15 min at 37°C. DAB colour development solution was added and incubated for 5 min. After re‐staining, the sections were dehydrated and transparent, and finally sealed with neutral resin and coverslips.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were transferred to 8‐well Lab‐Tek II CC2 chamber slides (Thermo Scientific) and incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 1 hr to allow cells to adhere to the surface. The cells were then fixed in 10% formalin diluted in PBS. Cells were subsequently washed with PBS, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X‐100 in PBS, and then washed with 0.1% Triton X‐100. Cells were stained using anti‐IMPDH2 (1:500, Abcam ab129165) diluted in Closure Buffer. Alexa Fluor 488 coupled goat anti‐rabbit secondary antibody (ThermoFisher Scientific A‐11001) was used to detect primary antibody binding and DAPI was used to stain nuclei. Vectashield sealer (Vector) is applied to the cells and a 24 × 50 mm coverslip is sealed on the slide with clear nail polish. Imaging was performed using a 63× oil immersion objective of a Leica Microsystems TCS SP8 Advanced confocal laser scanning microscope. The number of cells containing filaments under each treatment condition was quantified as described in the legend, and all statistics were calculated in Graphpad Prism.

Bacterial load determination

Livers and spleens were collected and homogenized in sterile PBS. All samples were serially diluted in sterile PBS and cultured on 5% sheep blood agar plates. Plates were incubated for 24 h in 37°C, and colony enumeration was performed the next day.

Bioinformatics analysis

The functions of the proteins were annotated by Gene Ontology (GO, http://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) using InterProScan software according to the protein sequence alignment method. Then, proteins were classified according to three GO annotation categories: biological processes, cellular components and molecular functions. In addition, functional enrichment analysis was performed on the basis of GO and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, http://www.genome.jp/kaas‐bin/kaas_main) pathway analysis, and the corresponding functional and pathway with p < 0.05 (two‐tailed Fisher exact test) were considered statistically significant.

Integrated analysis of metabolomics and proteomics

In order to compare and integrate the results of two different histological results, we obtained standard pathways and molecular interaction networks with standard settings using IPA software. The p‐value was used to select the most important results. Prior to import into IPA, metabolites were transformed into a Human Metabolomics Database (HMDB) marker using metabolic analysis.

Molecular docking

The crystal structure of IMPDH2 was obtained from the online Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 1NF7). The 3D structure file of the active compound is downloaded using the ZINC database (http://zinc.docking.org/). The ligand and receptor molecules need to be energy minimized before docking by removing the water molecule from the receptor molecule (in the PDB file), adding a polar hydrogen atom, assigning an electrical charge, and adding a magnetic field. The location of the original ligand compound was used as the binding site, and all substructures within a radius of 0.65 nm were used as the active pocket portion of the binding site. Autodock molecular docking software (version 2.5) was used to dock the active component to the receptor protein molecule and output the receptor–ligand docking model file (PDBQT) and binding energy. The docking results were visualized using Pymol as well as Discovery Studio Visualizer software. The aim is to examine the binding mode of the compounds to the corresponding targets and the binding free energy to determine the affinity between them and the accuracy of the system pharmacology.

Molecular dynamics simulation

100 ns molecular dynamics simulation of the structure of the complex of the small molecule with the lowest docking energy to the receptor was performed using GROMACS to observe the stability of the complex. The protein file was first preprocessed and the force field was set. Then, the structure was solventized and a periodic boundary solvent model was selected and a solvent environment of physiological saline was added so that the overall charge was neutral. After the construction of the complex model of the submerged water box, the energy optimization was carried out first, and the step size time step was set to 2 fs, and then the kinetic simulation was carried out. The force field used in the simulation was mainly CHARMM force field, other parameters were set to default values, and the RMSD values were analysed at the end of the simulation.

Construction and purification of IMPDH2 expression vector

The Brucella H37Rv genome was used as a template and the pET28a expression plasmid was used as a vector to clone the alr gene into pET28a, constructed the pET28a::alr recombinant expression plasmid, expressed and purified it to obtain recombinant Brucella IMPDH2. A screening model for this enzyme inhibitor was constructed and optimized by measuring the rate of optical density change of the reaction product NADH at 340 nm and detecting the enzyme reaction activity; applying the model to screen the compound library; and measuring the IC50 of the active compound.

Enzyme inhibition test

The analysis was performed in 96‐well microplates with a final volume of 200 μL in reaction buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 100 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, 3 mM EDTA and 100 μm NAD+) containing recombinant IMPDH2 (150 nM). The reaction was started by adding 100 μm IMP and NADH production was monitored after 30 min by reading the absorbance at 340 nm. For the initial screening assay, the indicated compounds were tested at a final concentration of 10 μm and MPA (10 μm) was used as a positive control. A total of eight concentrations of each compound were used to determine dose–response curves, and IC50 values were calculated using GraphPad Prism 5.

Enzyme inhibition pattern determination

To determine the inhibitor pattern of oxymatrine, enzyme kinetic experiments were performed as described previously. Recombinant IMPDH2 was incubated with paclitaxel (0.5 or 1 μm) in analytical buffer (100 mmol Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 100 mmol KCl, 1 mmol DTT, 3 mmol EDTA, 100 mmol micron N A D+) for 30 min at room temperature. The reaction was started by adding different concentrations of IMP (10–400 μm) and the production of NADH was detected at 340 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. Enzyme kinetic data were analysed using Linewaver‐Burk plots. In three independent experiments, the data are shown as mean values.

Thermal displacement analysis

A total of 0.2 mg/mL of HcProRS (corresponding to 3.45 M) was incubated with the compounds in a reaction buffer containing 50 mM HEPES pH 7.0, 150 mM KCl and 10% (v/v) ethylene glycol in the absence or presence of 1 mM L‐Pro and 1× thermal displacement dye. Aliquots (20 L) were transferred to 96‐well polymerase chain reaction plates in triplicate. After centrifugation to remove air bubbles, plates were measured in an Applied Biosystems protein thermal displacement machine and incremented at a rate of 0.05°C/s over a temperature gradient from 4°C to 95°C. Fluorescence was detected by protein thermal displacement software. Data analysis was performed by Boltzmann sigma curve fitting to calculate the melting temperature (Tm).

Data analysis

Prior to statistical analysis, ratios (bacterial counts or mRNA levels) and percentages (fluorescence microscopy) of fold change were log‐transformed. An unpaired Student's t‐test was performed on the transformed data to determine whether the differences between groups were statistically significant (p < 0.05). When more than two treatments were used, statistically significant differences between groups were determined by one‐way ANOVA. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 6B software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA).

RESULTS

Analysis of the dynamics between Brucella and host mRNA and proteins during infection

A total of 565 differentially expressed proteins were identified by quantitative proteomics analysis of four groups of patient serum. Then, the 565 differentially expressed proteins were divided into three groups according to the different stages of infection (Figure 1B–D and Figure S1D). Among them, 31.5% of the differential proteins are immunoglobulin and immune response‐related proteins, which also indicates that host immune response is one of the most important aspects in the interaction between Brucella and human body. Based on the information on these differential proteins, subcellular localization was examined by CELLO software (http://cello.life.nctu.edu.tw/) (Figure 1I) (Huang et al., 2020). The results showed that these proteins were mainly distributed in the cytoplasm, endoplasmic reticulum, ribosome and periplasm, with 54%, 28%, 9%, 8% and 1% of up‐regulated proteins and 68%, 14%, 13%, 4% and 1% of down‐regulated proteins, respectively (Figure S1A–C).

Analysis of the dynamics between Brucella and host mRNA and proteins. (A) Overview of cohort and the study design; (B–D) Volcano plots of quantified proteins in healthy group versus acute phase group, acute phase group versus chronic phase group, and chronic phase group versus recovery group; (E) Volcano plot of differential proteins in infection experiments in rats; FGH KEGG enrichment analysis in healthy group versus acute phase group, acute phase group versus chronic phase group, and chronic phase group versus recovery group; (F–H) KEGG enrichment analysis was performed to compare the healthy group with the acute phase group, the acute phase group with the chronic phase group, and the chronic phase group with the recovery group; (I) Results of subcellular localization of differential proteins; (J) Proteome and transcriptome mapping of B. abortus infection; (K) Gene sets obtained from the MSigDB that are modulated by B. abortus infection on the RNA and protein level; (L) Stimulation (red) and suppression (blue) of host pathways (gene sets) by B. abortus infection; (M) Enrichment analysis of differential proteins in B. abortus‐infected mice.

To assess the main pathways involved in differential proteins, we performed GO annotation and KEGG analysis and examined the possible biological interactions of the proteins (Figure 1F–H). The results showed that proteins of the bacterial secretion system, ABC transporter and population sensing were significantly down‐regulated (Ratio > 1.5, p‐value < 0.05) and proteins involved in nucleotide metabolism as well as immune response‐related proteins were significantly up‐regulated in the acute phase compared to the pre‐infection phase. Compared with the chronic phase, the proteins of glucose metabolism and nucleotide metabolism were further up‐regulated in the acute phase. The nucleotide metabolism in the recovery period showed a cliff‐like downward adjustment compared with the chronic period. These results indicated that Brucella may promote its own persistent infection by regulating the synthesis and metabolism of nucleotides.

To gain insight into the changes in the organism during Brucella infection, we performed the infection experiment of B. abortus in vivo. Mice were inoculated intraperitoneally with 5 × 106 CFU B. abortus. Samples were collected on days 3, 7 and 15 for transcriptomic and proteomic analysis. A total of 19,850 mRNAs and 8174 proteins were quantified, providing a multidimensional picture of persistent B. abortus infection (Figure S1E). Overall, 7312 proteins (89.4%) were uniquely localized to overlapping mRNAs (Figure 1J). We found that 15 days after infection, B. abortus significantly altered 42.6% of transcriptomic data and 31.5% of proteomic data compared to control mice (Figure 1K). Most of these transcriptomes and proteomes are usually downregulated (negatively enriched). In addition, 79% of these genes that were downregulated at the RNA level were also significantly impaired at the protein level. In contrast, only 15% of the upregulated pathways on the RNA level matched the corresponding protein trends (Figure 1L). This suggests that B. abortus infection shuts down most of the non‐essential biological processes to divert the resources toward bacterial replication and persistence. Consistently, the upregulated biological processes and pathways mainly included immune response and inflammation‐related pathways, such as the PI3K/AKT pathway (Figure 1M). Most importantly, we found that various pathways associated with small molecule metabolites are equally regulated, including adenosine/purine nucleotide synthesis, carbon metabolism, and pyruvate metabolism. Overall, transcriptional changes in the host revealed a general stress response, while activation and inactivation events of many pathways in the pathogen indicated the adaptive capacity of Brucella during infection.

Changes in metabolism after Brucella infection

We were next interested in whether the changes in host proteins could be reflected at the level of endogenous metabolites. We performed a comparative analysis of the metabolomics of patient at each stage of infection and defined the threshold for significant changes (|log2FC| > 1.5 FDR < 0.05) (Figure S2A–C). To discover general metabolic trends in the host upon Brucella infection, we performed a metabolite set enrichment analysis for both significantly increased and decreased metabolites using the functional set definitions of the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) (Lasky‐Su et al., 2017).

A total of 321 differential metabolites were identified by UHPLC‐QE‐MS, of which 102 eligible metabolites were screened in the pre‐infection versus acute phase, mainly including glycine, serine, adenine, guanine, pyruvate, lactate and glutamate. The interaction network of these metabolites is complex and diverse, among which guanine, pyruvate and lactate have the highest node degree, which plays an important role in this network, indicating that Brucella infection can cause changes in host metabolism, especially in some nucleotides. There were 109 differential metabolites in the acute versus chronic phase, of which 60 were upregulated and 49 were downregulated, with core metabolites involving nucleotides and lipids. There were 110 differential metabolites in the chronic versus recovery period, of which 35 were upregulated and 75 were downregulated. Isoxanthine, glutamate, lactate and guanine were the core metabolites and their expression was decreased, indicating that Brucella can affect the synthesis of host ribonucleotides. KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the differential metabolites were mainly enriched in purine nucleotide synthesis, Warburg effect and glutamate metabolism. Metabolic shift is a prominent feature of Brucella infection suggesting that Brucella invasion may disrupt or interfere with host metabolism, and likely enable metabolic modification in the bacteria itself. The results of functional roles and pathways of all differential metabolites are consistent with proteomic data, and different metabolites and proteins are associated with nucleotide and energy metabolism.

To determine the metabolic pathway of Brucella in early infection, RAW264.7 cells were infected at a 100:1 ratio of bacteria to macrophages with B. abortus. Samples were collected at day 7 for metabolomic analysis. We compared metabolic changes in B. abortus‐infected and PBS‐treated macrophages and analysed the differential metabolites in the cells before and after infection (Figure S2D), showing that the differential metabolites were significantly enriched in the regulation of host cell energy and the biosynthesis of guanosine‐containing compounds (Figure S3D). This is consistent with the fact that B. abortus is substantially dependent on the energy provided by the host. To better provide a comprehensive understanding of biological systems in response to B. abortus infection, proteomics and metabolomics data were integrated to acquire the shared pathways and interaction between DEPs and DEMs using Ingenuity Pathways Analysis (IPA) software (Hsu et al., 2019). Based on the interactions between these proteins and metabolites, an interaction pathway map between cellular metabolism and host‐essential factors was constructed (Figure 3). Notably, we observed a significant increase in pyruvate, lactate and glutamate levels after B. abortus infection. The increase in these metabolites is similar to the Warburg effect, which is also known as aerobic glycolysis, and is characterized by the enhanced conversion of glucose to lactate (Figure 2I–N). Aerobic glycolysis is upregulated in cells that require increased biomass, such as rapidly proliferating cells and cancer cells (Zhang et al., 2020). Thus, similar to what occurs in cancer cells, B. abortus puts host cells in a hypermetabolic state with a Warburg effect, thereby regulating host metabolism to meet the high metabolic demands of rapid growth.

At the same time, B. abortus is able to up‐regulate host ribonucleotide synthesis, and this alteration allows B. abortus to ensure an adequate supply of nucleotides for itself. B. abortus lacks the enzymes of de novo purine biosynthesis machinery and is therefore strictly dependent on the host supply. Four enzymes were identified in our screen that were significantly enriched in de novo nucleotide metabolism, hypoxanthine‐guanine phosphate ribosyltransferase (HGPRT), adenine phosphate ribosyltransferase (APRT), inosine‐5′‐monophosphate dehydrogenase 2 (IMPDH2) and xanthine oxidase (XO).

We generated a stable small hairpin RNA (shRNA)‐mediated knockdown based on lentiviral transduction (Figure 2A–D). For each enzyme of de novo purine metabolism, a significant reduction in infectivity was detected using at least two different shRNA sequences (Figure 2E–H). In addition, we explored the consequences of complete loss of IMPDH2 using CRISPR/Cas9 knockout technology. Complete knockdown of IMPDH2 protein was confirmed by Western blot (Figure 2P). Bacterial growth and proliferation capacity were significantly inhibited after IMPDH2 silencing (Figure 2O). We conclude that successful B. abortus infection requires an intact de novo purine biosynthesis pathway and that IMPDH2 is a host factor necessary for B. abortus reproduction.

Persistent Brucella infection increases IMPDH2 enzyme function

In the mapped metabolic network, the de novo nucleotide biosynthesis pathway was considered as a significant metabolic pathway. Screening of four key host factors showed that IMPDH2 is a promising target for inhibition of Brucella proliferation. Ribonucleotides play a central role in cell physiology, maintaining optimal nucleotide levels through a complex network of regulation. The de novo nucleotide biosynthesis pathway is upregulated when nucleotide demand is high, for example during cell proliferation (Calise et al., 2018). IMPDH2 is an important rate‐limiting enzyme of guanine de novo synthesis pathway, which relies on NAD to exert its activity, catalysing inosine monophosphate (IMP) to xanthine monophosphate (XMP). If its intracellular activity is blocked, guanosine synthesis will be blocked, thus affecting DNA synthesis and inhibiting cell proliferation(Figure 4G) (Schiavon et al., 2018). Most IMPDH2 monomers contain two domains: the catalytic domain, which is a (beta/alpha)8 barrel, and the subdomain containing two CBS domains (named for the homologous domains in cystathionine beta synthase; also known as Bateman domains) (Figure 4A) (Makowska‐Grzyska et al., 2012). The active form of IMPDH2 is a stable tetrameric filament composed of four monomers, in which the CBS structural domain protrudes from the four corners of the tetramer, while the (β/α)8 barrel domain is buried in the central region of the tetramer(Figure 4B). If the tetrameric filaments break down, then IMPDH2 will lose its function.

Increased IMPDH2 enzyme function can be observed in the proteomics of brucellosis patients (Figure 4C). Enrichment of differential metabolites showed that the pathway of de novo purine biosynthesis increased (Figure S3A–C). The substrate IMP and product GMP of IMPDH2 increased, which further proved that the enzyme function of IMPDH2 increased (Figure 4D). The accumulation of intermediates was also observed. In fact, we found that general enrichment of the de novo purine biosynthesis pathway among the host genes with increased expression during infection, which is consistent with increased ribonucleotide synthesis. Similarly, it can be observed that the enzyme activity and mRNA expression of IMPDH2 increased from the differential proteins of mice infected by B. abortus (Figure 4E).

To gain insight into macrophage changes in B. abortus infection, we performed the experiment of B. abortus infection in RAW264.7 cells. RAW264.7 cells were infected at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100 (Mailybayeva et al., 2017). The results of the bacterial counts showed an increase in the number of bacteria early in the infection (1–3 days) and then a decrease in the next 21 days, which we defined as the acute phase of the infection. Between 30 and 60 days after infection, the bacterial count remains essentially unchanged, which is defined as the chronic phase of infection (Figure 4H).

To verify this result, we examined the expression of IMPDH2 in macrophages. The results showed that IMPDH2 was highly expressed in B. abortus‐infected macrophages (Figure 4F). RT‐qPCR was conducted to measure the mRNA expression levels of IMPDH2 between infected cells and normal cells. The results showed that the expression level of IMPDH2 mRNA was significantly (p < 0.0001) higher in infected samples than in normal samples (Figure 4F). Consistently, compared with uninfected macrophages, immunofluorescence staining of IMPHD2 showed that IMPDH2 showed red fluorescence, and the nucleus showed blue fluorescence. After image merging, IMPDH2 stained positive cells showed rose red fluorescence. Compared with the control group, the number of IMPDH2‐staining positive cells/mm2 was higher in the infected group and the IMPDH2 was mainly expressed in the nucleus, indicating enhanced IMPDH2 enzyme function in these cells (Figure 4I). We also compared IMPDH2 expression in acute and chronic infection and showed that IMPDH2 expression was elevated in the acute phase (p < 0.01) and decreased in persistent infection compared to the acute phase (p < 0.05) (Figure 4L,N).

In addition, we also analysed four datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus database, which also validated the results. In the GEO database, we used four datasets (GSE14704, GSE96071, GSE100914 and GSE28190). These datasets are genomic and transcriptomic analyses of Brucella infected mouse macrophages or THP‐1 macrophages. The results showed that the expression levels of IMPDH2 were different in normal macrophages and infected macrophages. The expression of IMPDH2 was significantly higher in infected cells than in normal cells (p < 0.01) (Figure 4J). We also found this phenomenon in the genomics of brucellosis patients. In order to further determine the expression level of IMPDH2 in clinical samples of human brucellosis, we performed data mining in publicly available datasets using the GEO database (GSE107554). The dataset contains CD4+ T cell samples from 7 acute B. abortus infections and 8 chronic infections as well as 8 uninfected individuals. Upregulation of IMPDH2 mRNA was found in 12 of all 15 samples when compared to the control samples (Figure 4K). Interestingly, the level of IMPDH2 mRNA was higher in the acute phase of infection compared to that of the chronic phase (Figure 4M).

Host factor targeting is effective in vivo and in vitro

Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) is an ester derivative of mycophenolic acid (MPA). MMF has a unique immunosuppressive effect because it can selectively inhibit the proliferation and function of T and B lymphocytes by specifically inhibiting IMPDH and reducing the synthesis of guanine nucleotides (Lv et al., 2018). We evaluated the effect of different concentrations of MMF on the activity of RAW264.7 cells, and the results showed that MMF showed some toxic effects on the cells after 24 h, and the toxic effects increased with the increase of the drug concentration (Figure 5A). Cells had the best viability at MMF concentration of 0.1 μg/mL, and the cell survival rate was 97.40% (p < 0.01). To determine whether MMF has an anti‐B. abortus effect, RAW264.7 cells were infected with B. abortus at MOI 100 and MMF was added at a concentration of 0.1 μg/mL 4 h later. Immunofluorescence staining for IMPDH2 showed a dramatic increase in IMPDH2 levels on day 7 of infection. In the presence of inhibitors, the expression level of IMPDH2 was significantly reduced (Figure 5C). These results are consistent with the western blot shown in Figure 5D. The results of intracellular viable counts of B. abortus showed that the number of B. abortus and the production of infected offspring were also significantly reduced or even completely disappeared (Figure 5B). On the other hand, we showed that the levels of Th1 cytokines (interferon gamma (IFN‐gamma)) and tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐alpha) were reduced in infected RAW264.7 cells treated with MMF, as measured by ELISA (p < 0.01) (Figure 5G and Figure S5A). MMF significantly blocked infectivity at the concentration of 0.1 μg/mL. The addition of 10 μm GMP (IMPDH2‐catalysed specific purine product) partially rescued the infection. On the other hand, no intervention against Brucella infection was observed with the addition of AMP (10 μm) (Figure 5F). This confirms that the inhibition of B. abortus infectivity by MMF is mediated via its effect on IMPDH2. To further verify the effect of GMP, AMP on IMPDH2 activity, we infected RAW264.7 with B. abortus to induce intense IMPDH2 expression and assembly, and then supplemented the medium with guanosine and adenosine for 1 h. Nucleosides in culture medium can be taken up by cells and converted to the corresponding nucleotides, thus bypassing the requirement for de novo ribose synthesis (Du et al., 2013). After guanosine was added, the level of IMPDH2 protein remained unchanged, but it would cause the rapid decomposition of IMPDH2 tetramer filaments, while adenosine would not change the number and shape of filaments (Figure 5H). In addition, the decomposition of IMPDH2 filaments by guanosine was not associated with the change of IMPDH2 protein level (Figure 5E). Therefore, guanosine and/or its downstream metabolites can promote the decomposition of IMPDH2 filaments.

To demonstrate the in vivo efficacy of targeting IMPDH2, we applied MMF to a potent mouse model of B. abortus infection. MMF was administered by gavage twice daily at a dose of 200 mg/kg (Rother et al., 2018). In the early stage of infection, especially in the first 3 days, the infected group showed more symptoms, including unresponsiveness, slow movement, wasting, individual mice showed bowed head and shrunken back, and respiratory difficulties. No death was observed in either the administered or infected groups over the 15‐day course of the experiment. Infectious B. abortus load in mouse liver and spleen was significantly reduced at day 4 post‐infection (Figure 5K,L). Consistent with the reduced bacterial load, MMF‐treated mice had a significant increase in body weight (Figure 5J). Parallel measurements of liver and spleen pathology showed that the MMF‐treated uninfected mice had normal tissue morphology, while the livers of buffer‐treated infected mice showed increased pathological changes. The liver parenchyma was heavily infiltrated by mixed inflammatory cells and the normal structure of the liver lobules was lost. The number of microgranulomas (MG) containing macrophages and polymorphonuclear cells was significantly increased. The spleen of mice exhibited a severe inflammatory response characterized by dilated neutrophils and red medullary sinuses. Follicular depletion was another significant change observed at day 7 post‐infection. In addition, MG‐like structures were found in the spleen. In contrast, in the livers of MMF‐treated infected mice, well‐defined inflammatory foci consisting of macrophages and some neutrophils were shown. The anatomical structure of spleen is similar to that of uninfected mice, showing no lymphodepletion, and only germinal center activity was detected in mice (Figure S4). The levels of inflammatory factors showed that the levels of TNF‐α and IFN‐γ were also significantly lower (Figure 5I and Figure S5B), and the concentration of myeloperoxidase (MPO) was reduced (p < 0.001) (Figure S5C), indicating an attenuated inflammatory response. Western blotting showed increased levels of IMPDH2 expression and increased mRNA levels in splenic T cells of infected mice compared to control mice, and decreased levels of IMPDH2 in the drug‐administered group (Figure 5N). In conclusion, these results suggested that inhibitory regulation of IMPDH2 reduces the intracellular persistence of B. abortus.

IMPDH2 promotes B. abortus replication through mTOR signalling pathway

To explore other possible mechanisms of IMPDH2 in the progression of brucellosis, we performed a gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on an infected mouse model (Figure 6A). The results of GSEA showed that the gene set KEGG_MTOR_SIGNALLING_PATHWAY was enriched in IMPDH2 high expression samples, indicating that this gene set was positively correlated with the high expression of IMPDH2. To our knowledge, mTOR is a key kinase downstream target of the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway, which is associated with cell cycle progression and cell proliferation (Hu et al., 2014). Therefore, we further investigated whether the proliferation‐promoting effect of IMPDH2 was dependent on PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling. Phospho‐AKT (p‐AKT(Ser473)), phospho‐GSK‐3β (p‐GSK‐3β) and phospho‐mTOR (p‐mTOR) proteins were increased through the expression of IMPDH2. To address this hypothesis, we treated macrophages with high expression of IMPDH2 with an AKT inhibitor (LY294002). We found that LY294002 significantly decreased the expression levels of p‐AKT, p‐GSK‐3β and p‐mTOR (Figure 6B). These observations confirmed that IMPDH2 could promote bacterial proliferation through activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway.

Similarly, we also explored the possible interaction between IMPDH2 and mTOR in the stage of T cell activation and proliferation. T cell activation is dependent on increased production of purine nucleotides and is associated with IMPDH2 filaments assembly (Johnson & Kollman, 2020). We isolated and activated mouse spleen T cells using antibodies against the TCR co‐receptors CD3ε and CD28 (Parayath et al., 2020). Strikingly, within 24 h, IMPDH2 assembled into linear and cyclic components in the majority of T cells (Figure 6D,F). Western blot showed that filament assembly was accompanied by a dramatic increase in IMPDH2 levels (Figure 6G). mTOR is a major regulator of multiple metabolic pathways during T cell activation. Recently, mTOR has been proved to promote purine biosynthesis (Schmitt & An, 2017). Therefore, we explored whether mTOR activity has an effect on IMPDH2 filaments assembly. We stimulated T cells overnight to establish IMPDH2 filaments and then treated them with the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin for 1 h. Immunofluorescence staining showed almost complete disassembly of IMPDH2 filaments (Figure 6E,H). The rapid breakdown of IMPDH2 filaments after treatment with mTOR inhibitors means that their maintenance requires a continuous mTOR signalling. Furthermore, filament disassembly is not due to changes in IMPDH2 protein expression, supporting the existence of an mTOR‐dependent regulatory mechanism (Figure 6I).

Oxymatrine is a novel and selective IMPDH2 inhibitor

At present, mycophenolic acid (MPA) preparation is the most widely used and systematically studied drug among the clinical IMPDH inhibitors. However, MPA has a narrow therapeutic range, large inter‐individual variability in pharmacokinetics, and interactions with combined medications, which severely limits its use (Go et al., 2018). Therefore, specific, efficient and low‐toxic natural IMPDH2 inhibitors have important clinical applications.

In order to discover more natural IMPDH2 inhibitors, we established a high‐throughput in vitro screening system. First, we produced recombinant human IMPDH2 (hIMPDH2) using an Escherichia coli (E. coli) bacterial expression system and purified it with immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) columns. IMPDH2 can be efficiently expressed in E. coli with >90% purity (Figure 7C).

In order to explore new IMPDH2 inhibitors, we collected 150 natural products with different chemical structures. We tested the inhibitory effect of these compounds on hIMPDH2 enzyme activity. MPA was used as a positive control (Figure 7B). MPA showed an effective inhibition of hIMPDH2 with an inhibitory concentration (IC50) value of 28.79 ± 0.68 μm. Among these compounds, oxymatrine (purity >98%, purchased from Undersun Biologics, Xi'an), one of the major alkaloid components of Sophora roots (Li et al., 2022), showed the strongest inhibitory effect on hIMPDH2 with an IC 50 value of 4.10 ± 0.14 μm (Figure 7E). These data suggested that oxymatrine is a novel inhibitor of hIMPDH2. In elution experiments, the enzymatic activity of IMPDH2 was reversed when oxymatrine was diluted 10‐fold, suggesting that oxymatrine is a reversible IMPDH2 inhibitor (Figure 7F). TSA is a fast and convenient fluorescence method that can be used as a strong evidence of direct interactions (Andreotti et al., 2015). The purified recombinant hIMPDH2 were subjected to thermal scanning in the absence and presence of the oxymatrine, and melting temperature (Tm) was calculated from the melt curve. We observed that IMPDH2 itself has a Tm of about 71.5°C. The addition of oxymatrine effectively stabilized IMPDH2 protein, and Tm increased by more than 5°C. In general, any small molecule that produces a Tm > 2.0°C over the control can be considered as a potential inhibitor (Batista et al., 2020). These demonstrated a direct interaction between oxymatrine and IMPDH2 (Figure 7G and Figure S5E).

Molecular docking is a computational tool for predicting the binding ability and binding mode of proteins and ligands. It is based on the “lock‐and‐key model” of protein–ligand interactions, which calculates and predicts the conformation and orientation of ligands at the active site of proteins to determine the degree of binding, and plays an important role in the prediction of targets in drug organisms (March‐Vila et al., 2017). Molecular dynamics simulations were then used to further reveal the detailed interaction model between oxymatrine and IMPDH2. The crystal structure of IMPDH2 was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 1NF7) with the original ligand and water molecule removed. Molecular docking was performed using Autodock molecular docking software (version 2.5), using PyMOL to visualize the binding pattern of oxymatrine to surrounding active pocket residues. The aim is to examine the binding mode of the compounds to the corresponding targets and the binding free energy to determine the affinity between them and to verify our screening results. We observed that the main backbone of oxymatrine is deeply buried in the matrix binding pocket of IMPDH2. Two hydrogen bonds were formed between oxymatrine and IMPDH2, including Arg332 and Ser68 (Figure 7H and Figure S5D). The occupation of substrate binding pocket by oxymatrine explains for its reversibility inhibition against IMPDH2. Molecular dynamics simulations have been proved to be a good strategy to improve virtual screening (Dai et al., 2020). In order to optimize the binding pattern of compound and IMPDH2, 100 ns MD simulations were carried out and the lowest energy structures were extracted. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) curves of the protein–ligand complexes over time for the molecular dynamics simulations, the images showed that after 10 conformations, the system no longer showed large fluctuations and the process stabilized toward and converged to approximately 0.36 (Figure 7L). It showed that the protein–ligand complex reached a good equilibrium state, and the protein–ligand binding reached the tightest and strongest state, indicating that the compound could bind stably at the binding pocket of IMPDH2.

The inhibitory effect of oxymatrine on the growth of RAW 264.7 cells was assessed by MTT assay, which showed that oxymatrine significantly inhibited the viability of RAW 264.7 cells in a dose‐dependent manner. The viability of RAW 264.7 cells decreased dramatically at oxymatrine concentrations of 0.1–3 mg/mL. However, higher concentrations of oxymatrine (>3 mg/mL) had a tendency for saturation inhibition.(Figure 7I). Therefore, the highest non‐cytotoxic concentration (0.3 g/mL) with no significant effect on the cell viability was used for subsequent experiments to further investigate the molecular mechanism of oxymatrine action. On this basis, we tested the inhibitory effect of oxymatrine on B. abortus. The addition of 0.3 g/mL of oxymatrine to B. abortus‐infected macrophages resulted in the observation that oxymatrine significantly reduced the proliferation and progeny production of B. abortus (Figure 7J).

We next tested whether oxymatrine affects GMP biosynthesis in RAW264.7 cells by targeting hIMPDH2 and evaluated the effect of guanosine on oxymatrine inhibition of cell growth. There are two pathways of purine nucleotide biosynthesis: the de novo pathways and the salvage pathways, of which guanosine is the salvage pathways that maintains guanine nucleotides (He et al., 2010). Therefore, we hypothesized that exogenous guanosine supplement might restore the intracellular guanosine monophosphate (GMP) pool via salvage pathway, thereby impairing the anti‐proliferative activity of oxymatrine. As expected, the addition of exogenous guanosine at a final concentration of 200 μm significantly attenuated the cytotoxicity of oxymatrine (Figure 7I). To investigate the targeting of oxymatrine to IMPDH2, we silenced IMPDH2 in macrophages with shRNA (Figure 7M). Silencing of IMPDH2 significantly impaired the intracellular survival of B. abortus (Figure 7K). In addition, silencing of IMPDH2 reduced the sensitivity of macrophages to oxymatrine (Figure 7N). Taken together, these results suggested that the natural product oxymatrine exhibits potent anti‐Brucella activity by interfering with the purine nucleotide biosynthetic pathway through inhibition of hIMPDH2 catalytic activity.

DISCUSSION

Brucellosis is a common zoonotic disease that is still endemic in the Middle East, Central and South America, and northern China (Jenner et al., 2009). In this study, we have unravelled the proteomic and metabolomic atlas of persistent Brucella infection, that sheds light to open questions in Brucella–host interactions and uncovers bacterial‐induced perturbations of host cell circuits driving pathogenesis and disease progression. We collected metabolomics data of the infected macrophages, integrated them in a genome‐wide reaction pair network, and identified metabolic subnetworks in host cells and Brucella that are modularly regulated during infection. The up‐ and down‐regulation of these metabolic networks suggests that pathogens utilize a wide range of host‐derived compounds, leading to the recognition of infection‐specific metabolic alterations. Using this efficient and reproducible infection model, we have for the first time mapped and quantified the entire proteome and metabolome during the acute and chronic phases of infection. Using this atlas, we have validated pathways and metabolic signatures associated with brucellosis with a depth and understanding unparalleled to date, and correlated them with a cancer‐like metabolic signature, namely Warburg‐like transformations in infected cells. For certain depleted host metabolites, Brucella activates specific metabolic pathways to make use of newly acquired substrates, such as nucleotides. In addition to changes in Brucella metabolism, we identified adaptations in host metabolism and found indications for Brucella manipulation of host metabolism, for example, by upregulating the de novo synthesis of purine nucleotides by the host during infection to meet the needs of bacterial replication. This study identified IMPDH2 as a host target for the control of Brucella infection, providing insight into the enigmatic biological activity of Brucella. Importantly, our findings revealed a previously unappreciated role of IMPDH2 in the proliferation of intracellular bacteria.

IMPDH, a key node in de novo guanine nucleotide biosynthesis, is an important enzyme associated with cancer, immunosuppression and antivirals, as rapidly proliferating cells rely heavily on this enzyme to provide nucleotides for various cellular processes (Xu et al., 2017). There are two isoforms of IMPDH, IMPDH1 and IMPDH2. Although these isoforms have very similar catalytic activities, they are differentially expressed in different cell types. IMPDH2 is the major isoform overexpressed in tumours and highly proliferative cells, but no inhibitor has been identified to date that is more selective for IMPDH2 than IMPDH1 (Plana‐Bonamaisó et al., 2020). Brucella lacks the enzymes of the de novo purine biosynthesis machinery and is therefore strictly dependent on the host supply. IMPDH2 activity was upregulated in B. abortus‐infected macrophages to efficiently maintain host nucleotide levels. Thus blocking IMPDH2 effectively reduces replication and progeny production in B. abortus, and although the purine salvage pathway may be sufficient to ensure the survival of host cells, as demonstrated by the viability of IMPDH2 knockout cells, the very high amount of nucleotides required for intracellular bacterial replication cannot be met without de novo synthesis. IMPDH2 likewise plays a particularly important role in the immune response, where T cell activation is dependent on increased purine nucleotide production and is associated with filament assembly of IMPDH2. A growing number of studies have shown that the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is involved in regulating cellular events such as cell growth, adhesion and proliferation (Huang et al., 2017; Prossomariti et al., 2020). Interestingly, we found that KEGG_MTOR_SIGNALLING_PATHWAY was significantly enriched in B. abortus‐infected cells with high expression of IMPDH2. These findings drove us to hypothesize that IMPDH2 might promote cell cycle transition by targeting mTOR to regulate the expression levels of cell cycle regulators. Thus, our data support that activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway was required for B. abortus invasion and replication.

However, our experiment also has some flaws. MMF is an immunosuppressant, and its therapeutic effects in mice may arise not only indirectly from a reduction in B. abortus burden but also directly from the anti‐inflammatory effects of MMF, for example, by reducing rapidly proliferating B and T lymphocytes. The extent to which the immunosuppressive effect of MMF can contribute to the control of Brucella infection is uncertain. So the next step in our study is to determine whether the immunomodulatory effect of MMF reduces its potential value against Brucella infection. Indeed, reduced lymphocyte expansion has also been shown to be beneficial in mouse models of sepsis (Górski et al., 2020). Also, MMF supports bacterial clearance when combined with antibiotic therapy (Malekinejad et al., 2012). Thus, our study provides, to some extent, proof of concept for the effectiveness of host‐targeted approaches against Brucella infection. In the future, it is necessary to improve and optimize the current formulation of MMF and antibiotics in order to improve the in vivo efficacy against Brucella.

In conclusion, our results provide convincing evidence that IMPDH2 is a host‐specific target for the control of Brucella infection. Finally, the multi‐omics atlas of B. abortus infection is a useful and unique resource dataset for researchers to validate individual hypotheses in Brucella–host interactions and brucellosis biology. our findings could potentially help to identify the pathogen's “metabolic Achilles heel” for future therapeutic intervention measures.

Although bioactive molecules of plant origins have been utilized for various purposes, the application of anti‐Brucella activity has been ignored for a long time. Medicinal plants have provided fertile ground for the discovery of modern medicines. Nonetheless, these plant‐derived extracts remain a poorly characterized reservoir of anti‐infective agents (Tsou et al., 2016). In this work, we identified several novel inhibitors of IMPDH2 from a library of natural products. Of these, oxymatrine is the most effective one, with high selectivity for IMPDH2. The selective interaction of oxymatrine on IMPDH2 was revealed using biophysical and biochemical methods. Collectively, our findings showed that the natural product oxymatrine is a selective inhibitor of IMPDH2 with anti‐Brucella activity and the most promising candidate for the development of novel antibiotic drugs against Brucella‐associated infections. Furthermore, we believe that the availability of inhibitors will stimulate the scientific community's interest in this area in an attempt to eliminate the deadly Brucella from the population.
