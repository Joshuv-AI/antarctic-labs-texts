Long-term Patency of Retrograde Bypass Using a Distal Stump of the Parietal Superficial Temporal Artery for Moyamoya Disease
Authors: Takeshi SHIMIZU, Shingo TOYOTA, Motohide TAKAHARA, Kazuhiro TOUHARA, Tatsuya HAGIOKA, Yuhei HOSHIKUMA, Takamune ACHIHA, Tomoaki MURAKAMI, Maki KOBAYASHI, Haruhiko KISHIMA
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
There have been a number of anastomosis methods of bypass techniques reported for moyamoya disease. However, there are yet no randomized controlled trials conducted on the anastomosis method. Retrograde blood flow of the superficial temporal artery (STA) may be used as one of the donor options. Here, we examined the tolerability of retrograde bypass using a distal stump of the parietal STA (dsPSTA). Anastomosis between the dsPSTA and middle cerebral artery (MCA) was performed for consecutive patients with moyamoya disease whose parietal STA was visualized to be longer than 10 cm using contrast-enhanced computed tomography preoperatively. Retrospectively, we have examined its patency and clinical outcome. Retrograde dsPSTA-MCA bypass was performed in 22 hemispheres of 17 patients. The patency of retrograde dsPSTA-MCA bypass in all 22 anastomoses could be confirmed during follow-up periods (mean: 5.5, range: 2-15 years). No recurrence of ischemic events was observed. The dsPSTA-MCA bypass using retrograde blood flow has been determined as one of the many promising anastomosis methods, and long-term patency was achieved in moyamoya disease.

Introduction

Moyamoya disease, as first defined by Suzuki and Takaku in 1969,1) is as a rare, progressive cerebrovascular disorder that is characterized by an abnormal vascular network and stenosis or occlusion of the terminal internal carotid arteries. Since Crowell and Yasargil first reported a case of superficial temporal artery (STA)-middle cerebral artery (MCA) bypass in 1969,2) it has been widely performed to prevent recurrent ischemic stroke in patients with not only steno-occlusive disease but also with moyamoya disease.3-7) STA-MCA bypass for patients with moyamoya disease has been found to reduce the risk of stroke, improve cerebral hemodynamics, and achieve long-term higher brain function and postoperative ADL.4,8,9)

Various anastomosis methods of bypass techniques have already been reported. The end-to-side anastomosis of M4 and the parietal branch of STA is known to be a common low-flow bypass, but double STA-MCA bypass using both STA branches as donors may be performed to augment blood supply.5) Retrograde blood flow of STA may be used as one of the donor options.10,11) For each case, the anastomosis method must be examined, as preoperative anatomical conditions and hemodynamics differ from patient to patient. However, there are yet no randomized controlled trials conducted on the anastomosis method. Bypass using the retrograde flow of the distal stump of the parietal STA (retrograde dsPSTA-MCA bypass) has been used in our institution for >15 years as one of the donor options to achieve abundant bypass flow. In this study, we aim to examine the tolerability of this retrograde bypass.

Materials and Methods

Participants

All patients diagnosed with moyamoya disease via magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan and angiography following ischemic events (transient ischemic attack or cerebral infarction) were included in this study. After receiving antiplatelet therapy, multiple STA-MCA anastomoses, including retrograde dsPSTA-MCA bypass, were performed in consecutive patients with moyamoya disease whose STA from the level of zygomatic bone to the distal end of parietal branch was visualized to be longer than 10 cm using contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) from December 2004 to June 2020. This study was conducted following the recommendations of the Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided their written informed consent.

Surgical procedure

A curved-linear skin incision of approximately 12 cm was made along the course of the PSTA. The underlying subcutaneous tissue was dissected using high-power bipolar cautery with cutting mode to expose the STA for approximately 10 cm (Fig. 1A, B). The frontal branch was additionally dissected beneath the frontal skin flap for approximately 4 cm and was then cut. This was covered with cottonoid and soaked with papaverine hydrochloride and was posteriorly retracted. The underlying galea aponeurotica and temporal muscle are subsequently cut using a monopolar probe, exposing the skull. An oval-shaped craniotomy of approximately 6 × 8 cm was performed. The dura was opened in a cruciform fashion to avoid the major branch of the middle meningeal artery, which may be functioning as an already existing collateral. After determining the recipient artery for the anastomosis on the brain surface, the PSTA was clamped and cut (Fig. 1A, C). Care should be taken that enough length of the distal STA is saved, as this often becomes too short, making subsequent procedure cumbersome. In most cases, the proximal stump of the frontal STA was anastomosed to the temporal artery, whereas the proximal stump of the PSTA was anastomosed to the precentral or central artery. Meanwhile, the dsPSTA was anastomosed to the central or angular artery (Fig. 1D, E). The graft patency and retrograde blood flow from dsPSTA were intraoperatively confirmed using micro-Doppler probe and impedance cardiography (Fig. 1F).

Postoperative assessment

MRI images, single-photon emission CT scan, and CECT scan were performed on all patients on the day postoperatively to evaluate the perioperative complication and the patency of anastomosis, including retrograde bypass using dsPSTA. Any neurological sign that was consistent with the image findings taken via MRI and SPECT and left/right discrepancy measured via in vivo optical spectroscopy 5100C (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was considered symptomatic infarction or hyperperfusion syndrome. MRI was performed at least every year for outpatient follow-up, and CECT or angiography was performed as necessary to confirm the patency of the anastomosis. All patients were followed up for at least 2 years.

Results

The clinical data of patients are summarized in Table 1. Retrograde dsPSTA-MCA bypass was performed in 22 hemispheres of 17 adult patients with moyamoya disease (13 women and 4 men, age: 10-79 years, mean age: 42.5 years). The patency of retrograde dsPSTA-MCA bypass could be confirmed intraoperatively in all 22 anastomoses. No perioperative complication, except for transient aphasia in two patients due to hyperperfusion syndrome, was observed. The mean follow-up period was 5.5 years (range: 2-15 years). No recurrence of ischemic or hemorrhagic events in the operated site was reported. CECT that was taken the day postoperatively has confirmed the patency of retrograde bypass in all cases (Fig. 2). Angiography 6 months after surgery has confirmed the development of retrograde blood flow of dsPSTA via collateral circulation from the occipital artery (OA), which supplies blood flow to the frontal lobe (Fig. 3). CECT, which is performed at least once a year, has confirmed that all the retrograde dsPSTA-MCA anastomoses were patent during the follow-up periods (mean: 5.5 years).

Discussion

In this present study, we have highlighted the tolerability of anastomosis. Results confirm the long-term patency of retrograde dsPSTA-MCA bypass in patients with moyamoya disease.

However, the long-term patency of the STA-MCA bypass for moyamoya disease has rarely been discussed. In general, bypass patency has been considered high in moyamoya disease. In fact, Kraemer et al. reported a good long-term outcome of STA-MCA bypass after a mean follow-up of 38.2 months.6) In this study, all anastomoses were patent 3 months postoperatively, and no patient experienced any postoperative symptoms related to stroke or hemorrhages during the follow-up periods. Kan et al. have also reported the good patency of double-barrel STA-MCA bypass for 54 cases, including 28 patients with moyamoya disease, with a mean follow-up time of 22.1 months.5) The antegrade bypass is deemed useful, but comprehensive reports on the usefulness of our introduced retrograde bypass remain unavailable. Therefore, this study is significant because it shows the long-term patency of retrograde bypass after a mean follow-up of 5.5 years. Patients with the longest follow-up period have been confirmed to preserve retrograde bypass for 15 years. Long-term patency of this low-flow bypass may be primarily attributed to the high demand of the recipient that supplies the eloquent area, such as the primary motor cortex.

The anatomical rationale of this present technique is the prominent collaterals between the STA and OA.11,12) Additionally, the distal stump of certain peripheral arteries is known to be supplied with sufficient collateral flow if a vascular network exists between adjacent arteries.13) Our experience revealed the development of the collateral circulation from OA to dsPSTA, which then becomes apparent as a source of blood flow over time. Angiography 6 months after the surgery shows that the time required for retrograde blood flow to reach the brain is slightly delayed as compared with the anterograde bypass, but retrograde bypass provides abundant blood flow (Fig. 4). Thus, this bypass often improved bilateral perfusion even in patients with bilateral misery perfusion, as can be confirmed by SPECT at 1 year after surgery. Therefore, this retrograde bypass can contribute to good long-term outcomes even in moyamoya disease, which is considered progressive. Neurological deficits related to this bypass were not observed, and transient hyperperfusion syndrome in 2 of 22 cases disappeared within a few days after strict blood pressure control. Hence, retrograde bypass is considered safe.

However, our result does not confirm whether retrograde bypass can be applied to atherosclerotic disease. In a study by Matano et al., a decreased bypass patency in 7.1% of patients with atherosclerotic disease was reported within a mean follow-up period of 24.7 months.7) Koizumi et al. reported that the signal intensity of the donor artery of an established STA-MCA bypass decreases within 1 year postoperatively in 18% of patients with atherosclerotic steno-occlusive disease.3) Therefore, the patency rate of bypass in moyamoya disease is expected to be higher than that in atherosclerotic disease. Additionally, the possibility of indirect revascularization support cannot be ruled out in the case series of moyamoya disease. Thus, our results must be carefully examined and not be generalized in patients with atherosclerotic disease.

Notably, this retrograde bypass was used in combination with antegrade bypass in all patients. Thus, we could not determine the efficacy of retrograde dsPSTA-MCA bypass as single direct revascularization. Then, it should be regarded as one of the options as rescue bypass when standard bypass is difficult because of anatomical reasons or when the additional anastomosis is required because of unexpected problems, such as when the planned bypass is occluded during surgery and cannot be reconstructed. The fact that even early postoperative angiography showed abundant blood flow from this retrograde bypass suggests that this bypass may make a useful contribution to cerebral perfusion (Fig. 3). However, postoperative angiography was performed in only one case, which could be a limitation; thus, further validation is needed to determine the applicability of this interpretation to all the cases. Conversely, the retrograde flow from the dsPSTA is thought to be a potentially significant alternate donor in practical clinical application.10,14,15) We have previously reported that approximately 80% of the systemic pressure is the mean arterial blood pressure of the dsPSTA.11) Nagm et al. reported enough cut-flow and anastomosis flow with an angiographically demonstrable retrograde filling of the distal side of PSTA.14) Considering these findings, a preoperative check of retrograde filling of the distal side of PSTA by external cerebral angiography manually compressing the proximal side of PSTA may be useful to predict the efficacy of the retrograde bypass. Angiography using this manual compression may be also useful postoperatively. The effect of this bypass can be estimated if the target vessel is manually compressed, considering the OA, contralateral STA, or posterior auricular artery as a donor for additional bypass after retrograde bypass.

In conclusion, the dsPSTA-MCA bypass using retrograde blood flow is useful as one of the various anastomosis methods, wherein it achieved long-term patency in moyamoya disease.
