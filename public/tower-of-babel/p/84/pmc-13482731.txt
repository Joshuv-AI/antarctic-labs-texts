Murine models of pulmonary fibrosis: mechanisms, limitations and translational insights
Authors: Karen F. Lou, McKenzie M. Fulcer, Mary J. Lotesto, Jennifer M. Davis, Tatiana P. Ortiz Serrano, Ruihua Ma, Karen M. Ridge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic, progressive, irreversible interstitial lung disease characterised by excessive collagen deposition, aberrant tissue remodelling, and impaired gas exchange, ultimately leading to respiratory failure and death. IPF carries a poor prognosis, with limited therapeutic options; currently approved treatments slow disease progression but do not reverse established fibrosis, and lung transplantation remains the only definitive therapy. Moreover, the prevalence and incidence of IPF continue to rise worldwide. These challenges underscore the urgent need to better understand the pathological mechanisms driving IPF, enabling the development of more effective therapeutic strategies. Robust and appropriate animal models are essential for investigating disease pathogenesis and therapeutic response. Although multiple murine models of pulmonary fibrosis are widely used, no single model fully recapitulates the clinical, pathological and temporal features of human IPF. In this review, we systematically summarise commonly used and new murine models of pulmonary fibrosis, emphasising induction methods, technical requirements, the time course of fibrosis development and persistence of fibrosis. Additionally, we highlight the advantages and limitations of each model, discuss how they inform mechanistic and translational studies, and provide practical guidance for selecting appropriate experimental systems based on specific scientific questions, with the goal of improving rigor and relevance in preclinical pulmonary fibrosis research.

Introduction

Human pulmonary fibrosis is a chronic, progressive, often fatal lung disease [1–3]. The most common form is idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), defined as pulmonary fibrosis of unknown aetiology [4]. It arises from repetitive injury to the alveolar epithelium, leading to type II alveolar epithelial (AT2) cell hyperplasia and accumulation of immune cells, fibroblasts, myofibroblasts and excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) [5, 6]. These changes drive progressive lung scarring, irreversible ECM deposition, alveolar architecture destruction, and ultimately respiratory failure. Functionally, disease progression manifests as declining forced vital capacity and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO), reflecting impaired gas exchange [7]. Clinically, patients typically present with progressive dyspnoea, chronic cough and bibasilar crackles. High-resolution computed tomography reveals reticular opacities, honeycombing and distorted airway architecture, consistent with advanced fibrotic remodelling [4, 8]. Incidence continues to rise [1, 9–12]. Despite antifibrotic therapies and improved clinical management, median survival remains only 3–5 years after diagnosis [13, 14].

Several risk factors increase susceptibility to pulmonary fibrosis, including advanced age, genetic predisposition, cigarette smoking and occupational or environmental exposures [15]. Pulmonary fibrosis is usually diagnosed at advanced stages, when fibrotic lesions are already well established; early pathological events are difficult to capture. Emerging evidence from post-COVID-19 pulmonary fibrosis [16–19] suggests an inflammatory phase preceding overt fibrotic remodelling. Consistently, IPF lungs exhibit mild but persistent inflammation, characterised by patchy infiltration of lymphocytes and plasma cells within the interstitium [4]. These observations support a contributory role for inflammation in both the initiation and progression of fibrotic lung disease.

Current treatment options remain limited, with only three US Food and Drug Administration-approved therapies, namely pirfenidone, nintedanib and nerandomilast [20–23], which slow disease progression but do not reverse established fibrosis. Lung transplantation remains the only curative treatment.

Given the incomplete understanding of IPF pathogenesis and the paucity of therapies, robust animal models recapitulating the clinical features and progressive and irreversible nature of human IPF are essential. These models enable mechanistic studies of disease initiation and progression, identification of therapeutic targets and preclinical evaluation of therapies and biomarkers for early diagnosis. In this review, we critically evaluate currently available murine models of pulmonary fibrosis, highlighting defining features, strengths, limitations and translational relevance to human disease.

Methods

We employed a structured search of PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) to ensure comprehensive literature coverage. Search terms included “pulmonary fibrosis”, “idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis”, “mouse model”, “murine”, “bleomycin”, “asbestos”, “silica”, “radiation”, “cGAMP”, “SAVI”, “cigarette smoke”, “virus”, “TGF-b1 overexpression”, “Sftpc”, “Sftpa1”, “Trf1” and related terms. Reference lists of landmark articles, reviews and major clinical guidelines were manually screened to identify influential studies not captured in the primary search. Only English-language publications were included. Priority was given to studies published 2020–2026, including foundational studies, recent advances and evidence addressing the gap between murine experimental findings and clinical outcomes in human pulmonary fibrosis.

Murine models for pulmonary fibrosis

Experimental murine models are essential for dissecting the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying pulmonary fibrosis, identifying therapeutic targets and evaluating candidate interventions. Fibrotic burden is commonly assessed by histological analysis of lung collagen deposition using Masson's trichrome and/or picrosirius red staining, together with quantitative measurement of total collagen content using the Sircol collagen assay. Lung mechanics are typically evaluated using the flexiVent system to measure parameters such as lung compliance and airway resistance. However, DLCO, a standard clinical indicator of gas exchange in humans, is rarely measured in mice because of technical limitations. The small lung volume of mice leads to a low signal-to-noise ratio and their rapid respiratory rate precludes the controlled breath-hold manoeuvre required for conventional DLCO measurement. These limitations increase variability and reduce reliability, making it difficult to determine whether histologic fibrosis is accompanied by physiologically meaningful impairment. As a result, murine models may not fully capture the functional burden of disease observed in patients, limiting translational interpretation. A recent study demonstrated the utility of longitudinal micro-computed tomography for tracking the temporal progression of pulmonary fibrosis in mice [24]. This approach enables nonterminal monitoring of disease progression and therapeutic response in the same animals over time. Integrating longitudinal data from both patient-based clinical studies and translational murine models may facilitate the development of more informative experimental systems and help identify potential drivers for pulmonary fibrosis progression. However, limited availability of specialised instrumentation and the technical demands of repeated long-term monitoring likely limit the utilisation of these approaches.

Murine models of pulmonary fibrosis can be broadly classified by induction method, including chemical or biological agents, radiation exposure, viral infection and genetic modifications (figure 1). Each model recapitulates select features of human disease, such as epithelial injury, immune cell infiltration, fibroblast activation and excessive ECM deposition, while also presenting distinct limitations. Importantly, the severity and reproducibility of fibrosis are influenced by experimental variables, including mouse strain, sex and age. A clear understanding of the strengths, limitations and context-dependent variables of each model is therefore critical for selecting the most appropriate system to address specific mechanistic or translational questions. In the following sections, we provide a comprehensive overview of commonly used murine models of pulmonary fibrosis, emphasising their pathological characteristics, experimental utility and relevance to human IPF.

Chemical or biological agent-induced models

Bleomycin-induced model

The bleomycin-induced model is one of the most widely used and clinically relevant experimental models for pulmonary fibrosis. Bleomycin is a water-soluble glycopeptide derived from an antibiotic complex produced by Streptomyces verticillus [25]. It is used clinically as a chemotherapeutic agent [26], but its pulmonary toxicity limits use, as it can cause interstitial pneumonia and fibrosis in some patients [27, 28]. Consistent with these clinical observations, bleomycin induces pulmonary fibrosis in multiple species, including dogs [29], rats [30] and mice [31–33].

In mice, pulmonary fibrosis can be induced via several routes of bleomycin administration, including intratracheal (i.t.) instillation (1.5 mg·kg−1=1.5 U·kg−1 or 0.025–0.07 mg=0.025–0.07 U per mouse) [33–35], intravenous injection (120 mg·kg−1=120 U·kg−1) [36] or intraperitoneal injection (35 mg·kg−1=35 U·kg−1, twice a week for 4 weeks) [37, 38], with i.t. delivery being the most commonly employed method. A single i.t. dose is sufficient to induce fibrosis, although disease severity varies substantially by strain. C57BL/6 mice are more susceptible than DBA/2, Swiss or BALB/c strains [39]. Sex-dependent differences have also been observed; male C57BL/6 mice develop greater lung function impairment than females after bleomycin treatment [40], mirroring sex-related differences observed in IPF patients [41–43]. Mechanistically, bleomycin injures the pulmonary endothelium and induces type I alveolar epithelial cell (AT1) death [44, 45], initiating sustained immune cell-mediated inflammation that drives fibrotic remodelling [5, 33, 34, 44].

The bleomycin model reproduces key temporal phases of fibrotic lung disease, including an early injury and inflammation phase (days 0–7) characterised by injured alveolar epithelial cells (AECs) and immune cell recruitment, followed by a fibroproliferative phase (days 14–28) characterised by progressive accumulation of fibrotic lesions [33, 34, 46]. However, single-dose bleomycin does not fully capture the progressive, irreversible nature of IPF, as fibrosis largely resolves by ∼6 weeks post-instillation [32, 47].

To better model the persistent and progressive features of IPF, repetitive bleomycin dosing regimens have been developed [31, 32]. These include models using three i.t. doses of bleomycin (1 mg·kg−1=1 U·kg−1, every other week) [32] or extended protocols employing up to eight i.t. doses of bleomycin (1 mg·kg−1=1 U·kg−1 or 0.04 mg=0.04 U per mouse, every other week) [31, 32]. In these repetitive-dose models, fibrosis becomes evident ∼2 weeks after the final dose, with increased lung collagen content, reduced lung compliance and elevated bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) cell counts compared with controls. Importantly, repetitive dosing also prolongs fibrosis remodelling, with fibrotic lesions remaining detectable until week 24 after the initial dose, and more closely recapitulates the aberrant epithelial repair and persistent injury responses characteristic of human IPF [31, 32].

In summary, the bleomycin model offers the following key advantages: 1) strong clinical relevance to human disease, 2) relatively simple and low-risk experimental protocol, 3) rapid fibrosis onset, and 4) recapitulation of key pathological features of IPF, including epithelial injury, fibroblast accumulation and lung function impairment (table 1). Nevertheless, significant limitations remain, including 1) substantial inter-animal variability in fibrosis severity at identical doses, 2) nonuniform distribution of fibrotic lesions across lung lobes and 3) reversibility in single-dose bleomycin protocols, which contrasts with the progressive and irreversible course of IPF in patients (table 1).

Asbestos-induced model

The asbestos-induced pulmonary fibrosis model closely recapitulates human asbestosis, a fibrotic lung disease caused by chronic occupational exposure to asbestos fibres [48, 49]. Asbestos is a naturally occurring, highly heat-resistant mineral [50]. Once deposited in the lung, asbestos fibres persist for prolonged periods, inducing AEC injury, oxidative stress and sustained recruitment of alveolar macrophages, leading to chronic inflammation [33, 51–53] (figure 2). Continued release of inflammatory and profibrotic mediators drives progressive ECM deposition and development of asbestosis [51].

Epithelium injury, persistent inflammation, fibroblast activation and excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition drive the development of pulmonary fibrosis. External insults, including exposure to bleomycin, asbestos, silica, cigarette smoke, radiation and viral infection, as well as intrinsic factors such as increased endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and telomere shortening in epithelial cells, increase the susceptibility of pulmonary epithelium to injury. Following epithelial damage, injured epithelial cells secrete chemokines, such as C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), which promote the recruitment of immune cells. In response, macrophages, lymphocytes, and neutrophils infiltrate the lung and initiate inflammation responses. Persistent inflammation leads to repetitive micro-injury to the epithelium, further amplifying tissue damage. In parallel, damaged epithelial cells produce pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), which trigger fibroblast activation and enhance ECM production. Infiltrating immune cells additionally contribute to the profibrotic milieu by producing mediators such as transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), further stimulating fibroblast activation and ECM deposition. Ultimately, excessive accumulation of ECM by activated fibroblasts leads to the formation of fibrotic lesions in the lung. AT1: type I alveolar epithelial cell; AT2: type II alveolar epithelial cell; cGAS-STING: cyclic guanosine monophosphate–adenosine monophosphate synthase–stimulator of interferon genes.

In mice, pulmonary fibrosis is induced by asbestos exposure through inhalation [54] or i.t. instillation [33, 52]. Lung collagen accumulation becomes evident by ∼4 weeks after asbestos administration [33, 55]; the resulting fibrosis is irreversible. Genetic background significantly influences disease susceptibility, with C57BL/6 mice being more susceptible than 129J mice [56].

A distinguishing feature of the asbestos model is the persistent recruitment of monocyte-derived alveolar macrophages [34]. Unlike the single-dose bleomycin model, in which macrophage recruitment is largely restricted to the first 7 days and resolves by day ∼21, macrophages continue to accumulate after asbestos exposure [34]. This sustained macrophage accumulation is thought to drive chronic inflammation and fibrotic remodelling [52, 57, 58].

Overall, the asbestos-induced model offers several important advantages, including 1) strong translational relevance to human asbestosis, 2) relatively rapid fibrosis development within ∼4 weeks and 3) faithful recapitulation of repetitive epithelial injury, chronic inflammation and the persistent, progressive disease observed in patients [59] (table 1). However, use of asbestos necessitates strict regulatory oversight, specialised containment facilities and comprehensive personal protective equipment, which may limit use of this model (table 1).

Silica-induced model

The silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis model recapitulates key pathological features of silicosis, a lung disease commonly caused by chronic occupational exposure to silica particles [60]. Silica consists of silicon dioxide [60] and, upon inhalation, deposits in the distal lung where it is phagocytosed by alveolar macrophages [61, 62]. This phagocytic uptake triggers robust inflammatory responses. In parallel, deposited silica particles directly injure surrounding lung cells, including AECs [60], leading to the release of intracellular contents that further amplify inflammatory signalling (figure 2). In murine models, silica-induced lung injury activates the cyclic guanosine monophosphate–adenosine monophosphate synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway in response to double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) released from damaged cells [63, 64]. Sustained production of inflammatory and profibrotic mediators subsequently activates fibroblasts, promotes collagen deposition around silica particles and drives progressive pulmonary fibrosis [61, 65, 66].

In mice, silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis can be established by intranasal [67], i.t. [68] or transoral/oropharyngeal administration [69, 70]. Acute inflammation develops within 1–2 weeks, followed by chronic lung inflammation and progressive ECM accumulation. Collagen deposition begins as early as 1 week after treatment and continues to increase over time. Importantly, silica-induced fibrosis in mice is progressive and irreversible, closely mirroring the pathological course observed in patients with silicosis [66]. Susceptibility varies by genetic background: DBA/2, New Zealand Black, MRL/MpJ and C57BL/6 mice are more susceptible, whereas C3H/HeN, BALB/c and CBA/J mice are relatively resistant [70, 71].

Most studies use a single administration of silica particles to induce silicosis [68–70]. To better model repetitive occupational exposure in humans, repeated inhalation protocols have also been developed [67]. In one such model, mice receive daily nasal instillations of silica (20 mg·mL−1, 80 µL) for 16 consecutive days [67], resulting in sustained inflammation and progressive fibrotic remodelling.

Overall, the silica-induced model demonstrates strong translational relevance by recapitulating chronic epithelial injury and inflammation, and progressive, irreversible fibrotic lung remodelling (table 1). Limitations include slower fibrosis development than many other models and the need for specialised protective equipment and strict safety procedures because of the hazardous nature of silica (table 1).

Cigarette smoke-induced model

Cigarette smoking is a well-established risk factor associated with the pathogenesis of pulmonary fibrosis [15]. Cigarette smoke-only models have primarily been used to investigate COPD-associated airway remodelling and fibrosis. In vitro, exposure of lung fibroblasts to 5% cigarette smoke extract (CSE) increases ECM production, including fibronectin and perlecan, particularly in fibroblasts derived from patients with COPD [72]. These findings support a mechanistic link between cigarette smoke exposure and fibrotic remodelling.

In vivo, studies have further examined the fibrogenic effects of cigarette smoke exposure. Investigators exposed C57BL/6J mice to smoke from 4–20 cigarettes intranasally for 6 months, 5–7 days per week, resulting in smoke-induced small airway remodelling and fibrosis [73, 74]. Mechanistically, cigarette smoke promotes fibroblast activation, proliferation, and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT). However, cigarette smoke alone typically causes COPD-related airway fibrosis rather than alveolar fibrosis and therefore does not fully recapitulate the pathology of IPF.

To better mimic the complex clinical setting, cigarette smoke exposure has been incorporated into “two-hit” pulmonary fibrosis models [75–77]. In a combined pulmonary fibrosis and emphysema model, multidose bleomycin was administered together with intranasal CSE [75]. Compared with bleomycin alone, the combination increased inflammatory cells and interleukin 1β (IL-1β) in BAL fluid and produced more severe airway emphysema. In another study, cigarette smoke exposure following repeated intraperitoneal bleomycin administration significantly increased lung collagen deposition compared with either treatment alone, likely through enhanced transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) signalling [77]. In contrast, pre-exposure to cigarette smoke was reported to attenuate bleomycin-induced fibrosis by altering lung macrophage subpopulations [78]. These findings suggest that the timing of cigarette smoke exposure relative to bleomycin treatment can substantially influence outcomes.

Another two-hit model combines cigarette smoke with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) treatment [76]. In this model, cigarette smoke exposure followed by repeated i.t. LPS administration resulted in greater collagen deposition than either treatment alone [76].

The cigarette smoke-based two-hit models better reflect the clinical context and are useful for studying how environmental exposures modify fibrosis progression (table 1). In contrast, the cigarette smoke-only model is more suitable for modelling COPD-associated airway fibrosis and requires prolonged exposure, which limits its utility for studying alveolar fibrosis characteristic of IPF (table 1).

Radiation exposure-induced model

Pulmonary fibrosis is a clinically significant late complication of thoracic radiotherapy for lung cancer [79]. Radiation exposure is widely used to induce progressive pulmonary fibrosis in mice and provides a valuable model for investigating fibrosis pathogenic mechanisms and treatment-related toxicity.

To induce lung fibrosis, mice typically receive a single thoracic irradiation dose of 12–20 Gy [80, 81] at a dose rate of 0.5–2.0 Gy·min−1 [80–83]. Susceptibility varies by genetic background: C57BL/6 mice are highly prone to developing pulmonary fibrosis, whereas BALB/cJ and C3H/HeJ strains are relatively resistant [84]. Increased lung collagen deposition is detectable as early as 4 weeks after irradiation [81, 82] and can persist for up to 24 weeks following radiation exposure [81, 85].

Radiation-induced pulmonary fibrosis is characterised by increased DNA damage, cellular senescence and oxidative stress [80, 85]. Accumulation of DNA damage and reactive oxygen species initiates inflammatory responses that result in repetitive micro-injury to lung tissue, thereby driving fibrosis progression [80]. Radiation also activates the cGAS-STING pathway in macrophages in response to cytosolic dsDNA, promoting M1 macrophage polarisation [82]. In parallel, oxidative stress activates the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 inflammasome in lung epithelial cells, increasing IL-1β production, which contributes to fibrosis by inducing fibroblast activation and proliferation [86].

The radiation-induced model offers several important advantages, including 1) relatively rapid fibrosis development, with detectable collagen deposition beginning ∼4 weeks after a single radiation dose, 2) faithful recapitulation of the progressive, irreversible nature of pulmonary fibrosis observed in patients, 3) suitability for studying DNA damage, oxidative stress and epithelial injury-related pathogenesis mechanisms of pulmonary fibrosis, and 4) strong clinical relevance, as pulmonary fibrosis is a well-recognised complication of thoracic radiotherapy (table 1). However, notable limitations of this model remain, including 1) the requirement for partial-body shielding to protect the head and abdomen to prevent off-target organ injury, and 2) the high cost of irradiator use and maintenance (table 1).

Viral infection-associated models

Viral infection represents a potential risk factor for IPF [15]. A meta-analysis of case–control studies demonstrates that chronic viral infection significantly increases the risk of developing IPF [87], supporting a contributory role for viral injury in fibrotic lung disease.

Influenza virus-induced fibrosis

Multiple studies demonstrate that influenza viruses can induce pulmonary fibrosis [88–90]. Yang et al. [89] showed that both influenza A virus (IAV) and influenza B virus (IBV) trigger fibrotic remodelling in BALB/c mice. Following intranasal delivery of 3000 plaque-forming units (PFU) of IAV or IBV, mice developed increased fibrotic foci at days 7 and 14 post-infection, with IAV inducing more severe fibrosis than IBV [89]. Mechanistically, both viruses activated TGF-β signalling and EMT in vivo and in vitro. Avian H5N1 influenza virus has also been used to model virus-induced pulmonary fibrosis [90]. BALB/c mice infected intranasally with 10 mouse infectious doses (MID50) of H5N1 reached peak lung viral titres at day 6 and completely cleared the virus by day 14. Despite viral clearance, lung histology and hydroxyproline measurements revealed increased fibrosis at day 30 post-infection, indicating that transient viral injury can initiate sustained fibrotic remodelling.

SARS-CoV-2-induced fibrosis

The COVID-19 pandemic has intensified interest in the long-term pulmonary sequelae of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection, including the development of pulmonary fibrosis [17–19]. Dinnon et al. [91] demonstrated that infection with a mouse-adapted SARS-CoV-2 virus (MA10) induces fibrotic lung disease in mice. In their study, 1-year-old BALB/c mice received 103 PFU of MA10, whereas 10-week-old BALB/c and 1-year-old C57BL/6J mice received 104 PFU intranasally. The infectious virus was cleared by day 15, with younger mice clearing the virus more rapidly than aged mice (day 7 versus day 15). Histopathological analysis revealed fibrotic lesions beginning at day 15 and persisting through day 120 post-infection. Age and strain influenced disease resolution, with approximately 40% of young BALB/c mice showed near-complete resolution by day 120, whereas aged mice exhibited persistent fibrosis. C57BL/6J mice were relatively resistant, with fibrotic lesions beginning to diminish by day 30 post-infection. Additional studies further support SARS-CoV-2-induced fibrogenesis. Infection of ∼1-year-old female BALB/c mice with 104 PFU of SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.5 variant increased fibrotic lesions at days 7 and 15 post-infection [92]. Using this model, Wu et al. [93] identified the TGF-β–CD147 signalling axis as a key driver of fibroblast activation and fibrosis following SARS-CoV-2 infection. Prior cigarette smoke exposure also exacerbated fibrosis compared with SARS-CoV-2 infection alone [94], highlighting the impact of environmental modifiers on post-viral outcomes. Given the heterogeneity and partial reversibility of fibrosis in this model, SARS-CoV-2 infection is well-suited for studying post-viral chronic lung sequelae but may be less optimal for investigating the mechanisms of stable, progressive pulmonary fibrosis.

γ-Herpesvirus-induced fibrosis

γ-Herpesvirus (γ-HV) infection provides another model of virus-driven pulmonary fibrosis. Mora et al. [95] demonstrated that intranasal infection with murine γ-HV 68 (MHV68) induces progressive pulmonary fibrosis in interferon-γ receptor-deficient (Ifngr1−/−) C57BL/6 mice. These mice exhibit a T-helper type 2 (Th2)-skewed immune response, consistent with the Th2-biased pulmonary phenotype observed in IPF patients [96, 97]. Following intranasal administration of 105 PFU of MHV68, both wild-type and Ifngr1−/− mice developed pulmonary inflammation; however, fibrosis developed only in Ifngr1−/− mice. Fibrotic lesions were detectable from day 15 to 180 post-infection, indicating chronic, persistent fibrosis. A major limitation of this model is its dependence on a specific genetic background, which may introduce confounding immunological variables.

Collectively, virus-induced fibrosis models demonstrate that pulmonary fibrosis can persist long after the infectious virus has been cleared, supporting the concept that viral injury can trigger long-term fibrotic lung remodelling (table 1).

Genetically modified models

Several gene variants have been linked to adult-onset familial pulmonary fibrosis, including surfactant protein C (SFTPC), surfactant protein A1 (SFTPA1) and regulator of telomere elongation helicase 1 (RTEL1) [98]. These discoveries have enabled the development of spontaneous murine pulmonary fibrosis models through genetic modifications.

TGF-β1 overexpression model

TGF-β is a pleiotropic cytokine produced by fibroblasts, monocytes, macrophages and other cell types [99, 100] that regulates cell growth, differentiation [101] and ECM synthesis [102] (figure 2). Elevated TGF-β levels are consistently observed in murine fibrosis models and in lungs from IPF patients [103–106], supporting the concept that TGF-β signalling is a common downstream pathway in pulmonary fibrosis and providing the rationale for TGF-β overexpression-based models [107].

Sime et al. [107] used an adenovirus vector to drive lung-specific expression of constitutively active porcine TGF-β1 in Sprague–Dawley rats. I.t. delivery of 109 PFU of TGF-β1-expressing adenovirus induced marked ECM deposition from day 14 to 65, but this approach lacks cell type specificity.

To address this limitation, investigators generated doxycycline-inducible transgenic C57BL/6 mice with epithelial cell-specific TGF-β1 overexpression [108–111]. Doxycycline increased Tgfb1 mRNA within 12 h and BAL TGF-β1 protein within 1 week. TGF-β1 level returned to baseline within 48 h after doxycycline withdrawal [109, 111]. Induction of epithelial-specific TGF-β1 overexpression triggered epithelial apoptosis and lung inflammation, followed by increased total lung collagen by day 10, which continued to rise for up to 2 months [111].

The TGF-β1 overexpression model offers several important advantages, including 1) rapid induction of pulmonary fibrosis that persists until approximately day 60, mimicking the chronic nature of human pulmonary fibrosis, 2) simple, noninvasive induction via doxycycline-containing drinking water (table 1), and 3) suitability for studying TGF-β signalling-induced fibroblast activation-related pathogenesis mechanisms of pulmonary fibrosis. However, this model also has notable limitations, including 1) exclusive activation of TGF-β signalling, which may overlook other critical drivers of pulmonary fibrosis pathogenesis, and 2) reliance on transgenic mice necessitating additional breeding steps, increasing experimental complexity and time (table 1).

Sftpc mutation-induced model

To model SFTPC-associated pulmonary fibrosis, investigators generated mice expressing the SftpcI73T mutation (isoleucine-to-threonine substitution at residue 73) selectively in AT2 cells following tamoxifen induction [112, 113]. 4–6 weeks after intraperitoneal tamoxifen administration, these mice developed spontaneous pulmonary fibrosis, with increased collagen accumulation and reduced static lung compliance. Mechanistically, SftpcI73T increased Tgfb1 and Ccl2 mRNA levels in AT2 cells before overt fibrosis, suggesting that mutant surfactant protein C promotes fibrogenesis through early cytokine and chemokine induction. This inflammatory milieu subsequently drives recruitment of monocytes, neutrophils and eosinophils [114]. Another widely used model involves the SftpcC121G mutation (cysteine-to-glycine substitution at residue 121) [115–117]. Tamoxifen-inducible SftpcC121G knockin mice develop increased collagen deposition and decreased static lung compliance within 4–7 weeks after tamoxifen administration [115, 116]. Notably, collagen accumulation at week 7 exceeds that at week 4, indicating persistent, progressive pulmonary fibrosis. Mechanistically, this mutation induces endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and sustained unfolded protein response activation in AT2 cells (figure 2), promoting profibrotic epithelial–mesenchymal crosstalk and accumulation of pathogenic fibroblasts expressing fibrosis-related genes [117].

Sftpa1 mutation–induced model

Mutations in SFTPA1 have been linked to pulmonary fibrosis in multiple patient cohorts [118–120]. To investigate the mechanisms, investigators generated mice carrying a tyrosine-to-histidine substitution at residue 208 (Sftpa1Y208H), mirroring a mutation identified in patients with pulmonary fibrosis [121]. The Sftpa1Y208H mice developed spontaneous, age-dependent pulmonary fibrosis; histological abnormalities, hydroxyproline levels, Col1a1 expression, and arterial oxygen tension were normal at 20 weeks but became significantly abnormal by 35 weeks, with increased mortality beginning around 40 weeks. Importantly, viral infection accelerated disease progression in this model. Infection of 20-week-old Sftpa1Y208H mice with IAV resulted in significantly increased collagen deposition at day 10 post-infection compared with controls, without differences in viral susceptibility. These findings suggest that the Sftpa1Y208H mutation in AT2 cells promotes fibrosis through heightened ER stress and necroptosis (figure 2), rendering the lung vulnerable to secondary environmental insults.

Telomerase-deficient models: AT2 cell-specific Trf1 deletion

A meta-analysis has shown that variants in telomere length regulatory genes are associated with increased susceptibility to pulmonary fibrosis, implicating telomere dysfunction in disease pathogenesis [122]. To establish causality, investigators generated mice with AT2 cell-specific deletion of telomere repeat binding factor 1 (Trf1) [123, 124]. As TRF1 is a key telomere shelterin protein, its deletion induces telomere dysfunction specifically in AT2 cells (figure 2). Following intraperitoneal tamoxifen administration every other day for 6 weeks, AT2 cell-specific Trf1-deficient mice developed significant lung fibrosis by week 16 post-induction. Notably, 42% of mice exhibited severe respiratory defects requiring euthanasia before week 8 [124], highlighting the high mortality risk associated with this model. A separate study using weekly intraperitoneal injections for 8 months similarly demonstrated severe pulmonary fibrosis in AT2 cell-specific Trf1-deficient mice compared with controls [123], supporting the model's ability to recapitulate chronic fibrosis. This model has been used to evaluate therapies such as lung cell transplantation [125] and to investigate the function of developmental transcription factors, including Sine oculis homeobox homolog 1 (SIX1), in fibrosis pathogenesis [126].

Overall, Sftpc mutations, Sftpa1 mutation, and telomerase-deficient models faithfully recapitulate fibrosis driven by IPF-associated genetic lesions, enabling mechanistic studies and evaluation of mutation-targeting therapies (table 1). They are particularly useful for dissecting epithelial cell dysfunction in fibrogenesis. However, because these mutations account for only a small proportion of pulmonary fibrosis cases, their generalisability to broader disease mechanisms is limited. In addition, the requirement for complex transgenic breeding increases experimental complexity and time (table 1).

cGAS-STING pathway aberrant activation-induced model

Several studies have demonstrated that cGAS-STING pathway activity is elevated during pulmonary fibrosis and persistent activation promotes fibrogenesis in multiple murine models [63, 127]. Gain-of-function mutations in STING, including N153S (asparagine-to-serine substitution at residue 153) and V154M (valine-to-methionine substitution at residue 154), cause STING-associated vasculopathy with onset in infancy (SAVI), a severe autoinflammatory disease in humans [128, 129]. Patients with SAVI can develop pulmonary fibrosis [129–131]. Consistently, transgenic mice heterozygous for the STING N153S or V154M mutations develop pulmonary inflammation, whereas fibrotic remodelling has been reported only in mice harbouring the V154M mutation [132]. Pharmacological and genetic inhibition of the cGAS-STING pathway attenuates experimental pulmonary fibrosis, further supporting this pathway as a potential therapeutic target [133, 134]. Together, these findings implicate aberrant cGAS-STING signalling in pulmonary fibrogenesis. However, because SAVI is a systematic autoinflammatory disease affecting multiple organs, and because SAVI mutant mice display reduced lifespan and increased mortality [132], the utility of these transgenic mice for dissecting general mechanisms of pulmonary fibrosis requires further evaluation.

Zhang et al. [127] recently developed a fibrosis model driven by cGAS-STING activation via direct i.t. administration of cyclic guanosine monophosphate–adenosine monophosphate (cGAMP), the second messenger that activates STING and its downstream signalling cascades, leading to production of type I interferons and other inflammatory cytokines [127, 135–138]. In this study, C57BL/6 mice received cGAMP (400 µg·kg−1, i.t.) every 7 days, resulting in significantly increased collagen deposition in the lung 21 days after the initial treatment. Mechanistically, cGAMP was proposed to induce fibrosis through a noncanonical cGAS-STING-PERK pathway, promoting damage-associated senescence by triggering translational reprogramming. This model provides a useful platform to investigate sustained cGAS-STING signalling and downstream effectors in lung fibrosis (table 1). However, its general applicability to broader fibrotic mechanisms requires further validation (table 1). In addition, because fibrosis was assessed at only a single time point, it remains unclear whether this model fully recapitulates the progressive, irreversible nature of human IPF (table 1).

Second-hit models of acute exacerbation

Acute exacerbation is an important clinical feature of IPF, associated with rapid respiratory deterioration and high mortality [139]. Accordingly, experimental models incorporating a secondary insult are valuable for studying mechanisms driving the abrupt worsening of fibrosis. Viral infection [140–142] or LPS [143–145] is commonly applied as a “second hit” to mimic acute exacerbation in murine fibrosis.

Vannella et al. [142] infected mice with 5×104 PFU of MHV68, followed by i.t. administration of fluorescein isothiocyanate or bleomycin at days 14, 21, 30, 45 or 70 after infection. Although MHV68 infection alone became latent by day 14 and did not induce fibrosis, combined infection and injury significantly increased collagen deposition compared with injury alone. Similarly, Qiu et al. [141] demonstrated that herpes simplex virus type 1 infection (5×104 PFU) administered 14 days after bleomycin (5.0 mg·kg−1=5.0 U·kg−1, i.t.) markedly increased fibrosis in C57BL/6 mice at days 21 and 28.

LPS has also been used as a secondary inflammatory trigger. Kimura et al. [144] administered bleomycin (1 mg·kg−1=1 U·kg−1, i.t.) on day 0, followed by LPS (0.5 mg·kg−1, i.t.) on day 7, resulting in increased fibrosis and inflammation compared with either insult alone. Jia et al. [145] used a modified regimen with bleomycin (2.5 mg·kg−1=2.5 U·kg−1, i.t.) on day 1, followed by three LPS doses (1 mg·kg−1, i.t.) on days 5, 7 and 9, leading to modestly increased fibrosis and elevated inflammatory markers (Il6, Tnfa and Il1b) by day 21.

Collectively, second-hit models incorporate infectious or inflammatory insults to better reflect the clinical context of acute exacerbation. They provide a useful framework to study triggers of rapid lung injury and accelerated fibrotic remodelling.

Conclusions

The murine models discussed in this review are grounded in clinical observations, reflecting a patient-anchored translational workflow, in which findings from human disease inform model development. Each model captures distinct aspects of pulmonary fibrosis pathogenesis, yet no single model fully recapitulates the complete spectrum of pathological features observed in patients, including disease persistence and spatial heterogeneity. Additionally, although cough has been associated with IPF progression [146], no currently available murine model reproduces this clinical feature. Consequently, careful alignment between the scientific question and model selection is essential (figure 3). For example, the single-dose bleomycin model is well suited for investigating the initial formation of fibrotic foci following epithelial injury but less appropriate for studying fibrosis progression or persistence because the resulting fibrosis is largely reversible (figure 3). In contrast, the TGF-β1 overexpression model enables investigation of fibrosis establishment and progression but does not recapitulate epithelial injury or injury-induced signalling events (figure 3).

The underlying scientific objective should therefore guide model selection. Models with a defined inflammatory phase, such as single-dose bleomycin or viral infection, are particularly valuable for studying early pathogenic transitions from inflammation to fibrosis and for identifying early biomarkers relevant to IPF diagnosis (figure 3). Conversely, models characterised by rapid and sustained fibrosis, including multidose bleomycin-, asbestos- and silica-induced models, are better suited for evaluating therapeutic efficacy (figure 3).

Mouse age represents another critical experimental variable. Because IPF typically develops in adulthood, adult mice (>12 weeks old) are appropriate for modelling disease onset. Given the strong associations between IPF and ageing (disease onset is rare before age 50 years in humans), the use of aged mice (>1 year old) further enhances clinical relevance. Studies have shown that aged mice (18–24 months old) developed more severe pulmonary fibrosis and delayed resolution following bleomycin or asbestos exposure compared with young adult mice (3–5 months old) [34, 147, 148]. In contrast, age may be less restrictive in genetic models, as disease-associated mutations can lead to earlier onset of pulmonary fibrosis [129–131].

Finally, the heterogeneous clinical course of IPF, ranging from acute exacerbation [139] to gradual progressive fibrosis, together with the diverse genetic and environmental backgrounds of affected patients, underscores the limitations of existing murine models. No current model fully captures the complexity of human disease. Investigators should therefore select the models that best address their specific scientific hypotheses while carefully considering experimental variables such as age, sex, strain and group size to maximise rigor, reproducibility and translational relevance.

Questions for future research

Can murine models be better aligned with human clinical end-points? Many studies rely on histology and collagen quantification, which only partially reflect human disease burden. Ideally, future studies would 1) incorporate lung function metrics, blood gas surrogates and longitudinal imaging and physiology monitoring, 2) develop cross-species biomarkers that translate from mouse to human (blood, BAL and/or imaging), and 3) align preclinical end-points with outcomes used in clinical trials. Emerging humanised mouse models, such as bleomycin-induced fibrosis in mice engrafted with human peripheral blood mononuclear cells [149], haematopoietic stem cells [149] or mesenchymal stem cells [150], may further improve translational relevance by enabling evaluation of human-specific therapies. In parallel, organoid systems represent an important complementary platform for mechanistic studies.

Which epithelial cell states drive persistent fibrotic remodelling and are they reversible? Multiple models highlight epithelial dysfunction, including ER stress, senescence, necroptosis and telomere dysfunction, in the development and progression of pulmonary fibrosis. In bleomycin-induced fibrosis, studies have reported expansion of keratin 8-high (KRT8high) transitional AECs [151], accumulation of hyperplastic and apoptotic AECs [31, 152] and increased AT2 cell senescence [153, 154]. In the SftpcC121G mutation model, Hoffman et al. [117] similarly identified expansion of KRT8+ transitional AECs that communicate with alveolar fibroblasts via TGF-β signalling. Comparative transcriptomic analyses indicate that bleomycin and TGF-β1 overexpression models share dysregulation of the ECM pathway but differ in injury-associated versus cell cycle programmes [154, 155]. Viral models also exhibit distinct epithelial responses, including impaired alveolar regeneration and altered transitional cell dynamics [156, 157]. However, the epithelial programmes that causally sustain fibrosis remain incompletely defined. Future studies would 1) define pathogenic epithelial cell states (e.g. transitional, stressed and senescent AT2 cell populations) across models, 2) test whether epithelial reprogramming or repair (e.g. integrated stress response modulation, metabolic rewiring or proteostasis restoration) can halt or reverse fibrosis in irreversible models, and 3) use lineage tracing and fate mapping to distinguish adaptive versus maladaptive epithelial responses.

What molecular events define the transition from epithelial injury and inflammation to irreversible fibrosis? Most murine models emphasise either early injury and inflammation (e.g. single-dose bleomycin, viral models) or established fibrosis (e.g. silica, asbestos, radiation), but few capture the transition point between these phases. Future studies would 1) use time-resolved, longitudinal analyses (transcriptomic, spatial, proteomic) across early, mid and late disease stages, 2) identify irreversibility checkpoints, including molecular or cellular states beyond which fibrosis no longer resolves, and 3) compare resolving (single-dose bleomycin, young post-viral) versus nonresolving models (silica, multidose bleomycin, genetic models) to define shared versus divergent pathways.
