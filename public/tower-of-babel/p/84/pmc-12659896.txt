Confocal laser scanning microscopy-based evaluation of penetration depth and image analysis of root canal wall debridement using ultrasonically activated heated sodium hypochlorite
Authors: Riddhi J. Kakodkar, Karan Y. Bhargava, Sanjyot Mulay, S. R. Srinidhi, Rahul Anand, Harsha Rajesh Nihalani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:The complex anatomy of the root canal system often limits the effectiveness of mechanical instrumentation alone, necessitating enhanced irrigation protocols for thorough disinfection.Aim:The aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of passive ultrasonic activation (PUI) with and without intracanal-heated sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) on dentinal tubule penetration and root canal wall debridement.Materials and Methods:Forty-eight extracted single-rooted human teeth were prepared and divided into three groups: conventional NaOCl irrigation, NaOCl with PUI, and intracanal-heated NaOCl with PUI. The penetration depth was assessed using confocal laser scanning microscopy, and canal cleanliness was evaluated histologically using hematoxylin and eosin staining based on standardized scoring criteria.Results:The group receiving intracanal-heated NaOCl with ultrasonic activation (Group III) showed significantly greater dentinal tubule penetration and cleaner canal walls across all root thirds compared to the other groups (Group I and Group II).Conclusion:Intracanal heating of NaOCl combined with passive ultrasonic activation significantly enhances irrigant efficacy in terms of penetration and debridement.

INTRODUCTION

Successful endodontic treatment requires complete chemomechanical debridement of vital or necrotic pulp tissue, infected dentin debris, and microorganisms.[1] The rationale of endodontic treatment is to identify the underlying causes and eliminate these factors. Complete root canal debridement is challenging due to the intricate anatomy, which includes lateral and accessory canals, fins, deltas, loops, and isthmi. These microanatomies are recalcitrant to mechanical instrumentation and can thus harbor remnant pulp tissue and infective microorganisms.[2] Mechanical instrumentation leaves at least 50% of the canal walls uninstrumented, which is why irrigation remains the primary method for disinfecting the complex anatomy of the root canal system. The most commonly employed endodontic irrigant is sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) owing to its tissue-dissolving and antibacterial properties. Several other factors that affect the efficiency of NaOCl are concentration, temperature, pH, volume, and contact time.[3]

The effectiveness of NaOCl may be further enhanced by heating the irrigant and employing various agitation techniques. Heating the irrigant can accelerate the chemical reaction and enhance tissue dissolution. Heated NaOCl provides the same benefits as higher concentrations, thereby reducing the risks associated with using more concentrated hypochlorite.[4] Govindaraju L. et al. demonstrated that extraoral heating is ineffective, as the irrigant loses the elevated temperature once introduced into the canal and attains equilibrium immediately.[5]

Even though NaOCl significantly lowers the quantity of microbes in the outermost layers of root dentin through conventional needle irrigation (CNI), bacteria that are more firmly established in dentinal tubules frequently remain unharmed. Using a variety of agitation strategies has helped overcome the previously mentioned limitations of CNI. Currently, some of the most popular and researched techniques are manual dynamic activation, Passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI), and sonic irrigation.[67] Passive ultrasonic irrigation (PUI) has demonstrated efficacy in eliminating organic tissue remnants, planktonic bacteria, biofilm-embedded microorganisms, and dentinal debris from the root canal system, thereby optimizing canal cleanliness and potentially enhancing endodontic treatment outcomes. PUI enhances the penetration, circulation, and flow of the irrigant, resulting in a significant reduction of hard-tissue debris from the intricate regions of the root canal.[8] As a result, this enhances the cleaning of the uninstrumented areas in the root canal space.[9] PUI coupled with irrigant heated intracanally results in superior canal debridement than ultrasonic activation alone.[10]

This in vitro study primarily aimed to evaluate the effect of ultrasonic activation in conjunction with intracanal-heated NaOCl on the dentinal tubule penetration using Confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM). In addition, root canal wall debridement was examined histologically.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample preparation

Forty-eight extracted single-rooted teeth with a single canal were included in this study. Samples were cleaned with an ultrasonic scaler and stored in a 0.1% thymol solution until further use. Decoronation of the teeth below the cementoenamel junction was performed using a low-speed diamond disc under continuous water coolant to standardize a root length of 14 mm across all samples. A 10 K-file (Mani, Inc., Japan) was inserted in the root canal until visible at the apical foramen, and the working length was determined by deducting 0.5 mm from this measurement. The root canals were cleaned and shaped with Hero Gold files (Micro-Mega, France) up to a size of 30.04%. During the entire canal shaping process, irrigation was carried out using 5 mL of 3% NaOCl (Prime Dental, India) with a side-vented irrigation needle between each file change. Subsequently, the root canals were rinsed with sterile saline and further irrigated with 3 mL of 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (Prime Dental, India) for 1 min to remove the smear layer, followed by a rinse with 3 mL of sterile saline. After the chemomechanical preparation, a mixture of 3% NaOCl, mixed with 0.1 wt% fluorescent rhodamine B isothiocyanate dye (HiMedia, India), was delivered into the canals using a 30G side-vented irrigation needle (Neoendo, Orikam Healthcare Pvt. Ltd., India) 2 mm short of the working length.

Experiment A: Evaluation of depth of penetration

Passive ultrasonic activation was performed using the blue tip of an Endo X ultrasonic activator (Waldent, India) for 30 s. In Group III, intracanal heating of the solution was done at 150°C for 5 s using a Calamus Dual (Dentsply International, Inc., USA) handpiece and passive ultrasonic activation for 30 s using an Endo X ultrasonic activator with a blue tip.

Transverse root sections (1 mm thick) were obtained from the cervical, middle, and apical thirds using a carborundum disc under continuous water cooling, as per the irrigation protocols designated for each group. Samples were then dried and examined under CLSM (Zeiss LSM 700) at ×10 to evaluate the depth of penetration of the irrigant.

Experiment B: Evaluation of the root canal wall debridement

Following chemomechanical preparation, 24 teeth were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 48 h at room temperature to preserve tissue integrity. Decalcification was subsequently performed using 10% formic acid for 3 weeks, with regular monitoring of the solution and specimen, and confirmed through radiographic evaluation. Decalcified tooth specimens were embedded in paraffin wax, sectioned into 5 μm thick slices using a Leica RM2235 microtome (Leica Microsystems, Germany), and subsequently stained with hematoxylin and eosin for histological examination. A Leica DM2500 microscope with Leica Application Suite (LAS) software (Leica Microsystems, Germany) was used to evaluate the sections, capturing images at ×40 and ×100 for detailed assessment of root canal wall debridement. Using the scoring criteria provided by Di Spirito et al., the quantity of debris in each section was evaluated by a blinded operator (RK).[11]

RESULTS

The data were tabulated into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A significance level of 5% (P < 0.05) and a study power of 80% were considered. The mean and standard deviation were calculated for continuous variables. Inter- and intragroup comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance, followed by Bonferroni post hoc test. A 95% confidence interval was applied.

For assessing the depth of penetration, no significant difference was observed between Group I and Group II (P = 0.055 and P = 0.118, respectively) at the cervical and middle thirds. However, comparisons involving Group III (I vs. III and II vs. III) showed statistically significant differences (P < 0.001). At the apical third, all intergroup differences were statistically significant (P < 0.001) [Table 1 and Figure 1].

DISCUSSION

Microorganisms have been regarded as the main causative factors of pulpal and periradicular disease. The remaining pulp tissue and biofilms in the primary root canal system, as well as eccentricities such as isthmuses, accessory and lateral canals, fins, deltas, and loops, serve as a source for reinfection or persistent infection.[1213] Conventional rotary instrumentation techniques often fail to reach the canal irregularities, contacting only ~40% of the apical surface area and potentially leaving debris and bacteria in uninstrumented areas.[9] To address the issues with the contemporary instrumentation techniques, it is therefore essential to optimize irrigation strategies. The most commonly employed irrigant is NaOCl due to its lubricating properties and its ability to mechanically remove debris. Furthermore, it is cost-effective and easily accessible.[14] NaOCl has the unique ability to dissolve tissue, a property confirmed by the saponification reaction.[15]

The effectiveness of NaOCl can be enhanced through various methods, including increasing its concentration, raising the temperature, and employing different activation techniques. Heating NaOCl increases its temperature, which in turn increases the kinetic energy of its molecules. This heightened kinetic energy overcomes intermolecular forces, making the solution more fluid (reducing viscosity) and lowering the surface tension. As a result, the solution penetrates deeper into the dentinal tubules, allowing it to reach microorganisms located further within.[16] According to research by Abou-Rass and Oglesby, raising the irrigant’s temperature enhances its capacity to dissolve tissue.[17] Heating of NaOCl amplifies its bactericidal properties and proteolytic activity, selectively dissolving organic smear layer components without altering inorganic constituents.[18]

NaOCl can be prewarmed externally using various devices, such as a beaker on a hot plate, a kettle, a baby bottle warmer, or by preheating a syringe or heating it in the canal using heat carriers (pluggers), ultrasonic inserts, or laser devices.[319] The Calamus Dual system was utilized in this study for the intracanal heating of NaOCl at 150°C for 8 s. It is an integrated obturation device with handpieces used for downpacking (pack) and backfilling (flow) of root canals.

Intracanal heating is more advantageous than extraoral heating of NaOCl, as preheating the irrigant leads to a significant increase in intracanal temperature during delivery, followed by a rapid decrease afterward.[1920] Studies using preheated solutions have shown that the temperature quickly equilibrates to body temperature. However, heat transfer may occur during the irrigation of the root canal system with preheated solutions, potentially affecting the surrounding periodontal tissues. Based on existing literature, it can be inferred that the presence of intact vasculature in the area, along with the thermal conductivity of the periodontal membrane and alveolar bone, may help dissipate the heat increase on the root surface.[21] Applying irrigants heated in the canal at brief but frequent intervals efficiently increase the irrigants temperature when being within the range of temperatures that are safe for periodontal tissues.[22]

Irrigation activation techniques can be broadly divided into three categories: manual, machine-assisted, and mechanical. CNI is a popular irrigation method that effectively debrides the canal, but in most clinical settings, this method does not create any hydrodynamic turbulence energy, thus leaving behind tissue and microbial biomass in the canals. The limited depth of penetration of the irrigant in the dentinal tubules of apical third using the CNI can be attributed to the vapor lock created by the biological breakdown of NaOCl into ammonium and carbon dioxide bubbles, which may limit irrigant replenishment between 1 and 1.5 mm apical to the needle tip, particularly in the apical third, resulting in a significant decrease in the debridement efficacy of CNI.[23]

Passive ultrasonic irrigation in root canals relies on acoustic microstreaming and cavitation. Acoustic microstreaming generates shear forces that remove bacteria and debris, while cavitation involves the formation and collapse of gas-filled bubbles, producing shockwaves that further clean the canal. Both of these mechanisms collectively serve to debride the canals which results in a greater tubular penetration of the activated irrigants. The smear layer can be effectively removed from the instrumented walls of the root canal systems by ultrasonic instrumentation of the canals and irrigation using a combination of irrigating solutions.[24]

The greater depth of penetration in Group III as compared to Group II and the control group in this study could be due to the fluid dynamics of passive ultrasonic activation coupled with the advantages of intracanal-heated NaOCl. Iandolo et al. in their research concluded that ultrasonic activation of intracanal-heated NaOCl greatly enhanced its penetration into dentinal tubules as compared to syringe irrigation or ultrasonic activation alone.[12] A study reported that the cleaning effect in straight canals lasts up to 3 mm in front of the oscillating file tip.[25]

This in vitro study also assessed histologically the root canal cleanliness by comparing the effectiveness of traditionally delivered NaOCl, ultrasonically activated NaOCl, and intracanal–heated ultrasonically activated NaOCl. Histological evaluation was performed using a Leica DM500 microscope equipped with LAS X software, which enabled detailed visualization of root canal walls and accurate assessment of debris presence after endodontic treatment.

van der Sluis et al. demonstrated in a study that the synergistic application of intracanal-heated NaOCl and ultrasonic agitation resulted in a significant reduction of debris within the root canal system, suggesting a potentially valuable adjunct to conventional endodontic cleaning and shaping protocols.[9] Similarly, another study reported that ultrasonically activated NaOCl, when intracanal heated, resulted in significantly less debris compared to both the groups using ultrasonically activated NaOCl and traditionally applied NaOCl. Iandolo et al. stated that intracanal heating of NaOCl at 180°C was more effective in achieving clean canal walls. In contrast, extracanal heating of NaOCl at 50°C resulted in a higher amount of debris, with the smear layer being more prominently present.[12]

In line with the findings of the study, the combination of ultrasonic activation and intracanal-heated NaOCl significantly enhanced dentinal tubule penetration and facilitated tissue dissolution. It resulted in improved cleanliness of root canal walls along the cervical, middle, and apical thirds. The results of this study are promising and have direct applicability to in vivo conditions. However, future research should incorporate a larger sample size and investigate a range of irrigation protocols and activation techniques.

CONCLUSION

Based on the limitations of the study, it can be inferred that intracanal heating combined with passive ultrasonic activation resulted in a greater depth of penetration of the irrigant, leading to cleaner root canal walls.
