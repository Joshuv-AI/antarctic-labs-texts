Targeting METTL16 attenuates mesangial cell viability and fibrosis in a high-glucose state by suppressing m6A modification and the expression of RAP1B
Authors: Weixu Wang, Yanhong Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMethyltransferase-like 16 (METTL16) is involved in regulating kidney disease progression. This study aimed to investigate the effect of METTL16 on the progression of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) and its potential mechanism.MethodsSV40-MES13 cells were transfected with METTL16 siRNA (siMETTL16), scramble control (siNC), or RAP1B siRNA (siRAP1B) under low glucose (LG) or high glucose (HG) conditions. Subsequently, RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays, cell viability assays, EdU staining, TUNEL staining, ROS staining, and MDA detection were carried out.ResultsIn SV40-MES13 cells, cell viability, the number of EdU-positive cells, the MDA level, METTL16 mRNA, and protein levels were greater in the HG groups than in the control group. Moreover, cell viability, the number of EdU-positive cells, and VIMENTIN protein levels were lower, whereas the apoptosis rate and ROS and MDA levels were greater in the HG + siMETTL16 group than in the HG + siNC group. RAP1B mRNA and protein levels and N6-methyladenosine modification levels were greater in the HG group than in the control group but lower in the HG + siMETTL16 group than in the HG + siNC group. Furthermore, the METTL16 protein bound to the RAP1B mRNA. Compared with those in the HG + siNC group, cell viability, the number of EdU-positive cells, and VIMENTIN protein levels were decreased, whereas the ROS and MDA levels were increased in the HG + siRAP1B group.ConclusionMETTL16 knockdown attenuates cell viability and fibrosis by reducing m6A modification and the expression of RAP1B in high glucose–treated mesangial cells, suggesting the potential of METTL16 as a treatment target for DKD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12860-025-00559-3.

Introduction

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD), characterized by the deterioration of kidney function in patients with diabetes, has presented a high incidence and prevalence in recent decades, with a prevalence ranging from 20% to 40% in diabetes patients worldwide [1–3]. Currently, despite lifestyle modifications, blood glucose management, such as glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, and blood pressure management, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEis) or angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), are applied in DKD patients, and the progression of kidney disease is inevitable, which vastly increases dialysis risk and impairs long-term survival in DKD patients [4–8]. Therefore, it is essential to explore the pathogenesis of DKD to delay or even block the progression of kidney disease to further improve the prognosis of DKD patients.

Methyltransferase-like 16 (METTL16), a family of N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methyltransferases, plays a vital role in various biological processes via m6A modification of downstream mRNAs via posttranscriptional regulation [9–11]. Recently, several studies have suggested that METTL16 might be involved in the regulation of kidney disease progression [12–14]. For example, one genome-wide association study indicated the potential association of METTL16 with the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [12]. Another study revealed that METTL16 expression is greater in kidney disease in an in vitro model than in a control model; however, this study did not explore the detailed mechanism by which METTL16 regulates kidney disease progression in detail [13]. Therefore, this study proposes the potential of METTL16 in regulating the progression of DKD.

To further explore the detailed mechanism by which METTL16 regulates DKD progression in detail, the RM2Target database (available at http://rm2target.canceromics.org/) was used, and the target gene of METTL16, which strongly reflects the relationship with renal progression, with a confidence score ≥ 3 and motif site ≥ 9, was selected. Finally, the GTPase Ras-proximate-1 (RAP1B) was selected among the 17 target genes. RAP1B is related to kidney injury and participates in normal physiological metabolism in the kidney [15, 16]. For example, one study indicated that the downregulation of RAP1B could exacerbate kidney injury [15]. In another study, RAP1B was shown to ameliorate glucose-induced mitochondrial dysfunction in renal tubular cells [16]. However, no study has reported the detailed mechanism by which METTL16 regulates RAP1B and further participates in the pathogenesis of DKD.

Although the findings of previous studies indirectly imply the potential of METTL16 in regulating the progression of DKD and that RAP1B might be a downstream gene, studies that clarify this hypothesis are lacking. In addition, clarification of this hypothesis could provide more ideas for the treatment of DKD and provide more evidence for a better understanding of the pathogenesis of DKD. Hence, in the present study, SV40-MES13 cells were transfected with METTL16 siRNA or RAP1B siRNA alone or in combination under high-glucose conditions to investigate the ability of METTL16 to regulate the progression of DKD and its potential mechanism.

Methods

Glucose treatment

SV40-MES13 cells (Immocell Biotechnology, China) were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Servicebio, China) (catalog number: G4612) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Servicebio, China) at 37°C and 5% CO2. For glucose treatment, the cells were cultured in 15 mM or 25 mM glucose-containing medium for 24 h and were referred to as the low-glucose (LG) and high-glucose (HG) groups, respectively. The normal cultured cells served as the control group (Control). After that, a cell viability assay, 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) staining, TdT-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining, reactive oxygen species (ROS) staining, malondialdehyde (MDA) detection, a transwell assay, a scratch assay, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and western blotting were performed.

Mettl16 modification experiment

SV40-MES13 cells were plated into 6-well plates (2 × 105 cells/well), cultured to 80% confluence, and then transfected with METTL16 siRNA (siMETTL16) or scramble control (siNC) (GenePharma, China) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA). Afterward, the transfected cells were cultured in medium containing 25 mM glucose and used as the HG + siNC group or HG + siMETTL16 group. The cells without transfection were exposed to standard medium (control group) or 25 mM glucose-containing medium (HG group). Following 24 h of treatment, the cells were harvested for further assays, as described above. Furthermore, methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP) was performed after treatment.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays

To determine the m6A modification level of RAP1B, the MeRIP assay was accomplished via the riboMeRIP m6A Kit (Ribobio, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Moreover, SV40-MES13 cells were cultured, and a RIP assay was performed with a RIP Kit (Beyotime, China). In brief, the cells were lysed and incubated with protein A/G agarose, which was pretreated with IgG or an anti-METTL16 primary antibody (Proteintech, China). Then, the RNA was purified and used for RT‒qPCR to measure the enrichment of RAP1B.

RAP1B modification experiment

SV40-MES13 cells were cultured and transfected with RAP1B siRNA (siRAP1B) (GenePharma, China) or siNC, as previously described. After transfection, the cells were cultured in medium containing 25 mM glucose and named the HG + siNC group or HG + siRAP1B group. The untransfected cells were cultured in 25 mM glucose-containing medium (HG group). Further assays, as mentioned above, were performed after 24 h of treatment.

Cell viability assay, EdU staining, and TUNEL staining

The viability of the SV40-MES13 cells after treatment was evaluated via a Cell Counting Kit-8 (Servicebio, China). In brief, the cells were treated with a mixture of buffers for 2 h at 37 °C, and the optical density was read to calculate the cell viability. For EdU staining, the cells were treated with EdU reagent (Beyotime, China) for 2 h, followed by fixation and permeabilization according to the kit protocol. The cells were then successively stained with Click additive solution and 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Servicebio, China). TUNEL staining was carried out to analyze the cell apoptosis rate via a TUNEL apoptosis kit (Servicebio, China). Briefly, the cells were fixed and permeabilized, followed by staining with TUNEL working buffer and DAPI buffer.

ROS staining and MDA detection

The ROS level of the SV40-MES13 cells after treatment was assessed with an ROS staining kit (Beyotime, China). In brief, 10 µM DCFH-DA solution was added for 30 min; then, fluorescence images were taken to measure the relative ROS level. For MDA detection, the protein was isolated and quantified via a RIPA or BCA kit (Servicebio, China). Next, the MDA concentration was assessed via an MDA detection kit (Beyotime, China). The concentration of MDA was normalized to the protein concentration.

Transwell and scratch assays

For the transwell assay, SV40-MES 13 cells were resuspended in FBS-free DMEM and seeded in a transwell insert. The plate was filled with 10% FBS-containing DMEM. The cells were cultivated for 24 h. Then, the invading cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet and counted with a microscope. For the scratch assay, the cells were seeded in a plate, and a wound was scratched with a 10 µL pipette. A picture of the wound was taken at 0 h after the wound was made. The cells were subsequently incubated for 24 h, after which images were taken. Finally, the wound healing area was calculated.

qPCR

RNA was isolated via an RNA extraction kit (Servicebio, China). qPCR was carried out via a cDNA synthesis kit (Servicebio, China) and a qPCR kit (Servicebio, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The primers for Mettl16, RAP1B, and β-actin are as follows (5’-3’): Mettl16 (forward, TACCGCTGGGTCAGAAGC; reverse TGCTTAGAGCCATCTCACAAA), RAP1B (forward, GCTCTGAGCCAGGTCTGAAG; reverse, AGTTCTTCAGACCTGGCTCA) and β-actin (forward, GGCTGTATTCCCCTCCATCG, reverse: CCAGTTGGTAACAATGCCATGT).

Western blotting

After treatment, SV40-MES13 cells were lysed with RIPA buffer and quantified via a BCA kit. The proteins were subsequently separated by electrophoresis and electrophoretically transferred to membranes (PALL, USA). After blocking, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at the appropriate dilution ratio. Secondary antibody incubation and enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection (Servicebio, China) were immediately performed. The following antibodies were used: primary antibodies against METTL16, VIMENTIN (Affinity, China), fibronectin (FN, Affinity, China), RAP1B (Proteintech, China), and β-ACTIN (Servicebio, China) and a goat-anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Servicebio, China).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted via GraphPad 9.0 software (GraphPad, USA). The t test was used for comparison in the RIP assay, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s test was used for other assays. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

HG treatment increased proliferation, oxidative stress, and METTL16 levels in SV40-MES13 cells

Compared with those in the control group, cell viability (both P < 0.05, Fig. 1A) and the number of EdU-positive cells (both P < 0.05, Fig. 1B) were both greater, whereas apoptosis rates (all P > 0.05, Fig. 1C) were not different between the LG and HG groups. The ROS level did not differ (P > 0.05, Fig. 1D), but the MDA level was greater in the HG group than in the control group (P < 0.05, Fig. 1E). These findings indicate that oxidative stress was increased. Both the mRNA and protein levels of METTL16 were greater in the LG and HG groups than in the control group (all P < 0.05, Fig. 1F-G). Considering the results above, the HG condition was chosen for the subsequent experiments.

Silencing METTL16 inhibited cell viability, migration, and fibrosis while increasing apoptosis and oxidative stress in HG-treated SV40-MES13 cells

METTL16 mRNA (P < 0.001, Fig. 2A) and protein levels (P < 0.01, Fig. 2B) were lower in the HG + siMETTL16 group than in the HG + siNC group, indicating successful transfection. Cell viability (P < 0.05, Fig. 2C) and the number of EdU-positive cells (P < 0.01, Fig. 2D) were lower in the HG + siMETTL16 group than in the HG + siNC group. The apoptosis rate was greater in the HG + siMETTL16 group than in the HG + siNC group (P < 0.01, Fig. 2E). These findings indicated that silencing METTL16 attenuated HG-induced cell proliferation and promoted the apoptosis rate.

Furthermore, scratch and transwell assays revealed that the cell counts and wound healing rates were lower in the HG + siMETTL16 group than in the HG + siNC group (P < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). Furthermore, the cell counts and wound healing rates were lower in the HG + siRAP1B group than in the HG + siNC group (P < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 1C-D). These findings indicate that silencing METTL16 inhibits the migration of HG-treated SV40-MES13 cells.

ROS (P < 0.01, Fig. 3A) and MDA (P < 0.05, Fig. 3B) levels were greater in the HG + siMETTL16 group than in the HG + siNC group. Furthermore, the VIMENTIN protein level was decreased (P < 0.01), whereas the FN level did not change (P > 0.05, Fig. 3C) in the HG + siMETTL16 group compared with the HG + siNC group. These findings showed that HG-induced fibrosis was alleviated by silencing METTL16. In addition, METTL16 silencing can induce oxidative stress.

METTL16 may enhance RAP1B expression through m6A methylation in HG-treated SV40-MES13 cells

RAP1B mRNA (P < 0.05, Fig. 4A) and RAP1B protein (P < 0.05, Fig. 4B) levels were greater in the HG group than in the control group. Moreover, the RAP1B mRNA (P < 0.01, Fig. 4A) and RAP1B protein (P < 0.05, Fig. 4B) levels were lower in the HG + siMETTL16 group than in the HG + siNC group. These findings implied that HG induced RAP1B expression, whereas inhibition of METTL16 alleviated this effect. MeRIP experiments revealed that the m6A modification level of RAP1B was greater in the HG group than in the control group (P < 0.01, Fig. 4C), whereas it was lower in the HG + siMETTL16 group than in the HG + siNC group (P < 0.01, Fig. 4C). These findings indicated that HG induction increased m6A modification in RAP1B, whereas inhibition of METTL16 reduced this effect. Furthermore, the RIP experiment revealed that the enrichment of RAP1B was greater in the anti-METTL16 group than in the IgG group, which implied that the METTL16 protein bound to the RAP1B mRNA (P < 0.001, Fig. 4D). These findings indicate that METTL16 may increase RAP1B expression through m6A methylation.

Discussion

Mesangial cell proliferation and mesangial matrix expansion are typical pathological features of DKD, which are manifested mainly by the accumulation of matrix proteins such as collagen IV, collagen V, laminin and fibronectin in the mesangium; then, the accumulation of these matrix proteins can crush the glomerular capillaries, leading to a decrease in the glomerular filtration rate and a reduction in the filtration surface area, thereby affecting the normal function of the kidney [17, 18]. Owing to the above implications of mesangial cells in the DKD process, high-glucose conditions are commonly used to culture mesangial cells to mimic the DKD cellular model for investigations [19, 20]. In this study, we also applied low-glucose and high-glucose conditions to SV40-MES13 cells and observed that high-glucose conditions promoted cell viability, as reflected by both the results of the CCK-8 assay and the EdU staining assay, but had a weaker effect on cell apoptosis and oxidative stress. These findings were consistent with those of previous studies [21]. We consider that the discrepancy between cell viability and apoptosis may result from the following: cell viability reflects increased cell number via cell growth and replication, whereas cell apoptosis reflects the genetic control of orderly cell death, which is one major type of cell death, and other types, such as pyroptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis, also contribute to cell death. In addition, the signals involved in cell viability and apoptosis overlap but are not exactly the same; therefore, high-glucose conditions increase cell viability but less strongly impact the apoptosis of SV40-MES13 cells. In addition, we discovered that METTL16 was upregulated by high-glucose treatment in SV40-MES13 cells. By combining the multiple functions of METTL16 via its regulation of methylation in various diseases [22–24], we speculated that METTL16 might be a potential target for attenuating DKD; therefore, subsequent functional experiments involving METTL16 modification were performed.

METTL16, an RNA m6A methyltransferase, plays multiple important roles in cellular bioprocesses and biofunctions by catalyzing m6A modification, affecting RNA stability, splicing, translation efficiency, and localization [25–27]. In this study, we found that METTL16 knockdown decreased cell viability and fibrosis but increased apoptosis and oxidative stress in high glucose–treated SV40-MES13 cells. In previous studies, the indirect role of METTL16 in regulating the progression of DKD was deduced. In contrast to previous studies, this study provides evidence of the role of METTL16 in DKD and clarifies the potential of METTL16 as a treatment target for DKD. We consider the following explanations: (i) METTL16 has been shown to increase cell viability while decreasing apoptosis in various diseases via the regulation of m6A modifications of target genes such as PRDM15, THRAP3, RBM25, USP4, and CIDEA [28–30], resulting in similar effects on high glucose-treated SV40-MES13 cells in this study. (ii) METTL16 promotes fibrosis by regulating m6A and HLA-DPB1 expression [31], and similar effects on high glucose–treated SV40-MES13 cells were detected in this study. (iii) METTL16 has been found to regulate oxidative stress by modifying the splicing, maturation, and degradation of MAT2A pre-mRNA [32], so it may have a similar effect on the high glucose-treated SV40-MES13 cells in this study. (iv) METTL16 can modify the viability, fibrosis, and oxidative stress of high glucose–treated SV40-MES13 cells via m6A modification of RAP1B, as proven by our subsequent experiments. Furthermore, the use of only VIMENTIN and FN as markers for fibrosis is not enough, and further studies could consider the addition of ECM components such as the COL I and COL III proteins to further determine the degree of fibrosis.

RAP1B is a small GTP enzyme that belongs to the Rap family and plays important roles in cell signal transduction, cell adhesion and cell cycle regulation [33]. RAP1B maintains a high level in proximal tubular cells under hyperglycemic conditions [16] and is dysregulated in the kidneys of diabetic rodents [34, 35]. In addition, RAP1B upregulation is closely involved in high glucose-induced mesangial matrix synthesis and renal glomerular mesangial fibrosis. In this study, we observed that METTL16 knockdown reduced m6A modification and the expression of RAP1B and further discovered that RAP1B knockdown decreased cell viability and fibrosis to some extent and increased oxidative stress in high glucose–treated SV40-MES13 cells. Our findings are in line with those of previous studies concerning the functions of RAP1B [16, 34, 35] and suggest that RAP1B is involved in the effect of METTL16 in high glucose-treated SV40-MES13 cells. Inspired by bioinformatics analysis showing that RAP1B is downstream of METTL16 in a DKD model, we successfully verified this hypothesis in a DKD model, which has not been previously reported. However, further in vivo studies are needed to verify these findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, after SV40-MES13 cells were transfected with METTL16 siRNA or RAP1B siRNA alone or in combination under high glucose conditions, METTL16 increased cell viability and fibrosis by increasing m6A modification and the expression of RAP1B in high glucose–treated mesangial cells. These findings support the potential of METTL16 as a treatment target for DKD.
