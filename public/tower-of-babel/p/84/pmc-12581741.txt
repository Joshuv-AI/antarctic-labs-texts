The impact of hypertension on hearing loss phenotypes and its causal mediation underlying abnormal BMI: findings from the China national health survey 2023
Authors: Haibo Huang, Yue Fan, Fangxu Yan, Yaoda Hu, Huijing He, Yawen Liu, Tan Xu, Xiaoli Zhu, Yingying Zhu, Wenwen Diao, Xin Xia, Ji Tu, Ang Li, Binbin Lin, Qihang Liu, Zhiming Lu, Weihao Wang, Da Xu, Zhili Chen, Zichao Wang, Xingming Chen, Guangliang Shan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationships of hypertension and its temporal (duration), phenotypic (stage), and hemodynamic (pulse pressure) parameter with hearing loss (HL) remain unclear. We aimed to (1) characterize hypertension parameter-specific effects on HL, and (2) quantify contribution of hypertension in mediating the association between abnormal BMI and HL, given that abnormal BMI has been associated with an increased risk of HL.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis using data from the 2023 China National Health Survey, including 5918 participants aged 20–88 years. Hypertension was defined as self-reported physician diagnosis, antihypertensive medication use, or averaged systolic/diastolic blood pressure ≥140/90 mmHg. Hearing outcomes were classified by frequency (low/mid, speech, and high) and severity (mild and moderate to complete). Binary and ordinal logistic regression model was employed to calculated ORs and 95% CI of hypertension parameters for frequency-specific general HL (binary) and ordinal severity of HL, respectively.ResultsA total of 2432 (41.1%) had hypertension and 2690–3915 (45.45%-66.16%) had HL. Those with hypertension, prolonged duration, high stage, and abnormal pulse pressure have higher hearing thresholds. In fully adjusted models, hypertension was independently associated with HL, particularly for high frequency, the ORs (95% CIs) were 1.69 (1.46, 1.95) and 1.57 (1.40, 1.76) for binary general and ordinal severity high-frequency HL, respectively. The associations between abnormal BMI and HL were mediated by hypertension significantly, the mediation proportions were 18%-51% for general HL and 21%-48% for severity.ConclusionHypertension was not only an independent risk factor for HL but a mediator in the relationship between abnormal BMI and HL, particularly at high frequency and the risk and severity of HL worsens with hypertension duration and stage.Keywords: Hypertension, hearing loss, BMI, mediation analysis

Background

Hearing loss (HL) is a prevalent yet underrecognized public health concern, affecting over 1.5 billion individuals globally [1], with substantial consequences for communication, cognitive function and overall quality of life [1]. Current estimates suggest that over 42% of people with any severity of HL are aged above 60 years [1]. While ageing remains the predominant risk factor, emerging evidence suggests that modifiable cardiometabolic risk factors [2–5], particularly hypertension, may play a critical role in auditory dysfunction [6–9].

Hypertension, one of the world’s leading risk factors for death and disability, affecting approximately 1.3 billion individuals [10], is a well-established contributor to microvascular impairment and has been implicated in a range of sensory and neurological disorders [11,12]. Given that the cochlea is highly dependent on a rich blood supply, hypertension is hypothesized to cause HL by impairing cochlear microcirculation [11,12]. However, its specific impact on HL, particularly high frequency, remains inadequately characterized. While several epidemiological studies have suggested an association [6–9], others have reported null findings [4,5]. These inconsistencies may due to methodological differences, including study design, sample size, definition of HL and confounding control. Furthermore, the frequency-specific effects of hypertension on HL remain unclear. Notably, the basal turn of the cochlea, responsible for high-frequency sound perception, is particularly vulnerable to ischemic injury due to its high metabolic demand and limited collateral blood supply [7,13]. Understanding the differential impact of hypertension on various frequency ranges of HL could provide novel insights into its pathophysiology and guide targeted interventions. In addition, we also knew little about the relationships of hypertension duration, blood pressure (BP) stages and pulse pressure (PP) with HL, as most prior studies have relied on a binary hypertension diagnosis.

Beyond its direct effects, hypertension is closely intertwined with metabolic disorders, such as abnormal BMI, which may further contribute to HL risk. It is estimated that the prevalence of overweight and obesity among Chinese adults are 34.3% and 16.4%, respectively [14]. A notable co-occurrence of obesity and hypertension was observed. Specifically, 23.4% of participants with hypertension were also obese, while 44.5% of obese participants had hypertension [15,16]. Several studies have demonstrated the negative effect of abnormal BMI on hearing function [17–19]. Overweight and obesity are generally associated with an increased risk of HL. However, whether and to what extent hypertension mediates the relationship between abnormal BMI and HL remain unclear.

In this study, we leverage a national population-based dataset to compressively examine the association between hypertension-related indicators (including hypertension disease, duration, BP stages, and PP) and HL across different frequency ranges and severity levels. By incorporating stratified and mediation analyses, we aim to (1) determine whether each of hypertension-related issues is independently associated with HL; (2) assess the dose-response relationship between hypertension-related indicators and severity of HL; and (3) investigate the mediating role of hypertension in the association between abnormal weight and HL. Given the rising global burden of hypertension, obesity, and HL, elucidating these interrelationships is essential for early cardiovascular risk management to mitigate HL.

Methods

Study design and population

The China National Health Survey (CNHS) is a large nationwide multi-ethnic population-based cross-sectional study conducted from 2012 to now, which represented the civilian China population [20]. CNHS included adults who were ≥20 years and lived in the survey region for 5 years or above for enhancing the representation of local residents’ health baselines, minimizing confounding biases. People with severe mental or physical disorders, pregnant women, persons on active military duty, or foreigners were excluded. These inclusion and exclusion criteria are applied to every phase of CNHS. The CNHS phase III demonstrated proactive recognition in auditory health governance by integrating pure-tone audiometry into its 2023 national survey protocol, establishing China’s first nationwide general population-based audiological survey. For this analysis, we used data collected from August to November 2023 in the CNHS phase III for Jilin and Jiangsu, which were the only two provinces to have completed full data collection and quality control for hearing assessments by the analysis period. The study has been carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Bioethical Committee of the Institute of Basic Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (No. 2022134). All study participants provided their written informed consent.

Data collection and definition

Assessment of audiometry

Pure-tone audiometry was measured for the audiometric threshold of each ear at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 kHz, which are important for comprehending speech information, using the modified Hughson-Westlake method [21]. Details are provided in the eMethod section. If the maximum value was not responded, the audiometric threshold would be recorded as the maximum value for the purpose of our analyses.

Hearing outcomes were classified by frequency (low/mid, speech, and high) and severity (mild and moderate to complete). Three pure tone averages (PTA) were derived from audiometric thresholds of the better ear across different frequency ranges: low/mid-frequency (PTA threshold at 0.5, 1, and 2 kHz), speech-frequency (PTA threshold at 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz), which covers the range essential for speech understanding, and high-frequency (PTA threshold at 4, 6, and 8 kHz) HL, which reflects the ability to detect high-pitched sounds such as consonants. For each of these PTA types, hearing status was categorized based on the World Health Organization criteria using the participants’ better hearing ear (i.e. lower 4-frequency PTA). The categories were defined as follows: normal hearing (<20 dB HL), mild hearing loss (20 to <35 dB HL), and moderate-or-greater hearing loss (≥35 dB HL) [1].

Assessment of BMI

Normal weight, underweight, and overweight/obesity was categorized according to the body mass index (BMI) (normal, 18.5 to < 24 kg/m2; underweight, ≤18.5 kg/m2; overweight/obesity, ≥24 kg/m2). BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared, where weight and height were measured under standardized conditions.

Assessment of hypertension

Hypertension was defined based on (1) self-reported physician diagnosis, (2) use of hypertension medications, or (3) an average systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥140 mm Hg or an average diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥90 mm Hg. SBP and DBP was measured three times on the upper right arm using an electronic BP device (Omron HEM-907, Japan) with the participant in a sitting posture by trained technicians. Details of hypertension duration, BP stages, and Pulse pressure (PP) are provided in the eMethod section.

Covariates

Information on sociodemographic, lifestyle factors, and medical history was obtained from standard questionnaires through face-to-face interviews (eMethod). Definitions, measurements, and categorizations are provided in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

A total of 6291 participants who completed the audiometric examination and structured questionnaire, 373 with a history of any signal of ear diseases (e.g. otitis media, cerumen, sudden HL, head injury and otologic surgery) were excluded, and the remaining 5918 were used in the analysis (Figure S1). Missing values of potential covariates were imputed as the reference group conservatively, unless otherwise noted (eMethod).

The characteristics of participants were reported as a mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) for continuous variables and percentage for categorical variables, as appropriate. Differences between the two groups (the hypertension and non-hypertension groups) were analysed using the Chi-square test to calculate the P values for categorical and the ANOVA or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, as appropriate. We used multivariate binary logistic regression models to estimate odds ratios (ORs), and their 95% confidence interval (CI) for the association of dependent variables (i.e. hypertension, duration, BP stage, PP group and BMI) with independent variables (i.e. HL phenotypes), respectively. Multivariate ordinal logistic regression models were performed with severity of HL (3 levels: normal [reference], mild, and moderate to complete) as the response variable to examine the association between these issues and severity of HL. Three models for each outcome were built to adjust for potential confounders (eMethod). The first model was adjusted for age groups (< 65/≥65 years) and sex. The second model was additionally adjusted for survey region (Jiangsu or Jilin), ethnicity (Han or Chinese Kerea), residential location (rural or urban), SES (high, middle, and low), smoking (no/yes), drinking (no/yes), tea (no/yes), coffee (no/yes), tinnitus (no/yes), earphone use(no/yes) and occupational noise exposure (no/yes). The third model was further adjusted for dyslipidaemia (no/yes), and diabetes (no/yes), and additionally adjusting for hypertension (no/yes) for BMI. We also modelled per 5-year duration increment and PP as a continuous variable. Stratified analyses were conducted by age group, sex, survey region and menopause status in females.

We conducted mediation analyses using the method by VanderWeele [22], which builds on natural direct and indirect effects and allows for interaction between the exposure variable and the explanatory factor, to evaluate to what extent the associations of abnormal BMI with increased risk of HL phenotypes were explained by causal risk factor for HL phenotypes, that is, hypertension. We regrouped BMI into abnormal (underweight and overweight/obesity) and normal (reference) for the interpretation of the results. We used the R package CMAverse, which provides a unified framework for causal mediation analysis under the counterfactual framework, to estimate proportion mediated to examine the extent to hypertension mediate the association between BMI and HL [23], while adjusting all potential covariates based on the logistic regression. CIs were derived from delta method (binary logistic regression) or 200 bootstrap replications (ordinal logistic regression). The validity of the results relies on key assumptions, including no unmeasured confounding between exposure, mediator, and outcome, and correct model specification. The proportion mediated could exceed 100% if there were unmeasured mediators that had a protective effect on the outcome [24].

We conducted several sensitivity analyses for robustness: (a) restricting to people without both dyslipidaemia and diabetes for eliminating cardiometabolic competing risks; (b) additionally adjusting for female menopausal status to account for its potential influence on hearing health; (c) additionally adjusting for the use of ototoxic medications in the subpopulation with available data (Jiangsu); (d) removing from the models the adjustment for tinnitus, since it is unclear if this disorder might be part of the HL process; (e) examining the potential impact of unmeasured confounders using mediational E-values. Details are provided in the eMethod section. All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed with SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Study and participant characteristics

Characteristics of the population aged 20–88 years are presented in Table 1, stratified by hypertension group. The median age was 55 years and 51.52% of the sample was female. The proportion of patients with hypertension was 41.09%. Individuals without hypertension were on average younger (mean age: 50.57, SD: 12.83) compared to those with hypertension (mean age: 59.32, SD: 10.74). Compared to those without hypertension, those with hypertension were more likely to be higher BMI. 69.28% of individuals with hypertension were overweight or obesity. They also have more high prevalence of HL and more severe, particularly of high frequency. Over half of individuals with hypertension have moderate to complete high-frequency HL (normal: 19.49%; mild: 28.82%; moderate to complete: 51.69%). Those with overweight or obesity have similar characteristics with those with hypertension comparing with normal weight (Table S2). In addition, the hearing thresholds generally increase with prolonged duration of hypertension and progression of blood pressure stages and those with abnormal blood pressure exhibit higher hearing thresholds compared to individuals with normal blood pressure, particularly in high frequency (Figure 1).

Associations of hypertension and its duration with hearing loss phenotypes

There were 2432 participants with hypertension, 1339 participants for 0 to <5 years, 377 participants for 5 to <10 years, and 683 participants for ≥10 years (Table 2). In fully adjusted models, compared with those without hypertension, those with hypertension were at higher risk for HL, particularly for high frequency, the ORs (95% CI) for binary general HL were: 1.30 (1.15, 1.47), 1.34 (1.18, 1.52), 1.69 (1.46, 1.95) in low/mid-, speech- and high-frequency HL, respectively; the ORs (95% CI) for ordinal severity were: 1.35 (1.20, 1.52), 1.41 (1.25, 1.58), 1.57 (1.40, 1.76) in low/mid-, speech- and high-frequency HL, respectively (Table 2). There were significant associations between duration group with both general HL and its severity, particularly for high frequency; every 5-year duration increment was associated with a higher risk of HL, the ORs (95% CI) for binary general HL were: 1.18 (1.06, 1.32), 1.28 (1.14, 1.43), 1.31 (1.13, 1.52) in low/mid-, speech- and high-frequency HL, respectively; the ORs (95% CI) for ordinal severity were: 1.13 (1.03, 1.24), 1.21 (1.10, 1.33), 1.21 (1.09, 1.33) in low/mid-, speech- and high-frequency HL, respectively (Table 2).

Associations between blood pressure stages and hearing loss phenotypes

There were 5894 participants with completed blood pressure measurements, 3094 with elevated BP, 701 in stage 1, and 127 in stage 2 (Table 3). BP groups was also significantly associated with HL, particularly with high frequency, just having elevated BP can increase the risk of HL; association increases as the BP stage progresses, the ORs (95% CI) for binary general high-frequency HL were: 1.37 (1.18, 1.58), 1.96 (1.55, 2.48), 1.90 (1.13, 3.18) of group at elevated BP, stage 1, and stage 2, respectively; the ORs (95% CI) for ordinal high-frequency HL were: 1.35 (1.19, 1.52), 1.61 (1.34, 1.93), 1.73 (1.19, 2.53) of group at elevated BP, stage 1, and stage 2, respectively (Table 3).

Associations between pulse pressure and hearing loss phenotypes

There were 899 with abnormal PP. Compared with normal PP, those with abnormal PP were at higher risk for HL and its severity, the ORs for binary general HL and ordinal severity were 1.31 to 1.79 (p < 0.05) and 1.33 to 1.47 (p < 0.05, respectively; every 1-mm Hg increment was mild associated with HL and its severity (Table S3).

Mediation effects of abnormal BMI

The posited relationships between variables in the mediation analysis are shown in the Figure S2. Individuals with overweight/obesity demonstrate progressively elevated hearing thresholds from low to high frequency (Table S2) and have higher risk of hypertension compared to normal-weight controls (Table S4), the ORs (95% CI) was: 2.48 (2.20, 2.79), while underweight was a protective factor of hypertension (OR (95% CI): 0.37 (0.22, 0.61)). Overweight/obesity was associated with HL and its severity significantly while there was no association between underweight and HL. The ORs (95% CI) of overweight or obesity for binary general HL were: 1.34 (1.19, 1.51), 1.36 (1.20, 1.54), 1.23 (1.08, 1.40) in low/mid-, speech- and high-frequency HL, respectively; the ORs (95% CI) for ordinal severity were: 1.33 (1.19, 1.48), 1.28 (1.15, 1.43), 1.20 (1.07, 1.33) in low/mid-, speech- and high-frequency HL, respectively (Table S5). Furthermore, the associations between abnormal weight and HL were mediated by hypertension significantly, the mediation proportions and 95% CIs were 18% (6%, 31%), 19% (7%, 31%), and 51% (13%, 90%) for general HL and 21% (11%, 35%), 27% (15%, 45%), and 48% (26%, 134%) for severity (Table 4).

Stratified analyses

The findings of the stratified analyses are illustrated in Tables S6–S7. The associations between hypertension and ordinal frequency-specific HL (e.g. severity of HL) were generally stronger in older participants. In sex stratified analyses, hypertension had similar effect on HL. Interestingly, in menopause status stratified analyses in female population, premenopausal had higher risk for high-frequency HL compared with postmenopausal. In survey region stratified analyses, the associations between hypertension and HL were generally stronger in Jilin province participants.

Sensitivity analyses

In sensitivity analysis, findings were similar to the main analyses (Tables S8–S12). The E-value estimates showed that an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with hypertension and abnormal BMI with a OR of about 3.00 and 3.04 in binary and ordinal, respectively to nullify the indirect effects (Table S12).

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of HL across different frequency ranges and severity levels, providing a nuanced understanding of how hypertension-related indicators influence auditory function. We demonstrated that hypertension is not only an independent risk factor for HL, but also a key mediator in the relationship between abnormal weight and auditory dysfunction, particularly at high frequency. Additionally, our findings suggest that prolonged exposure to elevated blood pressure may accelerate auditory damage over time.

Our study provides robust evidence of a significant association between hypertension and HL, particularly in the high-frequency range. When contextualized within the global literature, our findings largely reinforce the evidence for an association between hypertension and HL, while also highlighting methodological factors that may contribute to cross-population heterogeneity. These findings align with previous longitudinal and cross-sectional studies that have reported similar associations [6,8,9]. For example, Lin et al. found that individuals with history of hypertension was independently associated with a higher risk of HL (RR: 1.04; 95%CI: [1.01, 1.07]) during 774,096 person-years of follow-up, while Reed et al. observed an estimated hypertension-HL associations were greater at higher frequencies (1 kHz: 3.4 dB, 95%CI: 0.7–6.1; 4 kHz: 7.7 dB, 95%CI: 2.4–12.9; and 8 kHz: 9.0 dB, 95%CI: 3.1–14.9). However, some studies have reported conflicting results, possibly due to differences in study design, sample size and adjustments for confounders [4,5]. For instance, a retrospective study of electronic health records conducted in America did not find a significant association between hypertension and HL, but their study was limited by a lack of adjustment for confounders such as ototoxic medicine. This heterogeneity underscores that beyond true biological or environmental variation, study design elements, including population characteristics, covariate adjustment and hearing assessment protocols, are critical to consider when comparing results across different regions and cultural contexts. In our study, we were able to address this concern in a sensitivity analysis; adjustment for ototoxic medication in the Jiangsu subpopulation did not materially change our results, strengthening the robustness of our findings.

We also found a consistent dose-response relationship, with longer hypertension duration and higher BP stages associated with greater HL risk, which align with previous studies [11,25,26]. A hospital-based cross-sectional study conducted among hypertensive patients found the prevalence of HL worsens with the hypertension duration(≤3 years: 13.6%; > 3 years: 42.%) [11]. A case–control study found the hearing threshold increases as the grade of hypertension rises and the increases in the high frequency is greater than in the low frequency [25]. Similarly, high PP was significantly associated with HL, with higher PP conferring a greater risk. The elevated pressure pulse may contribute to HL by transmitting excessive pulsatile forces to the cochlea, potentially damaging its microvasculature and sensory cells. These findings reinforced the cumulative impact of hypertension on auditory function, potentially due to sustained damage to the vascular supply of the inner ear.

Mediation analysis demonstrated that abnormal weight significantly contributed to HL risk through hypertension, highlighting the intertwined roles of metabolic and vascular dysfunction [18]. Given their shared pathophysiological pathways, such as systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, obesity may exacerbate auditory impairment both directly and indirectly [18]. While hypertension appears to be a primary mediator, future longitudinal studies and interventional trials are warranted to determine whether targeted hypertension and weight management strategies can mitigate HL risk.

Our stratified analyses further revealed that the association between hypertension and HL was more pronounced in older adults and males, particularly at high-frequency range, suggesting potential age- and sex-related susceptibility. Age-related vascular changes, including increased arterial stiffness and endothelial dysfunction, may amplify the impact of hypertension on cochlear microcirculation. The stronger association observed in males may be attributed to sex differences in vascular physiology and hormonal regulation [27,28].

The potential mechanisms underlying the association between hypertension and HL arise from stria vascularis degeneration initiating cochlear pathophysiological cascades [29]. First, microcirculatory dysfunction instigated by hypertension reduces cochlear blood flow, inducing ischemic hypoxia that paradoxically increases strial capillary density while impairing endothelial function [13,30,31]. Furthermore, hypoxia-triggered oxidative stress accumulates mitochondrial DNA lesions [13], a process that interventional studies with antioxidants have shown to protect the cochlear hair cells and improving memory in rodent models [32]. Concurrently, pressure-induced natriuretic hormones suppress Na+/K+-ATPase α1 expression [33,34], jointly disrupting potassium recycling and endocochlear potentials [30,31]. In addition, ischemia-reperfusion injury establishes a self-amplifying loop: glutamate excitotoxicity during ischemia causes spiral ganglion calcium overload [35], while reperfusion-generated hydroxyl radicals selectively damage outer hair cells [36,37]. Longitudinal human cohorts have corroborated the clinical relevance of these pathways, showing that individuals with poor blood pressure control exhibit accelerated hearing decline over time [8]. Moreover, antihypertensive medications use may increase HL risk, which potentially through the inhibition of the endocochlear potential and alterations in Corti mechanics [9,38].

Furthermore, the different strength of association between hypertension and frequency-specific HL suggests distinct pathophysiological mechanisms at different frequencies. The basal turn of the cochlea, responsible for detecting high-frequency sounds, has higher metabolic demands and limited collateral blood flow, making it more susceptible to ischemic injury [13,39]. In the guinea pig model, the survival rate of outer hair cells (OHCs) from the cochlear base was found to be less than one-third of that from the apex after 5 h at room temperature [39]. Thus, hypertension-induced vascular compromise is likely to manifest initially as high frequency before affecting lower frequencies. The relatively weaker association with low/mid-frequency HL may reflect contributions from additional factors such as age-related cochlear degeneration and noise exposure.

To our knowledge, our study is the first to quantify hypertension parameter-specific effects on HL in a nationally representative Chinese general population survey, identifying hypertension as a mediator of BMI-HL. By comprehensively examining HL across frequency ranges and severity levels, our study assessed the impact of hypertension parameter-specific effects on auditory function. The robustness of our primary findings was confirmed through a series of sensitivity analyses. The use of mediation analysis further provides novel insights into the mechanistic pathways linking metabolic and vascular risk factors to HL. Methodologically, the study benefits from standardized and rigorous data collection protocols for both audiometric testing and blood pressure measurements, minimizing measurement bias. As the inaugural national collection of CNHS hearing data, this baseline assessment establishes critical reference values for future longitudinal cohort studies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The simultaneous measurement of exposure, mediator, and outcome means that reverse causality remains a possibility. Although we have controlled important potential confounders, residual confounding cannot be excluded due to the observational design. In addition, recall bias and misclassification bias also remain challenging to eliminate completely in the cross-sectional study. This would most likely attenuate the observed effect sizes, meaning our estimates might be underestimates of the true associations. Longitudinal studies are needed to confirm the observed relationships. Second, the underlying biological mechanisms of frequency- and severity-specific HL remain to be fully elucidated. Further research utilizing imaging techniques, biomarker analyses, and experimental models may provide deeper mechanistic insights. Third, recognizing BMI’s dynamic nature, future cohort studies will incorporate quarterly follow-ups to characterize its temporal trajectories. BMI is insufficiency as a solitary metric necessitates future integration of visceral fat area and skeletal muscle mass index for multidimensional assessment.

In conclusion, this study highlights hypertension as a significant, modifiable risk factor for HL, characterized by high-frequency specificity, a strong dose-response relationship with duration and severity, and heightened vulnerability in older adults and males. By establishing hypertension as a key mediator in the pathway from abnormal BMI to HL, our study illuminates a crucial mechanistic link within the cardiometabolic–auditory axis. Given the increasing burden of both conditions, integrating auditory health assessments into hypertension management strategies may offer new avenues for early intervention.

As hypertension remains a major global health concern [10], its impact on auditory function warrants clinical and public health attention. Even individuals with elevated blood pressure (prehypertension) face an increased risk of HL, underscoring the need for routine auditory screening, particularly for high-frequency thresholds, in hypertensive patients. Our findings emphasize the importance of proactive high-frequency HL screening in hypertensive individuals to enable timely intervention and mitigate long-term consequences. Effective blood pressure management through dietary modifications, increased physical activity, and antihypertensive treatment may offer dual benefits in reducing both cardiovascular and auditory morbidity [40–45].
