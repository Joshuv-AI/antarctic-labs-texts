Beyond Inflammation
Authors: Tianshu Gu, Tong Liu, Kang-Yin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: adaptive immunity, Asia, atrial fibrillation, immune remodeling, regulatory T cells, Th17

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most prevalent sustained arrhythmia worldwide and a leading driver of stroke, heart failure, and cardiovascular death, with a particularly steep trajectory in East Asian populations.1 The pathophysiology has classically been framed around electrical remodeling (ion-channel dysregulation) and structural remodeling (interstitial fibrosis and atrial dilatation).2

More recently, immune remodeling has emerged as an additional contributor to this scheme, anchored by the consistent observation that systemic markers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein and interleukin (IL)-6 are elevated in AF and track with disease persistence.3 What has remained less clear, particularly in Asian cohorts, is which arms of adaptive immunity are perturbed, and whether these perturbations differ between paroxysmal and persistent disease.

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Liu et al4 address this gap in a case–control study of 173 Asian AF patients (104 paroxysmal, 69 persistent) and 87 frequency-matched control subjects. T-cell subsets were quantified by multiparameter flow cytometry and serum cytokines by ELISA; associations with AF were tested in multivariable logistic models adjusted for left atrial diameter, left ventricular ejection fraction, and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR). The findings are internally consistent: effector Th1 and Th17 cells are expanded without a compensatory Treg increase, producing elevated Th1/Treg and Th17/Treg ratios. The Th1/Treg ratio emerged as the most robust independent correlate of AF (adjusted OR: 4.20; 95% CI: 1.72-10.24) and survived multiple-testing correction.

Three features distinguish the work. First, the authors prespecified their primary immune endpoints and applied Benjamini–Hochberg correction across families of related tests, which limits the false-positive risk inherent in profiling many cell subsets and ratios in parallel. Second, they examine subtype heterogeneity directly: paroxysmal AF displays a broad, network-wide Th-subset perturbation, while persistent AF shows a more selective polarization toward the Th1/Treg axis. This is consistent with the authors' own interpretation that early AF may involve a wider T-cell perturbation, whereas chronicity is sustained by a dominant Th1 signal with relatively insufficient Treg counter-regulation. Third, the peripheral findings converge biologically with emerging tissue-level evidence, including the senescent IFN-γ–competent CD8+ T-cell signal recently described in atrial tissue and single-cell coronary-sinus immune atlases of progressive AF.5 In a separate but related observation, left atrial diameter correlates positively with Th1/Treg, Th1/Th2, Th17/Treg, Th1%, Th17%, and absolute Th1 and Th17 counts, providing a modest internal link between the immune profile and atrial structural remodeling.

The cytokine signal that accompanies the cellular shift in Liu et al4 is informative. Stepwise increases in IFN-γ, IL-17A, IL-1β, and IL-18 across control subjects and paroxysmal and persistent disease echo the cellular Th1/Th17–Treg pattern and bring 2 complementary mechanisms into view. The Th1 arm offers a plausible link to electrical remodeling through connexin perturbation and conduction heterogeneity. The Th17/IL-17A axis, in turn, is biologically positioned for structural remodeling: IL-17A activates cardiac fibroblasts, alters the matrix metalloproteinase–TIMP balance, and promotes extracellular-matrix turnover—mechanisms now increasingly characterized in the context of cardiac and atrial fibrosis.6

A second mechanistic layer connects this adaptive program to the innate-immune signals that already inform AF risk assessment. Neutrophil-driven inflammation, including neutrophil extracellular trap formation, is elevated in AF and contributes to the prothrombotic atrial milieu,7 and the rise in NLR, IL-1β, and IL-18 reported by Liu et al4 is consistent with NLRP3-inflammasome activation, a known upstream stimulus for Th17 differentiation. The Th1/Th17–Treg signature is therefore best read not as a replacement for inflammatory markers such as NLR or hs-CRP, but as a cellular framework that places those simpler readouts in a more specific immunological context.

How firmly any of this can be held, however, is bounded by the design of the study itself—a point the authors are careful to make. The cross-sectional case–control design precludes inference about temporal ordering: whether Th1/Th17 expansion drives AF, accompanies it, or follows it cannot be resolved here. Peripheral blood is an integrated systemic readout, and circulating Th-subset frequencies do not necessarily reflect the cellular composition of the atrial myocardium or epicardial adipose tissue, where arrhythmogenic action presumably occurs. The cytokine subsample (n = 98), although stratified and apparently representative, is modest. Treg cells were enumerated by phenotype rather than functional suppression assay, leaving open whether Treg insufficiency in AF is quantitative, qualitative, or both. Th17 plasticity—the well-described conversion to ex-Th17/Th1-like states under chronic inflammation—could blur subset boundaries in cross-sectional snapshots. Finally, the single-center, single-ethnicity design, while a strength for the stated Asian focus, calls for replication across ethnicities, geographies, and treatment exposures; statins, renin–angiotensin system blockers, and oral anticoagulants all modulate immune readouts and were not analytically isolated here. Whether the immune signature carries prognostic information—for AF recurrence after ablation, progression from paroxysmal to persistent disease, or thromboembolic events—also remains to be determined.

Two priorities follow naturally. First, the cross-sectional associations reported here need to be tested in prospective designs, ideally examining whether baseline Th1/Treg and Th17/Treg track with progression of paroxysmal to persistent AF and with recurrence after rhythm-control intervention—outcomes most directly anchored in the present cellular and structural findings. Second, atrial tissue obtained at the time of ablation or cardiac surgery, interrogated by single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics, will be needed to confirm whether the peripheral signature mirrors the tissue compartment and to localize the effector programs implicated here.5

In the clinic, immune indexes such as Th1/Treg are unlikely to replace established markers; the more realistic question is whether they add discriminative information beyond NLR and conventional clinical variables, and whether they fit within stratification frameworks such as the 4S-AF scheme8 and holistic or integrated care management pathways.9,10 For East Asian patients, this is important given the recognized ethnic differences in clinical epidemiology11 and AF-related complications such as stroke and bleeding.12,13 Indeed, contemporary management algorithms have been refined to account for distinctive stroke and bleeding profiles14; such layered immune profiling would represent an incremental—not transformative—step. Past experience with inflammatory biomarkers in AF counsels patience: only a small fraction of candidate markers have achieved validated guideline-level utility.15

Stratification is the nearer translational goal; therapy, the more distant one. Several agents that intersect the Th1/Th17–Treg axis are under cardiovascular or precardiovascular evaluation: IL-6 pathway inhibition (eg, ziltivekimab) has shown biomarker effects in patients at high atherosclerotic risk16; anti–IL-17 antibodies and low-dose IL-2 strategies for Treg expansion are established in autoimmune indications but unexplored in AF; and colchicine, which dampens NLRP3-driven IL-1β/IL-18 signaling, has demonstrated benefit in chronic coronary disease.17 Whether any of these have a role in AF specifically remains an open question for prospective, mechanistically informed trials.

Liu et al4 report a reproducible Th1/Th17–Treg imbalance in Asian patients with AF, with subtype-specific patterns and a modest correlation with left atrial size. The findings are appropriately framed as hypothesis-generating and add a population-relevant data point to the immune-remodeling literature in AF. Their main value is in defining a more granular cellular framework for an inflammation hypothesis that has so far rested largely on nonspecific markers; whether this framework translates into prognostic or therapeutic utility will be determined by the prospective and mechanistic work it now invites.
