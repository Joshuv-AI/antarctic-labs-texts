Safety and efficacy of esketamine versus remifentanil as adjuncts to propofol sedation in children undergoing haematologic-oncologic procedures in China: a single-centre, randomised, controlled, crossover trial protocol
Authors: Shangxian Xu, Yu-Fan Yang, Jin-Meng Gao, Xia Chen, Zaixiang Tang, Fu-Hai Ji, Ke Peng, Qian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAdequate sedation is essential for paediatric patients undergoing invasive haematologic-oncologic procedures. The combination of propofol and remifentanil is commonly used yet is associated with respiratory depression. Esketamine has anaesthetic, analgesic and sympathomimetic properties and is known to cause less respiratory depression than other sedatives. This study aims to assess esketamine versus remifentanil in combination with propofol for invasive haematological-oncological procedures for paediatric patients.Methods and analysisThis prospective, randomised, double-blind, two-period crossover trial will include 80 paediatric patients aged 6–12 years, with American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status II to III, who are scheduled to undergo repeated invasive procedures including bone marrow aspirates and lumbar punctures under sedation. Participants will be randomised to two sequences: AB or BA. In sequence AB, children will receive propofol (3 mg/kg) and esketamine (0.5 mg/kg) in period 1 and propofol (3 mg/kg) and remifentanil (1 µg/kg) in period 2. In sequence BA, the order is reversed. The primary endpoint of this trial is the incidence of desaturation events, defined as SpO2<90% during the sedation and recovery. The secondary endpoints include: (1) the interruption rate of the procedure; (2) total dose of propofol; (3) the number of times esketamine or remifentanil is added; (4) recovery time; (5) distress behaviour; (6) a composite of sedation-related adverse events, including psychotomimetic effects (hallucinations, nightmares, fatigue, dizziness and headache), nausea, vomiting, bradycardia and hypotension; (7) operator satisfaction; (8) patient satisfaction and (9) anaesthetist satisfaction.Ethics and disseminationThe trial was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital of Soochow University (Approval No. 2025008). The results of this trial will be submitted for peer review and publication in a scientific journal.Trial registration numberChiCTR2500098533.Keywords: Paediatric anaesthesia, Anaesthesia in oncology, Child, China, Drug Combinations, Paediatric oncology

Introduction

Paediatric patients with haematologic oncology disorders frequently require multiple invasive procedures, including bone marrow aspirates (BMA) as well as lumbar punctures (LP) for diagnostic and treatment purposes.1,3 These procedures are associated with significant pain and distress, placing considerable stress on both children and their parents.4 5 Adequate analgesia and sedation are essential for effectively managing painful procedures in paediatric haematologic oncology patients.6 7

Propofol is the most widely used sedative-hypnotic agent for paediatric patients undergoing medical procedures. This short-acting sedative provides consistent sedation, a rapid onset of action, and complete recovery. However, propofol lacks analgesic properties, necessitating its frequent coadministration with an opioid to manage pain during invasive procedures. Remifentanil is a highly effective opioid that is rapidly hydrolysed by nonspecific esterases in plasma and tissues, providing non-cumulative opioid effects and enabling rapid recovery. The combination of remifentanil and propofol for paediatric sedation has gained widespread acceptance and is substantiated by an extensive body of literature despite the significant respiratory depressant effect of remifentanil.8,10

Ketamine is an N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor antagonist that induces profound analgesia and stimulates the sympathetic nervous system. Previous studies have shown that ketamine or ketofol can provide effective sedation and analgesia for brief, painful oncology procedures. However, side effects such as nightmares, vomiting, abnormal behaviour, transient diplopia and dream-like states have been observed.11,14 Fear of sedation and distressed behaviour has been reported in 17%–27% of children with leukaemia who require deep sedation for repeated painful procedures.15 Therefore, the optimal regimen for this vulnerable patient population should provide adequate analgesia and sedation while maintaining sufficient respiratory function and minimising adverse reactions. Strategies for managing procedure-related pain in paediatric haematologic oncology patients differ significantly across institutions and require more in-depth investigation.6 7 16

Esketamine, the S-enantiomer of ketamine, demonstrates approximately twice the potency of the racemic mixture. compared with other sedatives, it induces less cardiorespiratory depression and exhibits a lower incidence of psychotropic side effects than ketamine.17 Previous studies have demonstrated the use of esketamine in diverse clinical settings, such as endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography,18 same-visit bidirectional endoscopy19 and various paediatric procedures including paediatric 3Tesla (T) MRI,20 hydrostatic reduction of intussusception21 and management of paediatric forearm fractures,22 contexts that closely resemble the haematologic-oncologic procedures in our trial, which frequently necessitate effective analgesia and anxiolysis. Therefore, esketamine may serve as an ideal adjuvant to propofol sedation during haematologic-oncologic procedures.

In this randomised crossover controlled trial, we assess the safety, efficacy and adverse effects of esketamine compared with remifentanil as adjuncts to propofol for providing analgesia and sedation during paediatric haemato-oncology procedures.

Methods

Study design

This is a single-centre, prospective, randomised, double-blind, two-period crossover trial. The study will be conducted at Children’s Hospital of Soochow University from March 2025 to December 2025. A total of 80 children will undergo two separate sedation sessions (defined as period 1 and period 2) and will be randomly assigned to one of two treatment sequences (n=40 in each sequence): Sequence AB, where they receive propofol-esketamine during period 1 and propofol-remifentanil during period 2; or sequence BA, where they receive propofol-remifentanil during period 1 and propofol-esketamine during period 2. Each sedation session will be separated by a minimum 5-day washout period. The terminal half-life of intravenous esketamine is 287.50±110.20 min.23 Its psychotomimetic and dissociative effects are linked to peak plasma concentrations and usually resolve within 1–4 hours after a single bolus. Remifentanil has a context-sensitive half-time of 3–5 min, with any sedative or analgesic effects becoming undetectable within minutes after administration. Due to the rapid offset of both drugs’ primary effects, a 5-day washout period exceeds the time needed for complete elimination and resolution of their acute psychotropic and analgesic effects. This interval supports adequate pharmacokinetic clearance and reduces potential psychological or procedural carryover, ensuring each procedure is perceived as a separate event by the child and minimising the impact of prior sedation on behaviour. To further minimise potential psychological carryover effects, such as conditioned anxiety or needle phobia, that could influence the results, we will assess self-reported procedural anxiety using the Child Fear Scale (CFS). The CFS, a validated visual self-report tool with five facial expressions ranging from neutral (0=no fear) to terrified (4=extreme fear), is widely used to assess procedural fear and anxiety in children. The CFS will be administered immediately before the first procedure to establish a baseline and again immediately before the second procedure to measure anticipatory anxiety. The CFS score obtained prior to the second procedure will be included as a covariate in the analysis of behavioural outcomes to account for the potential influence of psychological carryover on the results.24 Figure 1 shows the study flowchart. Randomisation and blinding

The randomisation codes will be computer-generated using an online tool (https://www.sealedenvelope.com/simple-randomiser/v1/lists) by an independent biostatistician who is not involved in data management or analyses. Eligible patients will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio using block sizes of 2 and 4. The details of sequence allocation will be sealed in opaque envelopes and remain concealed until the conclusion of the study. Each envelope will be labelled with the study number, while ensuring that grouping information remains undisclosed. An independent research nurse will be responsible for opening the envelopes and preparing the medications accordingly. Propofol will be drawn into a 20 mL syringe. Esketamine will be diluted to a concentration of 1 mg/mL with physiological saline (0.9% NaCl). Remifentanil will be diluted to a concentration of 2 µg/mL with physiological saline (0.9% NaCl). The nurse will distribute the study medications (esketamine or remifentanil) in identical syringes labelled with study numbers only. It is impossible to visually distinguish the contents of these syringes, as both esketamine and remifentanil are clear and colourless solutions.

Study interventions

All paediatric patients will fast for 8 hours prior to the procedure and no premedication will be given. A researcher will screen patients the day before the procedure and meticulously document their baseline conditions, including status of drowsiness, nausea, perception, mood, heart rate (HR), non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP), and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2).

Before procedures, a trained nurse will provide psychological preparation using age-appropriate techniques, such as therapeutic play and distraction methods. From the moment the child enters the operating room until the completion of the entire procedure, continuous monitoring of ECG, HR, and SpO2 will be conducted. NIBP measurements will be taken at 3 min intervals. All children will receive supplemental oxygen at a flow of 2 L/min through a face mask. During the procedure, a comforting parent or guardian will remain present until the child loses consciousness, while a standardised and calming environment is maintained throughout. Intravenous sedation will be provided by the same anaesthesiologist. For all children in both study periods, intravenous propofol at a dose of 3 mg/kg will be administered to induce sedation. After induction, breathing condition is checked. If the child remains apneic for more than 30 s, manual ventilation via a face mask will be initiated to maintain SpO2 above 95% until spontaneous breathing resumes. All children are then placed in a left lateral ‘curled up’ position. Thereafter, children will be administered intravenous esketamine at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg or remifentanil at a dose of 1 µg/kg. Regardless of the experimental drug administered, the injection volume will be standardised to 0.5 mL/kg. After an equilibration period of 60 s, a BMA will be performed by inserting a needle into the anterior superior iliac spine. Thereafter, a LP will be conducted at the third and fourth lumbar intervertebral spaces. The level of sedation will be assessed using the Ramsey Sedation Scale score (RSS) every 30 s (table 2).20 26 When the patient moves or shows signs of awakening, propofol will be titrated in increments of 1 mg/kg to reach the target sedation level (RSS≥5, no response to a light glabellar tap or loud auditory stimulus) based on the anaesthesiologist’s judgement. If the patient still responds to painful stimuli during the procedure despite being at the target sedation level, an additional one-third of the initial dose of the study medication (esketamine or remifentanil) will be administered. In order to minimise the risk of side effects, the number of additional trial drugs should be restricted to a maximum of three. If the patient exhibits intolerance to the stimulation following three additional trial medications, they will be transitioned to inhaling sevoflurane via a face mask, and the case will be regarded as a ‘failed case’. The failure criterion is triggered only if, despite adequate baseline sedation (RSS≥5), the patient exhibits an involuntary reflex to the nociceptive stimulus, which is precisely what the study drug is intended to suppress.

During procedure and in the PACU, if desaturation events (SpO2<90%) occur, the jaw should be supported to ensure airway patency, and positive pressure ventilation via a mask should be initiated promptly. If these interventions fail to correct desaturation, tracheal intubation with subsequent positive pressure ventilation should be performed. Study outcomes

The primary endpoint of this trial is a safety outcome: the incidence of desaturation events during the sedation and recovery. Desaturation is defined as SpO2<90% during the period from administration of the experimental drug (either esketamine or remifentanil) until a modified Aldrete score of ≥9 is reached.

The secondary endpoints are categorised as follows:

Efficacy outcomes: (1) the interruption rate of the procedure. Interruption is defined as body movements making the operating physicians suspend the operation for anaesthetists administering rescue medication; (2) total dose of propofol; (3) the number of rescue doses of esketamine or remifentanil; (4) recovery time (from the end of procedure to a modified Aldrete score of ≥9 is reached); (5) distress behaviour, which is assessed using a modified scoring system wherein crying (0=not crying; 1=crying but responds to tender loving care; 2=crying and does not respond to tender loving care), movement (0=none; 1=restless; 2=thrashing), and agitation (0=patient asleep or calm; 1=mild agitation; 2=hysterical) are evaluated independently, yielding a maximum possible distress score of 6. This modified scale has been derived from the original tool developed and validated by Hannallah et al, which has been extensively used in paediatric anaesthesia research. Subsequent studies, including Glaisyer and Sury’s, have successfully applied this scoring system in paediatric oncology and perioperative settings, supporting its validation, reliability or appropriateness for the study population8 28 29; we recognise that other validated paediatric distress or emergence delirium scales, such as the Paediatric Anaesthesia Emergence Delirium (PAED) scale, are available. We chose the modified Hannallah scale for three reasons: its simplicity and feasibility in fast-paced haematologic-oncologic procedures; its established use in prior paediatric anaesthesia and sedation research; and its focus on observable distress behaviours: crying, movement, and agitation, which are most relevant to our patients and clinical workflow. We will conduct a prospective, nested substudy to evaluate the concurrent validity of the Hannallah scale and PAED score among first 20% of participants. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) >0.8 will be considered excellent concurrent validity of the Hannallah scale and PAED scale. The results of this substudy will be reported in the online supplemental materials.

Data management

Responsible researchers will systematically collect all original data, encompassing demographic information (age, sex, weight, height and BMI), baseline characteristics (status of drowsiness, nausea, perception, mood, ASA physical status, and baseline HR, NIBP, and SpO2), periprocedural data (CFS scores immediately before the first and second procedure, the interruption rate of the procedure; total dose of propofol; the number of times esketamine or remifentanil is added; recovery time; the time from the injection of esketamine or remifentanil to achieving a modified Aldrete score of ≥9; distress behaviour scores; PAED score from the first 20% of participants; a composite of sedation related adverse events; operator satisfaction; patient satisfaction; anaesthetist satisfaction). Recognising the potential for cumulative opioid tolerance to influence outcomes, the total number of prior procedural sedations involving opioids will be recorded for each participant and classified into three categories: 0, 1–5 or >5, based on established pharmacodynamic evidence.32 An independent researcher will meticulously document these findings in case report forms. Medical records, with all personally identifiable information anonymised, will be securely entered into a secure electronic database. On completion of the trial for all patients, the principal investigator and data manager will rigorously verify the completeness and accuracy of the recorded data. The data will then be imported into a designated format by the administrator and sent to a statistician for final statistical analyses.

Data and safety monitoring

An independent Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB), comprising a paediatric anaesthesiologist, an oncologist, and a biostatistician, will be established. Although this study does not include a formal interim analysis, the DSMB can recommend early termination of the trial for clear evidence of harm (desaturation, psychotomimetic events), with a stopping boundary of Haybittle-Peto p<0.001. This analysis will be performed by the study statistician for the DSMB on the completion of every 20 enrolled cases.

Data availability

Deidentified individual participant data collected during the trial, along with the study protocol, statistical analysis plan, and informed consent form, will be made available for legitimate research purposes under a controlled access policy. This approach safeguards participant privacy and ensures compliance with ethical standards for clinical research data. Data requests may be submitted starting 9 months after article publication and will be considered for up to 36 months thereafter. Requests should be submitted to the corresponding author (pengke0422@163.com) and will be approved only on receipt of a scientifically justified research proposal and a signed data access agreement.

Sample size estimation

We base our sample size estimations on primary outpoint. Keidan et al’s research on the use of propofol+remifentanil in paediatric haematologic-oncologic procedures has indicated that the frequency of desaturation events is approximately 20%.33 A study by Jeffrey S. Yu investigating esketamine as an adjunct to propofol in paediatric sedation showed a desaturation rate of 5%.2 Retrospective observational data from our own institution (unpublished), which compared propofol+esketamine (n=35) with propofol+remifentanil (n=19) in a similar paediatric population, showed desaturation rates of approximately 5.7% and 21% respectively. This indicates a reduction of approximately 75%. Assuming a desaturation incidence of 20% in the propofol+remifentanil regimen and a 75% reduction in desaturation events in the propofol+esketamine regimen, 72 participants will be required in a crossover-designed trial with α=0.05, 1-β = 0.8. Given a drop rate of estimate 10%, a total of 80 children will be needed. Sample size calculation will be performed with PASS software (V.19.0; IBM SPSS). A post hoc sensitivity power analysis will be conducted for the key secondary endpoint of psychotomimetic effects. Based on our retrospective data, the incidence of psychiatric adverse events is approximately 2%, regardless of whether patients receive remifentanil or esketamine. The non-inferiority margin (Δ) is set at 9%. The one-sided alpha level (type I error rate) is predefined as 0.025, and the statistical power (1−β) is set to 80%. Per-protocol analysis is employed, which is the standard approach for non-inferiority trials. Using these parameters, the required sample size is 78 (39 for each arm) assuming a dropout rate of 20%. Our finalised sample size is 80 patients with a randomised crossover design. Therefore, our study is well-powered to test the primary hypothesis of desaturation and the composite psychotomimetic endpoint.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables will be summarised as means±SDs or medians with IQRs where appropriate, and categorical variables will be summarised as numbers and percentages. The treatment effects will be analysed using ORs or mean difference with 95% CIs. Demographic data and baseline characteristics will be presented using descriptive statistics only. Following the standard crossover trial methodology, we will assess the treatment-by-period interaction, a statistical indicator of potential carryover effects, by incorporating a treatment×period interaction term into the initial model. A non-significant result (p>0.05, as prespecified) will suggest no meaningful carryover effect. If the interaction is significant, indicating potential carryover, we will conduct a sensitivity analysis using only first-period data, treating the trial as a parallel-group study. This secondary analysis will be presented alongside the primary crossover analysis to evaluate the robustness of our findings.

For the binary categorical variable, the difference between treatments will be compared using a random effects logistic regression model with intervention (propofol+esketamine or propofol+remifentanil), sequence, period, ASA status (III vs II), the time from drug administration to achieving a modified Aldrete score of ≥9, CFS score obtained prior to the second procedure and the categorised variable of total number of prior procedural sedations involving opioids as fixed effects and subject as a random effect. For continuous variable, the difference between treatments will be compared using analysis of variance or random effects linear model. Missing data are expected to be minimal due to the close monitoring inherent in sedation procedures. Nevertheless, should missing outcome data occur for the primary endpoint, we will perform multiple imputation as a sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of the complete-case analysis. Imputation will be applied to the primary outcome only, not to secondary outcomes or covariates.

To account for potential confounding effects of sedation depth, we will perform a subgroup analysis of the incidence of desaturation events (SpO₂ < 90%), stratifying the data by the maximum RSS score (5 vs 6). We will conduct a sensitivity analysis for the following key secondary endpoints: Total dose of propofol (mg/kg); Number of times the adjunct (esketamine/remifentanil) is added; Procedure interruption rate. For this analysis, we will exclude all procedures where the patient reaches an RSS of 6 at any point. By analysing this ‘RSS=5 only’ cohort, we can directly compare the propofol and adjunct requirements between groups under conditions of identical target sedation depth. If esketamine requires significantly less propofol than remifentanil in this ‘pure’ RSS=5 group, it provides strong, unconfounded evidence of its superior analgesic-sparing effect, unmasked by the effects of oversedation.

A hierarchical testing strategy will be employed to control the family-wise type I error rate across multiple endpoints in accordance with Food and Drug Administration Guidance on ‘Multiple Endpoints in Clinical Trials’. The endpoints will be evaluated in the following pre-specified sequence: (1) Primary endpoint: incidence of desaturation (assessed via a superiority test); (2) Key secondary endpoint: composite psychotomimetic effects (evaluated using a non-inferiority margin of 9%) and (3) All remaining secondary endpoints. Testing will proceed to the subsequent endpoint only on achieving statistical significance at the current level. For the composite psychotomimetic endpoint, a two-sided 95% CI for the risk difference will be calculated; non-inferiority will be concluded if the upper limit of this interval is below 9%. Statistical significance will be considered if p<0.05 (two-sided). Statistical analysis will be performed using SPSS (V.19.0; IBM SPSS).

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public do not participate in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination strategies of our research.

Discussion

This randomised crossover controlled trial will enrol a total of 80 paediatric patients to evaluate the effects of esketamine versus remifentanil as an adjuvant to propofol on the incidence of desaturation during haematologic-oncologic procedures. Additionally, we will compare the procedure interruption rate due to body movement, total dose of propofol administered, frequency of esketamine or remifentanil supplementation, recovery time, distress behaviour, and a composite of sedation-related adverse events between the two regimens. Sedation-related adverse events, including psychotomimetic effects such as hallucinations and nightmares, as well as fatigue, dizziness, headache, nausea, vomiting, bradycardia, and hypotension, are among the most commonly reported side effects associated with ketamine and esketamine in the existing literature.11,1418 We will also assess the satisfaction levels of the operator, patient and anaesthetist. The conduct and reporting of this trial will adhere to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines.34

Hilary and colleagues found that propofol and remifentanil achieved both immobility and recovery faster than sevoflurane and nitrous oxide for short paediatric oncology procedures in children. However, this technique is associated with a risk of cardiorespiratory depression, requiring specialised care from anaesthesiologists equipped with comprehensive resuscitation and monitoring systems.8 Respiratory depression is a well-documented side effect of remifentanil. Even with careful infusion, Keidan et al reported that 8 out of 41 children undergoing BMA experienced respiratory depression (respiratory rate <10 breaths/min or oxygen saturation <90%).33

Ketamine can be used for sedation, either alone or as an adjuvant to propofol. The incidence of transient hypoxaemia associated with ketamine use, depending on the specific study definition, ranges from 1% to 10%.11 Paulo and colleagues demonstrated that ketofol provided effective sedation, as evidenced by the high satisfaction levels reported among children undergoing procedural sedation and analgesia for BMA.12 Evans et al reported that 1.7% of paediatric patients experienced hypoxaemia (SpO 2<94%) during painful oncology procedures when using single-agent ketamine. Additionally, the reported incidence rates were 3.3% for bad dreams, 10.8% for vomiting and 4.2% for abnormal behaviour.11

As for esketamine, a recent study demonstrated that patients receiving esketamine for sedation during same-visit bidirectional endoscopy experienced an approximately 61% reduction in the incidence of desaturation and hypotension, along with a significant decrease in propofol requirements.19 Another randomised study revealed that a low-dose esketamine compared with alfentanil reduced the total dose of propofol during endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography, without impacting recovery, satisfaction or adverse events.18 Our research team previously established that the esketamine-propofol regimen provided effective sedation for paediatric 3 Tesla MRI, reduced the requirement for propofol and facilitated faster recovery, with no evidence of increased adverse effects.20 Wang et al demonstrated that a single 0.5 mg/kg dose of esketamine was generally safe and well tolerated in patients undergoing painless gastroscopy23; van de Bunt et al found that procedural sedation with esketamine at doses of 0.5–1.0 mg/kg appears to be safe for the reduction of intussusception in paediatric patients21; Patel et al provided evidence that fracture manipulation in children using esketamine at 1.0–1.5 mg/kg as procedural sedation is comparable in efficacy to other established methods.22 We conducted a systematic search of electronic databases (PubMed) from inception to October 25, 2025, for studies on esketamine for procedural sedation/anaesthesia in children (age≤18 years). We included all clinical trials (randomised and non-randomised) and observational studies that report on the use of intravenous esketamine for procedural sedation in children. Key data from each included study was extracted into a structured table, which is provided as online supplemental file 2. The role of esketamine in haematologic-oncologic procedures remains poorly defined. We evaluated the safety and efficacy of a 0.5 mg/kg dose of esketamine in paediatric patients with haematological malignancies, which falls within the dosage range reported in the aforementioned literature. To our knowledge, this will be the first randomised crossover, controlled study to evaluate whether esketamine as an adjunct to propofol-based sedation can reduce the incidence of respiratory complications during paediatric haematologic-oncologic procedures compared with the remifentanil and propofol regimen. A crossover design is chosen for this study instead of the more traditional randomised, parallel-group design because the within-patient variation is less than the between patient variation and thus required fewer patients. In addition, some of the known disadvantages of the crossover design (eg, larger dropout rate, instability of the patient’s condition and a potential carryover effect) are not anticipated in this study.

This study has several limitations that warrant acknowledgement. First, the sample size calculation was based on desaturation events rather than psychotomimetic events associated with esketamine. Respiratory depression and hypoxaemia are the most immediate, life-threatening risks during paediatric sedation, whereas psychotropic effects are typically transient and manageable with reassurance. Thus, maintaining adequate oxygenation and airway stability should be the primary focus of safety research. In randomised controlled trials, the primary endpoint should be objective and minimally prone to bias. Desaturation (SpO₂ < 90%) is a continuously monitored, quantitative and clear physiological measure. Psychotomimetic effects, however, are subjective, especially in children, and influenced by age, language ability and rater interpretation, leading to higher variability. An objective endpoint improves the validity and reliability of the main comparative analysis. For the composite psychotomimetic endpoint, we will conduct a non-inferiority test to evaluate treatment effects. The goal is to confirm that the respiratory benefits of esketamine are not achieved at the cost of an unacceptable increase in psychotomimetic adverse events. Second, patients younger than 6 years old, a considerable number of paediatric oncology patients, will be excluded from the study. The relevance of our findings will be strongest for school-aged children. This decision was made because children aged 6 to 12 represent a significant proportion of hospitalised leukaemia patients and are capable of clearly articulating their experiences with sedation. Given that the most common concerns regarding esketamine involve its cognitive-altering effects, this age group facilitates more accurate detection of any potential psychotropic side effects. Validated observational tools such as the COMFORT-Behavioural scale and the University of Michigan Sedation Scale for assessing sedation and behavioural state, the PAED scale for emergence behaviours and the FLACC (Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability) scale for differentiating pain-related agitation may be suitable for children under 6 years of age. However, to date, no validated observational tools are available for assessing the psychotomimetic profile beyond patients’ responses to follow-up questions. We plan to conduct a prospective, nested substudy to evaluate the concurrent validity of the Hannallah scale and PAED score among first 20% of participants. Third, this is a single-centre study carried out in a specific clinical setting. While this design ensures consistency in procedural protocols, sedation practices and outcome assessments, it may limit the generalisability of the findings. However, the results will provide critical evidence to guide the design and justification of a subsequent multicentre, pragmatic randomised controlled trial. We plan to initiate a 3 year, multicentre, randomised, parallel-group trial to address this issue following the completion of a 2 year preparatory phase. Such a larger-scale study would enable a comprehensive evaluation of the efficacy and safety of esketamine compared with remifentanil across diverse patient populations, clinical settings and institutional protocols. To ensure the robustness of behavioural assessments, a prospective validation substudy will be conducted in a nested cohort of the first 10 participants in our future multi-centred trial. Two independent, trained raters simultaneously assess participants during recovery. One rater administers the FLACC scale, while the other administers the PAED scale, with both raters blinded to each other’s evaluations. Reliability for each scale is assessed using the ICC, and concurrent validity between the FLACC and the PAED scale is evaluated through correlation analysis. Fourth, it is important to acknowledge that our sample size estimation is based on an expected 75% relative risk reduction in desaturation events, which indicates a large effect size. Although this assumption is supported by the pharmacological properties of esketamine and preliminary institutional data, we are aware that the actual treatment effect may be more moderate. If the actual reduction in desaturation events is smaller than expected, our study may lack the power to detect a statistically significant difference between the two regimens. In such a situation, a non-significant finding for the primary outcome would not definitely prove the equivalence between the treatments, and the results should be interpreted in light of this limitation.

Ethics and dissemination

The trial was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital of Soochow University (Approval No. 2025008) on 18 February 2025. This trial was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry on 10 March 2025 (identifier: ChiCTR2500098533; https://www.chictr.org.cn/bin/project/edit?pid=263748) before the first patient enrolment. The study protocol will require written informed consent from parents or legal guardians prior to enrolment (online supplemental file 3). In addition, for children, we will explicitly detail our process by an age-appropriate discussion (using tools like pictures or simple explanations) conducted by a trained clinician to obtain child assent (online supplemental file 4). To strengthen the autonomy and rights of paediatric participants, a child-friendly ‘right to withdraw’ card will be implemented. This card, incorporating age-appropriate illustrations and clear, simple language, will be provided directly to each child during the assent process. Children will be clearly informed that they may return the card to any member of the research team at any time to express their wish to pause or discontinue the procedure, or to withdraw from the study altogether, without facing any penalties or adverse effects on their clinical care. This tangible resource is intended to empower children by making their right to withdraw both practical and readily accessible. The card face and the back of the card are shown in figure 2. The results of this trial will be submitted for peer review and publication in a scientific journal.
