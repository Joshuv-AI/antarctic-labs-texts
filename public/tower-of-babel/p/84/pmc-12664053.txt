Compositional analysis of amino acids, fatty acids, minerals, and aroma compounds in buffalo worm (Alphitobius diaperinus) larval powder and recovery of bioactive compounds via ultrasound-assisted extraction
Authors: Aleksandra Purkiewicz, Gulsah Karabulut, Małgorzata Tańska, Eva Ivanišová, Ľuboš Harangozo, Małgorzata Starowicz, Renata Pietrzak-Fiećko, Bożena Łozowicka, Piotr Kaczyński, Gulden Goksen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to characterize the nutritional composition of the Alphitobius diaperinus larvae powder (protein and lipid contents, mineral profile, amino acid and fatty acid composition, and volatile aroma compounds) and to evaluate its bioactive potential by comparing the efficiency of phenolic and flavonoid compound extraction using ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) and conventional methods under varying conditions. The results showed that the insect powder exhibits high nutritional value, with protein and fat contents of 44.23 % and 26.87 %. A total of 36 volatile compounds were identified (total content of 621.97 μg/g), with 4-methylbenzaldehyde being the most abundant (179.60 μg/g). Increasing ultrasound power to 600 W for 10 min significantly enhanced the total phenolic content (129.00 ± 1.58 mg CE/100 mL) compared to lower power settings. For flavonoids, improved extraction efficiency was already observed at 450 W under the same time (40.22 ± 0.26 mg CE/100 mL). Antioxidant capacity, assessed via DPPH and ORAC assays, progressively increased with higher ultrasound intensity and prolonged extraction time, reaching the highest values at 450–600 W for 10 min (for DPPH: 1177.06 ± 4.93 μM Trolox/100 mL of extract, 1187.41 ± 2.47 μM Trolox/100 mL of extract, for U450–10 and U600–10; for ORAC: 2208.84 ± 2.05 μM Trolox/100 mL of extract, 2228.28 ± 1.83 μM Trolox/100 mL of extract, for U450–10 and U600–10). These findings confirm that UAE is a highly efficient technique for extracting phenolic and flavonoid compounds from A. diaperinus larvae powder. Therefore, A. diaperinus larvae powder, with its rich nutritional composition, diverse mineral content, and complex volatile profile, represents a promising ingredient for the development of functional foods with enhanced biological value.Keywords: Edible insect, Lesser mealworm, Extraction efficiency, Antioxidant capacity, Essential fatty acids

Introduction

The accelerating global population growth, coupled with environmental degradation and the need for sustainable food systems, has prompted intense interest in alternative protein sources that are both nutritionally adequate and environmentally sustainable (Karabulut et al., 2024). Among the most promising candidates are edible insects, which have been traditionally consumed in many cultures and are now gaining scientific and regulatory recognition worldwide (Carvalho et al., 2025, Gharibzahedi and Altintas, 2023, Ojha, Bußler, Psarianos, Rossi and Schlüter, 2021). Compared to conventional livestock such as poultry, pigs, and cattle, insects are more efficient at converting feed into edible biomass, require substantially less water and land, and produce fewer greenhouse gas emissions (Ojha, Bekhit, et al., 2021; Reilly et al., 2022). For example, the production of 1 kg of protein from insects uses approximately 1.5 times less water than poultry, 2.5 times less than pork, and 5 times less than beef, while land use can be 14 times lower than for beef (Liceaga, 2022; Ros-Baró et al., 2022). Furthermore, insects show higher feed conversion efficiency (FCE), with values such as 1.7 for insects vs. 5.0 and 10.0 for pork and beef, respectively (Dobermann et al., 2017). However, FCE values can vary considerably depending on the insect species and life stage (Otero et al., 2020).

One insect species of growing interest is the buffalo worm (Alphitobius diaperinus), a member of the Tenebrionidae family, also known as the lesser mealworm (Fuso et al., 2024; Kurečka et al., 2021; Rivas-Navia et al., 2025). It has traditionally been considered an agricultural pest but is now recognized as a valuable source of high-quality nutrients and bioactive compounds (Mazurek et al., 2023; Rumpold & Schlüter, 2015). A. diaperinus larvae thrive in organic-rich environments such as poultry farms and composting systems and are currently under investigation for their roles in waste bioconversion, protein recovery, and extraction of chitin and phenolic compounds (Rivas-Navia et al., 2025; Kurečka et al., 2021; Fuso et al., 2024). In a significant regulatory development, the European Commission approved A. diaperinus larvae for use as a novel food in dried, powdered, and frozen forms under Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2023/58, effective January 5, 2023 (European Commission, 2023; van Huis, 2023). This decision marks a turning point for the commercial use of insects in food and feed systems, reinforcing their potential as safe, functional, and sustainable ingredients (Tufen-Cetin & Cetin, 2025).

Nutritionally, A. diaperinus larvae have been shown to contain 40–60 % protein and 13–25 % lipids on a dry matter basis, depending on their developmental stage, diet, and rearing conditions (Oonincx & Finke, 2021; Soetemans et al., 2020). The protein fraction includes all essential amino acids in varying concentrations, while the lipid profile is rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), especially linoleic acid, which can comprise over 27 % of total fatty acids (Tańska et al., 2024). In addition to macronutrients, buffalo worm larvae are also valuable sources of micronutrients, including vitamins B6 and B12, sterols, and phospholipids, with documented health-promoting effects such as antioxidants, anti-inflammatory, and ACE-inhibitory activity (Carvalho et al., 2025; Gharibzahedi & Altintas, 2023; Tejada et al., 2022).

While the nutritional potential of edible insects is well established, their bioactive properties are increasingly coming into focus, particularly in relation to oxidative stress and chronic disease prevention. Edible insects are a source of phenolic compounds (0.08–5 g gallic acid equivalent/100 g depending on insect species) (Aiello et al., 2023) (quercetin, kaempferol, luteolin, salicylic acid, vanillic acid, ferulic acid, isovitexin, tricin, orientin) (Ruiz et al., 2025). They also contain bioactive peptides (antihypertensive, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial) (Ferrazzano et al., 2023). Their lipid fractions (13.41–33.40 g/100 g depending on the insect species) (Rumpold & Schlüter, 2013) include triacylglycerols, mono- and diacylglycerols, cholesterol, phytosterols, free fatty acids, and phospholipids (Tańska et al., 2024). These compounds exhibit antioxidant capacity comparable to that of fruits, vegetables, and algae (Aiello et al., 2023; Pyo et al., 2020). However, the content and efficacy of these compounds vary significantly based on species, developmental stage, and extraction method (de la Luz Sánchez-Estrada et al., 2024). Moreover, the aroma profile of edible insects, governed by volatile compounds, plays a crucial role in consumer acceptance and product integration. The taste of edible insects is usually described as buttery, sweetish, herbal, or crunchy, but their flavor profile depends on the species, stage of development, diet, and method of preparation, which significantly affects chemical changes and the final sensory experience (Park, Shin and Choi, 2024, Żołnierczyk and Szumny, 2021). Despite a growing number of studies on the nutritional and functional value of edible insects, few studies adopt an integrated approach that combines analyses of bioactive content, antioxidant capacity, nutritional value, and volatile aroma compounds (Perez-Santaescolastica et al., 2023; Reale et al., 2023).

In this context, ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) has emerged as a green and efficient technique for isolating bioactive compounds from food matrices, including insects. UAE enhances mass transfer and cellular structures and membranes disruption through acoustic cavitation, resulting in higher extraction efficiency, shorter processing times, and reduced solvent usage compared to conventional methods (Del Hierro et al., 2020; Otero et al., 2020). In recent years, the potential of UAE as a method enabling effective recovery of bioactive components from insect matrices while preserving their structure and biological activity has also been highlighted (Jiang et al., 2025; Mokaya et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2018). Recent applications of UAE in insect research have demonstrated its effectiveness in extracting phenolic compounds, fatty acids, and proteins from Tenebrio molitor, Acheta domesticus, and Bombay locusts (Kingwascharapong et al., 2021; Muñoz-Seijas et al., 2025). However, no comprehensive studies have yet reported the use of UAE for extracting bioactive compounds from A. diaperinus larvae, underscoring a significant gap in literature.

In addition to UAE, there are numerous modern and environmentally friendly methods for obtaining bioactive compounds, including supercritical fluid extraction (SFE), microwave-assisted extraction (MAE), enzyme-assisted extraction, extraction assisted by pulsed electric field, and the utilization of so-called “green” solvents (Chaves et al., 2020; Marianaccio et al., 2024; Marianaccio et al., 2025). Each of these techniques is characterized by different mechanisms of action, efficiency, and impact on the chemical composition of the extract. Nevertheless, according to the existing literature, UAE remains the most widely applied technique and has proven to be particularly efficient for extracting bioactive compounds. Furthermore, it is a highly efficient, easy-to-use, time-effective method that requires smaller amounts of solvent (Pan et al., 2022), and at the same time has a wide range of applications in the literature on biologically active components of edible insects (Gharibzahedi & Altintas, 2022; Jiang et al., 2025; Kingwascharapong et al., 2021; Mokaya et al., 2024).

Beyond improving extraction efficiency, UAE can also alter the molecular conformation and functional properties of proteins through cavitation-induced mechanical and thermal effects. The rapid collapse of cavitation bubbles generates intense shear forces and localized hotspots, which can partially unfold protein molecules, disrupt intramolecular hydrogen bonds, and expose hydrophobic and reactive sites (Liu et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2023; Goksen, 2023). These structural rearrangements may lead to decreased α-helix and increased β-sheet or random coil content, affecting protein solubility, emulsifying capacity, and antioxidant activity (Justino et al., 2024). Consequently, UAE not only enhances the release of phenolic and lipid compounds but can also modulate protein structure and bioactivity, offering additional functionality to the resulting extracts.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to comprehensively investigate the bioactive and nutritional composition of A. diaperinus larvae powder, with special emphasis on the efficiency of ultrasound-assisted extraction of phenolic and flavonoid compounds. The influence of extraction parameters (ultrasound power and duration) on antioxidant capacity and phenolic content was evaluated. In parallel, the proximate composition, amino acid and fatty acid profiles, mineral content, and volatile compounds in the A. diaperinus larvae powder were characterized to provide knowledge about understanding the value of larvae as a food with enhanced biological value ingredient.

Material and methods

Chemicals

The larvae of the buffalo worm (Alphitobius diaperinus) were obtained in powdered form from a commercial online supplier in 2023, Isaac Nutrition GmbH, located in Köln, Germany. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), sodium thiosulfate, and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were purchased from Chempur (Piekary Śląskie, Poland). Standards of the individual amino acids – alanine, arginine, asparagine, aspartic acid, cysteine, glutamic acid, glutamine, glycine, histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, proline, serine, threonine, tryptophan, tyrosine, and valine – were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, USA). Nitric acid (HNO3), formic acid, chloroform, ethyl alcohol, methyl alcohol, sodium chloride (NaCl), Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, sodium nitrite (NaNO₂), hexahydrate aluminum chloride (AlCl₃·6H₂O), 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 2,2′-azobis(2-methylpropionamidine) dihydrochloride (AAPH) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Proximate composition

Moisture and ash content were determined using AACC Method 44–19.01 and 08–01.01 (AACC, 1996), respectively. Fat content was measured by Soxhlet extraction according to AACC Method 30–25.01. Crude protein was determined using the semi-micro Kjeldahl method, with a nitrogen-to-protein conversion factor of 4.7, as described by Boulos et al. (2020).

Mineral composition

The analysis of mineral compounds was performed using a Flame Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer (Varian model AA 240 FS, Australia) equipped with a D2 lamp background correction system using an air-acetylene flame (air 13.5 L/min, acetylene 2.0 L/min, Varian, Ltd., Australia), described by Manditsera et al. (2019). The measured results were compared with a multielemental standard for GF AAS (CertiPUR®, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). A 1 g sample was digested with a mixture of HNO3 and distilled water (1:1). Samples were digested in a closed vessel, high-pressure microwave digester (MARS X-press, Matthews, NC, USA) for 55 min. After cooling to room temperature, the suspension was filtered through Munktell filter paper (grade 390.84 g/m2, Bärenstein, Germany) and diluted to 50 mL with distilled water. Then, the sample extracts were subsequently analyzed for mineral compounds using a fast sequential atomic absorption spectrophotometer.

Amino acid content

The analysis of the amino acid content was carried out using the gradient high-performance liquid chromatography technique described by Ryniewicz et al. (2020) and Łozowicka et al. (2021) with minor modifications. Stock amino acid solutions were prepared in 1 % formic acid in water at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. Individual stock standard solutions were used to prepare a standard mixture in the concentration range of 0.01–10 μg/mL. After homogenizing, the samples were placed in polypropylene tubes and extracted with water:methanol (80:20, v:v) with 0.1 % formic acid, using the QuEChERS (Quick, Easy, Cheap, Effective, Rugged, and Safe) technique. Prepared extracts were vortexed for 5 min and then were transferred to tubes and centrifuged for 10 min at 13000 xg (5810R Eppdendorf AG). Then, the resulting supernatants were filtered through a syringe filter into vials. The amino acid content was identified and calculated based on the standard. For analysis, 2 μL of sample extract was injected into the following analytical columns: KINETEX HILIC 1.7 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm (Phenomenex); (ii) KINETEX RP-C18 2.6 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm (Phenomenex); (iii) Hypercarb 5.0 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) coupled with an Eksigent Ultra LC-100 liquid chromatograph (Eksigent Technologies, Dublin, CA, USA). Separation was performed at 40 °C, with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min, using the following mobile phase system: A – water with 0.2 % formic acid and 20 mM ammonium formate, B – acetonitrile, according to the respective elution gradients. Analysis was performed using a 6500 QTRAP mass spectrometer (AB Sciex Instruments, Foster City, CA, USA) with electrospray ionization (ESI, positive mode). All amino acids were determined in multiple reaction monitoring mode (MRM).

Fatty acid profile

Lipid extraction from the powder was performed following the method of Folch et al. (1959), using a chloroform:ethanol mixture (2:1, v/v). The fatty acids contained in crude fat were esterified to produce fatty acid methyl esters (FAME) with a mixture of chloroform, methanol and sulfuric acid (100:100:1) according to method of Peisker (1964). Chromatographic separation was carried out using a gas chromatograph (Hewlett-Packard 6890, Waldbronn, Germany) with a flame ionization detector (FID) and a 100 m-long Supelcowax 10 capillary column with an internal diameter of 0.25 mm; liquid phase of Supelcowax 10 (Supelco, Sigma Aldrich subsidiary, St. Louis, MO, USA), layer thickness of 0.25 μm. The fatty acid methyl esters were identified based on their retention times that were compared with those specified for the mixture of fatty acid methyl esters in a standard mixture of Supelco 37 Component FAME (10 mg/mL in methylene chloride). ChemStation software (Agilent, Alpharetta, GA, USA) was used to calculate the percentage of fatty acids.

Volatile organic compounds

Headspace solid phase microextraction (HS-SPME) was performed according to the method presented by Goksen (2023). 1 g of buffalo worm powder and 2 mL of saturated NaCl solution were homogenized in an ultrasonic bath for 10 min. Then, the internal standard was added to the mixture. DVB/Carboxen/PDMS fiber (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was used for extraction of volatile compounds with the following conditions for headspace sampling: incubated at 60 °C for 15 min and then extracted at 60 °C for 45 min. Volatile compounds were trapped at 250 °C for 2 min in an injection port, then washed into the column. Determination of volatile components was carried out using gas chromatography (GC) technique with the use of an Agilent Technologies 7890 A chromatograph (Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies 5975C, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Agilent Technologies 7890 A chromatograph (Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies 5975C, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The temperature of the column was raised from 40 °C to 240 °C at a rate of 4 °C per minute, with a final standby at 240 °C for 5 min. Helium was adjusted to a constant flow with the rate of 1.0 mL/min. The injector and detector were set at 250 °C. The MS (electronic impact ionization) criteria were described in the following: mass range m/z of 30–4500, MS ionization electron energy of 70 eV. The constituents were assessed by comparing their mass spectra on the DB-Wax column using commercial aroma spectra databases on the Wiley 7.0 and NIST 2005 Mass Spectral Library. d4-pyrazine was used as an internal standard to determine the quantification.

Preparation of insect powder extracts

Two extraction methods, ultrasonic and conventional, were used to prepare the bioactive extracts. The preparation of extracts was as follows: 1.25 g of the insect powder was weighed, transferred to 50 mL centrifuge tubes, and extracted with 12.5 mL of 80 % ethanol. The selection of an insect powder-to-solvent ratio of 1:10 (g/mL) and the use of 80 % (v/v) ethanol as the extraction solvent were guided by previously published studies on the preparation of edible insect extracts (Ivanišová et al., 2023; Nino et al., 2021; Predescu et al., 2016). Based on the literature, a solid-to-solvent ratio of 1:10 has been indicated as the most effective for the extraction of phenolic compounds and flavonoids, as it provides an optimal balance between extraction efficiency and solvent consumption. A higher solvent amount relative to the solid material increases the concentration gradient between the solid and liquid phases, thereby enhancing the diffusion of phenolic compounds into the solvent. At the same time, a ratio of 1:10 is sufficient to achieve near-maximum extraction efficiency without excessive dilution or unnecessary increases in process cost and solvent volume (Predescu et al., 2016). Different powers (150, 300, 450, and 600 W) and extraction times (5 and 10 min) were used for ultrasound extraction (Sonics, 750 W, USA), resulting in 8 extracts, coded as U150/450/600–5/10 (U – ultrasound). Each UAE extract was prepared in duplicate. Extracts were centrifuged for 10 min at 13000 xg (5810R Eppendorf AG, Hamburg, Germany). Conventional extracts were obtained by conventional shaking (5 and 10 min) and centrifugation, resulting in two different extracts coded as C5/10 (C – conventional). Each of the extracts was obtained in liquid form. Three parallel extracts were prepared for each extraction condition. The extracts obtained were stored at −40 °C until analysis.

Determination of total phenolic content

For the determination of total phenolic content (TPC), 0.5 mL of the extract was transferred into a 10 mL volumetric flask. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (diluted 1:2 with distilled water) was added and mixed thoroughly. This was followed by the addition of 3 mL of 14 % sodium carbonate solution, and the mixture was made up to volume with distilled water and mixed again. The resulting solutions were incubated in the dark for 60 min at room temperature. After incubation, absorbance was measured at 720 nm using a spectrophotometer (FLUOstar OMEGA, BMG LABTECH, Hamburg, Germany). A reagent blank, in which the extract was replaced with 80 % ethanol, was also prepared and analyzed. TPC was calculated from a catechin calibration curve and expressed as mg catechin equivalent (CE) per 100 mL of extract (Del Hierro et al., 2020).

Determination of total flavonoid content

Total flavonoid content (TFC) was determined following the method described by Herald et al. (2012) and Yafang et al. (2011), with slight modifications. Briefly, 1 mL of the sample extract was mixed with 4 mL of deionized water in a 15 mL polypropylene test tube. Subsequently, 0.3 mL of 5 % NaNO₂ solution was added, and the mixture was allowed to stand for 5 min. Then, 0.6 mL of 10 % AlCl₃·6H₂O solution was added, followed by a 6-min incubation. Finally, 2 mL of 1 M NaOH and 2 mL of deionized water were added to complete the reaction. The final mixture was centrifuged at 13,000 ×g for 10 min using a 5810R Eppendorf centrifuge (Eppendorf AG, Hamburg, Germany). The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 415 nm using a spectrophotometer (FLUOstar OMEGA, BMG LABTECH, Hamburg, Germany). A reagent blank containing 80 % ethanol instead of the sample extract was also prepared. The flavonoid content was calculated from a catechin standard calibration curve and expressed as mg CE/100 mL of extract.

Determination of antioxidant capacity by DPPH radical scavenging activity

The DPPH method was performed according to the procedure described by Yang et al. (2014). DPPH stock solution was prepared by dissolving 27.8 mg of DPPH in 200 mL of methanol to obtain a final concentration of 0.35 mM. To conduct the spectrophotometric test, 100 μL of the extract was examined, and 900 μL of DPPH solution was mixed. The mixture was left for 16 min in the dark at room temperature (approximately 25 °C). Absorbance at 515 nm was measured using a spectrophotometer (FLUOstar OMEGA BGM LAB Tech) for reagent testing. The reagent sample contains 80 % ethanol solution instead of the extract being tested. A calibration curve was prepared using Trolox standard solutions ranging from 25 to 800 μM, and the antioxidant capacity of the tested samples was expressed as μM Trolox/100 mL of extract.

Determination of antioxidant capacity by ORAC test

Antioxidant capacity was determined using the oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC) assay, following the method of Huang et al. (2002) with slight modifications. Briefly, 25 μL of the extract was mixed with 150 μL of 10 nM fluorescein solution prepared in PBS (pH 7.4). After incubation for 15 min at 37 °C, 25 μL of 240 nM AAPH in PBS was added to initiate the reaction. Fluorescence (excitation at 485 nm, emission at 520 nm) was measured every 90 s intervals for 6 h. To process the results, the absorbance of the reagent sample was also measured. The reagent sample contained 80 % ethanol solution instead of the extract being tested. The ORAC values were calculated by determining the area under the fluorescence decay curve (AUC) and comparing it to the Trolox standard curve (6.25–100 μM). The antioxidant capacity of the analyzed samples was expressed as μM Trolox/100 mL, compared to a standard curve prepared for various concentrations of the Trolox solution.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in triplicate. The experimental results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. One-way ANOVA and Tukey's post hoc test were conducted at a 95 % confidence level, utilizing the SPSS 20.0 software package (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA).

Results and discussion

Proximate composition

The proximate analysis of buffalo worm powder indicated a high protein concentration, representing 44.23 % of its total mass (Table 1). A. diaperinus larvae are considered a rich source of protein, the amount of which ranges from 45.10 % to 50.54 % of their dry weight (Rumbos et al., 2019), though their protein content is influenced by various factors, such as diet, developmental stage, sex, species, environmental conditions, and analytical methods used (Kotsou et al., 2021; Janssen, Vincken, van den Broek, Fogliano and Lakemond, 2017, Skotnicka, Karwowska, Kłobukowski, Borkowska and Pieszko, 2021). Similarly, another study reported the protein content of buffalo worms as 49.51 % (Mazurek et al., 2023) and 59.55 % (Tańska et al., 2024). In terms of fat content, buffalo worm powder showed a fat concentration of 26.87 %, similar to studies by Sun et al. (2021) (27.9 %) and Müller Richli et al. (2023) (28.7 %). Lipid content in A. diaperinus ranges from 13 to 29 %, with an average content of around 24 % (Kurečka et al., 2021; Vrabec et al., 2015). In general, larvae and pupae tend to have higher fat content compared to adult insects (Mlcek et al., 2014). The ash content of buffalo worm powder was 3.14 %, while data in the literature indicate a content of 2–3 % (Soetemans et al., 2020). The ash of A. diaperinus primarily contains calcium, phosphorus, and potassium, which are essential for the proper functioning of skeletal, nervous, and muscular systems, as well as maintaining water and electrolyte balance (Riekkinen et al., 2022). It also includes trace elements such as iron, zinc, and copper, which are crucial for various biological processes (Siddiqui et al., 2024). Carbohydrate levels were relatively low, at 19.49 %, which is typical for insect-derived materials, as they generally contain less carbohydrate than fruits, vegetables, or whole grains. However, other studies have reported lower crude fiber content, typically ranging from 5 % to 7 % of dry weight (Skotnicka et al., 2021). The carapace of A. diaperinus contains chitin as a primarily structural component, whereas carbohydrates are mainly stored as glycogen, serving as an energy reserve (Abidin et al., 2020). Although A. diaperinus is not a significant source of fiber, it can contribute to daily fiber intake when consumed as part of a balanced diet (Siddiqui et al., 2024). This nutritional profile underscores the potential of buffalo worm powder as an ingredient for enhancing protein content and overall nutritional value in food products, making it a promising component in the development of nutrient-rich formulations.

Mineral composition

The mineral composition of buffalo worms revealed a diverse and nutritionally significant profile, with concentrations that can contribute significantly to daily mineral requirements, as detailed in Table 2. Buffalo worm powder exhibited the highest concentrations of potassium, sodium, calcium, and phosphorus among the minerals analyzed. When compared to the findings of Janssen et al. (2019), the present study reported similar levels of copper (17.9 ± 0.16 mg/kg vs. 21.9 ± 0.4 mg/kg), iron (48.7 ± 1.03 mg/kg vs. 53.5 ± 1.7 mg/kg), and manganese (4.7 ± 0.13 mg/kg vs. 5.4 ± 0.3 mg/kg). However, the zinc and phosphorus levels were considerably lower in the current study, with zinc and phosphorus content being 59.3 mg/kg and 2334 mg/kg lower, respectively, than those reported by Janssen et al. (2019). Notable variation in phosphorus content was also observed between studies, indicating potential influences from factors such as diet, rearing conditions, or analytical methodology. Previous research has highlighted that A. diaperinus larvae may contain higher iron and zinc levels than those found in beef (Latunde-Dada et al., 2016) and higher calcium levels than those in milk (Oonincx et al., 2015), underscoring their nutritional potential as a mineral-rich food source.

The potassium was the most abundant mineral in buffalo worm powder, at 7050 mg/kg. Considering the recommended daily intake for adults is 4700 mg/day, a small portion of the powder could easily fulfill or exceed daily potassium requirements. These make it an excellent source of this crucial electrolyte involved in muscle function, nerve signaling, and fluid balance. The phosphorus content was also high at 6066 mg/kg, compared to the recommended daily intake of 700 mg/day. This highlights its potential in supporting bone health, energy metabolism, and cellular functions, as a moderate portion of this powder could meet or exceed the daily phosphorus requirement (Linus Pauling Institute's Micronutrient Center). The sodium was present at 1513.2 mg/kg, exceeding the recommended daily intake of less than 1500 mg/day. Although the sodium is vital for fluid balance and nerve function, excessive intake could contribute to health issues such as hypertension, suggesting moderation in sodium-containing food formulations (Linus Pauling Institute's Micronutrient Center). The calcium content, at 1490.1 mg/kg, approached the daily recommendation of 1300 mg/day, supporting the role of entomophagy in promoting bone and dental health. The magnesium was found at 788.7 mg/kg, and with a recommended daily intake of 220–260 mg/day in adults, even moderate consumption of this powder could contribute significantly to meeting the magnesium requirements necessary for over 300 enzymatic reactions, including those involved in energy production and DNA synthesis. The zinc content of 81.3 mg/kg was considerable when compared to the recommended daily intake of 3.0–14.0 mg/day. This level of zinc can play an important role in supporting immune function, protein synthesis, and wound healing. The iron concentration of 48.7 mg/kg was also significant compared to the recommended intake of 7.5–58.8 mg/day, indicating that this powder could be a potentially effective source for preventing anemia and supporting oxygen transport in the body. The copper and manganese were present at 17.9 mg/kg and 4.7 mg/kg, respectively. Given that the daily requirement for copper is 0.9–1.3 mg/day and for manganese it is 1.8–2.6 mg/day, the buffalo worm powder provided more than adequate amounts of these trace minerals, both of which are important for enzymatic reactions, including antioxidant defense and iron metabolism.

Amino acid content

The amino acid profile of buffalo worm revealed a nutritionally valuable composition, with notable levels of both essential (4) and non-essential (9) amino acids (Table 3). Glutamic acid (655.39 mg/kg) is the most abundant, supporting neurotransmission and protein synthesis, while glutamine (52.74 mg/kg) contributes to muscle recovery and intestinal health. Essential amino acids such as leucine (30.79 mg/kg), lysine (49.63 mg/kg), and tryptophan (42.98 mg/kg) were present in significant quantities, highlighting buffalo worm's potential to support muscle protein synthesis, immune function, and cognitive health. Leucine and valine (33.14 mg/kg), as branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), promote muscle repair and growth. Among the essential amino acids, A. diaperinus larvae are richest in lysine and leucine, which are deficient in many plant protein sources (Sánchez-Muros et al., 2014). However, several essential amino acids, including methionine, phenylalanine, and threonine, were not detected, indicating that buffalo worms may need to be combined with other protein sources to provide a complete amino acid profile. The deficiency of these amino acids in A. diaperinus larvae is significant from the point of view of protein completeness. This protein cannot be considered complete if consumed as the sole source of protein. However, due to its high content of lysine and leucine – amino acids that are often deficient in cereals and legumes – A. diaperinus can be a complementary component in the diet (Tang et al., 2019, Nachtigall et al., 2025; Meyer-Rochow et al., 2021). On the other hand, combining insect protein with products rich in methionine and phenylalanine (e.g., cereals, sesame seeds, or nuts) allows for a more complete amino acid profile, increasing the nutritional value of the entire meal (Dodevska et al., 2022). Available studies indicate significant differences in the amino acid content of A. diaperinus larvae, evaluated by different amino acid determination methodologies, as well as feed composition and culture conditions, among other factors (Siddiqui et al., 2024). In contrast to our study, Rumbos et al. (2019), Hermans et al. (2021), and Kurečka et al. (2021) report the presence of other essential amino acids. In all the cited studies, the content of glutamic acid was at the highest level, indicating a common feature in the amino acid profile of buffalo worm larvae. Fluctuations in the amino acid content of A. diaperinus larvae may affect the overall assessment of their nutritional value and use in the food industry (Siddiqui et al., 2024). Considering the recommended daily intake of tryptophan (4–6 mg/day) (World Health Organization, 2024) for adults, a small amount of insect powder helps meet the need for this amino acid. Buffalo worms contained considerable quantities of hydrophobic amino acids like leucine, alanine, proline, and valine, alongside the basic amino acid lysine, which has been noted for its antioxidative properties (Navarro del Hierro et al., 2021). For either leucine, lysine, or valine, buffalo worm powder did not provide a good source of these amino acids, and meeting the daily requirement for this component would only be achieved with consumption of about 100 g of powder. The amino acid profile of buffalo worm powder demonstrated a high glutamic acid concentration at 655.39 mg/kg, indicative of its significant potential as a natural umami flavor enhancer. While other amino acids such as asparagine (65.55 mg/kg) and alanine (22.54 mg/kg) were present and may contribute to flavor complexity, their direct impact on umami is less pronounced. The absence of aspartic acid and other umami-related amino acids suggested that glutamic acid was the primary determinant of the umami taste profile. Compared to other protein sources like soy and whey, buffalo worm offered comparable levels of leucine, lysine, and tryptophan, making it a suitable alternative for supporting various physiological processes. Although it lacked certain essential amino acids, its rich content in key amino acids positions it as a viable ingredient for enhancing dietary protein intake. In a study, the most abundant of the essential amino acids of Bombay locusts were reported as arginine, leucine, and histidine, respectively (Kingwascharapong et al., 2021). The amino acid composition of edible insects differs largely among species and orders. However, high amino acid values have been found for phenylalanine + tyrosine in some species, and some insects are rich in tryptophan, lysine, and threonine (Rumpold & Schlüter, 2015). Despite its notable protein content, the calculated amino acid scores (AAS) for buffalo worm powder were below 1 % for most essential amino acids (e.g., leucine, valine), except tryptophan, which showed an AAS of 2.0 %. This suggests that while buffalo worm powder contains valuable amino acids, it should be complemented with other protein sources, including legumes, grains as well as animal proteins, like eggs and whey protein, to meet the full spectrum of essential amino acid requirements.

Fatty acid profile

The lipid composition of edible insects consists mainly of triacylglycerols, with small amounts of mono- and diacylglycerols, cholesterol, phytosterols, free fatty acids, and phospholipids (Tańska et al., 2024). The fatty acid profile of buffalo worms demonstrated a balanced distribution of saturated (SFA), monounsaturated (MUFA), and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), contributing to their nutritional potential (Table 4). The total SFA content was 35.72 %, primarily composed of palmitic acid (25.33 %) and stearic acid (8.89 %), which serve as energy sources but may pose cardiovascular risks when consumed in excess. The MUFA content, at 33.21 %, was primarily composed of oleic acid (31.23 %), a heart-healthy fatty acid known for its positive effects on cholesterol levels. The PUFA content was 31.00 %, with linoleic acid (LA, 29.10 %) as the predominant omega-6 fatty acid, which is essential for cell function but should be balanced with omega-3 intake (Gharibzahedi & Altintas, 2022). Alpha-linolenic acid (ALA, 1.90 %), a key omega-3 fatty acid, further enhances the nutritional profile, contributing to anti-inflammatory and cardiovascular health benefits (Otero et al., 2020). Besides LA and ALA, no other PUFAs such as eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) were detected in A. diaperinus (Rumbos et al., 2019). The n6/n3 ratio in A. diaperinus larvae was 15.32:1, which is significantly higher than the recommended optimal ratio of these acids in the human diet (approximately 4:1) (Mendez-Sanchez et al., 2024), but lower than that obtained by Mattioli et al. (2023) – 53.84:1. This is mainly due to the lack of long-chain fatty acids (PUFAs) – arachidonic acid (AA), DHA, and EPA – in A. diaperinus larvae. Available data indicate that edible insects have a limited ability to elongate LA and ALA into long-chain derivatives, resulting in the accumulation of fatty acids from the culture medium (Mattioli et al., 2023). Oonincx et al. (2015) report that if insects are to be an integral part of a sustainable and nutritious diet, it would be beneficial for their lipid profile to be characterized by a relatively high content of n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids, which would promote a more favorable n6/n3 ratio. An excessively high proportion of omega-6 acids in the diet, and thus a high n6/n3 ratio, can lead to chronic inflammation and poorer health, and promotes the development of cardiovascular disease (Palupi et al., 2025). Similar to the present study, Gharibzahedi and Altintas (2023) reported that the predominant fatty acids in A. diaperinus powders are linoleic acid (33.66 %), oleic acid (28.97 %), palmitic acid (24.98 %), and stearic acid (7.23 %). Roncolini et al. (2020) reported comparable results, whereas Tańska et al. (2024) noted a slightly higher stearic acid content (9.12 %). According to the available data, the levels of individual fatty acids in A. diaperinus powders exhibit substantial variability with SFA ranging from 27.6 to 40.6 %, MUFA from 21.3 to 44.0 %, and PUFA from 18.4 to 40.8 % (Rumbos et al., 2019). The fatty acid composition of edible insects is known to vary depending on environmental conditions, diet, developmental stage, and sex (Udomsil et al., 2019).

Volatile compounds

The volatile compound profile of A. diaperinus powder highlights the considerable chemical complexity of this biological matrix. Thirty-six compounds were identified, with a total content of 621.97 μg/g. Six aldehydes (47.74 %), fourteen hydrocarbons (23.73 %), seven substituted pyrazines (13.11 %), five alcohols (8.60 %), two aromatic hydrocarbons (5.36 %), and two ketones (1.46 %) were found (Table 5). The dominant volatile compound was 4-methylbenzaldehyde. The major compounds were aldehydes, such as 4-methylbenzaldehyde and benzeneacetaldehyde; predominant among hydrocarbons were undecane, 2,6,10,15-tetramethyl-heptadecane, and pentadecane; among substituted pyrazines were 2,5-dimethylpyrazine and 2-ethenyl-6-methylpyrazine; and among alcohols were 1-octen-3-ol and 2-methyl-1-decanol. The presence of aldehydes in A. diaperinus powder suggests the occurrence of Maillard reactions and Strecker degradation, which are characteristic of thermal processes and crucial for the formation of sensory properties – especially flavors described as nutty, malty, honeyed, or roasted (Perez-Santaescolastica et al., 2022). Perez-Santaescolastica et al. (2023) evaluated the aroma profile of seven unheated edible insect species, including A. diaperinus, and found that the dominant group of volatile compounds was carboxylic acids, accounting for 58 % of the total peak area. In the study of the cited authors, A. diaperinus also contained significant amounts of aldehydes, including 2-methylbutanal and 3-methylbutanal, which, as indicated, can result from the deamination of leucine and isoleucine. In the study by Reale et al. (2023), the highest concentrations of 2-ethylpiperidine and N-valeryl-l-proline-hexyl ester were determined among the volatile compounds (excluding fatty acids); however, these compounds were not detected in our study. The differences in volatile compounds may be due to the use of different drying methods during powder production (Perez-Santaescolastica et al., 2022). On the other hand, the same authors also identified pyrazine derivatives in buffalo worm powder, including 2-methylpyrazine, 2,5-dimethylpyrazine, and 3-ethyl-2,5-dimethylpyrazine, with total amounts of about 14 %, similar to our study. Pyrazine derivatives are counted among the products of the Maillard reaction, which play a significant role in shaping flavor characteristics and give products a roasted, coffee, or cocoa aroma and taste. Based on a comprehensive review of volatile compounds in edible insects, 2,5-dimethylpyrazine is the most commonly detected pyrazine in this matrix (Perez-Santaescolastica et al., 2022). Maillard reactions occur during the heat treatment of edible insects, especially during drying and roasting processes, leading to the formation of volatile compounds responsible for the diverse aromatic properties of the products. The profile of these aromatic compounds depends on the heat treatment method used. Microwave drying has been shown to lead to higher levels of pyrazine and pyrrole compounds, resulting in a distinct “roasted” aroma (Mishyna et al., 2020). In other studies comparing freeze-drying with oven drying, it was found that a greater amount of Maillard reaction products were formed in oven-dried samples (Khatun et al., 2021).

Total phenolic and flavonoid contents

Fig. 1a and b demonstrate that UAE significantly enhanced the extraction efficiency of total phenolic and flavonoid compounds from A. diaperinus larvae compared to conventional methods (C5 and C10). The U600–10 extract (600 W, 10 min) contained the highest total phenolic content (129.00 ± 1.58 mg CE/100 mL) and total flavonoid content (40.51 ± 0.26 mg CE/100 mL), suggesting that higher ultrasound intensity and prolonged exposure synergistically improve bioactive compound recovery.

Total phenolic and (a) total flavonoid contents (b) of buffalo worm extracts. U150/450–5/10 – ultrasound-assisted extraction at 150, 300, or 450 W for 5 or 10 min. C5 and C10 – conventional extraction for 5 and 10 min, respectively. Means marked with different letters (a, b, c) are significantly different (p < 0.05), indicating statistically significant differences in content of total phenolic compounds and flavonoids among the tested extracts.

Other extracts, including U150–10, U300–5/10, U450–5/10, and U600–5, also showed significantly higher phenolic contents than the conventional C5 extract. For flavonoids, the U450–10 and U600–5/10 extractions were notably superior, outperforming both C5 and C10 controls. This suggests that UAE effectiveness depends not on time or power alone, but rather on their optimized combination that maximizes cavitation-induced disruption of insect tissue matrices. The mechanism of UAE involves the generation and collapse of microbubbles (acoustic cavitation), creating localized high temperatures and shear forces that rupture cellular membranes and facilitate the release of intracellular phenolics and flavonoids (Gharibzahedi & Altintas, 2022; Pranil et al., 2024). These mechanical effects have also been linked to the higher phenolic compounds in Tenebrio molitor and Acheta domesticus extracts following ultrasound and microwave-assisted methods (Muñoz-Seijas et al., 2025; Nino et al., 2021). Gharibzahedi and Altintas (2022) demonstrated in their research that UAE was more effective in extracting phenolic compounds from lesser mealworm larvae oil compared to conventional extraction. The higher content of phenolic compounds when using UAE conditions indicates increased extraction efficiency, resulting from a more intense cavitation effect and better release of components from plant tissues (Senrayan & Venkatachalam, 2020). In the study by Pranil et al. (2024), UAE of oil from Acheta domesticus resulted in significantly higher levels of phenolic compounds compared to conventional extraction, indicating its greater efficiency in releasing bioactive compounds from the insect matrix. This phenomenon can be attributed to the cavitation effect, which leads to damage to cell membranes and facilitates the diffusion of phenols into the solvent.

Notably, U600–10 resulted in a 10.9 % increase in phenolic content compared to the best-performing conventional method (C10: 125.66 mg CE/100 mL), reinforcing the superiority of ultrasound techniques for phenolic recovery. In flavonoid extraction, significant improvements were observed primarily when ultrasound time was extended to 10 min at 450 or 600 W. Conversely, increasing ultrasound power alone from 150 to 450 W (5 min) was insufficient to induce marked increases in flavonoid content, indicating that longer sonication duration is critical, particularly at moderate intensities.

Although UAE generally enhances the release of phenolic compounds, the comparable TPC values observed across most ultrasound treatments (except U600–10) likely result from the saturation of mass transfer and partial degradation of phenolic molecules at moderate power levels. At 300–450 W for 5–10 min, the cavitation intensity was sufficient to rupture cell walls and liberate soluble phenolics, but further prolongation under the same power failed to improve diffusion efficiency because the available phenolic pool had already been exhausted. Moreover, prolonged ultrasonication can trigger radical-mediated oxidation of sensitive phenolics, explaining the plateau in total content. Similar findings were reported by Mareta et al. (2025).

This aligns with the findings of Muñoz-Seijas et al. (2025), who observed that UAE enhanced both total phenolics and antioxidant capacity in T. molitor beetles compared to temperature-assisted and CO₂-based extractions. Similarly, Pranil et al. (2024) reported that cricket oils extracted using UAE showed significantly higher total phenolic content and antioxidant capacity than oils extracted with Soxhlet or hot press methods, underlining UAE's broad applicability across insect matrices.

In comparison with previous studies on Acheta domesticus, the phenolic concentrations observed in this study were comparable or higher. For instance, Nino et al. (2021) identified a total phenolic content of 1.9–2.1 g GAE/100 g extract (i.e., 19–21 mg/mL) in methanolic extracts using microwave-assisted extraction (MAE), significantly lower than the catechin-equivalent values observed in this study using UAE. In contrast, conventional extraction (C5, C10) resulted in phenolic contents 116.32–125.66 mg/100 mL and flavonoid contents 38.44–39.37 mg/100 mL, showing only slight increases between 5 and 10 min. These values remained significantly lower than those of UAE-derived extracts. This performance gap can be attributed to the passive diffusion mechanism in conventional methods, which lacks the enhanced mass transfer and structural disruption provided by UAE (Phuangjit et al., 2024).

The ability to extract antioxidant-rich compounds such as phenolics and flavonoids from insect biomass supports the potential development of foods with enhanced biological value, nutraceuticals, and natural preservatives, owing to their known antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial properties (Gharibzahedi & Altintas, 2022; Shen et al., 2023; Yong et al., 2023; Skotnicka et al., 2023). These findings underscore the importance of selecting appropriate UAE parameters to achieve optimal levels of specific bioactive targets. Among all tested conditions, U600–10 emerged as the most effective, enabling superior recovery of phenolics and flavonoids from A. diaperinus larvae.

Antioxidant capacity by DPPH and ORAC tests

The DPPH and ORAC assays are used to measure the antioxidant capacity by examining how effectively antioxidants can scavenge stable radicals. Antioxidants neutralize DPPH radicals primarily through the donation of a hydrogen atom (H•). In the ORAC assay, antioxidants combat peroxyl radicals, also predominantly through hydrogen donation, effectively inhibiting their reactivity (Pyo et al., 2020). The antioxidant capacity of buffalo worm extracts as measured by DPPH and ORAC assays, are presented in Fig. 2. Although the total content of phenolic compounds and flavonoids in A. diaperinus extracts seems to be an important factor affecting their antioxidant potential, the presented results indicate that antioxidant capacity was not always directly correlated with their content. Increasing ultrasound power, without increasing extraction time, resulted in a significant increase in antioxidant capacity from 1114 μM Trolox/100 mL at 150 W to 1145 μM Trolox/100 mL at 600 W (DPPH assay) and from 2005.35 μM Trolox/100 mL at 150 W to 2128.28 Trolox/100 mL at 600 W (ORAC assay). In the case of phenolic compounds and flavonoids, exclusively increasing the ultrasound power, without increasing the extraction time, did not significantly affect their concentrations. This may suggest that other bioactive compounds, e.g., alkaloids, terpenoids, amino acids, and fatty acids, also contributed to the improved antioxidant properties of A. diaperinus extracts (Muñoz-Seijas et al., 2025). The comparison of the UAE (U150/450/600–5/10) and conventional extraction (C5, C10) for antioxidant capacity, measured by both DPPH and ORAC assays, revealed a clear superiority of the UAE. In both graphs, the highest antioxidant capacity was observed in U450/600–10 (1187.41 ± 2.47 μM Trolox/100 mL for DPPH; 2228.28 ± 3.9 μM Trolox/100 mL for ORAC assay), indicating that a combination of high ultrasound power and longer extraction significantly enhanced the recovery of antioxidants. This can be attributed to the acoustic cavitation effect, where the implosion of microbubbles generates shear forces that disrupt cellular structures and membranes, improving the release of bioactive molecules (Gharibzahedi & Altintas, 2022). Other UAE extracts, including U600–5 and U150/300–10, also showed higher antioxidant capacity, though slightly lower than U600–10, highlighting the importance of both power and time in maximizing extraction efficiency. Even at a lower power setting (U150, 1114 ± 6.79 μM Trolox/100 mL for DPPH and 2005.35 ± 10.70 μM Trolox/100 mL for ORAC), the UAE outperformed conventional methods, underscoring the superior efficiency of ultrasound-assisted techniques over passive diffusion methods. In contrast, conventional extraction methods (C5 and C10) showed the lowest antioxidant capacity results, indicating that they were far less efficient, even with longer extraction times (C10, 1030.56 μM Trolox/100 mL for DPPH and 1933.92 μM Trolox/100 mL for ORAC assays). The marginal improvement from C5 to C10 suggested that increasing time in conventional methods only minimally enhanced antioxidant recovery. These findings are consistent with previous studies by Kingwascharapong et al. (2021) and Navarro del Hierro et al. (2021), which highlighted the antioxidant capacity in ultrasound-extracted Patanga succincta, Acheta domesticus, and Tenebrio molitor. Similarly, high DPPH radical-scavenging activity, along with strong ABTS antioxidant capabilities, were also reported in T. molitor, Patanga brevitarsis, and Allomyrina dichotoma (Yong et al., 2023).

The antioxidant potential of A. diaperinus extracts can be attributed to the presence of bioactive compounds, including phenolics, flavonoids, and chitin derivatives (Gharibzahedi & Altintas, 2022). Phenolic compounds and flavonoids are well-known for their potent antioxidant properties, primarily through their ability to donate hydrogen atoms or electrons to neutralize free radicals, interrupting oxidative chain reactions (Pranil et al., 2024). Nevertheless, as (Psarianos et al., 2025) point out, in addition to phenolic compounds, chitosan, and vitamins are also important bioactive components whose presence may contribute significantly to the observed antioxidant activity. The presence of these compounds, in combination with phenols and flavonoids, is likely responsible for the high antioxidant activity of A. diaperinus extracts. In this study, no correlation analysis between individual bioactive components and antioxidant activity was performed, therefore this description is based on literature reports on potential mechanisms of action.

Conclusions

This study highlights the nutritional and bioactive potential of buffalo worm (Alphitobius diaperinus) powder as a promising ingredient in sustainable food systems. With a protein content of 44.23 % and a balanced fatty acid profile – including high levels of oleic and palmitic acids – the powder provides essential nutrients comparable to conventional protein sources. The mineral content, particularly potassium and phosphorus, exceeds daily recommended intakes, further enhancing its nutritional value. Although the amino acid profile is substantial, complementary protein sources may be required to provide a complete profile of essential amino acids. Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) was shown to be more effective than conventional methods in enhancing the extraction of phenolic and flavonoid compounds, especially under more intensive extraction conditions of 600 W for 10 min, which corresponded with increased antioxidant capacity. Although the stability of the extracted bioactive compounds was not directly evaluated, the increased recovery observed under UAE suggests improved extraction efficiency. Furthermore, the higher antioxidant capacity of extracts observed with shorter UAE durations compared to conventional methods suggests effective extraction other antioxidants. Overall, buffalo worm powder represents a valuable source of protein, essential fatty acids, and bioactive compounds suitable for incorporation into foods with enhanced biological value. Its rich nutritional profile and the efficient extractability of health-promoting compounds position it as a promising alternative protein source for advancing sustainable food production and future food security.
