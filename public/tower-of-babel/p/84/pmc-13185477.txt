The Meaning of Mangabey Molars (And Premolars)
Authors: Debbie Guatelli‐Steinberg, W Scott McGraw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe postcanine teeth of mangabeys (members of Cercocebus and Lophocebus) have figured prominently in discussions about the relationship between hard‐object feeding and dental form. Grey‐cheeked mangabeys (Lophocebus albigena) and sooty mangabeys (Cercocebus atys) both have thickly enameled posterior teeth. Thick enamel in the former is argued to be response to fallback consumption of hard‐object foods, whereas in the latter, this feature is thought to have evolved in response to routine consumption of hard‐object foods year‐round. Our recent analyses reveal aspects of dental form beyond thick enamel that render C. atys posterior teeth better suited to process hard‐object foods and resist fracture than those of L. albigena. We argue that the different feeding regimes of these two mangabey species had different effects on the evolution of their posterior teeth. Here, we detail these differences within the context of the fallback food paradigm. These results may ultimately help us detect modes of hard‐object feeding in the fossil record.

Introduction

That primate dental anatomy is functionally related to diet is axiomatic, and considerable effort has been devoted to examining how the architecture of primate jaws and teeth covary with the foods primates consume. The utility of such associations for reconstructing fossil diets depends on how tightly feeding behavior maps onto morphological structures and, critically, on our understanding of the selective forces shaping those structures. These issues lie at the center of ongoing debate over whether morphological variation is better explained by habitual loading or by less frequent but temporally critical loading events [1, 2]. With respect to how different foods influence the masticatory loading environment, the key question is: Do jaw and tooth form more closely reflect foods consumed most often or those eaten during critical “fallback” periods when preferred resources are scarce? Implicit in this dichotomy is the assumption that routinely consumed foods are relatively easy to process, whereas fallback foods are mechanically challenging and typically less preferred. As discussed below, the role of fallback foods in shaping dental morphology has become a central—and nontrivial—component of this debate.

The roots of the fallback food paradigm lie in optimal foraging research of the 1960s and 1970s, when behavioral ecologists emphasized trade‐offs between the effort required to locate, process, and consume foods and the energetic returns those foods provided. This framework proved particularly influential for understanding dietary choices during periods of resource scarcity, and a growing body of field evidence from the 1970s demonstrated that primates often shifted their diets seasonally (e.g. 3, 4). Among the earliest applications of the fallback concept was Hladik's observation that chimpanzees in Gabon consumed leaves and stems when preferred fruits were scarce [3]. Building on this work, Tutin and Fernandez [5] explicitly introduced the term “fallback foods” in their study of chimpanzees and gorillas. Subsequent field studies of bonobos (e.g 6) Kibale chimpanzees [7], gorillas [8], and mandrills [9] further documented increased reliance on terrestrial herbs during periods of fruit scarcity. Expanding this paradigm to all higher primates at Kibale, Wrangham et al. [10] concluded that when ripe fruit was scarce, chimpanzee and monkey diets diverged: chimpanzees relied primarily on piths as fallback foods, whereas monkeys shifted toward unripe fruits and seeds (10:949).

The grey cheeked mangabey—Lophocebus albigena—was one member of the Kibale primate community. This monkey had first been studied at Kibale by Waser [11] who highlighted great seasonal variation in its diet. For example, in 1 year, consumption of Diospyros abyssinica ranged from a low of 4% in September to a high of 65% in April. One year later, April consumption declined to 25%, indicating substantial interannual variation as well. Additional studies of grey‐cheeked mangabeys in Gabon [12, 13] and Cameroon [14] further underscored the great seasonality of this monkey's diet.

Lambert and colleagues provided further data on seasonal shifts in diet (aka ‘resource switching’) in the Kibale cercopithecines and used the fallback concept to interpret species differences in molar enamel thickness [15]. In their influential paper, Lambert et al. [15] found that red‐tailed guenons (Cercopithecus ascanius) ate ripe fruit year round, but that grey‐cheeked mangabeys (L. albigena) did not. During those months when ripe fruit was scarce, grey‐cheeked mangabeys “fell back” on hard seeds and bark. Indeed, the hardest food in the grey‐cheeked mangabey diet was the same food that Waser [11] showed was so seasonally variant: Diospyros abyssinica. Observing that L. albigena had much thicker enamel than that of redtail monkeys, Lambert et al. [15] argued that it is “not so much what is consumed most commonly (i.e., soft, fleshy fruit), that selects for enamel thickness but the hardness of foods that are consumed infrequently, when other more preferred foods are not available.” (15:367).

Lambert and colleague's 2004 paper [15] spurred evolutionary anthropologists to investigate the effects of fallback foods (Box 1) on multiple aspects of primate biology. Papers from a symposium on fallbacks foods and in primate evolution were published in 2009 special issue of The American Journal of Physical Anthropology, with several stressing the impact of fallback foods on dental form [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. In one paper [17], Marshall and colleagues stated that “… aspects of dental morphology such as enamel thickness, topography, and jaw robusticity, are best viewed as responses to evolutionary pressures imposed by fallback foods and not other classes of food” (17:606), lending primacy to fallback foods as the evolutionary sculptors of dental form. The notion that fallback foods could be a major selective force was subsequently embraced by paleoanthropologists (e.g. 21, 22).

Fallback and preferred foods.

The term fallback foods (FBFs) conventionally refers to items “…of poor nutritional quality and high abundance, eaten particularly during periods when preferred foods are scarce” (23:1220). Often assumed to be mechanically challenging resources and critical to survival despite not being consumed regularly, FBFs are argued to exert strong selection pressures on primate biology, particularly on food‐processing anatomy (e.g. 15, 16, 17). Food processing adaptations are those involved in consumption and include cranio‐dental and gut specializations. Preferred foods, on the other hand, “are selected disproportionately often relative to their abundance within the population's habitat.” (24: 1221). These foods are generally of high nutritional quality and are actively sought and harvested, thereby selecting for food‐harvesting adaptations [24]. Harvesting adaptations are those involved in food acquisition and include those related to locomotion, vision, cognition, and manual dexterity. Lambert and Rothman [25] suggest that these adaptative differences are best understood as a continuum, rather than as a dichotomy.

In a conceptual advance, Marshall and Wrangham [24] refined the concept of fallback foods by distinguishing between filler and staple FBFs. Staple FBFs are those that “can seasonally constitute the entire diet,” while filler fallback foods “never constitute the entire diet and presumably could not maintain the individuals feeding on them in the absence of other food resources” (24:1228). These authors predicted that “the degree of physiological, behavioral, and ecological adaptation is less complete in response to filler FBFs than to staple FBFs” (24:1228) because filler FBFs are less critical for survival and reproduction than staple FBFs.

Although the FBF concept has been embraced by many, it has also met with criticism, including the point that mechanically challenging foods do not always serve as fallbacks but can constitute staples that are the most selected food type each month year‐round [26]. Furthermore, Lambert and Rothman [25] pointed out that whether a food is a fallback should not be considered an intrinsic property of the food itself: foods may serve as fallbacks in some ecological contexts and preferred foods in others. Indeed, as Berthaume et al. [23] note, terrestrial herbaceous vegetation (THV) has been classified as a fallback food, but G.g. gorilla sometimes prefers THV to fruit. These observations blur the originally proposed mechanical distinction between fallback and preferred foods.

Following Marshall and Wrangham [24] and Lambert and Rothman [25], we suggest that foods categorized as either filler or staple fallbacks can likewise be understood as falling on a continuum (Figure 1). During periods of resource scarcity, one or more non‐preferred foods—each potentially substitutable by another—may contribute to an organism's diet. In such lean times, filler fallback foods (FBFs) that constitute only a small proportion of the diet may, in both theory and practice, be more easily replaced by alternative resources. If so, these foods are likely to exert weaker selective pressures than FBFs consumed at higher frequencies, particularly those approaching the level of staple fallbacks.

Continuum of filler to staple fallback foods: percentages correspond to the proportion of a diet, within a season, consisting of a fallback food. In theory, lower frequencies of consumption would suggest that a filler fallback can be more easily replaced, while at higher frequences a filler fallback may approach a staple fallback in dietary importance.

Based on our reading of the literature, gray cheeked mangabeys at Kibale rely on filler fallback foods. While in theory filler fallbacks can be more easily replaced than dietary staples [24], the extent to which L. albigena can replace the hard‐object foods they consume is unknown. Given the short—though potentially no less critical—length of the season during which filler fallback foods are consumed, we argue that the thick enameled premolars and molars of gray cheeked mangabeys would have been subject to very different loading regimes than those of sooty mangabeys who consume obdurate foods items year‐round. We contend that the postcanine dentition of sooty mangabeys is at greater risk of failure due to more frequent high‐impact loads associated with year‐round consumption of hard foods and therefore would be expected to exhibit structural reinforcements of their post‐canine dentition beyond thick enamel. Furthermore, the high frequency with which they consume hard‐object foods over a lifetime is predicted to amplify their fracture risk, exposing their postcanine teeth to greater opportunity for fracture and subsequent failure. Meanwhile, information on a thick‐enameled mangabey from West Africa was coming to light that told a possibly different tale about the relationship between enamel thickness and hard‐object feeding [26]. Feeding data from the Ivory Coast's Taï Forest revealed that the hardest food in the Cercocebus atys (sooty mangabey) diet, Sacoglottis gabonensis seeds, were the most “frequently consumed food every month as well as overall; monthly variation in consumption ranged from 19.1% (December) to 78.6% (May)” (26:415–416).” McGraw and colleagues [26] found no indication that C. atys relied on S. gabonensis as a dietary fallback. Rather, these seeds were consumed on a regular basis year‐round, comprising nearly 50% of this species' annual diet.

The recognition that sooty mangabeys at Taï consume hard‐object foods to a much greater extent than grey‐cheeked mangabeys suggests that additional differences in feeding biology may distinguish these species. Both taxa frequently use their incisors during initial food processing; however, published studies indicate that Lophocebus more often employs its incisors to process hard foods, whereas Cercocebus relies more heavily on its enlarged premolars to fracture hard seeds [14, 15]. This latter pattern has also been documented in C. atys [26, 27]. Although oral processing profiles are available for sooty mangabeys at Taï [26], comparable data have not been collected for L. albigena, precluding direct comparison of loading regimes between species.

Differences in the material properties of foods consumed by the two mangabey species may also be important [28]; however, this idea cannot yet be evaluated directly because food material properties were assessed using different methodological approaches across studies [15, 26]. Additional factors—including variation in food toughness, exposure to dietary abrasives, and bite force—may further contribute to observed differences in feeding behavior and craniofacial loading [27, 29, 30], but these avenues of comparison remain inaccessible at present due to methodological inconsistency.

What can be stated with confidence is that the two mangabey species differ markedly in their feeding regimes: one consumes mechanically challenging foods primarily during brief fallback periods, whereas the other does so year‐round. The fact that both have similarly thick enamel despite these contrasting foraging regimes [15, 31] may limit our ability to detect adaptation to different forms of hard‐object feeding in the fossil record. However, might there be other aspects of dental anatomy—beyond enamel thickness and related to the degree or mode of hard‐object feeding—that distinguish these mangabey species? We believe there are.

Here, we review dental adaptations to hard‐object feeding in primates and apply this understanding to the molar and premolar morphology of mangabeys. We frame our review within the fallback food paradigm and using existing knowledge of the feeding ecology of these two genera. We proceed from the following premise: because Cercocebus consumes hard foods more frequently than Lophocebus, its teeth are subjected to repeated high‐magnitude loading, a regime that can weaken enamel through fatigue stress [32, 33] and increase fracture risk. Furthermore, over an individual's lifetime, the greater frequency with which Cercocebus consumes hard foods exposes its dentition to a higher cumulative risk of damage and failure. Under this scenario, selection should favor molars more strongly adapted to hard‐object feeding in Cercocebus than in Lophocebus.

Hard‐Object Feeding, Dental Form, and the Dental Differences Between Lophocebus and Cercocebus

Over evolutionary time scales, mammalian tooth form is likely to have been influenced not only by natural selection, genetic drift, and gene flow, but also by developmental constraints (e.g., 34) and pleiotropy (e.g. 35). Although the combination of factors resulting in modern dental form is often difficult to parse, researchers have proposed that several dental features facilitate hard‐object feeding (Box 2). Whether these features are adaptations for hard‐object feeding or simply permit it is often difficult to determine.

Furthermore, some aspects of dental form that facilitate hard‐object feeding can potentially have multiple selective advantages, making it difficult to identify which of these (or combination of them) has had had the greatest influence on their evolution. Thick enamel is a prime example, as it may have more than one function: resistance to fracture [18, 36], abrasion [37, 38, 39], acid erosion [40] and adhesive wear [40]. In addition, high bite forces required to crush hard‐foods may cause abrasive wear [41]. Thus, even in the absence of a significant abrasives in a species' diet, thick enamel in a hard‐object feeder might relate to both fracture and abrasion resistance. The thickness of enamel is highly evolvable [42, 43], permitting its adaptability to various challenges to tooth crown integrity. It is usually not known for which of these potential functions thick enamel within a lineage originally evolved.

With these caveats in mind, in this section we review proposed links between dental form and hard‐object feeding (summarized in Box 2), with a focus on dental differences between the two mangabey genera. Our samples of C. atys derived from skeletons collected from the Taï Forest of the Ivory Coast. These skeletons were collected over a more than thirty‐year period by W.S.M. and members of the Taï Forest monkey project. The L. albigena sample was collected by Professor Randall Susman (Stony Brook University) near the Mambili River approximately 50 km north of Makoua in the Republic of Congo. In one of our studies, we included in a single L. aterrimus specimen from Democratic Republic of Congo's Lomako Forest. For comparison with mangabeys, in some of our studies, we also included Cercopithecus species that are not known to eat hard‐object foods. These are: Cercopithecus sp., Cercopithecus campbelli, Cercopithecus diana, and Cercopithecus petaurista, all from the Taï Forest, as well as Cercopithecus cephus from the Republic of Congo. All skeletons used in this study are housed in the Primate Laboratory of the Department of Anthropology, The Ohio State University.

Enamel Thickness and Enamel Thickness Distribution

Enamel thickness has traditionally been quantified for inter‐specific comparisons using the metric Relative Enamel Thickness, or RET [44]. To calculate RET, the average thickness of enamel in 2D thin or virtual sections is divided by the square root of the dentine area (the area bounded by the enamel‐dentine junction and the bicervical diameter; see Box 2) and multiplied by 100. The resulting number is unitless [44]. More recently enamel thickness has been measured in 3D on μCT crown renderings (e.g. 45). Despite the expectation of an association between RET and the hard‐object food consumption, Thiery et al. [45] found no association between RET (measured in 3D) and durophagy in a sample of upper second molars representing 32 primate species. This is not surprising because as Schwartz et al. point out [46], RET is inversely proportional to crown size, while fracture resistance is directly proportional to the size of the crown and the absolute thickness of enamel [46, 47].

Thiery et al. [45] did find, however, that cercopithecids that feed on hard‐object foods have enamel that is distributed more unevenly over their molar crowns than species that do not. This finding is consistent with Lucas et al.'s [47] prediction that in durophagous species, enamel would be thicker in areas of the crown most prone to failure during hard‐object feeding. Lucas et al. [47] argued, more specifically, that thick enamel at cusp tips might relate to the comminution of large hard‐object foods. High bite forces used to crush hard foods can induce subsurface cracks at the enamel‐dentine junction (EDJ) beneath cusp tips, where tensile stresses build up. Such cracks, known as radial fractures (RF), can develop into ribbon fissures if they reach the enamel surface, causing catastrophic failure. Thick enamel in cusp tips decreases the likelihood that such fissures would reach the enamel surface. Schwartz et al. [46], however, pointed out that thickened enamel at cusps tips could be equally protective against abrasion/attrition and could furthermore be a simple developmental consequence of selection for thick lateral enamel — enamel deposited on the sides of teeth.

Thick lateral enamel would protect teeth from margin fractures, which can form when biting either hard or soft objects [47]. Margin fractures (MF) occur when the crown base bulges during vertical compression, creating tensile “hoop stresses” that promote the formation of sub‐surface cracks. If these cracks extend along enamel sidewalls, they can ultimately result in catastrophic failure. Schwartz [48] found that in both Pongo and Homo lingual cusps of upper molars have proportionally thicker enamel than buccal cusps. In upper molars, lingual cusps are most directly involved in crushing and grinding, as they occlude with basins of the lower molars in the fashion of a mortar and pestle (Box 3). For this reason, they have been referred to as the “functional cusps” of upper molars (46 and Box 3). The lateral walls of upper molar lingual cusps in Homo and Pongo are proportionally thickened but those of Pan and Gorilla are not [46], possibly reflecting dietary differences among these great ape genera. Pongo's thickened lateral enamel might be protective against margin fractures resulting from its frequent consumption of hard foods (e.g. 20, 48, 49, 50).

Functional vs. non‐functional cusps.

The terms “functional” and “non‐functional” cusps have been used to distinguish between molar cusps that participate in different ways during the phases of the chewing cycle [48]. In one sense, these terms are misleading because both “functional” and “non‐functional” function in the chewing cycle. The distinction was originally meant, however, to highlight the participation of upper molar lingual cusps and lower molar buccal cusps in both crushing and grinding. Because we find the terminology convenient (to refer to both upper molar lingual cusps and lower molar buccal cusps) in the context of discussing crushing and grinding of hard‐object foods, we have used these terms in our previous work [51, 52, 53, 54] as we continue do here.

The chewing cycle consists of three “Strokes”: the Closing Stroke, the Power Stroke, and the Opening Stroke, and two “Phases”: Phase I and Phase II (90 and Figure 3). During the Closing Stroke, the mandible moves upward to meet the maxilla, bringing the functional cusps of the molars (lingual for upper molars, buccal for lower molars) into contact. In the next stroke, the Power Stroke, the mandible moves upward and laterally, in involving the cusps in shearing, crushing and grinding actions. In Phase I of the Power Stroke, functional molar cusps slide past each other until they reach centric occlusion. As they do so, food is sheared between the cusps and then crushed between the functional cusps and occlusal basins of the opposing tooth crown. In Phase II of the Power Stroke, the lingual sides of the lower molar functional cusps grind against the buccal sides of the upper molar functional cusps. In the final stroke, the Opening Stroke, molars cease contact as the jaw opens [55]. Figure 4.

Thick occlusal basin enamel also appears to be associated with durophagy, as it is differentially thickened in Pongo relative to Pan and Gorilla [48, 56, 57]. O'Hara [57] compared the thickness of occlusal basin enamel relative to average enamel thickness as a measure of enamel reinforcement in this crown region, calling this metric “proportional” occlusal basin enamel thickness. In a sample of six extant catarrhine genera, she found that proportional occlusal basin enamel thickness was greatest in durophagous taxa. In short, although neither thick nor unevenly distributed enamel appear to be definitive indicators of durophagy, both may be associated with it.

Lophocebus Versus Cercocebus

The thickness of Cercocebus and Lophocebus enamel has been measured in a variety of studies (e.g. 27, 31, 39, 45, 57, 58). Lophocebus and Cercocebus molars appear to have RET values at the top of primate enamel thickness ranges [31]. For Cercocebus published 2D RET values range from a low of 12.89 (Cercocebus torquatus M1, n = 3; SD = 1.659 [58] to a high of 22.68 (Cercocebus torquatus M2, n = 1; 28). For Lophocebus 2D RET values range from a low of 16.85 (L. albigena M1, n = 2; SD = 0.530 [58] to a high of 22.85 (Lophocebus aterrimus M2, n = 1; 28). Variation in RET values for each genus may relate in part to tooth type and whether measurements were performed on virtual μCT or physical histological sections [59]. We compared 2D RET measurements of Cercocebus and Lophocebus using μCT scans of relatively large samples (values given in Supplementary Table 1). We found the Lophocebus had greater RET values than Cercocebus for upper molars while the two genera were not significantly different in RET for their lower molars [51].

With respect to the distribution of enamel thickness, Thiery and colleagues' [45] found more unevenly distributed enamel in 3D in both Cercocebus and Lophocebus relative to other cercopithecids, though they did not compare the two genera. In our studies, although we did not find significant differences in 2D lateral enamel thickness for Cercocebus and Lophocebus, we did find that proportional occlusal basin enamel thickness is significantly greater in Cercocebus, for both upper and lower molars (Table S1) as well as for upper second premolars [52]. Greater occlusal enamel thickness in C. atys indicates greater fracture and abrasion resistance in this crown region.

Enamel Microstructure

The fundamental microstructural unit of mammalian enamel is the enamel prism (or enamel rod) [60]. Enamel prisms extend from the enamel‐dentine junction (EDJ) to the enamel surface, marking the path that ameloblasts—enamel forming cells—travelled during crown formation [61]. The organization of prisms within enamel can affect both fracture and wear resistance [60].

One such aspect of enamel prism organization is their orientation with respect occlusal wear surfaces. When the long axes of prisms are oriented parallel to the direction of force during vertical crushing, they make enamel stiffer [62] and increase its tensile strength [63]. For these reasons, Macho and Shimizu [62] argued that enamel prisms oriented at high angles to wear surfaces (roughly parallel to the direction of force) might represent an adaptation to load resistance. Their data suggested that this might be the case—Paranthropus robustus, a presumed hard‐object feeder (e.g. [64, 65]; but see [66] for an alternative perspective), had the highest wear angles to wear surfaces of all primates they studied. Yet, prisms oriented at high angles to wear surface may also provide resistance to abrasive wear (e.g. 67, 68). When prisms are oriented at low angles to enamel surfaces, abrasive wear can cause crystallites within them to be “plucked” from the surface [69]. Foster and Constantino [69] demonstrated that enamel was easier to scratch when prisms were at low angles to enamel surfaces and conversely, harder to scratch when prisms were at higher angles. High prism angles, then, may relate to both load and wear resistance.

While prism angle orientation is not uniquely associated with hard‐object feeding, the degree of enamel decussation, or the crisscrossing of enamel prisms, appears to be. When enamel prisms are parallel with one another, cracks can easily propagate along prism boundaries; however, they are hindered from spreading when enamel prisms are arranged heterogeneously [32, 70]. A high degree of complexity in decussated enamel, defined as “any microanatomical feature of dental enamel that increases the heterogeneity of enamel crystallite orientations” (70: 3) characterizes species that frequently consume hard‐object foods [70, 71, 72, 73]. Robust capuchins (Sapajus) have markedly complex enamel in their canines, which they use to break into hard‐object foods [70]. Orangutans, known hard‐object feeders, have more complex enamel decussation (in 3D) than chimpanzees and gorillas [71]. Sea otters, well‐known for fracturing shells of marine invertebrates with their teeth, also have complex enamel decussation [72] as do bone‐crushing hyenas [73].

Lophocebus Versus Cercocebus

We found that, across a range of wear facet angles, prisms approach Phase II wear facet surfaces at significantly higher angles in C. atys than they do in L. albigena or Cercopithecus (Cercop. petaurista and Cercop. diana) (53; Table S1). Cercocebus atys' higher prism angles would confer greater fracture and abrasion resistance upon its molar crowns. Comparing trigon basin decussation in one second molar each of C. atys, L. albigena, and C. cephus (Table 1), we found significant differences in decussation complexity among all species: Cercocebus atys had the greatest range of prism angles relative to the section plane, followed by Lophocebus and then Cercopithecus [54], suggesting that C. atys enamel is more resistant to fracture.

Mechanical Properties of Enamel

The mechanical properties of enamel may also relate to hard‐object feeding. Foster [41], who compared ten species of primates in terms of the hardness (H) and elasticity (E) of their molar enamel, found that “larger animals and animals that can generate high bite forces” had stiffer enamel (higher values of E) especially at the enamel surface of molar cusps, a finding he interpreted as an adaptation to “efficiently transmit bite forces into food particles while chewing” (41:iii). Foster further suggested that lower values of E near the EDJ would “make enamel more pliable” reducing “elastic mismatch with dentine,” and thus limiting “internal stress that can damage the crown.” (41:33). He therefore predicted that there might be steeper gradients in E between outer enamel and inner enamel near the EDJ in animals with more challenging diets: stiffer near the surface and more pliable near the EDJ.

Foster also found greater values of H towards enamel surfaces in larger animals and other animals that can generate high bite forces [41]. Greater H would resist wear [60] from high bite forces that can drive scratches across enamel surfaces (and would, of course, also be protective against dietary abrasives such as grit and/or phytoliths). We note, however, that Towle et al. [74] did not find similar relationships between body size and mechanical properties in 14 primate taxa.

Lophocebus Versus Cercocebus

We compared mechanical properties H and E of enamel within the trigon basin in one minimally worn upper second molar each of C. atys, L. albigena, and C. cephus [54]. The hardness of outer enamel in the trigon basin was significantly greater for C. atys than for either of the other two species. There was no significant difference for L. albigena and C. cephus with respect to outer enamel hardness. For E, C. atys had significantly lower values than the other two species in the inner enamel near the EDJ, while having significantly greater values than C. cephus (but not L. albigena) in the outer enamel (Table S1). Thus, as would be predicted based on Foster's work and from its elevated fracture risk, C. atys had a steeper gradient in E than L. albigena, which had steeper gradient than C. cephus.

Tooth Size and Strength

Large molars, especially wider ones, distribute occlusal loads over larger areas and can therefore withstand greater occlusal loads before their enamel begins to crack (e.g. 16, 75, 76). Thus, for example, sea otters, which have thin enamel can withstand high loads associated with breaking through shelled food objects in part because of their large molar base sizes [76].

The enlarged “molarized” premolars of some primates and hominins have also been interpreted as providing resistance to fracture (e.g. 27, 28). Scott and colleagues [77], however, argued that the enlarged premolars of hard‐object feeders may instead be a consequence of greater functional integration of premolars with molars, possibly because of a mesial shift in bite point. Fannin et al. [78] found evidence that enlarged premolars relative to M1s in cercopithecoids were associated with enhanced chewing performance as measured by fecal particle size. Enlarged premolars may therefore also contribute to more efficient food breakdown.

Taking into account both the size of the crown and the thickness of the enamel, Lawn and Lee [75] developed formulae to estimate critical loads (P) required to propagate enamel cracks to failure (Box 2). Schwartz et al. [46] developed a metric called “Absolute Crown Strength” or ACS (Box 2), that is easy to calculate and is highly correlated with values of estimated critical loads for both radial and margin fractures calculated with Lawn and Lee's formulae [75, 79]. Both the formulae of Lawn and Lee [75], as well as the formula for ACS [46] can be used to gain insight into the ability of crowns to resist fractures leading to failure. Because estimated critical loads for fracture can be calculated for each cusp, it is possible to assess whether “functional cusps,” or those most directly involved in crushing and grinding (Box 3) are particularly reinforced in hard‐object feeders (as in 73).

Lophocebus Versus Cercocebus

The earliest direct comparison in dental form between Cercocebus and Lophocebus revealed differences between the two genera in the size of their second premolars relative to their molars [80]. This difference extended to members of their separate (papionin) clades: Cercocebus and Mandrillus with enlarged premolars on one branch, and Lophocebus, Papio, and Theropithecus with smaller premolars, on the other [80]. In our work we found that, as was expected based on their second premolar size differences, Cercocebus and Lophocebus had significantly different ACS values, with those of Cercocebus being greater [52].

ACS comparisons among molars revealed statistically significant differences in ACS between Lophocebus and Cercocebus for upper molars, with non‐significant differences in a smaller sample of lower molars (Table S1). We were unable to compare PRF among the three genera, as most of the molars in our samples had some degree of wear (unworn cusps are necessary for PRF calculations). For PMF of functional cusps in both upper and lower molars, Cercocebus had greater values than Lophocebus, which in turn had greater values than Cercopithecus. For non‐functional cusps, there was no significant difference in PMF between Cercocebus and Lophocebus, but both genera had greater PMF values than Cercopithecus [54]. These findings suggest selective reinforcement of functional cusps in C. atys.

Crown Morphology

Crown morphology has important roles in the comminution of food as well as in resistance to fracture. Bunodont cusps have long been proposed as adaptations to hard‐object feeding (e.g. 16, 36, 79, 81). One hypothesis, the “Blunt Cusp Hypothesis” [23] proposes that relative to tall, high cusps, bunodont cusps are more efficient at crushing and grinding hard object foods (e.g. 36, 81). The “Strong Cusp Hypothesis” [23] suggests that bunodont cusps decrease stresses within enamel while crushing hard‐object foods, offering protection against fracture [16, 18]. It is possible that thick enamel stacked over the dentine horns of cusps simply makes them more bunodont [23], and if so, bunodonty might be a by‐product of selection for thick enamel in hard‐object feeders [23]. Bunodonty aside, the lophs of bilophodont molars may act as wedges [82] useful for breaking into hard‐object foods [83].

Ungar and Williamson [84] first introduced the use of landmark‐free GIS analysis of tooth surfaces to study enamel surfaces as landscapes. Efforts to map the topography of tooth crowns suggest that several metrics are associated with material properties of foods, presumably because they enhance food breakdown (see 33 for a review). In their analysis of 17 primate species' molars, Thiery et al. [85] found that two topographic measures, Drichtlet Normal Energy (DNE) and the Relief Index (RFI) were associated with hard‐object feeding. DNE is a measure of tooth surface curvature and represents tooth sharpness [23]. RFI is the ratio of the 3D surface area of a tooth to its 2D projection, making it a measure of relative crown height [23]. Primates that crack into brittle hard‐object foods tend to have lower DNE and RFI than those that crack either soft foods or hard and tough foods. Put simply, these findings suggest that species cracking hard and brittle food items tend to have molars with more rounded cusps and shorter crowns. By contrast, species that crack into food items that are both hard and tough tend to have higher DNE (greater tooth sharpness) [85], with their greater sharpness presumably allowing them to break down the tough element of their foods more effectively.

Cusp flare (Box 2), referring to a high degree of angulation between the cusp tip and cervix, is another aspect of dental morphology thought to relate to hard‐object feeding [86]. Singleton [86] found that within hominoids, hard‐object feeding is associated with greater molar flare. Experimental studies in humans have shown that lateral jaw movement increases as a function of food hardness [87, 88]. The association between hard‐foods and molar flare found by Singleton [86] may therefore relate functionally to lateral forces entailed in hard‐object mastication. Macho and Shimizu [62] argued that cusp flare is not necessarily related to hard‐object feeding but is more broadly related to buttressing crowns against high lateral forces that could be involved in chewing other mechanically challenging foods, such as tough foods. Molar flare is an ancient cercopithecoid feature, appearing as early as 22 Ma in the stem cercopithecoid Alophia and becoming more pronounced, along with bilophodonty, ca. 15.5 Ma [89], both features potentially having originated in a seed‐eating context [82, 83].

Lophocebus Versus Cercocebus

We had a small sample of complete cusp tips for Lophocebus (n = 2 upper molars, 1 lower molar). Although we could not meaningfully compare them statistically, cusp tips for Cercocebus were sharper (they had more acute cusp tip angles) than they were for Lophocebus. This finding is consistent with that of Thiery et al. [85] in which C. atys had greater DNE (sharpness) than did L. albigena (at the dentine surface). In a larger sample (see Table S1), we found that flare of lower molar functional cusps [51] as well as upper second premolar functional cusps [52] was significantly greater for Cercocebus than for Lophocebus, suggesting greater resistance to lateral forces in the former.

Relationship to Hard‐Object Foods

Potentially related to hard‐object feeding, Cercocebus has greater proportional occlusal basin enamel thickness (upper and lower molars). Cercocebus atys also has higher prism angles to Phase II wear surfaces in its upper molars, rendering them stiffer and thus more efficient at transferring chewing forces from occlusal surfaces to foods (though high prism angles to wear surfaces can also serve to resist abrasion). Multiple measurements from a single second molar of L. albigena and C. atys reveal more complex decussation and a steeper gradient in elastic modulus from inner to outer enamel of the trigon basin, both likely to be related to fracture resistance specifically. Similarly, ACS is greater in Cercocebus than Lophocebus (upper molars), while estimated critical margin fracture loads are greater in Cercocebus than Lophocebus specifically for functional cusps.

Cercocebus' greater functional cusp flare would buttress its crowns against lateral forces incurred when chewing hard foods. We previously pointed out that greater flare increases the bicervical diameter and dentine crown area measurements that are included in the calculation of RET, driving Cercocebus RET values below those of Lophocebus in the upper molars [51]. At the same time, an increase in the bicervical diameter resulting from greater flare also contributes to an increase in ACS. For this reason, we argued [51] that greater flare in Cercocebus appears to entail a trade‐off between ACS and RET. Cusp tip angles and DNE appear to further differentiate Cercocebus and Lophocebus, possibly related to differences in the toughness of their diets [85].

We note that there were also significant differences between Cercopithecus and Lophocebus for some measures, including for enamel decussation complexity (Lophocebus more complex than Cercopithecus), the gradient in elastic modulus (Lophocebus exhibiting a steeper gradient than Cercopithecus), and estimated critical margin fracture loads (greater for Lophocebus). We suggest that these differences between Cercopithecus and Lophocebus are related to the hard foods that Lophocebus consumes seasonally. More germane to our central argument, features likely to be related to durophagy in the dentition of Lophocebus are not as pronounced as they are in Cercocebus.

Taxonomic and Phylogenetic Context

Molecular evidence indicates that the papionins radiated in the Late Miocene into three main lineages: Macaca (Clade 1), Cercocebus and Mandrillus (Clade 2: the CM clade), and Lophocebus, Papio, and Theropithecus (Clade 3: the LPT clade) [90]. Fleagle and McGraw [80, 91] found a suite of craniodental and postcranial differences between the CM and LPT clades that they argued were functionally tied to their different feeding niches. Both Cercocebus and Mandrillus share foraging behaviors in which they aggressively harvest hard seeds and nuts from the forest floor. As Fleagle and McGraw [80, 91] pointed out, these behaviors are not found in the LPT clade, nor are the enlarged second premolars or skeletal features indicating powerful forelimb adduction that characterize the CM clade.

We do not yet know if the additional multiple differences in dental form between Cercocebus and Lophocebus that our research has revealed are shared with other members of their respective clades. A single UM3 of a Mandrillus specimen published in Lemmers [92] appears to have relatively high ACS and thick proportional occlusal basin enamel‐‐ our measurements on the image were 2.26 and 1.27, respectively. These values are higher than those of Papio molars, with average ACS values of 2.09 and average proportional occlusal basin enamel thickness of 1.15 [57]. However, a systematic study of the distribution of these traits in the two clades is clearly needed to adequately test the hypothesis that the dental differences between Cercocebus and Lophocebus have deep clade‐level roots.

Fannin et al. [93], citing evidence that dietary change preceded changes in dental form in early East African hominins, argued that the increasing incorporation of tough C4 plants in hominin diets drove the evolution of large tooth size and thick enamel through “behavioral drive.” A similar approach, tracking changes in diet and dental form in fossil papionins might help illuminate the evolution of CM and LPT dietary and dental differences. Unfortunately, the phylogeny of fossil papionins is not well enough resolved [94], nor is the dentition of fossil papionins sufficiently well‐studied, to be able to trace changes in dietary signals and the evolution of dental traits in the CM and LPT clades.

What is known is that the approximately 5 Mya South African fossil species Procercocebus antiquus, a likely member of the CM clade [94], exhibits larger second premolar to first molar ratios than equivalently‐aged Parapapio species [95] that are, in turn, not as large as those of modern Cercocebus and Mandrillus. This evidence suggests that P4 enlargement began at the base of the PM clade. On the other hand, microwear evidence does not suggest that either Procercocebus antiquus nor other Plio‐Pleistocene age papionins at Taung specialized on hard‐object foods [96, 97]. Thus, no argument can be supported, as present, for behavioral drive leading to P4 enlargement in the CM clade. Further exploration of microwear evidence coupled with documenting multiple aspects of dental form in the evolving CM versus LPT fossil lineages would help clarify how their dentitions evolved in relation to their changing diets.

Discussion

Our dental comparisons between C. atys and L. albigena suggest that in the former, hard‐object foods are associated with posterior teeth that are both more efficient at processing them and more resistant to fracture. We have suggested that these dental differences are related to the overall frequency with which C. atys consumes hard‐object foods (affecting fracture risk). The dental differences between the two species might be evolutionary adaptations to different dietary regimes (which we think is most likely, given their extensive dental as well as skeletal differences) or could more simply represent exaptations that make their current dietary regimes possible.

Some of the individual aspects of dental form we analyzed are more conservatively viewed as allowing the consumption of hard‐object foods, without necessarily representing adaptations for consuming them. For example, Schwartz et al. [46] observed that gorillas have high ACS and are known to consume hard‐object foods in at least one location [98]; yet, their thin enamel and long shearing crests are consistent with adaptation to tough, folivorous diets and not with hard‐object feeding. As Schwartz et al. (46:3) note, “While the presence of extensive shearing crests in Gorilla molars indeed represents an adaptation for efficiently processing tough, compliant vegetation…our data suggest that tooth strength sets constraints on feeding behaviour that in their case does not preclude the consumption of hard seeds.” Thus, the full picture of Gorilla molar form does not indicate adaptation to hard‐object food consumption, even though their crown strength makes hard‐object feeding possible.

As we noted earlier, assessing links between dental form and dietary adaptation is limited by our current understanding of the relative roles of natural selection, genetic drift, gene flow, developmental constraints, and pleiotropy in the evolution of particular dental features, which, moreover, may provide selective advantages related to multiple aspects of diet. We suggest that it is useful to evaluate the total morphological pattern of a species' dentition to gain greater confidence in adaptive hypotheses about dental form. Based on currently available evidence, we hypothesize that for C. atys, the total dental morphological pattern as we have detailed in this review, is an adaptation to frequent hard‐object feeding that may be shared with other members of the CM clade. A rigorous test of this hypothesis will require [1] broader taxonomic comparisons of dental morphological patterns in extant members of the CM clade and [2] examination of dental indicators of diet (e.g., dental microwear, stable carbon isotopes in enamel) in conjunction with analysis of dental form in the fossil ancestors of both the CM and LPT clades.

We wish to make a final point on fallback foods and primate dental form. Orangutans have served as a prominent example of fallback foods selecting for thick enamel (e.g. 20, 99). These large apes prefer ripe fruit across their range; however, they are known to consume large quantities of bark and leaves when preferred fruits are scarce, especially on the more seasonal island of Borneo [20]. Some authors have leveraged such dietary switching to support the idea that orangutan thick molar enamel evolved in response to consumption of low‐quality “filler” fallback foods (e.g. 20, 50), an argument paralleling explanations proposed for the thick enamel of L. albigena [15]. Research on the dentition of Pongo indicates that these great apes share many molar features with Cercocebus, which we have shown are associated with habitual consumption of mechanically challenging foods. For example, orangutan molars have thick proportional occlusal basin enamel [56, 57], more complex enamel decussation relative to gorillas and chimpanzees [71], enlarged second premolars relative to first molars (compared to both gorillas and chimpanzees; 20), high molar ACS (though less than that of gorillas [46], and strong molar flare (relative to gorillas and chimpanzees; 86).

If our interpretation of C. atys dental anatomy is correct, why are these features present in orangutans if the latter are not habitually processing mechanically challenging foods? Is the presence of these features at odds with the notion that orangutans are fallback eaters of hard/tough foods? For insight, we consider other aspects of orangutan masticatory anatomy and interpret dietary switching by orangutans in a slightly different manner. Taylor [100] used the fallback food concept to interpret differences in jaw morphology of Sumatran vs. Bornean orangutans. Using feeding data from multiple localities, she observed that although ripe fruit was preferred across all populations, orangutans on Borneo (Pongo pygmaeus morio and P. p. wurmbii) that more frequently consumed less‐preferred foods exhibited mandibular features indicative of greater load resistance, either to larger peak loads or more repetitive loading. Although comprehensive food material property data were not available for all populations, Taylor reasoned that the more robust mandibles of Bornean orangutans were associated with increased processing of hard and/or tough foods [100].

Perhaps, however, more significant for present purposes was Taylor's point that more generally, orangutans consume non‐preferred foods throughout entire annual cycles and in substantial quantities. Furthermore, Taylor observed that “…not all reportedly non‐preferred foods are truly fallback foods, in that many non‐preferred foods are consumed year‐round, and not simply when preferred foods are scarce” [100]. If such non‐preferred foods, which include leaves, woody tissues, and seeds, require increased loading, and if they are consumed year‐round, then it is reasonable to conclude that the masticatory apparatus (including teeth) of these orangutans is loaded with high bite force, at high frequency, or both, throughout the year. Such a conclusion does not nullify the possibility that dental form in orangutans evolved to process hard and/or tough foods during brief fallback periods. However, it does highlight that at least some orangutan populations consume mechanically challenging foods more than just during short periods of resource scarcity, a feeding pattern that could explain why features of sooty mangabey molars associated with frequent processing of mechanically challenging foods are also present in orangutans.

Conclusions

In this review, we synthesized results from recent studies documenting differences in dental form between C. atys and L. albigena. While differences in oral processing and/or dietary hardness may contribute to these dental differences, we argued that C. atys' feeding regime, in which hard‐object foods are eaten at high frequency year‐round, increases their risk of dental fracture through fatigue stress and overall exposure. In turn, that risk may explain why the posterior teeth of C. atys have multiple features that reduce their fracture risk, including high absolute crown strength, complex enamel decussation, and pronounced crown flare. Fallback foods have undoubtedly influenced primate dental form. Our comparison of mangabey postcanine teeth suggests that routinely eaten, non‐fallback hard‐object foods can have a more marked effect on dental form than hard‐object foods eaten intermittently as fallbacks.

We also argued that analysis of single aspects of dental form are less informative about hard‐object dietary adaptation than is the total dental morphological pattern, especially when it includes highly complex enamel decussation, currently the most reliable dental indicator of dietary adaptation to hard‐object foods. The difference in the total dental morphological patterns of C. atys and L. albigena appears to reflect their contrasting feeding regimes, in which there is a stark difference in the frequency with which they consume hard‐object foods throughout the year.

Orangutans also feed on non‐preferred items, such as bark, seeds, and unripe fruit throughout the year [100]. Their frequent consumption of these foods may explain why orangutans share aspects of dental form with C. atys despite their dietary and feeding differences. We do not suggest that one could perform an analysis of dental form in a fossil primate to discern whether hard‐object foods were eaten as filler fallbacks or as dietary staples. What we do suggest is that the degree of dental adaptation to hard‐object foods in fossil primates is likely to be influenced by how frequently hard‐object foods were eaten and how easily such foods could have been replaced by alternative resources (as might be the case for filler fallbacks eaten at low frequency). And, clearly, other factors are also expected to affect the degree of dental adaptation to hard‐object foods, such as the hardness of these foods and how they were processed both intra and extra‐orally. What needs to be better understood is how aspects of dental form believed to be associated with hard‐object foods covary with all these factors in living primates. Finally, how critical hard‐object foods are for survival and reproduction is likely to go beyond whether they were filler fallback, staple fallbacks, or frequently eaten non‐fallback foods to include their nutritional value. The possibility that some preferred hard‐object foods might provide critical nutrients, as the seeds of Mezzetia parviflora appear to provide for orangutans [50], could open a new avenue for research on relationships between hard‐object feeding and dental form in primates.
