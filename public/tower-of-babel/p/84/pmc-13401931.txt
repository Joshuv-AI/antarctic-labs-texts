Long-Term Effectiveness of Upadacitinib in Difficult-to-Treat Psoriatic Arthritis: A 104-week Real-World Clinical and Ultrasound Study
Authors: D Graceffa, F Sperati, M C Fargnoli, N Cameli, A Pacifico, I Demofonte, V Lora
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDifficult-to-treat psoriatic arthritis (D2T PsA) represents a challenging condition characterized by inadequate response to multiple therapeutic agents. Real-world data on the effectiveness of Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi) in this population remain limited.ObjectiveTo evaluate the effectiveness, safety, and treatment persistence of Upadacitinib (UPA) in patients with D2T PsA using both clinical and ultrasound assessments.Patients and MethodsThis observational retrospective study included patients with PsA who failed at least one conventional DMARD and two biologic DMARDs with different mechanisms of action. All patients received UPA 15 mg once daily as monotherapy. Treatment persistence was assessed over 140 weeks, while effectiveness data were collected at four time points following baseline (12, 24, 48, and 104 weeks). Clinical measures included: PASI; cDAPSA score; swollen/tender joints count; assessment of enthesitis and dactylitis; VAS scores for disease activity/pain; HAQ. At baseline and during the follow-up, ultrasound imaging was performed according to the EULAR-OMERACT ultrasound score.ResultsTwenty-five patients were enrolled. After 140 weeks, treatment persistence was 68%. A rapid and sustained improvement in disease activity was observed, with mean cDAPSA decreasing from 27.88±5.2 at baseline to 12.04±8.17 at week 12 and 4.88±4.54 at week 52. At one year, 70.6% of patients achieved complete remission and 23.6% low disease activity. Ultrasound findings confirmed the clinical improvement, with a significant reduction in the EULAR-OMERACT score and complete resolution of enthesitis after one year of treatment. Most patients (94.1%) maintained complete remission or low disease activity up to week 104. Skin condition also improved significantly as demonstrated by the marked reduction in PASI score which decreased from a baseline value of 2.22 ± 2.62 to 0.21 ± 0.36 after 52 weeks of treatment. The safety profile was favorable, with mild lipid and CPK abnormalities being the most common adverse events.ConclusionIn this real-world cohort of patients with D2T PsA, UPA demonstrated sustained clinical and ultrasound effectiveness with a favorable safety profile and good long-term treatment persistence.

Introduction

Treatment options currently available for the management of psoriatic arthritis (PsA) allow most patients to achieve and maintain complete remission or minimal disease activity.1–4 However, despite the sequential or combined use of multiple therapeutic agents, a substantial proportion of patients fail to respond to treatment (primary failure) or experience a progressive loss of response affecting the skin, the joints, or both domains (secondary failure).5,6 This subset of patients may be considered difficult to treat (D2T). The current definition of difficult-to-treat PsA (D2T PsA), formulated by GRAPPA and EULAR - although not yet fully established- includes not only cases of therapeutic resistance but also patients with relevant systemic comorbidities that may complicate clinical management.7–10 The main challenge in correctly classifying patients with D2T PsA lies in the need to assess treatment failure within the multi-domain clinical context of psoriatic disease. An interesting approach recently proposed suggests stratifying patients based on the persistence of inflammatory activity detected by ultrasound (US) despite ongoing therapy.11

Several strategies have been proposed for the management of patients with D2T PsA, ranging from combination therapies to the inhibition of new molecular targets, including IL-17A/F and Janus/tyrosine kinases; however, real-world data on the effectiveness of these approaches are extremely limited.12–15

Upadacitinib (UPA) is an oral, selective, and reversible inhibitor of Janus kinase 1 (JAK1). Based on the efficacy and safety demonstrated in the SELECT-PsA 1 and SELECT-PsA 2 randomized controlled trials (RCT), UPA 15 mg once daily was approved by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) for the treatment of active PsA in adult patients who have responded inadequately or are intolerant to one or more conventional DMARDs, either as monotherapy or in combination with methotrexate.16–18

This single-center study had the primary objective of evaluating the effectiveness and safety of UPA 15 mg once daily in patients with D2T PsA assessed both clinically and by US. Treatment persistence over an observation period of 140 weeks was evaluated as a secondary outcome.

Patients and Methods

This is an observational retrospective study focused specifically on patients with D2T PsA with ultrasound-detectable joint inflammation. D2T PsA was defined as primary or secondary failure of at least one conventional synthetic DMARD (csDMARD) and two biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs) with different mechanisms of action. At our center, in accordance with leading international guidelines, treatment is considered a failure if, after 12 weeks of therapy, the target of remission or low disease activity has not been achieved. Patients were recruited from the Experimental Clinical Dermatology Unit of the San Gallicano Dermatological Institute, Rome, Italy. Inclusion criteria were: age ≥18 years; active PsA classified according to Classification criteria for Psoriatic ARthritis (CASPAR)19 and previous failure of at least one csDMARD and two bDMARDs with different mechanisms of action. At baseline and at four scheduled time points (TPs), according to the standard outpatient clinical practice of our center, enrolled patients underwent dermatological assessment performed by a dermatologist (VL) trained in psoriasis management and rheumatological evaluation performed by a rheumatologist (DG).

Follow-up visits were performed after 12±4 (TP1); 24±4 (TP2); 52±4 (TP3); 104±4 (TP4) weeks of continuous treatment with UPA 15 mg once daily.

Clinical tools included: Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI); the swollen and tender joints count (SJC66/TJC68); presence or absence of enthesitis and dactylitis; visual analog scales (VAS) for global disease activity and pain; Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ). The clinical Disease Activity index for Psoriatic Arthritis (cDAPSA) was used as a composite score to evaluate treatment effectiveness.20

An US examination of joints and entheses was performed at all TPs by a single trained operator (DG) who was not blinded to patients treatment and medical history. US assessments were performed using an Esaote MyLab Eight system (Esaote SpA, Genoa, Italy) equipped with 6–18 MHz linear probe. In accordance with a recently published methodology,21 for each patient, the joint and enthesis with the highest baseline EULAR-OMERACT US composite score (target joint/enthesis) were selected for longitudinal follow-up and appropriate statistical comparisons. A semi-quantitative score ranging from 0 to 3 was used. It was calculated combining the severity scores for gray-scale (GS) and Power Doppler (PD) lesions: Grade 0 (normal); Grade 1 (minimal inflammatory involvement); Grade 2 (moderate inflammatory involvement); and Grade 3 (severe inflammatory involvement).22,23

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were reported as absolute frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables we expressed as means and standard deviations (SD). Data were analyzed both “as treated” and “as per intention to treat” using the last observation carried forward (ITT-LOCF) approach to handle missing data. In addition to the 5 scheduled TP points, a final observation TP—corresponding to the last visit before treatment discontinuation—was included in the statistical analysis. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Differences between continuous or ordinal variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon test, the paired Student’s t-test, or the Friedman test, as appropriate according to the data distribution and the number of time points considered. Nominal variables at different time points were compared using the McNemar test or the Cochran test, as appropriate. For multiple comparisons between TPs, the Bonferroni correction was applied (adjusted significance threshold: p ≤ 0.0125, based on 0.05/4). The Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate the time to treatment discontinuation. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software version 30 (SPSS Inc., Chicago IL, USA).

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Population

A total of 25 D2T PsA patients (12 females; 13 males) were enrolled. The mean age was 51.9±11.1 years. Eight patients (32%) were smokers, and most patients (56%) were overweight (average BMI = 28.6±4.2). The mean duration of joint disease at the start of UPA was 8.6±3.1 years. At baseline, all enrolled patients had active involvement of both joints and skin (mean baseline PASI = 2.22±2.6; mean baseline cDAPSA = 27.88±5.2). Twenty-one patients (84%) had plaque psoriasis, 2 patients (12%) were diagnosed with palmoplantar pustulosis, and 1 patient (4%) suffered from guttate psoriasis. Nail involvement was observed in 14 patients (56%). Baseline rheumatological assessment showed US-confirmed enthesitis in 14 patients (56%), while 3 patients (12%) had dactylitis affecting at least one finger. Two patients (8%) were diagnosed with spondylitis in addition to peripheral joint involvement; however, none of them reported inflammatory low back pain or other signs of active axial disease at the start of treatment with UPA. Furthermore, none of them were taking methotrexate. At study entry, all patients started monotherapy with UPA 15 mg once daily; none received concomitant methotrexate during the observation period.

Treatment Retention and Longitudinal Changes in Disease Activity

Five patients (20%) did not reach TP2 and three (12%) did not reach TP3 (Figure 1). Two patients (8%) discontinued therapy due to adverse events (1 for persistent flu-like syndrome; 1 for a new HBV infection). In this latter case, infectious disease screening performed before the start of UPA did not reveal any previous HBV infection, allowing us to rule out secondary viral reactivation due to therapy. Complete remission (cDAPSA < 4) was achieved in 70.6% of patients at TP3, while 23.6% of patients were in low disease activity (cDAPSA > 4 to ≤ 13). A slight decline in the proportion of patients in complete remission was observed at 104 weeks of therapy; however, it should be noted that the cumulative proportion of patients in low disease activity and complete remission remained stable at TP4. Consistent with the improvement in cDAPSA, the number of involved joints as well as enthesitis and dactylitis showed a significant improvement after treatment (p = 0.001).

In line with the clinical findings, patient-reported outcomes on functional status and disability also improved significantly, as demonstrated by the reduction in HAQ score from 1.15±0.5 to 0.48±0.28 after 1 year of therapy.

Findings from clinical examination were largely confirmed by US assessment. Specifically, the EULAR-OMERACT US score calculated on the target joint decreased significantly, from 2.24±0.52 at baseline to 1.05±0.83 and 0.71±0.59 after 6 and 12 months of treatment, respectively (p < 0.001). As expected, in the 6 patients who discontinued UPA due to treatment failure, the target joint persistently showed an US score > 2 at all assessments. Conversely, among patients who continued treatment throughout the follow-up, only one still had a score of 2 at assessment performed at week 52, while six patients showed minimal inflammatory activity on ultrasound of the affected joint (ultrasound score = 1).

The enthesitis assessed by US, also showed a marked improvement after treatment with UPA, with complete resolution after 1 year of therapy.

Of the 3 patients with dactylitis at baseline, only one had persistent disease at TP1, while no recurrence of dactylitis was observed in the other treated patients during follow-up.

Most of treated patients (94.1%) maintained complete remission or low disease activity until the last scheduled TP at 104 weeks of treatment.

Of the 2 patients with axial involvement, one discontinued treatment after 24 weeks due to the recurrence of peripheral arthritis, while the other showed overall clinical improvement in line with the average trend observed in the study population. During follow-up, no clinical signs of axial inflammatory involvement were observed in either patient.

Skin involvement also showed improvement, as demonstrated by the marked reduction in PASI score, which reached its nadir after 1 year of treatment (TP0 = 2.22±2.62 vs TP3 = 0.40±0.60; P< 0.001). All patients who continued treatment with UPA maintained PASI scores < 1 throughout the entire follow-up.

Safety

The most common adverse events were abnormal lipid profile, reported in 8 patients (32%) and increased CPK levels in 4 patients (16%). Both abnormalities were mild and did not require pharmacological intervention. No serious cardiovascular events or deaths occurred in our cohort.

Discussion

The definition of D2T PsA is still under discussion, but even more challenging is developing a shared therapeutic approach for managing this complex clinical condition.

Based on the limited immunological studies investigating drug resistance mechanisms, D2T PsA may result from the overlap of multiple, still unexplained immune escape mechanisms enabled by the flexibility of innate and cell-mediated immune responses.24

In this context, the identification of effective treatment options for these drug-resistant forms of disease remains one of the most pressing unmet needs in real-world clinical practice.

Our study explored effectiveness and safety of UPA in a cohort of patients with very high propensity for treatment failure. The UPA persistence rate observed in our study population was 68% at 104 weeks. Although these data should be interpreted with caution due to the small size of our sample, they were comparable to those reported in the SELECT PsA-2 study and its open-label extension, which showed a persistence of approximately 70% at 1 year in patients with previous inadequate response or intolerance to at least one biologic drug.25

Conversely, we observed higher persistence rates compared to two large cohorts based on Israeli and French insurance databases including more than 2,000 patients with PsA, which reported persistence rates of approximately 40–42% after 20 months of treatment.26,27

In line with our findings, the few real-world studies available in the literature, such as the UPJOINT study or the more recent study by Bakay et al, reported 1-year persistence rates between 60% and 70%.28,29 These studies mainly involved biologic-experienced patients who were not fully classifiable as D2T. Nevertheless, they reported similar efficacy outcomes to those observed in our cohort, particularly in composite disease activity scores, with remission or low disease activity rates of approximately 65–70% and complete resolution of enthesitis after 1 year of therapy.

A recent study conducted by Lopalco et al reported a higher treatment survival rate than ours (over 75% after one year of treatment).30 However, that study was affected by a potential bias related to the prevalence of fibromyalgia, which was identified by the authors themselves as a strong predictor of treatment failure. In our study, the extensive use of US across the entire cohort allowed us to reduce potential confounding factors such as osteoarthritis or fibromyalgia. Furthermore, enthesitis, which is often misdiagnosed, was objectively assessed by US.

In terms of efficacy, despite the higher propensity of treatment failure in our cohort, patients showed, a rapid and sustained response to UPA, with significant reduction in cDAPSA after only 12 weeks of therapy and approximately 68% of patients achieving complete remission or low disease activity. Furthermore, consistent with the results of the 3-year extension of SELECT PsA-2, our patients maintained their clinical response beyond 104 weeks. A slight decrease in the number of patients in complete remission was observed at 104 weeks of therapy. In our opinion, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as it is possible that the prolonged state of well-being led patients to return to activities involving greater mechanical load and joint stress, resulting in an increased perception of pain. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that there may have been a slight decline in the drug’s efficacy.

To date, there are no real-world studies evaluating long-term effectiveness of UPA using US. To our knowledge, the only available data derive from our previous study conducted on 14 patients with a 12-week follow-up.31 The UPARAREMUS study, conducted in patients with rheumatoid arthritis, reported findings consistent with ours regarding US remission rates achieved after 24 weeks of treatment with UPA.32

The favorable safety profile observed in our cohort was consistent with that reported in the aforementioned RCTs and real-world studies. Interestingly, despite the high percentage of obese and smoking patients in our study population, no clinically significant cardiovascular events were observed during follow-up.

Study Limitations and Conclusions

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective observational design and the relatively small sample size. In addition, no specific imaging investigation was performed to investigate the two patients with axial involvement. Despite these limitations, our findings demonstrating the high persistence, effectiveness, and safety of UPA in patients with D2T PsA suggest that the immunological mechanisms underlying failure of conventional biologic therapies may not necessarily apply to JAKi. Consequently, this class of drugs may represent a valuable therapeutic option for patients with PsA who have demonstrated a marked propensity for treatment failure or loss of clinical response.
