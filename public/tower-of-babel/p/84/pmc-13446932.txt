Reinforced Repair of Urethrovaginal Fistula Using Bovine Pericardial Patch
Authors: M. Rakhesh, M. M. Zameer, Vinay Chandrashekar, Ashley D’Cruz, Sanjay Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUrethrovaginal fistula (UVF) in children is rare and surgically challenging. We present an 11-year-old girl with UVF managed successfully with fistula closure reinforced with a bovine pericardial patch.

INTRODUCTION

Urethrovaginal fistula (UVF) in the pediatric age group is extremely uncommon. The causes include trauma, congenital anomalies, and complications of previous urologic surgery. Several studies have emphasized the importance of providing robust buttress support using vascularized pedicle flaps during repair. In our case, we utilized a biologic graft – bovine pericardium (BP) – which offers a durable and pliable scaffold suitable for reinforcement in scarred or delicate pediatric tissues.[12]

CASE REPORT

An 11-year-old girl presented with straining during urination and recurrent urinary tract infections. She had a history of pelvic trauma in early childhood that resulted in proximal urethral stricture, which was managed surgically. Despite this, her lower urinary tract symptoms persisted.

MRI showed thinning of the proximal urethra and a fistulous tract to the vagina; upper tracts were normal. Cystoscopy revealed a large urethrovaginal fistula measuring 1.5 cm × 1.5 cm, approximately 1 cm distal to the bladder neck. The distal urethra measured about 3 cm and appeared normal.

At surgery, urethral catheterization was difficult due to the fistula. This required cystoscopic placement of a guidewire and subsequent railroading of the catheter (Foley’s size 12 Fr).

The patient was positioned in the prone jack-knife position. With the help of a vaginal speculum, UVF was seen [Figure 1a]. Fistula edges were freshened, and dissection was carried out between the urethra and vagina. The urethral wall was approximated transversely [Figure 1b]. A commercially available small bovine pericardial patch (manufacturer: Developed by Glycar SA and distributed by Abbott) was placed over the urethral repair to reinforce the closure [Figure 2a]. The vaginal mucosa was closed longitudinally [Figure 2b].

Operative time was approximately 2 h, with minimal blood loss (<20 ml). Intraoperative leak testing was performed using saline instillation and revealed no leakage.

Postoperative recovery was uneventful. The urethral catheter was removed after 4 weeks. At 6 months follow-up, the patient remained continent with a good urinary stream, and postvoid residual urine was negligible on ultrasound imaging.

DISCUSSION

Pediatric UVF is rare, with limited reports describing its management. Repair principles focus on achieving a tension-free, multilayer closure, with careful freshening of edges and reinforcement when local tissue is insufficient. Adult UVF repair is well described, most commonly following obstetric injury, pelvic surgery, or radiation. Adult repairs often use labial fat (Martius flaps) or other local flaps for interposition, but these options are usually limited in prepubertal children due to smaller, hormonally immature tissues, minimal adipose bulk, and scarring from previous surgery. A few pediatric cases have described using rectus abdominis flaps, but such options are not always feasible.[34]

In this setting, biologic materials such as BP offer a practical alternative. BP is soft, durable, and integrates well with surrounding tissues, making it suitable for pediatric urethrovaginal fistulas, when native tissues may be hypoplastic or compromised by prior surgery. BP has been successfully used across multiple reconstructive urologic procedures – including urethral, bladder, and penile repairs. BP demonstrates high graft-take rates, low infection rates, and good long-term structural support. They undergo progressive host remodeling with fibroblast infiltration and neovascularization, resulting in durable integration and minimal chronic inflammatory response. BP can provide reinforcement comparable to adult interposition flaps while avoiding the limitations of small pediatric tissue volume.[5]

Alternative biologic grafts used in urethral reconstruction include porcine small intestinal submucosa (SIS), porcine dermal matrices, and human acellular dermal grafts. Porcine SIS has been used in adult and pediatric urethroplasty and fistula repair as a remodeling scaffold, but concerns regarding graft contraction and variable durability have been reported. Acellular dermal matrices are mainly reserved for complex reconstructions and are limited by cost and sparse pediatric outcome data. In this context, BP offers a favorable balance of strength, pliability, and availability, making it a practical option for reinforcement in complex pediatric urethral repair when local tissue is compromised.[678]

In this patient, the urethrovaginal fistula likely developed due to ischemia and fibrosis following prior urethral reconstruction. Several factors made the case particularly challenging. Pediatric anatomy – characterized by a short urethral length, narrow vaginal canal, and delicate tissues – combined with scarring from previous surgery, increases the risk of repair failure. The prone jack-knife position provided superior exposure for the transvaginal approach in this child, in contrast to adults in whom repair is typically performed in the lithotomy position. BP reinforcement provided a stable scaffold over the scarred proximal urethra, facilitating a tension-free multilayer closure.

Long-term follow-up is essential, as pediatric patients are at risk for graft contraction, stricture recurrence, or fistula reformation. The 6-month follow-up in this case represents short-term success and cannot predict long-term outcomes. Clinical assessment, uroflowmetry, and imaging as indicated are recommended. While literature on UVF in children remains limited, this case supports the feasibility and efficacy of BP reinforcement in complex pediatric urethral reconstruction.

An additional point of interest in this case was preserved continence despite a large fistula distal to the bladder neck. This was likely due to an intact bladder neck mechanism and preserved distal urethral sphincter function.

CONCLUSION

Urethrovaginal fistula in children can be successfully repaired using multilayer fistula closure reinforced with a bovine pericardial patch. The technique provides a reliable repair, restores urinary function, and achieves continence. Long-term follow-up is essential to monitor urethral patency and graft integrity.
