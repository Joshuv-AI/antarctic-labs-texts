Antiseptic chitosan acetate-chlorhexidine spray for wound cleaning
Authors: Lokitha Paduvetnaya, Joel Maria Joju, Tanushree Suresh, Anjana Ajithkumar, Harishkumar Madhyastha, Jayakumar Rangasamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe presence of microbial biofilms in acute and chronic wound infections creates a significant dermal complication as these biofilms interfere with wound healing and complicate the regenerative process. Effective cleaning of wound bed remains essential because it eliminates necrotic materials, biofilms, and contaminants leading to faster treatment and decreased secondary infections. A novel antiseptic spray formulation using chitosan acetate (CA) and chlorhexidine (CHX) was developed aiming infection control. In the present study, an antiseptic CA-CHX spray, with poly (vinyl alcohol) (PVA) as the film-forming agent was formulated. FTIR studies confirmed the incorporation of CHX in the CA-CHX spray. FESEM images showed spherical shape of the particles with an average size of 1μm. The CA-CHX spray exhibited excellent spraying ability, remarkable film-forming property and stability across different temperature ranges. In vitro antibacterial and anti-biofilm activity was studied against Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and MRSA. In vivo fly infection model using wild-type Drosophila melanogaster further proved antibacterial property of the CA-CHX spray against these bacteria. To understand the cytotoxicity effect of CA and CA-CHX spray, cell viability study was conducted against the L929 cells. These research findings demonstrated that the developed antiseptic CA-CHX spray could be effectively used for wound cleaning.

Introduction

Wound infection initiates through microbial contamination and colonization, followed by disruption of the immune system, thus delaying the wound healing process [1]. The presence of biofilms at the wound site also contributes towards the delayed healing process and decreases the efficacy of antibiotic and antiseptic agents [2]. Proper debridement and cleaning of the wound site helps to remove the necrotic tissues or biofilms that stall the healing process [3]. Clinicians uses different techniques such as surgical, autolytic, enzymatic, mechanical, biological, and topical solutions in order to remove biofilms and necrotic tissues from the wound micro-environment [4]. In this aspect, effective debridement and cleaning of the wound sites to free it from infection causing organisms and biofilms gains importance. Traditionally, Povidone-iodine, hydrogen peroxide, octenidine, sodium hypochlorite, and polyhexanide are some such currently used antiseptics for cleaning wounds [5, 6].

The cationic polymer chitosan possesses many properties like biocompatibility, non-toxicity, biodegradability, and antibacterial property which enable its use in biomedical applications [7]. In order to overcome the limitation of low water solubility of chitosan, chemical modifications are made on the chitosan structure to form its derivatives [8]. Chitosan acetate (CA) is one such water-soluble chitosan derivative. CA retains the antibacterial properties of chitosan as the aqueous solution of CA has similar structure as that of chitosan which is dissolved in acetic acid [9]. In this work we have made use of CA to develop a topical antiseptic spray solution. Chlorhexidine (CHX) is a common biguanide antiseptic which is being used clinically. Its ability to cause damage to the bacterial membranes and precipitation of cell components impart antimicrobial property to CHX [6].

Clinically, many topical solutions are being used for wound care and management. Excellent coverage of the wound site, uniform distribution of the bioactives, ease of use, sterility, and low irritability makes the use of topical sprays prominent over other topical dosages [10]. Later on, film-forming spray solutions gained importance. These film-forming solutions can form films over the wounds and thereby increase the contact time of the bioactives in the formulation [11, 12]. Another advantage of film-forming solutions is that, they can be used on wounds of any shape, size, and depth. In short, film-forming spray solutions use thin films, which are ideal for rough and irregular surfaces for providing extended drug release, more permeable and easier dermal penetration [11, 13]. The novelty of the present work includes, unlike prior studies, this work introduces a sprayable formulation of chitosan acetate, chlorhexidine and PVA with combined properties of film-formation and antibacterial efficiency. The film that forms over the wounds will prolong the contact time of the chlorhexidine in the formulation to act on the infectious organisms. In this way, the film-forming and the antibacterial activity of the spray will help in effectively reducing the infections at the wound site. The film also enables rapid and uniform coverage of irregular wound surfaces, and creates a protective barrier over the wound site.

In this work, we formulated CA-CHX antiseptic spray for wound cleaning. Poly (vinyl alcohol) (PVA) is added to this mixture to introduce film-forming property to the spray solution [14–17]. The CHX will give antibacterial property to the spray solution and this will help in effectively eliminating the pathogens from the wound site. The film-forming aspect of the spray formulation will help in closer and increased contact time of the bioactives with the infection-causing organisms at the wound site.

Materials and methods

Materials

Chitosan acetate (DD > 88%, Water soluble, Viscosity: 50–80 cP) was purchased from Swakit Biotech Pvt Ltd, Bengaluru, India. PVA (98%, typical MW: 13,000–23,000 g/mL) was obtained from Sigma Aldrich, USA. Chlorhexidine (MW: 505.45 g/mL) was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry (TCI), Japan. Tween-20 was procured from SD Fine Chem-Limited (SD Fine Chemical Ltd., Mumbai, India). Luria Bertani broth and Agar-agar powder were supplied by HiMedia, India. Primary cultures of E. coli, S. aureus, and P. aeruginosa strains were taken from the Microbiology laboratory, AIMS, Kochi, India

Preparation of CA and CA-CHX spray

Chitosan acetate (CA) flakes were dissolved in water to prepare a 1.5% (w/v) chitosan acetate solution. 5% (w/v) of PVA solution was then prepared by heating at 60 °C and were added to CA solution in the ratio of 5:1 (CA:PVA). This obtained CA spray was used as the control throughout the study. To prepare CA-CHX spray, chlorhexidine (CHX) solution was prepared by dissolving it in 2% (v/v) Tween-20 (1.5 mg/mL), and later on mixed with CA solution in the ratio 1:1 (Fig. 1). Thus prepared CA and CA-CHX spray formulations were stored in spray bottles for further studies. Parameters such as pH, density and viscosity (Brookfield Viscometer) of both spray solutions were measured and noted.

Preparation of CA-CHX spray

Field emission scanning electron microscopy

Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) imaging was utilized to study the surface features along with microstructural characteristics of the developed CA and CA-CHX spray solutions. The solutions were sprayed onto stubs and were kept at room temperature until it has completely dried. FESEM analysis of samples were performed using JEOL JSM-6490LA at an accelerating voltage from 5- 15 kV.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) spectral analysis was used to determine the chemical composition of the CA and CA-CHX sprays. The technique served both to verify the presence of CA, PVA, and CHX components in the spray formulation as well as to understand their potential molecular interaction patterns. Shimadzu Corp, Tokyo was used to record spectra through mid-infrared scanning from 4000–500 cm⁻¹.

Thickness of the film formed by the sprays

The prepared formulations were sprayed onto petri plates and stored for 6 h in a 37 °C incubator to test their film-forming ability. The thickness of spray films was measured multiple times with a Mitutoyo screw gauge and the average thickness of the films were calculated and recorded.

Stability of the spray solutions

To conduct the stability test of the sprays, vials containing both CA and CA-CHX spray solutions were stored at two different temperatures, 4 °C (refrigerator) and 37 °C (incubator). The stability of solutions was observed for a period of 28 days with constant monitoring for changes in physical appearance and possible color changes. Photographic images were taken after.

Spray angle of the spray solutions

The spray angle test for the prepared spray solutions was conducted by spraying the solutions onto a filter paper, kept at a distance of 15 cm (d) away from the nozzle of the spray bottle. In vitro antibacterial study

The in vitro antibacterial activity of CA-CHX spray was evaluated against E. coli, S. aureus and the clinical isolate MRSA by the well diffusion method, while keeping CA spray as the control. Luria Bertani (LB) agar plates were uniformly spread with the respective overnight bacterial suspensions and wells were created. 70 µL of CA and CA-CHX spray were then loaded into the wells. The zone of inhibition was measured after incubating these plates for overnight at 37 °C [13]. This study was performed in triplicate. To further evaluate the antibacterial efficacy of the developed CA-CHX spray, five different clinical isolates of S. aureus were streaked in lines onto the LB agar plates and wells were dug in the middle. 70 µL of CA-CHX spray was added and the plates were incubated overnight. The zone of inhibition around the samples were observed at the end of the incubation period.

In vitro anti-biofilm assay

The anti-biofilm study was conducted as per previous protocol with slight modifications [20]. To conduct in vitro biofilm inhibition assay, E. coli and S. aureus were grown in 1 mL of LB broth containing 2% glucose onto sterile coverslips placed in the wells of a 12-well plate. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h for biofilm formation. Following the incubation, all the solutions were removed and fresh LB broth and 2% glucose solutions were added in to the wells along with CA (70 µL) and CA-CHX (70 µL and 140 µL) sprays mixed with 100 mg of 2% chitosan gel. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After the incubation period, the samples were extracted and the coverslips were washed with 1 mL PBS and allowed to dry overnight. These coverslips were then stained using 0.1% acridine orange dye for 5 min to observe qualitative inhibition of the biofilm. The qualitative inhibition of biofilm was observed under a fluorescence microscope and the ImageJ software was utilized during the conversion of these images into surface plots. The stained coverslips were then incubated overnight with 1 mL of 70% acetone to extract the dye and quantify the results. The fluorescence intensity was determined at an excitation of 400 nm and emission of 525 nm.

In vivo antibacterial study using Drosophila melanogaster

Banana-agar based culture medium maintained at 37 °C were used to feed the Drosophila melanogaster flies for in vivo antibacterial studies. The prepared banana-agar paste was mixed with boiled water to form a runny consistency and were transferred into glass vials. To infect, female flies between ages of two and five days were chosen. Each treatment group was tested in triplicate in batches of 10 flies. The flies were anesthetized by keeping them in ice and infected by injecting on the dorsal thorax using a 25-gauge needle dipped halfway into culture of S. aureus and E. coli. Flies were reintroduced into vials containing CA and CA-CHX sprays following infection. The percentage of surviving flies was then used to express the survivability, which was obtained at 0, 3, 6, 9 and 12 h after infection [21].

In vitro cell viability study of CA and CA-CHX spray

Alamar blue assay was performed using L929 fibroblast cells, to evaluate the cytotoxic effects of CA and CA-CHX spray formulations [20]. In a 12 well plate, cells were seeded at a density of 105 cells/well. Cells were maintained in complete DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution. Spray formulations were added into each well and was incubated for 24 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After incubation, media was discarded followed by a PBS wash. 10% AlamarBlue reagent was added and incubated for 4 h. Absorbance was then measured at 570 and 600 nm using a microplate reader.

Results

Preparation and characterization of CA and CA-CHX sprays

The antiseptic spray formulation was developed by dissolving 1.5 g of CA in 100 mL of water. A 5% (w/v) PVA solution was then combined with the CA solution in the ratio 5:1 (CA:PVA) and then mixed with 0.075% (w/v) CHX to formulate the CA-CHX spray. CA spray without CHX was also prepared and used as control throughout the entire study. The pH of the spray solutions was maintained at 6.5. The density of the CA and CA-CHX spray was 1.02 ± 0.03 and 0.98 ± 0.03 g/mL respectively whereas the viscosity of the solutions was 3.7 and 3.3 cP respectively. The FESEM images of CA and CA-CHX spray formulations are given in Fig. 2A. The CA-CHX spray particles displayed smooth and dense surface (magnification - 5000 X and 10,000 X) as shown in Fig. 2A.

A FESEM images of CA and CA-CHX spray (B) FTIR spectra of CA (chitosan acetate), CHX (chlorhexidine), and CA-CHX spray

In the FTIR spectrum of chitosan acetate, the -OH and -NH₂ stretching bands produced a wide peak at 3400 cm⁻¹ together with peak assignments at 1655 cm⁻¹ (amide I), 1580 cm⁻¹ (amide II), and 1020–1070 cm⁻¹ for C–O–C stretching (Fig. 2B) [22]. Double bond stretching and N-H stretching with C-H bending appeared in chlorhexidine between 3310–3360 cm⁻¹ and 1610–1630 cm⁻¹ and 1510–1580 cm⁻¹ respectively. A more intense broad -OH/NH stretching signal appeared at 3400 cm−1 in the chitosan acetate-chlorhexidine spray formulation. Peaks displayed between 1090–1140 cm⁻¹ (C–O stretching) and 2920–2940 cm⁻¹ (C–H stretching) indicates PVA presence in the spray formulations.

Sprayability and film-forming ability of CA and CA-CHX sprays

The CA spray was prepared by dissolving 1.5 g of CA in water, and then mixed with 5% (w/v) PVA whereas to prepare CA-CHX spray, 0.075% (w/v) CHX was additionally added to the above mixture. These spray formulations had water-like consistency, making it easier to spray thus confirming the sprayability (Fig. 3A).

Photographic images showing (A) the sprayability of CA and CA-CHX spray and (B) the film formed by CA-CHX spray

The CA-CHX spray formed thin film upon spraying and drying (Fig. 3B). The thickness of the films formed by the control and the CA-CHX spray was measured and tabulated. The average film thickness of the CA and CA-CHX spray formulation was found to be 0.184 ± 0.002 mm and 0.078 ± 0.001 mm, respectively.

Spray angle of CA and CA-CHX spray

Spray angle is the indication of the solution’s capability to reach wounds of different size, shape and depth. The spray angle of the prepared CA and CA-CHX spray formulations were calculated using the formula mentioned in the experimental section. The diameters of the spray pattern were measured to obtain the radii, the (d/r) values and their corresponding tan-(1) (d/r) values were calculated. The average spray angles of the formulations were found to be 71.106 ± 0.32° and 75.03 ± 0.16° respectively for CA and CA-CHX spray.

Stability test of CA and CA-CHX spray

To conduct the stability test, the prepared CA and CA-CHX spray formulations were stored in vials and kept at two temperatures, at 4 °C in a refrigerator, and 37 °C in the incubator for 28 days to analyse their stability. The developed spray formulations showed no changes in appearance (Fig. 4), including formation of precipitations or discolouration, viscosity variations, or sedimentation during the whole investigation period, indicating the stable nature of the spray formulations.

Stability of the prepared spray formulations up to 28 days at temperatures of 4 °C and 37 °C

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity of the developed CA and CA-CHX spray was examined via agar well diffusion assay against gram-positive S. aureus, and gram-negative E. coli bacteria. The zone of inhibition exhibited by CA-CHX spray for E. coli and S. aureus was recorded to be 16.75 ± 1.60 mm and 18.75 ± 1.17 mm, respectively (Fig. 5A–C). The observed zones of inhibition indicate that the CA-CHX spray is effective against both bacteria, with slightly greater activity against S. aureus than E. coli (Fig. 5A–C). Figure 5B shows the activity of the developed CA-CHX spray against the MRSA strains indicating the potential of the CA-CHX spray in inhibiting clinical strains.

In vitro antibacterial activity of CA and CA-CHX spray against (A) ATCC strains of E. coli and S. aureus (B) clinical isolate of S. aureus (MRSA) and (C) quantification of zone of inhibition

Anti-biofilm activity

The anti-biofilm activity of CA-CHX spray was also tested against E. coli and S. aureus using the visualization of a 3D surface plot (Fig. 6A). In the case of S. aureus, CA-CHX at 70 μL decreased the surface area coverage and the vertical height of biofilm peaks, which is an indication of biofilm inhibition. Similarly, at 140 μL of CA-CHX spray, the biofilm structures were nearly absent, exhibiting a high anti-biofilm effect. The same was largely true for E. coli, and in this case, 70 µL of CA-CHX concentration substantially reduced the biofilm density, and 140 µL further reduced the surface growth, with only some structural remains being visible. Together, these findings demonstrated that CA-CHX spray exhibited strong anti-biofilm effects against gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria. The quantitative study of biofilm reduction (Fig. 6B) further gives the evidence of the ability of the CA-CHX spray in inhibiting biofilms formed by E coli and S. aureus.

In vitro (A) qualitative and (B) quantitative anti-biofilm study of CA and CA-CHX spray

In vitro antibacterial activity using D. melanogaster fly model

The in vitro antibacterial activity of the CA and CA-CHX spray was also determined using wild-type Drosophila melanogaster (fruit fly) infection model, where the flies were infected with E. coli and S. aureus, and their survival was monitored. Figure 7A represents the survival curves after treating the E. coli infected flies with the CA-CHX spray. In the group infected with E. coli, the flies treated with CA-CHX displayed ≈ 70% survival after 12 h of the experiment, while the untreated control group displayed quick mortality, as the survival decreased to less than 10% at the experiment endpoint. The CA spray treated flies displayed intermediate survival of ≈ 20% after 12 h. A similar tendency was also observed in the S. aureus-infected model (Fig. 7B). The CA-CHX-treated group had ≈ 80% survival, whereas flies in the untreated control group once more showed the sharpest drop in viability after 12 h. The CA spray group revealed moderate protection with ≈ 20% survival.

Survival plots of in vivo antibacterial activity of CA and CA-CHX spray against (A) E. coli and (B) S. aureus using D. melanogaster (C) cell viability in L929 cells for 24 h

In vitro cell viability study of CA and CA- CHX spray

The biocompatibility of CA and CA-CHX spray was studied using L929 fibroblast cells for 24 h. Cell alone, CA spray, CA-CHX spray at various volumes such as 10 µL, 35 µL, and 70 µL were the groups used for this study. The results showed 100% cell viability for cells alone and 84% cell viability for CA spray. But the CA-CHX spray used at different volumes not showed any cell viability (Fig. 7C).

Discussion

In this work, chitosan acetate (CA) and chlorhexidine (CHX) based film-forming antiseptic spray has been developed. The water-soluble CA provides biocompatible and non-toxic properties to the spray whereas CHX imparts the broad-spectrum antibacterial ability. PVA in the preparation helps in film-forming ability of the spray. Characterization of the spray solution by FESEM revealed even distribution of CA-CHX spray particles. The surface of the particles displayed a smooth, homogenous appearance and the CA-CHX spray particles showed size around 1 µm. FTIR showed the characteristic peaks of CA, CHX and PVA in the final CA-CHX formulation. A more intense broad -OH/NH stretching signal appeared in the CA-CHX spray formulation can may be attributed to the extensive hydrogen bond interactions between all components. Weakening of both amide and aromatic signals verifies the formation of ionic interaction between CA and CHX [23]. The sprayability test showed that the prepared solution can be evenly distributed with adequate wound coverage. Spray angle is the indication of the solution’s potential to reach wounds of different size, shape and depth. The developed spray solution showed good spray angles, as the previous studies on film-forming spray formulations reported that a spray angle below 85° is considered good [24]. The film that the spray formed will let the active ingredients in the preparation to have sufficient contact time with the wound surface in order to express its effects. Length of time exposure to the wound surface and management of drug delivery can be regulated through film-forming properties, which simultaneously provide defence against external contaminants [25]. The stability test of the spray indicated the capability of the solution to be kept at 4˚C and 37˚C. The spray solutions stayed stable without undergoing precipitation, or contamination for up to 28 days at these temperatures. The stable composition allows antimicrobial properties to remain effective while it guards against ingredient breakdown.

The CHX in the composition contribute towards the antibacterial effect of the spray. The cationic molecules in CHX attach to negative bacterial cell membranes through an interaction with the gram-negative phosphate groups. This chemical binding causes the bacterial membrane instability causing cells to leak cellular components such as potassium ions and phosphorus through the cell membrane. High concentration levels of CHX are known to cause the coagulation of cytoplasmic proteins and nucleic acids while killing the bacteria [26, 27]. The spray showed antibacterial activity against both S. aureus and E. coli. The inhibition zones for E. coli was slightly lower than the zone of inhibition obtained for S. aureus. This could be reflecting comparable levels of resistance, possibly due to structural defences, such as outer membrane barriers and active efflux systems. The antibacterial activity of the CA-CHX spray was further proved by evaluating the efficiency of the spray against the clinical isolates. Further the in vitro anti-biofilm studies were conducted and the surface plots detected dense vertical projections, indicating that both strains had thick and extensive biofilm structures within the untreated bacterial control groups. These results foster the strong inherent ability of S. aureus and E. coli to develop biofilms under the experimental conditions. The addition of CHX to the formulation showed inhibition of the biofilms. Both 70 µL and 140 µL of the spray solutions were able to reduce the biofilm significantly compared to the bacterial control with p < 0.001. However, the concentration of 140 µL of CA-CHX spray showed enhanced biofilm inhibition compared to 70 µL of CA-CHX spray (p < 0.001). The CA-CHX spray formulation had better impact on the biofilm reduction in both E. coli and S. aureus, as compared to the control CA spray. In vivo studies on D. melanogaster flies were conducted to further prove the antibacterial efficiency of the CA-CHX spray. The bacteria-infected flies showed substantial survival when treated with the spray. These findings mean that the CA-CHX is better than the CA spray at enhancing survival of flies against lethality caused by the bacteria. The cell viability test was conducted in L929 fibroblast cells to learn more about the biocompatibility of the prepared spray. The CA (control) spray showed 84% viability whereas the CA-CHX spray did not showed any cell viability. This may be explained on the basis of the use of higher concentration of the antiseptic agent CHX along with Tween-20. Earlier reports such as Faria et al. suggested the concentration of 0.002% CHX showed toxic to fibroblast cells and undergoes apoptosis and necrosis [28]. But in this study we used CHX higher than the reported value. We chose concentration of CHX as 0.075% in order for the CA-CHX spray to effectively inhibit the microorganisms at the wound site. Similarly, in this study, we used tween-20 for solubilizing the CHX drug. Tween −20 is also known to exert toxicity to L929 cells. In Lu et al., tween-20 showed inhibition of L929 cell proliferation approximately 50% at concentrations 0.06–0.08% [29]. The lack of cell viability showed by CA-CHX spray is may be due to the combined effect of CHX and tween-20. But the spray formulation developed in this study is intended primarily for wound cleaning rather than wound healing. The main aim of formulating this spray is to rapidly reduce the bacterial burden on the wound surface, thereby minimizing the risk of infection. This creates a cleaner environment for subsequent healing interventions. The spray is applied briefly and then removed, limiting the duration of cellular exposure and the likelihood of tissue damage. Thus, the primary utility of this formulation lies in its ability to clear bacterial contamination efficiently and not in promoting cellular proliferation or tissue regeneration. The importance of the prepared CA-CHX spray lies in the combined effect of the film-forming as well as the antibacterial efficiency of the spray. The spray when sprayed on to the wound will form a very thin film over the wound when kept there for a short period of time. This will aid in the better and increased contact time of the antibacterial agent CHX in the formulation to act on the wound with improved performance when compared to commercially available wound spray products. The in vitro studies confirmed the antibacterial as well as anti-biofilm effectiveness of the spray, which inhibit and reduce the infection-causing organisms at the wound site. These results point towards the potential of CA-CHX spray to be used as an antiseptic film-forming spray solution for cleaning wounds.

Conclusion

In the current study, we developed a chitosan acetate (CA) and chlorhexidine (CHX) antiseptic spray formulation that integrates antimicrobial performance with delivery techniques based on film formation to solve existing wound care limitations, specifically in biofilm-associated infection management. The FESEM examination showed specific spherical structural feature of the CA-CHX spray, while the FTIR depicted successful incorporation of the components in the formulation. The CA-CHX spray successfully exhibited good sprayability, spray angle together with effective film formation, which will enable smooth application on irregular wound surfaces. The spray was found to be stable at 4 ˚C and 37 ˚C, and these temperature conditions can be considered as the storing temperatures. The in vitro antibacterial and anti-biofilm studies indicated that the CA-CHX spray exhibited antibacterial effects against E. coli and S. aureus, with significant inhibition zones and biofilm reduction. Furthermore, in vivo wild-type D. melanogaster fly model showed the potential of CA-CHX spray to be used as an effective antiseptic. The biocompatibility of the prepared CA-CHX spray was conducted in L929 cells and shows cytotoxicity. In conclusion, CA-CHX spray formulation represents a promising biocompatible, effective, and versatile topical spray for wound cleaning.
