Diagnosing acute bilirubin encephalopathy in neonates using MRI-based deep learning model
Authors: Kun Huang, Jin Wang, Qinzhu Yang, Gengbiao Zhang, Hongyi Zheng, Yi Gao, Wenbin Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeonatal acute bilirubin encephalopathy (ABE) severely endangers the neonatal health. However, early clinical symptoms of ABE are nonspecific, often leading to missed diagnoses. The current study endeavors to establish a computer-assisted integrated model for clinical assessment and diagnosis of ABE.MethodsDiagnostic data from the ABE group and the hyperbilirubinemia without concurrent ABE (non-ABE) group were retrospectively analyzed. Patients were divided into a pre-training cohort, a training cohort, and two test cohorts. The training cohort and test cohort 1 were used to train and test a deep learning (DL) model integrating multimodal, self-supervised, and multi-instance learning. Test cohort 2 was used to compare the DL model with the radiologists.ResultsA total of 1048 magnetic resonance images from 262 patients were analyzed. The accuracy of the DL model and the area under the curve were 86.3% and 91.2% and 91.1% and 89.3% in test cohorts 1 and 2, respectively.ConclusionsThis study integrated clinical and radiological data into DL models to accurately diagnose ABE, close to the proficiency level of senior radiologists. It provides a convenient, low-cost evaluation model for patient management decisions and physician diagnoses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06150-1.

Background

Neonatal hyperbilirubinemia is one of the most prevalent neonatal diseases, primarily manifesting as neonatal jaundice [1]. It is caused by dysregulation in bilirubin metabolism. Some evidence indicates that approximately 60% of full-term infants and 80% of preterm infants exhibit jaundice during the first week of life [2, 3]. Most cases of jaundice are “physiological,” with the majority of affected infants recovering through short-term phototherapy [4]. Only about 0.01% of newborns may develop acute bilirubin encephalopathy (ABE), also known as kernicterus, due to the accumulation of bilirubin, potentially leading to neonatal death or permanent neurodevelopmental delay [5].

Currently, the severity of neonatal hyperbilirubinemia is assessed based on bilirubin levels, with excessively high levels potentially resulting in ABE [6]. However, evidence suggests that individual factors significantly influence the level of bilirubin in the brain; thus, bilirubin levels alone cannot serve as the sole criterion for assessing ABE [7]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), in conjunction with observations of neonatal neurological manifestations, is the primary and widely used method for the evaluating and clinically diagnosing ABE [8]. MRI sequences typically include T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and diffusion-weighted imaging. These images provide diverse biological parameters for neuropathological changes associated with ABE, with multiple studies indicating the presence of characteristic imaging markers in standard magnetic resonance images (MRIs) for identifying ABE [9–11]. The pathological basis of these imaging markers may be related to bilirubin neurotoxicity, which induces astrocyte responses and leads to the accumulation of bilirubin in neurons, ultimately damaging neuronal cell membranes [12]. However, early clinical symptoms of ABE are either absent or mild and non-specific, greatly increasing the probability of missed diagnosis based on subjective interpretation of imaging markers alone. This delay can result in missing the optimal treatment window, thereby affecting the long-term prognosis and survival of the affected infants [13]. Distinguishing whether or not infants with hyperbilirubinemia have concurrent ABE remains a challenging issue [14].

Artificial intelligence has been extensively used for the prediction and prognosis of diseases. In particular, deep learning (DL) and machine learning algorithms for classifying medical images have gained widespread recognition among clinical physicians [15]. Herein, we propose a DL model that fuses radiological and clinical characteristics to establish a classifier for distinguishing between ABE and hyperbilirubinemia without concurrent acute bilirubin encephalopathy (non-ABE). This model may facilitate early screening and prediction of neonates with ABE, thereby mitigating further bilirubin-induced neonatal nervous system damage. Additionally, the model will offer clinicians an objective, non-invasive, reproducible, and more accurate diagnostic method.

Methods

Ethical statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and the Medical Science Research Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Shantou University Medical College. Additionally, during the data collection process, we performed data cleaning by removing personal information and retaining only numerical identifiers.Since this study is a retrospective study, the ethics committee approved the waiver of informed consent.

Study patients

Since this study involves DL models and considering the potential biases that may arise during the training process, we collected data from infants born in different regions and at different times to ensure the diversity of the data. The data were obtained from routine MRIs and clinical records of 2468 neonates with hyperbilirubinemia, who were treated at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Shantou University Medical College between May 2020 and November 2023, including those diagnosed with Acute Bilirubin Encephalopathy by physicians. All neonatal patients had recurrent jaundice symptoms and met the criteria for hyperbilirubinemia in serum markers, requiring MRI examination to determine whether ABE had occurred in the brain [16]. Additionally, all neonate patients underwent MRI within one week of jaundice onset. Although patients were treated with phototherapy upon admission for the diagnosis of hyperbilirubinemia, they still met our inclusion criteria based on blood gas indicators afterward, requiring further MRI for diagnosis.

Considering that the patients are neonates and it is not advisable to administer sedatives multiple times for MRI scans, all patients underwent a single MRI examination. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) gestational age greater than 35 weeks, (2) age at admission less than 28 days, (3) total serum bilirubin (TSB) levels above the 95th percentile on the neonatal bilirubin hour-specific chart [17], (4) absence of intracranial infections or hemorrhages, (5) absence of systemic diseases such as sepsis that could affect brain function and metabolism, (6) absence of neonatal asphyxia or neonatal respiratory distress syndrome, (7) absence of cerebral developmental abnormalities, and (8) the MRI was performed within the first week of jaundice onset in these cases. A summary of the inclusion and exclusion criteria for patients is presented in Fig. 1, a total of 262 patients were included in the study. Based on the 2004 American Academy of Pediatrics guidelines for the management of neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, patients were divided into two groups: non-ABE and ABE, with 125 neonates in the ABE group and 137 in the non-ABE group. Specifically, the classification criteria included clinical features such as the onset time and degree of jaundice post-birth, neurological assessment indicators (fever, lethargy, convulsions, crying, muscle tone, responses to external stimuli, and sucking reflex), and laboratory tests (total serum bilirubin, indirect bilirubin, direct bilirubin, albumin, bilirubin/albumin ratio, albumin/globulin ratio, neonatal anemia, and neonatal hemolytic disease), and MRI findings of symmetric high signals in the bilateral globus pallidus on T1-weighted imaging (T1) and T2-weighted imaging (T2). All patients were diagnosed by two attending radiologists and one experienced pediatric clinician according to the 2004 American Academy of Pediatrics guidelines for the management of hyperbilirubinemia in newborns.Fig. 1Inclusion and exclusion criteria. For subsequent deep learning research, we selected 47 high-quality MRI images exclusively from 137 non-ABE patients for pre-training in the self-supervised learning module to reduce bias in the downstream task models. These 47 images were used only for the pre-training stage and were excluded from the subsequent classification model training and testing phases to avoid data leakage. The remaining images from 215 patients, including 90 non-ABE patients and 125 ABE patients, were then divided into training cohort, test cohort 1, and test cohort 2 in a 3:2:1 ratio through random seeds to ensure adequate model training while also avoiding overfitting due to an insufficient number of test samples.

Data acquisition and preprocessing

All MRIs were acquired using a 3.0T MRI scanner (GE Signa HDxt 3.0T MR system). The imaging protocol included the following sequences: axial T1, axial T2, and axial diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI). The echo times for these three sequences were 24.0, 109.1, and 74.5 ms, respectively; the repetition times were 2000, 4600, and 5200 ms, respectively; with a slice thickness of 5.0 mm and a spacing of 1.5 mm. The three modalities were selected as inputs and the following formula was used to calculate the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps from the axial DWI as the fourth modality input.

All MRIs were subjected to a slicing procedure, with slices containing the globus pallidus (GP) selected by two radiologists each with five years of experience. The segment anything model was utilized to skull-strip and remove background noise from all slices, preventing irrelevant information from affecting the outcomes [18]. The processed images were uniformly resized to 368 × 368 pixels, and image intensity values were normalized to a range of 0 to 1.

Development of the classification model

A self-supervised multi-instance model (SSMIM) for classifying ABE and non-ABE. The model was divided into three modules: the pre-training block, the extraction block, and the classifier. The SSMIM model is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Most DL models for medical imaging use initial parameters that are pre-trained and fine-tuned on the public ImageNet database [19]. This involves pre-training on natural images with three channels (RGB) and then employing transfer learning strategies to train on medical images with one channel (grayscale). However, there exists a certain domain discrepancy between natural and medical images, which can affect the outcomes of DL models for medical imaging in terms of texture, shape, and other features. The present model included a self-supervised learning block to address this issue [20]. A portion of the dataset was used for the pre-training block in the self-supervised learning block. This block aims to define the spatial relationships between “instances” in each “bag”. It also ensured that the model did not experience accuracy degradation due to the transfer learning strategy in the downstream ABE classification task, thereby enhancing the model’s generalization ability [21].

In the clinic, the inter-slice spacing and slice thickness of MRI sequences are relatively large, which results in the loss of substantial three-dimensional information from the images. Consequently, most DL models can only utilize two-dimensional MRI slices as input. The signal intensity of the GP in hyperbilirubinemia patients is critical for diagnosis. However, due to varying bilirubin accumulation in the brains of neonates, a single slice does not often adequately represent the signal intensity in the GP region. The multi-instance learning block in the current model effectively addresses this issue [22]. In this approach, a patient was considered a“bag”, and all MRI slices of the GP region for that patient were referred to as“instances”. Instances were unlabeled, while the “bag” was labeled. The label indicated whether the patient was an ABE patient. Compared with conventional two-dimensional slice models, this block aims to extract richer distribution information in a “bag” during training, thereby increasing the size of the dataset [23]. We also incorporated a spatial attention module into the model, enabling it to better learn deep radiological features between instances and achieve superior performance [24].

After obtaining deep radiological features through the DL model, the diagnostic strategy of physicians was simulated by integrating image features with clinical information. Contrary to the linear layer classifiers used in conventional DL classification models, we employed a distributed non-linear model, the XGBoost classifier, which demonstrated superior performance [25].

Within this model, the AdamW optimizer was utilized, with a learning rate set at 0.001, and the loss function was the cross-entropy loss function. The model was trained for 200 epochs using a cosine annealing learning strategy.

Statistical analysis

DL analysis was conducted using Python version 3.9 and PyTorch version 2.0.1, and statistical analysis was performed using the R language (version 4.3.3). For DL analysis, model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curves (ROC), area under the ROC curve (AUC), 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score. Continuous features were analyzed using the Levene test to assess the homogeneity of variances, and the Mann-Whitney U test was applied to evaluate differences between groups. For classification features, Fisher’s exact test was used to determine the independence between variables. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Model performance

The training results of the model are presented in Table 2. Four modalities were employed as inputs in test cohort 1: axial T1, axial T2, axial DWI, and ADC. Deep radiological features from these four modalities were fused to create the fused modality, resulting in five predictive outcomes. When axial T1, axial T2, axial DWI, ADC, and fused modality were used alone as input, the accuracies for ABE compared with non-ABE classification were 76.7% (95% CI: 65.4%, 94.2%), 71.2% (95% CI: 72.3%, 94.7%), 65.8% (95% CI: 57.8%, 82.8%), 75.3% (95% CI: 63.2%, 86.2%), and 84.9% (95% CI: 73.6%, 94.7%) respectively; and AUCs were 86.5% (95% CI: 74.3%, 96.7%), 81.0% (95% CI: 76.1%, 97.9%), 63.8% (95% CI: 57.8%, 87.5%), 79.2% (95% CI: 70.4%, 94.3%), and 89.3% (95% CI: 76.6%, 97.8%) respectively. Simulating the diagnostic strategy of clinicians, clinical characteristics filtered through Pearson correlation analysis were integrated into the deep radiological features and input into the XGBoost classifier for classification prediction. The final SSMIM achieved an accuracy of 86.3% (95% CI: 72.3%, 94.7%) and an AUC of 91.1% (95% CI: 77.1%, 97.8%). Overall, the clinical characteristics integrated with the imaging feature modalities showed improvements in accuracy, sensitivity, and AUC compared with individual modalities. Furthermore, conventional classification DL models, Residual Network 50 (Resnet50) [24], and Visual Geometry Group Network 16 (VGG16) [25] models were compared with the SSMIM model. The results are shown in Table 2. The classification prediction accuracies for axial T1imaging using the Resnet50 and VGG16 models were 68.0% (95% CI: 61.8%, 74.2%) and 54.1% (95% CI: 47.5%, 60.8%)and AUCs were 71.1% (95% CI: 64.7%, 78.7%) and 55.7% (95% CI: 48.0%, 63.6%) respectively. These data suggested that the two conventional classification models were not suitable for the dataset in the present study, particularly the VGG16 model, which could not completely distinguish between ABE and non-ABE. Meanwhile, the SSMIM model significantly outperformed conventional classification models. Additionally, clinical characteristics alone were used for classification prediction, and the performance was still inferior to that of SSMIM. The AUCs of each model are depicted in (Fig. 3b).

Considering the "black box" nature of DL models, we further validated the interpretability of the model. On one hand, we employed SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values on test cohort 1 to more intuitively assess the contributions of clinical and imaging features to the classifier. As shown in Fig. 4, the contributions to the model were as follows: the SHAP value of radiological feature was +0.31, the SHAP value of TSB was +0.07, the SHAP value of albumin was +0.06, the SHAP value of days of jaundice was +0.05, the SHAP value of age was +0.03, and the SHAP value of reaction was +0.01. On the other hand, we used the Wilcoxon test to analyze the intensity distribution of GP region signals in the images of test cohort 1 and visualized the model's prediction process via Class Activation Maps (CAM) [26]. As shown in Figs. 5 and 6, although the p-values for the intensity distribution of GP region signals between ABE and non-ABE images were both greater than 0.05, indicating no significant differences in the signal intensity distributions between these two types of images, Fig. 6 demonstrates that our model successfully localized the GP region in images from all four modalities. Discussion

Wu et al. [27] and Zhang et al. [28] successfully classified ABE and non-ABE utilizing DL models. They used two-dimensional MRI slices as input to a conventional DL model for classifying ABE and non-ABE, achieving relatively good performance. The novelty of this study compared to previous work can be summarized as follows: (1) by using DL models to integrate multimodal imaging information and clinical characteristics to simulate the decision-making process of physicians, the SSMIM model demonstrated excellent performance in classification tasks. (2) This study overcame the challenge of inconsistent spacing and slice thickness in neonatal MRIs by employing a multi-instance approach to represent varying numbers of brain slices among neonates. (3) A self-supervised approach was employed, enabling downstream task models to comprehend richer image information during learning, thereby achieving superior performance in the classification task.

In the clinic, total serum bilirubin, albumin, and albumin-bound bilirubin levels, among other blood gas indicators, can assist physicians in diagnosing ABE. However, these parameters alone do not serve as exclusive indicators for diagnosing ABE [29, 30]. Our data showed that integrating filtered clinical characteristics with the XGBoost classifier Yielded an accuracy of only 64.4%. Therefore, imaging examinations are still required to further assist physicians in the diagnosis. Yi et al. [31] utilized the GP/putamen signal intensity ratio as radiological diagnostic criteria. Visual inspection of GP signal intensity values is a more common method in the diagnostic process of radiologists. As described in Fig. 5, we used the Wilcoxon test to analyze the signal intensity distribution of the GP region, and no significant differences were found in the results. However, as shown in Fig. 6, CAM revealed that voxels in the GP contributed most significantly to the network’s predictive outcomes in the DL model. This indicates that the SSMINET model outperforms the simple signal intensity calculating and relies on the signal of the GP for patient classification. Moreover, it avoids diagnostic results being influenced by human bias from visual inspection alone.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the amount of data included is relatively small, and although the use of a multi-instance approach for data augmentation somewhat mitigates this issue, the prediction results are still slightly affected. Secondly, the data utilized in the experiments are not research-specific images but rather standard clinical brain MRI sequences, functional MRI techniques, such as magnetic resonance spectroscopy, are required for accurate diagnosis [32].

Therefore, in future research, we plan to collaborate with multiple medical centers to collect more MRI images and improve the model's normalization process through methods such as domain adaptation, further enhancing the model's generalization ability. We will also set up a dedicated sequence for ABE MRI examination, incorporating more diverse MRI images, including functional imaging such as MRS, DTI and so on. Moreover, we will use more advanced DL feature fusion methods, such as Transformer models [33] or large language models [34], to improve the model's accuracy. Additionally, we aim to develop this model into an independent software tool to assist clinicians in making quick diagnoses and improving work efficiency. Lastly, we plan to explore the application of this model in predicting long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes in ABE patients in future research. Although our current study primarily focuses on the diagnosis during the acute phase, we recognize that the neurodevelopmental outcomes of ABE patients are closely related to early diagnosis and timely treatment. Therefore, future studies will focus on the model's predictive ability in long-term patient follow-up, including long-term prognostic assessments of neurocognitive and motor function.

Conclusions

In summary, the proposed SSMIM model, based on multimodal MRI radiological features and clinical characteristics, demonstrates good diagnostic performance in evaluating ABE. This study provides physicians with an economical, non-invasive, and effective model for assisting in the diagnosis and clinical management of ABE, reducing the diagnostic timeframe for this disease, and preventing ABE-associated trauma in patients.
