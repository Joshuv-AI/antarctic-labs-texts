Post-marketing surveillance of cordyceps products online: identifying falsified products and regulatory challenges
Authors: Lalita Wirasathien, Napatsorn Sriputhon, Mayuree Tangkiatkumjai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe outbreak of acute kidney injury linked to Cordyceps products in Thailand prompted an assessment of their quality. The study aimed to identify the falsification of dietary supplements containing Cordyceps species and to evaluate legal compliance.MethodsA survey and experimental studies focused on oral Cordyceps products selected through convenience sampling, sold online. Chemical and microscopic examinations were performed, and thin-layer chromatography was used to identify cordycepin and adenosine in the products. Steroid test kits were employed to detect corticosteroid adulteration. The products’ registration number and advertising approvals by the Thai FDA were verified.ResultsSixty-five products were included in the study. 54.3% of the products were falsified, involving the substitution of C. sinensis with C. militaris or the absence of the labeled species and the incorrect form of Cordyceps. The prevalence of products containing cordycepin and C. militaris was 53.8%. Among the products, 11% were unregistered, and 6.2% were found to be adulterated with corticosteroids. The prevalence of products with legally compliant physical labeling was 78.5%. However, 73.8% of the products made disease claims through social media.ConclusionsThe study highlights that the regulation of dietary supplements containing Cordyceps in Thailand does not fully comply with legal standards. Therefore, the public should be aware of these issues. The Thai FDA should enhance its post-market surveillance system for health products to protect better consumers, which is regularly inspect the quality of herbal products registered through the notification system and strictly regulate the proper labeling of these products, including clearly indicating the herbal species, form of active ingredient (such as extract or powder), and the amount of active ingredient in the extract (e.g., cordycepin).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-026-05363-w.

Background

Cordyceps, called ‘Dongchongxiacao’, is a Chinese caterpillar fungus that consists of the stroma of the fungus, Cordyceps sinensis (Berk.) Sacc. (Fam. Hypocreaceae), parasitized on the larva of some species of insects (Fam. Hepialidae), and the dead caterpillar [1]. C. sinensis is well-known as a Chinese herbal medicine that promotes well-being and is popular worldwide.

The Food Division of the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in Thailand has permitted dietary supplements containing C. sinensis and C. militaris since 2019 [2]. Marketed in Thailand, this supplement requires only product notification from the Thai FDA before distribution, rather than complete registration. This registration system requires manufacturers to submit the concentrations of adenosine (no more than 170 milligrams per 100 g) and cordycepin (no more than 30 milligrams per 100 g) in their Cordyceps products [2]. Since then, the number of these dietary supplements has been increasing. The regulation of the Thai FDA and the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) Harmonization of Traditional Medicines and Health Supplements framework requires product registration, labelling for health supplements, and three types of claims for health supplements, i.e., nutritional claims, functional claims, and disease risk reduction claims [3]. Currently, Cordyceps products in Thailand are registered as dietary supplements for nutritional claims only.

In 2023, 473 products containing Cordyceps, including tea, coffee, energy drinks, and chicken essence, were registered as dietary supplements and food items [4]. Cordyceps extracts found in Cordyceps products in Thailand were not purified and contained a variety of compounds from Cordyceps. Cordyceps advertising in Thailand, promoting their use for treating kidney disease and other chronic illnesses, has led many patients with chronic kidney disease or non-communicable diseases to use Cordyceps products for self-care.

Due to the scarcity of C. sinensis and concerns about heavy metal contamination [5], the mass production of cultivated C. militaris has increased worldwide, substituting natural C. sinensis. The primary difference between these two species lies in their main compounds: adenosine is the key compound of C. sinensis, while cordycepin is predominant in cultivated C. militaris [6, 7]. The natural form of C. sinensis contains only very low levels of cordycepin [7]. Adenosine and cordycepin are markers to identify C. sinensis and C. militaris, respectively [8]. The literature suggests methods for differentiating these two species using microscopic techniques and chemical evaluation through thin-layer chromatography [9, 10].

Adulteration and counterfeiting of herbal products are on the rise worldwide [11–13]. In Thailand, 20% of herbal products contained undeclared substitute species or none of the labeled species [14]. Because natural C. sinensis is rare and expensive, manufacturers often substitute it with the more affordable cultivated species, C. militaris. However, many commercial Cordyceps products are still labeled as containing C. sinensis, leading to potential mislabeling and consumer deception [15]. This counterfeit is challenging the Cordyceps market worldwide. Additionally, Cordyceps products labeled C. sinensis in the online market in Thailand are cheaper than expected, with most products costing no more than 1,000 baht per unit. This raises awareness that such products may not contain authentic C. sinensis.

Since 2020, Cordyceps products labeled C. militaris have been linked to cases of acute kidney injury (AKI) in Thailand, despite several studies suggesting that Cordyceps may have potential benefits in treating kidney diseases [16–18]. Cordycepin, a compound found in C. militaris, exhibits anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting cyclooxygenase-2 (COX) [19]. However, COX-2 inhibition is known to cause vasoconstriction and reduce renal blood flow, which can adversely affect kidney function [20]. Additionally, adverse effects of cordycepin-enriched products, such as nausea and diarrhea, may lead to kidney hypoperfusion, particularly in dehydrated patients [21]. Thai patients may overuse Cordyceps products, and high doses of cordycepin could contribute to AKI.

Other potential causes of Cordyceps-induced nephrotoxicity include arsenic contamination of natural C. sinensis [5, 22], and the presence of mycotoxins, which may be produced during the large-scale cultivation of C. militaris [21, 23]. Furthermore, corticosteroid adulteration in herbal products has been reported in Thailand and may also be linked to AKI [24, 25]. Corticosteroids in some Cordyceps products are unlikely to be natural, as Cordyceps species do not biosynthesize steroidal compounds [26]. The detection of these substances, therefore, indicates possible intentional adulteration. Manufacturers may add corticosteroids, such as prednisolone or dexamethasone, to enhance the perceived therapeutic effects of the products, such as reducing inflammation, increasing energy, or alleviating fatigue, thereby mimicking the claimed benefits of Cordyceps [27]. Similar cases of corticosteroid adulteration have been reported in traditional herbal preparations and dietary supplements in several Asian countries, raising serious safety and regulatory concerns. Globally, the prevalence of herbal products adulterated with corticosteroids has been reported to range from 1.4% to 48% [11, 28, 29].

A reporting retrospective cohort study in Thailand found that patients who consumed herbal products, which may be adulterated with corticosteroids, were associated with 1.7 times the risk of adrenal insufficiency, compared with those who did not take them [30]. Dietary supplements for arthritis in the U.S. were found to contain adulterated glucocorticoids and induced adrenal insufficiency among 12 consumers who took them [31].

The high prevalence of Cordyceps products labeled as C. militaris in Thailand from 2020 to 2022 coincided with reports from healthcare providers of AKI linked to Cordyceps products among eleven cases [16]. Subsequently, most Cordyceps products sold in Thailand were labeled as C. sinensis rather than C. militaris. Despite their widespread use, systematic post-marketing surveillance of Cordyceps products in Thailand remains limited.

This study aimed to: (1) assess the Cordyceps species present in the products, compared to their label to identify falsified Cordyceps products in Thailand; (2) determine the prevalence of corticosteroid adulteration in Cordyceps products; (3) evaluate the prevalence of legally registered Cordyceps products and their compliance with labeling regulations; and (4) measure the rate of compliance with advertising law for Cordyceps products.

Methods

Sample and study design

This study used a mixed-methods approach, combining survey and experiment studies. Oral dietary supplements containing Cordyceps as an active ingredient, sold online in Thailand from May to October 2023, were included. Samples were purchased online, as oral Cordyceps products in Thailand are mainly sold online, and Thai consumers have recently become more likely to buy these products online. The exclusion criteria were drinking and topical products containing Cordyceps as an adjunctive ingredient, as these products are less likely to be associated with adverse effects on the kidneys. The sample size of this study was calculated using the Yamane formula [32]. The total number of Cordyceps product registrations in Thailand was 473 in 2023, with a sampling error of 10%. This study used convenience sampling to purchase 83 products from online retailers across Thailand.

This study collected and analysed commercially available Cordyceps products from online retailers in Thailand. No human or animal subjects were involved. Therefore, ethical approval was not required. All procedures complied with the Thai FDA regulations and national exemptions for post-marketing testing of commercial products.

The survey assessed the prevalence of legal Cordyceps product registrations, their labels, and advertising regulatory compliance. Meanwhile, the experiment tested whether C. militaris or C. sinensis was contained in the products as indicated on their labels.

Tests of cordyceps products

Chemical evaluation

The preparation of samples involved extracting 500 milligrams (mg) of Cordyceps powder or one soft gelatin capsule using methanol, with ultrasonication assistance. The solvent was evaporated to obtain the extract, then redissolved in 1 mL of methanol. The sample extracts were tested using thin-layer chromatography (TLC) [10]. Two authentic samples of C. sinensis and C. militaris were prepared as powders and extracted using the same method as the sample preparation. The samples labeled “Cordyceps extracts” were tested solely by chemical evaluation.

The marker standards, adenosine and cordycepin, were purchased from Axxo Chemicals and Services Co., Ltd., a dealer in Thailand. The purity of both marker standards (adenosine and cordycepin) exceeds 98%.

Thin-layer chromatography was prepared using silica gel GF254 as the stationary phase. The mobile phase consisted of two systems: mobile phase 1 with a ratio of ethyl acetate, methanol, and water at 10:1.5:1, and mobile phase 2 with a ratio of chloroform to methanol at 4:1 [10]. The results of the TLC tests were read using ultraviolet light at 254 and 366 nanometers. Standard compounds of adenosine and cordycepin at 1 mg/ml were used. Samples with a prominent adenosine band, matching the standard compound, were identified as C. sinensis, while those with a prominent cordycepin and adenosine band were identified as C. militaris (Fig. 1). The TLC findings, observed under ultraviolet light at 366 nanometers, confirmed the presence of a band consistent with C. militaris.

The Rf values of adenosine and cordycepin were approximately 0.39 and 0.41, respectively, in mobile phase 1 (ethyl acetate, methanol, and water at 10:1.5:1), and approximately 0.24 and 0.34, respectively, in mobile phase 2 (chloroform to methanol at 4:1). In this study, mobile phase 1 was primarily employed because it utilizes safer solvents. Mobile phase 2 was applied only when the interpretation of TLC results obtained with mobile phase 1 was unclear, such as when additional spots in the sample appeared near the marker position and could obscure the spot with an Rf value corresponding to the reference marker.

Microscopic methods

Samples were stained with glycerin-water, chloral hydrate, and iodine test solutions. Cordyceps products labeled as containing Cordyceps powder, except for those labeled as Cordyceps extract, were tested using microscopic methods [9]. Figure 2 indicates authentic C. sinensis and C. militaris stained with a glycerin-water solution. Microscopic methods were used to cross-validate TLC findings. Only Cordyceps products labeled as a powder were microscopically tested. Microscopic observation of C. militaris tissue shows only clusters of intertwined, curved hyphae forming network-like structures (B). In contrast, the microscopic tissue of C. sinensis exhibits papillae-like surface structures and epidermal trichomes (A), which are absent in C. militaris. These tissue characteristics serve as key diagnostic microscopic features for identifying Cordyceps species.

Testing for corticosteroid adulteration

An immunochromatographic steroid test kit was used to detect corticosteroid adulteration in Cordyceps products [33]. The steroid test kit is a rapid, qualitative corticosteroid test developed by the Department of Medical Sciences of Thailand and produced and distributed by the Thai Government Pharmaceutical Organization [33, 34]. The sample preparation included grinding the sample powders or preparing solutions, which were then extracted using the corticosteroid extraction solution provided by the test kit. Four drops of the extracted sample were placed in the oval channel of the kit plate. The test detected more than one microgram/ml of dexamethasone and 50 micrograms/ml of prednisolone. A positive result was indicated by a red line appearing on label C on the plate (Fig. 3). In cases of invalid results, the samples were retested.

Assessment of compliance with labeling and online advertising standards for cordyceps products

Cordyceps products were legally checked for their registration numbers and advertising approvals via the Thai FDA website (https://porta.fda.moph.go.th/FDA_SEARCH_ALL/MAIN/SEARCH_CENTER_MAIN.aspx and https://alimentum.fda.moph.go.th/MAIN_SEARCH/MAIN/SEARCH_USER). Their legal label must include: (1) name; (2) registration number; (3) address of manufacture or import; (4) amount of dietary supplement; (5) amount of active ingredients; (6) warning statements as follows: “No effect in preventing or treating diseases”, “The intake of various nutrients should come from consuming a variety of main foods, covering all five food groups, in appropriate proportions.” and “Children and pregnant women should not consume.“; (7) manufacturing and expiry dates; and (8) usage [35].

Data collection

Samples were recorded by their product information from their label, i.e., product registration number, ingredients, dosage form, recommended dose, expiry date, warning statements, manufacturer, assessing advertising the products on their website or commercial platforms, such as Lazada® or Shopee®, and the result of the corticosteroid test and lab analyses of identification of Cordyceps spp.

Statistical analysis

Frequencies and percentages were reported, and descriptive statistics, including the median and range, were calculated. The prevalence of falsified Cordyceps products, products containing cordycepin, legally compliant products, and those with corticosteroid adulteration in Thailand was also reported. The prevalence was defined as the rate of those occurrences over a six-month period.

Results

Only 65 Cordyceps dietary supplements were included in the study, as many products were sold out, or their production or importation had been discontinued.

Characteristics of cordyceps products based on their labels

Forty percent of the products did not specify the species of Cordyceps. Over half of the products were labeled C.sinensis (53.8%), while only four were labeled C. militaris. Most of them (57.0%) were labeled as containing Cordyceps extract. The median amounts of Cordyceps in extract and powder form were 325 and 350 mg per capsule, respectively. The median number of additional ingredients in the products was three (range: 0–14). The top three additional ingredients were vitamins, reishi, and ginseng (Fig. 4). Three products contained only Cordyceps, identified by their label. The maximum daily doses of extract and powder indicated on the labels were 1,200 and 2,675 mg daily, respectively (Table 1). The prevalence of falsified cordyceps products

Overall, 54.3% of the products were falsified regarding the substitute or absence of the labeled species and the form of Cordyceps. Of the 39 products labeled as Cordyceps species (C. sinensis; N = 35 and C. militaris; N = 4) examined, 19 (48.7%) were found to be falsified regarding the actual species of Cordyceps. (Fig. 5). Nearly half of the products labeled C. sinensis were identified as C. militaris. Eleven products (16.9%) did not contain detectable Cordyceps ingredients.

The prevalence of cordycepin-containing Cordyceps products was 53.8% (N = 35). Additionally, 35 of 65 (53.8%) products were identified as C. militaris (Supplementary Table 2). The labeled doses of Cordyceps powder and extract, identified as C. militaris, ranged from 200 to 900 mg daily and from 229 to 1,200 mg daily, respectively.

Among the absence of Cordyceps (N = 11), two products (No. 40 and 59) were labeled to contain the lowest amounts of Cordyceps, at 30 and 20 mg per unit, respectively, which may be too low to detect by the TLC method. Other products were labeled to contain Cordyceps ranging from 100 to 790 mg per capsule, with labels indicating three as extract, three as powder, and 3 with unspecified Cordyceps forms. Three products were labeled as containing 100, 150, and 200 mg of Cordyceps, but no Cordyceps was detected. These findings indicated that such products were falsified, compared to other products with similar characteristics that contained detectable amounts of Cordyceps. Among the eleven products, one product could not be identified as containing the Cordyceps species due to interference from additional ingredients, such as magnesium oxide, vitamin C, and vitamin B complex, during chemical analysis.

Samples one and eleven were labeled “Cordyceps powder” but showed no Cordyceps tissue under microscopic examination. These two products may instead contain Cordyceps extract. The findings from the microscopic and chemical analyses are provided in Supplement Table 2 and TLC photos.

The prevalence of corticosteroid adulteration

Four products (6.2%) of the registered products were found to be adulterated with corticosteroids. Types of corticosteroids were not examined.

The prevalence of legally compliant products

11% (N = 7) of the products were not registered with the Thai FDA. Of these, three products lacked a registration number, while four displayed either a canceled registration number (N = 3) or a fake number (N = 1). One product was sold despite being past its expiration date. The prevalence of legally compliant physical labeling was 78.5%. Among the non-compliant physical labels, 11 products failed to display the required disclaimers, and three products were not registered with the Thai FDA and carried illegal labels. This study found that the active ingredient and the image of Cordyceps on the label were not relevant. For instance, product number 60 was labeled C. sinensis, but the image on the label was C. militaris. On the contrary, a label on the product number 36 displayed C. militaris, while a picture of C. sinensis was shown on the label.

Only one product (1.5%) advertised health claims permitted by the Thai FDA online and displayed its advertising license number. Forty-eight products (73.8%) made disease claims through online advertising. Seventeen products (26.2%) did not apply for an advertising license from the Thai FDA and did not make disease claims. The most common health claims were reducing blood glucose levels (11%), boosting immunity (11%), decreasing serum lipid levels (8%), and enhancing sexual performance (8%) (Fig. 6). The median number of health claims made online per product was five. Three products (4.6%) - one registered and two unregistered - were physically labeled with claims of treating diseases.

Discussion

Cordyceps products are widely used globally as dietary supplements or traditional remedies, including in Thailand. Although generally regarded as safe, healthcare providers in Thailand have reported cases of acute kidney injury related to the consumption of dietary supplements labeled with C. militaris [16]. Subsequent analyses in this study revealed that some products were contaminated with corticosteroids and contained high amounts of C. militaris. Corticosteroids inhibit phospholipase A2 and decrease mRNA for COX-2 [36]. A higher cordycepin concentration has a greater COX-2 inhibitory effect [19]. Both corticosteroids and excessive cordycepin can induce vasoconstriction of the afferent arterioles, leading to reduced renal perfusion and AKI. These findings highlight potential toxicological mechanisms involving both pharmacological and adulteration-related effects. To our knowledge, this is the first nationwide post-marketing surveillance study of Cordyceps products in Thailand, providing public health and regulatory insights relevant to regional and global markets.

The Cordyceps products in this study reflect the quality of those sold online in Thailand. More than 50% of the products in this study were falsified by the substitution or absence of the labeled species, which is higher than the prevalence of falsification in general herbal products in Thailand (20%), as reported by Ichim (2019) [14]. Among the products labeled as C. sinensis, 53.8% were identified as C. militaris. The substitution of C. sinensis with C. militaris has been reported globally [15]. The absence of the labeled species may be influenced by the poor quality of some Cordyceps products. Potential causes include the degradation of active compounds in Cordyceps due to high temperatures [37], or the complete absence of Cordyceps from the product. This issue requires close monitoring and intervention by the health authorities.

The product labeling was ambiguous (products no.26, 37, 38, and 43), as it stated both ‘cordyceps extract’ and, in parentheses, ‘cordyceps powder type’. This raised the possibility that the ingredient might be in powdered form; therefore, microscopic examination was conducted. Products no. 26, 38, and 43 were found to contain C. militaris by microscopic tests and were confirmed as powder, whereas product no. 37 was an extract. Moreover, the active ingredient and the image of Cordyceps on the label are not relevant, leading to consumer misunderstandings. The Cordyceps product label in Thailand is less likely to comply with the law. Under the product label standard, not labeling the registration number, the species of Cordyceps, the active ingredients, the dosage form, the warning label, or the required disclaimer statement is a significant concern. It should be reported to the Thai FDA, which should enforce the law and regularly monitor marketed products.

The most concerning issues were that 6.2% of the products were adulterated with corticosteroids, and 11% were not registered with the health authority. However, the prevalence of Cordyceps products adulterated with corticosteroids in this study was lower than in previous studies in Thailand, which reported that 15–50% of herbal products were adulterated with corticosteroids [24, 25].

Most of Thailand’s Cordyceps products (53.8%) contained cordycepin and C. militaris. In this study, the labeled daily doses of products containing identified C. militaris ranged from 200 to 900 mg per day for powder forms and 229 to 1,200 mg per day for extract forms. The Thai FDA’s recommended active ingredient levels for dietary supplements suggest a daily intake of 230 mg of C. militaris powder or no more than 0.03%of cordycepin. These levels are part of the positive list of Cordyceps products, enabling expedited product registration through notification [38]. The daily doses of these products raise concerns about the potential for individuals to consume high doses of C. militaris. The Thai FDA’s policy on the positive list of active ingredient levels for dietary supplements shortens the approval period of these products. The findings from this study suggest that this policy should be implemented alongside robust post-marketing surveillance to ensure the sustained quality of dietary supplements.

The Chinese Pharmacopoeia recommends a 3-9-gram dose of C. sinensis for therapeutic effects [1]. However, there is a limited recommended dose in other Cordyceps species or other forms of Cordyceps, such as C. militaris and Cordyceps extract, for which a recommended dose is generally 1 to 3 g per day [39]. Therefore, the amount of Cordyceps products in Thailand registered as a dietary supplement is insufficient for therapeutic effects. Additionally, the Thai FDA limits the dose in dietary supplements containing 230 mg of C. militaris powder, no more than 0.03% of cordycepin, and no more than three grams of C. sinensis [38]. Recently, no Cordyceps products have been registered as a medication in Thailand.

40% of the Cordyceps products in this study did not label the species of Cordyceps, and 9.2% did not specify the form of Cordyceps, such as powder or extract. Each form is consumed at a different dose. The Thai FDA should revise regulations for herbal dietary supplements to mandate clear labeling to protect consumer rights.

Most Cordyceps products in this study complied with physical labeling requirements (78.5%). However, nearly three-quarters of the products made overclaims through online advertising, and 4.6% were physically labeled with disease claims. Advertising dietary supplements on social media is more likely to spread misinformation [40]. Improving health literacy and educating people on assessing the accuracy of health information are essential to preventing misunderstandings about the benefits of dietary supplements. Health authorities should enforce regulations to address this issue.

This study’s findings implied that (1) Cordyceps products in Thailand require only product notification from the Thai FDA. To ensure the quality of the products, the Thai FDA should regularly inspect these products after launching them into the market; (2) the Thai FDA should strictly regulate the proper labels of herbal dietary supplements, which indicate herbal species, form of active ingredient, such as extract, powder of herbal medicine, as different forms of herbal medicine use different doses and provide different risks of adverse effects. For herbal extracts, products should indicate the concentration or amount of the extract’s primary active ingredient. For instance, Cordyceps extracts should provide a concentration of cordycepin in their extract. Thirdly, overclaiming online advertising for dietary supplements is a significant issue in Thailand, and the Thai FDA should closely monitor and enforce against this practice. The Thai FDA should strengthen regulatory enforcement to ensure the safety of herbal products. Therefore, detecting falsified and adulterated Cordyceps products in this study highlights the need for stronger post-marketing surveillance in Thailand, including stricter control over online sales and advertising, as well as information sharing among ASEAN regulators through mechanisms such as the ASEAN Post-Marketing Alert System.

The limitation of this study was that the included products may not sufficiently represent all Cordyceps products available in Thailand. However, researchers purchased as many products as were accessible online. As the samples were purchased from online retailers, they did not represent any specific region of Thailand. The study findings should, therefore, be interpreted with caution. Particular types of corticosteroids were not measured, as this study aimed to screen for corticosteroid adulteration. Additionally, this study did not measure the amount of adenosine and cordycepin. The microscopic method is unable to test samples that are too sticky.

The TLC method used in this study to detect chemical compounds in Cordyceps products had limited sensitivity for small amounts of adenosine and cordycepin. As a result, two products containing 20 and 30 mg of Cordyceps per unit were not detected. However, the TLC method is cost-effective and straightforward compared to DNA-based authentication techniques. Lastly, this study did not measure the quantities of Cordyceps in the products.

Conclusions

This study raises awareness among consumers and health authorities about the quality of Cordyceps products in Thailand. The issues identified with Cordyceps product situations may reflect similar problems in other dietary supplements. Key concerns include the sale of falsified herbal products online, exaggerated claims in online advertising, and some products posing risks to consumers due to a lack of proper registration or corticosteroid adulteration, as well as significant challenges for herbal and dietary supplements worldwide. Effective post-marketing surveillance is essential to ensure the quality and safety of dietary supplements. For instance, the Thai FDA should regularly inspect the quality of herbal products registered through the notification system and strictly regulate the proper labeling of these products, including clearly indicating the herbal species, form of active ingredient (such as extract or powder), and the amount of active ingredient in the extract (e.g., cordycepin).
