Gravity‐Dependent Modulation of Downbeat Nystagmus: Insights From Velocity‐Storage Dysfunction
Authors: Ji‐Hyung Park, Eun‐Hyuk Choi, Jorge Otero‐Millan, David S. Zee, Jeong‐Yoon Choi, Ji‐Soo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveDownbeat nystagmus varies with head position, a phenomenon termed gravity‐dependent modulation. We aimed to clarify its mechanism using a velocity‐storage model.MethodsIn 10 patients with downbeat nystagmus due to cerebellar disorders, we recorded eye movements at different pitch‐ and roll‐axis head positions. Sine‐wave fitting of the nystagmus intensity as a function of head position decomposed the slow‐phase velocity of nystagmus into a gravity‐dependent component—the amplitude and phase shift of the sine‐wave term—and a gravity‐independent constant offset. To probe mechanisms, we applied a velocity‐storage model simulating the estimation of rotational velocity, gravity orientation, and inertial acceleration, and incorporated a gravity‐estimator lesion that produced a gravity‐estimation bias.ResultsThe gravity‐dependent component during pitch‐axis modulation had a median amplitude of 3.8°/s (interquartile range [IQR] = 2.6) and a phase shift of 158.5° (57.9). During roll‐axis modulation, it had an amplitude of 2.1°/s (1.5) and a phase shift of −0.9° (147.6), with two subgroups showing phase shifts near ±90°. The amplitude was significantly larger during pitch‐ than roll‐axis modulation (p < 0.05). The lesion model generated persistent rotational cues that modulated downbeat nystagmus intensity as observed in the patients and additionally explained the interindividual variation in phase shifts and the weaker modulation of downbeat nystagmus during head roll.InterpretationThese findings refine our understanding of cerebellar vestibular processing and provide a computational framework for positional modulation of downbeat nystagmus. The concept of a biased gravity estimate may further account for several clinical phenomena, including atypical patterns of positional nystagmus.

Introduction

Downbeat nystagmus is a clinically relevant sign in patients with vertigo [1, 2], and is commonly associated with central vestibular disorders [3]. Downbeat nystagmus due to central lesions often shows positional modulation [4, 5], and may be referred to as central positional nystagmus when spontaneous nystagmus is minimal in the upright position [6]. Clinical and experimental observations suggest that this modulation reflects central processing of canal and otolith cues [7]. Because canal cues arise mainly during motion into a new position, impaired canal processing typically affects the immediate response after positional change. This manifests as paroxysmal downbeat nystagmus during movement from the upright position to the supine or supine head‐hanging position [8], with an initial peak after positioning, followed by decay with the canal time constant [9]. Meanwhile, otolith cues predominate after the head comes to rest. Accordingly, impaired otolith processing is thought to induce persistent downbeat nystagmus without decay, although its intensity varies with head position [10]. Because positional modulation is used synonymously with gravity modulation, this study defines positional modulation of downbeat nystagmus as the effect of otolith‐related central processing.

Regarding the origin of downbeat nystagmus, previous studies delineated gravity‐independent and gravity‐dependent components and suggested that otolith‐related central processing generates the gravity‐dependent component underlying positional modulation [4]. In most cases, this component increases in the prone position, where the head‐centered gravity vector is directed toward the nose, and decreases in the supine position, where it is directed toward the occiput. Overall, downbeat nystagmus intensity follows sine‐wave modulation according to the head‐centered gravity vector, with the upright position as the zero‐crossing reference point [4, 11], suggesting a key role of impaired central gravity estimation.

Previously, gravity‐dependent modulation of downbeat nystagmus was primarily explained by a static otolith‐ocular reflex model for the pitch axis, analogous to the static ocular counter‐roll during roll tilt, with reflex overactivity due to floccular dysfunction [4, 5]. The flocculus and paraflocculus modulate the vestibulo‐ocular reflex (VOR) through inhibitory effects on vestibular nucleus neurons [12, 13], particularly those associated with the horizontal and anterior canal afferents [12]. Lesions involving these structures impair VOR adaptation and induce downbeat nystagmus [14, 15]. However, gravity information is processed in the velocity‐storage circuit that comprises the bilateral vestibular nuclei, nodulus, and uvula [16, 17]. The nodulus and uvula update the head‐centered gravity direction by integrating the prior gravity information and rotational cues during position changes [18, 19, 20, 21]. Therefore, nodulo‐uvular lesions may be more relevant to the gravity‐dependent component, which remains insufficiently addressed.

Accordingly, we aimed to explore the mechanism of gravity‐dependent modulation of downbeat nystagmus using a velocity‐storage model with lesions involving the nodulus and uvula.

Methods

Patients and Evaluation

We recruited 10 patients with downbeat nystagmus due to cerebellar dysfunction from various etiologies (Table S1). Eye movements were recorded without visual fixation using three‐dimensional video‐oculography (SLMED, Seoul, Korea; 120 Hz), first in the upright sitting position and then in head orientations along the pitch axis—prone, supine, and supine head‐hanging—and the roll axis—right‐ear‐down and left‐ear‐down. To minimize canal‐related transient effects [8], each head position was attained slowly over about 10 s and maintained for at least 30 s. For analysis, we excluded the first 10 s after positioning and selected at least 10 s of recording during which the slow‐phase velocities (SPVs) of nystagmus remained most stable. Average vertical SPVs were then calculated for each of the six head orientations. Details of SPV measurement are presented in the Figure S1.

Coordinate System

We adopted a right‐hand head‐centered coordinate system with roll, pitch, and yaw axes (Figure 1A). Positive linear directions were defined as forward, leftward, and upward, respectively, and positive rotations followed the right‐hand rule. With this convention, the SPVs of downbeat nystagmus are expressed as negative values; the prone position is assigned a positive pitch angle (+90°), and the supine and supine head‐hanging positions are assigned negative pitch angles (−90° and−120°).

Calculation of Gravity‐Dependent and Independent Components, and Validation

As previously suggested, downbeat nystagmus includes gravity‐independent and gravity‐dependent components [4]. Following this suggestion, we fitted the SPVs across head positions to a sine function: SPV=A·sinX+φ+B Here, A, X, φ, and B denote amplitude, head orientation angle, phase shift, and offset, respectively, and the argument of the sine function is in degrees. Thus, “A·sinX+φ” and “B” represent the gravity‐dependent and gravity‐independent components, respectively. For each patient, sine‐wave fitting was performed for pitch‐ and roll‐axis head orientations using nonlinear least‐squares fitting. The fitted phase shift was normalized to (0°, 360°) for pitch‐axis modulation and (−180°, 180°) for roll‐axis modulation. For validation, our data were compared with those reported by Marti et al. [4]. Their parameters were transformed to match our coordinate system, as upward eye velocity and face‐up orientation were defined as positive in their study. Details of the fitting procedure are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

The fitted amplitudes, phase shifts, and offsets were compared between our data and those of Marti et al. using Mann–Whitney U tests. Additionally, Marti et al. reported that roll‐axis modulation was weaker than pitch‐axis modulation [4]. To reaffirm this finding, pitch‐ and roll‐axis modulation amplitudes were compared within each dataset using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. Data are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs).

Velocity‐Storage Model With Pathological Lesions

The deterministic velocity‐storage model we adopted has been described previously and is limited to vestibular signal processing (Figure 1B) [21, 22, 23]. The model includes estimators for rotational velocity, gravity direction, and inertial acceleration, with rotational and somatogravic feedback mechanisms. These components are connected sequentially, so an error in one stage propagates downstream.

Based on the relationship between head position and nystagmus intensity, we assumed that a lesion near the gravity estimator would generate nystagmus. This assumption was supported by previous findings: Monkeys with nodulo‐uvulectomy showed gravity‐dependent modulation of horizontal nystagmus during roll tilt, and patients with midline cerebellar lesions exhibited similar modulation in the supine ear‐down positions [24, 25].

In the velocity‐storage model (Figure 1B), the gravity estimator sends estimated gravity information to the inertia estimator and rotational feedback generator, where the otolith cue (gravitoinertial acceleration, GIA) also converges. The vector difference between GIA and estimated gravity is coded as the inertial cue in the inertia estimator, which can trigger the translational VOR. Meanwhile, the vector cross product of GIA and estimated gravity generates a rotational cue in the rotational feedback generator, contributing to the angular VOR.

Previously, a lesion involving the rotational feedback pathway simulated the rotational cue responsible for apogeotropic horizontal nystagmus observed in patients with cerebellar disorders [25]. Therefore, we made a similar lesion involving the cerebellar gravity estimator that lowers the gain to 0.5 for downstream transfer of estimated gravity information and adds a small bias directed toward the chin (negative yaw in Figure 1A) in head‐centered coordinates (vector coordinates [0, 0,−0.05 g], where g is the magnitude of gravitational acceleration) (Figure 1C). The estimated gravity is computed as the sum of the GIA vector and the caudal bias vector. Consequently, a rotational cue (GIA × estimated gravity) is generated due to the difference between the two vectors. We then tested three lesion conditions: Lesions involving information transfer from the gravity estimator to the rotational feedback generator, to the inertia estimator, or both (Figure 1D).

After identifying the most plausible lesion site, we simulated eye velocity and position using a model composed of the neural integrator distributed across the brainstem and cerebellum, and the brainstem saccadic generator triggered by positional bias [26, 27].

Results

Positional Modulation of Downbeat Nystagmus

In our raw data (before sine‐wave regression), the median SPV of downbeat nystagmus was −3.13°/s (IQR = 2.39) in the upright position. It increased in magnitude to −8.15°/s (4.05) in the prone position, decreased to −2.02°/s (2.64) and−2.91°/s (4.36) in the supine and supine head‐hanging positions, and was slightly attenuated to −2.26°/s (3.02) and−2.33°/s (2.23) in the right‐ and left‐ear‐down positions, respectively. These results were consistent with the previous data [4].

The amplitudes and phase shifts of the gravity‐dependent component showed no significant differences between the two datasets (Mann–Whitney U test, p > 0.05) (Figure 3C–E).

During roll‐axis modulation, in contrast, the sine‐wave fits were heterogeneous across patients in both datasets (Figure 3F,G). Although individual fitting curves were generally symmetric around the upright position, some patients showed decreased SPV in both ear‐down positions, with phase shifts close to +90°, whereas others showed increased SPV, with phase shifts centered at −90°. Without considering the heterogeneous phase shifts, the amplitude and phase shift of the gravity‐dependent component showed no significant differences between the two datasets (Mann–Whitney U test, p > 0.05) (Figure 3H–J). When patients were further clustered by phase shifts, the modulation patterns appeared highly similar between the two datasets (Figure 3K–M).

Finally, the modulation amplitude was greater during pitch‐axis modulation than during roll‐axis modulation in both datasets (Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, p < 0.05) (Figure 3N,O).

Prediction of Vestibular Neural Processing in Patients

Using the velocity‐storage model, we simulated rotational and inertial cues during pitch‐axis head‐position changes from the upright position (0°) to one of −180°, −120°, −90°, 0°, or +90° (Figure 4). Each simulation lasted 60 s, with the head‐position change occurring at t = 20 s during a 1‐s constant‐angular‐velocity rotation. Under normal conditions, the calculated cues were transient, with no persistent rotational or inertial cues thereafter.

Then, we repeated the simulation under pathological conditions, with lesions placed at three different locations. First, when the lesion affected the pathways from the gravity estimator to both the rotational feedback generator and the inertia estimator, the amplitude and duration of per‐rotatory cues increased slightly, but no post‐rotatory cues were generated after the end of rotation. Therefore, we disregarded this as a potential lesion location (Figure 4A).

Second, when the lesion affected only the pathway to the inertia estimator, it generated an inertial cue that intensified downbeat nystagmus in the prone, supine, and supine head‐hanging positions, with minimal effects in the upright and upside‐down positions. In contrast, the rotational cue intensified downbeat nystagmus in the supine and supine head‐hanging positions but attenuated it in the prone position (Figure 4B). When these effects were combined, downbeat nystagmus increased except in the upright and upside‐down positions, with greater intensity in the supine and supine head‐hanging positions than in the prone position, which was inconsistent with the observed modulation pattern. Reversing the lesion‐induced bias from [0, 0, −0.05 g] to [0, 0, 0.05 g] made the simulated pattern closer to the observed data. However, the simulated data showed significant oscillations before stabilization (Figure 4C). In contrast, the observed eye velocities stabilized rapidly without significant oscillation after position change (Figure S2). Therefore, neither cue generated by this lesion could account for the observed modulation pattern.

Finally, when the lesion affected only the pathway to the rotational feedback generator, the resulting rotational cue caused downbeat nystagmus to intensify in the prone position and diminish in the supine and supine head‐hanging positions, consistent with the modulation pattern observed in our patients (Figure 4D). Meanwhile, the inertia cue attenuated downbeat nystagmus in the prone, supine, and supine head‐hanging positions, with minimal effects in the upright and upside‐down positions. The combined effects were expected to account for the patients' data of gravity‐dependent downbeat nystagmus. The rotational and inertial cues also remained stable from the onset, consistent with the persistent, stable downbeat nystagmus observed in the patients.

Simulation: Gravity‐Dependent Component During Pitch Modulation

The simulated results localized the lesion responsible for gravity‐dependent modulation to the pathway from the gravity estimator to the rotational feedback generator. To simulate eye movements, we utilized only the rotational cue and excluded the inertial cue, based on the following considerations: First, in generating the translational VOR, the inertial cue should be appropriately integrated into velocity signals that vary with binocular distance and ocular infinity, making this process difficult to simulate due to a lack of experimental data. Second, during the constant‐velocity off‐vertical‐axis rotation, where the generated SPVs are a combination of angular (bias) and translational (modulation) VOR [28, 29, 30], the bias component is consistent with the stable nature of the persistent positional nystagmus. Therefore, we only used the rotational cue generated by the lesion. With this lesion, the simulated downbeat nystagmus replicated the patient data in different head positions (Figure 5). Importantly, by changing the offset value (gravity‐independent component), the spectrum of positional modulation of downbeat nystagmus can be classified into three patterns. First, with zero offset—indicating no nystagmus in the upright position—downbeat nystagmus developed in the prone position, and upbeat nystagmus occurred in the supine and head‐hanging positions (Figure 5A). Second, with a negative offset—indicating spontaneous downbeat nystagmus was present in the upright position—it was intensified in the prone position and attenuated or even reversed to upbeat nystagmus in the supine and head‐hanging positions (Figure 5B,C). Third, with a positive offset—indicating spontaneous upbeat nystagmus was present in the upright position—it was attenuated or reversed to downbeat nystagmus in the prone position and was intensified in the supine and head‐hanging positions (Figure 5D). Simulated eye velocities plotted against head‐rotation angle followed a sine function, consistent with our and Marti's data (Figure 6A).

Simulation: The Phase Shift During Pitch‐Axis Modulation

In both the current study and the study by Marti et al. [4]., the gravity‐dependent component was not symmetrical around the head‐upright position. Specifically, this component generated upward SPVs (downbeat nystagmus) in the head‐upright position in some patients and downward SPVs (upbeat nystagmus) in others. This difference can be ascribed to the presence of a phase shift. To explain this interindividual difference, we adjusted the direction of the lesion‐induced bias by rotating it about the pitch axis, thereby placing the head‐centered bias obliquely—either backward (between the chin and occiput: [−0.035 g, 0, −0.035 g]) or forward (between the chin and nose: [0.035 g, 0, −0.035 g]). Accordingly, with an oblique backward bias (Figure 6B), the gravity‐dependent component produced downbeat nystagmus in the upright head position and was nullified at 45° backward head tilt in the pitch axis. With an oblique forward bias (Figure 6C), the direction of nystagmus in the upright position and the tilt direction of the null point were reversed. These findings therefore explained the variations in the gravity‐dependent modulation.

Simulation: Gravity‐Dependent Components During Modulation About Oblique and Roll Axes

Marti et al. reported modulation of downbeat nystagmus with changes in head positions along the mid‐pitch‐roll axis, namely the right anterior–left posterior or left anterior–right posterior canal axis, as well as along the roll axis [4]. The amplitude of the gravity‐dependent component became smaller with rotation along the mid‐pitch‐roll axis and further decreased with rotation along the roll axis. These findings were also replicated in our data, which demonstrated diminished modulation of downbeat nystagmus along the roll axis. Therefore, we tested whether our model could explain these modulation patterns. Our simulation with pure downward bias (0, 0, −0.05 g) showed that with different head positions along the right anterior‐left posterior canal axis, the variation of nystagmus (amplitude of the sine‐wave fit) decreased slightly. Furthermore, along the roll axis, the gravity‐dependent modulation of downbeat nystagmus disappeared (Figure 6D–F). These simulation results were close to the observations from both the current study and Marti's study, except that the observed modulation along the roll axis was small but clearly not zero.

Simulation: The Phase Shift During Roll‐Axis Modulation

The discrepancy—small but non‐zero roll‐axis modulation in the clinical data vs. no modulation in the simulation—needs to be addressed properly. Under roll axis modulation, a pure downward gravity bias would generate a rotational cue leading to pure torsional nystagmus. However, if the bias is directed obliquely forward or backward, the forward component would generate rotational cues that lead to vertical nystagmus.

This led us to examine whether the model could reproduce the above idea and the observed findings during roll modulation. We used the same lesion‐induced bias as in the previous subsection, placing the head‐centered bias obliquely either backward (−0.035 g, 0, −0.035 g) or forward (0.035 g, 0, −0.035 g). Under these conditions, the model reproduced a small but non‐zero gravity‐dependent modulation of downbeat nystagmus, with phase shifts of ±90° (Figure 6G,H). This was also consistent with the observed data of phase shift clustering around ±90° as shown in Figure 3K–M.

Discussion

This study evaluated positional modulation of downbeat nystagmus in patients with cerebellar dysfunction and reaffirmed the reported pattern of modulation [4]. Using a pathologically modified velocity‐storage model [18, 19, 20, 21], we simulated the gravity‐dependent component responsible for this modulation. The key mechanism involved a centrally generated rotational cue originating from a mismatch between otolith‐derived GIA and a biased gravity estimate. This idea successfully accounts for persistent downbeat central positional nystagmus in cerebellar disorders.

From the Otolith‐Ocular Reflex Model to the Velocity‐Storage Model

Previous studies on downbeat nystagmus were largely descriptive [2, 31], but Marti et al. advanced the field by delineating the gravity‐dependent component underlying positional modulation and explaining it with the otolith‐ocular reflex mechanism [4, 5]. In this model, analogous to the static ocular counter‐roll during head tilt, the reflex shifts the primary eye position upward in the prone position and downward in the supine position. Consequently, upward and downward eye drifts generate downbeat and upbeat nystagmus in the prone and supine positions. While this reflex is normally suppressed by floccular Purkinje cells in healthy subjects, it may become evident with cerebellar dysfunction. In patients with downbeat nystagmus, this resultant eye drift interacts synergistically with the upward slow‐phase velocity in the prone position and antagonistically in the supine position [5]. Although this mechanism explains many clinical observations, some features lack direct experimental evidence [5]. Floccular Purkinje cells are influenced by pitch angle [32, 33], but their role in modulating the otolith‐ocular reflex remains unexplored. Therefore, Marti et al. left room to consider alternative mechanisms involving the nodulus and uvula [5].

Accordingly, we adopted a velocity‐storage model to explain positional modulation of downbeat nystagmus. One possible explanation is that a gravity‐independent component arises from a persistent downward rotational signal in the velocity‐storage output or direct angular VOR pathway and is then modulated through normal canal–otolith interaction. This signal could alter the estimated gravity, creating a mismatch with GIA and an inertial cue linked to the translational VOR [18, 28]. However, this mechanism would predict antagonistic angular and translational VOR effects in the prone position, leading to attenuation of downbeat nystagmus, and synergistic effects in the supine position, leading to augmentation of downbeat nystagmus. This is opposite to the observed modulation pattern. Moreover, rotational and somatogravic feedback may limit the persistence of such an angular VOR signal.

We therefore focused on another possibility within the velocity‐storage model, in which rotational feedback generated from a biased gravity estimate plays a key role [20, 21, 22, 23]. The existence of rotational feedback has been validated by two experimental phenomena: The bias term observed in off‐vertical‐axis constant rotation [28, 29, 30], and the dumping phenomenon of post‐rotatory or optokinetic after‐nystagmus [28, 34, 35]. The rotational feedback is a centrally generated rotational cue, derived from the difference between GIA and estimated gravity. It has been thought to compensate for the rotation aftereffect during tilt motion, thereby further rotating the estimated gravity toward the otolith‐relayed GIA [20, 21]. Given this context, generating a persistent rotational cue requires a sustained difference between the estimated gravity and GIA. We therefore assumed a bias of a fixed amplitude and direction relative to the head‐centered coordinates in the information transmitted from the gravity estimator to the rotational feedback generator. The concept of a fixed bias in head‐centered coordinates was inspired by previous studies [25]. The neuronal populations in the nodulus and uvula can be classified according to the physical variable they represent: Gravity, inertia, or GIA [16]. Neurons in each group have preferred activation vectors, and their collective activity encodes the physical variable in the 3D space. Therefore, if the efferent pathway from the gravity estimator is partially damaged, the collective neuronal activity representing gravity will be biased in the head‐fixed coordinates [36].

Normally, the internally estimated gravity aligns with the GIA across static head positions, producing no mismatch. With the head‐fixed bias, however, the difference between GIA and internally estimated gravity follows a sine‐wave pattern. This difference generates a rotational cue that varies with head position.

Explaining Conversion From Downbeat in Upright to Upbeat Nystagmus in the Supine Position

The sine‐wave relationship between head position and the SPV of the gravity‐dependent component, with the upright position as the zero‐crossing point, implies that the prone position generates downbeat nystagmus (i.e., upward SPV), while the supine position induces upbeat nystagmus (i.e., downward SPV) (Figure 5A). When combined with the gravity‐independent downbeat component, downbeat nystagmus is intensified and attenuated in the prone and supine positions, respectively, which is a characteristic pattern of positional modulation observed in the patients (Figure 5B,C). Notably, when the amplitude of the gravity‐dependent component exceeds the gravity‐independent downbeat component, a conversion to upbeat nystagmus may occur in the supine or supine head‐hanging position (Figure 5B).

Conversely, if upbeat nystagmus is present in the upright position, it will be attenuated in the prone position and intensified in the supine position (Figure 5D). This phenomenon—where spontaneous nystagmus generated by a gravity‐independent component shifts according to the added gravity‐dependent component—is analogous to fixation–induced nystagmus, in which spontaneous nystagmus arising from vestibular bias changes according to the additional cerebellar pursuit‐related bias [37]. Our explanation also aligns with previous accounts of alternating skew [38, 39], as well as head‐tilt‐dependent modulation of both vibration‐induced nystagmus and periodic alternating nystagmus [40, 41]. Taken together, these phenomena point to a generalized pathomechanism whereby a sustained deviation in the perceived direction of gravity affects the central processing of otolith cues and leads to various position‐related pathological eye movements.

Explaining Individual Variation of Downbeat Nystagmus During Pitch‐Axis Modulation

The average data from our study and Marti's study showed that the zero‐crossing point of the gravity‐dependent component was near the upright position (with a phase shift of about 180°). However, the zero‐crossing point varied considerably across subjects, corresponding to a phase lead or lag in the sine‐wave relationship. By adjusting the lesion‐induced bias either forward or backward, our model accounted for the interindividual phase variation. This observation provides an important insight for clinical evaluation. If the zero‐crossing point is placed equidistant from the sitting and supine positions (phase lead), the change in nystagmus intensity between sitting and prone positions would be negligible (Figure 6B). Likewise, if it is placed midway between the sitting and prone positions (phase lag), nystagmus intensity would be similar in the sitting and supine positions (Figure 6C). Thus, assessing only a few discrete positions without considering the phase shift may lead to misjudgment regarding the presence of a gravity‐dependent component suggestive of cerebellar dysfunction.

Weak or Negligible Modulation Along the Oblique and Roll Axes

We also used the model to simulate positional modulation of downbeat nystagmus along the oblique and roll axes. In both studies, the modulation amplitudes of the gravity‐dependent component along these axes were smaller or negligible compared with pitch‐axis modulation. When a head‐fixed pure downward bias was applied to our model, the rotational feedback generator yielded a rotational cue consisting of equal pitch and roll halves during oblique axis modulation, whereas it consisted solely of a roll component during roll‐axis modulation. This explains the smaller amplitude of the gravity‐dependent downbeat component during oblique‐axis modulation and its near absence during roll‐axis modulation.

Unified Framework for Pitch and Roll Phase Shifts

In both the current study and Marti's data, roll‐axis phase shifts around +90° were associated with pitch‐axis phase shifts < 180°, whereas those around −90° were associated with pitch‐axis phase shifts > 180° (Figure 6I). A single framework explains these patterns: An oblique forward bias accounts for the former group, and an oblique backward bias for the latter. With an oblique forward (or backward) bias, the forward (or backward) component generates upward (or downward) rotational feedback in the head‐upright position and the opposite feedback in the upside‐down position. However, in the right‐ or left‐ear‐down positions, the same forward (or backward) component generates horizontal, but not vertical, rotational feedback. Taken together, an oblique forward (or backward) bias can shift the zero‐crossing point of the sine‐wave modulation of downbeat nystagmus along the pitch axis, while it does not affect the zero‐crossing point along the roll axis, which remains fixed at the ear‐down position.

Clinical Implications and Broader Application

Proper diagnosis of downbeat nystagmus of central origin is essential, yet it can be clinically challenging when spontaneous nystagmus is subtle and appears only in specific head positions, thereby mimicking variants of benign paroxysmal positional vertigo such as cupulolithiasis of the anterior or posterior canals [42, 43]. Understanding the interaction between the gravity‐independent offset and gravity‐dependent sinusoidal modulation with variable phase shift may help differentiate central from peripheral positional nystagmus.

This framework can also explain horizontal central positional nystagmus associated with cerebellar dysfunction, which is mostly apogeotropic and sometimes geotropic in ear‐down positions [25, 44, 45, 46]. In the right‐ or left‐ear‐down positions, the antero‐posterior component of oblique bias may shift the estimated gravity vector from the GIA within the horizontal plane, eliciting horizontal rotational feedback. When this component is directed forward, it produces left‐beating nystagmus in the right‐ear‐down position and right‐beating nystagmus in the left‐ear‐down position; conversely, when directed backward, it leads to geotropic nystagmus.

Lastly, the same framework may apply to other clinical conditions in which the mechanisms underlying positional modulation of nystagmus and vertigo intensity remain unclear, such as acute unilateral vestibulopathy [47]. Vestibulopathy may introduce a bias in the GIA signal transmitted from the otolith to the central vestibular system, generating a mismatch between the central gravity estimate and the actual GIA, thereby producing positional modulation of nystagmus.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, we focused solely on the gravity‐dependent component. Therefore, our interpretation is limited to the modulation of downbeat nystagmus in relation to gravity‐related effects. The gravity‐independent component could be explained by flocculus‐ and paraflocculus‐related mechanisms such as asymmetry of the vertical angular VOR, impaired smooth pursuit, and gaze‐holding deficits, as reviewed in Marcelli et al. [1]. Second, our data were obtained from patients with diffuse cerebellar dysfunction, and imaging evidence of selective nodulo‐uvular involvement is lacking. Thus, contributions from other mechanisms, including the previously suggested floccular mechanism [5], cannot be excluded. Third, the head‐fixed bias for gravity estimation is a key component of our proposed mechanism. Experimental evidence for such a head‐fixed constant bias is needed to strengthen this hypothesis. Fourth, although both our data and Marti's study demonstrated a consistent pattern of nystagmus modulation, the small number of patients in our study limits generalizability. Finally, we did not perform quantitative analysis of torsional nystagmus. Although the rotational cue during the roll‐axis modulation could theoretically produce torsional nystagmus, it was negligible in all patients based on visual inspection of video eye recordings. The lower gain of torsional than vertical or horizontal VOR could explain this phenomenon [48].
