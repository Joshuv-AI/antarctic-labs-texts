VB5 modulates osteoclast metabolic reprogramming and suppresses differentiation by blocking PKM2 nuclear translocation
Authors: Hongming Zhou, Hengren Li, Ming Fu, Heng Yang, Xingang Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoporosis is a multifactorial skeletal disorder characterized by increased bone resorption and impaired remodeling balance. Osteoclast differentiation is accompanied by metabolic reprogramming toward enhanced glycolysis, yet whether nutritional factors modulate osteoclast metabolism remains incompletely understood. Vitamin B5 (VB5), a precursor of coenzyme A (CoA), has been implicated in metabolic regulation, but its role in osteoclast biology is unclear. An ovariectomy (OVX)-induced osteoporosis mouse model was established and fed VB5-deficient, normal, or VB5-supplemented diets. Bone microarchitecture was assessed by micro-computed tomography and histomorphometry. Osteoclast differentiation was evaluated in RAW264.7 cells and bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs). Metabolic flux analysis, molecular docking, co-immunoprecipitation, and Western blotting were performed to investigate the involvement of the PANK–CoA–PKM2 axis. VB5 supplementation attenuated OVX-induced bone loss and reduced osteoclast numbers in vivo, whereas VB5 deficiency exacerbated bone deterioration. In vitro, VB5 and its metabolic derivative CoA suppressed osteoclast differentiation without inducing significant cytotoxicity. Mechanistically, VB5 increased intracellular CoA levels in a PANK-dependent manner. CoA interacted with PKM2, reduced Y105 phosphorylation, and decreased nuclear translocation, accompanied by suppression of glycolytic and oxidative phosphorylation activity. Pharmacological stabilization of PKM2 partially reversed molecular alterations associated with VB5 deficiency. VB5 is associated with inhibition of osteoclast differentiation and attenuation of estrogen deficiency–induced bone loss, potentially through modulation of metabolic reprogramming involving the PANK–CoA–PKM2 axis. Introduction

Osteoporosis is a common metabolic bone disorder characterized by reduced bone mass and deterioration of bone microarchitecture, leading to increased fracture risk, particularly in aging populations1–3. The development of osteoporosis reflects a complex disruption of skeletal remodeling, involving interactions among osteoclast-mediated bone resorption, osteoblast-driven bone formation, systemic hormonal regulation, mechanical loading, and age-related metabolic changes4. Extensive clinical and experimental evidence indicates that osteoporosis is a multifactorial disease rather than the consequence of dysregulation in a single cellular pathway5.

Within this multifaceted remodeling process, osteoclasts play an essential role in bone resorption and contribute to bone loss under pathological conditions6. Although excessive osteoclast activity alone is unlikely to fully account for the pathogenesis of osteoporosis, modulation of osteoclast differentiation and function remains a central component of current therapeutic strategies1,7. Indeed, several clinically effective antiresorptive agents have been developed and widely used to reduce fracture risk, underscoring the continued relevance of investigating osteoclast biology in skeletal diseases8.

Recent studies have demonstrated that osteoclast differentiation and resorptive activity are accompanied by pronounced metabolic adaptations9. In particular, shifts in cellular energy metabolism—including increased reliance on glycolytic pathways—appear to support the high energetic and biosynthetic demands of osteoclastogenesis10. These observations suggest that metabolic regulation may influence osteoclast behavior and represent a potential avenue for modulating bone resorption. However, the molecular mechanisms linking metabolic reprogramming to osteoclast differentiation remain incompletely understood.

Pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) is a key regulator of cellular metabolism with both enzymatic and non-enzymatic functions11. Beyond its canonical role in glycolysis, PKM2 can undergo post-translational modifications and subcellular redistribution, including nuclear translocation, which has been implicated in metabolic regulation and transcriptional control in multiple cell types12,13. Previous studies have shown that coenzyme A (CoA) can interact with PKM2 and influence its phosphorylation status and subcellular localization, thereby modulating metabolic flux14,15. Whether such regulatory mechanisms operate in osteoclasts and contribute to their differentiation has not been fully explored.

Vitamin B5 (VB5) is an essential micronutrient and a biosynthetic precursor for CoA synthesis16. VB5 has been implicated in diverse metabolic and immune processes; however, its role in skeletal biology and osteoclast function remains poorly defined17,18. While nutritional factors are increasingly recognized as modulators of bone health, the extent to which VB5 influences osteoclast metabolism and differentiation, as well as the underlying molecular pathways involved, remains unclear.

Based on these considerations, we hypothesized that VB5 modulates osteoclast differentiation by influencing cellular metabolic programs, potentially through regulation of the PANK–CoA–PKM2 axis. To test this hypothesis, we investigated the effects of VB5 on osteoclast differentiation, metabolic activity, and PKM2 signaling using both in vitro osteoclast models and an ovariectomy-induced bone loss model in mice.

Materials and methods

Animals

Ten-week-old female C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from an accredited experimental animal supplier. All mice were acclimated in the animal facility for 7 days prior to the start of the experiments. Animals were housed under controlled conditions (temperature 22 ± 2 °C, humidity 50–60%) with a 12 h light/dark cycle and had ad libitum access to standard chow and water. Mice were housed in polycarbonate cages containing corncob bedding, with five mice per cage.

Baseline body weight was measured immediately before the initiation of dietary intervention, and mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a random number table based on body weight. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University (approval number: 2023–044) and were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Dietary intervention and VB5 supplementation

Mice were randomly assigned to one of the following dietary regimens:

Normal diet,

Normal diet,

VB5-deficient diet

VB5-deficient diet

VB5 supplementation.

VB5 supplementation.

The normal diet contained 16 mg/kg of VB5, whereas the VB5-deficient diet contained no VB5. Both diets were custom-formulated and provided by Xietong Biology (China).

VB5 supplementation was performed via drinking water only, as previously described15. VB5 (Solarbio, China) was dissolved in drinking water, and the concentration was calculated based on the average daily water intake to achieve an approximate dose of 50 mg/kg/day. No additional VB5 was added to the solid diet in the supplementation group. Dietary intervention was initiated 2 weeks prior to ovariectomy and maintained throughout the entire experimental period.

Ovariectomy (OVX) and sham surgery

After 2 weeks of dietary intervention, mice underwent bilateral ovariectomy (OVX) or sham surgery. The surgical procedure was performed according to previously published protocols19. Mice were anesthetized with 2.0% isoflurane in N₂O/O₂ (70:30); euthanasia was performed under deep anesthesia (2.5% isoflurane) by CO₂ inhalation at 20% of the chamber volume per minute.

A midline dorsal incision of approximately 1 cm was made to expose and gently exteriorize the ovaries. In OVX mice, the ovaries were ligated with absorbable sutures and surgically removed. In sham-operated mice, the ovaries were exteriorized but neither ligated nor removed. The incision was closed with interrupted sutures.

Postoperatively, mice were monitored daily for general condition, body weight, and wound healing, and were allowed to recover under standard housing conditions. All mice were euthanized 10 weeks after surgery for sample collection.

TEPP-46 rescue experiment

To evaluate the rescue effect of TEPP-46 on VB5 deficiency–associated phenotypes, mice fed either a normal diet or a VB5-deficient diet underwent OVX surgery and subsequently received pharmacological treatment according to previously reported protocols. TEPP-46 (30 mg/kg, MCE) or an equivalent volume of vehicle (PEG300, MCE) was administered via intraperitoneal injection every other day for 10 weeks15.

Mice were assigned to the following experimental groups:

Normal diet + PEG300,

Normal diet + PEG300,

VB5-deficient diet + PEG300,

VB5-deficient diet + PEG300,

Normal diet + TEPP-46,

Normal diet + TEPP-46,

VB5-deficient diet + TEPP-46.

VB5-deficient diet + TEPP-46.

Dietary regimens and surgical procedures were consistent with those described above.

Sample collection and allocation

At the experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized under deep anesthesia. Blood samples were collected by cardiac puncture, with approximately 0.8–1.0 mL of whole blood obtained from each mouse. Blood samples were allowed to clot at room temperature and then centrifuged to obtain serum, which was stored at − 80 °C until further analysis.

The right femur from each mouse was used for micro–computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis and subsequent histological evaluation, while the left femur was used for protein extraction. A total of five femora per group were used for micro-CT and histological analyses, and femoral tissues from three mice per group were used for protein expression analysis.

Isolation of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs)

Following euthanasia, femurs and tibiae were aseptically excised and briefly sterilized by immersion in 75% ethanol for 5 min. After rinsing with phosphate‑buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual ethanol, epiphyses were removed and the marrow cavities were flushed with complete culture medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin‑streptomycin). The resulting marrow suspension was passed through a 70‑μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min. Erythrocytes were lysed using a commercial lysing buffer (G2015, Servicebio, China) for 5 min at room temperature with gentle mixing. After centrifugation and removal of the supernatant, cells were resuspended in complete DMEM containing 30 ng/mL macrophage colony‑stimulating factor (M‑CSF; 416‑ML, R&D Systems, USA) and seeded into culture dishes. Non‑adherent cells were removed after 24 h, and the adherent bone marrow‑derived macrophages (BMDMs) were maintained in M‑CSF‑containing medium for 4 days with a half‑medium change on day 3. For osteoclast differentiation, BMDMs were harvested and seeded in 96‑well plates at a density of 8 × 103 cells/well, then stimulated with 30 ng/mL M‑CSF plus 50 ng/mL receptor activator of nuclear factor‑κB ligand (RANKL,Novoprotein, China) for 5–6 days. Medium was replaced every 2–3 days until multinucleated osteoclasts formed.

Micro-CT and bone histomorphometry

The right femurs (n = 5 per group) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for no longer than 48 h, then transferred to phosphate-buffered saline and stored at 4 °C. Micro-CT scanning was performed within 7 days after fixation to minimize decalcification.

Scanning was conducted using a PerkinElmer Quantum GX micro-CT system (PerkinElmer, Quantum GX2, USA). Images were acquired at 90 kV and 88 μA with an isotropic voxel size of 36 μm. The region of interest was set at the distal femoral metaphysis, starting 0.5 mm proximal to the distal growth plate and extending proximally over 100 consecutive slices. Scans were performed along the longitudinal axis of the femur.

Raw data from the PerkinElmer system were reconstructed using Skyscan NRecon software, followed by three-dimensional morphometric analysis with CTVox software. Bone histomorphometric analysis was performed as previously described20.

Cell culture

RAW264.7 cells (Zhongqiao Xinzhou Biotechnology, China) and BMDMs were cultured in DMEM (11965092, gibco, Grand Island) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Shanghai Wenren Biotechnology, China) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Servicebio, China) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. For osteoclastogenesis induction, cells were stimulated with 50 ng/mL recombinant RANKL (Novoprotein, China).

F-actin staining and TRAP staining

BMDMs and RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with RANKL and the specified compounds for 5 days. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with Phalloidin (Solarbio, China). For TRAP staining, cells were fixed with TRAP fixing solution (Solarbio, China) for 1 min at room temperature, rinsed with water, and stained using a TRAP staining kit (Solarbio, China).

Cytotoxicity assessment

Cytotoxicity was evaluated using a CCK-8 assay kit (Solarbio, China). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (20,000/well), treated with varying concentrations of test chemicals for 24 or 72 h, followed by CCK-8 reagent addition. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Measurement of CoA and VB5 concentrations

CoA was purchased from Solarbio (China). CoA and VB5 concentration assay kits were obtained from Abcam (Ab102504, UK) and J&L Biology (JL46633, China), respectively. All experimental procedures followed previously established protocols文献.

Molecular docking

The binding affinity and binding sites between CoA and the PKM2 were investigated using LibDock in Discovery Studio 2019. The structural data file of CoA was obtained from PubChem, while the 3D crystal structure of the PKM2 was acquired from Uniprot. Partial diagrams of molecular docking were generated using PyMOL software.

Co‐immunoprecipitation

CoA-agarose immunoprecipitation was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cell lysates were prepared in buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4), 0.5% NP-40, 2 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, and complete protease inhibitor cocktail. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min, then incubated with CoA-agarose beads (Sigma, USA) for 2 h at room temperature. After four washes with lysis buffer, bead-bound proteins were eluted for subsequent immunoblotting analysis.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from cells and tissues using RIPA lysis buffer containing phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (Beyotime, China). Protein concentration was quantified via bicinchoninic acid assay (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and electrophoretically transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. After 1-h blocking with 5% non-fat dried milk at room temperature, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA). Antibody-antigen complexes were visualized using the Amersham ImageQuant 800 chemiluminescence detection system. Antibody specifications are detailed in Table 1, with identical processing protocols applied throughout. Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to β-tubulin (or GAPDH). All Western blot data represent at least three independent biological experiments.

Metabolism assays

Cellular metabolic parameters were measured using an XF96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent, USA). Cells were seeded at 1 × 104 cells/well in 96-well plates. Following 24-h incubation, growth medium was replaced with assay medium containing 10 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, and 2 mM glutamine, and cells were equilibrated for 60 min at 37 °C in a non-CO2 environment. For glycolytic assessment, sequential exposure to 0.5 μM rotenone/antimycin A (Rot/AA) and 50 mM 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) was performed, while mitochondrial stress testing involved treatment with 1 μM oligomycin, 1 μM FCCP, and 0.5 μM rotenone. Data acquisition and analysis were conducted using Wave Software (Agilent, USA) through standardized protocols.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. For in vivo experiments with small group sizes, non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis tests were applied when data normality could not be assumed. When significant, post-hoc multiple comparisons were performed. Two-factor ANOVA was used to evaluate interactions between estrogen status and VB5 availability. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

VB5 attenuates bone loss in OVX-induced osteoporotic mice

To investigate the effect of VB5 on osteoporosis, an ovariectomy (OVX)-induced osteoporosis mouse model was established, followed by dietary interventions with different levels of VB5. Serum VB5 levels were significantly higher in the supplementation group and significantly lower in the deficiency group compared with the control group (Supplementary Fig. 1A), confirming the effectiveness of the dietary interventions.

Micro-computed tomography (microCT) analysis of the distal femur, together with H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining, demonstrated that VB5 supplementation markedly attenuated OVX-induced bone loss. Quantitative morphometric analysis showed that VB5 supplementation significantly increased bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular number (Tb.N) (Fig. 1A–D and Supplementary Fig. 1B).

TRAP staining further revealed that VB5 supplementation significantly reduced the number of osteoclasts (Fig. 1E). To explore the underlying mechanism, the expression of osteoclast-related proteins in femoral tissues was examined. Western blot analysis showed that the protein levels of NFATc1, CTSK, and c-FOS were decreased in the VB5-supplemented group and increased in the VB5-deficient group compared with controls (Fig. 1F).

In addition, the expression of osteogenic differentiation–related proteins in femoral tissues was assessed, and no significant differences were observed following VB5 intervention(Supplementary Fig. 1C).

Collectively, these in vivo findings indicate that VB5 attenuates OVX-induced bone loss primarily by suppressing osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption, without significantly affecting bone formation.

VB5 suppresses osteoclast differentiation in a concentration-dependent manner in vitro

RAW264.7 cells and BMDMs were treated with varying concentrations of VB5 (0.5, 1 and 10 mM).CCK-8 assay results indicated that none of these concentrations significantly affected cell viability (Fig. 2A, B). In addition, flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that VB5 at these concentrations did not significantly induce apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 1D, E). Subsequently, to evaluate the effect of VB5 on RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation, RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with RANKL in the presence of different concentrations of VB5 (0.5, 1 and 10 mM). Western blotting analysis revealed that VB5 dose-dependently and significantly reduced the protein expression levels of osteoclast-specific markers, including NFATc1, CTSK, and c-Fos (Fig. 2C). Therefore, a concentration of 10 mM VB5 was selected for subsequent experiments.

VB5 inhibits osteoclast differentiation through CoA in a pantothenate kinase (PANK)-dependent manner

We next investigated how VB5 affects osteoclast differentiation. As VB5 is a precursor for CoA biosynthesis, we observed increased intracellular CoA levels in osteoclasts exposed to excess VB5 (Supplementary Fig. 1F)1. This prompted us to examine whether VB5 influences osteoclast differentiation via CoA. After determining non-toxic CoA concentrations, we supplemented cell culture media with 0.3 mM CoA during osteoclast differentiation induction (Supplementary Fig. 1G).

CoA treatment significantly reduced the expression of osteoclast-related genes (NFATc1, CTSK, c-Fos) in RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 4A). TRAP staining and actin ring formation assays further confirmed that CoA inhibits RAW264.7 cell differentiation (Fig. 4B, C). Similar suppressive effects of CoA on osteoclast differentiation were observed in BMDMs (Fig. 4D–F).

VB5 inhibits glucose metabolism during osteoclast differentiation

Prior studies demonstrate that VB5 suppresses both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) in Th17 cells15. We therefore hypothesized that VB5 might exert similar metabolic effects during osteoclast differentiation. To test this, we performed metabolic flux analysis on VB5-treated RAW264.7 cells. VB5 exposure significantly reduced glycolytic capacity, as measured by extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) (Supplementary Fig. 1H). More importantly, VB5 substantially impaired mitochondrial respiration (Fig. 6A). Correspondingly, CoA treatment markedly suppressed both glycolysis (Supplementary Fig. 1I) and OXPHOS (Fig. 6B).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that elevated VB5 or CoA levels significantly suppress glucose metabolism in osteoclasts.

VB5 impairs PKM2 nuclear translocation

To elucidate the cellular mechanism underlying VB5/CoA-mediated inhibition of osteoclast differentiation, we investigated whether VB5 regulates PKM2. Molecular docking analysis revealed potential binding between VB5 and PKM2 (Fig. 6C). Subsequent co-immunoprecipitation assays confirmed a strong interaction between CoA and endogenous PKM2 (Fig. 6D).

Western blot analysis demonstrated that PKM2 expression increased progressively during osteoclast differentiation and represented the predominant isoform (Fig. 6E). Given that PKM2 nuclear translocation depends on its monomeric/dimeric states (versus tetrameric state), we examined whether VB5 or CoA affects PKM2 oligomerization21. Nuclear fractionation assays showed that both VB5 and CoA significantly reduced nuclear PKM2 levels (Fig. 6F).

Since phosphorylation at Y105 inhibits PKM2 tetramerization and promotes nuclear localization, we analyzed this modification in osteoclasts exposed to VB5 or CoA15. Both treatments substantially decreased PKM2 Y105 phosphorylation across multiple time points (Fig. 6G, H).

To functionally link PKM2 phosphorylation/nuclear translocation to osteoclast differentiation, we used TEPP-46—a small-molecule activator that stabilizes PKM2 tetramers, thereby preventing phosphorylation and nuclear translocation. Western blot analysis (Fig. 6I) showed that TEPP-46 treatment partially normalized PKM2-related molecular alterations observed under VB5-deficient conditions in OVX mice.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that VB5 inhibits osteoclast differentiation by modulating PKM2 phosphorylation status, oligomerization, and nuclear translocation.

Discussion

Osteoclast-mediated bone resorption is a central component of bone remodeling and contributes to bone loss under pathological conditions22. Osteoclast differentiation and resorptive activity are highly energy-dependent processes23. In addition to canonical signaling pathways and lineage-specific transcriptional programs, accumulating evidence indicates that metabolic reprogramming plays a critical role in osteoclast differentiation and function24. In particular, enhanced glycolysis has been proposed as a key metabolic adaptation supporting mature osteoclast activity6. However, whether nutritional factors, including specific vitamins, can directly modulate osteoclast metabolic programs remains incompletely understood.

Vitamins are essential micronutrients involved in diverse physiological processes25. Beyond the established role of vitamin D in bone homeostasis, several other vitamins—including vitamins A, C, E, K, and members of the B complex—have been implicated in skeletal health25,26. Observational and experimental studies have suggested associations between vitamin B12, B1, and B6 and bone metabolism or fracture risk27–29. In contrast, the role of VB5, CoA, in bone remodeling has not been clearly defined.

In the present study, we examined the impact of dietary VB5 status on osteoclast differentiation and bone structural parameters in vitro and in an ovariectomy (OVX) mouse model. Our findings indicate that VB5 supplementation was associated with reduced osteoclast differentiation and lower expression of osteoclast-related markers in vitro. In OVX mice, VB5 supplementation was associated with improved trabecular bone parameters, whereas VB5 deficiency was associated with reduced bone volume indices. These observations suggest that VB5 status may influence bone remodeling under estrogen-deficient conditions.

Mechanistically, our data suggest that VB5-derived CoA may interact with PKM2 and be associated with reduced PKM2 Y105 phosphorylation and decreased nuclear localization, accompanied by altered glycolytic activity in osteoclasts. These findings support a potential link between VB5 availability and metabolic regulation during osteoclast differentiation. However, while the present data strongly implicate PKM2 as a responsive metabolic node, definitive demonstration of causality will require more comprehensive genetic approaches, such as conditional PKM2 knockout or rescue models. Thus, the proposed PANK–CoA–PKM2 axis should be considered a working mechanistic framework rather than a conclusively established pathway.

Importantly, previous studies have reported that VB5 may suppress osteoclast differentiation through modulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and apoptosis-related pathways. For example, Ma et al. demonstrated that VB5 attenuated osteoclastogenesis partly by enhancing antioxidant responses and regulating FoxO/Nrf2 signaling30. These findings raise the possibility that the anti-osteoclastogenic effects of VB5 involve redox regulation in addition to metabolic remodeling.

The present study proposes a complementary but distinct perspective by linking VB5-derived CoA to PKM2-associated metabolic reprogramming. Rather than representing mutually exclusive mechanisms, metabolic regulation and redox homeostasis are likely interconnected processes. PKM2 has been implicated not only in glycolytic flux control but also in redox balance and transcriptional regulation under oxidative stress conditions. Therefore, it is plausible that VB5-induced CoA accumulation may coordinately influence PKM2 activity, glycolytic metabolism, ROS handling, and downstream transcriptional programs. In this context, metabolic and antioxidant pathways may operate in parallel or sequentially to regulate osteoclast differentiation.

Further studies are required to clarify the relationship between PKM2 modulation and ROS signaling in VB5-mediated regulation of osteoclastogenesis. Several limitations should be noted. The in vitro concentrations of VB5 were supraphysiological and selected to explore pharmacological effects; whether nutritionally achievable levels exert similar skeletal effects remains unknown. Bone formation parameters were not directly assessed in vivo, and the safety profile and pharmacokinetics of VB5 were not systematically evaluated. Therefore, the translational relevance of these findings requires further investigation. Importantly, the lack of body weight tracking prevents us from fully excluding growth-related effects of the VB5-deficient diet, while our restricted assessment of bone formation (n = 3) indicates that conclusions regarding osteoclast-specific mechanisms should be interpreted with caution.

Taken together, our findings suggest that VB5 status is associated with alterations in osteoclast differentiation and trabecular bone parameters in vitro and in an OVX mouse model. These results provide preliminary evidence linking vitamin-dependent metabolic pathways to bone remodeling. Given the supraphysiological concentrations used and the exploratory nature of the mechanistic analyses, further studies incorporating dose-ranging experiments, long-term intervention models, genetic validation, and additional preclinical systems will be necessary to define the physiological relevance and translational potential of VB5 in skeletal metabolism.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that VB5 mitigates bone loss in the young adult ovariectomized mouse model. Mechanistically, our in vitro data suggest that VB5 may influence osteoclastogenesis by modulating PKM2 activity, highlighting a potential role as a metabolic-epigenetic modulator. However, given the lack of direct genetic validation and the use of supraphysiological in vitro concentrations, these mechanistic insights remain preliminary. While our findings indicate that VB5 exerts its protective effects primarily through the inhibition of osteoclast activity, the limited assessment of bone formation suggests this interpretation should be approached with caution. Future studies, including comprehensive in vivo metabolic profiling and genetic knockout models, are required to fully elucidate the clinical and translational potential of VB5 in metabolic bone disorders (Fig. 7).
