The clinical benefit of adding anlotinib to chemoradiothearpy in high-grade gliomas: a systematic review, meta-analysis, and specific analysis on glioblastoma
Authors: Mohammad Amin Habibi, Seyed Hesam Hojjat, Bardia Hajikarimloo, Mohsen Dashti, Afsaneh Ghasemzadeh, Mahboobeh Tajvidi, Amirmohammad Bahri, Mohammad Shahir Eftekhar, Negin Safari Dehnavi, Amirhossein Kamroo, Ibrahim Mohammadzadeh, Milad Shafizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnlotinib, a novel multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitor, has shown promise in improving survival and managing associated complications. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of Anlotinib in patients with high-grade glioma, specifically glioblastoma (GBM). This study was conducted according to the PRISMA guidelines. A systematic search was conducted on PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus up to 9 December 2024. The included studies were appraised and assessed for quality and potential bias and further statistical analyses were performed using STATA v.17. A total of 17 studies with 473 patients were recruited, which included 204 patients with GBM. The pooled analysis resulted in a 6-month OS of 67% (95% CI: 42–92%) and a 1-year OS equal to 50% (95% CI: 36–64%) from the Anlotinib treatment. Progression-free survival at 6 months was 61% (95% CI: 46–75%), and at 1 year was 27% (95% CI: 13–41%), also showing similar promising outcomes. The overall response rate (ORR) and disease control rate (DCR) were reported at 55% (95% CI: 44–67%) and 90% (95% CI: 87–94%), respectively. The sub-group analysis revealed that adding anlotinb to temozolomide (TMZ) and radiotherapy had superior outcomes regarding OS, PFS, and DCR. Moreover, the highest ORR and complete response rate were achieved by Anlotinib plus stereotactic radiosurgery. Anlotinib demonstrates considerable efficacy in extending survival and achieving disease control in high-grade glioma patients when added to radiotherapy and TMZ. These findings can support its inclusion in therapeutic regimens, warranting further investigation in large-scale randomized controlled trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-025-02024-z.Keywords: Tyrosine kinase inhibitor, TKI, Glioma, Glioblastoma, GBM

Introduction

Gliomas are a heterogeneous group of primary brain tumors that present considerable challenges in neuro-oncology [1], Subtypes such as astrocytomas, oligodendrogliomas, ependymomas, and glioblastomas exhibit significant variability in biological behavior and prognosis [2]. Even though multimodal treatment strategies like maximal surgical resection, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy have gotten better, the outlook for people with high-grade gliomas is still not good. The median survival time for these patients ranges from 1 to 5 years, depending on the subtype and grade [3]. For lower-grade gliomas, survival may last for several years; however, progression to higher grades frequently occurs [4].

The infiltrative nature of gliomas, in addition to their molecular heterogeneity and resistance to standard therapies, contributes to the overall difficulty in achieving durable outcomes [5]. Current treatment challenges encompass the inability to attain complete surgical resection owing to the diffuse growth patterns of gliomas, the constraints imposed by the blood-brain barrier on systemic therapy delivery, and the tumors’ intrinsic resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy [6, 7]. These obstacles raise the sense of need for innovative therapeutic approaches targeting glioma-specific pathways while maintaining manageable toxicity profiles [8].

Anlotinib, a novel multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), has emerged as a promising candidate in glioma therapy [9–11]. Its mode of action entails the suppression of critical pathways integral to tumor angiogenesis and proliferation, encompassing vascular endothelial growth factor receptors (VEGFRs), fibroblast growth factor receptors (FGFRs), and platelet-derived growth factor receptors (PDGFRs). After these pathways are disrupted, Anlotinib reduces the vascular support required for tumor growth and progression. This makes it a possible treatment for people with different types of gliomas (Fig. 1) [11].

Mechanism of action of Anlotinib

Preliminary studies have indicated that Anlotinib may improve progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in glioma patients while maintaining a manageable safety profile [9, 10]. Commonly reported adverse events (AE) are known to be hand-foot syndrome and hypertension, being generally mild to moderate in severity, reinforcing its feasibility in clinical practice [10, 11]. However, data remain scattered across small-scale studies with variable methodologies, necessitating a comprehensive evaluation of its efficacy and safety; therefore, in this systematic review and meta-analysis, we aimed to synthesize the available evidence on the role of Anlotinib in high-grade glioma treatment, focusing on survival outcomes, response rates, and adverse event profiles.

Method

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [12] for the primary aim of assessing the efficacy and safety of Anlotinib in glioma.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus, with no language restrictions applied, up to December 9, 2024. Each database used specifically tailored search strategies by combining controlled vocabulary terms, as in PubMed and Embase, and free-text terms. The search query included Anlotinib and catequentinib for covering the Anlotinib-related terms, and glioma, glioblastoma, anaplastic astrocytoma, and gliosarcoma for covering the glioma-related terms. Detailed search strings are provided in Table S1.

Eligibility criteria

The PICO framework was designed as follows: Patients with high-grade glioma, Intervention: Anlotinib, Comparator: standalone administration of chemoradiotherapy (if not available, comparator is considered non), Outcome: elements related to Survival Rates, Response Rates, and Adverse Events in different grades and time frames.

Inclusion criteria

(1) English-language studies or Chinese-language studies under the condition that the full-texts were available; (2) Clinical studies on patients with Glioma; (3) Studies evaluating the outcomes after treating with Anlotinib; (4) Peer-reviewed Original studies, including case series (> 5 patients), case-control studies, cross-sectional studies, randomized and non-randomized clinical trials, cohort studies, and conference abstracts with peer-reviewed articles.

Exclusion criteria

(1) non-English studies and Chinese-language studies whose full-texts were not available; (2) in vivo or in vitro studies; (3) studies on patients without glioma; (4) studies on patients who were not treated with Anlotinib; (5) case reports, conference abstracts without peer-reviewed articles, review articles, commentary articles, or book chapters; (6) grey literature reports without peer-reviewing.

Study Selection, data Extraction, and quality assessment

Title and abstract screening for eligibility was performed by two independent reviewers, followed by full-text review for articles that met the inclusion criteria. Notably, the third reviewer’s discussion and consultation resolved the discrepancies. Afterwards, data were extracted using a standardized form that included characteristics of the study (e.g., study design, country, and sample size), patient demographics, treatment regimens, and outcomes such as OS, PFS, Local Control (LC), and radiological response rates according to the RANO criteria, and adverse events. Notably, we utilized the Cochrane risk-of-bias (ROB 2) tool for the quality evaluation of all included randomized controlled trials [13, 14], and the “ROBINS-I” for retrospective studies [15].

Statistical analysis

The right effect size was chosen using the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [12]. The random effect model and restricted maximum likelihood (REML) approach were used for the meta-analysis (Chap. 9: Analyzing data and executing meta-analyses). Together with 95% CIs, pooled estimates of survival outcomes, radiological response rates, and adverse event rates were computed. Significance of the heterogeneity was defined as chi-square P-value < 0.05, and interpretation of I2 metrics was conducted as follows: negligible heterogeneity as 0% to 40%, moderate heterogeneity as 30% to 60%, substantial heterogeneity as 50% to 90%, and considerable heterogeneity as 75% to 100% (Chap. 9.5.2: Identifying and measuring heterogeneity). Notably, Statistical significance was defined as a p-value < 0.05. By utilizing leave-one-out approach, the sensitivity analysis was performed in the analysis domains, where each included study was sequentially removed to assess its influence on the overall effect size, and if a single study significantly altered the pooled effect estimate, its influence was further investigated.

Egger’s test was also employed to assess potential publication bias. By using subgroup analyses, outcomes across different treatment regimens were compared (Table S2). Meta-regression was then performed in context of Glioma and also GBM to assess the significant factors of outcomes and sources of heterogeneity (Table S3 and Table S4). The safety profile of study was shown in supplementary file. All statistical analysis was done using the STATA v.17 program and the Risk-of-Bias plots were generated using “robvis” tool [16].

Results

Study selection

Database searches yielded a total of 3,792 records: PubMed (644), Scopus (1,650), Web of Science (139), and Embase (1,359). After we eliminated 1,806 duplicate records, we evaluated 1,986 distinct records based on their title and abstract. We excluded 1,856 records because they did not meet the inclusion criteria. 130 full-text reports were requested for retrieval, and full-texts of 13 reports were not found. Following full-text assessment, 100 reports were excluded for the following reasons: 87 due to irrelevance to the study scope, 11 due to duplication, and 2 due to the wrong patient and population. In the end, this systematic review and meta-analysis included 17 studies, all under retrospective cohort or phase II clinical trial design, with a total of 473 patients, and 204 patients with glioblastoma were included (Fig. 2a).

Quality assessment and risk of bias

Utilizing the “RoB2” and “ROBINS-I”, the studies were assessed for possible biases and quality of study conduct. RoB2 was used to assess the quality of randomized clinical trials, and ROBINS-I was applied for observational studies. 9 trials comprising 10 records underwent the RoB2 quality assessment. 4 records yielded an overall “Some concerns” score for risk of bias, and 6 records yielded an overall “Low” score for risk of bias. Given the single-arm design of many studies, no proper assessment could be made for bias arising from the randomization process. The most frequent domain of bias was biases due to the selection of the reported study, with 3 records of “Some concerns”. Other domains of biases (deviation from intervention, missing outcome data, and measurement of the outcome) were found in 2 records. 8 studies comprising 8 records underwent the ROBINS-I quality assessment. 1 record yielded an overall “Serious” score for risk of bias; 4 records yielded an overall “Moderate” score for risk of bias, and 3 records yielded an overall “Low” score for risk of bias. The most frequently witnessed domain of bias was due to missing data: 1 record yielded a “Serious” score, 1 record yielded a “Moderate” score, and 2 records with “No information”. Bias in the selection of the reported outcome and the selection of participants did not qualify as “Low” risk of bias in 3 records. Biases due to deviation from the intended intervention were qualified as “Moderate” risk of bias in 2 records. The least concerning domains of bias were biases due to confounding and measurement of outcomes, with only 1 record not receiving “Low” risk of bias for each domain.

Study characteristics

A total of 17 studies involving 473 patients were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. Among these studies, 8 were retrospective cohort studies or case-control studies, while 9 were clinical trials. Notably, 2 studies were multicenter, while others were conducted in one institution. Patients in the included studies, aged from 4 to 75 years. Glioma patients were mainly presented with either Brainstem, Thalamus, or Multifocal lesions (Tables 1 and 2).

Overall survival (OS)

6-Month OS

A total of 4 studies were included in the analysis of 6-month OS. The pooled survival rate was 67% (95% CI: 0.42–0.92). A considerable and significant heterogeneity was observed among studies. (I² = 87.2%, P < 0.01) (Fig. 3a).

Pooled overall survival at 6-month (a), 1-year (b)

Subgroup analysis revealed statistically significant differences in favor of patients with concurrent radiotherapy compared to those who underwent either alone or in concurrence with TMZ treatment (P = 0.01). The comparison between the Anlotinib group, the Anlotinib & Radiotherapy group, and the Anlotinib & TMZ group also resulted in a significant favor for radiotherapy (P < 0.01). No statistically significant difference was observed between the group who received prior combined chemo/radiotherapy and the group who also received additional immunotherapy (p = 0.15). Notably, the most frequent tumor location subgroup analysis revealed that those with brainstem tumors have a significantly higher 6-month OS compared to patients with multifocal tumors (p = 0.01). Additionally, RCT studies demonstrated a significantly higher 6-month OS compared to retrospective studies (p = 0.01).

Furthermore, meta-regression analysis identified significant influences on the 6-month OS from female gender (β = 1.3925, p = 0.011), age (β = 0.0153, p = 0.027), grade 4 of tumor (β = 0.8291, p = 0.031), methylation of MGMT (β = 1.4632, p = 0.037), wild type of IDH (β = 0.8011, p = 0.040), and also male gender (β = 0.9519, p = 0.085), while grade 3 of tumor (β = 1.8081, p = 0.094), no-methylation of MGMT (β = 0.9389, p = 0.053), and mutation of IDH (β = 1.9971, p = 0.062) were not found to be significant moderators.

1-Year OS

A total of 8 studies were included in the analysis of 1-Year OS with the pooled survival rate of 50% (95% CI: 0.36–0.64). A statistically significant and considerable heterogeneity was observed among studies (I² = 75.24%, P < 0.01) (Fig. 3b). Additionally, the results in the GBM patients were 67% (95% CI: 0.47–0.87), with a statistically significant and considerable heterogeneity observed among the 3 included studies (I² = 71.17%, P < 0.01).

Subgroup analysis revealed a non-statistically significant difference in the concurrence of either radiotherapy or TMZ (P = 0.09). The comparison between the Anlotinib group, the Anlotinib & Radiotherapy group, the Anlotinib & TMZ group, and the Anlotinib & TMZ & Radiotherapy group resulted in a significant difference, with a tendency for higher 1-year OS for the group treated with concurrent TMZ and radiotherapy (P < 0.01). No statistically significant difference was observed in 1-year OS of patients with either brainstem or multifocal tumor groups (p = 0.11). There was also no statistically significant difference in the comparison between the previous treatment of the chemo/radiotherapy group, the group that received additional immunotherapy, and the surgery group (p = 0.90). Additionally, significant differences were observed in the subgroups based on the study’s type and source of data in favor of RCTs and multicenter studies (p < 0.01 and p < 0.01, respectively).

Meta-regression analysis also identified significant influences on the 1-year OS from female gender (β = 1.0944, p = 0.001), male gender (β = 0.9096, p = 0.008), age (β = 0.0111, p < 0.001), grade 4 of tumor (β = 0.5080, p = 0.030), methylation of MGMT (β = 1.3068, p = 0.010), no-methylation of MGMT (β = 0.8052, p = 0.001), and wild type of IDH (β = 0.6311, p = 0.001), while grade 3 of tumor (β = 0.0111, p = 0.093) and mutation of IDH (β = 1.5195, p = 0.178) were not found as significant moderators.

Overall adverse events

Given the discrepancies between the studies for reporting the AEs, we decided to perform a narrative synthesis to better illuminate the AEs associated with the treatment regimen. The following narration reports the AEs at any grades (I, II, and > II), and grade > II biotoxicities.

Yang et al. investigated the clinical outcomes and safety profiles of Anlotinib in two different protocols: standalone Anlotinib and Anlotinib as an adjuvant to TMZ [17]. The standalone Anlotinib arm had only grade I or II toxicity: 1 patient with hand-foot syndrome, 3 patients with mucositis, 7 patients with hypertension, 2 patients with gastrointestinal reaction, 2 patients with leukopenia, 1 patient with hypothyroidism, 1 patient with proteinuria, 3 patients with hypertriglyceridemia, and 1 patient with bleeding. Similarly, in the other cohort, Anlotinib as an adjuvant to TMZ, patients had only grade I or II AEs: 3 patients with hand-foot syndrome, 4 patients with mucositis, 8 patients with hypertension, 9 patients with gastrointestinal reaction, 8 patients with anemia, 3 patients with thrombocytopenia, 9 patients with leukopenia, 1 patient with hypothyroidism, 3 patients with proteinuria, 7 patients with liver dysfunction, 2 patients with hypertriglyceridemia, 1 patient with bleeding, and 4 patients with abnormality in ECG.

Yang et al. investigated the clinical outcomes and safety profiles of Anlotinib in the Anlotinib as an adjuvant to the TMZ protocol [18]. This study followed NCI-CTCAE. The Anlotinib as an adjuvant to the TMZ arm had the following biotoxicities: 13 patients with hand-foot syndrome at any grades, 2 patients with grade > II hand-foot syndrome, 9 patients with hypertension at any grades, 7 patients with anemia at any grades, 4 patients with gastrointestinal reaction at any grades, 5 patients with neutropenia at any grades, 9 patients with thrombocytopenia at any grades, 2 patients with grade > II thrombocytopenia, 12 patients with leukopenia at any grades, 8 patients with proteinuria at any grades, 11 patients with hyperbilirubin at any grades, and 1 patient with grade > II hyperbilirubin.

She et al. investigated the clinical outcomes and safety profiles of Anlotinib in the Anlotinib as an adjuvant to the TMZ protocol [19]. The Anlotinib as an adjuvant to the TMZ arm had the following biotoxicities: 4 patients with hand-foot syndrome at any grades, 1 patient with grade > II hand-foot syndrome, 2 patients with mucositis at any grades, 1 patient with grade > II mucositis, 5 patients with hypertension, 4 patients with grade > II hypertension, 1 patient with rash at any grade, 7 patients with anemia, 1 patient with thrombocytopenia at any grades, 1 patient with liver dysfunction at any grades, 1 patient with hypertriglyceridemia at any grades, and 1 patient grade > II cerebral infarction.

Yin et al. investigated the clinical outcomes and safety profiles of Anlotinib as a standalone protocol [20]. This study followed NCI-CTCAE. No grade > II AEs were observed: 1 patient with hand-foot syndrome, 4 patients with fatigue, 4 patients with hypertension, 2 patients with short-term memory loss, 1 patient with drowsiness, 1 patient with edema, and 1 patient with joint pain and hoarseness.

Yang et al. investigated the clinical outcomes and safety profiles of Anlotinib in the Anlotinib as an adjuvant to the TMZ protocol [21]. The following grade > II AE was observed in this study: 12 patients with grade > II lymphopenia; lymphopenia was most likely attributed to the TMZ.

Death rate (DR)

Pooled Rate of Death in the 4 validated included studies was 71% (95% CI: 61–81%), with insignificant and negligible heterogeneity among them (I² = 0.00%, P = 0.59) (Fig. 8).

Subgroup analysis revealed no statistically significant difference between the concurrent administration of TMZ compared to Anlotinib alone (P = 0.17), and there was also no significant difference in the comparison between the Anlotinib group, the Anlotinib & TMZ group, and the group receiving Anlotinib & TMZ & radiotherapy at the same time (P = 0.39). Tumor location also made no statistically significant difference as compared between the groups of brainstem and thalamus (p = 0.17).

Even though meta-regression analysis identified female gender (β = 1.6231, p = 0.004), male gender (β = 1.2509, p = 0.001), age (β = 0.0164, p = 0.029), grade 3 of tumor (β = 2.295, p = 0.009), grade 4 of tumor (β = 0.9271, p = 0.008), no methylation of MGMT (β = 1.0014, p = 0.001), and wild type of IDH (β = 0.8799, p = 0.001) to have significant influences on DR, while methylation of MGMT (β = 1.7890, p = 0.059) and mutation of IDH (β = 2.3862, p = 0.095) were associated with no significant influence on it.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we conducted a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis across all key outcomes, and the results demonstrated that pooled outcomes of “6-month and 1-year OS-ranging from 0.57–0.74 and 0.45–0.54, respectively- similar to 6-month and 1-year PFS- ranging from 0.56–0.62 and 0.20–0.31, respectively-, remained stable (p values < 0.001) when individual studies were removed. Similar results were obtained in all domains where no substantial variation was observed and no single study excessively influenced the meta-analysis, except for the adverse effects of “Anemia at any grade, Gastrointestinal reactions at any grade, Headache at any grade, and events of Grade 3 or higher in the context of Hypertriglyceridemia, Liver dysfunction, Mucositis and mouth ulcers and Neutropenia” which indicated moderate to severe (P values higher than 0.05) shifts in effect size when removing, suggesting some variability in toxicity reporting and that these adverse events may not be a reliable indicator of the intervention’s effect across studies, however these outcomes require further investigation due to inconsistencies across studies. Overall, the sensitivity analysis confirmed the reliability of our findings.

Publication bias

Egger’s test suggested significant publication bias for 6-month OS (t = −4.54, p = 0.0452), 1-year OS (t = −2.25, p = 0.0653), 6-month PFS (t = −1.98, p = 0.0792), 1-year PFS (t = 0.42, p = 0.7003), overall response rate (t = −1.43, p = 0.1762), and disease control rate (t = −3.95, p = 0.0023). No significant publication bias was observed for complete response rate (CR) (t = 2.85, p = 0.0462), progressive disease rate (PD) (t = 4.26, p = 0.0021), stable disease rate (SD) (t = 1.64, p = 0.1291), or death rate (t = −0.12, p = 0.9181). Significant publication bias was also noted for adverse events, including hand-foot syndrome of any grade (t = 2.44, p = 0.0331), mucositis or mouth ulcers of any grade (t = 3.09, p = 0.0214), hypertension of any grade (t = 2.55, p = 0.0241), and anemia of any grade (t = 3.22, p = 0.0486). No significant publication bias was detected for hand-foot syndrome grade ≥ 3 (t = 1.07, p = 0.3620), mucositis or mouth ulcers grade ≥ 3 (convergence not achieved), hypertension grade ≥ 3 (t = 1.96, p = 0.1070), neutropenia any grade (t = 0.54, p = 0.6436), neutropenia grade ≥ 3 (convergence not achieved), thrombocytopenia any grade (t = 5.27, p = 0.0012), or thrombocytopenia grade ≥ 3 (t = 1.76, p = 0.147). For overall adverse events, Egger’s test indicated significant publication bias for the overall AE rate of any grade (convergence not achieved) and the overall AE rate grade ≥ 3 (t = 2.69, p = 0.0741). Additional adverse events, such as gastrointestinal reactions of any grade (t = 3.62, p = 0.0684), rash of any grade (t = 0.82, p = 0.4964), and fatigue of any grade (t = 1.62, p = 0.2042), demonstrated no significant publication bias based on the results provided. Convergence issues were noted for select adverse events with limited study numbers.

Discussion

Gliomas remain one of the most challenging malignancies in neuro-oncology, characterized by aggressive progression, poor prognosis, and limited therapeutic options [10, 30, 31]. Our systematic review and meta-analysis consolidated the present evidence on the efficacy and safety of Anlotinib in the treatment of gliomas and GBM patients, with the results demonstrating significant improvements in domains such as PFS, OS, ORR, and DCR. From the point of our findings, it could be suggested that the addition of Anlotinib might improve the clinical outcomes of glioma patients [9, 10]. The current protocols and standard of care for glioma patients rely on the maximal surgical resection followed by Adjuvant chemo/radio/immunotherapy regimens and mainly TMZ administration, with the outcomes being often limited by tumor recurrence and resistance [31, 32]. Based on our findings, it could be suggested that the addition of Anlotinib to the frequently applied TMZ might enhance the durability of the clinical management [9, 10], also our findings align with the pharmacological mechanisms of Anlotinib, targeting key pathways of angiogenesis and tumor proliferation [11, 33]. Anlotinib’s multi-target mechanism, which inhibits VEGFRs, FGFRs, and PDGFRs pathways, is poised to be an effective agent in disrupting tumor angiogenesis and specifically useful in the management of gliomas, as gliomas have been demonstrated to have an angiogenic nature [11, 33]. In addition to the VEGFRs, FGFRs, and PDGFRs pathways, the mTOR signaling pathway has been demonstrated as a central regulator of tumoral expansion, such as in breast cancer [34], renal cell carcinoma [35], and glioma [36]. In line with the inhibition of the angiogenic factors, Anlotinib has shown promise in disrupting the mTOR signaling axes [37], to ultimately shorten the life span of glioma cells and increase their cellular death rates [38]. Another mechanism through which Anlotinib could exert its anti-tumoral effects is the disruption of cellular metabolism. Due to the pharmacological effects of Anlotinib in glioma cells, amino acid metabolism, energy production, and glycerophospholipid cycles could all be disrupted in a manner that is likely to be closely associated with the mitochondria [39]. Given the role of ROS in damaging the mitochondrial DNA [40], it could be hypothesized that the anti-glioma functions of Anlotinib might involve damage to the structure of the mitochondrial DNA.

While previous studies demonstrate that the anti-angiogenic application of TKIs, such as bevacizumab, in glioma treatment is limited by severe adverse events [41], the manageable safety profile of Anlotinib in the included studies suggests that it could be considered without significant compromise to patient quality of life [9–11, 33]. The safety profile of Anlotinib, enhances the feasibility of its possible integration for routine clinical practice, as common adverse events such as hand-foot syndrome, hypertension, and hematologic toxicities are shown to be generally mild to moderate, with severe (Grade ≥ 3) events being relatively infrequent [23]. Altogether, our findings could suggest that the application of Anlotinib as an adjuvant in the treatment of glioma might improve the clinical outcomes without imposing life-threatening AEs on the patients. However, this study is limited by statistical and methodological issues, and all the results of this study shall be interpreted with maximum caution. Large-scale cohorts and trials with extended follow-up periods are warranted to ensure the clinical outcomes and safety profiles of the addition of Anlotinib.

Negative results

As a well-balanced study should discuss both positive and negative findings, while Anlotinib demonstrated promising efficacy in terms of ORR (55%, 95% CI: 44–67%) and DCR (90%, 95% CI: 87–94%), several subgroup analyses failed to show statistically significant benefits. Notably, in the 1-year OS analysis, the comparison between different treatment regimens revealed non-significant differences between Anlotinib monotherapy and combination therapies (p = 0.09). Notably, the same results were obtained for 1-year PFS (27%, 95% CI: 13–41%), where no statistically significant advantage was observed in specific treatment subgroups (p = 0.11). Importantly, the non-significant difference marks the inconclusive findings regarding this clinical outcome of Anlotinib, and it is more likely that the addition of Anlotinib could not outperform the conventional treatment approach.

Publication bias and its implications

Publication bias is an inherent limitation that often favors the dissemination of positive results while underrepresenting negative or inconclusive findings, and similarly our study, utilizing Egger’s test detected significant publication bias in several key outcomes, including 6-month OS (p = 0.0452), 1-year OS (p = 0.0653), and overall disease control rate (DCR) (p = 0.0023), and certain adverse events, such as hand-foot syndrome (p = 0.0331) and hypertension (p = 0.0241), suggesting that milder toxicities may be underreported in the literature. In this study, the Trim-and-Fill method was not applied, as the number of included studies was already limited, and additional imputed studies could introduce further uncertainty into the meta-analysis. Given the results of Egger’s test indicating the inflation of Anlotinib’s pooled effect size, it could be concluded that the positive results might have been more preferentially published. Future studies that prioritize prospective trial registration, mandatory reporting of all endpoints, including non-significant results, and comprehensive safety reporting may mitigate these effects, and additionally, the inclusion of conference abstracts and unpublished data in future systematic reviews and meta-analyses may help counterbalance this bias and provide a more accurate representation of Anlotinib’s clinical utility. Meta-analysis studies should incorporate Trim-and-Fill or Bayesian approaches to adjust for potential bias and improve the robustness of findings.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Even though the systematic approach of this meta-analysis, utilizing the comprehensive synthesis of available evidence, rigorous statistical assessments, publication bias evaluations, and the inclusion of both efficacy and safety outcomes of Anlotinib in glioma and GBM management, enhances the credibility of the findings, certain limitations such as high level of heterogeneity (I² values, generally being higher than 75%) among the studies-particularly in terms of patient populations characteristics, treatment protocols of different dosages and regimens, and outcome measures- can introduce variability in the pooled estimates, and even though the random-effects model was employed to account for this variability, substantial heterogeneity may still impact the reliability and generalizability of our findings, so to resolve this issues, we conducted meta-regression and subgroup analyses to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. However, the results of meta-regression might be considered “fragile” given the < 10 studies in the meta-regression. Especially, “fragility” of compensating statistical analysis for important moderators, MGMT and IDH status, could introduce biases in the survival and tumor control outcomes. Additionally, the relatively small sample size, with 473 patients across the included 17 studies, including 204 GBM patients, where the limited number of participants-particularly in subgroup analyses, may reduce statistical power while increasing the risk of type II errors. As several analyses, such as 6-month and 1-year OS, were based on fewer than 5 studies, the reliability of these estimates may be restricted, and the generalizability of findings could be decreased, especially for specific treatment subgroups. Future large-scale, multicenter RCTs are essential to confirm these results and improve external validity. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of our findings, by systematically excluding studies with extreme results or lower methodological quality, we evaluated the stability of the pooled estimates in all domain except for the adverse effects of “Anemia at any grade, Gastrointestinal reactions at any grade, Headache at any grade, and events of Grade 3 or higher in the context of Hypertriglyceridemia, Liver dysfunction, Mucositis and mouth ulcers and Neutropenia” suggesting the variability in toxicity reporting and the and the unreliability of these adverse events due to inconsistencies across studies. Although many of the studies followed the NCI-CTCAE, there were still uncertainties in some of the studies regarding the criteria for assessing the AEs. We tried to better elucidate the AEs using the narrative synthesis, yet the interpretation of AEs demands extreme caution. Another limitation of this study was the inaccessibility to some of the Chinese-language literature. Although being found eligible in the screening, the full texts of these studies could not be retrieved, and reasonable requests to the corresponding authors were left unanswered. We believe that the inclusion of the aforementioned studies could help improve the reliability of our findings. In addition, this study only included records with a clear history of being peer-reviewed; hence, the grey literature records were not considered. Although the grey literature records did not undergo a clear external peer review, the generalizability of our study could be limited by not considering the grey literature. Last but not least, this study is severely limited by the inflation of the Antolinib’s pooled effect size due to the preferential publication of positive results. Egger’s test indicates the possibility of the presence of publication bias, which damages the generalizability of the results of this study. The possible lack/absence of the negative results, especially the clinical outcomes with a significant p-value of publication bias in Egger’s test, could substantially change the findings of out meta-analysis.

Future directions and implications

The findings of this paper could be integrated with the state-of-the-art computational insights and AI applications. Various aspects, such as target identification [42], virtual screening [43], and drug repurposing [44], have been accelerated by AI tools, which could be further extended into Anlotinib’s investigation in glioma. Besides, the AI applications could be applied to predict survival, recurrence, and treatment responses [45], which helps deepen our understanding of the clinical benefits of Anlotinib against glioma. The results of this study may open new windows for further research in the context of applications of Anlotinib for the management of Glioma patients, where Biomarker-driven studies that can identify subgroups of patients that are most likely to benefit from Anlotinib, may enhance personalized treatment strategies [23, 46], and studies that investigate the administration of Anlotinib in concurrence with immunotherapy or advanced radiotherapy techniques may yield synergistic effects, improving outcomes [47–49]. A possible suggestion for future studies could be an investigation into the dual administration of anti-CD40 monoclonal antibodies and Anlotinib. For illustration, CD40 is a cell-surface receptor with significant upregulation in glioma [50], and it is importantly involved in the tumor expansion, tumor angiogenesis via VEGF [50]. Considering the anti-angiogenic roles of Anlotinib, the possible synergism between anti-CD40 targeting and Anlotinib could help increase the clinical outcomes of glioma patients.

Conclusion

Anlotinib’s efficacy could be considered thoughtworthy in extending survival outcomes and achieving disease control in high-grade glioma patients, with a manageable safety profile. Subgroup analysis results reveal the considerable outcomes of adding Anlotinib to TMZ and radiotherapy in 6 and 12-month OS and PFS, similar to DCR and concurrent Anlotinib administration with SRS being associated with the highest ORR and CR rates. Given the unavoidable limitations, biases, and statistical “fragilty” in this study, future studies are needed to ensure the clinical outcomes of Anlotinib.
