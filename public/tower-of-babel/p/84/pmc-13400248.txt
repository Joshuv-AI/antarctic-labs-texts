Tetrathiomolybdate reduces bone cancer pain and tumor-associated osteoclast activation through copper-dependent LOX signaling.
Authors: Haonan Yu, Xinru Yuan, Xin Li, Jiayu Tong, Chengya Huang, Xingji You, Jingxiang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bone cancer pain (BCP) affects most patients with bone metastases and is poorly managed by current analgesics. This study evaluated tetrathiomolybdate (TTM), a clinical copper chelator, for its potential to simultaneously target osteoclast activation and copper-dependent tumor growth in BCP. Using a mouse model with intrafemoral Lewis lung carcinoma cells, TTM (20–40 mg·kg−1 daily) significantly reduced mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia, even when treatment began after pain establishment. TTM suppressed osteoclast differentiation markers in bone tissue and bone marrow-derived macrophages cultures. It also directly inhibited tumor cell viability in vitro and reduced tumor burden in vivo by 43%. Mechanistically, TTM downregulated lysyl oxidase (LOX) in tumor bone, and exogenous LOX partially reversed the inhibitory effect of TTM on osteoclasts differentiation. These findings suggest that TTM alleviates BCP potentially through inhibition of copper-dependent tumor progression and attenuation of LOX-associated osteoclast activation. Our results support further investigation of copper-dependent signaling pathways as potential modulators of tumor-associated bone destruction and cancer-induced pain.

Introduction

Bone cancer pain (BCP) affects 65–75% of patients with skeletal metastases, involving a “vicious cycle” where metastatic tumor cells disrupt bone homeostasis, leading to excessive osteoclast activation and nociceptor sensitization (Coleman et al., 2020; Mantyh, 2014). Current treatments provide insufficient pain relief in over 40% of patients, as existing therapies target individual components but fail to address the fundamental interconnection between tumor growth and bone destruction (Zajączkowska et al., 2019; Falk and Dickenson, 2014).

Copper dysregulation represents a promising therapeutic target, as elevated copper levels promote tumor progression and correlate with poor prognosis in various malignancies (Denoyer et al., 2015; Blockhuys et al., 2017). Critically, copper-dependent enzymes such as lysyl oxidase (LOX) play dual roles in both tumor microenvironment remodeling (Wang et al., 2022) and osteoclast activation (Cox et al., 2018), suggesting that copper chelation could simultaneously target multiple pathological processes in BCP. Tetrathiomolybdate (TTM), a copper chelator with an established clinical safety in Wilson's disease (Wu et al., 2025), has demonstrated antitumor activity in recent preclinical studies and clinical studies (Ishida et al., 2010; Brewer et al., 2000). By forming stable, non-utilizable complexes in vivo, TTM depletes bioavailable copper, thereby inhibiting copper-dependent enzymes (Maiti et al., 2024). This mechanism confers a highly selective cytotoxicity for tumor cells over normal cells at therapeutic concentrations (Ishida et al., 2010). However, although copper chelation has shown antitumor potential, its role in modulating tumor–bone interactions and pain signaling in BCP remains largely unexplored.

LOX, a copper-dependent enzyme that is highly expressed by tumors (Xing et al., 2025), represents a key mechanistic link explaining how copper chelation could simultaneously target both tumor progression and bone destruction (Cox et al., 2019). In experimental models, LOX overexpression increased metastatic bone destruction by 3-fold through a dual mechanism: direct enhancement of RANKL-dependent osteoclastogenesis and robust IL-6 production that creates an imbalance between bone resorption and formation. LOX also supports tumor cell attachment and survival within the bone matrix, facilitating bone marrow dissemination. This LOX-IL6 axis simultaneously promotes osteoclast activity while inhibiting osteoblast differentiation, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of bone destruction (Reynaud et al., 2017; Di Mauro et al., 2024). Given this pathway, TTM's potential for cancer pain management through LOX inhibition remains a strategically important yet unexplored area.

We hypothesized that TTM may alleviate BCP through inhibition of copper-dependent tumor growth and osteoclast activation, with potential involvement of LOX-associated signaling. In this study, we investigated TTM on pain-like behaviors, tumor burden, osteoclast activation, and bone destruction in a murine BCP model and further explored whether LOX-associated osteoclast regulation contributes to the effects of TTM.

Methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6 mice aged 8–10 weeks and weighing 20–25 g were obtained from Shanghai Bikai Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Mice were housed under standard laboratory conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle, controlled temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 50–60%, and free access to food and water. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Research Council, 2011) and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai Chest Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University approval number: KS(Y)22,070.

Bone cancer pain model

The BCP model was established as previously described with modifications (Zhang et al., 2025). Briefly, mice were anesthetized with 2% sevoflurane and placed in the supine position. A 25-gauge needle was inserted through the patellar ligament into the intramedullary space of the right femur. Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) cells were suspended in PBS and slowly injected into the femoral cavity at a density of 2 × 106 cells in 10 μL PBS. The injection site was then sealed with bone wax, and the wound was sutured. Sham-operated mice underwent the same surgical procedure but received an injection of PBS only.

Drug administration

TTM (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was dissolved in sterile saline and administered intraperitoneally at doses of 20 or 40 mg·kg−1 daily, based on previous studies demonstrating efficacy in cancer models (Ramchandani et al., 2021). Two treatment protocols were employed: early intervention (days 1–21 post-inoculation) and late-stage treatment (days 10–21 post-inoculation). Sham animals received equivalent volumes of saline.

Behavioral assessment

Mechanical allodynia was assessed using calibrated von Frey filaments (Stoelting Co., Wood Dale, IL, USA) as described previously (Peng et al., 2024). Mice were placed in individual Plexiglas chambers on an elevated wire mesh platform and allowed to acclimatize for 30 min. The paw withdrawal threshold (PWT) was determined using the up-down method with a series of von Frey filaments (0.008–1.4 g) applied perpendicularly to the plantar surface of the ipsilateral hindpaw for 3–5 s. For Paw withdrawal latency (PWL) assessment, a single 0.16 g von Frey filament was applied to the same site with sufficient force to cause slight bending. The time from application to a clear withdrawal response (licking, shaking, or rapid lifting of the paw) was recorded with a stopwatch, using a 10 s cut-off time to prevent tissue damage. The PWL test was repeated three times, and the average latency was calculated. For each animal, both PWT and von Frey-evoked PWL measurements were repeated three times, and the average values were used for statistical analysis. Behavioral testing was performed by an investigator blinded to group allocation.

Bone marrow-derived macrophage cell culture and osteoclast differentiation

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMMs) were isolated from femurs and tibias of 6–8-week-old C57BL/6 mice as described (Zhang et al., 2025). Cells were cultured in α-MEM containing 10% foetal bovine serum, 100 U·mL−1 penicillin, 100 μg·mL−1 streptomycin, and 30 ng·mL−1 macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA).

For osteoclast differentiation, BMMs were seeded at 1 × 104 cells per well in 96-well plates and stimulated with M-CSF (30 ng·mL−1) and RANKL (50 ng·mL−1, R&D Systems) for 7 days. In this background, BMMs receive only M-CSF and RANK, defining the positive control group. From day 3 onwards, either TTM (1 μg·mL−1) alone or TTM plus CuCl2 (10 μM) was added, defining the TTM group and the TTM + CuCl2 group, respectively. BMMs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained for tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) activity using a commercial kit (Sigma-Aldrich). TRAP-positive multinucleated cells (≥3 nuclei) were counted as mature osteoclasts.

Cell culture and CCK-8 assay

LLC, RAW264.7, and NIH3T3 cells were cultured under standard conditions in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. All cell lines were confirmed negative for mycoplasma contamination using the PCR-based mycoplasma detection kit (cat# C0301S, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China).

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells per well) and treated with various concentrations of TTM (0–960 μM) for 48 h. After treatment, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated according to the manufacturer's instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

In vivo bioluminescence imaging

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (2–3% in oxygen). At 14 days post-injection of luciferase-transfected LLC tumor cells into the femur, in vivo bioluminescence imaging was performed using an IVIS Spectrum system (PerkinElmer) to monitor tumor growth. D-luciferin substrate (150 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally 10 min prior to imaging. Images were acquired with an exposure time of 1–5 min until signal saturation was avoided. Bioluminescence intensity was quantified using Living Image software by drawing regions of interest (ROIs) around the tumor-bearing femur, and signal intensity was expressed as total photon flux (photons/s).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNA-easy Isolation Reagent (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China) and reverse-transcribed using cDNA synthesis kit (DBI® Bioscience, Ludwigshafen, Germany). Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (DBI® Bioscience, Germany) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System. Relative mRNA levels were calculated using the comparative Ct (∆∆Ct) method, with β-actin as an internal control. H&E and TRAP histological analysis

Femurs were harvested on day 21, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, decalcified in 10% EDTA for 2–3 weeks, and embedded in paraffin. Serial sections (6 μm) were prepared and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or TRAP according to the manufacturer's instructions (Solarbio, America). For histological and immunostaining quantification, images were acquired from comparable anatomical regions of the tumor-bearing femur under identical microscope settings within each experiment. For each sample, three non-consecutive sections and three to five fields per section were analyzed. Regions with tissue damage, folding, or staining artifacts were excluded. Thresholds for positive staining were set using ImageJ and kept constant across all groups within the same staining experiment. TRAP-positive multinucleated cells containing three or more nuclei were counted as osteoclasts. Immunostaining signals were quantified as mean fluorescence intensity or percentage of positive staining area, as indicated in the figure legends.

Copper measurement

Serum and bone marrow copper concentrations were determined using a copper assay kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Bone marrow was flushed from femurs using PBS, and copper content was normalized to protein concentration using the Bradford assay.

Statistical analysis

Sample size was determined based on our previous experience with this bone cancer pain model and on prior studies using similar experimental designs. A formal a priori power calculation was not performed. In accordance with animal welfare considerations, group sizes were selected to use the minimum number of animals necessary while allowing detection of biologically meaningful differences. For in vitro experiments, sample sizes were based on routine practice in our laboratory and on previous studies with similar endpoints. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons, or two-way repeated measures ANOVA for time-course studies. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Both doses significantly attenuated mechanical allodynia compared with the BCP group, with the higher dose demonstrating superior efficacy (Fig. 1B). To simulate clinical late-stage intervention, a separate cohort received TTM treatment starting on day 10 post-inoculation. Remarkably, both doses rapidly reversed established pain behaviors. The 40 mg·kg−1 group showed significant improvement in PWT by day 14 (P < 0.01 vs BCP), with concomitant prolongation of withdrawal latency to 0.16 g von Frey filament stimulation (Fig. 1D-F).

To confirm that analgesic effects were mediated by copper chelation, we measured copper levels in serum and bone marrow. BCP induction significantly elevated serum copper levels compared with sham-operated controls (2.8 ± 0.2 vs 1.9 ± 0.1 μg·mL−1; P < 0.001), consistent with previous reports of copper dysregulation in cancer (Denoyer et al., 2015). TTM treatment (40 mg·kg−1) effectively normalized serum copper concentrations (2.1 ± 0.1 μg·mL−1; P < 0.001 vs BCP; Fig. 1G). Similarly, bone marrow copper content was elevated in BCP mice and restored to control levels following TTM administration (Fig. 1H), confirming effective copper chelation at both systemic and target sites.

TTM produces rapid analgesic effects after a single administration in BCP mice

To determine whether the analgesic effect of TTM was independent of tumor burden reduction, we performed an acute experiment in which TTM was administered once to tumor-bearing mice on day 14 after tumor implantation, when tumors had already formed and pain-related behaviors were established. After a single administration, TTM rapidly attenuated pain-related behaviors, with analgesic effects emerging at 1 h and reaching a peak at 2 h after treatment. Both 20 mg/kg and 40 mg/kg TTM showed analgesic effects, with 40 mg/kg producing a greater effect. This acute analgesic effect was no longer observed at 8 h after administration (Fig. 6 A). Notably, tumor size did not change within this short time window, suggesting that the rapid analgesic effect of TTM occurred before measurable tumor suppression. Discussion

The present study investigated the therapeutic potential of TTM, a clinically approved copper chelator, as a candidate treatment for BCP in a murine model. Our findings suggest a potential therapeutic mechanism by which copper chelation reduces tumor cell viability/metabolic activity and attenuates LOX-dependent osteoclast activation, indicating that TTM-mediated copper chelation may represent a potential multimodal therapeutic avenue for BCP that warrants further investigation (Fig. 7).Fig. 7Schematic diagram of the proposed mechanism by which TTM-mediated copper chelation modulates the tumor–bone–pain interaction in bone cancer pain. Copper homeostasis dysregulation is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of cancer progression, promoting tumor angiogenesis, proliferation, and metastasis through activation of HIF-1α, VEGF, and MEK/ERK pathways (Njenga et al., 2023). Our results demonstrate that BCP induction significantly elevates both serum and bone marrow copper concentrations, which are effectively normalized by TTM treatment. The selective cytotoxicity of TTM towards LLC cells whilst sparing normal macrophages supports its therapeutic potential, with the observed IC₅₀ value (21.11 μM) consistent with previous reports and falling within clinically achievable concentrations (Huang and Chen, 2025; Mao et al., 2025). The reduction in tumor burden achieved with early TTM intervention compares favorably with conventional chemotherapeutic agents (Shi et al., 2025) and suggests potential for combination therapy approaches.

The clinical relevance of our findings is underscored by TTM's efficacy in both early intervention and late-stage treatment protocols. Whilst early treatment achieved superior pain relief, the rapid analgesia observed with late-stage intervention (significant effects within 2–3 days) addresses a critical clinical need, as most patients present with established BCP requiring immediate pain relief (Xie et al., 2025; Morisawa et al., 2018). The dose-dependent effects observed in our preclinical model provide preliminary information for future translational studies, as the 40 mg·kg−1 dose showed greater analgesic efficacy while maintaining an acceptable safety profile in mice. However, these findings should not be interpreted as a direct basis for clinical dose selection. Although TTM doses up to 120 mg/day have been reported to be tolerated in Wilson's disease (Roberts and Schilsky, 2008), these data should be considered only as background safety information and cannot be directly extrapolated to cancer pain patients.

Beyond its anti-tumor effects, our study reveals that TTM may alleviate BCP, at least in part, by suppressing LOX-mediated osteoclast activation. LOX is a copper-dependent amine oxidase involved in collagen cross-linking, extracellular matrix remodeling, tumor progression, and bone metastasis (Löser et al., 2023). Previous studies have shown that LOX promotes osteoclast differentiation through enhanced RANKL expression and direct effects on osteoclast precursors (Morisawa et al., 2018). Previous studies have shown that hypoxia-induced tumor-derived LOX can generate premetastatic bone destruction by promoting osteoclast-mediated bone resorption (Reynaud et al., 2017). Moreover, LOX, but not LOXL2, has been reported to promote bone metastasis formation and bone destruction in triple-negative breast cancer, highlighting member-specific functions within the LOX family (Di Mauro et al., 2024). These findings support the relevance of LOX-mediated osteoclast activation in tumor-associated bone destruction. Consistently, our rescue experiments showed that LOX supplementation reversed the anti-osteoclastic effects of TTM, supporting the involvement of the LOX–osteoclast axis in TTM-mediated protection against bone destruction and BCP. Nevertheless, because TTM acts as a copper chelator, its biological effects may extend beyond LOX inhibition alone. Other LOX family members and copper-dependent pathways related to extracellular matrix remodeling, oxidative stress, angiogenesis, inflammation, tumor metabolism, and nociceptive sensitization may also contribute to the protective and analgesic effects of TTM. Thus, the copper–LOX–osteoclast axis identified in this study should be viewed as an important contributing mechanism rather than the sole mechanism underlying the effects of TTM.

The dual inhibition of tumor growth and osteoclast activation addresses the fundamental “vicious cycle” that perpetuates BCP, wherein tumor-induced bone destruction releases growth factors that further promote tumor proliferation and pain sensitization (Aielli et al., 2019; Sato et al., 2023). This multimodal mechanism may explain TTM's superior efficacy compared with single target approaches and positions copper chelation as a rational therapeutic strategy. The copper-dependent nature of both tumor proliferation and LOX activity suggests that this therapy could be particularly effective in cancers with high copper dependency, such as lung, breast, and prostate cancers, the primary sources of bone metastases (Alzibdeh et al., 2025). This specificity may also reduce off-target effects compared with broad-spectrum chemotherapeutic agents.

TTM's established clinical safety profile in Wilson's disease provides a significant advantage for rapid clinical translation. However, long-term copper depletion risks, including potential effects on hematopoiesis and neurological function (Takahashi, 2022; Kuang et al., 2025), warrant careful monitoring in clinical trials. The identification of serum and bone marrow copper levels as potential biomarkers could facilitate personalized dosing strategies and optimize therapeutic outcomes while minimizing adverse effects.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the use of a single tumor cell line (LLC) may limit the generalizability of our findings to other bone-metastatic cancers. Second, the 21-day treatment period does not allow evaluation of the long-term efficacy and safety of TTM. Third, although our data suggest that copper-dependent LOX signaling contributes to osteoclast activation, tumor-associated bone destruction, and BCP progression, the precise downstream mechanisms remain to be clarified. In particular, the present study does not directly establish a causal link between LOX modulation and nociceptive signaling. Although acute TTM treatment reduced mechanical allodynia and spinal c-Fos expression, these findings cannot determine whether the analgesic effect is mediated by direct regulation of nociceptive neurons or indirectly through changes in osteoclast activation, bone destruction, and the tumor-associated bone microenvironment. Further studies using additional tumor models, longer treatment periods, and LOX-specific mechanistic approaches combined with neuronal activity analyses are needed to better define the therapeutic potential and analgesic mechanisms of TTM in BCP.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study suggests that TTM alleviates pain-related behaviors in a murine BCP model, at least in part by reducing copper availability, suppressing tumor growth, and inhibiting LOX-associated osteoclast activation. These findings identify copper metabolism as a potential link among tumor progression, osteoclast-mediated bone destruction, and pain sensitization. However, further studies using additional tumor models, female mice, LOX-specific interventions, positive therapeutic controls, and long-term safety assessments are needed to validate the therapeutic potential of copper chelation for BCP.
