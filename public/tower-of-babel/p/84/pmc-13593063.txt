Enarodustat combined with oral iron supplementation for iron-deficient non-dialysis-dependent chronic kidney disease anemia patients: Synergistic effects and safety
Authors: Ying Lu, Ganru Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to evaluate the synergistic effect and safety of enarodustat combined with oral iron supplementation in the treatment of iron-deficient non-dialysis-dependent chronic kidney disease (NDD-CKD) patients with anemia. This single-center retrospective cohort study enrolled 120 iron-deficient NDD-CKD patients with anemia who had not received erythropoiesis-stimulating agents or intravenous iron within the preceding month. According to the treatment protocols administered, the participants were divided into a combination therapy group (n = 64) and a monotherapy group (n = 56). The combination therapy group (n = 64) received enarodustat combined with ferrous succinate sustained-release tablets, while the monotherapy group (n = 56) received ferrous succinate sustained-release tablets alone. Patients were followed for 8 weeks to evaluate hemoglobin (Hb) levels, iron metabolism parameters, erythropoietic parameters, and adverse events. After 8 weeks of treatment, Hb levels in the combination therapy group were significantly higher than those in the monotherapy group (P < .05). The achievement rate of Hb ≥ 100 g/L was 82.81% in the combination group versus 57.14% in the monotherapy group (P < .01); the achievement rate of Hb ≥ 110 g/L was 62.50% and 44.64%, respectively (P < .05). Serum ferritin, transferrin saturation, and serum iron in the combination therapy group were all significantly higher than those in the monotherapy group (all P < .001). The increase in reticulocyte count and hematocrit was more pronounced in the combination group (P < .001), while red blood cell count showed a numerical increase without reaching statistical significance. There was no significant difference in the overall incidence of adverse events between the 2 groups (P > .05), and no serious adverse events occurred in either group. This study indicates that enarodustat combined with oral iron supplementation may have a certain synergistic effect on iron-deficient NDD-CKD anemia compared with oral iron alone, potentially helping to improve hematological indicators and promote erythropoiesis, with an acceptable safety profile. This combined regimen may provide a preliminary reference for the management of NDD-CKD anemia.

1. Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) has become a global public health problem, with anemia being one of the most common and serious complications of CKD.[1] The prevalence of anemia in non-dialysis-dependent CKD (NDD-CKD) patients increases significantly with declining renal function, affecting approximately 25% of patients with stage 3-5 CKD.[2] The pathophysiological mechanisms of NDD-CKD anemia primarily include insufficient erythropoietin (EPO) production, iron metabolism disorders, and chronic inflammatory states.[3] Iron deficiency is an important contributing factor to NDD-CKD anemia, including both absolute and functional iron deficiency.[4] Elevated hepcidin levels in CKD patients inhibit intestinal iron absorption and iron release from macrophages, contributing to functional iron deficiency.[5,6] This limitation underscores the need to explore combination strategies involving agents that can enhance iron utilization by targeting hepcidin.

Oral iron supplementation is the first-line treatment for iron deficiency in NDD-CKD patients. However, intestinal iron absorption disorders caused by elevated hepcidin in CKD patients remain the main limiting factor for oral iron efficacy.[7] Enarodustat is a novel hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor (HIF-PHI) that stimulates endogenous EPO production by stabilizing hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF).[8] Unlike traditional erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs), enarodustat can mimic physiological oxygen-sensing mechanisms and, in addition to increasing EPO production, can also regulate iron metabolism-related gene expression, including downregulating hepcidin and upregulating iron transporters, thereby improving iron utilization efficiency.[9] Currently, enarodustat has been approved in Japan for treating CKD-related anemia and is under clinical development in the United States and South Korea.[10] Clinical trials have demonstrated that enarodustat can effectively correct and maintain hemoglobin (Hb) levels in NDD-CKD patients with acceptable safety.[11] However, studies evaluating the combination of enarodustat with oral iron supplementation in the Chinese population remain limited.

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of enarodustat combined with oral iron supplementation in treating iron-deficient NDD-CKD anemia patients. By comparing the combined therapy with iron supplementation alone, this study sought to explore whether this combination could improve Hb levels and optimize iron metabolism parameters, with the expectation of providing a clinical reference for the management of NDD-CKD anemia.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design

This study was a single-center, retrospective observational cohort study designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of enarodustat combined with oral iron supplementation in treating iron-deficient NDD-CKD anemia patients. Treatment allocation was nonrandomized and based on clinical practice; no blinding was applied. The study was conducted in the Department of Nephrology at the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (a tertiary care hospital) from April 2024 to May 2025.

2.2. Study subjects

According to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2024 Clinical Practice Guideline for Anemia in Chronic Kidney Disease and the Chinese Clinical Practice Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of Renal Anemia (2021 Edition),[12,13] this study included patients aged 18 to 80 years with confirmed NDD-CKD and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 15 to 59 mL/min/1.73 m2. All patients were diagnosed with iron-deficiency anemia, defined as Hb < 100 g/L, while meeting at least 1 iron-deficiency indicator: serum ferritin (SF) ≤ 100 ng/mL or transferrin saturation (TSAT) ≤ 20%.

This study excluded the following patients: those with acute kidney injury or rapid renal function deterioration (eGFR decline > 30% within 3 months), history of malignancy or active malignancy, active bleeding, or significant bleeding history within the past 3 months; patients with severe cardiovascular disease (New York Heart Association heart function class III-IV, unstable angina, myocardial infarction or stroke within the past 6 months), pregnant or lactating women, and severe liver dysfunction (alanine aminotransferase or aspartate aminotransferase >3 times the upper limit of normal); and patients allergic to study drug components, use of ESAs or intravenous iron within the past month, expected survival <6 months, poor compliance, unable to take medication or follow-up as prescribed (defined as documented treatment discontinuation, failure to complete scheduled follow-up visits, or physician-recorded poor medication adherence in medical records).

As this was a retrospective cohort study, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed; all eligible patients meeting the inclusion criteria during the study period were included in the analysis. During the study period, a total of 186 patients with NDD-CKD and anemia were reviewed for eligibility. Of these, 66 patients were excluded for the following reasons: recent use of ESAs or intravenous iron within the past month (n = 18), severe cardiovascular disease (n = 10), acute kidney injury or rapid renal function deterioration (n = 8), poor compliance or loss to follow-up (n = 8), active malignancy or history of malignancy (n = 6), severe liver dysfunction (n = 5), active bleeding or significant bleeding history within 3 months (n = 4), expected survival <6 months (n = 3), pregnancy or lactation (n = 2), and known allergy to study drug components (n = 2). Finally, 120 patients met all inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis.

2.3. Grouping and treatment methods

Retrospective grouping was performed based on the treatment regimen patients received. The combination therapy group (n = 64) received enarodustat combined with ferrous succinate sustained-release tablets, where enarodustat starting dose was 2 mg once daily after meals, with the dose adjusted according to Hb levels (2–8 mg/d), targeting an Hb range of 100 to 120 g/L; ferrous succinate sustained-release tablets 200 mg elemental iron once daily between meals on an empty stomach. The monotherapy group (n = 56) received only ferrous succinate sustained-release tablets 200 mg elemental iron once daily between meals on an empty stomach. Both groups received medication for 8 weeks and standard CKD management, including blood pressure control, glucose management, and nutritional guidance.

2.4. Observation indicators and detection methods

The primary efficacy endpoints of this study included Hb levels, Hb target achievement rates, and iron metabolism indicators. Hb was measured using an automated hematology analyzer (Sysmex XN-3000; Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Hyogo Prefecture, Japan), with normal ranges of 120 to 160 g/L for males and 110 to 150 g/L for females. Hb target achievement rate was defined as the proportion of patients with Hb ≥ 100 g/L and Hb ≥ 110 g/L. (The KDIGO 2024 guideline recommends a target range of 100 to 115 g/L, while the Chinese Guideline for the Diagnosis and Treatment of CKD Anemia [2021] recommends a target range of 110 to 120 g/L.)[12,13] Iron metabolism indicators included SF, measured by chemiluminescent immunoassay with a normal range of 15 to 150 ng/mL; TSAT, calculated as (serum iron × 100)/(total iron binding capacity × 1.25) with a normal range of 20% to 50%; and serum iron, measured by colorimetric method with a normal range of 9 to 30 μmol/L.

Secondary efficacy endpoints included reticulocyte count (detected by flow cytometry, normal range: 0.5%–1.5%), red blood cell count (normal range: 4.0–5.5 × 1012/L for males, 3.5–5.0 × 1012/L for females), and hematocrit (normal range: 40%–50% for males, 35%–45% for females), both measured by automated hematology analyzer. Safety indicators included adverse reaction records (all adverse events occurring during treatment classified by system), renal function indicators (serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, eGFR calculated using CKD-EPI formula), liver function indicators (alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase), and cardiovascular indicators (blood pressure and heart rate monitoring). Baseline characteristic data included the collection of patient age, gender, body mass index, underlying diseases (diabetic nephropathy, hypertensive nephropathy, chronic nephritis, etc), and medication history.

Follow-up time points were set at baseline (T0, before treatment), 4 weeks of treatment (T4), and 8 weeks of treatment (T8). Each follow-up included hematological examination, iron metabolism indicator detection, and safety assessment. All laboratory tests were completed in the same laboratory using standardized operating procedures. Data entry used a double-checking system, with regular equipment calibration and quality control sample testing. Adverse reaction monitoring used standardized assessment forms.

Treatment adherence was retrospectively assessed based on medical records. Adherence was evaluated by reviewing prescription refill records and documenting patient self-reports during follow-up visits. Patients who completed all scheduled follow-up visits and had continuous prescription records throughout the 8-week study period were considered adherent. Patients with incomplete follow-up or documented treatment discontinuation were classified as nonadherent.

2.5. Statistical methods

SPSS 26.0 statistical software (IBM Corporation, Armonk) was used for data analysis, and GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego) was used for creating charts. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess data normality. Normally distributed quantitative data were expressed as “mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s),” with between-group comparisons using an independent-samples t test and within-group pre- and posttreatment comparisons using a paired t test. Categorical data were expressed as “number (percentage) (n [%])” and compared using the χ2 test or the Fisher exact test. For the primary outcomes, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for between-group differences to provide estimates of effect size and precision. P < .05 indicates statistically significant differences.

3.2. Comparison of Hb levels between the 2 groups

Results showed that combination therapy was associated with greater improvements in anemia. At baseline, there was no significant difference in Hb levels between the combination therapy group and monotherapy group (90.37 ± 6.22 vs 89.59 ± 7.36 g/L, P = .533). After 4 weeks of treatment, Hb levels were significantly higher in the combination therapy group (99.14 ± 7.50 vs 95.40 ± 6.95 g/L; mean difference: 3.74 g/L, 95% CI: 1.15–6.33; P = .006). This difference persisted at 8 weeks (107.98 ± 8.96 vs 103.54 ± 10.07 g/L; mean difference: 4.45 g/L, 95% CI: 1.01–7.88; P = .012). The achievement rate of Hb ≥ 100 g/L at 8 weeks was significantly higher in the combination therapy group than in the monotherapy group (82.81% vs 57.14%; difference: 25.67%, 95% CI: 9.75%–41.59%; P = .002). The achievement rate of Hb ≥ 110 g/L was also higher in the combination therapy group (62.50% vs 44.64%; difference: 17.86%, 95% CI: 0.24%–35.47%; P = .049) (Fig. 1).

3.3. Comparison of iron metabolism parameters between the 2 groups

Iron metabolism parameters showed that the combination therapy group had significant advantages in improving iron utilization. After 8 weeks of treatment, both combination therapy and monotherapy groups showed significant increases in SF, TSAT, and serum iron compared with baseline (P < .001). Compared with the monotherapy group, after 8 weeks of treatment, the combination therapy group had significantly higher SF, TSAT, and serum iron than the monotherapy group (P < .001). These results suggest that enarodustat may enhance iron absorption and utilization (Table 2).

3.5. Comparison of adverse reactions between the 2 groups

The incidence of adverse reactions was comparable between the 2 groups. The combination therapy group had a total adverse reaction incidence of 20.31%, while the monotherapy group had 16.07%, with no statistically significant difference (P > .05). The main adverse reactions were gastrointestinal reactions, with incidences of 10.94% in the combination therapy group and 7.14% in the monotherapy group. Nervous system adverse reaction incidences were 6.25% and 5.36%, respectively. Cardiovascular system adverse reactions were 3.13% and 3.57% in both groups, respectively. No serious adverse events occurred in either group, indicating that enarodustat combined with oral iron supplementation has good safety and tolerability (Table 4).

4. Discussion

This study found that enarodustat combined with oral iron supplementation improved Hb achievement rates at 8 weeks. The combination therapy group achieved higher Hb target rates compared with the monotherapy group, reaching the KDIGO-recommended threshold of 100 g/L in a substantially greater proportion of patients.[12] Rapid and effective anemia correction not only improves patients’ quality of life but may also slow CKD progression and reduce cardiovascular event risk.[14,15] More importantly, the observed difference between groups appears to extend beyond a simple additive effect.

This finding is consistent with the research results of Akizawa et al, which showed that HIF-PHI class drugs can synergistically improve CKD anemia through multiple mechanisms.[11] The mechanism of enarodustat and oral iron interaction is manifested at multiple levels. Enarodustat, as an HIF-PHI, stabilizes HIF-α subunits and not only upregulates EPO transcriptional expression but also simultaneously regulates the expression of iron metabolism-related genes.[16] Studies have shown that activation of the HIF signaling pathway can downregulate hepcidin expression while upregulating divalent metal transporter 1 and ferroportin expression, thereby improving intestinal iron absorption and cellular iron release.[17] The interplay between erythroferrone (ERFE) and hepcidin has been characterized in iron-deficient patients, demonstrating that ERFE suppresses hepcidin to promote iron mobilization and absorption, which provides a mechanistic context for understanding the iron metabolism improvements observed in combination therapy.[18] The improvement in iron metabolism parameters in this study is consistent with this mechanism. These results suggested that enarodustat may help address functional iron deficiency caused by elevated hepcidin due to inflammatory states in CKD patients.[19] The rapid increase in reticulocyte count further supports the existence of interactive mechanisms. The enhanced reticulocyte response observed in the combination therapy group suggested that bone marrow hematopoietic function received more potent stimulation. This enhanced early hematopoietic response may be related to the combined effect of adequate iron supply and increased endogenous EPO production.[20]

This study’s results are consistent with recent clinical trial results of HIF-PHI class drugs. Chen et al found in a large study including 3872 NDD-CKD patients that the Hb response rate of roxadustat combined with iron therapy was significantly higher than iron therapy alone (71.2% vs 51.8%).[21] A network meta-analysis comparing different HIF-PHIs demonstrated that these agents effectively reduce hepcidin levels while improving iron utilization parameters in NDD-CKD patients.[22] Furthermore, real-world evidence from postmarketing surveillance studies has confirmed the effectiveness and safety profile of HIF-PHIs in routine clinical practice, with sustained Hb improvements observed across diverse patient populations.[23] Moreover, the combination therapy achievement rate observed was numerically higher than previously reported rates with HIF-PHI monotherapy, suggesting potential additional benefit from concurrent iron supplementation.[11] Additionally, the improvement in iron metabolism parameters observed in this study was better than previous studies using oral iron alone.[11] Notably, compared with intravenous iron, the treatment strategy combining oral iron with HIF-PHI may have better compliance and tolerability.[24]

Safety analysis showed that the combination therapy regimen has good tolerability. The total adverse reaction incidence was comparable between the 2 groups, with no serious adverse events occurring. The main adverse reactions were gastrointestinal reactions, consistent with known side effects of oral iron.[25] Importantly, the combination therapy group did not increase the incidence of adverse reactions, indicating acceptable drug interaction safety between enarodustat and oral iron. The incidence of nervous system and cardiovascular system adverse reactions was low in both groups with no notable differences, consistent with enarodustat’s safety characteristics. Compared with traditional ESAs, HIF-PHI class drugs show potentially favorable cardiovascular safety due to their physiological mechanism of action.[26,27]

Although this study did not directly assess patient-reported outcomes, the observed Hb improvements are clinically relevant. Anemia in CKD patients is associated with fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, and impaired well-being.[28] A recent consensus document from the European Anaemia of CKD Alliance emphasized that anemia has a substantial impact on quality of life and work productivity, and that appropriate anemia management should be recognized as a key component of CKD care.[29] Future studies evaluating combination therapy should incorporate validated instruments such as the Kidney Disease Quality of Life questionnaire to assess patient-centered benefits.

These findings should be interpreted within the context of current anemia management approaches. Rather than positioning combination therapy as universally superior, our results suggest that adding enarodustat to oral iron may be considered as a step-up strategy for patients with suboptimal response to iron supplementation alone. A stepwise approach, initiating treatment with oral iron and escalating to combination therapy based on individual response, may optimize outcomes while ensuring appropriate resource utilization. This individualized strategy aligns with current guideline recommendations for anemia management in NDD-CKD. The enarodustat combined with oral iron treatment strategy may have potential clinical applications in NDD-CKD anemia management. This combination therapy regimen was associated with improvements in hematological parameters and iron utilization, providing clinicians with a treatment option that balances efficacy and safety. As enarodustat’s clinical development continues in South Korea and China, and with expected global development, this combination regimen may potentially offer an additional option for NDD-CKD anemia clinical management.[10] However, standardized treatment protocols still need to be developed for clinical application, including drug dosages, monitoring frequency, and discontinuation criteria, to ensure treatment safety and efficacy. The recently updated UK Kidney Association clinical practice guideline on anemia of CKD may provide further guidance for HIF-PHI application in clinical practice, potentially promoting broader adoption of this treatment modality.[30]

This study has several limitations that need consideration. The sample size is relatively small, and the observation period is only 8 weeks, which limits the assessment of long-term efficacy and safety. Serum hepcidin and ERFE levels, which are key mediators in the proposed mechanism of action, were not measured in this study. While the improvements in iron metabolism parameters observed in the combination therapy group are consistent with hepcidin suppression, direct measurement would provide more definitive mechanistic evidence.[31] As treatment allocation was based on clinical practice rather than randomization, selection bias was unavoidable. Physicians may have preferentially prescribed combination therapy to patients with more severe anemia or those with prior inadequate response to iron supplementation, which could influence the observed treatment effects. The retrospective observational design also meant that potential confounding factors such as differences in inflammatory status, comorbidities, and other unmeasured variables could not be fully controlled, despite comparable baseline characteristics between the 2 groups. Data on dietary iron intake and nutritional supplements were not collected, which may have influenced treatment response. Outcome assessors were not blinded to treatment allocation, which may introduce observer bias. From a statistical standpoint, the use of independent comparisons at each timepoint rather than repeated measures analysis may not fully account for within-subject correlations over time. Future large-sample, multicenter, randomized controlled trials with hepcidin measurements are needed to validate these findings and explore the underlying mechanisms. Additionally, emerging drug delivery technologies such as nanocarriers may offer potential advantages for targeted delivery of iron supplements, which warrants exploration in future studies.[32,33] Meanwhile, optimal combination regimens of different doses of enarodustat with different types of oral iron still need exploration.[24]

5. Conclusion

In this retrospective, single-center cohort study, enarodustat combined with oral iron supplementation was associated with greater improvements in Hb levels, higher Hb target achievement rates, more favorable iron metabolism parameters, and enhanced erythropoietic response compared with oral iron alone. No serious adverse events were observed. These preliminary findings suggest that the combination regimen may offer potential benefits for iron-deficient NDD-CKD anemia management.
