Comparative efficacy and safety of anticoagulation strategies in continuous renal replacement therapy: a real-world cohort study
Authors: Qing Ou, Dan He, Wenjuan Yan, Xiuying Shi, LengZhen Zhang, Li Tang, Yucheng Li, Zhiyong Fan, Yanan Liu, Ranghui Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe optimal anticoagulation strategy for continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) remains controversial. Real-world comparisons of contemporary agents—regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA), nafamostat mesylate (NM), and unfractionated heparin (UH)—are limited.MethodsIn this single-center retrospective cohort study, we analyzed 420 CRRT sessions (from 197 unique patients) conducted in four intensive care unit (ICU) wards at an academic medical center in China between September 2025 and November 2025. The primary efficacy outcome was filter lifespan, with Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST) analysis adopted as the primary method (due to violation of the proportional hazards assumption). Propensity score matching (PSM) and mixed-effects Cox models were used as sensitivity analyses, adjusting for patient, treatment, ward, and machine factors. Multiplicity adjustment (Holm-Bonferroni) was applied for pairwise comparisons.ResultsAmong 420 sessions, RCA was the most frequently used approach (59.0%), followed by NM (21.2%), UH (12.6%), and no anticoagulation (4.0%). Median filter lifespan was longest with RCA (65.5 h, IQR 48.0–72.0) compared to NM (32.0 h, IQR 15.5–52.5), UH (40.0 h, IQR 26.0–72.0), and no anticoagulation (12.5 h, IQR 6.0-19.5; P < 0.001). RMST analysis confirmed that RCA was associated with significantly longer mean filter survival time compared to NM (difference 18.2 h, 95% CI 10.5–25.9; adjusted P < 0.001) and UH (difference 14.6 h, 95% CI 5.8–23.4; adjusted P = 0.046). The NM vs. UH comparison showed no significant difference (difference − 3.6 h, 95% CI -12.1 to 4.9; adjusted P = 0.52). After PSM, results remained consistent. Major bleeding events were rare (n = 9, 2.1%), but the small number of events precludes meaningful between-group safety comparison. However, 20.9% of RCA sessions failed to achieve the protocol-specified post-filter ionized calcium target, reflecting a metabolic calcium monitoring challenge unique to citrate anticoagulation. Twenty-eight-day mortality (patient-level analysis, n = 197) did not differ significantly across groups after adjustment (overall 36.5%, 72/197).ConclusionIn this retrospective data analysis of real-world practice, RCA provided superior filter lifespan compared to NM and UH for CRRT, while NM showed intermediate efficacy. Although major bleeding events were uncommon, the small number of events precludes definitive safety conclusions. Vigilant monitoring remains necessary regardless of the agent employed. The choice of anticoagulation should balance filter efficacy, bleeding risk, metabolic monitoring capabilities, and institutional resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12882-026-05197-1.Keywords: Continuous renal replacement therapy; Anticoagulation, citrate; Nafamostat mesylate; Heparin; Filter clotting; Bleeding complications; Real-world evidence

Introduction

Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) is a cornerstone in the management of critically ill patients with acute kidney injury (AKI) and fluid overload [1]. Effective anticoagulation of the extracorporeal circuit is essential to maintain filter patency, ensure treatment efficacy, and minimize blood loss and resource utilization [2]. However, anticoagulation in this population is particularly challenging due to the frequent presence of coagulopathies, thrombocytopenia, and elevated bleeding risk [3, 4].

An optimal anticoagulant regimen for CRRT should maximize filter lifespan while minimizing bleeding and metabolic complications. Over the past few years, regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA) has emerged as a preferred approach in many centers due to its regional anticoagulant effect and favorable bleeding profile, as supported by randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses [5, 6]. Unfractionated heparin (UH) remains widely used globally despite its association with higher bleeding risk and the inherent challenges of managing systemic anticoagulation in critically ill patients [7]. Nafamostat mesylate (NM), a short-acting serine protease inhibitor, is extensively employed in Asia for its perceived safety advantages, particularly in patients with bleeding tendencies [8]. Argatroban is an alternative for patients with heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, and the selection of an anticoagulation strategy is a key component of CRRT care quality indicators [9].

Although several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses have compared these strategies [10–13], their relative performance in routine clinical practice, characterized by patient heterogeneity, variable monitoring, and protocol adaptations, remains incompletely understood. In this context, real-world data (RWD) are particularly valuable for complementing RCT findings by reflecting actual clinical practice patterns and outcomes, especially in complex, critically ill populations where RCT enrollment may be limited [14]. Previous real-world studies of CRRT anticoagulation have been limited by single-center designs [15], limited detail on protocol implementation and monitoring adherence [16], or insufficient granularity on specific causes of filter failure.

To address these gaps, we conducted a retrospective analysis of comprehensive clinical data from over 400 CRRT sessions conducted at a single academic medical center. Our primary objectives were to: (1) compare filter lifespan across different anticoagulation strategies; (2) evaluate the incidence of bleeding and failure to achieve therapeutic anticoagulation targets; and (3) identify factors associated with premature filter clotting. We hypothesized that RCA would be associated with superior filter longevity and a reduced risk of hemorrhagic complications, albeit potentially accompanied by distinct metabolic (calcium) monitoring demands.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study used de-identified clinical data from consecutive patients who underwent CRRT between September 2025 and November 2025 in four intensive care unit (ICU) wards at a single academic medical center in China. Our department is one of the largest single-site ICUs in South China, with 78 beds capable of delivering CRRT and 18 CRRT machines. The four wards operate under the leadership of the same departmental director. All four wards followed the same institutional CRRT anticoagulation protocol. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (Protocol NFEC-2026-265), with a waiver of informed consent due to the study’s retrospective nature.

Participants

We included all adult patients (≥ 18 years) who underwent CRRT for more than 4 h during the study period. Indications for CRRT included: (1) AKI stage 2 or 3 by KDIGO criteria; (2) severe fluid overload refractory to diuretics; and (3) severe metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.2) or hyperkalemia (K+ > 6.5 mmol/L) not responsive to medical therapy. Acute-on-chronic renal failure was included if the acute component independently met KDIGO AKI criteria as assessed by a critical care nephrologist at the time of CRRT initiation. Exclusion criteria were: (1) incomplete documentation of anticoagulation strategy; (2) missing key outcome data (filter cessation time or reason); or (3) treatment with experimental anticoagulants.

Handling of multiple sessions per patient: Because a single patient could undergo multiple CRRT sessions during the study period (each with independent circuit initiation and anticoagulation assignment), the session was used as the primary unit of analysis. The 420 eligible sessions were performed in 197 unique patients (median 2 sessions per patient, range 1–6). To account for within-patient correlation, we performed a sensitivity analysis using a mixed-effects Cox model with patient-level random effects (Supplementary Table S5). The distribution of sessions per patient is provided in Supplementary Table S8.

Data collection

Trained research nurses extracted data from electronic medical records and standardized CRRT flow sheets using a predefined electronic case report form. Data quality was assessed through random audits performed on 10% of included cases.

Variables and definitions

Exposure: anticoagulation strategy

Based on the primary anticoagulant documented at the time of CRRT initiation, treatment strategies were categorized as: (1) RCA, (2) Nafamostat mesylate (NM), (3) Unfractionated heparin (UH), (4) Argatroban, (5) Combination therapy (concurrent use of ≥ 2 anticoagulants), (6) No anticoagulation.

Prescribing patterns: Anticoagulation selection was based on a combination of clinician preference (attending intensivist’s usual practice), patient bleeding risk (RCA or NM preferred for high-risk patients; UH reserved for lower-risk patients), and contraindications (e.g., severe liver failure or hypocalcemia precluded RCA). This non-random assignment is acknowledged as a limitation (channeling bias).

Anticoagulation protocols

The institutional protocols for each anticoagulation strategy were as follows: For RCA, a citrate solution (4% trisodium citrate) was infused pre-filter at an initial rate of 1.5-2.0 mmol/L of blood flow, targeting a post-filter ionized calcium (iCa2+) of 0.25–0.35 mmol/L. Calcium gluconate was infused centrally to maintain systemic iCa2+ ≥ 1.00 mmol/L. For UH, an initial bolus of 1000–2000 IU was administered, followed by a continuous infusion of 5–10 IU/kg/h, titrated to achieve an activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) of 40–60 s. For NM, a continuous pre-filter infusion of 20–40 mg/h was administered to target an activated clotting time (ACT) of 140–180 s. These targets were used as the basis for defining “therapeutic range” in the monitoring analysis [17–19].

Outcomes

Primary Efficacy Outcome: Filter lifespan, defined as duration from circuit initiation to cessation attributed to documented filter clotting (including indications such as “filter clotting”, “venous chamber clotting”, or corresponding codes) or planned termination for non-clotting reasons (e.g., circuit expiry, goal achievement). Circuits discontinued due to machine failure, patient death, or other non-clotting reasons were censored at the time of discontinuation. Filter lifespan was chosen as the primary efficacy outcome because it is the most direct, session-level measure of anticoagulation efficacy for circuit patency—a key operational and clinical outcome in CRRT. Mortality was not selected as the primary endpoint because it is influenced by numerous factors beyond the anticoagulation strategy for a single circuit, and many patients underwent multiple sessions with different anticoagulants, making attribution problematic.

Primary Safety Outcomes: (1) Major bleeding: defined according to the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis (ISTH) criteria for non-surgical patients [20]: fatal hemorrhage; symptomatic bleeding in a critical anatomical site or organ; or bleeding associated with a decline in hemoglobin level of ≥ 20 g/L, or necessitating transfusion of ≥ 2 units of packed red blood cells within 24 h. Standardized bleeding assessment is critical, as discrepancies in event identification have been documented across different anticoagulation settings [21]. (2) Failure to achieve therapeutic anticoagulation target: For RCA, failure to maintain post-filter ionized calcium within the protocol-specified therapeutic range (0.25–0.35 mmol/L), as documented in monitoring records. For NM/UH, failure to maintain ACT/aPTT within the institutional therapeutic range (ACT 140–180 s, aPTT 40–60 s).

Secondary Outcomes: (1) Incidence of any documented filter clotting; (2) Proportion of sessions with anticoagulation monitoring performed; (3) Proportion of monitored sessions achieving therapeutic range; (4) 28-day all-cause mortality (patient-level analysis, n = 197 unique patients).

Covariates

The following patient demographics and clinical characteristics were included:

Patient factors: Age, sex, past medical history (atrial fibrillation, venous thromboembolism, valvular heart disease, liver failure, pre-admission anticoagulant/antiplatelet use, concurrent malignancy), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes separately), mechanical ventilation, agitation/delirium, high platelets, high hematocrit, indication for CRRT.

Treatment factors: CRRT mode, dilution type, blood flow rate, total duration of CRRT during admission (patient-level).

Machine factors: Brand (Fresenius, Gambro, Others) with specific filter cartridges (Fresenius AV600S, Gambro ST100) [22, 23], ward (four ICU wards, adjusted in sensitivity analyses).

Process measures: Electronic prescription use, anticoagulation monitoring performed.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as median (IQR) and mean ± SD, and compared across groups using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables were presented as counts (%) and compared using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test.

Filter lifespan analysis: Because preliminary examination of Kaplan-Meier curves suggested crossing of survival curves (potential violation of proportional hazards assumption), we formally tested the proportional hazards (PH) assumption using Schoenfeld residuals. Given evidence of PH violation (global test P = 0.04), we adopted Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST) analysis as the primary method for comparing filter lifespan, which does not require the PH assumption [24]. RMST estimates the mean survival time up to a predefined time horizon (72 h, corresponding to the maximum protocol-specified filter change interval in this institution, beyond which circuit replacement was routinely scheduled per ward protocol). Pairwise comparisons were adjusted for multiple testing using the Holm-Bonferroni method [25]. To account for within-patient correlation (multiple sessions per patient), shared frailty models (gamma frailty with patient-level random effects) were used as the primary method, with cluster-robust (sandwich) variance estimation as a complementary approach.

Propensity score matching (PSM): To address potential selection bias, we performed 1:1 propensity score matching without replacement (caliper = 0.2) for the three primary pairwise comparisons (RCA vs. NM, RCA vs. UH, NM vs. UH). Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression, including all baseline covariates listed in Table 1 (age, sex, mechanical ventilation, CRRT mode, blood flow rate, machine type, pre-admission anticoagulation, comorbidities, malignancy, CRRT indication). Balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (< 0.1 considered balanced). Results after PSM are presented in Table 2 and Supplementary Table S1.

Multivariable modeling: Cox proportional hazards models with cluster-robust standard errors (sandwich estimator with patient-level clustering) were used to estimate hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Shared frailty models (gamma) with patient-level random effects served as the primary analysis. Time-segmentation (0–24 h, 24–48 h, > 48 h) Cox models were used as sensitivity analyses [26].

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses: Pre-specified subgroup analyses were conducted according to CRRT mode (CVVH vs. CVVHDF/CVVHD) and mechanical ventilation status. Sensitivity analyses included: (1) redefining the endpoint as “any unplanned circuit cessation”; (2) multiple imputation for missing covariate data (affecting < 5% of cases); (3) mixed-effects Cox model with patient random effects; (4) exclusion of patients with liver failure; (5) inclusion of ward as a covariate; (6) additional adjustment for pre-session coagulation parameters (INR, aPTT, platelet count) measured within 6 h before CRRT initiation, available for 368 sessions (87.6%).

Effect size reporting

As recommended for observational studies, all treatment comparisons are reported with effect sizes (RMST differences or hazard ratios) and 95% confidence intervals. Holm-Bonferroni adjustment was applied for pairwise comparisons. Post-hoc power calculations were not performed, as they are inherently circular and provide no additional information beyond the observed confidence intervals.

Analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance, with Holm-Bonferroni adjustment applied for pairwise comparisons as noted.

Results

Study population

Of the 445 CRRT sessions screened for eligibility, 420 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). The 420 eligible sessions were performed in 197 unique patients (median 2 sessions per patient, range 1–6; see Supplementary Table S8). Safety and monitoring outcomes

Major bleeding events were rare (n = 9/420, 2.1%), with no significant differences between the RCA (1.6%), NM (2.2%), and UH (3.8%) groups (P = 0.58). Anticoagulation monitoring was performed in 92.7% of RCA sessions, 78.7% of NM, and 77.4% of UH sessions (P < 0.001). Among monitored sessions, the proportion achieving the protocol-defined therapeutic range was significantly higher in the RCA group (79.1%) compared to the NM (64.3%) and UH (68.3%) groups (P = 0.023). Consequently, the rate of failing to achieve the therapeutic target was 20.9% for RCA, compared to 35.7% for NM and 31.7% for UH (P < 0.001 for RCA vs. NM/UH) (Table 3).

Secondary outcomes: 28-day mortality (patient-level)

In patient-level analysis (n = 197 unique patients), 28-day all-cause mortality was 36.5% (72/197). After adjustment for age, mechanical ventilation, baseline SOFA score (where available), and malignancy, there was no significant difference in mortality across anticoagulation strategies (RCA: 35.1% [39/111], NM: 37.8% [14/37], UH: 36.4% [8/22], no anticoagulation: 41.2% [7/17]; adjusted P = 0.34). Ten patients treated with argatroban (n = 4) or combination therapy (n = 6) were excluded from between-group mortality comparison due to small sample sizes; the overall mortality of 36.5% includes all 197 patients. Mortality data were available for all 197 patients. Each patient was counted once for mortality analysis, using the anticoagulant strategy from their first CRRT session for classification. Detailed results are presented in Supplementary Table S6.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

The superior filter lifespan with RCA was consistent across all subgroups (all P for interaction > 0.05; Supplementary Table S4). Sensitivity analyses using alternative endpoints, multiple imputation, mixed-effects Cox models (accounting for within-patient correlation), exclusion of patients with liver failure, inclusion of ward as a covariate, and additional adjustment for pre-session coagulation parameters (INR, aPTT, platelets; available for 368 sessions) yielded similar results (Supplementary Table S5).

Small groups

The no anticoagulation group (n = 17 sessions) yielded the shortest filter lifespan (12.5 h) and the highest incidence of clotting (70.6%). Due to the limited sample sizes in the argatroban (n = 4 sessions) and combination therapy (n = 9 sessions) groups, no definitive conclusions could be drawn; outcomes were variable in these groups.

Discussion

In this real-world cohort of 420 CRRT sessions (197 patients), RCA was associated with significantly longer filter lifespan than NM and UH, even after extensive adjustment for confounders using propensity score matching and accounting for non-proportional hazards with RMST analysis. The median filter lifespan observed with RCA (approximately 65–72 h) aligned with benchmarks from RCTs and guidelines [4, 5], demonstrating its effectiveness in routine practice. The roughly twofold higher clotting hazard with NM and 70% higher hazard with UH (in sensitivity Cox analyses) highlights the comparative efficacy advantage of citrate’s regional anticoagulation mechanism, which effectively inhibits thrombin generation within the extracorporeal circuit without systemic effects [17].

Our finding that NM was associated with an intermediate filter lifespan (32 h) differs from some Asian single-center studies reporting comparable filter lifespan between NM and RCA [18]. This discrepancy may reflect several factors: (1) our multi-ward design captured broader practice variation, including potential differences in NM dosing protocols and monitoring frequency; (2) suboptimal NM dosing or monitoring (ACT was not checked in 21% of NM sessions), which is critical given NM’s short half-life [8]; and (3) pharmacological differences in inhibiting contact activation within CRRT circuits, as NM primarily inhibits thrombin (factor IIa) and factor Xa, as well as factors XIIa and kallikrein [8], whereas citrate chelates ionized calcium to inhibit the coagulation cascade more proximally [17]. These results suggest that NM may be a suitable alternative to RCA, particularly for bleeding-prone patients. However, clinicians should anticipate a shorter filter life and maintain diligent anticoagulation monitoring.

Comparison with existing literature and novel contributions of this study

This study offers several distinctive contributions beyond previous RCTs and real-world studies. First, to our knowledge, this is the first real-world three-arm comparison (RCA vs. NM vs. UH) with detailed session-level monitoring data from a major academic center. While RCTs have established RCA’s superiority over heparin, our study extends this evidence by including NM, an agent widely used in Asia but underrepresented in international literature. Second, we explicitly quantified the “monitoring burden” trade-off: while RCA achieved the longest circuit life, it also had the highest rate of metabolic target failure (20.9%)—a finding rarely quantified in prior real-world studies. Third, we addressed the rarely acknowledged statistical issue of non-proportional hazards in CRRT filter survival analysis by using RMST as the primary method, providing more robust estimates. Most prior comparative CRRT filter lifespan studies applied Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank tests or Cox regression without formally verifying the proportional hazards assumption [12, 28], a practice that risks biased comparisons when survival curves cross over time. Fourth, we provide practical, actionable insights for centers deciding between RCA, NM, and UH based on local monitoring capabilities and patient bleeding risk, rather than simply recommending one agent over others. Finally, our frailty model approach to handling within-patient correlation provides a methodologically sound template for future CRRT anticoagulation studies using session-level data.

The safety profile observed reveals important clinical trade-offs. The overall major bleeding rate was low (2.1%) across all groups, contrasting with older studies reporting higher rates with UH [6], likely reflecting contemporary, protocol-driven practice at our institution. However, with only nine major bleeding events in total (RCA n = 4, NM n = 2, UH n = 2, no anticoagulation n = 1), the study was substantially underpowered for safety comparisons. Therefore, these bleeding data are presented as descriptive only, and no between-group safety inferences should be drawn. The absence of statistically significant differences must not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent safety across strategies. Safety comparisons require substantially larger sample sizes or prospective designs with bleeding as a primary endpoint.

Our data highlight a significant difference in the monitoring burden and metabolic (calcium) management challenge. RCA sessions yielded the highest monitoring rate, yet a significant proportion (20.9%) failed to meet the target post-filter ionized calcium range. While not a direct measure of severe hypocalcemia, these findings consistently underscore the metabolic burden inherent to RCA, representing the “cost” of its superior circuit life. Consequently, this necessitates rigorous, protocolized monitoring, especially in patients with liver dysfunction or shock, where citrate metabolism may be impaired [17]. The high frequency of target non-achievement underscores the importance of nurse training, the availability of point-of-care testing, and institutional readiness for RCA implementation [29].

Our study has several notable strengths. The availability of detailed, session-level data on anticoagulation monitoring offers unique insights into real-world implementation gaps. Advanced statistical methods, including RMST (addressing PH violation), propensity score matching, multiplicity adjustment, and mixed-effects modeling, strengthen causal inference despite the observational design by reducing selection bias and accounting for within-patient correlation [26].

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, residual confounding from unmeasured severity indices (e.g., SOFA scores, platelet counts, fibrinogen, antithrombin III) may persist despite our adjustments. Second, the choice of anticoagulant was non-random, reflecting clinician preference and local protocols, which could introduce channeling bias; we have attempted to mitigate this with PSM and multivariable adjustment. Third, some safety events, particularly minor bleeding, may have been underdocumented, and the study was not powered for mortality. Fourth, detailed numerical values for safety monitoring (e.g., specific post-filter ionized calcium, ACT, aPTT values) were not available for all sessions; our analysis of “failure to achieve therapeutic target” was based on documented monitoring results, which indicates management challenge but does not allow precise quantification of severity. Fifth, the study was conducted in a single center in China with an ethnically homogeneous population; findings may not be generalizable to other healthcare systems or more diverse populations. Sixth, time-dependent confounding is a potential concern. Although we adjusted for baseline covariates and pre-session coagulation parameters (INR, aPTT, platelet count) where available, unmeasured time-varying factors could still influence both anticoagulant selection and filter lifespan. Sensitivity analyses adjusting for these pre-session coagulation indicators yielded results consistent with the primary findings. Nevertheless, residual time-dependent confounding cannot be fully excluded.

Clinical and operational implications

These findings have important clinical and operational implications. For institutions with established monitoring protocols and adequate nursing support, it would be reasonable to employ RCA as a first-line agent for CRRT anticoagulation given its superior filter longevity, which may translate into reduced nursing workload, fewer circuit changes, and lower overall cost despite higher initial consumable expenses [27]. When RCA is contraindicated (e.g., severe liver failure, pre-existing hypocalcemia) or unavailable, NM appears preferable to UH, though with adjusted efficacy expectations. The extremely limited filter lifespan observed in the absence of anticoagulation underscores the necessity of reserving this strategy for patients at peak hemorrhagic risk with a brief anticipated duration of CRRT. Furthermore, clinicians must consider the substantial operational burden associated with frequent circuit replacements [28].

Future research

Future research should focus on several key areas. First, protocolized, biomarker-guided dosing algorithms for all anticoagulants, potentially incorporating point-of-care viscoelastic testing or anti-Factor Xa levels, could optimize efficacy and safety [29]. Second, the development of personalized strategies based on individual thrombotic-bleeding risk profiles, perhaps using machine-learning models that incorporate dynamic clinical data, holds promise [30]. Third, implementation studies examining barriers to optimal anticoagulation monitoring, especially in resource-limited settings, are needed to improve real-world outcomes, as highlighted by large epidemiologic studies of AKI in critical care [31]. Moreover, for patients at peak hemorrhagic risk with a brief anticipated duration of CRRT, the strategy of no anticoagulation—though associated with very short filter lifespan—may be considered, as systematic review evidence supports its safety profile in selected high-bleeding-risk populations [32]. Robust cost-effectiveness analyses comparing RCA, NM, and UH across different healthcare systems would provide valuable insights for policy-making and resource allocation, given the significant economic burden of AKI and its treatment.

Conclusion

In this retrospective data analysis of real-world clinical practice, regional citrate anticoagulation was associated with longer filter lifespan compared with nafamostat mesylate and unfractionated heparin for CRRT. Nafamostat mesylate showed intermediate efficacy, while no anticoagulation resulted in a very short filter lifespan. While major bleeding events were uncommon across all strategies, the small number of events precludes definitive safety conclusions. RCA posed a greater challenge in achieving metabolic (calcium) targets, necessitating careful monitoring. Therefore, in settings with appropriate monitoring capabilities, it would be reasonable to employ RCA as a first-line agent for CRRT anticoagulation given its superior filter lifespan, while recognizing the need for individualized choice based on bleeding risk, metabolic monitoring capabilities, and institutional resources.
