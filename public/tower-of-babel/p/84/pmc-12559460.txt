MitoSiege‐Driven Catalase Collapse: A GSH‐Responsive, Mitochondria‐Targeted COF Prodrug for Amplified Chemodynamic Therapy
Authors: Qihang Ding, Bo Wang, Zixuan Zhan, Paramesh Jangili, Junyang Chen, Rakesh Mengji, Hyeonji Rha, Yang Li, Jian Tian, Jong Seung Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemodynamic therapy (CDT), leveraging Fenton reactions to generate hydroxyl radicals (•OH) from intracellular hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), offers a promising cancer treatment strategy due to its high specificity and low systemic toxicity. However, the targeted delivery of •OH‐producing prodrugs using covalent organic frameworks (COFs) remains a significant challenge. Here, we report a mitochondria‐targeted COF‐based nano prodrug, COF‐31@P, designed for enhanced CDT efficacy. COF‐31@P is composed of a Fenton‐like copper complex and the hydrogen peroxide enzyme inhibitor 3‐amino‐1,2,4‐triazole (3‐AT), linked via disulfide bonds that are selectively cleaved by tumor‐specific glutathione (GSH). This cleavage triggers the release of active components, resulting in robust •OH generation and effective eradication of cancer cells. The platform demonstrates precise mitochondrial targeting, high therapeutic efficiency, and excellent biocompatibility in vivo. By combining organelle targeting and multi‐synergistic •OH generation production, our COF‐31@P represents a significant advancement in CDT and holds strong potential for clinical translation.

Introduction

Cancer is a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality in both developing and developed nations.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] Cancer therapeutic strategies, including chemotherapy, surgery, and radiotherapy, offer limited efficacy in improving patient survival and quality of life. These limitations stem from chemotherapy resistance, surgical trauma, and the adverse side effects associated with radiotherapy.[ 4 , 5 , 6 ] In response to these challenges, Chemodynamic therapy (CDT) has emerged as a minimally invasive and highly selective therapeutic approach. CDT leverages Fenton or Fenton‐like reactions to convert endogenous hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) into hydroxyl radical (•OH), the most cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS).[ 7 , 8 , 9 ] This innovative therapy offers several advantages over conventional treatments, including tumor‐specific activation, minimal invasiveness, and reduced systemic toxicity.[ 4 , 10 ] To further enhance the efficacy of CDT, recent research has focused on optimizing Fenton catalysts, tailoring therapies to the tumor microenvironment (TME), and regulating H2O2 levels by inhibiting catalase activity.[ 11 ] Despite these advancements, the clinical translation of CDT is still limited by the lack of a drug delivery system that can simultaneously induce Fenton reactions, respond to TME cues, and promote localized •OH generation.[ 12 , 13 , 14 ] Moreover, the short lifespan and diffusion range of •OH radicals necessitate strategies that ensure their interaction with biological macromolecules to maximize oxidative damage and therapeutic efficiency.[ 15 , 16 , 17 ] Targeted therapy systems capable of transporting therapeutic agents to specific cells or organelles represent a promising solution. By reducing the distance between •OH radical generation sites and critical biomolecules, these systems enhance the oxidative damage, increase therapeutic efficiency, and promote cell death. This study explores the development of such a system, aiming to address existing limitations and advance the clinical potential of CDT.[ 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 ]

Covalent organic frameworks (COFs) are a class of porous material composed of shape‐defined organic monomers interconnected by dynamic covalent bonds.[ 22 , 23 ] These materials are highly customizable and feature uniform nanosized pores,[ 24 ] making them promising nanocarriers for integrating diverse functional components. Nanoscale COFs have been extensively studied for their therapeutic applications in phototherapy, drug delivery, immunotherapy, and radiotherapy.[ 25 , 26 ] As organic carriers, COFs offer a selective platform for incorporating therapeutic agents,[ 27 , 28 ] ensuring targeted delivery and desired therapeutic outcomes while minimizing the physiological toxicity often associated with metal‐rich inorganic materials.[ 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 ] These dual advantages position COFs as a versatile platform for advancing both preclinical and translational clinical investigations on COFs.[ 13 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 ] However, despite their potential, the use of organelle‐targeting COF‐based nanomaterials for CDT remains unexplored.[ 39 , 40 , 41 ] This study aims to address this gap by developing COF‐based nanocarriers capable of organelle‐specific targeting, with a focus on enhancing the efficacy and precision of CDT.

In this study, we therefore synthesized a nano prodrug COF‐31@P based on COFs that targets mitochondria, incorporating a copper complex for Fenton‐like reaction and a catalase inhibitor 3‐AT, which are linked by disulfide bonds. The over‐expression of glutathione (GSH) in tumor mitochondria facilitates the cleavage of these disulfide bonds, simultaneously releasing the copper complex and 3‐AT. The copper complex catalyzes the decomposition of abundant H2O2 into toxic ROS, specifically •OH, while 3‐AT inhibits catalase activity to sustain the H2O2 levels, thereby amplifying the Fenton‐like reaction. Additionally, GSH consumption through disulfide bond cleavage further reduces its capacity to scavenge ROS, enhancing the overall therapeutic effect (Figure 1). Through cellular imaging and cytotoxicity assays, we demonstrated that COF‐31@P effectively targets mitochondria, releases its therapeutic payload in response to GSH, generates a substantial amount of •OH radicals, leading to efficient tumor cell eradication via CDT. Furthermore, COF‐31@P exhibits excellent tumor enrichment and biosafety in vivo, making it a promising candidate for CDT‐based tumor therapy. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of a mitochondria‐targeting COF‐based nanomaterial that integrates GSH consumption and catalase inhibition to enhance the efficacy of CDT, marking a significant advancement in the field.

The synthesis of mitochondria‐targeted COF materials and the design of the prodrug mechanism, along with the operational principle of CDT for tumor therapy via the synergistic Fenton‐like reaction facilitated by copper complex and inhibition of catalase through COF material loading prodrug.

Results and Discussion

To confirm the cleavage of disulfide bonds and the subsequent release of the copper complex prodrugs, we designed a fluorescent prodrug, C2 conjugated with a rhodamine dye. The synthesis of C2 involved a multistep process, including nucleophilic substitution and click reactions initiated from di‐(2‐picolyl)amine. Detailed synthetic procedures are provided in Figure 2a and Supporting Information (Scheme S1). We were able to identify and characterize the fluorescent prodrug C2, incorporating a copper complex and a disulfide bond, through the use of 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy in conjunction with high‐resolution mass spectrometry (Figures S1–S11). As shown in Figure 2a, the disulfide bond in C2 was efficiently cleaved in the presence of GSH, resulting in the release of the copper complex and the formation of rhodamine derivative as a reaction intermediate. This intermediate subsequently underwent electrophilic aromatic substitution by attacking an electron‐deficient carbon atom, resulting in the production of rhodamine dye. These reactions facilitated the GSH‐induced release of the copper complex. To further investigate the interaction between C2 and GSH, we conducted a titration experiment using absorption spectroscopy, during which GSH was incrementally added to C2. Addition of GSH resulted in a notable shift in the absorption peak from 300 to 350 nm (Figure S15). Correspondingly, there was an increase in fluorescence intensity at 500 nm (Figures 2b,c), which allowed us to plot it as a function of GSH concentration with respective detection limits set at 3 mM (Figure 2d). These results also confirmed that the disulfide bond‐linked copper complex prodrug can be effectively cleaved in the presence of GSH, leading to the release of the active copper complex. The observed spectral changes and fluorescence enhancement provide strong evidence for the GSH‐responsive behavior of C2, validating its potential for targeted drug release in environments with high GSH concentrations, such as tumor mitochondria.

After validating the fluorescent disulfide bond‐linked copper complex prodrug, we extended this GSH‐responsive molecular platform to synthesize a prodrug C1 derived from the catalase inhibitor 3‐AT and characterized its structure (Figures S12–S14). As illustrated in Figure 2e, the disulfide bond in C1 was efficiently cleaved in the presence of GSH, leading to the release of the copper complex and the catalase inhibitor 3‐AT. To further explore the interaction between prodrug C1 and GSH, we conducted high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis on the reaction of C1 with GSH. The HPLC analysis revealed that prodrug C1 exhibited a single peak at 10.2 min (Figure 2f). After the addition of GSH for 1 h, the original peak of C1 diminished, and a new peak appeared at 8 min. Upon extending the incubation time to 4 h, the original C1 peak completely disappeared, while the new peak at 8 min intensified, coinciding with the retention time of 3‐AT (8 min). These findings indicated that prodrug C1 releases copper complexes and catalase inhibitor 3‐AT upon interaction with GSH. The observed changes in HPLC profiles provide clear evidence of the controlled and efficient release of active therapeutic components under conditions of high GSH concentration, such as those found in tumor environments. This GSH‐triggered release mechanism highlights the potential of prodrug C1 as a targeted therapeutic agent for enhancing chemodynamic therapy through simultaneous copper complex activation and catalase inhibition.

We synthesized the COF framework via a Schiff base reaction between 4′,4′″,4′″″‐(1,3,5‐triazine‐2,4,6‐triyl)tris(([1,1′‐biphenyl]‐4‐amine)) and 2′,5′‐dihydroxy‐[1,1′:4′,1″‐terphenyl]‐4,4′′‐dicarbaldehyde. Subsequently, the framework was functionalized with bromine‐substituted triphenylphosphine groups, which possess mitochondrial targeting capability, to obtain COF‐31. Building on this, we further employed COF‐31 as a drug delivery platform to encapsulate the prodrug C1, leading to the formation of the nanomedicine COF‐31@P (Figure 3a). The nanomedicine was comprehensively characterized to confirm its structural and functional properties. The scanning electron microscope (SEM) image (Figure 3b) revealed uniformly dispersed, spherical‐like nanostructures with smooth surfaces, indicating that the copper‐based prodrug was successfully loaded without compromising the COF framework. Hydrodynamic diameter measurements (Figure 3c) of COF‐31@P revealed an average size of approximately 180 nm. To assess the stability of COF‐31@P, dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis was conducted. The particle size of COF‐31@P remained relatively constant after storage in H2O and PBS for 7 days, further confirming its favorable colloidal stability in both aqueous and physiological environments (Figure S16). The inset transmission electron microscope (TEM) image further confirmed the uniform morphology and dense core structure, suggesting efficient prodrug incorporation. Zeta potential analysis (Figure 3d) showed a noticeable shift from 12.5 mV for pristine COF‐31 to 10.3 mV for COF‐31@P, indicating surface charge modification due to prodrug loading. Powder X‐ray diffraction (PXRD) analysis was employed to evaluate the crystallinity and structural ordering of COF‐31@P (Figure 3e). A prominent and sharp diffraction peak at 2θ = 0.6°, which was characteristic of the (100) reflection plane, indicates a well‐defined long‐range periodicity and an ordered two‐dimensional layered structure with large pore channels. The distinct nature and high intensity of this peak reflected the high crystallinity of the COF framework. The absence of any significant broad peaks or additional reflections at higher angles suggested a high degree of phase purity and minimal amorphous content. The sharpness of the (100) reflection and the lack of secondary peaks underscored the material's uniformity and structural integrity.

Energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDX) mapping (Figure 3f) confirmed the homogeneous distribution of C, N, O, S, and Cu throughout COF‐31@P. The presence of Cu validates the successful encapsulation of the copper‐containing prodrug, while S signals the integration of sulfur‐containing functional groups from the prodrug. The EDX spectrum of COF‐31@P showed prominent Cu peaks at ∼1.0 keV (Cu Lα), ∼8.0 keV (Cu Kα), and ∼8.9 keV (Cu Kβ), confirming the successful loading of the prodrug, as Cu was absent in pristine COF‐31 (Figure 3g). Additionally, the distinct sulfur (S Kα) peak at ∼2.3 keV served as further evidence of prodrug incorporation, reflecting the presence of sulfur‐containing functional groups specific to the drug. The co‐localization of the strong Cu and S signals together validated the effective integration of the prodrug into the COF‐31 structure. As shown in Figure 3h,i, the high‐resolution X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra of the Cu 2p region confirmed the successful incorporation of the copper‐based prodrug into the COF‐31 framework. Distinct peaks at ∼932.5 eV (Cu 2p 3/2) and ∼952.3 eV (Cu 2p 1/2) indicated the presence of both Cu⁺ and Cu2⁺ oxidation states, with an additional peak at ∼934.8 eV and characteristic shake‐up satellite features, further confirming partial Cu2⁺ coordination. The survey spectrum also revealed sulfur (S 2p) peaks, supporting the incorporation of sulfur‐containing prodrug moieties, and reinforcing the structural and chemical integration within COF‐31. The coexistence of Cu⁺/Cu2⁺ suggested that the prodrug retained its redox activity post‐loading, ensuring its stability and potential therapeutic efficacy. Collectively, these results demonstrated that COF‐31@P retained its structural integrity and exhibited uniform prodrug loading, establishing a strong foundation for its potential therapeutic applications. The in vitro prodrug loading capacity of COF‐31 was evaluated using fluorescence analysis (Figure 3j). The results indicated that the prodrug loading efficiency of COF‐31 was approximately 15% w/w. Atomic absorption spectroscopy was used to measure the Cu concentration in the supernatant after 6 h of preloading with 3 mg of COF‐31. The loading capacity after 6 h was calculated as 0.665 mg, with an encapsulation efficiency of 11.1% (Figure S17).

The release behavior of the prodrug was assessed by measuring copper ion concentrations using inductively coupled plasma (ICP). COF‐31@P was exposed to different GSH concentrations, and copper ion levels were recorded after varying reaction times. As shown in Figure S18, prolonged GSH exposure and higher GSH concentrations gradually increased copper ion levels, indicating that the prodrug releases its active components in response to GSH. To evaluate copper ion release following prodrug activation, we conducted ICP‐MS analysis under conditions of elevated H2O2, acidic pH, and GSH presence. Cu release was most pronounced under acidic pH conditions, with a higher release observed under GSH conditions compared to that under H2O2 conditions (Figure S19). We evaluated the nano prodrug COF‐31@P in H2O to assess its ability to generate •OH. Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) can be oxidized by •OH, and the resulting oxidation product can be quantified by measuring its absorbance at a specific wavelength. This allows for the analysis of •OH content within a sample. TMB was used to detect the ability of COF‐31@P at different concentrations to react with H2O2 to generate •OH. The absorbance results showed that the generation of •OH increased with the increase of COF‐31@P concentration (Figure S20).

To investigate the cellular internalization of COF‐31 and COF‐31@P, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was performed on CT26 cells. The fluorescence intensity of COF‐31@P was higher than that of free COF‐31, peaking at 4 h, indicating enhanced cellular uptake and accumulation (Figure 4a). Specifically, after 4 h of incubation, the fluorescence intensity of COF‐31@P was 1.37 times higher than that of free COF‐31 (Figure 4b). This enhanced uptake may be attributed to the lack of regulatory effects after drug release in the free COF, whereas the drug‐loaded COF, upon releasing the prodrug, may exert cytotoxic effects on tumor tissue, leading to the formation of additional pores. These structural changes could further enhance the subsequent penetration and accumulation of COF‐31@P, establishing a positive feedback loop. Moreover, COF‐31@P was incubated with CT26 cells at a concentration of 100 µg mL−1 for 4 h. The Cu content was then analyzed by ICP spectroscopy, showing a total uptake efficiency of 28.01% (Figure S21). To investigate the cellular uptake pathways of COF‐31@P, we employed chlorpromazine as a clathrin inhibitor and Dynasore as a caveolin inhibitor. Confocal imaging demonstrated that the fluorescence intensity in the chlorpromazine‐treated group was markedly reduced compared to other groups, indicating a significant decrease in COF‐31@P uptake. These results suggest that COF‐31@P primarily enters cells through clathrin‐mediated endocytosis (Figure S22). To investigate the intracellular distribution of COF‐31@P in cancer cells, co‐staining experiments were conducted using CT26 cells and commercially available organelle dyes such as Mitochondria Tracker Green. As shown in Figure 4c, COF‐31@P exhibited a specific accumulation within the mitochondria of CT26 cells, emitting red fluorescence that showed strong colocalization (Pearson correlation coefficient = 0.93) with Mitochondria Tracker Green. The unique characteristics of COF‐31@P, including their high charge density and pronounced positive charge, likely influenced their lipophilicity and electrophoretic force, facilitating preferential localization to the mitochondria.

To evaluate the generation of •OH at the cellular level, we employed 2′,7′‐dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH‐DA) as a fluorescent probe for ROS detection. Figure 5a,b demonstrated that CT26 cells treated with COF‐31@P displayed higher levels of green fluorescence than untreated control cells or those treated with COF‐31 or prodrug alone. We further investigated the intracellular generation of •OH using the hydroxyphenyl fluorescein (HPF) probe. Compared with the COF‐31 and prodrug groups, COF‐31@P demonstrated the highest fluorescence intensity, suggesting the most pronounced •OH production within the CT26 cells (Figure S23). This enhancement can be attributed to two factors. Firstly, COF‐31@P targets the mitochondria in tumor cells, which contain higher levels of H2O2. The copper complex within the prodrug then reacts with this increased H2O2, enhancing therapeutic efficacy through a Fenton‐like reaction. Secondly, the catalase inhibitor (3‐AT) promotes the generation of ROS by maintaining elevated levels of H2O2, thereby enhancing the effects of oxidative stress and posing a threat to tumor cell survival. Furthermore, as shown in Figure 5c, the ROS generation in the COF‐31@P + GSH group is higher than that in the COF‐31@P group, indicating that the nanomedicine exhibits excellent TME responsiveness, enabling GSH‐triggered release of the copper complex and catalase inhibitor within tumor cells. The prodrug significantly enhanced H2O2 levels in CT26 cells upon the release of the catalase inhibitor 3‐AT. As a critical substrate for the Fenton‐like reaction, H2O2 was catalyzed by Cu2⁺ released from the prodrug to generate highly reactive •OH, which subsequently elevated intracellular ROS levels. The markedly increased ROS production observed in the COF‐31@P + H2O2 group compared to the COF‐31@P group further demonstrates that the Fenton‐like reaction effectively facilitates H2O2 decomposition to generate •OH, exacerbating oxidative stress‐induced cellular damage. Quantitative analysis (Figure 5d) further corroborated that the intracellular ROS levels are significantly elevated in response to both GSH and H2O2, validating the prodrug's TME‐responsive activation and catalytic amplification of oxidative stress. The oxygen‐responsive probe [Ru(dpp)3]Cl2 was introduced to CT26 cells in the Control, COF‐31, Prodrug, and COF‐31@P groups (200 µg mL−1) and incubated for 4 h. The significant decrease in oxygen content in the COF‐31@P group indicated that the catalase inhibitor effectively suppressed the conversion of H2O2 into O2 (Figure S24).

To investigate the pharmacological effects of COF‐31@P, a staining experiment was conducted using Calcein‐AM (fluorescent dye indicating live cells) and PI (fluorescent dye indicating dead cells) to assess the efficacy of CDT in eliminating tumor cells and determine the cell death‐inducing potential of the nano prodrug. In Figure 5e, negligible cell death was observed in cells treated with PBS or COF‐31 alone. However, when exposed to COF‐31@P, a significant increase in red fluorescence and minimal green fluorescence was observed in the cell images. These findings suggest that COF‐31@P effectively induces tumor cell death during CDT.

A systematic cytotoxicity evaluation of COF‐31@P against CT26 cells was conducted using 3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. Both COF‐31@P and its prodrug demonstrated antitumor activity, with a pronounced dose‐dependent cytotoxic effect. The half‐maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were calculated as 50.08 µg m−1 for the prodrug, 2.93 mg mL−1 for COF‐31, and 17.67 µg mL−1 for COF‐31@P (Figures 5f and S25). These results not only indirectly validated the excellent biocompatibility of the COF framework but also highlighted that the mitochondria‐targeting strategy of COF‐31 significantly enhances CDT‐induced tumor cell eradication.

Furthermore, Annexin V‐FITC/PI dual‐staining flow cytometry analysis (Figure 5g,h) revealed that the apoptotic rate of CT26 cells treated with COF‐31@P reached 72.15%, significantly surpassing that of the COF‐31 and prodrug groups. These findings further substantiated the mitochondria‐targeting capability of the COF‐31 framework in potentiating CDT‐induced apoptosis. Consistently, in the GSH depletion assay (Figure 5i), the COF‐31@P‐treated group exhibited a significantly higher GSH consumption compared to the prodrug group, indirectly confirming its superior CDT catalytic activity. Notably, comparative cytotoxicity assessments across different tumor cell lines (HepG2 and Caco‐2) revealed that CT26 cells exhibited the most pronounced sensitivity toward COF‐31@P, suggesting a high cellular uptake efficiency or an enhanced susceptibility to CDT‐induced oxidative damage. This antitumor efficacy further underscores the therapeutic potential of COF‐31@P for precision‐targeted colorectal cancer treatment (Figure S26). To investigate the mechanism of cuproptosis, Control, COF (200 µg mL−1), Prodrug (30 µg mL−1), and COF‐31@P (200 µg mL−1) were administered for group treatment over 4 h. The cuproptosis‐related proteins DLAT, FDX1, and LIAS were subsequently analyzed using Western Blot assays. Prodrug and COF‐31@P both led to a significant reduction in the expression levels of proteins associated with cuproptosis. Notably, the effect of COF‐31@P on protein expression was more pronounced compared to that of Prodrug (Figure S27).

To systematically evaluate the biosafety profile of COF‐31@P, its effects on mouse embryonic fibroblasts (3T3 cells) were assessed in vitro. Following a 24‐h exposure to 400 µg mL−1 of COF‐31 and COF‐31@P, no significant decrease in cell viability was observed, indicating the absence of apparent cytotoxicity (Figure 5j). This finding was further corroborated by live/dead cell staining assays (Figure S28), which demonstrated that cell morphology remained intact, with no evident cellular damage. Moreover, even at a concentration of 60 µg mL−1, the prodrug‐treated group (Figure S29) did not exhibit any discernible cytotoxic effects on 3T3 cells. These results collectively validated the excellent biocompatibility and safety of COF‐31@P, demonstrating its ability to effectively induce CDT‐mediated tumor cell apoptosis while maintaining minimal toxicity toward normal cells. The in vivo biosafety of COF‐31@P was further evaluated in female BALB/c mice. The hemolytic properties were examined using a standardized hemolysis assay (ASTM E2524‐08), which revealed that COF‐31@P, even at concentrations up to 200 µg mL−1, did not induce any detectable hemolysis (Figure S30), highlighting its excellent hemocompatibility. We measured the levels of biochemical indices for the liver and kidney function—alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total protein, creatinine (CR), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN)—in the serum of mice after treatment with COF‐31@P. We found that the levels of these indices were all within the normal range for mice and had no significant difference from the Control group (Figure S31).

Additionally, pharmacokinetic analysis demonstrated that COF‐31@P exhibited a significantly prolonged blood circulation half‐life (t 1/2 = 3.02 h) compared to the prodrug (t 1/2 = 0.69 h). This result suggests that COF‐31 functions as an efficient nanocarrier, not only enabling the precise mitochondrial‐targeted delivery of the prodrug but also significantly extending its systemic circulation time, thereby enhancing antitumor therapeutic efficacy (Figure S32). The copper ion content was further quantified in the liver and spleen of mice at various time points following the tail vein administration of the COF‐31@P using ICP‐MS. The highest concentration of copper ions in the liver was observed 60 min post‐injection. Furthermore, the copper ion levels in the liver consistently exceeded those in the spleen throughout the observation period, suggesting that the prodrug may predominantly undergo hepatic metabolism (Figure S33). In summary, COF‐31@P demonstrates good biocompatibility and biosafety in both in vitro and in vivo models, along with favorable pharmacokinetic properties; these attributes strongly support its potential application in CDT and precision cancer treatment.

After confirming the excellent biosafety and biocompatibility of COF‐31@P, we further investigated its in vivo biodistribution and tumor‐targeting accumulation capacity. Fluorescence imaging technology was employed to quantitatively analyse the biodistribution of COF‐31@P across various organs and tumor tissues, and fluorescence imaging technology was employed for quantitative analysis. The results indicated that COF‐31@P reached peak fluorescence intensity in tumor tissue at 6 h post‐injection, followed by a gradual decline until the end of the experiment (Figure 6a,b). This suggests that COF‐31@P efficiently accumulates at tumor sites via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, facilitating its targeted localization in vivo.

The anti‐tumor efficacy of COF‐31@P was further validated using CT26 tumor‐bearing models. BALB/c mice were injected with CT26 cells subcutaneously, and when the tumor size reached approximately 100 mm3, treatments were administered via intravenous injection of PBS buffer, free COF‐31 (10 mg kg−1), prodrug (1.5 mg kg−1), and COF‐31@P (10 mg kg−1). Tumor size and body weight measurements were taken every 2 days. The growth rate of CT26 tumors treated with PBS and COF‐31 showed rapid progression, resulting in a ∼10‐fold and ∼14‐fold increase in tumor volumes, respectively, after 15 days of treatment (Figure 6c). In comparison, the average tumor volume in the group treated with free prodrug increased by a factor of 10 after 15 days of treatment. However, when treated with COF‐31@P, a minimal change in tumor size was observed during the initial 8 days following injection, and tumor growth was effectively limited to only a ∼3‐fold increase after 15 days of treatment (Figure S34). Tumor weight statistics on day 15 further validated the significantly enhanced therapeutic efficacy of COF‐31@P compared to all other treatment groups (Figure 6d). Notably, no significant fluctuations in body weight were observed among the mice across different groups throughout the treatment period, indicating the biosafety of the formulation (Figure 6e). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, from mice in different treatment groups following therapy. No noticeable inflammatory lesions or tissue damage were observed, underscoring the excellent biocompatibility of COF‐31@P (Figure 6f). To further assess the therapeutic efficacy of each treatment, H&E staining, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick‐end labeling (TUNEL) immunofluorescence staining, and Ki67 immunohistochemical staining were conducted on tumor tissues (Figure 6g). H&E staining revealed pronounced tumor cell shrinkage in the COF‐31@P group, indicative of extensive tumor necrosis. Consistently, TUNEL staining exhibited the most intense green fluorescence in the COF‐31@P group, suggesting the highest level of apoptosis. Additionally, Ki67, a proliferation marker, showed the lowest expression in the COF‐31@P group, confirming a predominant apoptotic state of the tumor cells. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that COF‐31@P effectively suppresses colorectal tumor progression, exhibiting a potent CDT effect.

Conclusion

We have successfully synthesized the nano prodrug COF‐31@P by employing a mitochondrial‐targeting COF as the delivery platform for catalase inhibitors and copper complexes. The morphology of COF‐31@P was thoroughly characterized, revealing spherical nanostructures with an average particle size of approximately 180 nm, uniform morphology, a dense core structure, and ordered macroporous channels exhibiting two‐dimensional layered structural characteristics. In vitro experiments demonstrated that the disulfide bonds within the prodrug can be efficiently cleaved by GSH, releasing copper complexes with Fenton‐like reactivity and catalase inhibitors to suppress H2O2 consumption. Cellular experiments further confirmed that COF‐31@P exhibits exceptional stability, outstanding biocompatibility, and precise mitochondrial targeting. Upon mitochondrial targeting, COF‐31@P effectively releases the prodrug. The GSH‐triggered release of the prodrug enables the copper complex to react with H2O2 via a Fenton‐like reaction, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, the catalase inhibitor (3‐AT) sustains elevated H2O2 levels, further promoting ROS (•OH) generation and increasing oxidative stress, ultimately threatening tumor cell survival. In vivo validation of COF‐31@P in CT26 tumor‐bearing mice demonstrated its tumor accumulation via the EPR effect, as well as its therapeutic efficacy through effective CDT. Moreover, COFs demonstrate significant advantages over metal‐organic frameworks, polymer micelles, and inorganic nanoenzymes in the context of CDT, owing to their tunable pore structures, high specific surface areas, excellent biocompatibility, and favorable electronic conductivity (Table S1). COF‐31@P incorporates mitochondrial targeting and enables the synergistic generation of multiple hydroxyl radicals. This strategy effectively addresses the key limitations of conventional CDT, including low catalytic efficiency, TME barriers, and the insufficiency of single‐mode treatment. These findings highlight the potential of COF‐based delivery systems to improve CDT efficacy and advance precision cancer treatment.
