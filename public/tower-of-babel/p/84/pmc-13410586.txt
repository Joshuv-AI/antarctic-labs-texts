Epidemiology and risk factors of intestinal parasitic infections among pregnant women in the Northern Region of Ghana
Authors: Abdul-Samad Alhassan, Prince Ottah, Denzel Opoku-Kwabi, Emmanuella Akumeniwaa Nkrumah, Grace Zaghe, Abraham Awonboro Adiak, Alberta Serwah Anning, George Ghartey-Kwansah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntestinal parasitic infections (IPIs) pose a significant public health threat in endemic regions, where multiple species of parasites co-circulate, resulting in polyparasitism and an increased disease burden. Despite efforts to control these infections, the complex interactions between different intestinal parasites, and environmental determinants remain poorly understood. This study aimed to investigate the distribution and co-occurrence patterns of major intestinal parasites as well as associated factors influencing IPIs.MethodsA retrospective facility-based study was conducted using hospital records of pregnant women over a 12-month period in Northern Ghana to assess the prevalence and associated factors of intestinal parasitic infections (IPIs). Stool samples had been examined using the formol-ether concentration microscopy technique. Data were cleaned and analyzed in R using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and logistic regression analyses to examine associations between IPIs and factors such as age group, malaria status, season, and stool consistency.ResultsA total of 1,606 participants were involved in the study. The overall prevalence of IPIs was 20.86% (95% CI: 18.94–22.91), while the co-infection rate was 1.74% (95% CI: 1.21–2.51). The most frequently identified parasites were Entamoeba histolytica/dispar/moshkovskii complex (n = 89, 5.5%), intestinal flagellates (n = 86, 5.4%), and Giardia lamblia (n = 33, 2.1%). The most common co-infections were Entamoeba coli with intestinal flagellates (n = 10, 0.6%) and Entamoeba coli with Entamoeba histolytica/dispar/moshkovskii complex (n = 9, 0.56%). Significant associations were observed between IPIs and the presence of red blood cells (RBCs) in stool (χ² = 31.92, df = 1, p < 0.001), white blood cells (WBCs) in stool (χ² = 35.07, df = 1, p < 0.001), and stool consistency (χ² = 21.41, df = 4, p < 0.001). Other factors such as malaria status (χ² = 1.24, df = 1, p = 0.264), presence of yeast-like cells in stool (χ² = 0.89, df = 1, p = 0.345), and season (χ² = 1.33, df = 1, p = 0.248) were not significantly associated with IPIs.ConclusionThis study demonstrates a considerable burden of IPIs among pregnant women in Northern Ghana, with protozoan parasites being the predominant Intestinal parasites identified. The findings highlight the importance of routine parasitological screening, the need to improve sanitation and hygiene interventions, and targeted health education during antenatal care to reduce the burden of IPIs and improve maternal health outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Intestinal parasitic infections (IPIs) remain a major public health challenge, affecting over two billion people worldwide, particularly in developing countries [1, 2]. These infections are highly prevalent in tropical regions, where poor sanitation, limited healthcare access, and environmental conditions facilitate their transmission [3, 4]. Common parasites such as Giardia lamblia, Entamoeba histolytica, and hookworms disproportionately affect vulnerable populations and contribute to health complications like malnutrition, anemia, and increased morbidity [1, 5, 6].

Co-infections with multiple parasites are also common, and further complicates disease outcomes. For instance, a study conducted in Tanzania has reported high rates of co-infection, with 26.5% of schoolchildren harboring at least two parasite species, including Schistosoma mansoni and hookworms [7]. These infections are particularly concerning among high-risk groups such as children, immunocompromised individuals, and pregnant women [7, 8]. In pregnancy, IPIs can exacerbate maternal anemia, compromise nutritional status, and negatively impact fetal development, increasing the risk of adverse birth outcomes [9–11]. Despite these risks, research on IPI burdens among pregnant women remains limited, particularly in resource-constrained settings.

Most IPIs are classified as neglected tropical diseases (NTDs)due to their widespread impact on marginalized communities and their association with inadequate sanitation and healthcare [12, 13]. Although research on NTDs has advanced in recent years, the focus often remains on diseases affecting wealthier nations, leaving IPIs underrepresented [14]. In Ghana, studies have documented high IPI prevalence among children, pregnant women, and prison inmates in some regions [15–17]. However, there are still significant gaps in understanding the regional distribution, risk factors, and the health implications of IPIs among pregnant women, particularly in Northern Ghana, where socioeconomic and environmental conditions may influence infection patterns.

Given these gaps, this study aims to investigate the prevalence, co-infection patterns, and associated risk factors of IPIs among pregnant women attending a health facility in Northern Ghana. By addressing this knowledge gap, the findings could help inform a nuance understanding of IPIs dynamics in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective facility-based study was conducted over a 12-month period to determine the prevalence, co-infection patterns, and associated risk factors of IPIs among pregnant women at the Tamale Teaching Hospital (TTH). Data were extracted from medical records in the maternity ward of TTH, using secondary sources including laboratory test results and patient files. All retrieved data were systematically compiled into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet for cleaning, coding, and preprocessing prior to statistical analysis.

Study population

The study population comprised pregnant women who attended the maternity ward of the health facility and underwent diagnostic testing for intestinal parasitic infections (IPIs) using microscopy.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study included retrospective data of pregnant women who were residing in Northern Ghana and received antenatal care at the Tamale teaching Hospital. Pregnant women who i were recoded to have taken antiparasitic medication within the past four weeks, those with severe medical conditions at the time of their visit to the clinic, non-residents, participants with incomplete data were excluded from the study.

Microscopy

Stool specimens were collected during routine antenatal care visits at Tamale Teaching Hospital and processed using the Formol-Ether concentration technique by Medical laboratory Scientist, in accordance with the Center for Diseases Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines [18]. Five milliliters (5 ml) of the fecal suspension was strained through gauze into a 15 ml centrifuge tube, and the volume was adjusted to 15 ml using 10% formalin. The sample was then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and 10 ml of 10% formalin was added to the sediment and mixed. Four milliliters (4 ml) of ethyl acetate was added, and the mixture was shaken vigorously for 30 s before being centrifuged again at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The top layer was carefully decanted, and the sediment was resuspended in a drop of 10% formalin. A portion of the suspension was mounted on a slide, covered with a coverslip, and examined under a light microscope using a ×40 objective lens to detect and identify parasites [19].

Data collection and preprocessing

Data were extracted from the hospital’s records (stool routine examination results, blood films for plasmodium parasites, marital status, age) and entered into Microsoft Excel for initial preprocessing. Preprocessing steps included cleaning the data by checking for inconsistencies, duplicates, and missing values, standardizing variables to ensure uniformity in data format and classification, and subsetting relevant variables for statistical analysis, including demographic factors, clinical characteristics, and laboratory-confirmed IPI results.

Data analysis

The cleaned dataset was imported into R statistical software for further analysis. The analysis involved descriptive statistics to calculate proportions and summarize IPI prevalence and co-infection rates, inferential statistical tests to assess associations between IPIs and potential risk factors were performed using Chi-square tests for categorical variables and logistic regression analysis where applicable. For regression analysis, crude odds ratios were obtained using univariate logistic regression models, with each model excluding missing observations for the variable under consideration. Adjusted odds ratios were obtained using multivariable logistic regression based on complete-case analysis. All statistical tests were conducted at a 95% confidence level (p < 0.05) to determine statistically significant factors influencing IPIs.

Results

Participants demographics

A total of 1,606 participants were enrolled in the study, with ages following an approximately normal distribution. The majority of participants (83.7%) were between 20 and 35 years old, with a mean age of 30 years. The minimum and maximum ages of the participants were 16 and 40 years respectively (Fig. 1a).

Infection prevalence and proportions

Among the 1,606 participants, 335 individuals were diagnosed with intestinal parasitic infection (Table 1), resulting in an overall infection prevalence of 20.9% (95% CI: 18.94–22.91). Notably, 28 of the infections were co-infections, representing a prevalence of 1.7% (95% CI: 1.21–2.51), with the most common combinations including Entamoeba coli and intestinal flagellates, E. histolytica and E. coli, and E. histolytica and intestinal flagellates. Less frequent co-infections included Hymenolepis nana and intestinal flagellates and Strongyloides stercoralis and E. histolytica. While some parasites, such as E. coli, E. histolytica, and intestinal flagellates, were highly prevalent, others, including Balantidium coli and Taenia solium, were much rarer (Fig. 1b; Table 2). Among the observed co-infections, E. histolytica and intestinal flagellates were the most frequently occurring.

Seasonal distribution of infection

The prevalence of IPIs was slightly higher during the rainy season at 21.82% (95% CI: 19.37% – 24.49%) compared to 19.28% (95% CI: 16.33% – 22.60%) in the dry season. Despite this seasonal variation, the overall composition of parasite infections remained consistent across both seasons. Parasites such as E. coli, E. histolytica, and intestinal flagellates were consistently more prevalent, whereas H. nana, hookworms, and S. mansoni were detected at much lower frequencies (Fig. 2a). There was no distinct monthly trend in parasite infection distribution. However, peak infections occurred during the rainy season, particularly in May and June, while the lowest infection rates were observed in the dry season, particularly in December and February (Fig. 2b).

Associated factors and predictors of intestinal parasitic infections

There was no statistically significant association between age group and infection status (χ² = 2.29, df = 2, p = 0.319) or malaria status (χ² = 1.24, df = 1, p = 0.264). However, significant associations were observed between infection status and the presence of white blood cells (WBCs) (χ² = 35.07, df = 1, p < 0.001), red blood cells (RBCs) (χ² = 31.92, df = 1, p < 0.001), and stool consistency (χ² = 21.41, df = 4, p < 0.001). No significant association was found between infection status and the presence of yeast-like cells (χ² = 0.89, df = 1, p = 0.345) or season (χ² = 1.33, df = 1, p = 0.248) (Table 3).

To further evaluate predictors of intestinal parasitic infections (IPIs), univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted. The crude odds ratio (COR) and adjusted odds ratio (AOR) analyses showed that age group, malaria status, season, and the presence of yeast-like cells were not significantly associated with IPIs. The presence of WBCs, and RBCs, remained significant additional laboratory findings in the diagnosis of IPI (Table 4).

Discussion

Understanding the distribution of intestinal parasitic infections (IPIs) is essential for improving control strategies and mitigating their impact on public health. This study examined the prevalence, seasonal variations, and associated factors of IPIs among pregnant women in Northern Ghana. The findings provide valuable insights into the burden of IPIs in this population and highlight potential areas for intervention.

The overall prevalence of IPIs was 20.86%, which aligns with studies conducted in similar tropical regions where intestinal parasites remain endemic. For instance, a study conducted by Deku and colleagues found a comparable prevalence of 21.20% in Berekum, Ghana [20]. However, Abaka-Yawson et al. reported a relatively lower prevalence of 14.30% among pregnant women in Kasoa Polyclinic [21]. Berekum and Northern Ghana consist mainly of rural and semi-rural communities, with a large portion of the population engaged in farming. In contrast, Kasoa is a rapidly urbanizing area where most residents are involved in non-agricultural occupations. Therefore, the differences observed could be attributed to differences in study settings or socioeconomic and environmental factors influencing parasite transmission [22–24].

The relatively high prevalence observed may reflect environmental and sanitation-related challenges that persist in many rural and peri-urban communities in Northern Ghana. Many communities in Ghana, especially in the Northern Regions, continue to face challenges with access to clean water, proper waste disposal, and improved toilet facilities [25]. Open defecation remains common in both rural and peri-urban settings including Ghana, creating an environment where parasites can easily thrive and spread [26–28]. These environmental factors increase exposure risk, hindering efforts to break the transmission cycle. Moreover, overcrowded living conditions and limited health education around hygiene practices can exacerbate the situation, particularly for vulnerable groups like pregnant women whose immune systems may already be compromised [29]. Addressing these sanitation gaps is therefore critical in reducing the burden of IPIs in the region.

Co-infection of protozoan parasites and helminths is commonly observed in studies in different populations [1, 6, 30, 31]. For instance, Ngui and colleagues reported a high prevalence of polyparasitism among indigenous communities in Malaysia, with Trichuris trichiura, Ascaris lumbricoides, and G. lamblia commonly occurring in mixed infections [32]. Similarly, Forson and colleagues, in a study conducted in Ghana, found that co-infections with protozoan and helminth parasites were frequent among school children [29]. In this study, the 1.74% prevalence of co-infections observed suggests that polyparasitism is not uncommon in the study population. The observed pattern of co-infection may be due to overlapping risk factors such as poor sanitation, shared transmission routes, and co-exposure to multiple parasites in endemic regions [33, 34]. Co-infection with multiple parasites during pregnancy pose significant health risk including anemia, low birth weight, and increased risk of complications like stillbirth and preterm delivery [11] and therefore needs critical redress.

The presence of red and white blood cells (RBCs and WBCs) in stool typically reflects underlying gastrointestinal pathology. Invasive protozoa such as E. histolytica can disrupt the intestinal mucosa and cause epithelial ulceration and bleeding. Other protozoa, including G. lamblia, may also induce intestinal irritation and inflammatory changes [35, 36]. While the detection of RBCs in stool can also be attributed to non-infectious conditions such as hemorrhoids, anal fissures, or inflammatory bowel diseases [37], the high burden of invasive parasitic species observed in our study population strongly suggests a parasitic etiology for many of these cases [38].

Leukocytes (WBCs) in stool are widely recognized as markers of intestinal inflammation and mucosal immune activation. Although fecal leukocytosis is classically associated with invasive bacterial enteropathogens like Shigella spp., Salmonella spp., and Campylobacter spp. [39], studies have documented similar findings in parasitic infections. In particular, protozoa such as G. lamblia, Cryptosporidium spp., and Blastocystis spp. have been reported to elicit leukocyte responses in stool, especially in immunocompromised individuals [40, 41].

In our analysis, stool samples containing RBCs and WBCs were significantly associated with intestinal parasitic infections. The significant association between stool RBCs/WBCs and IPIs likely reflects underlying intestinal mucosal inflammation and tissue disruption accompanying parasitic infection. These findings suggest that inflammatory stool microscopy findings may provide supportive evidence of gastrointestinal pathology in parasitized individuals, particularly in resource-limited settings where advanced diagnostics are unavailable. Furthermore, the identification of RBCs may aid in differentiating between pathogenic species like E. histolytica and morphologically similar non-pathogenic species such as E. coli.

There was a modest increase in IPI prevalence during the rainy season compared to the dry season, but this difference was not statistically significant and is consistent with other studies that found comparable seasonal changes [42, 43].

Stool characteristics may play a clinically important role in diagnosing IPIs [44]. Loose stools remained significantly associated with higher odds of infection after adjustment compared with formed stool, semi-formed stools. Protozoan intestinal infections commonly produce mild-to-moderate gastrointestinal disturbances rather than severe acute watery diarrhoea, hence the predominance of semi-formed and loose stool samples among infected participants is biologically plausible. Stool consistency was found to be strongly associated with IPIs, with semi-formed showing the highest infection rate. However, the strong association observed could also be influenced by the disproportionately large number of semi-formed stool samples, which may have influenced the observed distribution across stool categories. Moreover, multivariate analysis indicated that factors such as age group, malaria status, and season did not significantly influence the risk of infection. These findings contrast with some previous studies suggesting that these factors may influence IPI prevalence [34, 45].

The lack of a significant association between age and IPIs in this study may reflect the homogeneity of the study population, which was primarily composed of young to middle-aged pregnant women or a confounding effect of shared risk exposures which may dilute possible age-related differences. In contrast, studies such as that of M’bondoukwé and colleagues, conducted in more diverse populations, including children and the elderly, have reported age-related differences in infection susceptibility. Furthermore, the absence of a strong seasonal effect may be due to year-round exposure risks, stable parasite reservoirs, or behavioral patterns that do not drastically change between seasons. That is, IPIs in this population may be more influenced by other factors such as hygiene practices and socioeconomic status [46].

Overall, the findings suggest that IPIs remain an important maternal health concern in Northern Ghana, findings concerning RBCs and WBCs were observed as additional laboratory diagnosis which may indicate the presences of inflammation and can reflect an underlying gastrointestinal pathology associated with infection. Continued improvements in sanitation, antenatal screening, and public health education may help reduce the burden of infection in this population.

However, this study has some limitations. The findings may not be fully generalizable since the study was conducted in a single health facility. In addition, the diagnosis of intestinal parasitic infections relied on stool microscopy, which has limited sensitivity and cannot reliably differentiate some closely related parasites, particularly the Entamoeba histolytica complex and certain intestinal flagellates. Despite these limitations, the study provides important baseline information on the burden of intestinal parasitic infections among pregnant women in Northern Ghana.

Conclusion

This study revealed a considerable burden of intestinal parasitic infections (IPIs) among pregnant women in Northern Ghana, with protozoan parasites, particularly E. histolytica/dispar/moshkovskii complex and G. lamblia, being the most commonly identified organisms. Co-infections were also observed, indicating ongoing exposure to multiple enteric parasites within the study population.

The findings highlight the potential public health implications of IPIs among pregnant women, particularly in settings with persistent sanitation and hygiene challenges. Stool inflammatory findings, including the presence of RBCs and WBCs, were significantly associated with infection status, suggesting underlying gastrointestinal mucosal inflammation associated with parasitic infections. In contrast, malaria status and seasonality were not significantly associated with IPIs in this study population.

The study also emphasizes the diagnostic limitations of stool microscopy in differentiating morphologically similar species such as E. histolytica dispar/moshkovskii complex, which may lead to overestimation of pathogenic infections.

Strengthening routine parasitological screening during antenatal care, improving water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) interventions, and expanding community health education may help reduce the burden of IPIs among pregnant women. Future studies incorporating molecular diagnostic techniques and environmental or behavioral risk assessments may provide deeper insights into transmission dynamics and infection patterns in the region.
