Global prevalence and guideline adherence of antibiotic prescribing in dental implant surgery: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Hossam Almadhoon, Madiha Alshayyah, Mohammed Abu Eida, Aislinn Cook, Michael Sharland, Nabeel Almotairy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis systematic review and meta-analysis synthesised global prevalence, regimens, guideline adherence, and factors associated with antibiotic prescribing in dental implant surgery.MethodsMEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science were systematically searched from January 2010 to October 2025. Dentist surveys, patient-record studies, and register-based studies were included. Prevalence was pooled using random-effects meta-analysis with Freeman-Tukey transformation; regimen and appropriateness were narratively synthesised. Risk of bias and evidence certainty were assessed using JBI and GRADE tools.ResultsThirty-nine studies (7266 dentists; 137,207 patients) were included. Pooled prevalence of routine antibiotic prescribing was 61.3% (95% CI: 53.4–68.8%; I²=96.8%) with extreme heterogeneity (24.9–88.8%). Combined pre- and post-operative regimens predominated (52.6%), typically involving 5–7-day post-operative courses. Amoxicillin was the most frequent choice (50.9%). Prescribing increased with procedural complexity, reaching 97.3% for compromised cases. Guideline-concordant prescribing was low (1–28%), with a declining temporal trend. Overall evidence certainty was very low.ConclusionsAntibiotic prescribing for implant surgery varied widely but was often high, frequently exceeding evidence-based indications. Post-operative regimens predominated despite no demonstrated benefit over a single pre-operative dose. Reducing unnecessary antibiotic use in implant surgery may require procedure-specific international guidelines and targeted stewardship, though more robust evidence is needed to determine effective strategies.Keywords: Dental implants, Antibiotic prophylaxis, Prescription behaviour, Practice patterns, Therapeutic use, Humans

Introduction

Antibiotic prescribing in dental implant surgery is a common clinical practice worldwide, yet robust evidence supporting routine use is limited [1]. Antibiotics during implant surgery are often prescribed to reduce post-operative infection or early implant failure [2], [3], [4]; however, several systematic reviews of randomised clinical trials comparing prophylaxis with no prophylaxis in healthy patients generally show no consistent or clinically meaningful benefit [1], [5], [6]. In reviews that reported a significant reduction in early implant failure, a single pre-operative prophylactic dose was found to be sufficient compared with no antibiotic, whereas evidence that post-operative courses provide additional benefit remains limited [7], [8].

International and national clinical guidelines generally discourage routine antibiotic use for uncomplicated implant placement or recommend limited prophylaxis only in specific circumstances [9], [10]. Nevertheless, prescribing practices in real-world settings often extend beyond these recommendations. For example, a survey of different European dentists showed that routine antibiotic prophylaxis for implant surgery in healthy patients is common, with prescribing rates exceeding 60% in several countries [11]. Prolonged post-operative antibiotic use and combined pre- and post-operative regimens also remain common [11], reflecting uncertainty in clinical decision-making. Dentists often justify antibiotic use to prevent consequential outcomes, such as implant failure, alongside concerns about medico-legal risk and patient expectations, particularly in the absence of harmonised guidelines [4].

The risks associated with unnecessary antibiotic prescribing are well established. Excessive or prolonged antibiotic use can lead to adverse drug reactions and opportunistic infections such as Clostridioides difficile colitis [12], [13]. Critically, it also fuels the emergence of antimicrobial resistance, a global health concern [14], since dentistry is a significant contributor to outpatient antibiotic consumption [15], [16]. Inappropriate use is widespread; one U.S. analysis found that 80.9% of antibiotics prescribed by dentists for prophylaxis in cases involving acute conditions were not guideline-concordant [17]. This represents a missed opportunity for antibiotic stewardship in dentistry.

Current knowledge regarding global antibiotic use in implant surgery remains limited, with no consolidated estimate of the frequency or patterns of antibiotic use in implant dentistry. The true extent of inappropriate prescribing, defined as antibiotic use without guideline support or clear clinical indication, remains uncertain. Accordingly, a comprehensive synthesis is required to elucidate global prescribing patterns, identify patient groups or procedural contexts associated with particularly high antibiotic use, and assess the degree to which real-world practices align with established guidelines. Such an evidence base would help identify priority areas for targeted antibiotic stewardship efforts in implant dentistry. Therefore, the primary aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to estimate the global prevalence of antibiotic prescribing in dental implant surgery. The secondary aims were to 1) stratify antibiotic prescribing habits based on self-reported dentist surveys, or patient record- and registry-based studies; 2) characterise prescribing patterns including antibiotic choice, dosage, timing, and duration; 3) assess the proportion of prescriptions that are non-adherent to established guidelines; and 4) examine factors associated with increased antibiotic use.

Materials and Methods

Protocol and registration

The protocol for this systematic review and meta-analysis was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420251270822). The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the latest updates of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Table S1) [18].

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility was defined using the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and Study design (PICOS) framework. The population comprised dentists (including general practitioners, oral surgeons, periodontists, and other specialists) who perform dental implant surgery or patients undergoing dental implant surgery. The intervention included studies that report on the practice of antibiotic prescription in relation to dental implant surgery, including surveys of dentists' prescription habits and clinical studies reporting prescription data. Comparisons were not applicable. Outcomes of interest included prevalence of prophylaxis use, prescription appropriateness, factors (patient, dentist, organisation, country) associated with antibiotic prescription, and drug type, dose, route, and timing. Eligible study designs included cross-sectional studies, surveys of dental practitioners, clinical record audits, registry/prescription database studies, and cohort or case-control designs that report prescribing practice/prevalence.

Inclusion criteria

Studies were included if they reported prescription practices or prevalence in relation to dental implant surgery procedures.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted across three electronic databases: MEDLINE via PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science Core Collection from January 2010–15 October 2025. No restrictions were applied regarding study language or type. Search strategies combined free-text keywords and MeSH terms relating to antibiotic prescribing, dental implant surgery, and prescribing patterns, using proximity operators (Table S2). In addition, backwards and forward citation tracking of all eligible studies was performed to identify further relevant publications.

Study selection

All retrieved records were imported into Mendeley reference management software, where duplicates were removed using the duplicate removal function and subsequently verified manually. Study selection was carried out in two phases: initial screening of titles and abstracts to exclude irrelevant records, followed by full-text assessment to determine final eligibility. Both stages were independently conducted by two reviewers (M.Al. and M.Ab.). Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, with consultation from senior reviewers (H.A. and N.A.) when necessary.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently and in duplicate by two reviewers (H.A. and M.Al.) using a customised data extraction form developed specifically for this review. Prior to formal extraction, both reviewers pilot-tested the form on five included studies to ensure clarity and consistency. Extracted data included: (1) study characteristics (first author, publication year, study design, and country); (2) dentist-related factors (sample size, age and sex distribution, dental specialty, academic level, source of dental implant education, years of experience, and work sector); (3) Number of implant procedures/insertions; (4) patient-related factors (sample size, age, sex distribution, living place); (5) antibiotic prescription pattern (prevalence, prescription in relation implant surgery procedure and patients’ health status, drug type, timing, route, dose, frequency, and duration). Disagreements were resolved through consensus or consultation with a third reviewer (N.A.).

Risk of bias assessment and certainty of evidence

The risk of bias of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute’s (JBI) tools for prevalence studies [19]. A scoring system was applied to the tool questions by calculating a cumulative score for each study as follows: 1 mark was assigned for 'Yes' answers, zero mark for other responses (No or Unclear), while not applicable questions were excluded from the calculation. The percentage of positive responses (Yes questions) to the total number of applicable questions (Yes, No, or Unclear) was calculated. Based on the resultant percentage, each study was classified as low (80%–100%), moderate (51%–79%), or high (equal to or less than 50%) risk of bias. The assessment was independently performed by two reviewers (M.Al and M.Ab), with disagreements resolved through consensus or discussion with a third reviewer (H.A).

The evidence certainty concerning the prevalence of dentist-reported routine antibiotic prescribing during dental implant surgery was assessed using the GRADE framework (GRADEpro, http://gradepro.org) [20]. Assessments considered risk of bias, between-study inconsistency, indirectness, and imprecision. Domains related to the upgrading of evidence were not applied, as they are not relevant to our study. The overall certainty was classified as high, moderate, low, or very low, in accordance with the GRADE guidelines [21].

Data analysis

Quantitative and narrative syntheses were undertaken. Proportions were pooled using random-effects meta-analysis to estimate the prevalence of dentists reporting routine antibiotic prescribing for dental implant surgery. Random-effects models were selected a priori because true prevalence estimates were expected to vary across surveys owing to between-study heterogeneity [22]. Proportional meta-analyses used inverse-variance weighting with Freeman-Tukey double-arcsine transformation and restricted maximum likelihood estimation of between-study variance [23]. Pooled estimates were generated for overall routine prescribing and for prespecified subgroups, including prescribing timing (pre-operation only, post-operation only, or both pre- and post-operation), dental implant complexity (simple, multiple/complex, or compromised implant placement), specific procedures (e.g., prosthetic placement, bone augmentation, sinus lifting), and patient risk factors (e.g., cardiovascular disease, artificial joints, prior implant failure, smoking). To facilitate comparability across heterogeneous procedural descriptions, implant interventions were classified into three categories: simple placement (single or straightforward immediate implants without active infection, with or without flap elevation), multiple or complex placement (multiple or uncomplicated multi-implant cases, with or without flap elevation), and compromised placement (implant placement in the presence of active infection). Where sufficient data were available, pooled estimates were also generated at the country level. Temporal trends were explored where prescribing prevalence was reported by calendar year or where a dominant year could be assigned based on data-collection timing (≥50% of participants surveyed within a given year). Trends were summarised descriptively and, where feasible, supported by pooled estimates across predefined time periods. Geographic variation in prescribing prevalence was visualised using world maps, and temporal patterns were illustrated using bubble plots weighted by study sample size. Prescribing patterns, including antibiotic class, duration of administration, timing of pre-operative dosing, and typical dosing regimens, were also summarised descriptively. Studies reporting patient-level antibiotic use, prescribing appropriateness, and determinants of prescribing were synthesised narratively owing to heterogeneity in study design, outcome definitions, and analytic approaches. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test [24]. Publication bias was evaluated by visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s test [25]. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with two-sided tests and a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

A total of 5216 studies were identified from the database search, and 5 were obtained from the forward/backward citation screening. After de-duplication, 3170 remained for the title/abstract screening phase, where 3123 studies were excluded. Study risk of bias

The risk of bias assessment showed that six studies had a low risk of bias, 20 had a moderate risk, and 13 had a high risk (Table 1). Table S4 shows the detailed assessment of individual studies. A consistent methodological strength across most studies was the use of standardised measures to assess antibiotic prescribing, together with clear descriptions of study settings and outcomes. The most common limitations were related to sampling methodology, including non-representative or poorly described sampling frames, limited justification of sample size, reliance on predominantly descriptive analyses without adjustment for confounding, and inadequate reporting or handling of response rates, all of which limit the generalisability and robustness of the findings.

Dentists’ self-reported antibiotic prescribing habits in implant surgery cases

Antibiotic prescription prevalence

Dentists’ antibiotic prescribing prevalence for implant surgery varies across geographical locations (Fig. 2A; Table S5). While the prescribing estimate was low for France (24.9%), it was moderate for the Netherlands (43.7%), Saudi Arabia (46.0%), Jordan (49.4%), and the United Kingdom (53.1%), and higher for Spain (73.9%), Germany (74.8%), India (80.2%), and Sweden (81.3%).

Global distribution and temporal trends in routine antibiotic prescribing for dental implant surgery. (A) Country-level prevalence of dentists reporting routine antibiotic prescribing for implant surgery. (B) Temporal distribution of study-level estimates of routine antibiotic prescribing, with bubble size proportional to sample size.

Over the past 15 years, the prevalence of antibiotic prescribing for implant surgery has shown an overall declining trend, although prescribing rates remain highly variable (Fig. 2B). Pooled prevalence was highest in studies from 2008 to 2012 (77.6%, 95% CI 49.2–96.5), decreased in studies published between 2015 and 2022 (58.9%, 49.5–68.0), and was lower in the most recent study from 2025 (37.0%, 31.7–42.5) [54].

The survey results indicated that 61.3% of respondents routinely prescribe antibiotics for implant surgery (95% CI: 53.4–68.8%, 23 studies; Fig. 3). The dentists’ antibiotic prescription timing in relation to the implant surgery procedure was also variable, where 16.8% reported prescribing only pre-implant surgery operation, 22.6% post-operation, and 52.6% reported prescribing both pre- and post-operation (Table 4).

The surveyed dentists reported certain clinical scenarios where antibiotics are often prescribed (Table 4). They reported higher antibiotic prescription when the case complexity increases, where 97.3% of dentists prescribed antibiotics for compromised cases (e.g., in the presence of infection) compared to 71.6% for simple cases. Higher antibiotic prescribing was also reported on implant surgery cases requiring bone augmentation or sinus lifting procedures (80.7%–81.2%). Respondents further reported higher prescribing for established medical risk conditions (e.g., 73.8% for cardiovascular disease, 66.4% for immunodeficiency) or discretionary risk factors such as smoking (57.4%) and poor oral hygiene (32.5%). However, a lower antibiotic prescription was reported for dental implant prosthetic and impression-taking procedures (2.7%–2.8%).

Antibiotic prescription regimens

Antibiotic regimens reported in dentist-based survey studies are shown in Fig. 4. Amoxicillin was by far the most frequently used antibiotic (50.9%), followed by amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (34.7%), with other types of antibiotic agents prescribed infrequently (Fig. 4A). Antibiotic administration pre-implant surgery often began before the day of surgery, with 51.6% of prescriptions initiated 1–2 days prior to implant placement, compared with 47.4% administered 1 h before or immediately preceding the procedure (Fig. 4B).

Post-operative antibiotic course duration was typically prolonged, where most studies reported durations beyond 4 days, with 5 and 7 days being mostly followed (33.4% and 38.2%, respectively) (Fig. 4C–D). Considerable variation in dosing was observed. For amoxicillin, regimens of 500 mg and 2000 mg were most reported (37.3% and 22.4%, respectively), whereas prescriptions for amoxicillin–clavulanic acid were dominated by the 875/125 mg formulation (53%) (Fig. 4E).

Dentists-related factors for higher prescription

Ten studies assessed associations between dentists’ sociodemographic characteristics and antibiotic prescribing in implant surgery, with highly heterogeneous findings. Several survey-based studies reported no significant associations between prescribing patterns and dentists’ age, sex, academic qualification, years of experience, implant volume, speciality level, or practice setting [28], [30], [41], [46].

Conversely, dental practice setting and specialty level were associated with dentists' prescribing habits in other studies. Private practitioners prescribed antibiotics more frequently than those in academic or public settings, particularly for pre- or post-operative use [39], [56]. Differences were also observed across dental specialities, with lower post-operative prescribing among periodontists and more frequent use of amoxicillin–clavulanate among maxillofacial surgeons pre- and post-operatively [45]. In a multinational European survey, male dentists had significantly higher odds of routine implant-related antibiotic prescribing, while years since graduation and implant annual volume showed no association [11].

Evidence regarding clinical experience and education was inconsistent. Greater experience was associated with higher prescribing in some studies [26], [45], [47], whereas participation in antibiotic-related training and engagement with scientific literature were associated with lower prescribing, independent of academic qualification [47].

Patient record and registry evidence of dental implant antibiotic prophylaxis

Evidence from patient record- and registry-based studies indicated that antibiotic prophylaxis is commonly prescribed in implant-related care, with usage varying by procedure type and timing. In Sweden, prophylaxis for bone augmentation procedures with implant surgery decreased from 88.5% (2010–2011) to 73% (2014–2015) [60]. A recent study showed lower overall prophylaxis use for implant placement (45.6%), but substantially higher rates for complex procedures, including bone augmentation alone (82.9%), closed sinus lift with bone augmentation (95.5%), and immediate implant placement with bone augmentation (92.3%) [57]. Similarly, a 2022 German study reported 87.6% overall prophylaxis for implant surgery, rising to 96.9% with bone augmentation and remaining high at 82% without it [62]. A multicountry study (Poland, Bulgaria, Greece, Portugal) from 2015 to 2016 showed frequent antibiotic prescription both pre- (54.3%–58.4%) and post-implant surgery (91.2%–96.3%), with post-operative use consistently higher [59].

Appropriateness of antibiotic prescribing in implant surgery

Evidence regarding antibiotic prophylaxis appropriateness for implant surgery, derived from dentist surveys and patient records, was limited, heterogeneous, and variably defined across studies.

Survey studies indicated high appropriateness but used broad criteria. Most dentists either withheld antibiotics for healthy implant cases or prescribed them for high-risk patients. In Italy, 35.2% of dentists reported no antibiotic prescribing for healthy patients, 75.1% for high-risk patients, and 55.1% made overall guideline-concordant decisions [49]. Similarly, in France, 75.1% of dentists did not prescribe antibiotics for healthy implant patients [48].

Conversely, patient record-based studies showed low guideline concordance. In Sweden, concordant prophylaxis for bone augmentation (by type, dose, and timing) was 28.2% in 2010–2011 and 25.3% in 2014–2015 [60]. A Lebanese study [58] found only 1% guideline-adherent prophylaxis in dental implant consultations. US registry analyses reported that only ∼7% of antibiotic prescriptions for dental implant patients with specified cardiac comorbidities were appropriate [17], [61].

Publication bias, heterogeneity, and certainty of evidence

Visual inspection of the funnel plot for overall antibiotic prescribing suggested mild asymmetry, but Egger’s regression test showed no evidence of small-study effects (p = 0.399), indicating no statistically significant publication bias (Figure S1). High heterogeneity (I2 >50%) was consistently observed across the overall and subgroup analyses, indicating considerable between-study variability in reported antibiotic prescribing practices.

The certainty of evidence for all prevalence outcomes of dentist-reported routine antibiotic prescribing for dental implant procedures was very low due to serious risk of bias, very serious between-study inconsistency (heterogeneity), and serious/very serious imprecision (wide 95% CI) in some outcomes (Table S6).

Discussion

Antibiotic prescribing for dental implant surgery was common across the included studies, though study-level estimates ranged widely from 24.9% to 88.8%, reflecting marked heterogeneity in prescribing culture, guideline specificity, and practice context. The overall pooled prevalence (61.3%) should therefore be understood as a descriptive summary of a highly variable evidence base, rather than a stable or generalisable estimate; the extreme between-study heterogeneity (I²=96.8%) and very low certainty of evidence preclude definitive conclusions about the true global rate. What is consistent across several included studies, however, is that prescribing was frequently high regardless of procedural complexity or patient risk. This is in contrast to the clinical evidence, where three recent systematic reviews of randomised trials found no significant benefit of routine prophylaxis in preventing early implant failure in healthy patients [5], [6], [8]. Moreover, where any benefit was observed, a single pre-operative dose may suffice, with post-operative prescription offering no additional protection. This study is the first to report global antibiotic prescribing behaviour in relation to implant surgery procedure, extending the descriptive evidence from Bernabeu-Mira and colleagues [2] into a pooled prevalence estimate in addition to a narrative synthesis of appropriateness, regimen patterns, and patient-level data across 39 studies, 7266 dentists, and 137,207 patients.

The gap between evidence and clinical practice is partly explained by the absence of harmonised, procedure-specific international guidance. While the American Dental Association and the Faculty of General Dental Practice UK have issued guidelines on antibiotic prescribing in healthy dental patients [9], [10], neither addresses implant surgery procedures, and neither the International Team for Implantology nor the European Association for Osseointegration has produced universal recommendations. The qualitative literature consistently identifies medico-legal concerns, perceived patient expectations, and habit as the primary drivers, largely independent of clinical knowledge [4]. This is reinforced by Becker et al.'s study involving a 33-country European survey [11], in which male sex independently predicted higher prescribing odds while experience and implant volume did not, pointing to attitudinal rather than competence-related drivers. Notably, engagement with antibiotic-related education was associated with lower prescribing regardless of qualification level [47], suggesting these barriers are modifiable, but not through guideline dissemination alone. These results were confirmed in a recent systematic review with high-evidence certainty that audit with personalised feedback and behaviour-change messaging reduces inappropriate prescribing, whereas guideline dissemination alone was ineffective [63].

The results of the current study suggest that prescribing patterns appear to differ depending on the clinical scenario. Prescribing prevalence was higher, plausibly reflecting greater clinical severity, reaching 97.3% for compromised cases involving active infection and 80–81% for bone augmentation and sinus lifting. This compares with the 71.6% rate for straightforward implant placement in healthy patients, for which no comparable justification seems to exist. The common rationale, prevention of implant failure, is undermined by the fact that early failure is driven predominantly by factors that antibiotic prophylaxis is unlikely to modify. Smoking increases the risk of implant failure more than doubles (OR 2.59, rising to 5.90 in the maxilla) [64], a history of periodontitis carries a hazard ratio of approximately 2.0 [65], and bone quality and volume, implant positioning, bruxism, surgical technique, and peri-implant maintenance all contribute independently [3], [66], [67]. That post-operative antibiotic continuation confers no benefit over a single pre-operative dose, as evidenced by Romandini et al.’s network meta-analysis [7], further undermines the rationale for extended prophylactic regimens.

The antibiotic regimen findings shown in the present study highlight the prescribing frequency problem. Despite a single pre-operative dose being the evidence-based standard when prophylaxis is indicated [7], [8], 52.6% of surveyed dentists self-reported combined pre- and post-operative prescribing, with post-operative durations of five to seven days predominating. There is no well-established pharmacological basis for this, and the scale of resulting antibiotic exposure matters: dentistry accounts for approximately 10% of global outpatient antibiotic consumption [16]. Drug selection, amoxicillin (50.9%) and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (34.7%), is broadly appropriate, but selecting the right agent while prescribing the wrong regimen for the wrong indication does not constitute adequate stewardship.

A critical methodological limitation of survey-derived estimates is that self-reported prescribing is not equivalent to observed behaviour, given susceptibility to social desirability, hypothetical scenarios, and non-response biases. Patient-record and registry-based studies, by contrast, capture actual dispensing events and permit direct assessment of guideline concordance against defined criteria, advantages that substitute their inherent limitations of incomplete clinical records and coding variability. Critically, patient-level data also permit direct assessment of guideline concordance against defined criteria, whereas survey-based appropriateness estimates tend to rely on broader self-assessment and cannot be independently verified. Accordingly, the patient-record and registry-based evidence synthesised in this review provides a counterpoint to the self-reported survey results. The findings of patient-record and registry-based studies in the current review found that guideline-concordant prophylaxis was recorded in only 1% of implant consultations in Lebanon [58], 25–28% of bone augmentation cases in Sweden [60], and approximately 7% of implant-related prescriptions for cardiac patients in the United States [17], [61], all settings with existing national guidance. The problem is therefore not an absence of standards, but an absence of standards that are specific, credible, and consistently applied. The wide variation in survey-reported prescribing, from 24.9% in France to 80.2% in India and 81.3% in Sweden, is unlikely to reflect genuine differences in patient risk and more plausibly reflects differences in guideline specificity, prescribing culture, and stewardship targeting. It should also be noted that these are self-reported estimates and may not fully correspond to actual prescription rates in each country. Notably, high rates in Scandinavia and Germany, despite well-developed stewardship infrastructure and low overall antibiotic consumption, suggest existing programmes have not reached this procedure-specific context. A declining temporal trend in dentist prescribing, from 77.6% in 2008–2012 studies to 37% in the most recent 2025 estimate, is consistent with gradual cultural change, though it is based on sparse data from a single recent study [54].

This prescribing pattern is not limited to implant surgery; a systematic review and meta-analysis of dentist surveys reported that around one in five dentists prescribe antibiotics for pulpitis, a condition managed primarily by operative treatment alone [68]. What distinguishes implant surgery is that a conditional rationale does exist for specific scenarios, making the appropriate-inappropriate distinction more nuanced. The evidence supports withholding antibiotics in healthy patients undergoing straightforward placement, while being more permissive for complex procedures involving bone augmentation, active infection, or poorly controlled systemic conditions such as diabetes or immunosuppression [6], [69], [70]. In clinical practice, however, prescribing does not follow this distinction, as rates remain high across simple cases, regimens exceed what even permissive guidelines would endorse, and adherence to defined appropriateness criteria is strikingly low wherever it has been measured.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of the current meta-analysis. Evidence certainty was very low across all prevalence outcomes, driven by serious risk of bias, extreme between-study heterogeneity, and imprecision in several subgroup estimates. Survey data likely underestimates true prescribing rates through social desirability effects. Additionally, definitions of guideline adherence vary across studies, limiting the synthesis of appropriateness data. Geographic coverage is also weighted toward Europe and high-income settings, and the absence of patient-level outcome data means that the clinical burden of the current prescribing pattern cannot be directly quantified. Future research should move beyond prevalence surveys toward prospective designs linking prescribing decisions to clinical outcomes in defined patient subgroups, enabling more precise estimates of both the harm from current practice and the gains achievable through targeted stewardship.

Conclusion

Despite substantial variability across settings and very low certainty of evidence due to extreme heterogeneity, antibiotic prescribing for implant surgery was frequently high relative to available evidence. Guideline adherence was poor among studies that assessed it, and predominant extended post-operative regimens likely led to significant, though unquantifiable, unnecessary antibiotic exposure. These findings suggest that reducing unnecessary antibiotic use requires procedure-specific international guidance, targeted stewardship for implant practitioners, and audit-based behavioural interventions, although more robust evidence is needed to determine effective strategies.
