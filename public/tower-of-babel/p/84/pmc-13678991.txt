Age-dependent effects of TRPA1 deficiency on pain and osteoarthritis pathology following joint injury in mice
Authors: Karsyn N Bailey, Erika Barboza Prado Lopes, Farshid Guilak, Timothy M Griffin
Published: 2026

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a leading cause of disability worldwide, characterized by progressive degenerative changes in articular cartilage and other tissues within the synovial joint, leading to chronic pain and dysfunction [1]. Although the structural changes that develop during OA progression are well described, the mechanisms underlying joint pain and their relationship to tissue-level structural changes remain incompletely understood. Pain frequently emerges early in disease progression and is not always proportional to the extent of cartilage degeneration, suggesting that distinct molecular pathways contribute to nociception in OA independent of structural damage [2–6]. Furthermore, since pain is a primary motivator of surgical intervention in end-stage OA, elucidating its relationship with joint pathology may help identify novel, non-surgical options for patients who may otherwise not be ideal candidates for joint replacement.

The synovium, joint capsule, and infrapatellar fat pad (IFP) are richly innervated by peptidergic C-fiber nociceptors that express a variety of transmembrane ion channels involved in joint pain, including members of the transient receptor potential (TRP) family [7–12]. These channels, along with other key mechanosensitive and nociceptive sensors such as Nav1.7, Nav1.8, and Piezo2 [13–16], act as cellular sensors for mechanical, thermal, chemical, and oxidative stimuli [17–21]. Among the TRP channels, the transient receptor potential ankyrin 1 (TRPA1) channel is particularly sensitive to oxidative stress [22]. TRPA1 activation leads to cation influx, membrane depolarization, and the release of pro-inflammatory neuropeptides such as substance P and calcitonin gene-related peptide, resulting in pain-related behavior and neurogenic inflammation [7, 23–28].

Recent human studies have identified TRPA1 as a key mediator linking inflammation, oxidative stress, and matrix degradation in OA. TRPA1 is functionally expressed in human OA chondrocytes, synoviocytes, and sensory neurons and is upregulated by inflammatory mediators including interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-17 (IL-17), lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and resistin [8–10, 29]. Its activation increases intracellular calcium influx and upregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6), matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-1, MMP-3, MMP-13), and prostaglandin E₂, all of which contribute to cartilage breakdown and synovial inflammation [8–10]. Furthermore, TRPA1 expression is increased in fibrotic synovial and capsular tissues in end-stage OA, implicating this channel in tissue remodeling and joint contracture [30].

Preclinical models support a causal role for TRPA1 in OA pathogenesis: both genetic deletion and pharmacologic inhibition of TRPA1 reduce pain behaviors, inflammation, and cartilage degeneration in chemically-driven rodent models of OA [7, 24–28, 31–34]. TRPA1 can be activated by environmental irritants such as allyl isothiocyanate [19] and endogenous pro-oxidant molecules such as hydrogen peroxide and 4-hydroxynonenal [27, 35–37]. Emerging evidence indicates that sensory nerve activity itself may influence the course of OA. Clinical trials of nerve growth factor (NGF) inhibitors, which provide potent analgesia, have reported cases of rapidly progressive OA and increased joint damage [38–40], suggesting that suppressing nociceptive signaling can alter joint loading and structural disease progression. These observations highlight the complex interplay between pain pathways and cartilage health and underscore the importance of understanding how analgesic mechanisms affect joint biomechanics. Together, these findings suggest that TRPA1 integrates inflammatory and nociceptive signaling in OA and may represent a promising therapeutic target. However, its role in post-traumatic OA and the potential interaction between TRPA1-mediated pain modulation and disease progression remain unclear.

A potential complexity in targeting TRPA1 for OA pain relief is that pain reduction could alter joint loading, potentially impacting structural disease progression. This trade-off has not been well studied in OA models. Moreover, although OA is primarily an age-related disease, the influence of aging on TRPA1-mediated pain and joint pathology is poorly understood. We therefore hypothesized that TRPA1 contributes to mechanical hypersensitivity and joint degeneration after injury and that these effects vary with age. We compared pain response, joint pathology, metabolic activity, and gait biomechanics in wildtype (WT) and TRPA1-deficient (TRPA1−/−) young and adult mice.

Materials and methods

Animals

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Oklahoma Medical Research Foundation (OMRF) and conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (protocol numbers 12–47, 12–57 and 15–58). C57BL/6J (WT) mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA), and TRPA1-/- mice were obtained from Dr. Diana Bautista (UC Berkeley). Young mice were 14 weeks old (n = 11–13 per genotype) and adult mice were 48 weeks old (n = 8 per genotype) at the time of surgery. Mice were housed in temperature-controlled rooms (20 ± 1 °C) on a 14:10 h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to chow and water.

Destabilization of the medial meniscus surgery

Unilateral OA was induced by destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) in male mice under isoflurane anesthesia, as previously described [41–44]. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, placed on a warming pad, and the surgical site was shaved and prepared with Betadine. A 3-mm longitudinal incision was made from the distal patella to the proximal tibial plateau. The joint capsule immediately medial to the patellar tendon was incised with a #15 blade and opened with micro-iris scissors. The infrapatellar fat pad was bluntly dissected to expose the intercondylar region and visualize the meniscotibial ligament of the medial meniscus. The medial meniscotibial ligament was transected with microsurgical scissors, a microsurgical knife, or a #11 blade directed proximolaterally to destabilize the medial meniscus. The joint capsule was closed with continuous 8 − 0 tapered Vicryl suture, the subcutaneous layer with 7 − 0 cutting Vicryl, and the skin with tissue adhesive. Buprenorphine was administered perioperatively at 0.09 mg/kg intraperitoneally. The contralateral knee was left intact and served as an unoperated control. Mice were monitored daily for wound healing and signs of distress, and body weight was monitored weekly.

Pain behavior assessment

Mechanical sensitivity was evaluated weekly using the up-down staircase von Frey method to determine the 50% paw withdrawal threshold [23, 45]. Prior to testing, mice were habituated to the testing room, polished metal mesh floor, and Plexiglas cubicle for 2 days (30 min per day). On test days, mice were placed in test cubicles for 30 min prior to von Frey filament application. Pre-surgery, filaments with target forces ranging from 0.07 to 4.0 g were applied perpendicular to the plantar surface of the hind paw following the up-down staircase protocol. Post-surgery, filaments with target forces ranging from 0.04 to 2.0 g were used. The threshold force required to elicit paw withdrawal (approximating the 50% withdrawal force) was determined twice on each hind paw and averaged on each testing day, with sequential measurements separated by at least 5 min. Both ipsilateral (DMM) and contralateral (control) hind paws were tested, with the experimenter blinded to animal genotype. This assay measures distal mechanical sensitivity and was not used as a direct measure of knee-joint nociceptive threshold.

Gait biomechanics

At 7 weeks post-surgery, wheel running biomechanics were evaluated in young mice using a custom force-instrumented running wheel (FRW) to measure gait kinematics and gait kinetics, specifically the tangential (i.e., braking and propulsive) and normal (i.e., body weight support) foot-rung reaction forces as previously described [46, 47]. Spontaneous running distance, speed, and gait biomechanics were recorded while animals were individually housed in a custom cage containing the FRW from 5 PM until 9 AM the following morning. The light cycle and environmental conditions of the testing room matched the standard housing conditions. The FRW contains a single instrumented rung; therefore, a trial was defined as an individual foot-rung contact captured during voluntary running when the force on the instrumented rung exceeded the acquisition threshold. Trials were excluded if the wheel was substantially accelerating or decelerating, if the contact was from a forelimb, or if the strike was not cleanly isolated (e.g., paw contact with multiple rungs or slipping). Ipsilateral and contralateral hindlimb forces were evaluated based on the average of 12–13 valid trials per limb that passed these quality-control procedures for consistent-speed running and individual foot-rung contact. FRW testing was performed only in young mice because voluntary running in the FRW system declines with age and was expected to be insufficient or highly variable in older mice.

Intra-articular mustard oil experiments

Independent experiments on WT mice were included to determine whether acute intra-articular activation of TRPA1 in the knee using mustard oil produces measurable alterations in voluntary running and FRW gait biomechanics. Twelve-week-old WT mice (male, n = 4; female, n = 3) were individually housed in the FRW system for two sequential nights. The first night served as the pre-injection baseline, and mice received an intra-articular knee injection of 5 µl of allyl isothiocyanate (i.e., 10 mM mustard oil; Sigma), a specific, well-described agonist of TRPA1 [48] or saline prior to gait biomechanics testing, as described above.

Voluntary cage activity and energy expenditure

Spontaneous cage activity and energy expenditure were assessed in young mice before DMM surgery and again 5–6 weeks after surgery. Cage activity was measured using force-sensing platforms placed beneath the home cage, and metabolic activity was assessed by indirect calorimetry, as previously described [49]. Data were analyzed separately during the light and dark phases to account for circadian differences in mouse activity. Ambulatory activity and energy expenditure were averaged within each phase and compared with pre-surgical baseline values. Dark-phase values were emphasized because mice are most active during this period.

Histology

At 8 weeks post-surgery, mice were euthanized, and both knees were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified, and embedded in paraffin as previously described [50]. Coronal Sect. (6 μm) were stained with hematoxylin/Safranin-O/Fast Green. OA severity was graded using the Modified Mankin scoring system for cartilage degeneration, and osteophyte severity scores were assigned for each compartment [51, 52]. Sections were evaluated by two experienced graders blinded to genotype and limb status (DMM versus control). Representative images were selected to reflect the mean Modified Mankin score for each genotype.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Prism 11 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Data are presented as mean ± SEM. For von Frey withdrawal-threshold data, the primary outcome was the 50% paw-withdrawal threshold. Normality was assessed using q-q plots of the von Frey data and model residuals. Because q-q plots did not show substantial deviations from normality, von Frey withdrawal-threshold data from young mice were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with genotype and time as factors, with repeated measures for time. Post hoc comparisons were corrected for multiple comparisons where applicable. Gait, cage activity, and energy-expenditure outcomes were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA with Tukey post hoc tests. Histologic scores were analyzed using two-way ANOVA without repeated measures with Tukey post hoc tests. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Loss of TRPA1 reduces mechanical hypersensitivity after injury in young mice

Mechanical sensitivity was assessed weekly following DMM in 14-week-old male WT and TRPA1-/- mice (Fig. 1). Paw-withdrawal thresholds decreased after surgery in both WT and TRPA1-/- mice, consistent with increased mechanical sensitivity after joint injury. In the injured DMM limb, TRPA1-/- mice exhibited significantly higher paw-withdrawal thresholds than WT mice at 1 and 3 weeks post-injury (p < 0.05) with trends toward higher paw-withdrawal thresholds at 6 weeks (p = 0.05) (Fig. 1A). In the uninjured control limb, withdrawal thresholds were significantly higher in TRPA1-/- mice at 2 weeks post-injury compared with WT mice (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1B). Graphical representation of change in withdrawal threshold normalized to pre-surgery baseline thresholds can be seen in Supplemental Figs. 1A and 1B.

TRPA1 deficiency reduces early mechanical hypersensitivity after injury in young mice. Mechanical sensitivity was assessed weekly following DMM in 14-week-old male WT and TRPA1−/− mice (n = 11–13 per genotype). 50% paw withdrawal thresholds are shown for injured (DMM) and contralateral control limbs over 7 weeks (A, B). TRPA1−/− mice exhibited higher withdrawal thresholds at 1 and 3 weeks in the injured limb and at 2 weeks in the control limb compared with WT mice. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Tukey post hoc testing. *p < 0.05 vs. WT at corresponding timepoint

Loss of TRPA1 exacerbates OA pathology after DMM in young mice

Eight weeks after DMM, histological analyses revealed more severe OA development in TRPA1-/- mice compared with WT controls (Fig. 2). In the medial tibial compartment, osteophyte severity was greater (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2C) and Mankin scores were significantly higher in TRPA1-/- mice (Fig. 2F, p < 0.05) in the injured limb. No genotype differences were observed in the medial femur (Fig. 2E) or the lateral compartments (Fig. 2G and H). Representative Safranin-O/Fast Green–stained sections illustrate greater cartilage erosion and osteophyte formation in injured TRPA1-/- knees compared with WT (Fig. 2A and B).

Modified Mankin scores for whole joint, medial femur, medial tibia, lateral femur, and lateral tibia compartments (D-H). TRPA1−/− mice demonstrated increased osteophyte severity and higher Modified Mankin scores in the medial tibial compartment compared with WT mice (F). Data are presented as mean ± SEM. TRPA1 deficiency alters gait biomechanics after DMM

Gait biomechanics were evaluated in young mice using a force-instrumented running wheel to assess joint loading and locomotor patterns (Fig. 3). TRPA1-/- mice exhibited significantly more negative peak braking forces in both the injured and uninjured limbs compared with WT animals (Fig. 3D, p < 0.05). Additionally, injury resulted in gait asymmetry in TRPA1-/- mice, with significantly more negative peak braking force on the injured side compared with the uninjured side (Fig. 3D). TRPA1-/- mice demonstrated a trend toward increased contact time in both limbs (p = 0.05) (Fig. 3C). Mean peak propulsive force was significantly lower in the injured limb of TRPA1-/- mice compared with the injured limb of WT mice (Fig. 3E), and TRPA1-/- mice ran significantly slower than WT mice independent of injury (Fig. 3A). Force-trace profiles showed asymmetrical gait biomechanics between the injured and uninjured limbs of TRPA1-/- mice (Fig. 3F), whereas WT animals maintained more balanced load distribution across limbs (Fig. 3G).

Representative force traces from WT and TRPA1−/− mic, with dashed lines representing +/- 1SD for the control limb force trace, and dotted lines representing +/- 1SD for the DMM limb force trace. Data represent the mean of 12–13 valid trials per limb per animal and are shown as mean ± SEM. Two-way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc testing. *p < 0.05

Intra-articular mustard oil alters voluntary running and gait biomechanics

In WT mice, intra-articular mustard oil injection to activate local TRPA1 in the knee altered voluntary running and gait biomechanics (Supplemental Fig. 2). Mustard oil injection reduced voluntary wheel running and produced asymmetric gait biomechanics in the FRW, including lower normal force impulse, lower maximum propulsive force, reduced foot-rung contact time, and reduced duty factor in the affected limb compared with the contralateral limb.

Voluntary cage activity and energy expenditure after DMM in young mice

To determine whether genotype differences in voluntary activity contributed to altered joint loading, spontaneous cage activity and energy expenditure were assessed before and after DMM surgery (Supplemental Fig. 3). During the dark phase, WT mice demonstrated a reduction in ambulatory activity (p = 0.05) and significant reduction in energy expenditure (p < 0.05) following DMM compared with pre-surgical levels. In contrast, TRPA1−/− mice did not exhibit a reduction in dark-phase activity or energy expenditure after surgery. No significant differences were observed during the light phase.

Mechanical sensitivity after DMM in adult mice is not significantly altered by TRPA1 deficiency

Mechanical sensitivity was assessed weekly following DMM in 48-week-old WT and TRPA1-/- mice (Fig. 4). In the injured DMM limb of adult mice, there were no significant genotype differences in withdrawal thresholds in TRPA1-/- mice when compared with WT mice at any time point (Fig. 4A). In the uninjured control limb, TRPA1-/- mice demonstrated significantly higher withdrawal thresholds at 2 weeks post-injury (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4B).

TRPA1−/− mice show trends toward reduced control-limb hypersensitivity at 2 and 3 weeks compared with WT mice. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. OA development in adult mice is independent of TRPA1

At 8 weeks post-DMM, histologic evaluation of adult mice showed no significant genotype differences in cartilage degeneration or osteophyte formation (Fig. 5). Quantitative Mankin scores for both the medial and lateral compartments were comparable between WT and TRPA1-/- mice (Fig. 5D, E, F, G and H). Representative sections show similar cartilage preservation and osteophyte morphology across genotypes (Fig. 5A and B). In contrast to the young cohort, TRPA1 deficiency did not alter the cartilage degeneration following DMM.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the role of the TRPA1 ion channel in mechanical sensitivity and joint degeneration using a mechanical instability model of OA in young and adult mice. In the DMM model of OA, loss of TRPA1 in young mice attenuated early post-injury mechanical hypersensitivity but exacerbated OA pathology. In contrast, in older mice, TRPA1 deficiency did not significantly alter OA pathology or withdrawal thresholds, although trends toward genotype differences were observed at select timepoints. These results highlight an age-dependent interaction between TRPA1-mediated sensory signaling and OA disease progression.

Our findings in young mice are consistent with previous studies of inflammatory and chemically induced arthritis, in which TRPA1 blockade or genetic deletion reduced mechanical hypersensitivity [7, 31–33]. TRPA1 is abundantly expressed in peptidergic nociceptive C-fibers innervating the knee [7–12, 53] and is activated by inflammatory mediators, reactive oxygen species, and mechanical stimuli [17–22]. Human OA tissues also show upregulation of TRPA1 in chondrocytes and synoviocytes, driven by IL-1β, IL-17, LPS, and resistin [8–10, 29]. Activation of TRPA1 in these cells triggers calcium influx and increases expression of IL-6, MMPs, and prostaglandin E₂, amplifying inflammation and catabolism within the joint [8–10]. Following DMM surgery, TRPA1-/- mice maintained higher paw-withdrawal thresholds during the early post-injury period, supporting a role for TRPA1 in mechanical hypersensitivity during the acute phase of OA. Because sensitivity was measured at the plantar hind paw rather than directly at the knee, these behavioral outcomes are interpreted as distal mechanical sensitivity associated with joint injury. The bilateral reduction in withdrawal thresholds observed after unilateral DMM may reflect secondary hypersensitivity, systemic effects of surgery, or stress-related changes; sham-operated controls would be needed to distinguish among these possibilities. Prior work in the DMM model has similarly reported decreased von Frey withdrawal thresholds in both hind paws after DMM, but not sham surgery, with changes apparent as early as 14 days postoperatively [54]. Taken together, prior studies and our results indicate that TRPA1 contributes to neuronal and non-neuronal pathways relevant to early OA.

Paradoxically, despite reduced mechanical sensitivity, TRPA1-/- mice exhibited more severe OA pathology, including significantly higher osteophyte scores and cartilage degeneration in the injured medial compartment. One potential explanation is that reduced nociceptive feedback in TRPA1-/- mice led to altered joint loading, as suggested by their more negative peak braking forces during running. In addition, WT mice demonstrated reduced voluntary activity and energy expenditure following DMM, whereas TRPA1-/- mice maintained activity levels comparable to baseline (Supplemental Fig. 3). Maintenance of activity in the setting of reduced mechanical sensitivity may have contributed to altered joint loading patterns and increased structural degeneration in TRPA1-/- mice. Intra-articular mustard oil experiments further support the relationship between knee TRPA1 activation and gait biomechanics (Supplemental Fig. 2), although these acute agonist experiments do not replace direct knee-joint nociceptive threshold testing in the DMM model. Even subtle changes in load distribution can accelerate cartilage wear in unstable joints. Similar trade-offs between analgesia and accelerated joint degeneration have been reported for other pain-modulating interventions [4, 55], underscoring the complexity of targeting nociceptors in OA and the importance of preserving protective sensory feedback. Beyond pain perception, joint-innervating sensory fibers provide physiologic mechanosensory and proprioceptive input that helps regulate neuromuscular coordination, dynamic joint stabilization, and load distribution. Disruption of these protective neural pathways may therefore contribute to altered gait mechanics and increased structural degeneration observed in TRPA1-/- mice.

In older mice, TRPA1 deficiency did not significantly alter withdrawal thresholds or cartilage pathology, although trends toward genotype differences were observed at select timepoints, suggesting that age-related changes in sensory or tissue responses blunt the contribution of TRPA1 to disease progression. Age itself appeared to attenuate control-limb hypersensitivity in both genotypes compared with young mice, potentially due to age-related changes in nociceptor density, conduction, or central processing. These results suggest that the contribution of TRPA1 to OA-associated sensitivity and pathology is modulated by age-related alterations in joint tissue biology and sensory processing. However, baseline withdrawal thresholds differed between young and adult cohorts, and therefore, age-related comparisons should be interpreted with these limitations in mind.

Collectively, our results indicate that TRPA1 contributes to OA-associated mechanical sensitivity, particularly during the early post-injury phase, and that its inhibition could provide analgesic benefit. Our results align with human studies demonstrating upregulated TRPA1 expression in OA joint tissues [8–10, 29, 30] and extend those observations by showing that the channel’s role in sensitivity and pathology depends on age and joint stability. Pharmacologic TRPA1 antagonists have shown preclinical efficacy in reducing pain-related behavior and inflammation [7, 31]. However, the concurrent worsening of structural pathology in young TRPA1-/- mice raises caution regarding long-term TRPA1 blockade in patients with early joint injury or instability. The absence of a deleterious effect in older mice on joint pathology suggests that age and disease stage may be critical considerations in evaluating TRPA1-targeted therapies for OA.

In interpreting the findings of this study, several potential limitations should be considered. The behavioral assessments relied on plantar von Frey testing, which measures distal mechanical sensitivity rather than knee-joint nociceptive thresholds directly. Although the supplemental mustard oil experiments support the ability of intra-articular TRPA1 activation to alter voluntary running and gait biomechanics, these acute agonist experiments were performed in separate cohorts and cannot fully establish the source of mechanical sensitivity after DMM. The absence of sham-operated controls also limits interpretation of contralateral limb hypersensitivity, as these changes could reflect secondary hypersensitivity after unilateral joint injury, systemic effects of surgery, or environmental stress. In addition, behavioral testing was limited to the first 7 weeks after DMM, and histologic OA grading was performed at a single post-injury timepoint; therefore, this study primarily captures early post-injury sensitivity and established structural pathology at 8 weeks but may not reflect later pain-related outcomes or transient inflammatory changes associated with TRPA1 signaling. The use of global TRPA1 knockout mice also prevents distinction between neuronal and non-neuronal TRPA1 effects and may include developmental compensatory changes, which could be addressed in future studies using pharmacologic inhibition or inducible, cell-specific TRPA1 deletion. Finally, biomechanical analyses were limited to voluntary running at a single timepoint in young male mice and may not fully represent habitual joint loading patterns, age-related differences, or potential sex-specific effects on TRPA1 function and OA outcomes.

Future work should evaluate the role of TRPA1 in OA pathology under varying mechanical loading conditions, investigate its contribution to central and peripheral sensitization, and assess therapeutic TRPA1 blockade [56] in age- and sex-diverse cohorts. Longitudinal behavioral testing, direct knee nociceptive assays, sham-operated controls, and molecular approaches will help clarify the mechanisms linking TRPA1-mediated sensory signaling and OA disease progression.

Conclusions

TRPA1 deletion reduced early post-injury mechanical hypersensitivity in young mice but worsened OA pathology and altered gait biomechanics, supporting a trade-off between analgesia and structural outcomes. In older mice, TRPA1 deletion did not significantly affect OA pathology or withdrawal thresholds, suggesting an age-dependent role in disease progression.
