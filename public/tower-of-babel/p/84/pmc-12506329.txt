CATATOnia treatment with transcranial direct current electrostimulation: protocol of a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled bicentric superiority study (CATATOES)
Authors: Mylène Moyal, David Attali, Alice Le Berre, Noomane Bouaziz, Anton Iftimovici, François Ramon, Adèle Henensal, Louise Dahan, Laetitia Canivet, Ghita Dadi, Clément Debacker, Maliesse Lui, Sylvain Charron, Anaëlle Alouit, Khaoussou Sylla, Rossella Letizia Mancusi, Thierry Gallarda, Arnaud Cachia, Martine Gavaret, Boris Chaumette, Catherine Oppenheim, Dominique Januel, Marion Plaze, Alexandre Haroche
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCatatonia is a severe psychomotor syndrome with high morbidity and mortality. Lorazepam and electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) are the main recommended treatments. Lorazepam-resistant catatonia accounts for about 30% of cases, particularly in neurodevelopmental disorders. ECT is crucial in these situations but faces challenges of tolerability and accessibility. Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), a promising intervention, is emerging as a non-invasive, cost-effective neurostimulation tool and has demonstrated efficacy and safety in case reports and small series of catatonic patients (N < 10). The CATATOES study aims to assess the efficacy and safety of tDCS using a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, bicentric superiority trial involving 70 patients with catatonia. Secondary objectives aim to highlight the mechanism of action of tDCS and to explore the pathophysiology of catatonia.MethodsSeventy participants with lorazepam-resistant catatonia will undergo 20 sessions of active or sham tDCS, administered twice daily at two mA for 20 min, with anodal stimulation in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and cathodal stimulation in the left temporoparietal junction (TPJ). In addition to daily clinical assessment, patients will undergo (i) structural, functional and diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), (ii) 64-channel resting state electroencephalogram (EEG), and (iii) blood test with inflammatory, neurogenesis and genetic markers, before, during and after the treatment course.Discussion Catatonia is in critical need of therapeutic improvement. We anticipate that this study will pave the way for the routine use of tDCS in the treatment of lorazepam-resistant catatonia, ultimately contributing to a reduction of morbidity, mortality, and associated costs of catatonia. Introduction

Catatonia is a severe psychomotor syndrome observed in psychiatric disorders, such as psychotic and mood disorders and other medical conditions [1, 2]including neurodevelopmental disorders [3]. Catatonia is common in psychiatric wards, with a prevalence going from 9 to 30% [1]. This syndrome is often underdiagnosed, mainly due to its heterogeneity, leading to increased morbidity and mortality [1, 4] linked to malignant catatonia and complications of dehydration, denutrition and decubitus [5].

In addition to addressing the underlying causes, lorazepam and electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) are commonly used to treat catatonia effectively [6]. While lorazepam proves effective in the majority of cases, chronic catatonia, especially in neurodevelopmental disorders and schizophrenia, may lead to lorazepam-resistant episodes or paradoxical reactions [3, 7–10]. ECT, pivotal in malignant catatonia, life-threatening situations and lorazepam-resistant episodes, faces challenges in tolerability and accessibility [10–14].

Acknowledging the need for alternative treatments to reduce the duration of untreated catatonia, associated morbimortality and hospitalisation costs, transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is emerging as a promising intervention. As a non-invasive, cost-effective neurostimulation tool, tDCS has demonstrated efficacy and safety in case reports and series of catatonic patients [15–21] with anodal stimulation in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and cathodal stimulation in the left temporoparietal junction (TPJ). This montage has already shown promising results and safety in alleviating auditory hallucinations and negative symptoms in schizophrenia [22, 23]. We have further demonstrated the effectiveness of this montage in an open-label study, demonstrating its therapeutic potential in treating catatonia associated with schizophrenia and mood disorders [15]as well as in patients with a rare genetic disorder, Phelan-McDermid syndrome and chronic catatonia [24].

Despite the promising outcomes, the mechanism of action of tDCS in catatonia remains unclear. Studies in healthy subjects and various conditions, including disorders of consciousness, have demonstrated changes in short- and long-range functional and structural connectivity post-tDCS sessions, correlating with clinical improvement [25–33]. In catatonia, structural and functional dysconnectivity has been observed across cortico-cortical and cortico-subcortical tracts [34–41] including the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), prefrontal cortex (PFC), supplementary motor area (SMA), primary motor cortex (M1), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), amygdala, basal ganglia, thalamus, and cerebellum, respectively [34–43]. We hypothesize that the DLPFC–TPJ montage may enable the induced electric field to engage a network comprising the prefrontal, motor, anterior cingulate, and temporoparietal cortices—regions implicated in the pathophysiology of catatonia [44]. Figure 1 shows the distribution of the induced electric field generated by this montage, modeled using the ROAST toolbox [45] (Realistic vOlumetric Approach to Simulate Transcranial Electric Stimulation). Moreover, the same frontotemporal montage has been demonstrated to elicit dopaminergic release within polysynaptically connected subcortical structures, most notably the left executive striatum—a region of particular relevance in catatonia, where a striatal hypodopaminergic state has been implicated [35].

The CATATOES study aims to assess the efficacy and safety of tDCS in a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, bicentric superiority trial involving 70 patients with catatonia. Our secondary objectives include highlighting the mechanism of action of tDCS in patients with catatonia using functional and diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) along with a 64-channel resting state electroencephalogram (EEG) before and after the tDCS course. Furthermore, we aim to investigate the pathophysiology of catatonia by integrating MRI, EEG, and blood tests with inflammatory, neurogenesis and genetic markers [46–48].

Methods

Patients

CATATOES is a bicenter study with the University Hospital Group Paris Psychiatry and Neurosciences (GHU) and health public establishment (EPS) Ville Evrard. The inclusion criteria are: (1)age over 18 years old, (2)suffering from an episode of catatonia according to DSM-5 criteria, (3)persistence of DSM-5 catatonia criteria after 24 h on lorazepam, or contraindication to lorazepam, or poor tolerance to lorazepam, (4)patient (or guardian or trusted person) who has given written informed consent, beneficiary of a social security scheme. Exclusion criteria are: (1)malignant catatonia (i.e. the existence of clinical severity criteria leading to transfer to intensive care, systolic blood pressure < 90mmHg or > 180mmHg; diastolic blood pressure < 60mmHg or > 110mmHg; body temperature < 36.0 °C or > 38.0; Heart Rate < 40 or > 120), (2)patients treated with ECT, (3)pregnant or breast-feeding women, (4)patient with contraindications to tDCS, i.e. defibrillator or pacemaker wearer, brain stimulator, presence of intracranial metals, uncovered craniectomy or after trepanning. As catatonia is a transnosologic condition, we decided to include all patients with catatonia regardless of the underlying diagnosis. We did not set an upper age limit, as catatonia is prevalent among older adults [49]who often face greater barriers to accessing ECT [50]. Moreover, tDCS is well tolerated in this population, offering a favorable benefit–risk balance [51]. In the case of a treatable etiology of catatonia, the patient will receive the recommended etiological treatment. The two participating centers will screen patients among hospitalised and outpatient populations.

Protocol design

This study adopts a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group, bicentric superiority design using a sequential group adaptive approach, see Fig. 2. Randomisation at the inclusion visit (V0) assigns patients to active or sham tDCS in a 1:1 ratio, stratified by center and duration of the catatonic syndrome (greater or less than six months), see Fig. 2. The neuroConn DC-Stimulator-Plus tDCS device (neuroConn, Ilmenau, Germany) will be uniformly used across centers. Our protocol will involve anodal stimulation in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and cathodal stimulation in the left temporoparietal junction (TPJ). Participants will undergo 20 sessions of active or sham tDCS, administered twice daily with 20 min between sessions, at two mA for 20 min. The short interval between sessions has been shown to enhance excitability changes by inducing late-phase effects [52] and was well tolerated in a previously reported case of catatonia [53]. Sham tDCS involves a ramp-up at the session’s onset and a ramp-down at the end, each lasting 4 s at an intensity of 2 mA, ensuring the double-blind procedure [54]. Additionally, to guarantee the double-blind nature of the study, the rater i.e. the medical doctor evaluating the patient, will not be involved in conducting the tDCS sessions. Treatment will begin after the first evaluation visit (V1), with follow-up visits after each treatment day (V2 to V11). The primary endpoint, evaluating the percentage of responders, defined as a ≥ 30% reduction in Bush and Francis Catatonia rating scale (BFCRS) [55]is assessed after 10 tDCS sessions. To ensure consistency, the same rater conducts all longitudinal BFCRS assessments for each participant. Participants will be followed up for 28 days post-treatment with a weekly visit (V12 to V14). The CATATOES study involves the inclusion of 70 patients. Using a sequential adaptive approach, an initial futility analysis will be conducted after 22 participants have completed the study, followed by a second interim analysis after 44 participants, assessing both efficacy and futility thresholds based on O’Brien-Fleming spending functions. Should the second interim analysis fall within a promising zone, an adaptive sample size re-estimation will be performed based on conditional power analysis, with the results provided to the Independent Data Monitoring Committee (IDMC). The IDMC will closely examine the safety and efficacy data from these interim analyses and provide recommendations for the continuation of the study. In cases of malignant catatonia—defined by clinical signs [56] such as body temperature < 36.0 °C or > 38.0 °C, autonomic instability (e.g., tachycardia, labile or elevated blood pressure, diaphoresis, tachypnea, cyanosis), and/or extreme rigidity, impaired consciousness, refusal of food or fluids, and dehydration, as well as laboratory abnormalities (e.g., elevated creatine phosphokinase [CPK], leukocytosis, abnormal serum iron, elevated transaminases, creatinine, hyperglycemia, or significant electrolyte imbalances such as hypo-/hypernatremia)—or in any life-threatening situation, patients will be withdrawn from the study and referred for treatment with electroconvulsive therapy (ECT).

Measures

Clinical assessment

Clinical assessments will include anamnestic data (socio-demographic characteristics, treatment history, medical history) and information on current treatment (name and dosage of treatments). To note, drug treatments, particularly lorazepam, may be modified throughout the protocol. Psychometric assessments will include the Bush Francis catatonia rating scale (BFCRS) to assess catatonia severity as well as the Montgomery and Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS), the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), the Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) to evaluate underlying psychiatric disorder, and a patient satisfaction scale. The clinical examination will include an overall clinical examination of heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, cardiovascular, respiratory, neurovegetative physiological parameters, skin conductance, and accelerometry data. Adverse events (itching, burning, scratching, cutaneous eruption, headache) will also be collected.

Biological sampling

Biological blood samples will include biochemical, renal, hepatic, endocrine, inflammation and neurogenesis tests at three times points, i.e. V1, V6 and V11, notably regarding the hypothesis on neuro-inflammation [46, 47]. Whole genome sequencing will also be realised if the patient consents to it to detect single nucleotide variants and copy number variants associated with catatonia [48]. A cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) sample will be collected at V1 as a routine exam to rule out encephalopathy and to explore inflammation and neurogenesis [6]. Patients can be included even if they refuse or cannot benefit from all the complementary exams.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

All scans will be performed on a 3 T MRI (Vantage Galan 3T/XGO; Canon Medical Systems) with a 32-channel head coil at the GHU Paris Psychiatry and Neuroscience imaging platform. MRI will be done at 2 times points V1 and V11 with the following sequences : 3D T1-weighted MPRAGE structural imaging with 180 sagittal slices, matrix = 256 × 256, field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm2, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3, TR = 2500ms, TE = 3ms, TI = 950 ms, FA = 9°, BW = 279 Hz; 3D sagittal FLAIR with 170 slices, image matrix = 224 × 224, FOV = 230 × 230 mm2, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3, TR = 7000ms, TE = 445.5ms, TI = 2200ms, BW = 558 Hz; Diffusion Imaging using a spin-echo echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence with the following parameters: TE/TR = 87/8336 ms, image matrix = 128 × 128, FOV = 256 × 256 mm2, voxel size = 2 × 2 × 2 mm, 70 slices, 3 shells: 60 diffusion directions at b-value of 2000 s/mm2, 30 directions at b = 1000 s/mm2 and 15 directions at b = 300 s/mm2, b = 0 s/mm2 acquired every 15 directions, and seven b = 0 s/mm2 acquired with reverse phase encoding direction; Resting-state functional MRI using a gradient-echo T2*-weighted echo planar imaging sequence in an axial orientation with TR = 2600ms, TE = 30ms, matrix = 72 × 64, FOV = 216 × 192 mm2, 43 slices, slice thickness = 3 mm, 234 acquired volumes; Perfusion images with a pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling sequence (pCASL) with TR = 4950ms, TE = 12.3ms, labeling duration = 1800 ms, post labeling delay = 1800 ms, FOV = 256 × 256 mm2, image matrix = 64 × 64, 23 slices, slice thickness = 6 mm.

Electroencephalogram (EEG)

An EEG will be recorded at 2 times points V1 and V11 with the eyes closed in the resting state. At the GHU neurophysiological department, we will use a 64-channel EEG cap (from Biosemi).

Statistical power estimates

In line with the primary objective, responders are defined as patients who show at least a 30% reduction in the BFCRS score after 10 tDCS sessions. Given the characteristics of the target population—hospitalised subjects suffering from catatonia and unresponsive to lorazepam—a proportion of at least 35% of patients responding to the tDCS treatment is considered clinically significant. Additionally, up to 5% of patients in the control group are estimated to show a 30% decrease in BFCRS score in the absence of treatment. The sample size was estimated using a Group-Sequential Superiority Test for two proportions, which determined that, with a bilateral alpha level of 0.05 and a beta of 0.10, a sample size of 33 patients per group is required.

Given the patient characteristics, the dropout rate for the primary objective evaluation period is estimated at a maximum of 5%. Consequently, the study will enroll 70 patients (35 in the tDCS group and 35 in the sham tDCS group). This sequential group superiority design will be implemented with an approximate statistical power of 90%, employing O’Brien-Fleming spending functions to define efficacy and futility boundaries.

Statistical analysis design

All statistical analyses will be carried out using RStudio. The baseline is defined as the last assessment performed before the start of study treatment at V1, see Fig. 2. All data collected after the start of the study treatment are defined as post-baseline. Baseline characteristics will be summarised by treatment group. For continuous measures, mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR) for asymmetric variables will be summarised by treatment. Categorical variables will be described by the proportion in each category and summarised by treatment with participant numbers and percentages.

The primary objective will assess treatment response, defined as a ≥ 30% reduction in the BFCRS score from baseline. Responders (≥ 30% reduction) and non-responders (< 30% reduction, including those needing ECT or who died) will be classified dichotomously. Treatment efficacy will be expressed as an adjusted Odds Ratio, calculated using a binary logistic regression model with center and catatonia duration (≥ 6 months vs. <6 months) as covariates. The significance level will be adjusted to account for the number of interim analyses, with final hypothesis testing at an overall bilateral significance level of 0.05 and corresponding 95% confidence intervals provided.

For longitudinal variables (repeated measures), extended mixed models using latent classes and latent processes will be used, with treatment (tDCS/Sham-tDCS) and baseline characteristics as ex-post controls. To control for potential variability in the timing between sessions, the interval duration will be recorded and included as a covariate in the extended mixed-effects models as well as treatment equivalent dosage. Subgroup analyses will be performed to assess the consistency of the treatment effect between subgroups without multiplicity adjustment. The following subgroups will be evaluated for the primary endpoint: underlying diagnosis (psychotic disorder, mood disorder, neurodevelopmental disorder), associated treatment, and initial severity (BFCRS < median value, BFCRS > = median value). The treatment effect for the total population and the subgroups will be represented using Forest plot graphs. To handle missing data, for the analysis of the primary endpoint, in the event of study withdrawal before evaluation of the primary endpoint (V6), the last BFCRS value recorded will be used according to the Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF). In the event of treatment discontinuation due to ECT or death, the patient will be considered a non-responder regardless of the last BFCRS test result recorded. For all other variables, in the event of a significant proportion of missing data (> 10% of subjects), a sensitivity analysis using multiple conditional imputation by chained equations (MICE) techniques will be carried out, according to a conservative principle, using the mean BFCRS score at inclusion as the central position parameter and an estimate of intra-patient variability in the placebo group as the dispersion parameter.

Image analysis

From structural MRI, we will extract volumetric data by region of interest (FreeSurfer 7.0.0) and at voxel level (Statistical Parametric Mapping). Following on from our previous work [57]we will also extract qualitative sulcogyral patterns and quantitative gyrification index (BrainVisa and FreeSurfer software).

From diffusion MRI, microstructural regional analyses using neurite imaging data derived from the Neurite Orientation Dispersion and Density Imaging (NODDI) biophysical model of diffusion.

Network analysis of structural and functional connectivity will be performed from white diffusion MRI derived matter fiber tractography and resting-state functional MRI.

We will run quantitative resting state EEG analysis using EEG markers reflecting the neuronal excitation and inhibition balance, including power spectral density, excitation/inhibition (E/I) ratio, microstates [58–61]as well as connectivity and source localisation analysis. The EEG data will be analysed with the MNE EEG software on Python.

Discussion

The CATATOES study represents a pioneering effort as the first randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, superiority-designed trial evaluating the efficacy of tDCS in patients with catatonia. Despite the prevalence and substantial morbidity-mortality burden associated with this syndrome, the dearth of studies employing robust protocol designs is a noteworthy gap in the current literature [1, 62]. This study, with a large number of catatonia patients, answers an urgent need for improvement in the field, as asked by the latest publication [1]. tDCS emerges as a promising intervention, especially given its potential implications in addressing the challenges posed by lorazepam-resistant catatonia. In acute episodes where lorazepam proves ineffective, tDCS emerges as a potential strategy to enhance prognosis while awaiting electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), especially in light of limited ECT availability and the requisite pre-ECT work-up, including anaesthesia consultation.

In chronic catatonia associated with neurodevelopmental disorders [3, 8]where extended courses of ECT are often necessary [63, 64]tDCS presents an appealing preventive measure. In particular, tDCS offers the advantage of bypassing the cognitive impairments and anaesthesia risks associated with ECT. For example, in Phelan McDermid syndrome (PMS), characterised by deletion or mutation of the SHANK 3 gene, a rare condition often associated with catatonia (up to 50% of patients), tDCS led to improvement in all PMS cases with recovery of speech, sphincter continence and tranquilization [24]. In addition, two patients with catatonia in Down syndrome regression disorder (DSRD) were successfully treated with tDCS [18]as was a patient with Rett syndrome involving the MEPC2 gene [20].

Beyond clinical outcomes, the CATATOES study is designed to provide mechanistic insights into the neurobiology of catatonia through multimodal imaging and electrophysiological approaches. One of the study’s central aims is to characterize how tDCS modulates brain network activity and connectivity patterns in catatonia, using functional and diffusion MRI and high-density EEG. This approach offers a unique opportunity to examine how stimulation of key cortical hubs—particularly the DLPFC and TPJ—may influence broader neural circuits implicated in motor, emotional, and cognitive regulation [35]. Using functional resting-state MRI, diffusion MRI, and resting-state EEG before and after treatment will enable us to identify changes in connectivity. In addition, this study aims to delve deeper into the pathophysiology of catatonia and to identify diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers. We believe that, regarding the excitation/inhibition (E/I) imbalance hypothesis in the pathophysiology of catatonia, supported by the efficacy of GABA-A agonists [2]anti-NMDA encephalitis [46] and neuroimaging studies [44, 65]we anticipate that EEG markers reflecting neuronal E/I balance will contribute to diagnostic biomarkers and enhance our understanding of catatonia’s pathophysiology [58]. In addition, whole genome sequencing and immunology testing will contribute to classifying the underlying cause of catatonia and provide crucial insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms [46, 48].

Limitations

This study will address several methodological issues. The inclusion of acute and chronic catatonia, despite likely differing underlying pathophysiology and tDCS effects, is carefully managed through randomisation based on the duration of the catatonic episode. Similarly, as catatonia is a transnosologic condition probably linked to various mechanisms, it may elicit different responses to tDCS. Therefore, patients will be meticulously analysed in subgroups during further analysis. While MRI feasibility in catatonia patients has been demonstrated [66]we acknowledge potential challenges in those with agitation and remain vigilant in addressing these obstacles. Additionally, the sham tDCS is commonly employed but exhibits significant variability [67]. Therefore, we carefully established the parameters to ensure double-blindness and minimize potential effects. Finally, missing data will be handled using the Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF) method. However, given the well-known fluctuations of catatonia symptoms over time, the LOCF method may have limitations in this context [68].

Conclusion

The CATATOES study is the first randomised clinical trial to evaluate the efficacy of tDCS in a large sample of patients with catatonia. Catatonia strongly needs therapeutic improvement, and we hypothesise that this study will encourage the use of tDCS in routine treatment for lorazepam-resistant catatonia and thus help reduce the morbidity and cost associated with it.
