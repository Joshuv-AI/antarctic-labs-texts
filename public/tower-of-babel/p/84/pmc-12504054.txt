Mitochondrial blood‐based biomarker is related to cardiorespiratory fitness and aging in a sex‐dependent manner
Authors: Riley E. Kemna, Amanda Szabo‐Reed, Hana D. Mayfield, Paul J. Kueck, Jenae Pennington, Casey S. John, Brittany M. Hauger, Heather M. Wilkins, Eric D. Vidoni, Jill K. Morris
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONA sedentary lifestyle increases the risk for Alzheimer's disease (AD), whereas exercise has been shown to benefit brain health. Physiological factors, such as female sex, are linked to lower cardiorespiratory fitness and can increase the risk of AD, which might impact exercise benefits to the brain. Exploring cellular mechanisms underlying fitness in older adults is essential to understanding exercise and AD risk and how sex might impact this interaction.METHODSWe collected blood from 34 cognitively healthy older adults (age 65+, 18 male, 16 female) enrolled in the COMbined Exercise Trial (COMET; NCT04848038). Subjects underwent a blood draw and clinical assessments for cardiorespiratory fitness and body composition. Blood was collected in ACD tubes, and lymphocytes were isolated. Fluorescent stains used were MitoTracker, Annexin V, MitoSOX, TMRE (tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester), and Hoechst, analyzed by flow cytometry, and used to calculate a composite mitochondrial function index (MFI).RESULTSAs expected, males had higher lean mass and VO2peak than females (p = 0.01), but groups did not differ in body mass index (p = 0.51). Males had a higher MFI compared to females (p = 0.01). Within each sex, we observed unique metabolic relationships. In males, there was an age‐associated decline in MFI (R 2 = 0.382, p = 0.01). In females, our systemic measure of mitochondrial superoxides had a negative relationship with lean mass (R 2 = 0.648, p < 0.01) and oxygen uptake efficiency slope (OUES) (R 2 = 0.271, p = 0.04).DISCUSSIONWe combined an MFI with measures related to fitness in a cognitively healthy older adult population. We explored physiological factors that impact cardiorespiratory fitness, such as sex. We observed relationships between mitochondrial superoxides and OUES and lean mass in females, whereas males had higher MFI overall. Sex‐dependent differences in mitochondrial function and superoxide might be an underlying factor of variable cardiorespiratory fitness between sexes and could help explain differences in AD risk.Highlights Mitochondrial blood‐biomarker shows sex‐dependent relationships in aging.Mitochondrial function index is higher in older adult males.Mitochondrial function index declines with age in males.Mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) are negatively associated with fitness in females.Mitochondrial ROS are negatively associated with lean mass in females.

INTRODUCTION

A sedentary lifestyle and low cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) are associated with an increased risk of dementia, including Alzheimer's disease (AD). 1 , 2 Prior work has shown a decline in CRF, which is associated with frontal and subcortical atrophy in nondemented individuals. 3 It is critical to understand the mechanisms underlying fitness to understand its impact on brain health and how it might modulate the risk of AD. Sedentary lifestyle is directly associated with worsened cognitive impairment 4 and is indirectly related to elevated AD risk through increased comorbidities such as metabolic and cardiovascular diseases, which can subsequently increase risk of AD development. 5 AD risk is also increased in females, who are twice as likely to develop AD compared to male individuals, and two‐thirds of all individuals with AD are female. 6 While factors such as longevity, hormone changes with menopause may play a role, females also have lower CRF than men. Prior research supports that exercise adaptation after training involves increased CRF, 7 increased mitochondrial mass, 8 as well as increased mitochondrial metabolism and mitochondrial proteins, 9 which are essential for adenosine triphosphate (ATP) generation for cellular function. These exercise effects could be particularly beneficial in AD, as impaired mitochondrial function and increased oxidative stress have been observed in AD pathology. 10 , 11

Here, we sought to understand how measures of mitochondrial function relate to CRF in a sedentary older adult population, as this population is at disproportionate risk of AD. We combined an index score of mitochondrial function 12 with cardiorespiratory fitness and body composition measures to explore underlying cellular mechanisms related to fitness in older adults. We further examined the impact of sex‐dependent differences in the relationship between mitochondrial function and CRF, as it is well‐described that females have lower measures of cardiorespiratory fitness, such as VO2 peak and oxygen uptake efficiency slope (OUES), compared to males, and female sex confers a higher risk of AD.

METHODS

We performed a cross‐sectional analysis to investigate the relationship between mitochondrial function and cardiorespiratory fitness and to determine if this might describe underlying mechanisms between exercise and brain health. We analyzed blood‐based mitochondrial biomarkers and leveraged graded exercise test outcomes to understand how mitochondrial and fitness measures are related.

Participants

Thirty‐four cognitively healthy sedentary older adults (age 65‐80) were selected as a convenience sample from the COMbined Exercise Trial (COMET) (NCT02913664) and were balanced for sex. COMET was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) (Study #146904) and is in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were enrolled upon informed consent. Individuals were excluded if they were considered active, which is defined by Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines of 30 minutes of moderate physical activity most days of the week, or if they engaged in progressive resistance training 2 or more times a week, as described by Telephone Assessment of Physical Activity (TAPA). 13 Extensive discussion of study inclusion and exclusion criteria was previously described. 14 Sex and age were self‐reported by the participants. All other demographic information, including height, weight, apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype, body composition, blood biomarkers, and fitness measures, was collected during the study visit by the study team.

Graded exercise test

Participants underwent a graded maximal exercise test to assess cardiorespiratory fitness, following a Modified Balke protocol with constant speed and 2% grade increases every 2 min. 15 The initial speed was determined before the graded exercise test based on heart rate response and rate of perceived exertion.

Blood collection

Following a minimum of a 4‐h fast, blood was collected into a Vacutainer tube with acid citrate dextrose (ACD) and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) anticoagulants. APOE genotype was determined from ACD whole blood by a single‐nucleotide polymorphism allelic discrimination assay using a Taqman Sample‐to‐SNP Kit (ThermoFisher) according to manufacturer instructions. 16 Individuals were considered APOE carriers if they were heterozygous or homozygous carriers of APOE4. The remaining ACD whole blood was processed and analyzed for mitochondrial function index (MFI) within 6 hours of collection. EDTA tubes were centrifuged at 1500 × g (4°C) for 10 minutes, and plasma was collected. Plasma was stored at −80°C for later analyses.

Plasma biomarker analysis

Plasma pTau217 was analyzed by Lumipulse G1200 (Fujirebio) from EDTA plasma according to manufacturer instructions.

Anthropometric measures

Body mass index was calculated from height and weight. In addition, body composition was measured by dual‐energy x‐ray absorptiometry (DXA). Measures collected included fat mass, fat‐free mass, and body fat percentage.

Mitochondrial function index

Measures for MFI were analyzed as previously described. 12 ACD whole blood was added to Accuspin tubes (#A1805, Sigma‐Aldrich) with warmed Histopaque‐1077 (#10771, Sigma‐Aldrich) and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 15 minutes. Lymphocytes were isolated and added to a separate tube with Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) (#14025092, Gibco). Lymphocytes were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 minutes, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were resuspended in HBSS. Lymphocytes were aliquoted into four separate tubes, and fluorescent dyes were added as follows: TMRE/Hoechst, MitoSOX/Hoechst, MitoTracker Red, and a blank negative. Tubes with lymphocytes, Hank's balanced salt solution (HBSS), and dyes were incubated at 37°C in a CO2 incubator for 30 min. After incubation, lymphocytes were immediately centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 minutes, washed with HBSS, and centrifuged again at 3000 rpm for 5 minutes. Lymphocytes were resuspended in HBSS or Annexin V binding buffer + Alexa Fluor 488 and placed on wet ice until analysis by flow cytometer (Cytek Aurora).

Statistical Analysis

The difference in number of APOE4 carriers was tested by Pearson chi‐squared test. Group differences between sexes of continuous variables (i.e., age, BMI, RERrest, RERpeak, lean mass, body fat percentage, OUES, VO2peak, MFI, pTau217) were analyzed by unpaired t‐test. Continuous variables were analyzed by ordinary least squares (OLS) regression with age and APOE4 as covariates. pTau217 was skewed between groups and was log‐transformed for statistical analysis. Sex was used as a predictor and tested for both significant interactions and sex stratified analyses. OLS regression results were reported with R‐squared values and p‐values. Regression estimates, 95% confidence intervals, R‐squared values, and p‐values can be found in Tables S1–S4. The significance threshold was set at α = 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed in IBM SPSS (29.0) and SAS (9.4). Figures were made in GraphPad (Prism 10).

RESULTS

Study cohort demographics

Our study assessed 34 older adults with a mean age of 70.9 years, an average BMI of 30.06 (kg/m2), and 26.4% of participants were APOE4 carriers. Split by sex (Table 1), male and female groups were not significantly different in age (p = 0.51), BMI (p = 0. 51), number of APOE4 carriers (p = 0.87), respiratory exchange rate (RER) at rest (p = 0.72), or peak RER (p = 0.67). Males had higher lean mass (p < 0.01), OUES (p < 0.01), VO2peak (p = 0.01), and MFI (p = 0.01) compared to females. Females had higher body fat percentage compared to males (p < 0.01). Each male and female group included two individuals who identified as Black or African American and one who identified as Latino or Hispanic. Plasma pTau217 did not differ between males and females (p = 0.61).

Sex effects in mitochondrial function index

We characterized baseline measures of mitochondrial function, including mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS), membrane potential, mitochondrial mass, and apoptotic cell death. These measures were used to calculate a “mitochondrial function index” (MFI) score to assess mitochondrial function. Here, we observe significant differences in overall MFI between males and females, with lower MFI in females (p = 0.01) (Figure 1A). An interaction between age and sex approached significance (p = 0.09). We observed an age‐dependent reduction in MFI with age, specifically in males (p = 0.01, R 2 = 0.382), with no relationship in females (Figure 1B). Annexin V, a measure of apoptotic cell death, trended higher in females (p = 0.051) (Figure 1C), and membrane potential (TMRE) trended lower in females (p = 0.08) (Figure 1D). Mitochondrial ROS and mitochondrial mass were not significantly different by sex (Figure 1E,F).

Reduced lean mass and sub‐maximal fitness are related to mitochondrial ROS in females

We observed a significant interaction between lean mass and sex (p = 0.04). Subsequent analyses were then stratified by sex, where we observed that lean mass and resting mitochondrial ROS were negatively related in females (p < 0.01, R 2 = 0.648), and there was no observed relationship in males (Figure 1G,H). OUES is a measure of oxygen uptake efficiency during submaximal exercise. While we did not observe a significant interaction between OUES and sex, we conducted stratified analyses according to our a priori hypotheses to investigate sex effects. OUES was negatively related to resting mitochondrial ROS in females (p = 0.04, R 2 = 0.271), with no relationship observed in males (Figure 1I,J).

DISCUSSION

Low CRF is associated with AD diagnosis and is lower in females, 17 however, the underlying mechanisms of how these factors impact disease risk are still unclear. Mitochondrial respiration declines in aging 18 and is associated with low CRF in older adults. 19 , 20 Here, we measured characteristics of mitochondrial function, which were combined into a composite MFI and used to investigate how mitochondrial function relates to CRF in a sedentary older adult cohort. We found sex‐specific relationships of the MFI and mitochondrial ROS with measures of lean mass and OUES.

The MFI comprises of mitochondrial ROS, membrane potential, mitochondrial mass, and apoptosis. Mitochondrial membrane potential is a critical component of ATP generation, and is dependent upon hydrogen ions pumped into the mitochondrial intermembrane space. 21 Mitochondrial ROS are essential for mitochondrial adaptation and are produced during exercise. However, ROS accumulation, as is seen in aging, can result in mitochondrial and cellular capacity to uptake oxygen and generate ATP through oxidative phosphorylation. 22 Apoptosis is programmed cell death, which is an essential regulatory mechanism to prevent cellular irregularities that can result in aberrant cells. 23 Measures of these different mitochondrial characteristics were used to calculate an MFI in a sedentary older adult population.

Due to female sex conferring increased risk of AD, we explored how these measures were impacted by sex. Studies of sex‐dependent differences in mitochondrial function have been variable, with some showing females having higher respiration, 24 while others show increased respiration in males. 25 However, a blood‐based biomarker of mitochondrial function, such as an MFI, in relation to sex has never been explored. Our study showed that males had higher MFI compared to females. We did not observe sex‐dependent differences in individual mitochondrial outcomes; however, females trended toward increased apoptotic measures and reduced membrane potential.

The observed sex‐dependent differences in MFI are likely influenced by many underlying mechanisms. One of which could be hormonal effects of estrogen, as mitochondria are known to have estrogen receptors that can result in downstream gene transcription and translation of mitochondrial DNA. 26 We observed an overall lower MFI in females compared to males, which could be partially influenced by low levels of estrogen, given that our cohort was all of post‐menopausal age. This could be relevant in the case of AD, where a decline in estrogen is thought to be tied to an increased risk of AD development. 27 Meta‐analyses show that mid‐life estrogen therapy could be a viable intervention to reduce AD risk. 28 While estrogen is known to act within the brain, our understanding of its role in AD development and the underlying mechanisms is still limited, and the benefit of estrogen therapy could also be, in part, due to improvements in cellular bioenergetics.

Prior research has shown that males have higher mitochondrial ROS, 29 which we did not observe in our cohort, and this could be due to a low sample size. We observed that baseline mitochondrial ROS was negatively correlated with OUES and lean mass in females. OUES is a measure of submaximal fitness and has been shown to be a reliable measure of CRF in older adults. 30 OUES does not require maximal effort during exercise testing, resulting in a safer and more reliable fitness assessment in our cohort. Lean mass is directly related to CRF. 31 These relationships suggest elevated mitochondrial ROS is an underlying factor of reduced fitness in older females, which could contribute to their increased risk for diseases like AD, where oxidative damage and mitochondrial ROS are thought to play key roles in disease pathology. 10 , 11 This supports studying an exercise intervention to reduce AD risk, as improved fitness and increased lean mass could reduce cell‐damaging mitochondrial ROS.

Other studies have shown that measures of peripheral blood cell respiration are related to fluorodeoxyglucose‐positron emission tomography measures of brain glucose metabolism, 32 whereas other work did not observe relationships between peripheral blood cells and metabolites within the brain measured with magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging. 33 This could suggest that measures of mitochondrial function in peripheral blood cells are reflective of functional measures from the brain, but do not correlate with cerebral metabolite profiles. Other work has shown peripheral mononuclear blood cells (PBMCs) contained lower mitochondrial protein and DNA content from individuals with AD compared to healthy controls, 34 suggesting peripheral impairment of mitochondrial function, as is observed in the brain in AD. 10 However, future work in this field is highly relevant and necessary to develop peripheral biomarkers of changes in cerebral metabolism.

Strengths of this study include our application of a novel blood‐based mitochondrial biomarker in a study cohort of well‐characterized older adults that were balanced in age, sex, and APOE4 genotype. However, it is important to consider the limitations of this study, including a small sample size with limited racial and ethnic diversity. This also limited our ability to evaluate the effect of comorbid conditions such as diabetes or vascular disease. Related biomarkers such as blood glucose and lipids have not yet been analyzed, as the study is ongoing. These limitations could impact the generalizability of our findings and warrant further investigation into these relationships and replication of findings in a larger, more diverse cohort.

This study aimed to leverage a blood‐based biomarker of mitochondrial function to explore how underlying cellular mechanisms might impact cardiorespiratory fitness. These relationships are crucial to understand, as exercise has been shown to benefit brain health and reduce AD risk. Our observed correlations of mitochondrial ROS with lean mass and OUES suggest that higher CRF might reduce cell‐damaging ROS, particularly in female subjects. Further investigation of these complex relationships is necessary to understand the mechanisms that fully underlie exercise and brain health. It is also unclear how MFI will change with exposure to an exercise intervention designed to improve CRF and other physical health parameters, including muscular strength and body composition.
